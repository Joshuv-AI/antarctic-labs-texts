Spatial metabolomics reveals the effects of Acanthopanax senticosus on region-specific alterations in neurotransmitters and metabolites levels in the brains of α-syn transgenic Parkinson’s disease model mice
Authors: Qi Zheng, Yi Lu, Donghua Yu, Liangyou Zhao, Yu Wang, Pingping Chen, Fang Lu, Shumin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcanthopanax senticosus (Rupr. et. Maxim.) Harms (ASH), a widely used traditional Chinese herbal medicine, possesses various therapeutic effects, such as the ability to tonify the spleen and kidney, strengthen muscles and bones, and promote consciousness and nerve relaxation. Previous research has indicated its good efficacy in treating Parkinson’s disease (PD). We aimed to investigate the effects of ASH on changes in neurotransmitter and metabolite levels within the brain tissue of α-synuclein (α-syn) transgenic mice with PD. Additionally, we sought to explore the potential mechanisms by which ASH treats PD and the targets of action of ASH. α-Syn transgenic mice were chosen as a suitable model of PD. Hematoxylin‒eosin (HE) staining was used to detect changes in neurons in the substantia nigra. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to measure tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) activity in the substantia nigra and the areal density of α-syn in the striatum. Moreover, we measured the pole climbing time and autonomous activity to assess motor ability. Nontargeted matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI-MSI) was used to investigate changes in neurotransmitter levels in each brain region of the mice and further identify the regions in which ASH exerts its effects. ASH reduced the climbing time and increased the number of autonomous movements in PD model mice. HE staining, along with IHC, indicated that ASH interfered with the accumulation of α-syn, increased TH activity, and mitigated neuronal damage. The MALDI-MSI results revealed that ASH could significantly increase dopamine (DA) levels in brain tissue by increasing the levels of 3-methoxytyramine (3-MT) in the striatum and cerebral cortex, acetylcholine (Ach) in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc), γ-amino butyric acid (GABA) in the hippocampus (Hip), and 3,4-dihydroxyphenylglycol (DOPEG) in the globus pallidus interna/substantia nigra reticulata (Gpi/SNr) complex, while decreasing 5-HIAA levels in the Hip, cerebral cortex, and striatum. Additionally, the behavioral correlation analysis suggested that the SNc may be a crucial region for the ability of ASH to ameliorate behavioral dysfunction in PD model mice. ASH can regulate DA release by modulating the metabolism of neurotransmitters in brain tissue, particularly in the SNc, thereby influencing the basal ganglia circuit and alleviating the symptoms of PD, and monoamine oxidase (MAO) plays a vital role throughout this metabolic process.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11011-025-01673-z.Keywords: Acanthopanax senticosus, Parkinson’s disease, MALDI-MSI, Dopamine, Neurotransmitters and metabolites

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a slowly-progressing multisystem neurodegenerative disease that primarily affects elderly people. The clinical manifestations of PD may include quiescent tremors, muscle rigidity and bradykinesia (Váradi 2020), and the symptoms may deteriorate over time. As PD progresses, patients may also experience gastrointestinal dysfunction, depression, cognitive dysfunction and other mental disorders (Samii et al. 2004; Aarsland et al. 2017). In severe cases, patients may lose the ability to take care of themselves. The main pathological mechanism is damage to dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) and the appearance of Lewy bodies composed of remnant neurons, which significantly decrease dopamine (DA) delivery to the striatum (Balestrino and Schapira 2020). However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in patients with PD remain unclear, primarily because of the intricate structure of the central nervous system (CNS). The pathogenesis of CNS disorders may involve interplay between various brain regions and the modulation of neurotransmitter release, along with neuronal death resulting from excitotoxicity. However, numerous nonmotor symptoms (NMSs), including cognitive and affective disorders, are also frequently observed in the later stages of PD. These findings suggest that dysfunction of the neurotransmitter network may be a significant feature of the biochemical alterations associated with PD. Research has shown that other neurotransmitter systems, including the noradrenergic, cholinergic, and 5-hydroxytryptaminergic systems, also play roles in PD (Scatton et al. 1983). With advancements in technologies such as neuroelectrophysiological monitoring, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), and optogenetics, researchers have found that dysfunction of neural circuits, which are regulated by multiple neurotransmitters, plays a critical role in the onset and progression of PD. In addition to the significant reduction in DA levels in the CNS during the progression of PD, other neurotransmitters, including acetylcholine (Ach), glutamate (Glu), and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), also play roles in disease pathogenesis (Zheng-Hai et al., 2017; Jamwal and Kumar 2019), and serotonin (5-HT) is also depleted in the striatum (Ehringer and Hornykiewicz 1960; Buddhala et al. 2015).

According to traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), PD is “flutter disease”, i.e., a syndrome of deficiency in origin and excess in superficiality (Zheng et al. (n.d.)). Deficiency in origin refers to liver and kidney deficiency, and qi and blood deficiency, and the main manifestation is the blockage of brain orifices such as phlegm, blood stasis, wind and toxins. The main treatment strategies are nourishing the liver and kidney, calming the liver and extinguishing the wind (Dong et al. 2023). Acanthopanax senticosus (Rupr. et. Maxim.) Harms (ASH) is a widely used TCM compound extracted from the dried roots and rhizomes of Eleutherococcus senticosus Maxim (Rupr. et. Maxim.), which replenishes qi, strengthens the spleen, tonifies the kidney and calms the nerves (Committee 2020). It is mainly used for the treatment of kidney deficiency caused by lumbar debility, body weakness, insomnia and dreaminess and has adaptogenic effects. Studies have shown that adjunct therapy with ASH can improve the clinical outcomes of patients with PD, extending the duration of the beneficial effects of other medications and alleviating reduced mobility at the end of the dosing period (Kuang et al. 2004; Cheng and Xia 2010). ASH has a broad range of biological activities, and modern pharmacological studies have shown that it primarily consists of glycosides, flavonoids, lignin, polysaccharides and other chemical components, along with a range of trace elements and amino acids, which have anti-inflammatory, antidepressant, neuroprotective, anticardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease, antitumor, antiaging, antiradiation, and antianxiety effects and enhance immunity(Bocharov et al. 2010). Our previous animal and cell-based experiments confirmed that the ASH extract can inhibit oxidative stress by regulating peripheral metabolism (Zhang et al. 2015) and may exert antiparkinsonian effects by inhibiting α-synuclein (α-syn) induced cell death (Li et al. 2014), preventing mitochondrial dysfunction (Liu et al. 2018), decreasing mitophagy (Shuai-Nan et al., 2016), and inhibiting the inflammatory response by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway (Li et al. 2016).

Mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) has emerged as a novel molecular imaging technique that integrates mass spectrometry analysis with two-dimensional spatial imaging. This approach facilitates in the situ analysis of both known and unknown metabolites, providing chemically resolved spatial information (Buchberger et al. 2018). MALDI-MSI enables the spatial visualization of metabolites within tissues through laser ionization. A matrix is directly applied to tissue sections to form eutectics with metabolites. Upon irradiation with a laser beam, ionization of the matrix occurs (either through proton gain or loss), transferring charges to the metabolites, which leads to their desorption and ionization (Lee et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2022). MALDI-MSI has been extensively employed in biomedical research and can be used to quickly and directly ascertain the spatial distribution and levels of neurotransmitters and metabolites within neural tissue sections (Shariatgorji et al. 2019; Sun et al. 2019). Additionally, it could be used to identify the effective components of ASH responsible for its effects in the mouse brain. In this study, we employed MALDI-MSI to evaluate alterations in neurotransmitter levels within the CNS of an α-syn transgenic mice and to identify the specific regions where these changes occurred, with the aim of mapping the distribution of neurotransmitters in the brain tissue of PD model mice and the ASH-treated mice and thereby elucidating the potential mechanisms and sites of action of ASH in PD. Our findings provide novel insights and strategies that may aid the prevention and treatment of PD (Graphical Abstract).

Materials

ASH extract preparation

ASH was purchased from Wuchang County, Heilongjiang Province (lot number: 170634) and identified according to the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020). ASH extract was prepared as follows: 10 volumes of 80% ethanol were used to extract ASH 3 times for 2 h each, the sample was concentrated to 500 g/L using a rotary evaporator (based on the crude drug quantity), and the samples were purified using AB-8 macroporous adsorption resin. The adsorption flow rate was set at 2 BV/h, and elution was performed with 30% ethanol at a volume of 9 BV, with an elution flow rate of 1 BV/h. The eluate was subsequently concentrated through rotary evaporation and freeze dried to isolate the effective components of ASH (Liu et al. 2012), resulting in a powder yield of 1.75%. The research team identified the primary components of Acanthopanax senticosus, which included eleutheroside B, eleutheroside E, chlorogenic acid, oleuropein, isofraxidin, and puerarin (Lu et al. 2023).

Bovine serum albumin (BSA), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and diaminobenzidine (DAB) were purchased from Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Nos. GC305010, G0002, and G1212). Animal preparation

All animal experiments conducted in this study adhered to the Law on the Protection of Laboratory Animals (Directive 86/609/EEC) and were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Heilongjiang University of Chinese Medicine. Male C57BL/6 mice and α-syn transgenic male C57BL/6 mice (8 months old, specific-pathogen free (SPF) level, weighing 30 ± 2 g) were purchased from Jiangsu Jicui Yaokang Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Jackson, Tg(Prnp-SNCA*a53T)83Vle) (license number: SCXK (Jiangsu, China)2018-0008). The mice were raised at the Experimental Animal Center of Heilongjiang University of Chinese Medicine and maintained at a constant temperature (22 ± 1℃) in a humidity-controlled environment (50%±5%) on a 12:12 h light: dark cycle with free access to water.

After acclimatization to the new environment (7 days), the α-syn transgenic mice were randomly divided into a model group (PD group) and an ASH group. Male C57BL/6 mice of the same age were selected as the control group (Con) group, and there were 8 mice in each group. Mice in the ASH group were orally gavaged with ASH extract daily for 20 days, whereas those in the Con and PD groups were administered saline at the same time. The optimal dose of the ASH was 61.25 mg/kg (equivalent to 3.5 g/kg of crude drug), as determined in our previous study (Liu et al. 2012; Zhang et al. 2015).

Sample collection

All procedures were conducted under aseptic conditions. The mice were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital 12 h after the final drug administration and euthanized by exsanguination. The brains were then extracted, and the tissues were dissected on a board on ice, washed with saline to remove blood, water and hair; and immediately stored in liquid nitrogen. Finally, the samples were kept in a tin foil box at −80℃.

Methods

Neurobehavioral observations(Liu et al. 2012; Ren et al. 2016)

Pole climbing test

The pole climbing test is a classic method for assessing the motor coordination of PD model mice; in this test, the time taken for a mouse to descend from the top of a pole to the bottom (landing on both front paws) is measured. The apparatus consisted of a wooden pole (50 cm in height, 1.5 cm in diameter, wrapped with gauze to prevent slipping) with a wooden ball at the top. During the adaptive feeding phase, the mice were trained daily to climb from the top to the bottom of the pole, with additional training sessions conducted weekly following the intervention. The testing procedure was as follows: the tails of the mice were held, their heads were positioned downward at the top of the pole (all limbs held on the top of the pole), after which they were allowed to climb down naturally, and the climbing time was recorded. The time for each mouse to stand on the top of the pole was recorded as A, and the time for the mouse to climb to the bottom of the wooden pole (with both forelimbs touching the bottom platform of the pole) was recorded as B. The final climbing time for each mouse, C, was calculated as A-B. Each mouse underwent three trials with a minimum interval of 30 min between each test, and the average time was computed. If a mouse slipped or jumped off the pole, the time was not recorded, and the test was repeated after an additional 30-minute interval.

Autonomous activity test

The autonomous activity test was used to measure spontaneous changes in the locomotor activity of mice. The mice were placed into a spontaneous activity counter (Beijing Jinotai Technology Development Co., Ltd., Model: ZZ-6) in a quiet environment, with one mouse assigned to each grid to allow free movement. Following a 2-minute preadaptation period, the instrument was activated, and the number of spontaneous movements by the mice over a 5-minute interval was automatically recorded (Gao et al. 2020). A single vertical movement was registered each time a mouse stoop upright, and a horizontal movement was registered each time a mouse took a step. Total autonomous activity was calculated by summing the numbers of vertical and horizontal movements. The instrument was wiped with an alcohol-soaked cloth immediately after each trial to eliminate any residual odor from the previous mouse that could affect the movement of the subsequent mouse, and the next trial commenced only after the alcohol had fully evaporated to ensure accurate results.

Hematoxylin‒eosin staining

Brain tissues from three mice in the Con group, the PD group and the ASH group were randomly selected. The tissues were fixed with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h and then embedded in paraffin. The samples were subsequently sectioned and stained with hematoxylin for 8 min. After being rinsed with tap water to remove excess dye from the tissue sections, the samples were treated with 1% hydrochloric alcohol and stained with eosin for 5 min. The sections were washed with tap water again until they were colorless. Finally, the samples were dehydrated using a gradient of ethanol solutions, made transparent with toluene and mounted with neutral resin for the morphological analysis.

Immunohistochemical staining for Tyrosine Hydroxylase (TH) and α-syn

The brain tissues of three mice from each group were randomly selected and fixed with 4% neutral formaldehyde for 24 h. The tissues were then dewaxed using an environmentally friendly dewaxing solution and subsequently hydrated with anhydrous ethanol. Following antigen retrieval, the slides were immersed in a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 25 min. The sections were placed in PBS (pH 7.4) on a decolorizing table and washed three times for 5 min each to block endogenous peroxidase activity. The tissue sections were evenly covered dropwise with 3% BSA and blocked at room temperature for 30 min. After washing with gentle shaking to remove the blocking solution, primary antibodies against TH (5206 S, CST) and α-syn (2642 S, CST) diluted at a 1:50 ratio were added, ensuring full contact with the tissue sections. The slides were then incubated in a humid chamber at 4℃ overnight. After this incubation, the slides were placed in PBS (pH 7.4) and washed by shaking on a decolorizing shaker 3 times for 5 min each. After a brief drying period, the sections were incubated with a goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody at room temperature for 50 min. After color development with diaminobenzidine (DAB), the cell nuclei were stained with hematoxylin, and the sections were dehydrated, made transparent, and sealed with a neutral adhesive. Finally, the sections were observed under an optical microscope (Nikon, E100), and images were captured. The hematoxylin-stained nuclei appeared blue, while DAB staining (brownish-yellow) indicated positive staining. Positive staining was analyzed using ImageJ image analysis software; TH expression was assessed as the percentage of TH-positive cells, and α-syn expression was evaluated by calculating the areal densityof α-syn staining.

Matrix-Aassisted Laser Desorption Ionization‒Mass Spectrometry Imaging (MALDI-MSI)

We further investigated the mechanism of action of ASH in the treatment of PD and aimed to understand how ASH exerts its therapeutic effects on brain tissue by conducting a comprehensive literature review. We identified a potential relationship between the effect of ASH with neurotransmitters. Consequently, we employed MALDI-MSI to explore the potential mechanisms and sites of action of ASH in the treatment of PD.

Sample preparation

The mice were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium, and brain tissues were dissected on a board on ice and frozen in liquid nitrogen. The frozen mouse brain tissues were stored at −80 °C, placed in a precooled microtome for 1 h at −20 °C, sliced into 8 μm thick sections (a Leica CM1950 cryotome was used for tissue cryosectioning). The tissue sections were transferred to indium tin oxide (ITO) slides, and the ITO slides containing the tissues were vacuum dried for 30 min.

2,5-Dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHB) (15 mg/mL) in a 90:10% acetonitrile: water mixture was uniformly sprayed on the ITO slides using a TM-Sprayer matrix sprayer. The temperature was set to 60 °C, the flow rate was 0.1 mL/min, and the pressure was 5 psi. The gas was sprayed for 25 cycles, with a 5 s drying time between cycles.

Mass spectrometry conditions

MALDI timsTOF MSI experiments were performed on a prototype Bruker timsTOF flex MS system (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) equipped with a 10 kHz smartbeam 3D laser. The laser power was set to 80% and kept constant throughout the entire experiment. Mass spectra were acquired in positive mode. The mass spectra data were acquired in the mass range of m/z 50–1300 Da. The imaging spatial resolution for the tissue was set to 100 μm, and each spectrum consisted of 400 laser shots. The laser beam irradiated the tissue area on the target plate while the sample was continuously scanned. The tissue samples were ionized with a matrix under the excitation of the laser beam, and the ionized molecules were identified by the mass spectrometer. This process yielded mass‒charge ratio (m/z) information and raw peak intensity data.

Data processing

SCiLS Lab software was used to smooth the original data and standardize the root mean square (RMS) values. The relative intensity data for different m/z values at each spatial location were obtained and combined with data from a self-constructed database (Buck et al. 2022) and the human metabolome database (HMDB, https://hmdb.ca) for substance identification. Gaussian smoothing was performed for all the relevant peaks to visualize the distribution of the target substances within the tissue.

Statistical analysis

All the experimental data were statistically analyzed using SPSS 26.0 statistical software and are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). Comparisons between groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and statistical analysis was performed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons post hoc test or Student’s t test. A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. A histogram was generated with GraphPad Prism v.8.0.1 (GraphPad Software).

Results

ASH ameliorates PD-related behavioral dysfunction

The primary physical symptoms of PD include impaired coordination and reduced mobility. As illustrated in Fig. 1 (A and B), compared with mice in the Con group α-syn transgenic mice in the PD group presented significantly prolonged pole climbing times, diminished coordination abilities and a reduced number of autonomous activities, which align with the behavioral symptoms of PD (P < 0.01). Furthermore, compared with the PD group, the ASH group presented a notable decrease in pole climbing time and an increase in the number of autonomous activities (P < 0.01). These results indicate that ASH has the potential to ameliorate motor dysfunction in PD model mice.

Effects of ASH on pathological brain damage in α-syn rats

α-Syn is a soluble protein that is expressed presynaptically within the CNS. The aggregation of α-syn can induce proteotoxic stress, which subsequently leads to neuronal death in individuals with PD(Burré et al. 2018). TH is a characteristic marker of dopaminergic neurons, which can reflect changes in their number (Choudhury et al. 2019). As shown in Fig. 2 (B-E), the PD group showed significant decreases in both TH activity and the number of TH-positive neurons (P < 0.05), whereas significant α-syn accumulation increased (P < 0.01), confirming the successful establishment of the transgenic model. Following treatment with ASH, TH activity was significantly increased in PD model mice (P < 0.05), and this change was accompanied by an increase in the number of TH-positive cells and a decrease in α-syn accumulation (P < 0.05). These findings suggest that ASH can ameliorate neuropathological damage in PD model mice by increasing TH activity and reducing α-syn accumulation, thereby exerting a therapeutic effect.

Effects of ASH on neurotransmitters levels in mouse brain tissue sections

We employed MALDI-MSI to systematically examine the alterations in metabolites levels in brain tissue sections from the Con, PD, and ASH groups to improve our understanding of the changes in neurotransmitter metabolism in the brain tissue of PD model mice following ASH treatment. A thorough comparison of the levels of key neurotransmitters associated with PD and their metabolites across 7 different brain regions, i.e., the cerebral cortex, cerebellum, striatum, SNc, thalamus ventrolateral nucleus (THvn), globus pallidus interna/substantia nigra reticulata (Gpi/SNr) complex, and hippocampus (Hip), between PD model mice and ASH-treated mice was conducted. Specifically, the levels of neurotransmitters of the catecholamine pathway and their primary metabolites, such as DA, 3-methoxytyramine (3-MT), 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid (DOPAC), homovanillic acid (HVA), norepinephrine (NE) and its metabolite dihydroxyphenylethylene glycol (DOPEG), as well as the amino acid neurotransmitters GABA and Glu. Additionally, we examined the levels of Ach, taurine (2-aminoethanesulfonic acid), 5-HT and its metabolite 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA).

DA distribution in brain tissue sections from mice in the con, PD and ASH groups

DA, a neurotransmitter, is released by neurons to transmit signals in the brain, facilitating communication between nerve cells. It is closely linked to various physiological processes and neurological disorders. We employed MALDI-MSI to elucidate the significant changes in DA metabolism in the brain tissues of mice from the Con, PD and ASH groups. The distribution of DA was visualized in 7 brain regions (Fig. 3A and B). MALDI-MSI images revealed an uneven distribution of DA in the striatum and substantia nigra due to the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the PD group. There was a significant increase in DA levels in the SNc and striatum in the ASH group compared with the PD group (P < 0.05), with DA exhibiting the highest abundance in the striatum in both the PD and ASH groups. Alterations in the levels of monoamine neurotransmitters and their metabolites

Monoamine neurotransmitters, which include catecholamines (primarily DA) and indolamines (primarily 5-HT), play crucial roles in various functions such as memory, movement, emotion, and sleep. The levels of these neurotransmitters gradually decrease with age, resulting in symptoms including memory loss, motor dysfunction, and sleep disturbances. The pathological changes observed in patients with PD are attributed primarily to the loss of dopaminergic neurons, which decreases the synthesis rate of the monoamine neurotransmitter DA. This reduction subsequently leads to lower levels of DA and its metabolites, DOPAC and HVA. Decreases in the levels of DA and its metabolites constitute the major biochemical alterations associated with PD (Goldstein et al. 2016). The MASDL-MSI results revealed increased levels of DA across all brain regions in the ASH group compared with the PD group. Notably, significant increases in DA levels were detected in the cerebral cortex, cerebellum, striatum, SNc and THvn (P < 0.01 and 0.05). Although DA levels tended to be increased in the Gpi/SNr complex and Hip, these changes were not statistically significant. The log2-fold change (log2FC) in DA levels was the highest in SNc and THvn (log2FC = 1.10, 0.98). Moreover, the level of 3-MT, a metabolite of DA produced by catechol-O-methyl-transferase (COMT), was elevated throughout the brains of mice in the ASH group, although this trend did not reach statistical significance. Interestingly, we did not detect the presence of DOPAC, a metabolite of DA produced by MAO. Furthermore, compared with the PD group, the ASH group presented a significant increase in HVA (the metabolic end product of DA) levels. HVA is produced through the further metabolism of free DA in the synaptic cleft following its conversion into dihydroxyphenylacetic acid by MAO and aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH). Therefore, HVA levels can serve as an indirect indicator of fluctuations in DA levels within the brain. In the ASH group, the greatest increases in HVA levels were observed in the cerebral cortex (log2FC = 0.45), SNc (log2FC = 0.40) and striatum (log2FC = 0.37). These results suggest that the metabolites 3-MT and DOPAC, which are produced in the initial stages of the metabolic pathway, are predominantly converted into HVA.

5-HT levels were increased throughout the brains of mice in the ASH group; however the changes were not statistically significant. In contrast, 5-HT levels were decreased in the SNc, THvn, and Gpi/SNr complex. Additionally, 5-HIAA levels were significantly decreased in all brain regions in the ASH group (P < 0.05), with the most pronounced reductions observed in the Gpi/SNr complex (log2FC = −1.42), cerebellum (log2FC = −1.28) and THvn (log2FC = −1.20) (Fig. 5).

Alterations in amino acid neurotransmitters levels

Amino acid neurotransmitters are among the most abundant neurotransmitters in the CNS and play significant roles in neurodegeneration, brain injury, learning impairment, cognitive impairment, and other pathological processes. Amino acid neurotransmitters are classified into two categories on the basis of their excitatory or inhibitory effects on postsynaptic neurons: excitatory amino acids (EAAs) and inhibitory amino acids (IAAs).

In the ASH group, the major excitatory amino acid neurotransmitter Glu was downregulated in all brain regions except for the Gpi/SNr complex. Notably, significant decreases in Glu levels were observed in the cerebral cortex and striatum (P < 0.05), with the greatest log2-fold change in Glu levels detected in these regions (log2FC = 0.65, 0.49). The level of the inhibitory amino acid neurotransmitter GABA, a metabolite of Glu decarboxylation, was significantly decreased throughout the brain in the ASH group (P < 0.05) but was increased in the SNc and Hip (Fig. 5).

Alterations in the levels of cholinergic circuits and other DA-related neurotransmitters

Damage to the cholinergic system plays an important role in PD-related memory impairments (Arendt et al. 1995). The neurotransmitter of the cholinergic pathway is Ach. In this study, Ach levels tended to be increased across all brain regions in the ASH group, with statistically significant increases observed in the cerebellum and SNc (P < 0.05). The log2-fold change (log2FC) in Ach levels was the highest in the THvn and lowest in the Hip (log2FC = 1.16, 0.08) (Fig. 5).

Taurine, an antioxidant, exclusively plays physiological roles in brain functions, such as learning and memory. It can inhibit neuronal damage induced by EAAs and ameliorate cognitive impairment in PD model mice by inhibiting microglial activation(Wang et al. 2021). In our study, taurine levels exhibited a significant increasing trend across all brain regions in the ASH group, with significant upregulation noted in the cerebellum, cerebral cortex, Gpi/SNr complex, Hip, striatum, and THvn (P < 0.001). In addition, patients with PD exhibit marked loss of noradrenergic neurons. A reduction in noradrenergic projections is associated with sleep disorders, mood disorders, and cognitive dysfunction associated with PD (Espay et al. 2014). In this study, NE was not detected throughout the brain; however, the level of its metabolite DOPEG was increased throughout the brain, although the changes were nonsignificant (Fig. 5).

Correlation analysis of behavioral data, DA levels and neurotransmitter levels

In this study, we found that ASH ameliorated the behavioral dysfunction in PD model mice and reversed alterations in the levels of DA and other neurotransmitters. We predict that ASH can regulate neurotransmitters in the body, thereby influencing DA release, exerting an anti-PD effect, and ultimately contributing to the alleviation of movement disorders. The amount of DA released by dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and striatum is a key factor in PD. However, the brain is a precisely organized and complex structure, with various regions interacting and influencing one another. Therefore, we conducted Spearman correlation analyses to create a heatmap and network diagram illustrating the relationships between the DA levels in 7 specific brain regions and behaviors. The results indicated that autonomous activity was positively correlated with DA levels in all brain regions, with significant correlations observed for the SNc and cerebral cortex (P < 0.05). Conversely, the pole climbing ability of the mice was negatively correlated with DA levels in all the brain regions, with significant correlations identified for the striatum, SNc, THvn, cerebral cortex, and cerebellum (P < 0.05). These findings suggest that ASH can improve the behavior of PD model mice by modulating DA release in the SNc (Fig. 6A and B). However, the mechanism by which ASH modulates DA levels through the regulation of neurotransmitters in specific brain regions to improve the behavior of α-syn transgenic mice remains to be elucidated. We further examined the interaction between the levels of DA and neurotransmitters in these brain region and observed significant correlations between neuroticism and DA levels (Fig. 6C and D). DA levels in the cerebral cortex were significantly positively correlated with taurine levels in the striatum, cerebellum, and Hip, as well as with 3-MT levels in the striatum. Conversely, DA levels in the cerebral cortex were negatively correlated with 5-HIAA levels in the cerebral cortex, cerebellum, striatum, THvn, and Hip (|r| >0.8, P < 0.01). Additionally, DA levels in the cerebellum were positively correlated with taurine levels in the striatum and the Gpi/SNr complex but were negatively correlated with 5-HIAA levels in both the cerebellum and hip (|r| >0.8, P < 0.01). Striatum DA levels were positively correlated with 3-MT levels in the striatum and with taurine levels in the SNc and negatively correlated with 5-HIAA levels in the cerebellum and Hip (|r| >0.8, P < 0.01). Finally, DA levels in the SNc were positively correlated with taurine levels in the striatum, SNc, Hip, cerebellum, and THvn, as well as with 3-MT levels in the striatum, Ach levels in the SNc and cerebral cortex, and HVA levels in the striatum. DA levels in the SNc were negatively correlated with 5-HIAA levels in the cerebral cortex, cerebellum, striatum, Hip, and Gpi/SNr complex (|r| >0.8, P < 0.01). DA levels in the THvn were positively correlated with Ach levels in the THvn, 3-MT and 5-HT levels in the striatum, and taurine levels in the Hip but negatively correlated with 5-HIAA levels in the cerebellum (|r| >0.8, P < 0.01). DA levels in the Gpi/SNr complex were positively correlated with GABA levels in the Gpi/SNr complex and with 3-MT levels in the SNc (|r| >0.8, P < 0.01). Furthermore, DA levels in the Hip were positively correlated with GABA levels in the Hip and 3-MT and Ach levels in the cerebral cortex (|r| >0.8, P < 0.01). On the basis of these observations, we hypothesize that the abnormal expression of neurotransmitters contributes to the imbalance of DA levels in PD model mice. Additionally, ASH has the potential to modulate neurotransmitter transmission in the SNc, striatum, hip, cerebral cortex, and Gpi/SNr complex, leading to the restoration of DA levels across these regions and subsequently ameliorating behavioral abnormalities in PD model mice.

Discussion

PD is characterized by deterioration of the nigrostriatal pathway (Balestrino and Schapira 2020), which leads to a reduction in DA levels and disorders in the flow of related areas, subsequently resulting in dyskinesia within the extrapyramidal system. This experiment revealed that PD model mice presented prolonged pole climbing times and reduced autonomous activity, indicating damage to dopaminergic neurons and a decrease in DA levels within the striatum. Following ASH treatment, the pole climbing time was significantly reduced and autonomous activity was significantly increased, suggesting that ASH has the potential to enhance motor coordination ability in mice with PD and alleviate associated symptoms. HE staining revealed that neurons in PD model mice underwent apoptosis and atrophy, which could be reversed by ASH treatment. TH is a critical enzyme involved in DA synthesis, and the number of dopaminergic neurons can be indirectly assessed by measuring TH activity. The IHC results suggested that ASH can exert therapeutic effects on PD by increasing TH activity and reducing α-syn accumulation. Collectively, these findings support the use of ASH as a potential strategy to ameliorate the symptoms associated with PD.

PD is not only characterized by motor dysfunction but also associated with autonomic nervous system disorders, anosmia, sleep disturbances, depression, anxiety, and various other NMSs, which significantly affect patients’ quality of life. Studies indicate that cognitive impairment in patients with PD is closely associated with changes in neurotransmitter levels and brain circuits(Benke et al. 2000; Kehagia et al. 2013). Brain circuits are pervasive structures within the brain and are formed by neurons with diverse properties and functions that communicate through neurotransmitter transmission at synapses. Damage to these circuits may be a significant factor contributing to the development of various neuropsychiatric disorders (Kehagia et al. 2013; Yeom et al. 2020). Therefore, we utilized MALDI-MSI to conduct a comprehensive metabolomic analysis of brain tissue sections from mice to assess the distribution of neurotransmitters and their metabolites related to PD in specific brain regions. This enabled us to accurately map the distribution of various neurotransmitters associated with the catecholamine metabolic pathway, amino acid neurotransmitters, cholinergic neurotransmitters, the serotoninergic pathway, and their metabolites in mice from the PD and ASH groups. Our results indicated that the levels of DA were significantly increased in all studied brain regions in the ASH group compared with the PD group. Similarly, the levels of 3-MT tended to be increased in the regions where the DA level increased significantly, although the trends did not reach statistical significance. This observation may be attributed to the increases in 3-MT levels in the cerebral cortex, cerebellum, striatum, SNc, and Hip but decreases in the THvn and Gpi/SNr complex. Due to the localization of the COMT enzyme, 3-MT has been identified as a marker of DA release (Westerink 1985). Our findings confirm that ASH can exert a therapeutic effect on PD by increasing DA levels. Numerous previous studies have consistently indicated that HVA levels are reduced in PD patients and that HVA levels can serve as an indirect indicator of fluctuations in DA levels within the brain (Lewitt et al., 1992; Chia et al. 1993; Goldstein et al. 2008; Lewitt et al., 2011; Herbert et al. 2013). In this study, the HVA/DA ratio in each brain region was lower in the ASH group than in the PD group (Fig. 4A), indicating a significant increase in DA levels in the ASH group. This finding suggests that ASH may contribute to the maintenance of DA levels, potentially alleviating the symptoms of PD. Furthermore, DOPAC, a metabolite of DA produced by MAO was undetectable, suggesting that ASH may facilitate the metabolism and elimination of DOPAC. Alternatively, in the ASH group, the large amount of DA may have been preferentially metabolized to 3-MT through the action of COMT, with the majority of 3-MT and DOPAC subsequently being metabolized to HVA. This observation provides compelling evidence for MAO activity suppression. Given that MAO functions as the rate-limiting enzyme in DA degradation (Finberg 2019; Huot 2020; Nam et al. 2022), ASH likely attenuates DA depletion through modulation of MAO activity (Fig. 7B).

The indirect pathway involves the transmission of signals from the cerebral cortex to the striatum, to the GPe, to the STN, to the THvn through the Gpi/SNr complex, and finally back to motor areas of the cerebral cortex. (B) Partial DA metabolic pathway. The enzymes involved are indicated by arrows

The basal ganglia is closely related to the motor symptoms of PD. In individuals with PD, defects in dopaminergic signaling from the substantia nigra to the basal ganglia play a crucial role in triggering motor dysfunction(Herrero et al. 2002; Chen et al. 2009). The basal ganglia includes the striatum, globus pallidus externus (GPe), Gpi/SNr complex, SNc, and subthalamic nucleus (STN). The nigrostriatal dopaminergic projection system regulates the function and activity of the basal ganglia through both direct and indirect pathways (Kreitzer and Malenka 2008). The striatum serves as the primary receptor of motor signals within the basal ganglia circuit, and the primary neurotransmitters involved in these pathways are Glu and GABA. In individuals with PD, the degeneration or death of dopaminergic neurons can result in excessive activity of the STN‒Gpi/SNr pathway, leading to an imbalance between the activity of the direct and indirect pathways, which ultimately causes motor dysfunction (Sil’kis 2002). The results of this experiment indicated no significant increase in Glu levels within the Gpi/SNr complex; however, a decrease in Glu levels was observed in other brain regions and in the entire brain. These findings indicate that ASH can mitigate the neuronal damage caused by Glu excitotoxicity. GABA, which is formed through the decarboxylation of Glu, is located predominantly in the basal ganglia, Hip, and cerebellum(Boccalaro et al. 2020). The reduction in DA levels in PD model mice results in abnormal activation of the indirect pathway of the basal ganglia, leading to an increase in GABA levels in the thalamus, which ultimately triggers gait disturbances (O’gorman Tuura et al., 2018). Under normal conditions, GABAergic neurons in the striatum exhibit a low rhythm, resulting in minimal inhibitory output to the GPe and SNr. This output leads to heightened inhibitory signaling in the GPe, which in turn inhibits output from the STN and further suppresses the subthalamic nucleus–tegmental reticular nucleus loop, ultimately reducing the movement rhythm and inhibiting motor function. Concurrently, the GPi is markedly inhibited, which diminishes inhibitory signals from this region to the thalamus, thereby weakening excitatory signals from the thalamus to the cerebral cortex and halting motor signal transmission. Owing to damage to the SNc in PD model mice, dopaminergic activity between the substantia nigra and striatum is diminished, leading to the inhibition of the direct pathway and excitation of the indirect pathway. Consequently, we posit that ASH acts primarily on the indirect pathway by decreasing the levels of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA in the striatum. The reduction in the level of this neurotransmitter diminishes the inhibitory effect on the STN, subsequently increasing the release of the excitatory neurotransmitter Glu by the STN. As a result, Glu levels in the Gpi/SNr complex are increased, directly contributing to its excitatory activity and leading to an increase in GABA output, which subsequently increases GABA levels in the SNc (Fig. 7A).

Ach was the first neurotransmitter to be identified; it plays a key role in the cholinergic circuit and is closely related to cognitive functions in individuals with PD. The dynamic balance of DA and Ach levels is crucial for maintaining the homeostasis of the CNS. The functions of the DA and Ach neurotransmitter systems in the striatum are antagonistic. DA is transported to the striatum by dopaminergic neurons through the nigrostriatal pathway, where it plays a role in motor regulation in the basal ganglia. Thus, the balance of DA and Ach levels is vital for the regulatory function of motor activity via the basal ganglia. In this study, we observed that, in addition to a decrease in DA levels in the striatum, an imbalance between DA and Ach levels was observed. However, the Ach level in the ASH group was significantly greater than that in the PD group. These findings suggest that ASH may restore the balance of DA and Ach levels by protecting central acetylcholinergic neurons and increasing cholinesterase activity, ultimately leading to the amelioration of symptoms of PD in mice.

Taurine is one of the most abundant amino acids in the brain and can regulate various physiological functions. It plays a crucial role in neurotransmitter activity, and studies have shown that taurine can promote the formation of synaptic connections, regulate calcium homeostasis, and prevent the depolarization of the cell membrane caused by Glu, thereby protecting neurons from excitotoxic damage (Bosboom et al. 2009; Menzie et al. 2014). Additionally, taurine can also regulate the balance of neurotransmitters, reduce the activity of MAO and decrease the degradation of DA, thereby helping to restore motor function in patients with PD. It can also protect neurons by inhibiting microglial activation and the release of inflammatory factors through the modulation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (Wang et al. 2022). Our study revealed that after ASH treatment, taurine levels were significantly increased in all brain regions of the mice. These findings suggest that ASH can exert an anti-PD effect by modulating the taurine content in the CNS and inhibiting inflammatory responses.

The neurological changes associated with PD involve the progressive degeneration of noradrenergic and serotonergic neurons (Lian et al. 2018). Serotonergic neurons are located primarily in the raphe nucleus of the brainstem and are crucial for learning and memory. Their efferent fibers project widely to many brain regions, including the substantia nigra, striatum, globus pallidus, hypothalamus, thalamus, and cerebral cortex. In particular, the 5-HT receptor subtype 5-HT4 is particularly abundant in the striatum, where it can regulate the release of DA. Consequently, serotonergic neurons may play an important role in the regulation of motor disorders and the NMSs of PD (Xue-Mei et al. 2023). Animal experiments have revealed that 5-HT levels in the basal ganglia, including the caudate nucleus, putamen, globus pallidus, substantia nigra, thalamus, and Hip, are significantly decreased in PD model mice (Kamińska et al. 2017). This reduction in 5-HT levels may be linked to the loss of serotonergic neurons in the dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN) (Huot and Fox 2013). The 5-HT system functions primarily through its associated receptors, which can activate globus pallidus neurons via various receptor types. This activation, in turn, regulates the basal ganglia circuit and exerts control over the limbic system, cognition, and motor functions. Studies indicate that 5-HT receptors can modulate the release of various neurotransmitters, such as Glu, GABA, DA, NE and Ach. Consequently, a decrease in 5-HT levels may lead to an imbalance in basal ganglia activity, ultimately contributing to the development of PD(Nichols and Nichols 2008; Ahmad et al. 2023). In this study, following ASH treatment, 5-HT levels tended to increase across all brain regions, as expected. These results suggest that ASH may have a protective effect on serotonergic neurons. The Hip is known to have a high density of serotonergic neuronal projections(Köhler and Steinbusch 1982), and our findings indicated that 5-HT was most abundant in the Hip in the ASH group. This result may explain the observed elevation of DA levels in the Hip. The DA/5-HT ratio serves as a sensitive indicator of changes in motor ability. An increase in the DA/5-HT ratio within the brain can enhance motor function by amplifying stimulatory effects, promoting beneficial outcomes, and facilitating appropriate muscle coordination (Davis and Bailey 1997). In this study, the DA/5-HT ratio in the 7 brain regions was greater in the ASH group than in the PD group, which is consistent with the expected results, suggesting that ASH can enhance the motor abilities of mice. Additionally, the levels of 5-HIAA in the ASH group were the highest in the Hip and were greater than those of 5-HT throughout the entire brain. 5-HT is initially processed by type A monoamine oxidase to yield 5-hydroxyindole acetaldehyde, which is subsequently catalyzed by aldehyde dehydrogenase to produce 5-HIAA, an inactive metabolic end product. The significant reduction in 5-HIAA levels in the ASH group indicates a potential inhibitory effect on MAO activity. This inhibition impedes the enzymatic conversion of 5-HT into its metabolites, ultimately resulting in elevated levels of 5-HT within the brain.

NE is synthesized from DA through the action of dopamine β-hydroxylase (DβH) and is ultimately metabolized to DOPEG via MAO. In the brain, it is produced primarily in the locus coeruleus (LC) and projects to the basal ganglia, thalamus, and extensive cortical areas. Damage to the NE system is closely associated with both the motor symptoms and NMSs of PD. Our results indicate that DOPEG, a metabolite of NE, was not significantly upregulated throughout the brain, suggesting that ASH may influence noradrenergic neurons.

In the ASH group, the increases in DA and HVA were consistent across various brain regions, with the exception of the striatum, particularly in the cerebral cortex and substantia nigra. The high level of DA in these regions may exert a biological effect by altering the signal transduction pathways throughout the brain. MAO is the key enzyme catalyzing the degradation of monoamine neurotransmitters, including DA, 5-HT, and NE. Through oxidative deamination, MAO regulates the concentrations of these neurotransmitters. MAO-A exhibits high substrate preference for 5-HT and primarily metabolizes 5-HT and NE, while MAO-B demonstrates high affinity for DA and is principally responsible for metabolizing DA and phenethylamine in humans(Gray et al. 2014; Florczak-Wyspianska et al. 2017). On the basis of the findings, the levels of HVA and DOPAC in the ASH group, along with the significant decrease in 5-HIAA levels and the increasing trend in DOPEG levels, suggest that ASH may inhibit the activity of MAO, thereby slowing the metabolism of monoamine neurotransmitters. This action could lead to a reduction in the degradation of DA and the generation of free radicals, contributing to a neuroprotective effect (Fig. 7B). The striatum receives signals via dopaminergic projection fibers that originate in the SNc as well as glutamatergic nerve fibers from the cerebral cortex. ASH significantly decreased Glu levels in both the cerebral cortex and striatum, thereby counteracting the neurotoxicity associated with excessive Glu accumulation in the brain and restoring the balance of nerve conduction.

Conclusions

In summary, we performed spatial metabolomics with MALDI-MSI to comprehensively assess the distribution of PD-related neurotransmitters and metabolites associated with the noradrenergic, cholinergic, and serotonergic systems within the brains of mice. Our study suggests that ASH has a therapeutic effect on PD by modulating neural circuits such as the basal ganglia circuit. Specifically, ASH can increase 3-MT levels in the striatum and cerebral cortex, Ach levels in the SNc, GABA levels in the Hip, and DOPEG levels in the Gpi/SNr complex while decreasing 5-HIAA levels in the Hip, cerebral cortex, and striatum, thereby reversing the decrease in DA levels caused by PD. The results of the behavioral analysis revealed that the SNc may be the key region through which ASH ameliorates behavioral dysfunction in PD model mice. In addition, MAO plays a crucial role in neurotransmitter metabolism and DA release. These findings improve our understanding of the alterations in neurotransmitter metabolism in the brain tissue of PD model mice, providing insights into the mechanisms by which ASH may contribute to the treatment of PD.
