Exposure to ceftazidime-avibactam induced antimicrobial resistance and enhanced virulence in a Klebsiella pneumoniae strain
Authors: Jun Li, Haolan Wang, Zhaojun Liu, Yubing Xia, Yongmei Hu, Haichen Wang, Fengjun Xia, Mingxiang Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryTo prevent and control the emergence of ceftazidime-avibactam (CZA) resistance, unraveling its underlying mechanisms is critical. Accordingly, in this study, MDR Klebsiella pneumoniae (KP) strain (C357) was subjected to repeated in vitro exposure to CZA until resistance emerged. C357 acquired CZA resistance by the 15th generation (C357-15). On further assessing the fitness cost and potential mechanisms of resistance, C357-15 demonstrated significantly enhanced biofilm-forming capacity and elevated virulence. Moreover, the genes encoding type 3 fimbriae (i.e., mrkA, mrkB, mrkC, and mrkD) and quorum-sensing (QS) system-associated genes (i.e., lsrR) in C357-15 were upregulated. Notably, QS inhibitors significantly decreased the expression of type 3 fimbriae genes of C357-15. Thus, prolonged exposure to CZA induced both biofilm-forming ability and enhanced virulence in MDR-KP. Moreover, QS may promote the expression of type 3 fimbriae-related genes. In conclusion, QS may promote biofilm formation and enhance virulence either directly or by influencing type 3 fimbriae-related gene expression.

Introduction

In recent years, multidrug-resistant (MDR) Klebsiella pneumoniae (KP), particularly carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP), has spread globally at an alarming rate. Its rising detection rate and high associated mortality have made it a significant threat to clinical antimicrobial therapy.1,2,3 Reflecting on this urgency, on May 17, 2024, the World Health Organization included CRKP in the “Critical Group” of its most recent list of priority pathogens.4

Ceftazidime-avibactam (CZA), which is a novel β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combination, is presently recommended by the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) as the first-line treatment for infections caused by serine-producing Gram-negative bacilli.5 Considered as a promising therapeutic option for CRKP infections, CZA has gained widespread clinical use. However, its increasing utilization has coincided with the growing incidence of resistance. CZA resistance in CRKP strains has been reported across various countries and regions. According to the global surveillance data from 2020 collected through the Antimicrobial Testing Leadership and Surveillance program, merely 63.30% of CRKP isolates remained susceptible to CZA.6 These CZA-resistant CRKP infections represent a major challenge to effectively treating resistant Gram-negative infections. To help prevent the spread of this bacterium, emerging strategies such as immuno-informatics-based vaccine development and the use of natural compounds such as eugenol, carvacrol, and cinnamaldehyde have demonstrated promising potential against KP infections.7,8,9

CZA resistance arises through complex and diverse mechanisms. The most common mechanism involves the production of metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs), such as NDM, IMP, and VIM. Since avibactam does not inhibit the hydrolytic activity of MBLs, strains that produce these enzymes exhibit intrinsic CZA resistance.10 In addition, resistance can result from mutations in blaKPC, particularly in the omega loop region of the β-lactamase enzyme, which prevents avibactam from effectively binding to and inhibiting the enzyme.11 In recent years, several novel blaKPC variants, including KPC-33, KPC-35, KPC-36, KPC-70, KPC-121, KPC-123, KPC-228, and KPC-230, have been identified as contributors to CZA resistance.12,13,14 Other resistance mechanisms include the overexpression of resistance genes (blaKPC, blaSHV-12), mutations in lamB (which encodes the outer membrane maltoporin).15,16,17,18

Most research on CZA resistance to date has focused on CRKP strains, particularly on those that produce serine β-lactamases, in both in vivo and in vitro settings.16,19,20,21,22 However, with the broad clinical application of CZA, non-serine-producing MDR strains are also increasingly likely to encounter this antibiotic. Therefore, it is essential to determine whether these strains can develop resistance under CZA exposure and investigate the underlying resistance mechanisms. Thus, understanding these potential pathways is crucial for the prevention and control of emerging CZA resistance. The present study selected a clinically isolated MDR-KP strain (C357) that does not carry carbapenemase-encoding genes. This strain was repeatedly exposed in vitro to 1/2 the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) of CZA in order to induce resistance. Then, the biological fitness cost associated with resistance was evaluated by analyzing the growth kinetics, biofilm-formation ability, and virulence in a Galleria mellonella (G. mellonella) infection model. In order to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of resistance, high-throughput analysis, transcriptome sequencing, and reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) were performed.

Induction of CZA resistance in C357 accompanied by increased susceptibility to PB

In vitro, strain C357 was exposed to CZA at 1/2 MIC. After 15 passages, a CZA-resistant derivative (designated as C357-15) was successfully obtained. Then, the antimicrobial susceptibility testing of both C357 and C357-15 was performed using the broth microdilution method. The results indicated that aside from the shift from CZA susceptibility to resistance, the susceptibility profiles for other tested antimicrobial agents remained unchanged. Enhanced biofilm-formation ability and virulence in C357 induced by CZA in vitro

In order to evaluate the fitness cost associated with the evolution of strain C357 into the CZA-resistant derivative C357-15 under prolonged in vitro exposure to CZA at 1/2 MIC, comparative analyses were conducted on growth kinetics, biofilm-formation ability, and virulence using the G. mellonella infection model. The growth curve analysis revealed no significant difference between C357 and C357-15 (Figure 1A). However, the biofilm-formation assays revealed that C357-15 had significantly stronger biofilm-formation ability when compared to C357 (0.547 ± 0.071 vs. 0.259 ± 0.029, P = 0.001) (Figure 1B). In the virulence assay, the mortality rate of G. mellonella infected with C357 and C357-15 was 70% and 90%, respectively, indicating increased virulence in the resistant strain (Figure 1C). The type 3 fimbriae and QS system were significantly increased in C357 induced by CZA in vitro

The results revealed that the mrkA expression increased by 5.80-fold, the mrkB expression increased by 4.17-fold (4.261 ± 0.887 vs. 1.021 ± 0.248, P = 0.0003, the mrkC expression increased by 4.36-fold (4.462 ± 1.005 vs. 1.023 ± 0.274, P = 0.0001), the mrkD expression increased by 4.06-fold (4.142 ± 1.087 vs. 1.019 ± 0.230, P = 0.0005), and the lsrR expression increased by 2.95-fold (2.965 ± 0.593 vs. 1.006 ± 0.135, P = 0.0051), when compared to the corresponding pre-induction levels (5.898 ± 1.605 vs. 1.017 ± 0.225, P < 0.0001), suggesting a possible association with the enhanced biofilm-formation ability and virulence in C357 after induction by CZA in vitro (Figure 2F).

QS may promote biofilm formation either directly or by influencing type 3 fimbriae-related gene expression

In order to further determine the regulatory mechanisms of type 3 fimbriae, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed. The results revealed that its regulation was closely associated with the QS system. After the addition of QS inhibitors, no significant difference was noted in the biofilm-formation ability in strain C357 (0.259 ± 0.029 vs. 0.245 ± 0.064, P = 0.478). However, following CZA induction, the addition of QS inhibitors to strain C357-15 significantly reduced its biofilm-formation ability (0.547 ± 0.071 vs. 0.357 ± 0.094, P = 0.006) (Figures 3A and 3B). These findings suggest that the QS system plays a critical role in the evolution of CZA resistance in C357 during in vitro induction. Furthermore, the expression of type 3 fimbriae-related gene mrkA was also found to be significantly reduced after the addition of QS inhibitor (0.239 ± 0.013 vs. 1.002 ± 0.072, P < 0.0001), suggesting that the QS system may promote biofilm formation either directly or by influencing type 3 fimbriae-related gene expression (Figure 3C).Figure 3The biofilm-formation ability and expression of mrkA in C357 and C357-15 with and without the inhibitor of the QS system(A and B) Biofilm-formation assays: control, PBS; C357, strain before induction; C357-QS, strain before induction with the addition of a QS inhibitor; C357-15, strain after induction; C357-15-QS, strain after induction with the addition of QS inhibitor.(C) The expression of mrkA in the strain C357-15 without and with the inhibitor of the QS system; C357-15, strain after induction; C357-15-QS, strain after induction with the addition of QS inhibitor; ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.

Discussion

CZA is extensively used to treat infections caused by MDR Gram-negative bacteria. However, with its increasing clinical use, the emergence of CZA-resistant strains has become frequent. The mechanisms underlying CZA resistance are complex and multifactorial, involving the production of MBLs, mutations in key sites of KPC enzymes, and alterations in cell permeability, such as the loss of porins.10,11,15,16,17,18 Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms responsible for CZA resistance remain uncovered. In the present study, a clinical MDR-KP isolate from a newborn was subjected to repeated in vitro exposure to sub-inhibitory concentrations of CZA to elucidate both the fitness costs and potential resistance mechanisms involved in the transition from susceptibility to resistance.

Exposure to antimicrobial agents is a major driver of bacterial resistance. Multiple in vitro studies have revealed that exposure to CZA under selective pressure can induce the emergence of stably resistant strains.16,23 In addition, an in vivo study revealed that CZA-resistant strains appeared in three patients with CRKP infection on days 10, 15, and 19 of the treatment, respectively,24 which further supports the notion that the clinical use of CZA may lead to resistance development. In the present study, repeated exposure to sub-inhibitory concentrations of CZA successfully induced resistance in a non-carbapenemase-producing MDR-KP strain after 15 generations. Interestingly, it was observed that the CZA-resistant strain (C357-15) exhibited increased susceptibility to PB, as evidenced by the decrease in MIC from 2 μg/mL to 0.5 μg/mL. This finding suggests that the induction of CZA resistance is accompanied by changes in susceptibility to other antibiotics, potentially enabling the development of combination treatment strategies for otherwise untreatable resistant infections. Similar phenomena have also been reported in some previous studies. For example, CZA can restore β-lactam susceptibility in carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales,25,26 and a carbapenem-based combination therapy was proposed for treating infections caused by CZA-resistant but carbapenemase-producing KP strains.27,28 For Pseudomonas aeruginosa, the repeated induction with sub-inhibitory concentrations of CZA led to resistance, while simultaneously restoring susceptibility to TGC, despite the intrinsic resistance of P. aeruginosa to TGC.29 The restoration of sensitivity to certain antimicrobial agents following exposure to CZA may be closely related to gene mutations and the absence of efflux pumps, among other reasons.27,28,29 However, the specific mechanism underlying the increased sensitivity to PB remains unclear, warranting further in-depth investigation.

Bacteria often incur a fitness cost following the acquisition of antibiotic resistance. Choi et al. reported that hypervirulent KP strains that acquire colistin resistance exhibited reduced capsular polysaccharide production, impaired virulence, and notable fitness costs.30 Similarly, Capitani et al. reported that the ST512 strain developed CZA resistance while acquiring a hypermucoviscous phenotype.22 In addition, Guo et al. reported the development of hypermucoviscosity in KPC-producing KP strains resistant to CZA.31 In the present study, no significant difference was observed in the growth curves of C357 and C357-15. However, C357-15 exhibited markedly enhanced biofilm-formation ability and virulence. Since enhanced biofilm formation confers greater environmental adaptability, these findings underscore the importance of considering not only antibiotic resistance but also the simultaneous enhancement of bacterial survival and colonization capacity. Clinicians should remain vigilant of such concurrent evolutionary traits in resistant pathogens, since these may complicate infection control and treatment outcomes.

Biofilm protects bacterial cells from hostile environmental conditions, such as temperature fluctuations, nutrient limitation or deprivation, dehydration, and, importantly, antibacterial drugs.32 In the present study, it was observed that the biofilm-formation ability of CZA-resistant strains was significantly enhanced when compared to the parental strain before induction. The transcriptome sequencing of induced and uninduced strains (i.e., C357 and C357-15), followed by validation using RT-qPCR, revealed that differentially expressed genes were predominantly enriched in biofilm-formation pathways. These included genes, such as mrkA, mrkB, mrkC, mrkD, and mrkF, which encode the components of type 3 fimbriae.33 Previous studies have demonstrated that type 3 fimbriae play the key role not only in bacterial biofilm formation but also as an important virulence factor. In this study, the simultaneous increase in drug resistance and virulence observed in the strain C357 following CZA induction in vitro may be associated with the upregulation of type 3 fimbriae expression. Furthermore, the KEGG enrichment analysis further identified QS system genes (lsrA, lsrB, lsrD, lsrF, and lsrR) as related to significantly enriched pathways, and the biofilm-formation ability of the induced strain was significantly reduced upon QS inhibition, indicating that the QS system contributes to enhanced biofilm formation in resistant strains. Furthermore, the expression of type 3 fimbriae-related gene mrkA was significantly reduced after the addition of QS inhibitor, implying that the QS system possibly promotes biofilm formation either directly or by influencing type 3 fimbriae-related gene expression.

In conclusion, the present findings indicated that exposure to CZA can simultaneously enhance its biofilm-forming ability and virulence in MDR-KP. An in vitro induction of MDR-KP with CZA may enhance its biofilm-forming ability. QS might contribute to this enhancement by modulating the expression of type 3 fimbriae-related genes, thereby enhancing both the biofilm-formation ability and the virulence. Furthermore, the observation that continuous exposure to CZA might restore susceptibility to certain antimicrobial agents highlights the potential for combination antibiotic regimens based on these restored sensitivities, offering promising alternatives for the treatment of difficult-to-treat, drug-resistant infections.

Limitations of the study

Despite the valuable conclusions derived from this study, it has some limitations. For example, only one strain of MDR-KP was used in the research, and there is a need to conduct further verification of the related mechanisms at the protein level for this strain.

Experimental model and study participant details

Bacterial strain

K. pneumoniae C357 was collected from the hospital information system of the General Hospital of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. First, a lower respiratory tract specimen was collected from a newborn having a respiratory infection, after which the C357 strain was isolated. The K. pneumoniae C357-15 strain developed resistance to CZA after an in vitro induction. Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853, K. pneumoniae NTUH-2044, and ATCC-700603 were obtained from the laboratory stock.

G. mellonella

The experimental G. mellonella used in this study were at the larval stage (age: 2–3 weeks, body length: 2–3 cm, weight: 250–350 mg).

Method details

Collection of clinical information and identification of bacteria

The KP strain C357 was isolated from a lower respiratory tract specimen collected from a newborn with respiratory infection on January 10, 2020. The isolate was re-identified using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (Zhongyuan Huiji Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), with E. coli ATCC 25922 employed as the quality control strain. The study was approved by the Clinical Research Medical Ethics Subcommittee of the Medical Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (ID 202209541). Written informed consent was obtained from the participants enrolled in the study.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The MICs for CZA, meropenem (MEM), imipenem (IPM), aztreonam (ATM), polymyxin B (PB), ceftazidime (CAZ), and tigecycline (TGC) were determined using the broth microdilution method. All antimicrobial agents were obtained from Wenzhou Kont Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The quality control strains used were P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853, K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603, and E. coli ATCC 25922. All strains were sourced from the National Center for Clinical Laboratories (Beijing, China). MIC interpretations were made according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines.42 For TGC and PB, susceptibility breakpoints were interpreted using criteria from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing, respectively.43,44

In vitro selection of a CAZ-resistant isolate

The induction of CZA resistance in strain C357 was performed via serial passaging in subinhibitory concentrations of CAZ, based on a previously described protocol, with minor modifications.29,45 Briefly, 4 μL of the overnight culture of C357 was inoculated into 4 mL of Luria–Bertani (LB) broth supplemented with CAZ at 1/2 MIC of the original strain. Then, the culture was incubated at 37°C with shaking at 220 rpm for 16 h (Passage 1). Afterwards, 4 μL of the resulting culture was transferred into fresh LB broth that contained CAZ at the same concentration, and incubation was repeated under identical conditions for 16 h (Passage 2). This process was continued for 40 consecutive passages. The MIC values were assessed after every five passages using the broth microdilution method.42 Strains that developed MICs of ≥16/4 μg/mL were subjected to an additional 20 passages, in order to ensure the stability of the acquired resistance. CZA-resistant isolates confirmed after the extended induction period were stored at -80°C for future analyses.

Fitness cost

In order to assess the fitness cost of CZA resistance, the associated bacterial growth, biofilm formation, and virulence were evaluated. Growth curves were generated using a previously described method.46 Briefly, a single bacterial colony was adjusted to the 0.5 McFarland standard, diluted at 1:100 in LB broth, and incubated at 37°C with agitation. At 12 h intervals up to 36 h, 200 μL aliquots were collected, and the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was measured to monitor the growth kinetics.

The biofilm-formation ability was evaluated in all KP isolates by the crystal violet staining assay, as described elsewhere.47 All KP isolates were tested, with NTUH-K2044 and K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603 serving as the positive and negative controls, respectively. Then, absorbance at 570 nm (OD570) was measured, and the results were expressed in mean ±standard deviation from three independent experiments. The biofilm-formation ability was classified based on the absorbance values, as follows: if A ≤ Ac, the strain was considered non-biofilm-forming; if Ac < A ≤ 2Ac, the strain exhibited weak biofilm formation; if 2Ac < A ≤ 4Ac, the strain exhibited moderate biofilm formation; if A > 4Ac, the strain was considered a strong biofilm former.

In order to evaluate the changes in virulence before and after CZA induction, the G. mellonella infection model (Tianjin Huiyude Biotech Company, Tianjin, China) was used.48 Larvae were injected with 10 μL of overnight culture adjusted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL in saline. Then, K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603 and NTUH-K2044 were used as low- and high-virulence controls, respectively. Following the injection, G. mellonella were incubated in the dark at 37°C for five days. Survival was monitored daily, with saline-injected G. mellonella serving as the negative control. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Whole-genome sequencing and assembly-based analysis

The genomic DNA of C357 was isolated using the MagAttract HMW DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and subjected to next-generation high-throughput sequencing (NGS) on the HiSeq 2000TM platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) with 2 × 100-bp paired-end reads. PacBio Sequel II and the DNBSEQ platform (Beijing Genomics Institute, Shenzhen, China) were used for sequencing. De novo genome assembly was performed with the SPAdes Genome Assembler (version 3.11.0). The whole-genome sequence was annotated using the RAST tool (version 2.0, https://rast.nmpdr.org/). The capsular type was determined using Kaptive (version 3.1.0, https://kaptive-web.erc.monash.edu/). The multilocus sequence typing (MLST), antimicrobial resistance genes, and plasmid replicons were identified through MLST, ResFinder, and PlasmidFinder tools available at the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (https://genomicepidemiology.org/services/).

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis of C357 before and after induction of sub-CZA

Prokaryotic transcriptome sequencing technology was used to determine the transcriptomic differences between the parent and CZA-induced strains, and infer the possible mechanisms underlying the evolution of drug resistance during induction. The experimental procedures were based on previously reported protocols.49 Next, pure single colonies of strains before and after CZA induction were selected and cultured for total RNA extraction. After confirming the RNA quality, mRNA was purified, and ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and transfer RNA were removed using commercial rRNA/transfer RNA depletion kits (Ambion, TRIZOL, Shanghai, China). Then, the resulting mRNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA, and sequencing libraries were constructed accordingly. Subsequently, sequencing was performed, and the raw RNA-seq data were subjected to clustering and differential expression analysis. Following the initial screening, Gene Ontology (GO, http://geneontology.org/40,41) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, http://www.kegg.jp/) enrichment analyses were conducted to identify the significantly enriched terms and pathways. The functional annotation of identified genes within the GO and KEGG categories enabled the identification of key genes that were potentially involved in resistance mechanisms. In order to quantify the gene expression differences between the induced and control strains, differential expression analysis was conducted separately for bacterial and host transcriptomes using the edgeR R package on the Windows platform. A corrected P < 0.05 was considered to indicate a significant difference.

Real-time reverse transcription-quantitative PCR assay (RT-qPCR)

In order to validate the transcriptomic results, the expression of genes that encode type 3 fimbriae (mrkA, mrkB, mrkC, and mrkD) and quorum-sensing (QS) system-regulated genes (lsrR) was assessed using the RT-qPCR method. RNA was extracted from bacterial cultures using the TRIzol method. Then, cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and RT-qPCR was conducted using the ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). The 16S rRNA gene served as the internal reference, and the uninduced parent strain was used as the control. The gene expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method, where ΔCt represents the difference between the Ct values of the target and reference genes, and ΔΔCt represents the difference between the ΔCt values of the induced and control strains. The expression in the control strain was normalized to 1, and the relative expression in the induced strain was recorded as X = 2-ΔΔCt. Statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. The significance of expression differences before and after CZA induction was determined using Student’s t-test.

QS inhibition test

The biofilm-formation ability and the expression of mrkA were detected in C357 and C357-15 strains without and with the addition of H2O2, which has been identified as an inhibitor of the QS system in previous article.50 Following the operating methods described in relevant literature,51 a single colony from an overnight culture was picked and inoculated into LB medium and incubated overnight at 37°C under 180 rpm shaking. After dilution in a ratio of 1:100, the culture was transferred into fresh LB medium and incubated with shaking until reaching an OD600 of 0.4–0.6. C357-15 was incubated with H2O2 at a final concentration of 0.1% for 5 min (without H2O2 serving as a control), and the biofilm-formation ability and expression of mrkA were detected.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as the mean ±standard deviation. Group comparisons were performed by Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance, using GraphPad Prism 9 software (as shown in Figures 1B, 2F, 3B, and 3C). P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.
