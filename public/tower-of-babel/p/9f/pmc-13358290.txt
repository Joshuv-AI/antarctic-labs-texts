Archived M184V/I Mutation and Risk of Virological Failure After Switching to Doravirine, Lamivudine, Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate: A National Observational Study (Dat’AIDS Cohort)
Authors: Romain Palich, Axel Ursenbach, Nolwenn Hall, Agathe Becker, Clotilde Allavena, Olivier Robineau, Laurent Hocqueloux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmong 1282 virally suppressed people with HIV switching to doravirine/lamivudine/tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, 40 (3.1%) experienced virological failure (VF) during follow-up (median 81 weeks). Archived M184V/I mutation (7.4% of study population) was not significantly associated with VF after adjustment (adjusted hazard ratio 1.36; 95%CI 0.51–3.66).

The M184V/I mutation in the HIV-1 reverse transcriptase gene is among the most frequently observed resistance-associated mutations. It emerges rapidly under selective pressure from lamivudine (3TC) or emtricitabine (FTC) and confers high-level resistance to both drugs [1]. Because of its early widespread use in combination antiretroviral therapy (cART), particularly during the 1990s and early 2000s, many heavily pretreated individuals harbor archived M184V/I mutations detected in historical plasma RNA and/or proviral DNA genotypes.

Despite its impact on in vitro 3TC/FTC susceptibility, the M184V/I mutation is associated with reduced viral replicative capacity and increased susceptibility to other nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) such as tenofovir and zidovudine [1]. Several studies have demonstrated that archived M184V/I does not significantly compromise virological suppression in people with HIV-1 (PWH) receiving maintenance regimens including lamivudine combined with second-generation integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs) or boosted protease inhibitors (bPIs) [2–4]. In contrast, data regarding non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI)–based regimens remain scarce.

Doravirine is a last-generation NNRTI with a favorable resistance profile, limited drug-drug interactions, and good tolerability. The fixed-dose combination of doravirine, lamivudine, tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (DOR/3TC/TDF) has demonstrated virological efficacy both in treatment-naive and virologically suppressed PWH switching cART, when no resistance to the component drugs is present [5–7]. The impact of archived M184V/I on the virological efficacy of this combination has not been well characterized. The objective of this national, observational study was therefore to assess whether a documented past M184V/I mutation is associated with an increased risk of virological failure (VF) after switching to DOR/3TC/TDF in virologically suppressed PWH.

METHODS

This was a national, multicenter observational cohort study conducted within the Dat’AIDS cohort, a French prospective database including adult PWH followed in routine clinical care, in 30 HIV centers [8]. Individuals living with HIV-1 were eligible if they initiated DOR/3TC/TDF before 31 December 2023, had plasma HIV-1-RNA <50 copies/mL at the time of switch, had at least 1 subsequent plasma HIV-1-RNA measurement, and had no documented resistance to tenofovir or doravirine prior to switching.

Archived resistance mutations were identified from all available historical genotypic resistance tests performed on plasma HIV-1-RNA and/or proviral HIV-1-DNA prior to switching. All genotypes were reinterpreted using the ANRS-MIE algorithm (v35, https://hivfrenchresistance.org).

The primary outcome was VF, defined as 2 consecutive plasma HIV-1-RNA ≥50 copies/mL or a single measurement ≥200 copies/mL after switching to DOR/3TC/TDF, provided that virological rebound resulted in discontinuation of treatment.

In terms of statistical analysis, baseline characteristics were compared according to archived M184V/I status. In order to compare the risk of VF based on the presence or absence of a past M184V/I mutation, weighted Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To account for baseline differences between groups, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores was applied. Variables included in the propensity score model were age, gender, country of birth, duration of virological suppression before switch, duration of HIV infection, and CD4 nadir. All analyses were performed using R-Studio software.

The Dat’AIDS cohort is approved by the French data protection authority (CNIL no. 1357652) and registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02898987). All participants signed a consent form for the use of their medical data.

RESULTS

Among 2434 virologically suppressed PWH who initiated DOR/3TC/TDF during the study period, 1386 (57%) had at least 1 available genotypic resistance test prior to switching. After exclusion of individuals with documented resistance to TDF and/or DOR, 1282 participants were included in the primary analysis (Table 1). Median age was 48 years (interquartile range [IQR] 39–56). Cisgender men accounted for 54% of participants and cisgender women for 44%. Median CD4 nadir was 258 cells/mm3 (IQR 142–394). Participants had a median duration of cART exposure of 10 years (IQR 5–16) and a median duration of virological suppression of 6 years (IQR 2–11). Median CD4 cell count was 657 cells/mm3 (IQR 487–871) at time of switch.

Archived M184V/I mutation was identified in 94 participants (7.4%), last documented a median of 7 years (IQR 3–12) before switching to DOR/3TC/TDF. Participants with archived M184V/I had a longer duration of HIV infection and cART exposure, a lower CD4 nadir, and a higher frequency of previous VF compared with those without M184V/I (Table 1).

After a median follow-up of 81 weeks (IQR 37–147) under DOR/3TC/TDF, VF was observed in 40 participants (3.1%). Kaplan-Meier estimates of VF according to archived M184V/I status are presented in Figure 1. Post-failure resistance genotyping was available for 15/40 participants (38%). Comparison of pre- and post-failure resistance profiles identified emergent resistance mutations to doravirine in 2 participants (P1, Y188L; P2, M230L, and F227C). One of the 4 participants had archived M184V/I prior to VF, and M184V was detected in 3/4 at VF, including therefore 2 cases of de novo M184V emergence.

VF occurred in 7/94 participants (7.4%) with archived M184V/I and in 33/1188 participants (2.8%) without archived M184V/I. However, after IPTW adjustment, archived M184V/I was not significantly associated with VF (adjusted HR 1.36; 95%CI 0.51–3.66; P = .50) (Figure 1).

DISCUSSION

In this large national cohort, archived M184V/I mutation was not significantly associated with an increased risk of VF after switching to DOR/3TC/TDF in virologically suppressed PWH, after adjustment for major baseline differences. However, confidence intervals remained compatible with clinically relevant differences and residual confounding cannot be excluded. These findings extend previous observations suggesting that isolated M184V/I mutations may not substantially compromise maintenance strategies based on second-generation INSTIs or bPIs [2–4], providing additional evidence in the context of doravirine-based regimens.

Although NNRTIs generally have lower resistance barriers than second-generation INSTIs and bPIs, doravirine has a favorable resistance profile within its class. In our study, VF was rarely accompanied by the emergence of resistance mutations, regardless of whether the virus harbored archived M184V/I. However, resistance testing at VF was incomplete and these findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Our results are consistent with a recent retrospective observational study in which archived M184V/I was also not associated with an increased risk of VF, albeit in a smaller sample size (N = 338, including 45 individuals with M184V/I) [9]. In addition, the ongoing DRIVE-OFF-ROAD study, a prospective pilot trial, is evaluating the maintenance of virological suppression after switching to DOR/3TC/TDF in PWH with historical M184V/I in whom the mutation is no longer detected on proviral DNA genotyping at baseline; final results are not yet available [10]. In the present observational study, information on the presence or absence of M184V/I on proviral DNA genotyping at the time of switch was not available.

Several studies have suggested the potential clearance or decay of resistance mutations within the HIV reservoir over time, due to both quantitative and qualitative alterations of integrated proviral DNA [11–13]. If confirmed, this phenomenon could at least partially explain why historical resistance mutations may not systematically translate into clinically relevant loss of virological control. Notably, a recent study demonstrated that doravirine-based regimens were able to maintain virological suppression despite the presence of previously documented NNRTI resistance mutations that would theoretically confer complete resistance to doravirine [14].

DOR/3TC/TDF offers several practical advantages, including avoidance of second-generation INSTIs and tenofovir alafenamide, both of which have been associated with weight gain and metabolic disturbances [15]. In addition, doravirine's favorable drug-drug interaction profile makes this regimen particularly attractive for aging PWH with comorbidities and polypharmacy. This population is also the most likely to harbor archived M184V/I mutations, given their complex treatment and virological histories during the 1990s and early 2000s. Our findings are therefore of particular clinical relevance in this subpopulation.

This study has several limitations. Its observational design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded despite the use of IPTW. Historical resistance data were incomplete, and participants with archived M184V/I had greater availability of historical resistance tests, likely reflecting longer HIV duration and more complex treatment histories. Consequently, some participants may have harbored undocumented archived M184V/I mutations, potentially leading to exposure misclassification and attenuation of observed associations. In addition, historical resistance information relied on both plasma HIV-1-RNA and proviral HIV-1-DNA genotypes, which provide complementary but non-equivalent information. While plasma RNA genotypes reflect replicating virus, proviral DNA genotypes capture archived viral populations and may not fully represent the replication-competent reservoir, resulting in imperfect concordance and uncertain clinical significance of some archived mutations. M184I may, in some cases, result from APOBEC-mediated editing of proviral DNA and may lack clinical relevance when present in defective viruses. This highlights the importance of careful clinical reasoning when prescribing cART, integrating a detailed analysis of the virological and treatment history of PWH rather than relying solely on resistance genotypes recorded in the medical chart. Finally, resistance testing at the time of VF was available only in a minority of failures, limiting interpretation regarding mechanisms of treatment failure and resistance emergence.

In conclusion, in this observational study, archived M184V/I mutation was not significantly associated with VF after switching to DOR/3TC/TDF in virologically suppressed PWH after adjustment. However, residual uncertainty remains and clinically relevant differences cannot be excluded. Prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings.
