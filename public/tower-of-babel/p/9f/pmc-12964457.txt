Piano performers' experiences of Tai Chi for physical comfort and mental well-being: a qualitative study
Authors: Peng Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe purpose of this study was to explore piano performers’ experiences of practicing Tai Chi and their perceptions of how it supports pain relief, mental regulation, and performance-related control.MethodsThis qualitative descriptive study conducted semi-structured, in-depth interviews with five piano performers who had practised Tai Chi for at least 1 year. The interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, and analyzed using thematic analysis with iterative coding, grouping, and clustering to derive core themes.ResultsThe participants reported integrating Tai Chi into daily warm-ups, practice breaks and pedagogical routines. Perceived benefits included reduced muscle tension and pain, improved bodily coordination and tone control through principles such as “lowering the shoulders and elbows,” and enhanced focus and emotional regulation before performance. Several participants described faster recovery from fatigue and increased efficiency during practice.ConclusionsTai Chi was described by participants as alleviating performance-related physical discomfort, supporting psychological calm, and improving aspects of pianistic control. While the findings are limited by the small sample, they suggest Tai Chi as a feasible adjunct for performers and teachers.Keywords: Tai Chi, piano performance, musculoskeletal pain, qualitative research, musician wellness

Introduction

Musicians, whether professional pianists, students, or amateurs, often face physical discomfort and emotional strain in their pursuit of music. This discomfort is a direct result of the demands of prolonged and intense practice. Sitting at the piano for hours on end, maintaining a static position while performing repetitive hand and finger movements can lead to musculoskeletal issues as well as psychological fatigue and stress. These challenges can impact performance skills and career development if left unaddressed (Ciurana Moñino et al., 2017). A study found that 79% of generally physically healthy music students reported a history of performance-related pain (Hay et al., 2022). While traditional biomedical approaches address these challenges, there is a growing interest in exploring alternative methods that integrate physical and mental well-being. One such practice, Tai Chi, a traditional Chinese practice that combines body movement, deep breathing, and relaxation, has gained attention for its potential to alleviate physical pain and enhance overall health.

In the context of piano performance, the core principles of Tai Chi align closely with the physical and psychological challenges commonly experienced by pianists. Piano playing involves prolonged sitting, repetitive fine-motor movements, and sustained muscular engagement, which frequently contribute to localized tension, repetitive strain, and inefficient use of physical force. Tai Chi's emphasis on slow, controlled movement, postural awareness, and whole-body coordination may help counteract these issues by promoting more efficient movement patterns and reducing unnecessary muscular effort. At the same time, the integration of breath regulation and mindful attention in Tai Chi has been suggested to support emotional regulation and reduce heightened arousal, which are central features of performance anxiety (Robins et al., 2012). From this perspective, Tai Chi offers a theoretical framework for addressing both the musculoskeletal strain and mental stress associated with intensive piano practice (Kong et al., 2016).

Studies have demonstrated its effectiveness in managing chronic conditions such as osteoarthritis, lower back pain, and osteoporosis, with long-term practice yielding more pronounced benefits (Kong et al., 2016). A comprehensive review of clinical studies spanning decades further highlights its value across 25 specific health conditions, encouraging healthcare professionals to adopt evidence-based recommendations for its use (Huston & McFarlane, 2016). Building on these findings, its application in the field of music has also begun to attract attention. Initial research suggests that Tai Chi can improve natural breathing, body alignment, and muscle relaxation for singers (Park, 2009). It may also contribute to balancing traditional and modern piano techniques through its Yin–Yang philosophy (Ying & Chiat, 2012).

Although existing research has documented the potential benefits of Tai Chi for relieving physical pain and supporting mental well-being, much of this work has focused on clinical outcomes or generalized health effects. Less attention has been given to how pianists themselves understand, integrate, and apply Tai Chi within the context of their daily practice and professional demands. In particular, there is limited qualitative research examining how performers make sense of Tai Chi in relation to their own experiences of physical strain, psychological stress, and performance-related challenges. To address this gap, the present study adopts a qualitative descriptive approach to examine how piano performers understand and use Tai Chi in everyday musical practice and how they relate these experiences to physical comfort and mental well-being. Literature review

Musician's musculoskeletal pain

Musculoskeletal discomfort typically develops over time, with localized pain spreading to a wider area and possibly progressing from an acute condition to a chronic one (Arendt-Nielsen et al., 2011). Owing to the limited availability of effective pain management techniques, individuals suffering from chronic musculoskeletal pain often experience psychological distress. Research indicates that 30 to 60% of chronic pain patients also struggle with depression (Crofford, 2015). For musicians, daily practice necessitates maintaining the same position for extended periods of time. Poor posture, repetitive movements, and prolonged practice can all contribute to muscle fatigue and discomfort. Corrêa's et al. (2018) study found that musculoskeletal discomfort is prevalent among keyboard musicians, affecting between 25.8 and 77.0%, particularly in the upper body, including the wrists and hands, as well as the neck and shoulders. However, this prevalence-focused evidence offered limited insight into how performers manage these symptoms in day-to-day practice or what self-regulation strategies they consider workable within performance routines.

Mental health issues of musicians

Musculoskeletal pain is a common physical problem among musicians, but psychological issues are equally prevalent and more complex. Vaag et al. (2016) conducted a large-scale online survey to systematically compare the prevalence of anxiety and depression symptoms between Norwegian professional musicians and the general workforce. The study involved 1607 Norwegian professional musicians and 2550 members of the general workforce, and the results indicated that the proportion of anxiety and depression symptoms was significantly higher in the musician group. The study also highlighted the psychological health challenges faced by professional musicians, particularly the influence of socio-psychological factors and individual characteristics on their mental distress, such as work-related stress, performance pressure, job insecurity, and social evaluation demands. However, the study did not explore ways to develop mental health programs specifically designed for musicians' unique needs.

In a systematic literature review, Fernholz et al. (2019) investigated the prevalence, risk factors, and intervention effects of performance anxiety among professional musicians. The findings indicated that performance anxiety is more prevalent in the musician population, with a higher incidence among female and younger musicians. Additionally, cognitive–behavioural therapy has emerged as the most commonly used intervention, demonstrating relatively substantial effects. Fernholz et al. (2019) emphasized the importance of conducting more systematic and cross-cultural investigations into mental health interventions designed for this professional population.

Li (2024) examined the multifaceted effects of piano performance on performers, with a particular emphasis on emotional expression, flow experience, and performance anxiety. The investigation employed a mixed-method approach, surveying 108 Chinese piano majors. Li (2024) found that flow experience significantly optimized performance and alleviated anxiety and that emotional expression was a comprehensive reflection of technical capability, intrinsic skills, and synchronization between personal emotions and the music's emotional qualities. However, Li's (2024) research sample was constrained to the Chinese cultural context and did not explore potential differences across other cultural backgrounds, underscoring the need for further investigation in this area.

Overall, existing studies document elevated levels of anxiety and depression among musicians and point to multiple contributing pressures. At the same time, the literature remains uneven in two respects: it emphasizes prevalence and risk factors more than performer-centred strategies, and it offers limited qualitative detail on how musicians experience and regulate psychological strain within everyday practice and performance settings. These gaps support the need for research that examines musicians' own perspectives on practices that integrate physical and psychological regulation.

Tai Chi philosophy

Tai Chi has its origins in China and is an ancient form of Chinese martial arts with a history spanning hundreds of years. It integrates meditation with physical movement, deep breathing, and relaxation to help alleviate chronic pain conditions like osteoarthritis (Wise, 2016). In Chinese, Tai Chi translates to “supreme ultimate fist” (Field, 2011, p.141). To engage in Tai Chi practice effectively, one must grasp its underlying principles. The art emphasizes bodily harmony and draws on the philosophy of Yin–Yang balance (see Figure 1) to inform each movement. In contemporary discussions of Tai Chi in health and performance contexts, this emphasis on balance is often associated with ongoing bodily regulation and coordination. According to this theory, all the elements in the universe have opposite yet interconnected counterparts. In our daily lives, there are many examples of yin and yang, such as summer, which represents yang, and winter represents yin. The sky is yang, and the earth is yin; men are yang, and women are yin.

Aside from the Yin–Yang balance, Tai Chi also encompasses philosophical concepts intimately connected to people's everyday experiences. In the Robins et al. (2012) study, participants who did Tai Chi for 12 weeks shared how the moves helped them deal with stress, showing the deep meanings behind the Tai Chi moves. For instance, the “Smoothing the Waters” form helped a mother guide her two children in resolving conflicts (p. 137). The “Embrace Tiger, Return to Mountain” form symbolizes confronting fear and transforming it into tranquillity and inner fortitude, inspiring practitioners to overcome challenges through compassion and courage (p. 137). The “Five Elements” form, through embodying the four seasons and the five elements, enable practitioners to comprehend the cyclical transformations of nature and human existence, and accept the impermanence and mutability inherent in life (p. 137). For the present study, these interpretations are relevant because they illustrate how Tai Chi forms can function as meaning-based cues for coping and emotion regulation, which connects more directly to musicians' stress-related experiences.

The physical and psychological impact of Tai Chi on musicians

In music education and performing arts, Tai Chi philosophy is gradually becoming a valuable topic of study owing to its unique insights regarding the flow of body and energy. Park (2009) focuses on incorporating Tai Chi's breathing techniques and movements into vocal practice, arguing that integrating selected Tai Chi movements and breathing practices may support singers' breath management, body alignment, and throat relaxation, with potential implications for vocal timbre and expressiveness. By applying Tai Chi's “Crown Inhalation” and “Abdominal Breathing” methods, students can concentrate more on Danjeon breathing, which can improve the natural flow of airflow and the coordination of the body (p. 62–63). In Tai Chi practice, Danjeon breathing is commonly understood as slow diaphragmatic breathing with attention directed toward the lower abdomen, which can improve the natural flow of airflow and the coordination of the body. Park (2009) emphasized that the principles of Tai Chi are closely linked to the breathing skills involved in singing, which can assist students in restoring natural breathing, achieving a balance between breath control and throat relaxation, and ultimately realizing a healthy and seamless singing performance.

This idea of natural balance between the body and energy has not only been explored in the field of vocal music but also been incorporated into the study of piano performance techniques. Ying and Chiat (2012), by drawing upon Tai Chi philosophy, analyzed how to achieve a balance between traditional and modern piano techniques. They noted that the traditional approach emphasizes the flexibility of the fingers but often disregards the collaboration of other body parts, which can easily lead to hand tension and limitations in expressiveness. Conversely, the modern approach emphasizes the involvement of the arms and shoulders to reduce the pressure on the fingers, but excessive reliance on this may diminish the precision of finger techniques. Borrowing the Tai Chi concept of “yin within yang, and yang within yin,” they proposed that by integrating traditional finger techniques and modern methods of body coordination, performers can attain overall balance and natural flow, avoiding the problems of incoordination caused by over-emphasizing a single approach.

Extending this discussion to pianist-oriented applications, Popa (2012) examined how Tai Chi principles have been proposed as resources for managing practice-related strain and supporting performance fluency. She pointed out that Tai Chi emphasizes the integration of breath and movement to achieve the flow of energy, an idea that aligns closely with the pursuit of fluency and coherence in piano playing. Particularly, the philosophical concept of “Stillness in Movement, Movement in Stillness” provides a theoretical foundation for performers to avoid local tension in a state of dynamic balance (p. 156). Popa (2012) proposed various Tai Chi-based exercise methods, such as adjusting body alignment, gentle movements, and Tai Chi breathing techniques. These methods not only help enhance the overall coordination of the body but also promote the effective flow of energy, offering a cross-cultural innovative approach to piano pedagogy and performance.

Utilization, safety, and efficacy of Tai Chi

Tai Chi is a relatively gentle and slow exercise that can be practised without complex equipment, making it convenient for any time and place. There are no specific practice frequency requirements, allowing participants to choose the set of moves according to their personal needs. Wayne et al. (2014), in a systematic review of 50 randomized controlled trials, reported no serious adverse events associated with Tai Chi practice; the adverse events that were reported were typically minor and musculoskeletal in nature, such as knee pain, lower back discomfort, or transient muscle soreness. However, the authors also noted substantial variability and generally poor quality in adverse event reporting, which limited more definitive safety conclusions. Similarly, Cui et al. (2019) concluded that Tai Chi appears generally safe, particularly for patients with heart failure. Additionally, Tai Chi is a low-cost form of exercise, making it affordable and accessible to a wide range of participants (Lan et al., 2013). Considering these safety and cost-saving factors, Tai Chi is widely accepted as an exercise option among various age groups, including the elderly. Overall, existing reviews suggest Tai Chi is generally safe when appropriately instructed, though conclusions about safety remain constrained by inconsistent adverse event reporting.

Previous research described Tai Chi practice as typically learned under the guidance of qualified instructors, often in group settings, with practice sessions commonly lasting around 30 min (Taylor-Piliae, 2003). While Tai Chi is part of traditional Chinese medicine and has the potential to treat chronic conditions, variations exist in instructors' expertise and knowledge, where certification standards differ between China and Western countries (Oh et al., 2018). Therefore, selecting a qualified instructor is crucial for receiving accurate and professional guidance when learning Tai Chi.

Methodology

Study design

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive research design to document piano performers' experiences with Tai Chi in a clear and practice-oriented manner. Qualitative descriptive research is well suited to studies that seek to remain close to participants' own descriptions and to present findings in everyday language instead of developing abstract theory or engage in intensive interpretive analysis (Conway, 2020; Patton, 2002). The selection of a qualitative descriptive approach was guided by the study's focus on how piano performers described their motivations for engaging in Tai Chi, their approaches to practice, and the changes they perceived in their physical and mental well-being over time. In contrast to phenomenological or other interpretive traditions, which emphasize identifying the essential structure of lived experience, this study concentrates on summarizing recurring themes and capturing the range of variation in participants' descriptions. This approach allows the findings to remain grounded in participants' accounts while maintaining relevance for applied music and health contexts.

Participants

The participants for this study were identified through personal referrals and targeted online searches. The researchers extended 9 interview invitations, of which 5 were accepted (participant demographics are presented in Table I). Although the sample size was small (n = 5), it was considered appropriate for the exploratory and in-depth nature of this qualitative descriptive study. The participants were selected because they had sustained experience with both piano performance and Tai Chi practice and were able to reflect on how these practices intersected in their professional lives.

The study was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board (IRB), ensuring the protection of participants' privacy and rights. To maintain participant privacy, the study was conducted anonymously, and no personally identifiable information was collected. During the recruitment process, potential participants were provided with detailed explanations regarding the study's purpose, methods, and data confidentiality procedures and were formally invited to participate only after obtaining their informed consent. Written informed consent to take part in the study was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. The study strictly adhered to the guidelines set forth by the ethics committee, safeguarding the rights of the participants and allowing each individual to freely choose whether to participate and withdraw at any stage without any adverse consequences.

Data collection and analysis

The data for this study were primarily gathered through online semi-structured, in-depth interviews conducted over a three-month period. Each interview lasted approximately 20–30 min and was flexibly adjusted based on the participants' specific circumstances. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants' consent and subsequently transcribed verbatim to facilitate analysis. Data collection and preliminary analysis proceeded concurrently. The interviews continued until sufficient detail was obtained to address the study aims, and consistent patterns in the participants' descriptions began to recur across the interviews. No substantially new themes emerged in the later stages of data collection, which aligns with the concept of information power in qualitative research (Malterud et al., 2016).

Data analysis followed a thematic analysis approach guided by qualitative descriptive principles (Conway, 2020). The researcher led the data analysis, and an independent researcher served as a secondary reviewer of the developing codes and themes. The researcher conducted the analysis by reading the interview transcripts multiple times to become familiar with the data and to note initial ideas related to participants' descriptions of their experiences with Tai Chi. Open coding was then used to identify meaningful segments of data and recurring ideas across participants' descriptions. These initial codes formed the basis for organizing the data into broader themes. Codes were developed inductively from the interview data and were refined through repeated comparison across transcripts. Throughout this process, brief analytic notes were used to document decisions related to code refinement and theme development.

During the grouping stage, the researcher compared and combined related codes in order to summarize key aspects of participants' descriptions. For example, themes related to the use of Tai Chi in music contexts included improved physical coordination, psychological relaxation, and perceived benefits for teaching and learning. This step helped clarify the central themes and organize the findings in a coherent way. After developing the initial themes, the researcher returned to the full dataset and reviewed all the coded segments with equal analytic attention to ensure that no participant perspectives were overlooked. The thematic structure was refined by removing redundant or weakly supported themes and by clarifying the boundaries between the remaining themes. The final set of themes provided a clear structure for presenting the findings in relation to the research questions. Each theme was supported by data from multiple participants, with attention paid to both recurring patterns and meaningful variation across accounts.

To enhance the credibility of the analysis, an independent researcher reviewed the coding process and thematic organization and provided feedback on the clarity and coherence of the findings. In addition, preliminary findings were shared with participants to invite feedback and confirm that the results reflected their experiences.

Results

Research question 1: how are piano performers introduced to Tai Chi, and what motivates them to adopt it?

Getting started with Tai Chi

This theme describes how participants were first introduced to Tai Chi through informal and context-specific encounters. Jack mentioned that during his college years, he was troubled by wrist pain from playing the piano and decided to try Tai Chi after a friend suggested that it might be helpful. Sam began learning Tai Chi due to family influence and recognized its physical and mental benefits. She described: “Through focusing on conscious breathing and movement, I experienced the unique charm of ‘dynamic meditation,’ which allowed me to stay present and alleviate the anxieties of daily life.” Ali's interest was piqued by his piano teacher introducing him to the concept of qigong in Chinese culture, leading him to learn Tai Chi to address muscle tension. Casey encountered a Tai Chi group practicing in the park and was captivated by their soothing, harmonious movements. This chance observation later motivated him to study Tai Chi formally as a means of addressing his shoulder and neck concerns. Susan turned to Tai Chi to address physical fatigue from piano practice, seeking a gentle way to regulate her body and mind. These descriptions indicate that participants' initial engagement with Tai Chi emerged from everyday experiences and practical needs related to piano practice.

Motivations for adopting Tai Chi

This theme captures participants' motivations for adopting Tai Chi, which centred on managing physical strain and supporting mental regulation associated with piano performance. Ali recounted his recovery experience, stating:

I was long troubled by dystonia1 caused by playing the piano. After trying passive treatments like massage and acupuncture with limited effect, it wasn't until my second Tai Chi instructor guided me that my hand tremors gradually improved, and I've now largely recovered. This has made me believe in the power of active treatment.

Similarly, Susan emphasized that Tai Chi facilitated her recovery in a more gentle manner, rather than relying solely on external interventions. Other interviewees mentioned that Tai Chi is not only effective for physical recovery but also provides a unique experience of mind‒body balance. Across interviews, these motivations were described as part of a broader effort to regain bodily control and psychological balance in demanding performance contexts.

Research question 2: how do piano performers incorporate Tai Chi into their daily routines and professional practice?

Integrating Tai Chi into daily practice

This theme describes how participants incorporated Tai Chi techniques into their daily routines alongside piano practice. Casey described performing simple Tai Chi warm-up exercises, such as “Pushing Hands” and “Embracing the Ball,” prior to his daily piano practice, as these movements help him relax his shoulders and wrists. Additionally, he engages in breathing exercises during his practice breaks, which serve to restore his focus and mitigate the physical fatigue and tension that can arise from prolonged practice. Sam reported utilizing several minutes of Tai Chi's dynamic meditation after her piano practice to regain energy, stating that “This brief practice helps me avoid the physical and mental exhaustion caused by high-intensity practice.” Similarly, Susan noted that she integrates Tai Chi's rotational and waist-twisting movements into her piano practice, perceiving them as a means of warming up and restoring her physical energy. These descriptions show that participants adapted Tai Chi practices flexibly to support the physical and mental demands of daily piano practice.

The integration of Tai Chi principles into professional practice

This theme focuses on how participants applied Tai Chi principles to their professional piano practice. Ali particularly mentioned the core Tai Chi concept of “lowering the shoulders and elbows,” which helped him avoid excessive wrist twisting while playing the piano, allowing for more natural force application and more stable tone production. Susan shared, “Through Tai Chi, I learned that the mind should precede the movement when playing the piano, which has helped me better control the tone and improve my focus.” Jack explained that by understanding the danjeon power concept in Tai Chi, he grasped the mechanism of transferring force. This allowed him to optimize the transfer of physical strength, directing energy more efficiently towards the piano keys and achieving a significantly improved performance outcome with less effort.

Application in piano pedagogy

This theme captures how participants incorporated Tai Chi techniques into their piano pedagogy to support students' physical awareness and relaxation. Ali mentioned, “Muscle tension disorder is a common issue that professional pianists may encounter. I also used to practice very hard. After incorporating Tai Chi, my teaching approach changed. I'll have students practice a stance-like movement, keeping their hands on the keyboard motionless for a few minutes. This can help them become aware of tense areas in their body and make adjustments.” Similarly, Susan reported guiding her students through Tai Chi movements to relax their shoulders and arms, which she observed enhanced their key sensitivity and mitigated fatigue from extended practice sessions. These teaching practices reflect participants' efforts to translate Tai Chi principles into pedagogical strategies for sustainable piano technique.

Research question 3: how do piano performers perceive the effects of Tai Chi on their physical health and mental well-being?

Perceived changes in physical comfort and tension

This theme reflects participants' perceptions of Tai Chi as a practice that alleviated and prevented physical tension associated with prolonged piano practice. Jack likened practicing piano and Tai Chi to a full-body workout, noting that as long as he employed the Tai Chi mindset of generating force from danjeon, he was able to avoid the soreness associated with localized exertion. Casey recounted a particular experience: “After a full day of rehearsal, my wrists and fingers were stiff to the point of being unable to lift them. Through a few minutes of Danjeon breathing and embracing the ball movements, my arms gradually released the tension, and the flexibility of my fingers quickly recovered.” Similarly, Susan stated that practicing Tai Chi significantly reduced the sense of pressure in her shoulders and neck, and her movements became more fluid and natural. Ali described perceiving notable relief in his muscle tension and a change in how he understood his physical well-being after practicing Tai Chi. Ali emphasized that, based on his own experience, Tai Chi felt more helpful to him than other approaches he had tried, such as massage and acupuncture, in managing symptoms he associated with dystonia.

Improvement in mental health

Tai Chi also plays an important role in relieving psychological stress. Susan stated: “Practicing Tai Chi has taught me how to slow down. Especially when preparing for a concert, a few minutes of Tai Chi practice can quickly calm me down and help me focus.” Sam further shared that the yin–yang balance concept of Tai Chi has taught her to accept her state, rather than relentlessly pursuing perfection. This mental shift has rendered her more at ease when confronting challenges. Ali mentioned that the meditative nature of Tai Chi helped him alleviate the psychological fear caused by his dystonia and restore a sense of psychological safety.

Perceived influences on professional performance

This theme describes how participants perceived Tai Chi as contributing to improvements in professional performance. Susan shared that she was able to mitigate the chilly sensation in her hands before performances by employing Tai Chi breathing techniques: “By directing my breath flow to my fingers, I was able to warm my hands and achieve more consistent playing.” Similarly, Casey expressed that Tai Chi has cultivated his awareness of the integrated connection between his physicality and the piano keys. Casey perceived this heightened bodily awareness as supporting greater consistency in tone production and technical execution. The holistic betterment of the physical and mental faculties has enabled the participants to approach their professional challenges with greater confidence.

Recommendations for practicing Tai Chi

The participants generally express the view that Tai Chi, as a gentle form of physical exercise, can help piano performers alleviate physical discomfort and psychological stress to a certain extent. However, they also emphasize the significance of proper practice, particularly the accuracy of postures and the scientific nature of the methods. Sam emphasized that although the slow pace and relaxing characteristics of Tai Chi can aid in stress reduction, improper postures may result in physical injury. Ali described his understanding that Tai Chi includes diverse practice styles, some of which he associated with greater physical force and confrontational elements. From his perspective, these styles were not appropriate for pianists and could pose a risk of injury if practised incorrectly. Consequently, they advise learners to seek guidance from professional Tai Chi instructors to ensure the standardization of movements. Susan added that to maximize safety and effectiveness, Tai Chi should be practised in accordance with one's own physical condition, and they also recommend seeking professional guidance rather than relying on self-study.

Discussion

This study explored how piano performers engage with Tai Chi, integrate it into their daily and professional practices, and the impact of Tai Chi on their physical health and mental well-being, revealing the unique value of Tai Chi in musical practice. The findings suggest that participants described encountering Tai Chi in diverse ways, often through informal means such as instructor recommendations, family influence, or personal observation. This pattern matters in two ways. One implication is that Tai Chi can be introduced through everyday interactions in studio and community settings, which makes it realistic to adopt it in music contexts without building a complex instructional system. Another implication is that informal diffusion can produce uneven instruction quality, so practical guidance and safety considerations become especially important when Tai Chi is recommended within conservatories or formal training programs. For the broader piano community, these pathways suggest a scalable, low burden entry point for wellness support that can be integrated into studio culture without major curricular restructuring.

The participants described Tai Chi as shaping performance and supporting physical and mental well-being, and they emphasized that these influences were closely connected (see Figure 2). Their descriptions suggest a unified practice logic in which technical coordination, physical comfort, and psychological regulation reinforced one another during practice and performance. This perspective extends prior studies that have largely emphasized Tai Chi's role in pain management or breath control (Kong et al., 2016; Park, 2009) by showing how performers interpreted Tai Chi as a meaning laden practice embedded in daily musical work, one that reorganized attention, movement, and self-evaluation.

Effects of Tai Chi on Piano Performers.

In terms of professional performance, the findings suggest that the principles of Tai Chi can be translated into practical kinaesthetic cues that support efficiency at the keyboard. The “lowering of the shoulders and elbows” concept, for example, was described as helping optimize force transfer during key playing, reducing unnecessary wrist twisting and supporting tonal stability. Popa (2012) discussed Tai Chi principles mainly at a conceptual level, and the present findings strengthen that connection by showing how performers applied those principles as moment-by-moment coordination cues in everyday practice. The participants described using shoulder and elbow release, wrist neutrality, and whole arm support as ongoing reference points for touch and tone control. They also noted that incorporating Tai Chi movements during practice breaks helped relieve muscle tension associated with long practice sessions. Under high practice loads, these brief movement resets can be understood as a way to interrupt tension build-up and maintain motor efficiency across extended work periods.

For physical well-being, participants linked Tai Chi to comfort and recovery that supported sustained practice under instrumental workload. The participants described how breathing and gentle movement supported comfort in the wrists, fingers, shoulders, and neck and improved flexibility. This aligns with Kong et al. (2016), who argued that Tai Chi's gentle motions and deep breathing can mitigate chronic pain. The present study extends this literature by clarifying why these changes were meaningful in a musician's context. Physical relief was often interpreted as enabling practice continuity and reducing the bodily cost of repetition heavy training. In this sense, recovery was valued as functional support for sustainable practice routines, not simply as symptom reduction.

For mental well-being, the findings highlight Tai Chi's regulatory role through breath, attention, and philosophical reframing. The participants described using Tai Chi breathing techniques to stay composed during concert preparation and to reduce performance anxiety. This is consistent with Robins et al. (2012), who found that Tai Chi can reduce psychological stress and improve concentration through slow movement and deep breathing. Beyond this general correspondence, the participants emphasized that ideas such as yin yang balance and “stillness in movement” served as usable regulation resources. They described learning to monitor arousal, soften harsh self-judgment, and accept outcome variability, which supported steadier preparation and fewer spirals under pressure. This shift toward acceptance can also be interpreted in mind and body terms. Reduced anticipatory hyperarousal may lessen simultaneous muscle activation and perceived tension, supporting both emotional steadiness and physical ease in demanding passages.

The findings also suggest clear pedagogical value when translated into brief, feasible studio routines that complement established technical training. Teachers might begin a lesson with a short standing awareness check, grounding through the feet, softening the knees, and releasing the shoulders. At the keyboard, a quick scan that focuses on release and alignment can help students connect posture with touch sensitivity and tone stability. During repetition heavy work, a breathing reset of about 60 to 120 seconds between runs may help reduce fatigue build-up and re-establish efficient movement. These routines can be framed as self-monitoring skill development. Students learn to notice early tension signals, redistribute effort, and recover efficiently, which supports key sensitivity and endurance. This translation also invites interdisciplinary dialogue between music education and somatic education, traditional medicine, and psychology while keeping the focus on musicians' professional demands.

Safety guidance is essential, especially given variation in training quality and the absence of standardized global certification. Although Tai Chi is often viewed as a lower cost and safer option compared to some medical interventions for chronic pain, it is not risk free (Cui et al., 2019; Lan et al., 2013; Ushida, 2015; Wayne et al., 2014). The participants emphasized learning movements with professional guidance and avoiding blind imitation to reduce hazards and improve outcomes. From an institutional standpoint, this matters because many certification programs are offered through private training institutions, and there is no single standardized global system that guarantees consistent instructional standards across settings. Conservatories and studios that recommend Tai Chi can adopt basic vetting practices, such as verifying the instructor training background, clarifying what certification entails, confirming experience with performer populations, and encouraging gradual progression and modification when discomfort arises. Clinical boundaries should also be explicit. When performers described improvement in conditions such as focal dystonia, these descriptions should be treated as perceived symptom change rather than clinical recovery. Dystonia is a complex neurological disorder that requires medical oversight, and Tai Chi should be framed as a complementary self-regulation practice, not a substitute for clinical care.

Conclusion

The purpose of this study was to understand the physical and psychological impact of Tai Chi on piano performers through listening to the real-life experiences of piano performers who practice Tai Chi. The findings indicate that participants perceived Tai Chi as supporting physical comfort, psychological regulation, and aspects of their professional performance. This study contributes to existing literature by offering a performer-centred perspective on Tai Chi that complements prior clinical and health-focused research. Rather than evaluating Tai Chi in terms of outcomes alone, the findings illustrate how piano performers understood and applied Tai Chi within their daily practice, professional performance, and pedagogical work. Although this study has limitations, such as a relatively small sample size, its results lay the foundation for future larger-scale, cross-instrument domain research. Overall, Tai Chi, as an innovative supplementary practice method, provides a feasible, practical approach for music educators and performers, further promoting the diversification of the music performance field.
