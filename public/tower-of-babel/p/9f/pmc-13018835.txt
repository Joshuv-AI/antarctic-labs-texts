iPSC-induced multilineage liver organoids, small intestinal organoids and brain organoids sustain pangenotype hepatitis E virus propagation
Authors: Fan Liu, Tianxu Liu, Xi Wu, Zihao Yu, Jincheng Tong, Lili Wang, Chenyan Zhao, Jianhui Nie, Youchun Wang, Kai Wang, Lin Bai, Pengyan Xia, Kai Wang, Guochao Wei, Zi-Zheng Zheng, Di Wu, Weijin Huang, Lin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatitis E virus (HEV), the leading global cause of acute viral hepatitis, lacks robust in vitro models for virology and pathogenesis research.ObjectiveWe evaluated induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-induced human liver, intestinal and brain organoids (hLOs, hIOs and hBOs) as platforms for HEV infection and replication.MethodsMultilineage organoids were infected with clinical HEV genotypes 1, 3 and 4. Viral tropism, host responses and antiviral efficacy were assessed.ResultsAll organoids supported the complete life cycle of HEV. hLOs exhibited infection in hepatocytes, cholangiocytes, macrophages and stellate cells, accompanied by elevated interleukin-6 levels, impaired hepatic function (reduced secretion of albumin and Factor IX) and increased levels of alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase, indicating hepatocellular injury. hIOs demonstrated broad epithelial (enterocyte, goblet, Paneth and endocrine) and mesenchymal infection, with disrupted barrier function (loss of tight junction proteins), proinflammatory cytokines upregulation and initiation of epithelial–mesenchymal transition. hBOs showed neuronal tropism, infecting glutamatergic, dopaminergic and GABAergic neurons, as well as astrocytes and oligodendrocytes. An increased number of TH+ dopaminergic neurons was observed. Ribavirin treatment in all three models partially reversed this phenotype.ConclusionThis study established iPSC-induced multilineage organoid infection models, confirming HEV’s capacity for pan-tissue infection and revealing potential pathogenic mechanisms in the liver, intestine and nervous system. This platform provides valuable tools for HEV virology research and antiviral drug development, underscoring the unique value of organoid technology in infectious disease research.

Introduction

Hepatitis E virus (HEV), a single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus, belongs to the Hepeviridae family and is the causative agent of hepatitis E.1 HEV is distributed worldwide and is most prevalent in developing countries; however, the seropositivity rate in many developed countries can exceed 10%, which is a significant concern.2 To date, at least eight genotypes of HEV (HEV1–8) have been identified across different species, with HEV1–4 being primarily responsible for human infection worldwide. HEV1 and HEV2 infect humans,3 whereas HEV3 and HEV4 are zoonotic.4 Recently, rat HEV (species Rocahepevirus ratti, HEVC1) has been characterised as an emerging human pathogen capable of causing chronic infection in immunosuppressed patients.5 Therefore, further research on HEV genotypes is essential to better understand viral transmission and pathogenesis.

Since the discovery of HEV, extensive efforts have been made to establish culture models for in vitro recapitulation; however, progress has been limited. To date, only one cell-adapted HEV strain, Kernow-C1/p6, has demonstrated replication in hepatoma cell lines with moderate efficiency.68 Widely used culture models, including Huh7, Huh7-Lunet BLR, HepG2/C3A, PLC/PRF/5 and HepaRG liver cancer cell lines, as well as A549 lung adenocarcinoma cells,814 and conventional two-dimensional (2D) monolayer systems, exhibit low infection efficiency with clinical strains because of poor similarity to hepatic tissue development and physiology. Consequently, studies have explored induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived hepatocyte-like cells (HLCs) or neurons, as well as cultures derived from adult primary human Sertoli cells and human intestinal cells, for HEV propagation.1518 Recently, three-dimensional (3D) hepatic epithelial organoids from primary foetal or adult biopsies were established and found to be susceptible to cell-adapted HEV strains in vitro.19 However, these models contain only one or two sub-lineages (eg, liver organoids typically consist of hepatocytes and/or cholangiocytes), limiting their utility for investigating HEV infection in more physiologically intact systems that include non-epithelial supportive lineages, such as mesenchymal or immune cells. Moreover, although HEV is hepatotropic, extrahepatic replication and manifestations—including neurological injury and intestinal infection—have been frequently reported in patients.15 18 Therefore, a robust in vitro platform for investigating extrahepatic infection pathogenesis is urgently needed.

Recently, the US Food and Drug Administration and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) jointly promoted the adoption of ‘New Approach Methodologies’, including organoid-based technologies, to progressively replace traditional animal testing.20 Addressing this need, we systematically developed human 3D multilineage liver, small intestinal and brain organoids from iPSCs and co-cultured them with clinical HEV strains. These multilineage mini-organs effectively supported HEV infection and reproduced pathophysiological features observed in patients, thereby providing a valuable tool for in vitro research on wild-type HEV.

Results

Establishment of multilineage liver organoids that support full life cycle of HEV infection

Since 2D HLCs support the infection with wild-type HEV strains,16 studies using tissue-derived 3D liver organoids have also shown HEV infection in both hepatic and biliary duct cells.19 In this context, we established multilineage liver organoids (hLOs) from human iPSCs via a co-differentiation approach (see Material and methods). These hLOs contained both hepatic epithelial cells (hepatocytes and cholangiocytes) and supportive cell types (Kupffer cells and hepatic stellate cells) (online supplemental figure S1).

To determine whether hLOs can support HEV infection, we inoculated them with three HEV subtypes derived from faecal samples: HEV1, HEV3a and HEV4. Viral RNA was detected in all three groups at 2 days post infection (dpi), with peak titres ranging from approximately 103–105 copies/mL (figure 1A–C). Among these, HEV3a supernatants showed increasing copy numbers with extended inoculation, whereas levels declined for the other two genotypes. As expected, hepatic parenchymal cells were the primary target cell type, as indicated by ORF2 protein localisation in albumin (ALB)+ hepatocyte-like cells across all three HEV subtypes (figure 1D). Quantitative immunofluorescence revealed that the proportion of HEV-infected hepatocytes for each genotype mirrored the viral load released into the supernatant (figure 1E), suggesting that supernatant titres reflect intracellular viral replication levels. To further validate the ability of hLOs to support the complete HEV life cycle, we conducted a secondary inoculation experiment using harvested viral supernatants. The results confirmed that hLOs supported progeny virus transmission (figure 1F–I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that hLOs are permissive to the complete life cycle of all tested HEV strains, supporting robust viral infection, replication and release of infectious progeny virions.

Notably, the cell-adapted P6 strain also exhibited high infectivity in hLOs, as demonstrated by active replication and detectable viral RNA in the supernatant (figure 1J). Ribavirin mediated suppression of P6 replication further confirmed the utility of this platform for HEV antiviral research.

HEV-infected non-parenchymal cell types of hLOs and impaired hepatic functionality

Because HEV3a displayed the highest infectivity, we used this subtype for subsequent analyses (online supplemental figure 2A). To examine cellular tropism beyond hepato-biliary epithelial cells (online supplemental figure 2B), we performed a comprehensive immunofluorescence analysis.21 22 In addition, co-localisation of ORF2 and vimentin (VIM)+ hepatic stellate-like cells was observed (online supplemental figure 2B), although the infection frequency was lower than in the other three lineages (4/60, online supplemental table 1). To validate HEV infection of hepatic stellate cells, sequential immunofluorescence staining was performed, co-localising ORF2 with both VIM and ALCAM (Activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule gene, also known as CD166, a specific stellate cells marker23) on adjacent sections. Detection of ORF2 signals in cells positive for either VIM or ALCAM confirmed HEV tropism for hepatic stellate cells (online supplemental figure 2C). Quantitative analysis of ORF2-positive cells revealed distinct cellular tropism, with hepatocytes showing the highest infection rate (22.35%), followed by cholangiocytes (18.96%) and Kupffer-like macrophages (8.7%). Hepatic stellate cells exhibited minimal susceptibility (1.7%) (online supplemental figure 2D).

We next investigated the functional consequences of HEV infection. ELISA analysis of supernatants showed 2.5-fold and 1.8-fold reductions in albumin secretion in primary and secondary HEV3a-infected hLOs, respectively, compared with untreated controls (online supplemental figure 2E). This aligns with clinical evidence showing lower serum albumin levels in patients with HEV.24 HEV infection also impaired secretion of coagulation Factor IX (FIX), a plasma protein critical for haemostasis, with 2.1-fold and 1.7-fold reductions in primary and secondary infections, respectively (online supplemental figure 2F). Additionally, HEV significantly increased interleukin (IL)-6 secretion in this 3D model (online supplemental figure 2G). Importantly, the model also reproduced a key clinical feature of HEV infection: elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels, which are well-established markers of hepatic injury (online supplemental figure 2H,I).25 These findings highlight the ability of HEV to infect non-parenchymal liver cell types and functionally recapitulate liver impairment in vitro.

Ribavirin alleviated inflammation and partially restored hepatic function

To further assess the clinical relevance of this infection model, we treated hLOs with ribavirin, an off-label therapy for chronic HEV infection.26 27 A 48-hour ribavirin treatment strongly inhibited viral RNA in the culture supernatant (inhibition rate~99%; figure 2A), with no cytotoxicity observed at the tested concentration (500 µM; figure 2B). Consistently, intracellular HEV was largely cleared but remained detectable, as indicated by ORF2 fluorescence signals just above the detection threshold (figure 2C,D). Moreover, treatment with the nucleoside analogue NITD008 (10 µM)28 significantly suppressed HEV replication, as evidenced by reduced viral RNA in the supernatant (figure 2E). These findings confirm productive HEV replication and validate the model as a platform for antiviral drug evaluation.

Notably, ribavirin reversed the HEV-induced increase in IL-6 secretion, restoring levels to near those of uninfected controls (290.0 vs 360.9 pg/mL, figure 2F). In addition, secretion of ALB and FIX, which was impaired by HEV infection, was mildly restored (figure 2G,H). These results reinforce prior observations of ribavirin’s anti-HEV activity,29 30 and demonstrate its ability to alleviate hepatic inflammation. Collectively, these data underscore the utility of hLOs as a platform for studying HEV infection and therapeutic interventions.

iPSC-induced human multiple-lineage small intestinal organoids are permissive for non-adapted HEV infection

HEV replication has been previously reported in human primary intestinal cells18 and swine intestinal tissue.31 To further characterise susceptibility in the human intestine, we established iPSC-induced intestinal organoids (hIOs), comprising both epithelial lineage and mesenchymal compartment (online supplemental figure 3A–D). Transcriptomic profiling at day 38 confirmed close similarity to adult primary intestine (online supplemental figure 3E,F). To mimic physiological host–pathogen interactions, we reversed the polarity of hIOs using a suspension culture method, as previously described.32 33 This exposed the apical epithelial surface to the surrounding medium, while the basal side faced the lumen (figure 3A).

On HEV3a inoculation, viral RNA was detected at 2 dpi, peaking at ~5×105 copies/mL at 6 dpi in hIOs culture supernatant (figure 3B,C). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed open reading frame 2 (ORF2) expression in multiple intestinal epithelial cell types (ECAD+, E-cadherin), including VIL1+ (Villin 1) enterocytes, LYZ+ (Lysozyme) Paneth cells, MUC2+ (Mucin 2) goblet cells and CHGA+ (Chromogranin A) endocrine cells (figure 3D; online supplemental figure 4A), with approximately 27% of the epithelium infected (online supplemental figure 4B). In contrast, a significantly higher infection rate (47.8% on average) was observed in the mesenchymal compartment compared with uninfected controls (p<0.001; online supplemental figure 4C). These results demonstrate that HEV can infect four distinct epithelial cell types within hIOs—a tropism consistently observed in the reinfection experiments.

In antiviral testing, ribavirin markedly reduces HEV levels in both extracellular (figure 3C) and intracellular compartments (figure 3D). However, as in hLOs, complete viral clearance was not achieved.

HEV infection disrupts the intestinal barrier in hIOs

The intestinal barrier, composed of a single epithelial layer interconnected by junction proteins and protected by mucus, is critical for maintaining homeostasis.34 35 Beyond nutrient absorption, it prevents systemic pathogen dissemination and modulates immune responses.36 37 Given HEV’s association with extrahepatic manifestations, including gastrointestinal symptoms,18 38 39 we hypothesised that infection compromises barrier integrity, thereby exacerbating disease progression. At 6 dpi, a time point chosen based on preliminary replication kinetics, quantitative PCR revealed significant downregulation of tight junction genes (CLDN1, ZO1 and OCLN) and CFTR, which regulates junction assembly and function (figure 3E–H). By contrast, MUC2 expression increased 5.5-fold (figure 3I). Immunofluorescence confirmed these findings at the protein level (figure 3J). Furthermore, reduced expression of epithelial marker ECAD and increased expression of mesenchymal markers NCAD and VIM (figure 3K–M) indicated activation of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT). Together, these results demonstrate systemic disruption of the intestinal barrier in hIOs, which may facilitate viral dissemination.

HEV infection also upregulated key inflammatory mediators, including interferon gamma 1 (IFN-γ1), IL-6, IL-1α and chemokine C-X-C motif ligand 5 (CXCL5) expression (figure 3N–Q). This aligns with findings by Marion et al18 showing that despite strong interferon induction, HEV replication persists in intestinal organoids.

Ribavirin moderately decreased inflammatory mediator expression and partially attenuated EMT progression; however, it failed to reverse HEV-induced barrier dysfunction within the short treatment window (figure 3E–Q).

hIOs also support the cultivation of HEV1 and HEV4 strains

To assess whether hIOs could be infected by other HEV subtypes, we tested HEV1 and HEV4. Both genotypes established productive infection in intestinal organoids, although HEV4 exhibited delayed RNA detection in the supernatant until day 4 post-inoculation (figure 4A,B). This temporal difference may reflect genotype-specific replication kinetics or distinct virus–host interactions. Consistent with these findings, immunofluorescence analysis confirmed ORF2 expression in epithelial cells for both genotypes (figure 4C). Quantitative analysis revealed infection of approximately 22.2% and 35.5% of intestinal epithelial cells by HEV1 and HEV4, respectively (figure 4D,E). Ribavirin effectively suppressed replication of both genotypes, as shown by significant reductions in HEV RNA and ORF2 expression in treated vs untreated groups (figure 4A–E).

hLO-hIO system recapitulated sequential gut-liver-gut infection of HEV

We next examined HEV’s cross-organ infection potential by inoculating hLOs with supernatant from HEV3a-infected intestinal organoids (gut-to-liver transmission) and, conversely, challenging hIOs with supernatant from infected liver organoids (liver-to-gut transmission). Cross-infection experiments showed that supernatant from HEV3a-infected intestinal organoids productively infected liver organoids (figure 5A,B), with ORF2 expression detected in hepatocytes (figure 5C). Conversely, supernatant from infected liver organoids established infection in intestinal organoids (figure 5D), with viral RNA detectable in supernatants as early as 2 dpi and replication observed in epithelial cells (figure 5E,F). Notably, ribavirin treatment inhibited viral replication in this liver-to-gut model (figure 5E). To further confirm infectivity, supernatant from liver-to-gut transmission was reinoculated into intestinal organoids, successfully establishing infection (figure 5G).

hBOs support the full life cycle of HEV infection

HEV’s extrahepatic manifestations, such as pancreatitis, glomerulonephritis, cryoglobulinaemia and neurological complications,4043 have increasingly drawn attention. Since the first report of HEV-related Guillain-Barré syndrome,44 the link between HEV infection and neurological disease has become a research focus. Steinmann’s group recently demonstrated HEV neuroinvasiveness in an iPSC-induced 2D neuron model.15 Building on this, we established 3D brain organoids (hBOs) from iPSCs (online supplemental figure 5A). These hBOs comprised three neuronal subtypes (TH+ dopaminergic, GABA+ GABAergic and vGLUT+ glutamatergic neurons) and two glial subtypes (GFAP+ astrocytes and Olig2+ oligodendrocytes) (online supplemental figure 5B), some of which expressed the mature neuronal marker SYN (online supplemental figure 5C). We then used this model to evaluate HEV neurotropism and functional impact.

Viral RNA was detected in culture supernatants following infection (figure 6A). Infectious progeny virus remained detectable after serial passage into second-generation hBOs, with titres reaching 104.7 copies/mL (figure 6B). To our knowledge, this is the first confirmation that brain organoids support the complete HEV replication cycle.

Immunofluorescence analysis showed broad ORF2 distribution across all neural lineages (figure 6C), with ~28.9% MAP2+ pan-neurons infected (figure 6D). Subtype-specific infection rates were ~15.4% in GABAergic neurons, ~51.9% in glutamatergic neurons and ~21.2% in dopaminergic neurons (figure 6E–G).

Because HEV infection was previously shown to reduce synaptic density in 2D neural,15 we examined changes in hBO sublineages. Expression of MAP2, GABA and vGLUT2 showed no significant differences between infected and control groups (figure 6H–J). In contrast, TH, a dopaminergic neuron marker, increased significantly (threefold, p<0.0001; figure 6K).

Ribavirin treatment reduced viral secretion by ~99% (figure 6A) and decreased replication in MAP2+ neurons (figure 6C,D). Viral loads were reduced across all neuronal subtypes (figure 6E–G). Notably, ribavirin reversed the HEV-induced increase in tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) expression (figure 6K). These findings provide important experimental evidence for HEV neurotropism and offer a platform for therapeutic development.

hBOs reveal permissiveness to HEV1 infection

Although HEV3 is more often associated with neurological disease than HEV1 or HEV4,23 we investigated the neurotropism of both genotypes in hBOs. Only HEV1 achieved productive infection, with viral RNA levels increasing significantly over time (figure 6L). Ribavirin treatment suppressed replication, eliminating detectable RNA in supernatants (figure 6L). In contrast, HEV4 failed to establish productive infection, as no viral RNA was detected during the observation period (figure 6M).

Discussion

HEV is predominantly hepatotropic, but growing evidence confirms its capacity for extrahepatic replication in tissues such as the intestine, placenta, testes and nervous system.15 17 18 45 However, most current research relies on single-cell models or simplified functional structures, which fail to recapitulate the tissue complexity and microenvironmental cues influencing HEV replication. This limitation has hindered a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying HEV extrahepatic replication and its pathological implications.

Here, using iPSC-induced organoid models of the liver, intestine and brain, we investigated the target cells of HEV infection and its functional effects. We demonstrated that liver and intestinal organoids robustly support productive infection with multiple clinical HEV genotypes (HEV1, HEV3a and HEV4), completing the full viral life cycle. In contrast, brain organoids permitted infection with HEV1 and HEV3a but showed no detectable release of HEV4 in the supernatant, revealing a genotype-specific tropism for the central nervous system. This observation is consistent with clinical reports that neurological manifestations are more frequently associated with HEV3 infections.234648

In hLOs, we observed co-localisation of HEV ORF2 with stellate cells, although at low frequency, providing a potential mechanism for chronic HEV-induced liver fibrosis. HEV infection also induced hepatocyte injury, as shown by elevated AST and ALT levels, and impaired hepatic function, evidenced by reduced albumin secretion.49 Furthermore, infection disrupted hepatocyte polarity, reflected by altered albumin distribution,5052 and triggered a proinflammatory response with IL-6 upregulation. Ribavirin treatment partially restored hepatic function while suppressing IL-6 expression. The nucleoside analogue NITD008, previously reported to inhibit HEV in cell lines,28 also effectively reduced replication in organoid, providing further evidence of productive infection. Collectively, these results confirm that hLOs represent a robust and reliable platform for antiviral drug screening.

In hIOs, HEV exhibited broad tropism, infecting both epithelial and mesenchymal cells. These findings corroborate that the small intestine serves as an extrahepatic replication site for HEV before hepatic dissemination.18 Notably, infection compromised barrier integrity and induced EMT. This aligns with prior reports of blood–brain barrier disruption in HEV-infected rabbits and Mongolian gerbils.5355 The observed upregulation of MUC2, a goblet cell marker, also suggests aberrant proliferation resembling goblet cell metaplasia described in clinical settings.56 Ribavirin treatment attenuated inflammatory cytokine expression, but our results emphasise the need for adjunctive therapies aimed at restoring intestinal barrier function and suppressing EMT. Future studies should elucidate how HEV disrupts barrier integrity and induces EMT, as reported for other enteric viruses.57 58

Notably, we established a bidirectional transmission cycle between intestinal and liver organoids. Supernatant from infected hIOs successfully reinfected hLOs, mirroring the natural gut-to-liver route. Conversely, supernatant from infected hLOs established productive infection in hIOs, recapitulating in vivo liver-to-gut transmission. This cross-infection model reinforces the intestine as an extrahepatic replication site for HEV,185961 potentially facilitating viral dissemination and faecal–oral transmission. Although antiviral treatment suppressed replication, progeny viruses from secondary infections remained infectious, demonstrating sustained dissemination across organoid types.

In hBOs, HEV exhibited broad cell tropism. Infection-induced cytoplasmic mislocalisation of OLIG2, which is a transcription factor normally restricted to the nuclear,62 and increased expression of TH, a key enzyme in dopamine synthesis. These findings align with studies that elevated dopamine supports replication of Japanese encephalitis virus63 and HIV.64 High dopamine levels may similarly enhance HEV replication. OLIG2 mislocalisation also suggests possible impairment of glial development, including neural differentiation and myelination. Whether HEV directly damages neurons or triggers immune-mediated pathology remains unresolved. Ribavirin reduced viral loads and may indirectly correct HEV-related neuronal abnormalities, such as TH expression, by lowering replication levels. These findings highlight ribavirin’s therapeutic potential in HEV infection of the nervous system.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study focused on HEV genotypes 1, 3 and 4. Although HEV2 is less prevalent, future work should evaluate its pathogenic profile to provide a more complete picture of HEV genotypes capable of infecting humans. Second, organoid sampling was performed only at single time points (days 3 or 6 post-infection), which may not fully capture infection dynamics. Future studies should incorporate live-cell imaging and multiple time points to define replication kinetics. Third, consistent with practices in HEV and other organoids research, replication was mainly assessed via genomic copy number using reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) standard. Although titration is not widely used in this field, the inherent 3D architecture of organoids, which prevents formation of uniform monolayers, poses technical barriers to viral titration. Future work should focus on developing organoid-based assays for determining the median tissue culture infectious dose of HEV, which would enable a more comprehensive assessment of infectivity. Finally, while specific markers were used in this study to identify cell types within the organoids, additional validation using single-cell sequencing and clinical samples remains essential. Such efforts would reinforce the reliability of the results.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that human liver, small intestinal and brain organoids support the complete HEV life cycle, including replication and secretion of infectious progeny from clinical isolates. The multicellular complexity of these systems provides a powerful platform for dissecting pathogenic mechanisms in the liver, intestine and brain, particularly in understudied extrahepatic sites.

Material and methods

Maintenance of human iPSC

The human iPSC line ChiPSC22 used in this study was purchased from Takara (Kusatsu, Japan, created by reprogramming human skin fibroblasts, RRID: CVCL_RN02, passage number 35–42) and maintained under feeder-free conditions as previously described.65 Briefly, cells were cultured with mTeSR1 medium (STEMCELL, Canada) on six-well plates coated with Matrigel (Corning, New York, USA) at 1:50 dilution in an incubator with 37°C and 5% CO2. Cells were checked daily for differentiation and passaged every 4 days.

Differentiation of hLOs

For differentiation of multiple-lineage liver organoids, the endoderm and mesoderm were co-induced as previously reported.66 67 Briefly, a density of 1.75×105 iPSCs/cm2 was used to attain approximately 80–90% starting confluency within 48 hours. Then, mTesR1 was replaced with endoderm-mesoderm co-induction medium for 4 days: 10 ng/mL BMP4 (R&D Systems, USA) and 100 ng/mL Activin A (R&D Systems, USA) in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 1×GlutaMAX (Gibco, USA), 10% mTeSR1, 2% NB27 w/o insulin (iORGANtech, China). Next, the cells were cultured with posterior foregut medium for another 4 days: 100 ng/mL Activin A, 1 ng/mL bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4), 5 ng/mL fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2, R&D Systems, USA) and 10 ng/mL vascular endothelial factor (VEGF, Peprotech, USA) in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 1×GlutaMAX, 1×NB27 (iORGANtech, China), and 1×NB2 (iORGANtech, China). At the end of differentiation day 8, the culture was harvested as single cells using TrypLE Select (Gibco, USA), and seeded onto a U-bottom low-attachment microplates (384-well, Greiner, Germany) to form spheroids, at a density of 250 viable cells per well. After that, cells were cultured with progenitor patterning medium for 3 days: 1 µM RepSox (Tocris, USA), 25 ng/mL Wnt3a (Wnt family member 3a, R&D systems, USA), 10 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF, Peprotech, USA), 10 ng/mL VEGF (Peprotech, USA), and 10 ng/mL Jagged (R&D systems, USA) in advanced DMEM/F12 (Dullbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 1×GlutaMAX, 1×NB27, and 1×NB2, and then with 2 µM retinoic acid (Tocris, USA) for another 3 days. At day 14, organoids were collected and transferred to a low-attachment six-well plate (Greiner, Germany), with the medium changed to hepatic maturation medium until day 25: 20 ng/mL oncostatin M (OSM, R&D systems, USA), 20 µM FH1 (BRD-K4477, Selleck, USA), 5 µM DAPT (γ-secretase inhibitor, Tocris, Minneapolis, USA), 20 ng/mL bone morphogenetic protein 7 (BMP7) (R&D systems, USA), 20 ng/mL FGF19 (R&D systems, USA) and 0.5 µM dexamethasone (DEX, Selleck, USA) in HepatoZYME (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 5% Matrigel. The medium was replaced every 3 days.

Differentiation of hIOs

For differentiation of multiple-lineage intestinal organoids, endoderm and mesoderm were co-induced for 4 days, as aforementioned. Then, the cells were cultured with mid/hindgut medium for another 4 days68: 500 ng/mL FGF4 (R&D Systems, USA) and 500 ng/mL Wnt3a in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 1×NB27, 1×NB2 and 1×GlutaMAX. Then, single cells at day 8 were harvested using TrypLE Select and seeded onto a U bottom low-attachment 96-well plate (Greiner, Germany), at a density of 5000 viable cells per well. At the end of day 8, the culture was treated with intestinal patterning medium for 3 days: 100 ng/mL EGF, 500 ng/mL R-Spondin 3 (R&D Systems, USA), and 150 ng/mL Noggin (R&D Systems, USA) in advanced DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1×NB27, 1×NB2 and 1×GlutaMAX. For the next day, day-11 formed aggregates were collected, embedded into Matrigel, and cultured with intestinal maturation medium until day 35: 100 ng/mL EGF, 0.5 µM A83-01 (Tocris, Minneapolis, USA), 5 µM 5AZA (Selleck, USA), 20 µM PD98059 (Tocris, Minneapolis, USA) and 5 µM DAPT in the same basal medium. The medium was replaced every 3–4 days.

Polarity reversal of hIO

Apical-out hIOs were prepared before infection by reversing the apical–basal polarity using suspension culture, as previously described.32 33 In brief, Matrigel-embedded day-28 hIOs were collected by incubation of Cell Recovery Solution (Corning, New York, USA) for 30 mins at 4℃, and centrifuged at 200 × g for 3 mins. Enriched organoids were washed with cold advanced DMEM/F12 medium for three times, and then transferred onto a low-attachment plate for 7-day suspension culture (up to day 35), with intestinal maturation medium (see Differentiation of hIOs). The medium was replaced every 3–4 days. Immunofluorescent staining of VIL1 and phalloidin was performed prior to infection experiments for apical-out examination.

Differentiation of hBOs

For differentiation of multiple-lineage brain organoids, iPSC was dissociated with accutase when reached 70–90% confluency, and seeded onto a U bottom low-attachment 96-well plate at a density of 5000 cells/well, using the embryoid bodies (EB) medium for 2 days: 10 µM Y-27632 in mTeSR1 complete kit for hES maintenance. Formed EB washed with DMEM/F12 was transferred onto a low-attachment plate and cultured with neural induction medium for 8 days: 0.2 µM LDN193189 and 0.5 µM A83-01 in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1% neural base 2 (NB2), 2% NB27, 0.5% non-essential amino acid (NEAA) and 0.5% GlutaMax. Then, the culture at day 10 was switched to neural expansion medium for 12 days: 10 ng/mL FGF2 in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1% NB2, 2% NB27, 0.5% NEAA and 0.5% GlutaMax. 10 ng/mL EGF was supplemented in day 16–21. Afterwards, the culture was switched to maturation medium until day 60: 25 ng/mL brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNT) Novoprotein, USA), 25 ng/mL Glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNT) Novoprotein, China), 0.5 µM FSK (Forskolin, Selleck, USA), and Vc (Vitamin C; Apexbio, USA) in BrainPhys (STEMCELL, Canada) supplemented with 5% Matrigel, 1% NB2, 2% NB27, 0.5% NEAA and 0.5% GlutaMax.

Extraction and purification of viruses

HEV strains were originally obtained from faeces collected from different patients with hepatitis E respectively (GenBank accession numbers: JQ655734.1,69 OQ801360.1,70 OM780138.1.71 The virus extraction from faeces was performed as described in a previous study.17 72 HEV-positive faecal samples were diluted in sterile phosphate buffer to prepare a 20% (w/v) suspension, which was centrifuged at 5000×g at 4°C for 20 min. The clarified suspension was first filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane, and then filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane to obtain the supernatant. For the control group, samples were prepared from HEV RNA-negative human stool specimens70 using the identical virus extraction protocol as the experimental groups, with all processing steps performed in parallel. Then the suspension to be purified was transferred to a 50 mL Falcon tube, centrifuged at 1000×g at 4°C for 10 min. The precipitate was discarded and the collected supernatant was again transferred to a 50 mL Falcon tube, and centrifuged at 10 000×g at 4℃ for 30 min. The precipitate was discarded and supernatant was transferred to a 35 mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 29 500×rpm at 4℃ for 120 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was dissolved in 1 mL of phosphate buffer saline (PBS). Next, 8%, 16%, 24%, 32% and 40% iodixanol solutions were prepared from a 60% Opti-Prep stock solution containing Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution, which is added slowly (900 µL) from the highest concentration to a 5 mL centrifuge tube. Then, 0.5 mL of suspended particles were loaded on top of the solutions in the centrifuge tubes, and centrifuged at 437 000 rpm at 4℃ for 18 hours. 24 fractions were collected from the top of the gradient. The first seven fractions were 0.5 mL each, and the subsequent fractions were 0.1 mL each. The supernatant (10–20 µL) was then used for RNA extraction and viral load measurement using the Viral DNA/RNA Kit (TransGen Corporation, Beijing, China), followed by RT-PCR analysis of HEV antigen using the GoTaq Probe 1-Step RT qPCR System Kit (Promega, Madison, USA). The primers and probes for HEV are listed in online supplemental table 1. HEV RNA was mainly concentrated in fractions 16–18.

HEV infection of human organoids

Before infection, hLOs, hIOs and hBOs that were suspended/embedded in Matrigel were collected by incubating with Cell Recovery Solution at 4℃ for 10 mins, 30 mins and 30 mins, respectively. The organoids were then washed three times using advanced DMEM/F12 medium, and transferred onto low-attachment microplates (6-well, Greiner, Germany) within their respective maturation mediums. For liver organoids, infection was performed in suspension using Matrigel-containing medium (5%) in low-attachment plates, whereas intestinal and cerebral organoids were directly infected in suspension within low-attachment plates. All infections were conducted at a dose of 1E6-1.5E6 RNA copies per well (based on total organoid cell count), followed by 6–24 hours incubation at 37°C with 5% CO2, with gentle pipetting every 6 hours to ensure homogeneity. The control group received HEV-negative extracts processed identically to the infected groups. Next, the organoids were collected and washed with DPBS (Dulbecco's Phosphate-Buffered Saline) three times, leaving the last wash as the baseline of infection. Afterwards, the three types of organoids were as indicated, with half of the medium being taken daily, while the fresh half was supplemented. For reinfection treatment, use the supernatant from the first round of HEV infection as the infection source, with a volume of approximately 100 µL added per well. For ribavirin treatment, prepare the culture medium containing 100 µM or 500 µM ribavirin and replace it with this liquid.

Real-time reverse-transcription quantitative PCR

All samples were rinsed with nuclease-free water (Ambition, New York, USA) before RNA extraction. Organoids were harvested in total, including those embedded in Matrigel. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) and 0.5–1 µg RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA using random hexamers and Oligo (dT) with Superscript III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA). Total RNA from the human adult liver (a 51-year-old male Caucasian), small intestine (pooled from five male/female Caucasians, aged 20–61 years) was purchased from TaKaRa and used as control samples for establishing hLOs, hIOs and hBOs, respectively. Quantitative PCR was performed on a QuantStudio 5 using the TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (TaKaRa, Kusatsu, Japan). Relative gene expression was calculated based on the average cycle threshold (CT) value of technical triplicates, normalised to housekeeping gene GAPDH, and reported as fold change (2-ΔΔCT), with a fold change of 1 assigned to the control sample. Undetected CTs were assigned a value of 35 to allow for fold-change calculations. PCR primers were designed using sequences from qPrimerDepot (http://primerdepot.nci.nih.gov); these are listed in online supplemental table 2.

RNA-seq and KeyGenes analysis

Total RNAs of day-28 hLOs and day-38 hIOs were prepared as aforementioned. Then, 10–20 ng total RNA samples were used for stranded RNA libraries establishment using RNA-seq library kit (Swift Biosciences, Ann Arbor, USA), and sequenced with an Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencer with 50 base single end reads. RNA-seq reads were aligned to human genome using STAR V2.7.1a (GRCh38).72 Gene counts were obtained using feature Counts.73 Next, RNAseq data of hLOs and hIOs was compared with different first, second trimesters and adult organs with KeyGenes (http://keygenes.nl/tool).

Immunofluorescence

All cultures were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 2 hours at 4°C and thoroughly rinsed with cold PBS before immunostaining. For cryosectioning, samples were immersed overnight in 30% sucrose, embedded in an optimal cutting temperature compound (Sakura, Torrance, USA), and sectioned at a thickness of 10 µm. Slides were then permeabilised for 30 min, blocked with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 5% donkey serum (Jackson Laboratory, West Grove, USA) for 30 min at room temperature, and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies in 1% donkey serum. The next day, AF488/594-labelled secondary antibodies in 1% donkey serum were added and incubated for 1 hour in the dark. The slides were washed with PBS, four times for 5 min each, after primary and secondary antibody staining. ProLong Gold with DAPI (4,6-diamino-2-phenyl indole, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) was used as the mounting medium. For whole-mount staining, the samples were subjected to the same steps except for incubation and washing in a tube rotator. All immunofluorescence images were acquired on a Nikon A1 HD25 confocal microscope using the NIS-Elements v5.21.00 software (Nikon, Okinawa, Japan). A complete list of the antibodies used is provided in online supplemental table 3.

Fluorescence qualification

All immunofluorescent qualification was performed using the image analysis software ImageJ. For qualification of the percentage of infected cells, images were assessed by using split colour channel, and fluorescence colocalisation was implemented followed by application of Mander’s overlap coefficients, with quantitative analysis conducted using the ‘Coloc 2’ plugin in ImageJ platform.

For the qualification of protein expression, images were converted to 8-bit images and the threshold was adjusted to correspond with the nuclear stain, which allows for measurement of total area. The total area was analysed using the ‘Analyze Particles’ tool of ImageJ. Percentage of positive cells was calculated by dividing the total area of positive cells over the DAPI area.

Flow cytometry

Liver organoids were isolated from Matrigel using Cell Recovery Solution (Corning, New York, USA), dissociated using TrypLE Enzyme (Gibco, USA), and strained through a 30 µm filter (PluriStrainer, USA). Single cells were fixed and permeabilised in Cytofix/Cytoperm (BD Biosciences, USA) for 20 min. The samples were then washed with Cyto-Fast Perm Wash solution (BioLegend, USA) and incubated with the appropriate antibodies. Negative controls stained with control IgG were used for sample measurements. Data were acquired on a CytoFLEX (Beckman, USA) using CytExpert V.2.0 software and further analysed using FlowJo X v10 (BD Biosciences, USA).

ALB, urea, FIX, IL-6, AST and ALT ELISA

To measure the indicated secretory proteins of hLOs, 250–300 organoids per well were seeded and cultured in ultra-low-attachment 24-well plates (Corning, New York, USA). The supernatants were collected after 24 and 72 hours from the culture and stored at −80℃ until used. ELISA kits for human albumin (Bethyl Laboratories, USA), urea (Bioassay Systems, USA), FIX (MultiSciences, China), IL-6 (MultiSciences, China), ALT (Absin, China) and AST (Absin, China) were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Colorimetric readings were observed at the specified wavelengths using a Biotek Synergy H1. Values were derived from standards using appropriate lines of best fit generated using Gene5 software. hLOs and culture supernatants were collected by centrifugation for 3 min at 300 g. Then, the hLOs were dissociated into single cells using TrypLE Express (Gibco, USA) and the data were normalised to 1×106 cells.

Compound toxicity assay

Mature liver organoids (≥day 23) were collected and suspended in 5% Matrigel medium. Approximately 100 organoids in 90 µL were seeded per well in a 96-well plate using a multichannel pipette, with six replicates per group. Blank wells received 100 µL maintenance medium. The plate periphery was filled with 100 µL DPBS to minimise evaporation and incubated overnight at 37°C with 5% CO₂. Compounds were diluted in DMSO and maintenance medium to achieve final concentrations of 10 µM Staurosporine or 500 µM RBV, then followed by mixing and 72-hour incubation.

Cell viability was assessed using CellTiter-Glo reagent (Promega, USA). After equilibration to room temperature for 30 min, 100 µL of reagent was added per well under light-protected conditions. The plate was orbital shaken at 500 rpm for 20 min, incubated for an additional 20 min, and luminescence was measured using a plate reader with an opaque seal applied to the bottom.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad V.8.0. All the data are presented as the mean±SEM. Statistical difference was analysed by two-tailed t-test, *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, ****p<0.0001.
