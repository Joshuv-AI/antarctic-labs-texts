Distal internal carotid artery dissection after carotid endarterectomy for a high cervical lesion suggesting an Eagle-like mechanism of shunt-related injury: illustrative case
Authors: Koki Ito, Eriko Kamijo, Kazuki Kushi, Tatsuki Mochizuki, Taku Nonaka, Bikei Ryu, Shinsuke Sato, Tatsuya Inoue, Yukiko Tsutsumi, Yasunari Niimi, Akitsugu Kawashima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDAlthough intraluminal shunt insertion during carotid endarterectomy (CEA) is widely used to maintain cerebral perfusion, distal intimal injury of the internal carotid artery (ICA) can occur as a rare yet consequential complication.OBSERVATIONSAn 82-year-old man with an elongated styloid process who underwent CEA for left ICA stenosis at the C2 vertebral level developed a new progressive high-grade stenosis immediately distal to the endarterectomy site, likely associated with shunt manipulation. Preoperative images demonstrated that the distal ICA segment at the C1 vertebral level was positioned between the styloid process anterolaterally and the transverse process of C1 posteromedially, in closer proximity to these osseous structures than on the right side. The dissected segment corresponded to the region of closest proximity between the ICA and the transverse process of C1. The patient underwent carotid artery stent placement for the distal dissected stenosis without neurological sequelae.LESSONSThis case suggests that an Eagle syndrome–like osseous-vascular configuration may predispose the artery to shunt-related injury during CEA. Meticulous preoperative assessment of the relationship between the ICA and both the transverse process of C1 and the styloid process may help mitigate the risk of distal ICA dissection in high cervical lesions with an elongated styloid process. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26320

Carotid endarterectomy (CEA) is a well-established treatment for internal carotid artery (ICA) stenosis.1 Intraluminal shunt insertion is commonly used during the procedure to maintain cerebral perfusion. Although rare, shunt-related complications, such as distal ICA intimal injury or dissection caused by the shunt tip or balloon, have been reported.2–5 We report a case of distal ICA dissection following CEA for a high cervical lesion. In this case, the distal segment of the left ICA was located in proximity to both the transverse process of C1 and an elongated styloid process, and 3D rotational angiography (3DRA) combined with cone-beam CT performed in the operative posture revealed that the origin of the dissection coincided with the region of closest proximity between the ICA and the C1 vertebra. This appears to be the first report to suggest that not only an elongated styloid process but also the relationship between the ICA and the transverse process of C1 during CEA may have contributed to the dissection. We speculate that this osseous-vascular relationship resulted in either direct mechanical stress during shunt insertion or reduced arterial mobility and compliance due to an osseous corridor formed by these structures. We present this case to highlight a potentially preventable mechanism of shunt-related distal ICA injury in high cervical CEA.

Illustrative Case

An 82-year-old man with severe aortic stenosis was scheduled for transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI). Preoperative MRI and MR angiography (MRA) incidentally revealed a left ICA stenosis. Subsequent carotid duplex ultrasonography confirmed the presence of severe left ICA stenosis (78.4% according to the European Carotid Surgery Trial method and 72.7% according to the North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial method), with a peak systolic velocity of 284 cm/sec in the stenotic segment.

One month after TAVI, CEA was planned to treat the left ICA stenosis. The lesion contained a calcified, vulnerable plaque extending distally to approximately the mid-C2 vertebral level, consistent with a high cervical ICA lesion (Fig. 1A– C). Although the lesion was located at a high cervical level, CEA was selected based on the presence of impaired renal function, advanced age, and a calcified lesion, which are known risk factors for carotid artery stent (CAS), as well as the feasibility of adequate surgical exposure. In retrospect, the distal segment of the left ICA (the C1 vertebral level) beyond the stenotic lesion was closely aligned with the styloid process anterolaterally and the transverse process of C1 posteromedially. The styloid process measured 38.4 mm on the right and 33.3 mm on the left, indicating bilateral elongated styloid processes. Although the right styloid process was longer, the left styloid process was oriented more medially and inferiorly, resulting in closer proximity between the left ICA and the surrounding osseous structures compared with the right side (Fig. 1D– F). Left CEA was performed under general anaesthesia with the neck extended and slightly tilted to the right (Fig. 1G). The distal ICA was exposed to the lower level of the C1 and a longitudinal arteriotomy was made from the common carotid artery into the ICA, followed by insertion of an intraluminal shunt (Mizuho Carotid Shunt Tube, Mizuho Co., Ltd.; proximal/common carotid limb outer diameter 7.0 mm, distal/internal carotid limb outer diameter 4.5 mm) (Fig. 1H). A shunt was used because prolonged clamping time was anticipated due to the high cervical lesion. Although mild resistance was initially encountered during distal shunt insertion, the shunt was advanced smoothly after adjusting the angle of insertion, and following shunt placement with confirmation of adequate intramural shunt flow, endarterectomy was performed. To secure the intimal edge, three distal tacking sutures were placed using 8-0 monofilament, and the arteriotomy was closed primarily with 5-0 monofilament. Intraoperative somatosensory and motor evoked potentials remained stable throughout the procedure, and no new neurological deficits were observed postoperatively.

MRI performed on postoperative day (POD) 2 revealed no new cerebral infarctions. However, although the MRA study performed on POD 2 and a CT angiography (CTA) examination performed on POD 5 confirmed improvement in the ICA stenosis at the endarterectomized site, a new high-grade stenosis, which had not been detected preoperatively, appeared immediately distal to the distal end of the endarterectomy (Fig. 2A). At our institution, CTA, MRI, and MRA are routinely performed within 1 week after CEA; CTA is omitted for a case of impaired renal function. In this case, despite impaired renal function, CTA was additionally performed because MRA suggested the presence of newly developed distal stenosis. MRA performed on POD 20 demonstrated further progression of this distal stenosis (Fig. 2B). The luminal diameter decreased from 2.2 mm to 1.7 mm, and the lesion length increased from 17 mm to 25 mm. The lesion exhibited a markedly narrowed true lumen with a newly apparent saccular false lumen, suggesting iatrogenic ICA dissection associated with intraluminal shunt insertion. Alternative etiologies such as vasospasm and atherosclerotic progression, were considered unlikely, as the lesion corresponded to the site of shunt insertion, showed progressive worsening rather than spontaneous resolution, and was not present on preoperative imaging. The dissected ICA segment lay in close apposition to the styloid process anterolaterally and to the transverse process of C1 posteromedially (Fig. 2C– E). Given that the lesion was considered to be dissection related, CAS placement was performed 33 days after CEA. Before CAS insertion, the neck was positioned in extension with slight rightward rotation to replicate the intraoperative posture used during CEA and to evaluate positional changes in the spatial relationship between the carotid vasculature and adjacent osseous structures. Fusion images were generated by combining maximum intensity projection (MIP) high-resolution 3DRA images of the left common carotid artery with cone-beam CT images obtained in the intraoperative posture of CEA. Because of the patient’s impaired renal function, only a single 3DRA study was performed; therefore, the positional relationship between the ICA and adjacent osseous structures in the neutral position could not be evaluated using MIP 3DRA–cone-beam CT fusion. Nevertheless, in the intraoperative posture of CEA, the transverse process of C1 was near the dissected ICA segment, and the origin of the dissection appeared to coincide with the region of closest proximity between ICA and C1 vertebra (Fig. 3A– C). Under general anaesthesia without intraoperative neuromonitoring, a double-layer micromesh stent (CASPER Rx, 6 × 30 mm; Terumo) was deployed to fully cover the dissected segment. Stent expansion was satisfactory with resolution of vascular mural irregularity and improvement in antegrade intracranial flow (Fig. 3D). No new neurological deficits occurred after CAS placement. Follow-up MRI and MRA performed on POD 2 showed no restenosis or intracranial infarction. The patient was discharged neurologically intact (modified Rankin Scale score 0) and remained asymptomatic at the 6-month follow-up with no neurological deficits.

Discussion

Observations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of a distal ICA dissection following CEA associated with the use of an intraluminal shunt, with the underlying mechanisms being attributed not only to an elongated styloid process but also to the anatomical association between the ICA and the transverse process of C1.

Although CEA is a well-established and generally safe procedure, rare shunt-related complications, such as distal intimal injury and dissection caused by the shunt tip or inflated balloon, have been described.3–5 Suzuki et al. have described mechanical interference between an elongated styloid process and a distal shunt balloon, leading to distal ICA dissection, with neck extension bringing the styloid tip closer to the ICA.2 This finding aligns conceptually with the carotid artery type of Eagle syndrome.

Eagle syndrome, first described by Watt W. Eagle, is a painful craniocervical condition caused by an elongated styloid process or an ossified stylohyoid ligament impinging on adjacent nerves or vessels.6 Classically, it presents as one of the following three patterns:7–9 the classic type, characterized by throat or neck pain, often following tonsillectomy; the carotid artery type, caused by irritation or compression of the ICA; and the jugular vein type, resulting from internal jugular vein compression. Beyond the classic elongated styloid paradigm, several reports have proposed a broader osseous-vascular entrapment mechanism, in which the ICA or jugular vein may be compressed between the styloid process anteriorly and the transverse process of C1 posteriorly.10–12 The juxtaposition and proximity of these osseous structures can accordingly contribute to the pathogenesis of Eagle syndrome.13 Several reports have indicated that in the jugular vein type of Eagle syndrome, contralateral neck rotation can accentuate the stenosis and increase the pressure gradient across the internal jugular vein.10,14 Moreover, the compressive effects of the transverse process of C1 become more evident with contralateral head rotation on dynamic CT venogram.15 Other reports have described positional transient ischemic attacks characterized by right-sided weakness provoked by leftward neck rotation, with symptoms resolving following resection of the elongated styloid process.16

The case reported herein extends these concepts of Eagle syndrome to include the risk of distal ICA dissection when performing high cervical CEA using an intraluminal shunt. As background anatomy in this case, the distal segment of the ICA was located in proximity to both the transverse process of C1 and the styloid process. Furthermore, when we replicated the intraoperative posture (neck extension with slight contralateral rotation) using 3DRA fused with cone-beam CT, the transverse process of C1 approached and partially overlapped the dissected ICA segment, and the dissection origin coincided with the region of closest osseous proximity. Two non–mutually exclusive mechanisms may underlie this distal dissection. First, direct mechanical contact between the distal shunt tip and the ICA wall could occur within a rigid, bone-constrained corridor. Second, styloid-C1 entrapment may reduce arterial mobility and compliance, thereby increasing resistance and amplifying intimal shear stress during shunt advancement or withdrawal. These findings are consistent with Eagle syndrome–like mechanisms, in which an elongated styloid process renders the ICA susceptible to interference from the adjacent bone during CEA surgical positioning.

Lessons

On the basis of our experience, we have identified certain factors that warrant consideration when performing CEA for high cervical ICA stenosis (with the shunt insertion level at C1) in patients with an elongated styloid process (generally > 30 mm). Notably, preoperative imaging should assess not only plaque morphology and distal landing zones but also potential osseous constraints. When preoperative imaging reveals that the ICA lies closer to the styloid process and the transverse processes of C1, CEA without shunt placement should be considered, if feasible, or CAS insertion may be preferable. If shunt insertion is necessary, the inflation volume and insertion depth of the balloon should be minimized, and the device should be advanced gently when resistance is encountered and with maximal caution, even when no obvious resistance is felt.

This study has certain limitations. First, this was a single-case observation, and we were unable to definitively establish the causal association between intraluminal shunt manipulation and distal ICA dissection. Second, dynamic imaging in both neutral and operative postures using the same modality was not feasible owing to renal dysfunction, thereby limiting direct positional comparison. Although efforts were made to reproduce the intraoperative neck position during imaging, exact replication could not be guaranteed. Third, we did not undertake quantitative assessments, such as the spatial distances between ICA, the styloid process, and the transverse process of C1, thereby precluding a precise measurement of osseous-vascular relationships.

In conclusion, osseous constraints associated with the styloid and the transverse processes of C1 can lead to the development of Eagle-like entrapment, thereby heightening the risk of distal intimal injury during shunt manipulation in high cervical CEA. Incorporating positional osseous-vascular assessment into preoperative planning and tailoring the shunt strategy may help prevent this uncommon but consequential complication.
