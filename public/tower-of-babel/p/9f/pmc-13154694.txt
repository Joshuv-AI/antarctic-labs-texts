Routes to cancer diagnosis for migrants in Denmark: a population-based nationwide cohort study
Authors: Anne Harbo Dahl, Henry Jensen, Alina Zalounina Falborg, Christian Morberg Wejse, Anne Mette Fløe Hvass, Per Kallestrup, Georgios Lyratzopoulos, Line Flytkjær Virgilsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMigrants are often diagnosed with cancer at more advanced stages than the background population, resulting in poorer prognosis. The route to cancer diagnosis is associated with the prognosis, but little is known about the diagnostic pathways for migrants. This study aimed to describe the routes to diagnosis (RtD) for migrants in Denmark and compare these with the RtD for patients of Danish origin.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide cohort study based on Danish registry data, including patients aged ≥ 18 years who were diagnosed with incident cancer between 2014 and 2018 (n = 154,647, including 8066 migrants). Using the UN M49 Standard, we categorised the study population into 15 groups based on country or region of origin (defined according to parental birth country or citizenship), including 14 migrant groups and a reference group of Danish origin. Each patient was assigned to one of six specified RtD. Using multinomial logistic regression models, we assessed associations between migrant groups and RtD.ResultsA total of 44.5% of all cancer patients were diagnosed through a primary care cancer patient pathway (CPP), with variations across migrant groups. Compared with patients of Danish origin, a higher risk of diagnosis via an unplanned hospital admission relative to a CPP from primary care was seen for migrants from the former Yugoslavia (relative risk ratio (RRR) 1.30, 95% CI 1.06–1.60), Germany (RRR 1.34, 95% CI 1.12–1.59), Western Asia (RRR 1.61, 95% CI 1.28–2.02), Türkiye (RRR 1.67, 95% CI 1.29–2.15) and Africa (2.02, 95% CI 1.58–2.60).ConclusionsSome migrant groups experienced higher diagnosis rates via unplanned hospital admission compared with patients of Danish origin, which could impact their prognosis. The results underline the importance of strengthening cancer diagnostic pathways for migrant populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15845-2.

Background

Migration is increasing worldwide, and the World Health Organization estimates that 281 million people currently live as international migrants [1], i.e. individuals residing in a different country than their usual country of residence [2]. The act of migration itself can be considered a social determinant of health [3], as it shapes the access to resources and opportunities in host countries. In this context, migrants often encounter several barriers to accessing healthcare [4]. Barriers that can affect cancer diagnosis include low awareness of cancer symptoms [5–8], language barriers [6, 7, 9], lower screening participation [10, 11] and difficulties accessing primary care services [5, 7, 9, 12]. Moreover, most studies show that migrants and patients with ethnic minority backgrounds have longer diagnostic intervals [13, 14] and are diagnosed at more advanced cancer stages [15–18] compared with the host populations, which may significantly impact the cancer prognosis.

A key predictor of cancer prognosis is tumour stage at diagnosis [19–21]. However, the route to diagnosis is also associated with the prognosis [22–24], with patients diagnosed following an unplanned hospital admission (often referred to as ‘emergency presentation’ in international literature) having the worst prognosis [23–25]. This association is only partly explainable by the level of tumour stage at diagnosis [25]. In Denmark, approximately 45% of all cancers are diagnosed through a fast-track referral to a cancer patient pathway (CPP) from primary care [24]. The one-year overall mortality for patients diagnosed through this route is 16% compared with 53% for patients diagnosed following an unplanned hospital admission [24].

It remains unknown if patients with a migrant background have different routes to diagnosis (RtD) than patients of Danish origin. Hence, we need more knowledge about the diagnostic routes for migrants with cancer in Denmark. We hypothesised that the RtD would vary across migrant groups and that migrants would be less likely to be diagnosed through a CPP initiated in primary care compared with patients of Danish origin. Thus, this study aimed to describe the RtD for migrants in Denmark and compare these with the RtD for patients of Danish origin.

Methods

Design

This nationwide cohort study was based on routinely collected Danish registry data and reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for cohort studies [26].

Setting

The population of Denmark comprises approximately six million inhabitants, and 12% are migrants [27]. The Danish healthcare system is funded by tax revenues, which provide free and equal access to most medical services, e.g. general practice and public hospitals [28]. Approximately 99% of the population is listed with a specific general practitioner (GP) [28]. The GP serves as a gatekeeper to secondary care and can refer patients for further diagnostic investigation, including CPPs, which are pre-specified diagnostic routes aimed at ensuring fast and standardised cancer workup. CPPs were implemented in Denmark between 2008 and 2010 and now cover approximately 40 different types of cancer [29]. National screening programmes exist for breast, colorectal and cervical cancer.

Study population

We included all patients registered with an incident malignant cancer diagnosis in the Danish Cancer Registry (DCR) [30] from 1 January 2014 to 31 December 2018 (n = 159,189), excluding non-melanoma skin cancer, thus including: C00-C43 & C45-C97 according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10). Following the exclusion of 4542 patients, the final study population comprised 154,647 patients with cancer, including 8066 migrants (Fig. 1).

Data collection

The data were collected from national Danish registers, including national clinical cancer databases. All data was linked at the personal level using the unique 10-digit personal registration number assigned to each resident in Denmark at birth or upon immigration [28].

Definition of migrants and country of origin

Data on migrant status (i.e. Danish origin or migrant) and country of origin were obtained from Statistics Denmark [31]. In line with Statistics Denmark, we defined migrants as individuals born outside Denmark, for whom neither parent meets the criteria of being both born in Denmark and holding Danish citizenship at the time of study inclusion. The country of origin was defined as the birth country or citizenship, if known, of the mother (first choice), the father (second choice) or the individual (third choice if no parental information was available).

There is no universally accepted definition of the term migrant, but it is often used to refer to a person who moves from one place to another, regardless of the reasons for moving, their legal status or the length of their stay [2]. In this context, a migrant can also be a refugee, a person whose status is defined by international law [2]. We acknowledge that the terms migrant and refugee are distinct and designate individuals in non-comparable situations. However, in this article, the term migrant will encompass individuals from various backgrounds and with different reasons for moving, such as to work, study or seek asylum under international law.

In our study population, migrants originated from a total of 157 countries. We categorised the study population into 15 groups based on the regions and subregions from the United Nations (UN) M49 standard country or area codes for statistical use [32] and the individual countries from which most migrants in our cancer cohort originated. This resulted in a reference group with patients of Danish origin (Denmark) and the following 14 migrant groups: Norway, Sweden, Poland, Germany, Türkiye, former Yugoslavia (referring to the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia and its successor states following the dissolution of the federation in the early 1990s), Eastern Europe (except Poland), Northern Europe (except Denmark, Norway and Sweden), Southwestern Europe (except the former Yugoslavia), Western Asia (except Türkiye), Southern Asia, Asia – others, Africa and Others.

Definition of routes to diagnosis

We based our definition of RtD on an algorithm developed in 2021 by Danckert et al. [24], which was formed in a Danish setting. The approach was conceptually similar to the English RtD algorithm first reported in 2012, although it was suitably modified to capture Danish data sources and diagnostic care organisation [24, 33–35]. The algorithm links individual-level healthcare interactions across multiple registers for the three months preceding a cancer diagnosis. Information on cancer diagnoses and diagnosis dates was based on data from the DCR. Information on hospital contacts, inpatient and outpatient visits and CPPs initiated from primary and secondary care was based on data from the Danish National Patient Registry (DNPR) [36], which covers both the public and private sectors. The Danish Breast Cancer Cooperative Group Database [37], the Danish Colorectal Cancer Group Database [38] and the Danish Quality Database for Cervical Screening [39] provided data on screen-detected breast, colorectal and cervical cancers, respectively. As originally, the algorithm assigned each patient to one of eight distinct RtD [24].

For this study, we excluded patients registered with cancer only on their death certificate in the DCR because of limited numbers. Due to small groups and similar prognosis [24], we combined the outpatient and planned hospital admission pathways, resulting in six RtD assigned in the following order: (1) Screening: patients diagnosed with breast, colorectal or cervical cancer through a national screening programme; for patients with cervical cancer, a positive cervix-cytological test taken up to 90 days before diagnosis indicated diagnosis through screening, (2) CPP from primary care: patients referred to a CPP by a healthcare professional in primary care within 90 days before cancer diagnosis, (3) CPP from secondary care: patients referred to a CPP by a healthcare professional in secondary care within 90 days before cancer diagnosis, (4) Unplanned hospital admission: patients admitted acutely to the hospital within 30 days before cancer diagnosis, with no previous routes before this, (5) Planned hospital admission: patients scheduled for a hospital visit for non-cancer reasons within 30 days before the cancer diagnosis, with no previous routes before this. This could be a planned hospitalisation (inpatient) or a planned visit to the hospital specialist clinic (outpatient), (6) Unknown: patients who could not be assigned to any of the other five RtD.

Screening differs from other diagnostic methods as it is offered to an asymptomatic subgroup of the population in certain age groups for specific cancer types (breast, colorectal and cervical cancers). In this study, we examined the screening route separately from all other routes, as done in a previous study [34]. Hence, routes other than screening are referred to as symptomatic routes.

Defining descriptive variables

From the DCR, we obtained information on age at diagnosis (categorised into four groups: 18–49, 50–59, 60–69 and 70 years or older), region of residence (Capital Region, Region Zealand, Region of Southern Denmark, Central Denmark Region and North Denmark Region) and cancer diagnosis (categorised into 12 main groups based on ICD-10).

From Statistics Denmark, we obtained information about sex, cohabitation status and income. Cohabitation status was classified as married/cohabiting or living alone. Income was defined according to the OECD-modified equivalence scale, averaged over the three years preceding the cancer diagnosis and categorised into low (first quintile), middle (second to fourth quintile) and high (fifth quintile) income, following the recommendations from a recent Danish national guideline [40].

From the DNPR, we obtained information on comorbidity for up to 10 years before the cancer diagnosis using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [41], excluding cancer. Scores were categorised into none (CCI score: 0), low (CCI score: 1–2) and high (CCI score: ≥ 3).

Statistical analyses

We provided a descriptive overview of all six RtD by assessing the proportion of each route, including 95% confidence intervals (CI), according to country or region of origin. The screening route was analysed separately from symptomatic routes. For symptomatic routes, multinomial logistic regression models were used to assess the RtD for patients according to migrant group compared with the reference group of Danish origin. The results are presented as relative risk ratios (RRRs) with CPP from primary care as the reference route. We present an unadjusted model and a sex- and age-adjusted model. Adjustments for age were done using restricted cubic splines with four knots according to Harrell’s recommended percentiles [42]. Cluster robust standard errors were used to account for the effect of clustering of patients at practice level. For the screening route, we used a logistic regression model to analyse screening vs. symptomatic routes. This analysis was stratified by cancer type and the specific age groups entitled to screening (23–64 years for cervical cancer, 50–69 years for breast cancer and 50–74 years for colorectal cancer). The analyses of cervical and breast cancer were adjusted for age, while the analysis of colorectal cancer was adjusted for sex and age. From the regression analyses, we estimated the predicted probability of being diagnosed through each RtD, based on marginal means computed with covariates at their observed values. We made a sensitivity analysis to compare the predicted probability of presenting in each RtD between migrant groups and the reference group of Danish origin.

Use of artificial intelligence

No generative artificial intelligence (GAI) models were involved in idea generation, study development, data management, analysis or interpretation of results. To check for spelling and grammar, Grammarly Pro and ChatGPT 4.0 were used, with the authors critically reviewing and revising each suggested alteration.

Results

Descriptive data

The study population comprised 48.5% females, and the median age was 68 years. The patient groups varied notably in their characteristics, including sociodemographic variables (Table 1), with migrant groups being on average younger and having lower incomes than patients of Danish origin. The countries that most migrants originated from in the aggregated groups (regions and subregions) are listed in Table 1; e.g., 85% of migrants from Western Asia originated from Iraq, Lebanon, and Syria. The most common RtD was the CPP from primary care, accounting for 44.5% of all cancer cases, whereas unplanned hospital admission was found in 15.6% of cases. The proportion of patients diagnosed through each route varied across migrant groups (Table 2).

Symptomatic routes to diagnosis according to migrant group

Across all migrant groups and the reference group of Danish origin, the CPP from primary care was the most likely RtD for symptomatic cancers. The CPP from secondary care and the unplanned hospital admission were the second and third most likely routes, with variations across the migrant groups.

When adjusted for sex and age, the predicted probability of being diagnosed with cancer through a CPP from primary care was 48.4% (95% CI 48.1–48.8) for patients of Danish origin, 44.0% (95% CI 40.7–47.3) for migrants from Germany, 39.6% (95% CI 35.1–44.1) for migrants from Türkiye, 40.0% (95% CI 36.1–43.8) for migrants from Western Asia, 43.1% (95% CI 39.2–47.0) for migrants from Southern Asia, 36.1% (95% CI 31.8–40.4) for migrants from Africa, and 42.8% (95% CI 37.8–47.8) for migrants from the Other group. Moreover, migrants from Germany, Türkiye, Western Asia and Africa had the highest predicted probability of being diagnosed through an unplanned hospital admission (Fig. 2a and b, Tables A1-A2 in the Appendix).

Discussion

Main findings

This register-based nationwide cohort study comprised almost 155,000 cancer patients, including 8066 migrants with cancer. We found that the RtD varied across migrant groups. Migrants from Germany, Türkiye, Western Asia, Africa, Southern Asia and the Other group had a lower predicted probability of being diagnosed via the CPP from primary care compared with patients of Danish origin. Additionally, the first four groups also had a higher predicted probability of presenting via unplanned hospital admission. The RRR estimates revealed a higher risk of being diagnosed through an unplanned hospital admission, a planned hospital admission or a CPP from secondary care relative to the CPP from primary care for several migrant groups compared with patients of Danish origin. Additionally, we saw significantly lower odds of being diagnosed with breast cancer via the screening route among migrants.

Methodological considerations

We used national administrative and health registers covering the entire Danish population, acknowledged for their reliability, completeness, and high data validity [28, 36]. This was a major strength in our study. As the algorithm [24] is based on these registers, misclassification of RtD is considered unlikely. We excluded relatively few observations due to missing data.

We included all international migrants in Denmark, resulting in heterogeneity in the migrant population. To accommodate this, we categorised the migrant population into subgroups by country or region of origin, aiming for detailed and granular classification. However, to ensure sufficient power, most migrants were grouped into broader (sub)regions, which may have masked potential differences in our analyses. Still, with 14 migrant groups, we were able to show meaningful differences. When analysing the screening route, we had to combine all migrant groups due to limited numbers in each group, which prevented us from showing differences between groups in this analysis.

Our aim was to describe group-level associations rather than estimate causal effects between the migrant groups and RtD. Therefore, we refrained from using fully adjusted regression models, which is in line with epidemiological guidance [43]. Thus, we did not adjust for variables such as income and tumour stage, as this could have masked important differences between the groups. However, we adjusted for sex and age, since the patient groups varied notably in age and sex distributions, and these factors influence both diagnostic patterns and cancer risk. This approach enhanced the comparability between migrant groups and the reference group while preserving relevant variations.

Although we did not stratify the results by cancer types, Table 1 suggests some variation in the diagnostic spectrum between the reference group of Danish origin and the migrant groups. This could partly be due to differences in age, but it is also in line with previous research showing considerable variation in cancer types across different migrant or ethnic minority groups [44, 45]. Further research should aim to determine whether the most common cancer types among potentially vulnerable migrant groups are linked to a higher likelihood of unplanned hospital admission.

Comparison with other studies

A recent UK study on ethnic inequalities in routes to diagnosis of cancer found that the majority of patients (36%) were diagnosed through a fast-track referral from the GP (known as the urgent suspected cancer route, the former Two-Week-Wait) or an elective GP referral (23%) [46]. This is similar to the finding in our study, where the majority of all cancer patients (45%) were diagnosed through a CPP from primary care. Martins et al. [46] focused on ethnic groups based on ethnicity information from primary care and hospital records, as opposed to migrant background. Therefore, the results are not directly comparable to ours. However, they found that Black and Other patients were less likely to be diagnosed via screening (5% for both) compared with White (8%), Asian (11%) and Mixed (9%) patients. This is partly in line with our findings, as we saw lower odds of being diagnosed with breast cancer via the screening route in the migrant group compared with patients of Danish origin. Unlike our findings, the UK study found that diagnosis through an emergency route was more common among White (19%) and Other (28%) patients than among Asian (14%), Black (15%) and Mixed (16%) patients.

A US study from 2024 on emergency diagnosis among older adults found that the proportion of emergency department involvement within 30 days before a cancer diagnosis was highest for non-Hispanic Black patients (28%), Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander patients (25%), and American Indian or Alaska Native patients (26%), whereas it was lowest among non-Hispanic White patients (23%) [47]. Another US study from 2022 showed that African Americans made up a larger share of patients diagnosed following an emergency visit within six months before their cancer diagnosis than among those not diagnosed after an emergency visit (12% versus 7%, CI for difference of 5.0% = 2% to 6%) [48]. These findings should be understood in the context of the American healthcare system, which differs markedly from the Danish model. Without a gatekeeping function in primary healthcare, emergency diagnosis may be the only available route for some patients. However, despite universal health coverage and equal access to healthcare in Denmark [28], our study still showed variations in RtD across migrant groups, with several migrant groups having a higher risk of being diagnosed via an unplanned hospital admission relative to a CPP from primary care compared with patients of Danish origin.

A study from New Zealand in 2023 found ethnic disparities in the rate of emergency hospitalisation within 30 days before lung cancer diagnosis, with the Pacific population (62%) and the Māori population (54%) having higher rates than the majority European population (47%) (age-standardised proportions) [49]. Although not directly comparable to our study regarding ethnic groups and cancer type(s), the finding of ethnic disparities in emergency presentation of lung cancer corroborates our results, where several migrant groups had a higher likelihood of being diagnosed through an unplanned hospital admission across all investigated cancer types compared with patients of Danish origin.

Danish and Nordic studies have shown low participation rates in cancer screening programmes among migrants [11, 50–52], which likely results in fewer cancer diagnoses being detected through screening compared to other diagnostic routes. In our study, migrants had reduced odds of being diagnosed with breast, colorectal and cervical cancer through screening compared with patients of Danish origin, although these figures were not statistically significant for colorectal and cervical cancer. However, we analysed migrants as a combined group, and future studies should seek to explore this in more detail.

We know from a previous Danish study that age is associated with the RtD [34]. The authors found that both the oldest and youngest patients were less likely than other age groups to be diagnosed through a CPP from primary care, and that older patients were more likely than younger patients to be diagnosed with cancer following an unplanned hospital admission. The associations between age and RtD were generally similar for men and women. In our study, most migrant groups had a lower median age than the Danish reference group. Once adjusted for sex and age, we saw that several migrant groups had a higher likelihood of being diagnosed through an unplanned hospital admission compared with the Danish reference group. This larger variation after adjustment was expected, since younger age is associated with a lower risk of unplanned hospital admission [34].

The finding that migrants from Germany had a higher predicted probability for diagnosis through an unplanned hospital admission and a lower predicted probability for diagnosis through a CPP from primary care compared with patients of Danish origin was surprising. However, migrants from Germany had a higher median age than all other groups, and the differences narrowed after adjusting for sex and age. Thus, the results could partly be explained by older age and, consequently, a higher risk of unplanned hospital admission among migrants from Germany.

Compared with patients of Danish origin, migrants from Türkiye, Western Asia, and Africa had the highest likelihood of being diagnosed through an unplanned hospital admission relative to a CPP from primary care. Migrants from Western Asia and Africa are primarily refugees from different time periods, whereas migrants from Türkiye constitute a large group of labour migrants in Denmark. Thus, the potentially most vulnerable migrant groups have different geographical origins and distinct migration histories. However, research indicates that ethnic minority groups in general have lower awareness of cancer symptoms and experience difficulties seeking medical help [5], which may partly explain the observed diagnostic pattern. Further, social inequality in cancer outcomes is well-known [53, 54] and should also be considered in relation to our findings.

Generalisability

Due to differences in healthcare organisations and welfare systems across countries, the findings of our study may not be directly applicable to other countries. Further, the recording of ethnicity data varies widely across Europe [55], and migration patterns differ globally and regionally. However, despite these differences, our results supplement studies from other healthcare systems, indicating a universal issue; many migrant groups have a higher risk of being diagnosed with cancer through unplanned hospital admissions. Thus, adapting the description of the RtD and migrant/ethnic groups to other healthcare systems could still be meaningful and relevant.

Conclusion

This nationwide cohort study found that RtD varied across migrant groups. Notably, migrants from Germany, Türkiye, Western Asia and Africa were less likely to be diagnosed via a CPP from primary care and more likely to be diagnosed via an unplanned hospital admission compared with patients of Danish origin. For all migrant groups combined, we also found significantly lower odds of being diagnosed via screening for breast cancer. The findings confirm our hypotheses, as we expected the RtD to vary across migrant groups and migrants to be less likely to be diagnosed through a CPP initiated in primary care compared with patients of Danish origin. The findings indicate that migrants, to some extent, are diagnosed via other routes than patients of Danish origin, which may impact the cancer prognosis. The increased likelihood of being diagnosed through unplanned hospital admission warrants further investigation, as well as future research could explore the link between RtD and prognosis for migrant groups in potentially vulnerable situations.
