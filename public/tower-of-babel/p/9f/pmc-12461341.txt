Biobased Itaconate Acrylic Pressure-Sensitive Adhesives via Emulsion Polymerization: Sustainable Design for High Peel Resistance
Authors: Wang Xue, Xin Zhang, Chaoying Sun, Chenlong Wei, Liqun Zhang, Hui Yang, Runguo Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pressure-sensitive adhesives (PSAs) are widely used materials, yet most of their raw materials are currently derived from nonrenewable petroleum resources. The PSA industry faces challenges including the depletion of petroleum resources, high solvent emissions, and carbon-intensive manufacturing processes. To address these issues, a series of poly­(dibutyl itaconate-co-butyl acrylate-co-acrylic acid-co-glycidyl methacrylate) (PDBAG) copolymers were synthesized via high-temperature semicontinuous emulsion polymerization, using fully biobased dibutyl itaconate (DBI) as a monomer. The structures and properties of PDBAG with varying DBI and glycidyl methacrylate (GMA) contents were systematically characterized. An optimal balance between adhesion and cohesion was achieved at a GMA content of 0.5 wt %. Remarkably, increasing DBI content from 30 wt % to 50 wt % enhances the high-frequency shear loss modulus (G″) from 55.5 to 87.3 kPa (measured at T = 40 °C, ε = 1%) and correspondingly improves the 180° peel strength with SUS from 10.7 N/25 mm to 16.5 N/25 mm. This enhancement mechanism is attributed to increased energy dissipation within the PSA network during peel deformation. They surpass commercial acrylic PSAs in both peel strength and shear holding power. Biobased PSA partially replaces petroleum-based acrylics in the polymer backbone and features simpler, more eco-friendly processing while maintaining outstanding adhesion. This establishes a sustainable pathway for next-generation adhesive development.

Introduction

Pressure-sensitive adhesives (PSAs) with long-lasting adhesion can effectively wet material surfaces and bond firmly under light pressure. By precisely regulating the linear viscoelastic properties, PSAs can ensure optimal bonding performance during application while achieving controlled peel forces without leaving residual adhesive. These adhesives find extensive applications in packaging, labeling, protective films, and medical device manufacturing. − The global PSA market has experienced sustained growth in recent years, with projections indicating it will reach USD 17 billion by 2030. Among various component categories of PSAs, acrylic-based PSAs currently account for the largest market share due to their excellent adhesion performance, high transparency, antiaging properties, ease of component control, and low cost. , Moreover, the PSA manufacturing industry faces environmental requirements to reduce solvent emissions. Emulsion-type acrylic PSAs, with a simple process and the absence of solvent emissions, have increased their market share. , However, conventional industrial monomers for polymerization, including butyl acrylate (BA), 2-ethylhexyl acrylate (2-EHA), methyl methacrylate (MMA), and acrylic acid (AA), are predominantly derived from nonrenewable petroleum resources. This critical dependency drives emerging research efforts toward developing high-performance biobased PSA alternatives with industrial viability. Such sustainable materials demonstrate reduced carbon footprints across their lifecycle, thereby advancing global carbon neutrality objectives outlined in the EU Green Deal and the Paris Agreement’s mandate to limit global warming to below 1.5 °C preindustrial levels. − Meanwhile, the emergence of new application scenarios, such as those in the fields of electronic display protection, medical consumables directly applied to the human body, and adhesive tapes for temporary auxiliary fixation, has put forward higher requirements for the peel resistance and holding power of PSAs. −

The synthesis characteristics of sustainable PSAs lie in the monomer sources and polymerization process routes. In emulsion polymerization, the process does not require specialized steps for water and oxygen removal. This system not only features low cost, operational simplicity, and stable and controllable reaction conditions, but also demonstrates significant environmental advantages. , Notably, water-based PSAs exhibit dual ecological benefits: they generate reduced air pollution during manufacturing processes while simultaneously minimizing potential harm to operators and application environments during practical use.

According to previous research results, 2-octyl acrylate (2OA) and tetrahydrogeranyl acrylate (THGA) can be used to replace petroleum-based acrylate monomers in a large proportion. − The biobased components of these monomers are the long-chain monohydric alcohol parts in the acrylate. Badia et al. prepared emulsion-type acrylic PSAs with high biobased content using 2-OA as the main monomer. The 180° peel strength was 6.6 N/25 mm, and the holding power was 15047 min. Droesbeke et al. also used emulsion polymerization with THGA as the main monomer to prepare acrylic PSAs. The 180° peel strength was 7.7 N/25 mm, and the holding power exceeded 10000 min. Such biobased acrylic PSAs can be equivalent to commercial acrylic PSAs in terms of performance and process. However, this approach cannot substitute the acrylic structure in the polymer main chain.

In addition, bulk biobased chemicals such as vegetable oils, − carbohydrates, lignin, cardanol, and vanillin have made new progress in the preparation of PSAs. The properties reported in the literature are shown in Table S1. Marinelli et al. prepared a PSA by directly curing epoxidized soybean oil (ESO) with a long-chain dibasic acid synthesized from the reaction of cardanol-based epoxy resin and sebacic acid (SA). The resulting PSA exhibited a 180°peel strength of 10 N/25 mm. Park et al. prepared a polyester PSA via chain extension of hydroxyl-terminated polyamide 11­(castor oil-derived) oligomers and amine-terminated dimer acid (soybean oil-derived) oligomers. This PSA showed an 180° peel strength of 8.8 N/25 mm and a holding power exceeding 5000 min. Paul et al. blended epoxidized cardanol (ECA) with sebacic acid (SBA) to form ECA-SBA copolyester prepolymers, then tuned the curing degree with vanillin epoxide (VE) to prepare a PSA with a 90° peel strength of 4.82 N/25 mm. Both Sivasankarapillai et al. and Gendron et al. prepared PSAs by blending polycarboxylate polyether (PCE) with lignin, leveraging specific interactions between functional groups in PCE and lignin OH groups. , This route offers a more industrially scalable process but limits the lignin content generally below 30 wt %. , Similarly, the content of carbohydrate monomers (e.g., isosorbide) in PSA is also typically less than 30 wt %. −

Itaconic acid (ITA) is a biobased monomer that is expected to replace acrylic acid (AA). It is an α,β-unsaturated dicarboxylic acid. ITA is produced in large quantities by the fermentation of Aspergillus terreus using carbohydrates derived from biomass such as straw and corn, and this has become the mainstream production process. , ITA has been listed as one of the most valuable biobased monomers by the U.S. Department of Energy. Dibutyl itaconate (DBI) can be synthesized from biobased ITA and biobased n-butanol. Biobased n-butanol can be produced via the acetone-butanol-ethanol (ABE) fermentation process. DBI is used in the fields of metal coatings and water-based inks, and its oligomers can also be used as plasticizers. Wang et al. prepared high-performance rubber composites using DBI and butadiene through the low-temperature redox emulsion polymerization process and established a thousand-ton production line. The value of DBI in the biobased materials field is driving the growth of its production.

DBI, as a biobased monomer, has an ester group density comparable to that of BA and slightly higher than that of 2-EHA (calculated based on the proportion of ester group in the molecular mass). The ester group has a certain polarity, which is beneficial for enhancing the interfacial interaction and increasing the adhesive strength. Therefore, DBI has potential in constructing biobased adhesives. The self-cross-linking monomer glycidyl methacrylate (GMA) effectively increased the cohesion of PDBAG through the functional-group reaction between the epoxide group and carboxyl groups of AA. By employing a self-cross-linking reaction system, this approach achieves one-step polymerization and eliminates the need for additional curing agents during adhesive application. This innovation further enhances the simplicity of emulsion preparation processes while reinforcing its advantages in low toxicity and environmental friendliness.

In this work, we synthesized poly­(dibutyl itaconate-co-butyl acrylate-co-acrylic acid-co-glycidyl methacrylate) (PDBAG) via high-temperature emulsion polymerization at 78 °C, an industrially viable preparation process. Biobased DBI partially replaced petroleum-based acrylic structures. Incorporating the self-cross-linking monomer GMA effectively enhanced the cohesion of PDBAG. For PSAs achieving an optimal balance between adhesion and cohesion, the effect of DBI content on structure and adhesion performance was systematically investigated. Experimental Section

Materials

DBI (96%) was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Company as a biobased monomer. BA (98%), GMA (98%), and AA (99%) were acquired from J&K Scientific Company for use as synthetic monomers. Ammonium allyloxynonylphenol polyoxyethylene (10) ether sulfate (DNS-86, 90%) was obtained from Macklin Biochemical Company as a polymerizable emulsifier. Ammonium persulfate (APS, 99.5%) was obtained from Aladdin Industrial Corporation (Shanghai, China) and used as an initiator. Other chemicals involved in the polymerization reaction are of industrial grade and commercially available. Polyethylene terephthalate (PET) corona-treated films were purchased from Shenzhen Lexin Plastic Industry Co., Ltd., and used as the substrate for PSA tapes. The commercially available acrylic latexes S0105 and H0405 were purchased from Shenzhen Jitian Chemical Co., Ltd. BA was filtered through 500-mesh neutral alumina to remove the polymerization inhibitor, and no further purification was carried out on other raw materials.

Synthesis of the PDBAG Latexes

According to the formulation in Table S2, the emulsifier, buffer, and majority of deionized water (DW) were added to a four-neck round-bottom flask. After slow stirring, all monomers were added to the flask and stirred at 350 rpm for 50 min to prepare the pre-emulsion. The feed amounts of monomers for different formulations are listed in Table . In a separate flask, 10 wt % of the pre-emulsion and the rinsing solution from the pre-emulsion flask (rinsed with 20 mL DW) were added and thoroughly mixed at 230 rpm. The flask was purged with nitrogen for 5 min to remove residual air. When the water bath temperature reached 78 °C, the initiator solution (0.1 g APS dissolved in 5 mL DW) was introduced. Once the emulsion developed a blue tint, the remaining pre-emulsion and a second initiator solution (0.4 g APS dissolved in 20 mL DW) were slowly added dropwise into the flask using two constant-pressure separatory funnels over 2 h. After the complete addition of the pre-emulsion, the reaction was continued for 1.5 h. The emulsion was cooled to 40 °C, and aqueous ammonia was added dropwise to adjust the pH to 7.5–8.0. Finally, the mixture was filtered to obtain PDBAG latexes. The polymerization equation is illustrated in Scheme .

Preparation of PDBAG Films

An appropriate amount of latex was taken and spread into a uniform film on a 3 mm-thick polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) mold. The film was dried in an oven at 105 °C for 2 h and then further dried at room temperature for 48 h. The resulting PDBAG film is then ready for subsequent testing.

Preparation of PDBAG PSA Tapes

The PDBAG latex was uniformly coated onto a 50-μm-thick PET film using an ASP-300 wire-bar coater (Aosipai Mechanical Technology Co., Ltd., China). The PSA tapes were then cured in an oven at 105 °C for 8 min. After covering with release paper, the PSA tapes were conditioned at 23 ± 1 °C and 50% ± 5% relative humidity (RH) for 24 h, resulting in PSA tapes with a dry film thickness of 30 ± 2 μm.

Characterization of PDBAG Films

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) of PDBAG films was recorded on a Tensor-37 spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) in attenuated total reflection (ATR) mode (400–4000 cm–1, resolution: 4 cm–1).

Near-Infrared Spectroscopy was recorded on a Ta/Na 100 Time-Resolved Absorption Spectrometer (Ruichuang, China) in attenuated total reflection (ATR) mode (315–2600 nm).

Raman spectroscopy tests were conducted on some samples using a DXR2Xi Raman spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US). The incident laser excitation was provided by an argon ion laser source operating at a 785 nm diode laser.

Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectrum of PDBAG was recorded on an AV600 NMR spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) at a frequency of 600 MHz. PDBAG films were first dissolved in deuterated chloroform (CDCl3) and then filtered to remove the gel.

The number-average molecular weight (M n) and weight-average molecular weight (M w) of PDBAG were measured by gel permeation chromatography (GPC). A Waters 1515 GPC System (Waters Breeze, USA) equipped with three columns (Styragel HT3_HT5_HT6E) and a Waters 2410 refractive index detector. The eluent was HPLC-grade tetrahydrofuran (THF) delivered at 1.0 mL/min. A Polystyrene standard was used as calibration. The polymer solution needed to be filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane.

To determine the glass transition temperature (Tg) of PDBAG, the films were placed in a differential scanning calorimetry DSC 3+ instrument (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). Under a nitrogen atmosphere with a flow rate of 50 mL/min, the samples were heated and cooled in the crucible at a rate of 10 °C/min. The samples were first heated from 20 to 60 °C and held for 5 min, then cooled from 60 °C to −60 °C and held for 5 min, and finally heated from −60 to 40 °C. The Tg was calculated based on the final heating process.

Thermal stability was evaluated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). The testing instrument was the TGA/DSC 3 ± thermogravimetric analyzer (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). Samples of 10 mg were heated from 30 to 550 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere with a flow rate of 50 mL/min.

The gel content (Gel) of the PDBAG film was tested by the following method. Approximately 200 mg of the film was dissolved in 100 mL of THF at room temperature for 48 h. The weight of the film was recorded as m1. Then, the insoluble substances and the solution were filtered through a 200-mesh copper screen. The gel was dried at room temperature for 6 h, and its dry weight was measured and recorded as m2. Gel is calculated by Formula . 1 Gel=m2m1×100%

To characterize the degree of cross-linking of the insoluble part of the PDBAG film, the swelling coefficient was used for testing. About 500 mg of the film was dissolved in 100 mL of xylene solvent at 23 °C for 48 h. Then, the insoluble substances and the solution were filtered through a 200-mesh copper screen. Immediately, the residual surface was wiped with nonwoven fabric. The wet weight was weighed and recorded as m3, and then after drying, the dry weight was weighed and recorded as m4. The swelling coefficient (Q) is calculated using formula (2). 2 Q=m3−m4m4×ρ0 where ρ 0 represents the density of xylene, which is 0.86 g/cm3.

The dynamic viscoelastic frequency spectrum of PDBAG films was tested using a RPA-2000 rubber process analyzer (Alpha, USA). Approximately 5 g of dry film was used for the test. The test conditions were a shear strain of 1%, a temperature of 40 °C, and a shear frequency ranging from 0.01 to 50 Hz.

Adhesion Properties Testing of PDBAG PSA Tapes

The holding power of the PSA was tested according to the standard of GB/T 4851-1998. Two 304 stainless steel plates (40 mm × 60 mm) were cleaned with ethanol. A PSA tape was cut into strips that were 25 mm wide and bonded between the plates with a bonding area of 25 mm × 25 mm. A 2 kg rubber roller was rolled over the assembly three times to eliminate air bubbles. After 20 min of equilibration, the bonded plates were mounted on a WKC-6S Temperature-Controlled Adhesive Tester (Saicheng Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., China) with a 1 kg load applied to the lower plate. Debonding time was recorded until 168 h, and adhesive residue on the upper plate was observed. Three replicates per formulation were tested, with median values reported.

The 180° peel strength of the PSA was tested according to the standard of GB/T 2792–2014. PSA tapes (24 mm × 170 mm) were bonded to ethanol-cleaned 304 stainless steel plates (SUS) or other materials (such as Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC), Polyethylene (PE), Polypropylene (PP)) with a 25 mm × 150 mm bonding area. After rolling with a 2 kg rubber roller and 20 min of equilibration, peeling tests were conducted at 300 mm/min using an HCL-50 Peel Tester (Beacon Auto Co., Ltd., China) with 180° geometry. Peel strength was calculated from force–displacement curves, and adhesive residue was assessed. Three replicates per formulation were tested, with median values reported.

The tack of the PSA was tested according to the standard GB/T 4852–2002, using the J. Dow method with a CZY-G tack tester (Saicheng Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., China). PSA tapes (100 mm length) were placed on a 30° inclined plane covered with a PET thin plate. Stainless steel balls of increasing diameters were released from a 100 mm acceleration distance above the tape. The largest ball retained on the adhesive surface defined the tack value.

The lap-shear tests were performed by bonding PSA tapes to different materials. In accordance with the GB/T 33334-2016 standard, the bonding area was set as 12.5 × 25 mm. After rolling with a 2 kg rubber roller and allowing 20 min for equilibration, the tensile tests were carried out at a speed of 5 mm/min using an HCL-50 Peel Tester (manufactured by Beacon Auto Co., Ltd., China). For each formulation, three replicates were tested, and the median values were reported.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of PDBAG Latexes

We successfully synthesized a series of PDBAG latexes with different DBI and GMA contents via a high-temperature semicontinuous emulsion polymerization. The feed amount of the five formulations is shown in Table . Each formulation underwent systematic characterization and testing. Taking formulation PDBAG-50–0.5G as an example: it contains approximately 50 wt % DBI and 0.5 wt % GMA, and is designated as sample P5. P1, P2, and P3 have different GMA contents, while P2, P4, and P5 have different DBI contents. As shown in Table , the yields of PDBAG latexes (formulations P1–P5) range from 96.2% to 98.6%. All latexes exhibit excellent calcium ion, mechanical, and storage stability. The viscosity of the PDBAG latexes, ranging from 94–116 mPa·s, facilitates uniform coating with a wire-bar coater.

Before synthesizing this series of formulations, we conducted experiments using 1–5 wt % (relative to the total mass of DBI and BA) of the functional monomer AA based on the ratio of DBI and BA monomers in formulation P1. The effects of the AA dosage on the latex viscosity and the adhesive properties of the PSA are presented in Tables S3 and S4. AA, as a polymerization monomer, introduces carboxylic acid functional groups into the polymer. After neutralization with aqueous ammonia, the latex underwent an alkali-thickening phenomenon. The more AA is used, the higher the viscosity of the thickened latex. We found that when the AA content is 1 wt %, the latex has strong fluidity, making coating slightly difficult. Since the Tg of poly­(acrylic acid) homopolymer is 106 °C, to prevent excessive hardening of the polymer segments, the dosage of AA was restricted to 2 wt %. , However, its holding power is only 4.3 h. To improve the holding power of the PSA, a small amount of GMA was introduced in subsequent formulations.

Characterization of PDBAG Films

The characteristics of the chemical groups of PDBAG films were analyzed by FTIR using ATR mode. Figure a shows the FTIR spectra of three main monomers BA, DBI, AA and the self-cross-linking monomer GMA. Their double-bond characteristic absorption peaks appear at 1640 cm–1. In addition, the spectrum of AA monomer shows a broad absorption peak in the range of 2800–3300 cm–1, which corresponds to the stretching vibrations of both associated and unassociated hydroxyl groups. As for GMA monomer, a small characteristic peak of the epoxy group appears at 923 cm–1. As shown in Figure b, the FTIR spectra of the five different formulations (P1–P5) are very similar, due to the similarity of the characteristic peaks of the main monomer BA and DBI, including their homopolymers. However, it can still be used to qualitatively verify the structure of the polymer and confirm that the PSA films have no monomer residue. The absorption peaks at 2961 cm–1 and 2873 cm–1 correspond to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the methyl group, respectively. The absorption peak at 2930 cm–1 is attributed to the asymmetric stretching vibration of the methylene group. Characteristic absorption peaks appear at 1731 cm–1 and 1168 cm–1, which are attributed to the CO stretching vibration and the C–O–C asymmetric stretching vibration, respectively. , Compared with Figure a, the characteristic double-bond peaks near 1640 cm–1 in all formulations have disappeared. Formulation P2 was subjected to Raman spectroscopy (see Figure S1). The polymer films revealed no characteristic scattering peaks for CC bonds within the 1500–1700 cm–1 range. Given Raman spectroscopy’s heightened sensitivity for detecting nonpolar CC bonds, these results indicate the absence of residual monomer in the product films.

In order to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the network structure of PDBAG, we employed tetrahydrofuran (THF) to dissolve samples, aiming to accurately measure the gel content. The polymer fraction that can be dissolved in THF was subjected to gel permeation chromatography (GPC) for the determination of molecular weight. Next, the film was fully swollen in xylene to test the swelling coefficient (Q), which was used to characterize the cross-linking density. After the polymer was swollen in xylene, it could still maintain a jelly like shape, which was convenient for the mass measurement. As shown in Table , the M w of formulations­(P1–P5)­ranges from 1.63 × 105–2.56 × 105 g/mol, and the gel content varies from 36%–78%. The Q of the polymer in xylene ranges from 21–51 cm3/g, which means that the cross-linking density of the PSA is much lower than that of vulcanized rubber and is similar to that of medium-strength hydrogels. Among them, the cross-linking network is mainly composed of chemical cross-linking bonds. At a GMA content of 0.5 wt %, when the dosage of DBI increases from 30 wt % to 50 wt % (formulations P2, P4, and P5), the gel content decreases from 68% to 58%, and the M w decreases from 2.56 × 105 to 1.63 × 105 g/mol. Due to its large steric hindrance, the DBI propagation rate coefficients are 2 orders of magnitude lower than those of acrylates, leading to two other pronounced effects. Free radicals are more likely to transfer to monomers, and the depropagation effect of DBI-terminated free radicals is non-negligible when the polymerization reaction is conducted at 78 °C. , Both of these factors lead to a decrease in molecular weight and gel content. In contrast, BA homopolymer exhibits high gel content and high molecular weight. , In the case of 30 wt % DBI (formulations P1–P3), as the dosage of the self-cross-linking monomer GMA increases from 0 to 1 wt %, the gel content of PDBAG increases from 36% to 78%. Meanwhile, the Q decreases from 51 to 21 cm3/g, indicating that both the cross-linking fraction and the cross-linking density of PDBAG increase. This verifies that a functional group reaction occurred between GMA and AA, which increases the cross-linking degree of the polymer.

The glass transition temperature (Tg) is an important physical quantity that characterizes the flexibility of polymer segments. A large number of experiments have shown that there is a correlation between the tack of PSAs and their Tg. As shown in Figure , the DSC curves of formulations (P1–P5) exhibit no melting or crystallization peaks within the temperature range of −60 to 20 °C. The Tg of formulations P1–P3 range from −32.8 °C to −33.2 °C. Although the addition of a small amount of GMA increases the degree of cross-linking in PDBAG, it has little effect on the movement of molecular chains. When the DBI content increases from 30 wt % to 50 wt % (formulations P2, P4, P5), the Tg of PDBAG increases from −33.2 °C to −26.4 °C. The two asymmetric side groups reduce the flexibility of the molecular chain and thus increase the Tg of PDBAG. In general, the increase in the Tg of the PSA reduces the ability of polymers to quickly wet the adherend. Therefore, DBI does not act as an acrylate soft monomer. DBI can reduce the gel and molecular weight, it does not act as a hard monomer to enhance the cohesion. This is the key reason to limit further increases in the biobased DBI content.

This is similar to the thermogravimetric analysis results of Hupp et al. on petroleum-based acrylic PSAs.

Rheological Analysis of PDBAG Films

The dynamic viscoelastic properties of PDBAG films were characterized. The storage modulus and loss modulus at specific frequencies are highly correlated with the adhesion and cohesion of PSAs. Therefore, testing the viscoelastic properties of polymers is an important approach for characterizing their microstructure and macroscopic adhesion properties. Based on the storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″), loss factor (tan δ) curves in Figure a–c, a comprehensive analysis was conducted according to different frequency regions.

Dynamic viscoelastic frequency spectra of PDBAG films with different DBI and GMA contents (T = 40 °C, ε = 1%): (a) storage modulus­(G′)-shear frequency curves, (b) loss modulus­(G″)-shear frequency curves, and (c) loss factor (tan δ)-shear frequency curves. (d)­The modulus of PDBAG films falls within the region of E.P. Chang’s viscoelastic window.

In the low-frequency shear region (f < 1 Hz), G′ is considered more relevant to the bonding process of PSAs. , As shown in Figure a, within the frequency range of 0.2 Hz–1 Hz, the curves of P1, P4, and P5 are similar, with only minor differences. However, the curves of P2 and P3 are significantly higher than those of the other three formulations. Although DBI reduces the M w and gel content of PDBAG, the incorporation of 0.5 wt % GMA effectively enhances the G′ of the PDBAG, ensuring sufficient cohesion. When comparing formulations (P1–P3), the G′ of P3 at 0.2 Hz increased by 107% compared to that of P1, indicating that GMA has increased the cross-linking density of PDBAG. When comparing formulations (P2, P4, and P5), the G′’of P5 at 0.2 Hz decreases by 37% compared to P2, suggesting that DBI reduces the bulk strength of PDBAG. It is worth noting that although P5 has a higher gel content and a lower swelling coefficient than P1, its G′(0.2 Hz) is lower than that of P1. This indicates that the molecular weight and physical entanglements of P1 have a more significant contribution to G′(0.2 Hz). One reason is that at low shear frequencies, the lower molecular weight and fewer physical entanglements in P5 result in a lower G′. Another possibility is that the physical entanglements in P1 are largely disrupted by the solvent, whereas they remain unaffected during dynamic viscoelastic testing. Additionally, this observation indirectly confirms that a swelling coefficient between 30 cm3/g and 50 cm3/g corresponds to a low cross-linking density. Regarding the loss modulus, as shown in Figure b, within the frequency range of 0.2 Hz–1 Hz, the G″ values of PDBAG for the five formulations show relatively small differences.

In the high-frequency shear region (f > 10 Hz), G″ is considered to be more related to the debonding process of PSAs, exhibiting more distinctive performance. − As shown in Figure b, within the frequency range of 10 Hz–50 Hz, the G″ of PDBAG with a high DBI content (P4 and P5) surpasses those of P1–P3. Notably, although the degree of cross-linking increases from P1 to P3, the variations in G″ among these formulations are relatively small. Specifically, at 50 Hz, the G″ of P5 (containing 0.5 wt % GMA) is 57% higher than that of P3. Additionally, at 50 Hz, the G′ of P5 is lower only than that of P3, which has a high cross-linking density. An increase in the DBI content significantly enhances the high-frequency loss modulus, indicating a greater dissipation of viscous energy. Increasing the DBI content directly leads to a reduction in both molecular weight and gel content. Consequently, the number of free ends within the polymer increases, and the molecular chains become more prone to slippage. Additionally, polymers with high DBI content contain oligomers that may exert a crucial internal plasticization function.

As shown in Figure c, in the frequency range of 0.2 Hz–50 Hz, the loss factor spectrum reveals that P5 has the largest loss factor, with values ranging from 0.6 to 0.8. The loss factor increases with increasing DBI content (P3–P5), whereas it decreases with increasing GMA content (P1–P3). Regarding the component content, the variation trend of the loss factor is similar to that of the G″. Formulation P3, which has the highest degree of cross-linking, has a loss factor of less than 0.3 in the low-frequency region. However, it increases at the fastest rate with rising frequency and behaves more like a solid. This phenomenon aligns with the principle that higher cross-linking density leads to lower viscous losses.

Furthermore, we preliminarily evaluated the adhesion properties of PDBAG PSAs using Chang’s viscoelastic windows for PSA1 in conjunction with the Dahlquist criterion. As shown in Figure d, plots of G′ and G″ at low and high frequencies for formulations P1–P5 reveal their positions predominantly within the central region of Chang’s PSA viscoelastic window, occupying portions of all four quadrantsconsistent with General Purpose PSA classification. All five formulations exhibit moderate modulus and dissipation characteristics. Notably, the storage modulus (G′) values of these PSAs are consistently below the empirical threshold of 300 kPa specified by the Dahlquist criterion.

Adhesion Properties of PDBAG PSAs

We conducted holding power (static shear) tests and 180° peel strength measurements using stainless steel (SUS) substrates. Tack performance was evaluated via the J. Dow method, which characterizes a PSA’s instantaneous bonding capability. To comprehensively assess the adhesion strength of biobased PDBAG PSAs on substrates with varying polarity, peel strength tests and lap-shear strength measurements were performed on both polar PVC and nonpolar materials (PE, PP). Commercial acrylic PSAs were introduced as benchmarks: high-peel-strength PSA-S0105 and general-purpose PSA-H0405 for comparative adhesion performance analysis.

Peel Resistance of PDBAG PSAs

Figure shows the 180° peel strength of PDBAG PSAs on substrates of different polarities. For SUS substrates, the 180° peel strength of formulations P1–P5 ranges from 8.9 to 16.5 N/25 mm. The peel strength of P5 (50 wt % DBI) reaches 16.5 N/25 mm, representing a 54% increase over P2. Wang et al. studied the ratio of the 16 Hz loss modulus to the 0.016 Hz storage modulus. A high ratio indicates that low-frequency storage modulus promotes wetting and bonding, while high-frequency loss modulus facilitates fibril formation and enhances peeling energy dissipation. , The DBI component increases the high-frequency loss modulus, enabling greater energy dissipation during rapid peeling, with G″ (50 Hz)/G′(0.2 Hz) increasing from 2.4 to 5.9. The ratio of G″ (50 Hz)/G′(0.2 Hz) for PDBAG PSAs is listed in Table S7. Conversely, the peel force decreases from 11.2 to 8.7 N/25 mm for P1–P3 due to increased degree of cross-linking, evidenced by a higher gel content (increasing from 36% to 78%) and a lower swelling coefficient (decreasing from 51 to 21 cm3/g). All P1–P5 formulations exhibit higher peel strength on SUS than commercial general-purpose PSA-H0405, with P5 approaching the performance of high-peel commercial PSA-S0105.

For the polar plastic PVC, the 180° peel strength of PDBAGs ranges from 17.8 to 35.5 N/25 mm, with cohesive failure consistently occurring within the adhesive. The significantly higher peel strength compared to two commercial PSAs indicates strong interfacial bonding between the PSA and polar plastics. P3 achieves the maximum peel strength with PVC due to its greatest intrinsic cohesive strength.

For nonpolar materials PE and PP, formulations P1–P3 achieve peel strength between 2.7 and 5.2 N/25 mm, still slightly higher than those of PSA-H0405 but inferior to PSA-S0105. However, comparing in P2, P4 and P5, peel strength on PE or PP decreases rapidly with increasing DBI content. This is primarily due to both reduced wettability on nonpolar materials and the inability of carboxyl groups and ester groups in the PSA to form strong interactions with C–H bonds. These results reveals limitations in correlating dynamic viscoelastic analysis with peel strength. When interfacial adhesion is weak, interfacial failure governs peel behavior, and the energy dissipation characteristics of the PSA bulk material cannot be utilized.

Shear Creep Resistance of PDBAG PSAs

On the other hand, when a PSA film bonds to a substrate via noncovalent interactions (e.g., van der Waals forces), prolonged external forces (e.g., gravity) can induce shear failure within the adhesive film or at the interface. As shown in Figure , the holding power for formulations P1–P5 ranges from 5.3 to 168 h. Notably, P1 has the lowest holding power (5.3 h). The M w (2.09 × 105 g/mol) and gel content (36%) of P1 are insufficient to provide adequate cohesion. It should be pointed out that this level of shear resistance remains practical for applications such as label PSAs. The incorporation of GMA initiates a self-cross-linking reaction, enhancing the G′ of PDBAG within 0.2 Hz–1 Hz. This is crucial for overall performance improvement, contributing to the bulk strength of the adhesive layer. When formulation P3 contains 1 wt % GMA, its holding power exceeds 168 h. Upon examining the steel plate surface after shear failure, the failure mode was found to be cohesive, with no debonding between the adhesive layer and the PET substrate. Instead, a portion of the adhesive layer remains bonded to the steel plate, demonstrating that progressively increasing the cross-linking degree continues to strengthen the cohesion of the PSA. Compared to P2 and P4, formulation P5 exhibits only a 50% reduction in holding power relative to P2, indicating similar cohesion levels. In contrast, at 0.2 Hz among formulations P1–P3, the G’′ of P3 increases by 107% relative to that of P1, but its holding power increases by more than 1915%. This demonstrates that cross-linking density (indicated by gel content and swelling coefficient) plays a decisive role in the shear creep resistance of PSAs. The higher holding power of P5 compared to P1 verifies the superior efficacy of chemical cross-linking. Conversely, physical entanglements are more prone to creep and chain slippage under prolonged shear. In conclusion, using GMA (0.5 wt %) effectively endows PDBAG with necessary cohesion, improving cohesive strength and shear resistance. P2–P5 show 2.6–8.8 times greater holding power than commercial PSA-H0405, while PSA-S0105 performs worse (only 2.3 h).

Holing power of PDBAG PSA tapes with SUS.

Furthermore, we evaluated the lap-shear strength of PSA tapes on various substrates. For different PSA formulations, the lap-shear strength primarily depended on cohesive strength. As shown in Figure , the lap-shear strength of P1–P5 range from 135–246 kPa on SUS substrates. The content of DBI and GMA exhibit consistent effects on both lap-shear strength and holding power, despite order-of-magnitude differences in static shear failure times. The influence of substrate materials on lap-shear strength varies significantly across adhesive formulations, exhibiting far less consistent patterns than those observed for peel strength. Overall, lap-shear strength is greater for tapes bonded to SUS and PVC than to PE and PP. This performance difference likely stems from the mixed cohesive-interfacial failure mode observed during testing, where interfacial adhesion contributes partially. Compared to commercial PSAs, the P2–P5 formulations exhibit markedly greater lap-shear strength than both PSA-S0105 and PSA-H0405. Static and dynamic shear tests on both polar and nonpolar substrates confirm the superior cohesive strength of PDBAG PSAs.

Tack of PDBAG PSAs

The tack of PDBAG PSA is shown in Figure . A larger ball number indicates better tack. Formulations P1–P3 with 30 wt % DBI have the same tack of 10#, which indicates that the introduction of a small amount of GMA does not affect the tack. The initial tack of PDBAG PSAs is slightly inferior to that of commercial acrylic PSAs. When the DBI content increasing from 30 wt % to 50 wt %, the tack of the PSA decreases from 10# to 5#. This reduction is primarily attributed to DBI restricting polymer segment flexibility and mobility, as evidenced by the increase in Tg. The main reason for the difference between the peel force and the initial tack is the different bonding action times. Second, the contact pressure and contact area are also different. The residence time of the ball at one position on the adhesive tape is not sufficient to form a stable bonding interface, and it only provides a short-term adhesion effect to make it decelerate. The wetting rate of the polymer in the PSA toward the steel ball plays a decisive role, while the energy consumption of the PSA during the peeling process plays a dominant role.

The commercial PSA-H0405 is a general-purpose acrylic latex with balanced adhesive properties. The peel strength of P3 on PVC is 603% and on SUS is 127% of commercial products. Its 180° peel strength on PE or PP (2.7–2.9 N/25 mm) is comparable to PSA-H0405. Due to the high 180° peel strength of P1–P5, we further selected the commercial high-peel acrylic PSA-S0105 for label applications to compare its performance with P5. These experiments reveal a correlation between peel strength and bonding time. According to the research by Lee et al., polar groups in PSAs reorient toward the SUS surface with prolonged bonding time. For example, the carboxyl group enhances the hydrogen bonding interactions between the adhesive and the substrate. , Based on their performance characteristics, PDBAG PSAs and PSA-H0405 are considered to exhibit a mechanism of hydrogen bond-enhanced peel strength.

Conclusion

A series of sustainable poly­(dibutyl itaconate-co-butyl acrylate-co-acrylic acid-co-glycidyl methacrylate) (PDBAG) PSAs, containing 30–50 wt % biobased DBI monomer, were synthesized via high-temperature semicontinuous emulsion polymerization. PDBAG is a lightly cross-linked polymer exhibiting high swelling and partial solubility in organic solvents. Its soluble molecular weight (M w) ranges from 1.63 × 105 to 2.56 × 105 g/mol, decreasing with increasing DBI content. The DBI component increases the ratio of G″(50 Hz)/G′(0.2 Hz) from 2.4 to 5.9, enhancing energy dissipation during peeling and boosting peel force on SUS materials.

The biobased itaconate PSA (with 30 wt % DBI) demonstrates significantly higher holding power and peel strength on polar substrates than the commercial general-purpose acrylic PSA-H0405. The biobased itaconate PSA (with 50 wt % DBI) shows remarkably superior holding power to the commercial high-peel acrylic PSA-S0105, with comparable peel strength on polar substrates but inferior peel strength on nonpolar substrates. The self-cross-linking biobased itaconate PSAs demonstrate superior practical utility to commercially available general-purpose acrylic PSAs in both peel strength and shear creep resistance. Its performance is highly tunable through formulation adjustments. DBI successfully achieves partial replacement of acrylic structures on the polymer main chain. More importantly, itaconate PSAs integrate advantages in simple and environmentally friendly preparation processes and excellent adhesive performance. Collectively, the DBI-acrylic monomer copolymer represents a promising high-performance biobased PSA platform. The emulsion-based self-cross-linking itaconate PSA is expected to emerge as a next-generation sustainable adhesive, demonstrating strong potential for industrial applications (e.g., adhesive tapes, protective films).
