Transforming challenging telephone conversations - investigating the effectiveness of an adapted ISBAR tool for delivering bad news
Authors: Mark R. O’Donovan, Nora McCarthy, Noreen Moloney, Evelyn Flanagan, Margaret McKiernan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommunication is a cornerstone of medical practice. Clear dialogue between healthcare professionals, patients, and families underpins trust, shared decision-making, and patient satisfaction. Remote consultations have increased reliance on telephone communication, introducing additional challenges. Communication is already a leading source of healthcare complaints, and remote interactions can further complicate clarity, empathy, and structure. To address these challenges, a structured telephone communication tool, ISBAR Plus, was developed. This tool builds on the established ISBAR framework (‘Introduction’, ‘Situation’, ‘Background’, ‘Assessment’, ‘Recommendation’), already used for clinical communication in healthcare settings, and adapts its core principles for telephone-based delivery of difficult information. The ISBAR Plus tool comprises ‘Prepare’, ‘Introduction’, ‘Situation’, ‘Background’, ‘Acknowledge shock’, ‘Recommendation’, with each element containing detailed description. A dedicated ISBAR Plus training programme was also designed to support nurses and doctors in applying the tool effectively in practice.AimTo evaluate the impact of the ISBAR Plus telephone communication tool and associated training programme on nurses’ and doctors’ ability to deliver bad news by telephone.MethodsA single-group pre–post quantitative study assessed simulated telephone communication performance of nurses and doctors when breaking bad news, before and after ISBAR Plus training. A ‘yes/no’ 20-point content and clarity checklist was used, together with a Global Rating Score (GRS) measuring overall communication quality. Participants completed a feedback questionnaire to evaluate the tool’s practicality and perceived value.ResultsSeventy-four participants completed the study. The proportion achieving full marks for content and clarity increased from 1% pre-training to 49% post-training. Overall communication quality scores improved from 15% to 51% (p < 0.001). Participant feedback was highly positive: 99% reported that ISBAR Plus was practical and applicable to clinical practice, and 91% recommended that the training be delivered annually to staff.ConclusionImplementation of the ISBAR Plus tool and structured training programme demonstrated an association between the intervention and improved performance in an educational context. A significant improvement in nurses’ and doctors’ performance and overall quality in delivering bad news via telephone in a simulated environment was observed. The intervention was well received and demonstrates strong potential as a practical, scalable approach to standardising and improving remote clinical communication.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09794-4.

Background

Introduction

Effective communication between patients and healthcare professionals is a cornerstone of safe, high-quality healthcare delivery [1]. It underpins trust, shared decision-making, and patient satisfaction [2]. Traditionally, such communication has relied on face-to-face interaction, enabling nurses and doctors to use both verbal and non-verbal cues to build rapport, assess emotional responses, and confirm patient understanding [3]. However, even in in-person interactions, significant challenges in effective communication have been documented, with poor communication being consistently identified as a leading cause of complaints and adverse events [4].

Breaking bad news (BBN) is among the most sensitive and emotionally charged forms of communication in medicine. Evidence points to poor communication during this important time of face-to-face interactions. The Irish Ombudsman’s A Good Death report (2014) identified persistent shortcomings in communication at the end of life, particularly regarding the “gap” between the information doctors or nurses intended to convey and what patients or families actually understood [5]. A follow-up report in 2018 reaffirmed that ineffective communication, particularly when delivering bad or distressing news, continued to feature prominently in healthcare complaints, and further training for doctors and nurses around communication was recommended [4]. Consistent with this, a systematic review by Anderson et al. (2019) found that targeted training could equip doctors and nurses with the necessary strategies to communicate more effectively with patients and families about prognosis and end-of-life issues [6].

The COVID-19 pandemic rapidly transformed communication practices, accelerating a shift from face-to-face consultations to remote interactions. Telephone consultations have improved accessibility and efficiency for many patients [7], but they also present distinct challenges: the loss of visual cues, increased risk of misunderstanding, and reduced opportunities to express empathy or respond to emotional cues [8, 9]. These limitations become particularly acute when clinical staff must deliver bad or distressing news remotely.

Although frameworks such as SPIKES [10] provide structured guidance for in-person communication, their adaptation for telephone-based encounters remains limited. Research during and after the pandemic shows that doctors and nurses often feel uncomfortable delivering bad news remotely, citing difficulties such as the inability to gauge non-verbal reactions, uncertainty when responding to silence, and diminished capacity to offer immediate emotional support [11]. Current research shows substantial inconsistency in the way telephone communication is organised and delivered [12]. Many studies provide only minimal explanation of how calls were conducted, offering little information about the use of structured frameworks, scripted approaches, or formal communication training for doctors and nurses [12].

Wolf et al. (2020) and others have stressed the need for education specifically tailored to the effective and compassionate delivery of bad news to patients in remote contexts [11, 13]. Although established frameworks such as SPIKES [10] provide structured guidance for delivering bad news in face-to-face settings, their suitability and adaptability for telephone consultations remain uncertain. Moreover, the SPIKES protocol, originally developed within oncology and palliative care [10], is not widely known or consistently applied among general hospital staff; in one study, only around one third of general surgery residents had ever received training in it [14].

These findings point to a clear and unresolved gap: existing breaking-bad-news frameworks do not adequately support doctors and nurses in delivering sensitive information by telephone, despite the increasing reliance on remote communication. This has created an urgent need for structured, practical tools that reflect the realities of telephone-based discussions.

Within hospital settings, structured communication tools such as ISBAR (Identify, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) are already well established and widely used to support clear, concise exchanges of clinical information, including by phone [15–18]. As doctors and nurses are familiar with using ISBAR to organise their conversations, adapting it to guide sensitive or complex communication tasks, such as breaking bad news remotely, may offer a feasible and effective approach.

In response to this recognised need for structured support in telephone-based delivery of bad news by healthcare staff, the ISBAR Plus tool was developed, together with an accompanying training programme designed specifically for breaking bad news by telephone.

Design of training tool & programme

ISBAR is used as the communication tool for clinical handovers including the escalation of care of a deteriorating patient. For that reason, it was considered appropriate to modify this to use for clinical healthcare professionals (nurses and doctors) communicating with patient families over the phone. Through numerous discussions with CNM2 Nurse Practice Development tutors, placement facilitators, University College Cork medical lecturers and consultants, the final ISBAR Plus tool was created (see Appendix 1). This paper does not explore its development, rather it looks at its impact.

The ISBAR Plus tool is prefaced by a Prepare phase which stresses the importance of ensuring the communication takes place in a private space with all needed information to hand. It then contains all the core original ISBAR elements of’ Identify’; ‘Situation’; ‘Background’; ‘Recommendation’, modified to aid communication by phone. The meaning of ‘A’ changes from ‘Assessment’ to ‘Acknowledge shock’. Identify contains the regular elements associated with ISBAR in terms of identifying yourself, the person you are talking to, the person you are talking about. It also includes the importance of making a good connection in this identify phase. ‘Situation, S’ involves establishing the situation, encouraging the person you are speaking with to fill you in on what they already know. ‘Background, B’ emphasises the need to follow up on the situation with a discussion on what has happened that necessitates the phone call, stressing the need for use of silence and pauses in this period, and use of simple language. ‘Acknowledge shock, A’ also encourages silence where needed, with listening being emphasised here. ‘Recommendation, R’ ensures that a discussion takes place on the next steps for the relative and for the hospital.

Following on from the development of the ISBAR Plus tool an ISBAR Plus training programme was developed by CNM2 Nurse Practice Development and the Director of Nursing, following Kern et al.’s six-step model of curriculum development [19]. The training programme takes approximately 40 min to complete. It consists of two instructors with a nursing background and trained facilitators observing course participants working in pairs and simulating a phone call, using low fidelity simulation, where bad news needs to be delivered to a family member. One participant makes the telephone call and the other acts as the family member who is being called. They then swap roles for a second attempt at this. Examples of breaking bad news scenarios used in training included a patient death and a health deterioration. Thereafter, there is a 15-minute presentation by the two facilitators which includes an overview of communication elements and challenging conversations. The participants are then introduced to, and gain familiarity with, the ISBAR Plus tool as a means of aiding challenging telephone conversations. Following this, the scenario-based simulated phone call is repeated by the original pairs.

It is necessary to examine the feasibility and effectiveness of any new development. Accordingly, an evaluation was undertaken to determine whether the developed training programme could be delivered as intended and whether the ISBAR Plus tool and training programme produced measurable improvements in communication performance.

Methods

Aim and objectives

Aim

The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of a newly developed communication tool, namely the ISBAR Plus tool, and its associated training programme designed to support doctors and nurses in breaking bad news over the telephone. Specifically, the study sought to determine whether structured training in a simulated environment could improve step-by-step communication performance and overall communication quality in this context.

Design and setting

This study was designed as a single-group pre–post quantitative evaluation of simulated telephone communication performance by nurses and doctors before and after ISBAR Plus training. Post-training participant feedback was collected using a structured questionnaire containing predominantly closed-ended items and one open-ended comment question. The pre-post ISBAR tool-based checklist scores and global rating scores formed the primary quantitative outcome, while the feedback questionnaire responses provided supplementary insights into participants’ experiences of the training.

The study took place in Mercy University Hospital (MUH), a Model 3 acute hospital providing a range of medical and surgical services, including emergency medicine, within the Southwest Region of Ireland. MUH is a teaching hospital affiliated with University College Cork (UCC) for undergraduate and postgraduate education. A mixed group of nursing and medical staff participated.

Ethical approval was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of the Cork Teaching Hospitals (Ref: ECM 4 (s) 19/10/2021 and ECM 3 (dddd) 16/11/2021). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of Declaration of Helsinki. All participants received a participant information leaflet and provided written informed consent prior to inclusion in the study.

Training and assessment procedure

Each participant attended one training session. Each training session was standardised and facilitators followed a script. During the session, participants completed a simulated telephone scenario involving breaking bad news. Their performance was assessed both before and after training in the use of the ISBAR Plus communication tool.

In order to evaluate communication parameters effectively, a fine-grained assessment approach was required, with clearly defined metrics to enable accurate evaluation [20]. Accordingly, a structured marking metric sheet specific to ISBAR Plus was developed (see Appendix 2), adapted with minimal contextual modification from the Harrison and Flanagan [21] metric sheet designed for clinical ISBAR communication. Adapted versions of this checklist have been widely used within the ISBAR literature although their psychometric properties have received little attention [22–24]. Prior to study commencement, observers received training in use of the checklist and study procedures. No formal inter-rater reliability statistics were assessed and should be considered in future validation work.

Observers and scoring

Seven clinicians comprising doctors and nurses acted as observers and assessors. They received preparatory training from the Director of Nursing and CNM2 Practice Development prior to data collection. This training reviewed the structure of the ISBAR Plus tool and ISBAR Plus training programme, the simulated scenarios, the marking criteria, and the role of the observer/marker to ensure consistency in scoring of parts a), namely the 20-point content and clarity checklist, and part b), the Global Rating Score (GRS) for overall communication quality. Assessors were blinded to the specific study aims, they were not blinded to the pre- and post-intervention status of participants.

Post-training feedback questionnaire

At the end of the ISBAR Plus training programme, participants completed a 10-question feedback questionnaire (see Appendix 3). The questionnaire included nine closed-ended questions and one open-ended question inviting additional comments.

Participants

A sample size of 62 people was calculated for this study based on a paired samples t-test with an effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.4, an alpha level of 0.025 and a power of 0.8. Given that no clear effect size was observed in similar pre-post ISBAR studies a value of 0.4 was selected representing the average estimated effect size in education research [25]. The chosen alpha level was 0.05 and the power was set at 0.8 [26], however, given there were two primary outcomes Bonferroni correction reduced the alpha value to 0.025 [27].

Due to staff interest, a larger sample of 74 clinical staff were recruited between 11th January and 8th July 2023. Recruitment was advertised via email and posters around the hospital.

Data collection

The magnitude of change in the marking sheet score was measured by dividing changes in participants’ scores by the mean baseline score [28] i.e. the change relative to the mean number of items correct at baseline. In addition, the change was divided by 20 minus the mean baseline score to illustrate the magnitude of potential improvement (i.e. the change relative to the mean number of items incorrect at baseline).

Fidelity score: Fidelity refers to the extent to which users adhere to the ISBAR Plus checklist elements during communication [28] in the simulated assessment and was measured as the total score on the 20-item checklist above. The fidelity score is calculated as the proportion of items addressed i.e. the total score divided by 20 [28].

Difficulty with ISBAR elements: For questions on the “easiest element of ISBAR” and the “hardest element of ISBAR” many participants selected more than one element. Thus, these two questions were combined into five ordinal variables, one of each element of the ISBAR, scored by whether participants ranked the element as the easiest (1), neither easiest or hardest (2), or hardest (3).

Data analysis

Quantitative analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.1) [29] using the mice [30] and miceafter [31] packages. Sample size was estimated for a paired t-test using the expected Cohen’s d with Bonferroni correction applied for two primary outcomes. Missing data were handled using multivariate imputation by chained Eqs [32, 33]. Given the mixed variable types and proportion of incomplete observations, predictive mean matching was used to generate 35 imputed datasets with 40 iterations [30, 34–36]. Estimates were pooled primarily using Rubin’s Rules [32]. Additional details regarding the imputation model, diagnostics, and sensitivity analyses (see Appendix 4) [37, 38].

Changes in continuous outcomes were assessed using pooled paired t-tests and reported with Cohen’s d effect sizes [32]. Changes in paired binary outcomes were analysed using the McNemar tests, with p-values pooled using the D2 method [39, 40]. Ranked difficulty scores for ISBAR components were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with pooled p-values estimated using the median method [41].

Statistical significance for multiple outcomes were assessed using the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Six training sessions involving and doctors (n = 50) and nurses (n = 24) occurred between 12th January and 13th July 2023. Gender was not recorded as it was not part of the analysis. The doctors involved had between 1- and 4-years work experience. Of the 24 nurses, roughly one-third of them had between 1- and 5- years work experience, with the remainder having between 6 and 15 years’ experience. Descriptive statistics for the participants’ experience and views on breaking bad news to a patient’s family are presented in Table 1. These illustrate that most participants had limited experience with breaking bad news with almost all (99%) having to break bad news to a patient’s family less than five times in the previous month; 56% reported that they did not have an overall consistent plan or strategy. Nearly half, 44% (95% confidence interval: 33%–56%) of the participants reported having no formal education on breaking bad news prior to the ISBAR Plus training. Similarly, a large proportion, 56% (45%–68%), reported they had no formal training on responding to the emotions of patients’ families.

Analysis of a) 20-point content and clarity checklist

As shown in Table 2, following the ISBAR Plus training in a simulated environment, 15 out of 20 items had a statistically significant improvement. ‘I’ did not statistically improve post training, while all scoring points representing the other categories of S-B-A-R demonstrated a statistically sufficient change. In terms of overall results, the mean result of the 20-point Global Rating Score for content and clarity increased from 13.30 to 18.51, corresponding to an absolute improvement of 5.21 items (95% CI: 4.36–6.05, p-value < 0.001), and a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.43). This represents an improvement of 39% (33%–45%) relative to the mean baseline score of 13.30, or an improvement of 78% (65%–90%) relative to the mean potential improvement score of 6.70 in the simulated environment. Closed questions

All available participants stated that the ISBAR Plus tool made sense to them (95% confidence interval: 95%–100%), 99% (91%–100%) felt the protocol is practical and can be used in their clinical practice, and 91% (81%–96%) thought the training should be provided to staff annually. One participant clarified that it should be delivered “every 2 to 3 years”. Which parts of the ISBAR Plus tool participants considered the easiest and the most difficult are presented in Fig. 3. Based on the mean ranking the easiest parts of the ISBAR were “I” (mean rank = 1.76) and “B” (2.86); followed by: “S” (3.42), “R” (3.47) and “A” (3.50). Applying Bonferroni-correction, Wilcoxon tests revealed that “I” was statistically significantly easier than all the other parts (p-values < 0.001); “B” was statistically significantly easier than “S” (p = 0.048), “R” (p < 0.001) and “A” (p0.004); and these were no statistically significant differences between “S”, “R” and “A” (p-values ≥ 1).

Open question

Not enough participants completed this question for a formal thematic analysis to be conducted; however, several themes can be tentatively identified from the available responses, namely: Overall positive perception of the ISBAR Plus tool; Perceived usefulness for telephone-based communication; Potential for broader clinical application; Value of a structured communication approach; Training and practice needs; Distinction between communication contexts; Importance of real-world experience. As formal thematic analysis was not feasible, these are exploratory observations only.

Overall, participants were overwhelmingly positive towards the use of the ISBAR Plus tool, although a small number raised concerns. Many comments reflected a broadly positive perception of the tool’s usefulness, particularly for telephone communication when breaking bad news. Participants described it as “a brilliant tool” and “a very useful tool,” indicating general acceptability. A related thread across responses was the perceived applicability of the tool across different clinical situations, with suggestions that it could be “incorporated into daily practice” and introduced as a standard resource for new staff.

Another commonly noted aspect was the value of structure. Several participants highlighted the benefit of having a “structured approach” to delivering bad news, with some indicating that having a copy readily available, such as next to the phone, would be helpful in practice.

A smaller number of responses pointed to training considerations. While the training was described as “very helpful,” one participant noted a “need for more practice,” suggesting that further exposure may be beneficial. In addition, some participants distinguished between communication modalities, with one noting that while ISBAR Plus is useful for telephone communication, the SPIKES model may be more appropriate for in-person conversations. Finally, one response emphasised the importance of real clinical experience, stating that “real situations are [the] only way to learn,” highlighting perceived differences between simulated training and real-world application.

These observations should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of responses but provide some preliminary insight into participant perspectives.

Discussion

Our pre-test post-test study investigated the feasibility and effectiveness of a new ISBAR training tool (ISBAR Plus) and the associated ISBAR Plus training programme in a simulated environment, in the context of breaking bad news over the phone. The results of this study demonstrated a strong association between the intervention and improved performance in an educational context. The ISBAR Plus tool and associated training was effective and well received in a simulated educational setting. The majority of participants (93%) improved their telephone conversation content and clarity and 76% improved their overall telephone communication quality. In addition, 99% considered it something they could use in their clinical practice, supported by open-ended responses which noted the advantage of having a structured approach to delivering bad news. Most (91%) thought the training should be provided to staff annually. The current training in the ISBAR Plus tool only takes about 40 min and the positive feedback illustrates that it may be a valuable addition to periodic staff trainings, particularly for those who lack experience in breaking bad news to patients’ families over the phone. The current study suggests that the “I” and “B” components of the ISBAR Plus training were easier than “S”, “A” and “R”. This is not surprising as these are the areas where the difficult conversation takes place. More focus on these components may be warranted in future training.

There is moderate evidence that ISBAR-related instruments can improve patient safety, especially when used to structure communication over the phone [42]; however results have been variable regarding their impact on communication quality [28]. For example, other studies found fidelity levels ranging between 53% and 87% and relative effect sizes between 6% and 152% [28]. Comparatively, the present study evaluating simulation-based communication performance found a superior fidelity estimate of 93% (95% CI: 90%–95%) for the ISBAR Plus based on a 20-item checklist; and a favourable relative improvement of 39% (33%–45%). For communication quality, the relative improvements in other studies ranged from 5% to 50% [28], and the current analysis found a relative improvement for the ISBAR Plus of 38% (32%–45%). Following ISBAR Plus training in a simulated environment, approximately half the participants in this study (49% and 51%, respectively) scored full marks for content/clarity and quality on the post-training telephone conversation compared with 1% and 15% pre-training.

While coming to the fore in COVID-19 pandemic time [43], as technology and telemedicine advances, telephone communication will likely become more prevalent as a method of disclosing bad news. When face-to-face meetings aren’t feasible, virtual communication between doctors or nurses and patients’ families can serve as a reasonably effective substitute. Applying techniques tailored specifically to telephone and video interactions can enhance the experience for both healthcare providers and family members [44]. Systematic review evidence suggests that structured telephone communication plays an important role in supporting families of critically ill patients when in-person contact is limited or unavailable [12]. However, the existing literature demonstrates considerable variability in how telephone communication is structured; descriptions of call structure were often limited, with insufficient detail regarding frameworks, scripts, or communication training used by doctors and nurses [12]. Importantly, few studies directly evaluated structured versus unstructured telephone communication using rigorous comparative designs [12]. While observational and quasi-experimental evidence suggests that structured approaches may improve family experience and perceived communication quality, high-quality randomised controlled trials are lacking.

The outcomes from this study were derived from simulated scenarios in educational settings, which limits the extent to which findings can be generalised to real-world clinical practice. While simulation offers a controlled and safe environment in which to practise communication skills in general terms [45–48], it does not fully replicate the emotional, relational, and contextual complexities of delivering bad news in real clinical settings. Consequently, improvements in structured communication and global performance scores observed in this study reflect performance may not directly translate to equivalent improvements in clinical practice. However, simulation remains a well-established and valuable method for developing communication skills [49, 50] and these findings provide encouraging evidence of its potential utility in the area of remote communication training. Further research in real-world or clinical environments would be required to evaluate the transferability and sustainability of these skills.

There is limited investigation into how structured telephone communication influences longer-term psychological outcomes, shared decision-making, or understanding of prognosis. While literature to date suggests that there are no differences in the psychological distress of family members receiving bad news from trained staff regardless of whether it is over the phone or in person [51], It is also worth noting that breaking bad news effectively over the phone may require different skills and competencies compared with in-person meetings [12, 13]. Within many countries, telephone communications are not currently a formal part of medical school training, unlike some countries such as Switzerland [52]. Formal training specifically on breaking bad news is often lacking, as reported by almost half (44%, 95% CI: 33%–56%) of the participants in this study.

Implementing use of the ISBAR Plus tool could enhance the quality and consistency of oral communication and support clear communication with reduced ambiguity when breaking bad news. Using the well-recognised ‘ISBAR’ as the foundation and extending its use, from face-to-face and phone communication between healthcare professionals to remote communication between healthcare professionals and families when breaking bad news, represents an exciting step forward. Overall, structured telephone communication appears to be a feasible and potentially beneficial strategy for maintaining family engagement in critical care contexts. Nevertheless, the evidence base remains heterogeneous and methodologically limited [12]. Future research should prioritise clearly defined, standardised telephone communication protocols; robust comparative study designs; and consistent outcome measures. Such work is needed to determine optimal frequency, content, and delivery methods, and to establish whether structured telephone communication meaningfully improves both experiential and psychological outcomes for families of deteriorating or critically ill patients.

Furthermore, the increasing use of video consultations raises exciting possibilities for future research. While telephone consultations are limited by the absence of visual cues, video platforms partially restore these elements, allowing doctors and nurses to observe facial expressions and aspects of non-verbal behaviour that are important when delivering distressing information [8, 9, 44]. In this context, adapting the ISBAR Plus tool for video consultations may offer additional value, combining the benefits of a structured communication framework with the ability to respond to visual and emotional cues in real time. This may enhance doctors’ and nurses’ capacity to demonstrate empathy, gauge patient understanding, and tailor responses more effectively than in purely audio-based interactions. Given the ongoing shift toward hybrid models of care, the potential to extend the ISBAR Plus tool into video-based settings represents a logical and potentially worthwhile area for further exploration.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study are that it was sufficiently powered to assess the primary outcomes of interest based on the sample size calculation and had only a small amount of incomplete data. Robust statistical methods were also applied including multiple imputation in accordance with recent methodological guidelines [33, 37, 38] which should improve the quality of estimates.

The following limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. The use of a single group pre-post design without a control or comparator group limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Future research with controlled and randomised studies is recommended and will address this limitation. The relatively large sample size and inclusion of a multidisciplinary group of doctors and nurses strengthen its relevance as an initial evaluation. Improvements observed following the intervention may not be attributable solely to the ISBAR Plus tool or training programme. Alternative explanations include repetition effects, increasing familiarity with the simulation format, and general learning or practice effects associated with repeated exposure to similar scenarios. While repeated exposure to simulation scenarios may have contributed to improved performance, this effect is inherent to many educational interventions and reflects the role of practice integral to skill development.

Participants may have modified their behaviour because they were aware they were being observed (Hawthorne effect), which could have contributed to improved post-intervention performance independent of the intervention itself. However, any Hawthorne effect may have been partially mitigated by the fact that participants were observed at both pre- and post-intervention stages, rather than only after the training. The consistent direction and magnitude of improvement suggest that the intervention represents a useful educational approach worthy of further investigation.

The assessment process introduces the potential for observer bias. Assessors were not blinded to the pre- and post-intervention status of participants, which may have influenced scoring and led to inflation of post-intervention ratings. Although assessors were blinded to the specific study aims, awareness of the timing of assessments could still have consciously or unconsciously affected their evaluations. In addition, the use of subjective elements within the Global Rating Score may further increase susceptibility to such bias. While this may affect the precision of the measured effect size, the provision of structured training to assessors prior to the assessment should have helped standardise the assessor’s approach and reduce variability unrelated to performance.

Conclusion

Effective communication, long recognised as central to safe and compassionate care, has been reshaped by the expansion of telemedicine. While telephone consultations offer practical and accessible alternatives to face-to-face interactions, they necessitate deliberate adaptation of communication strategies, particularly when delivering bad news or managing emotionally sensitive discussions. Ongoing education, structured communication tools, and organisational support are essential to maintain empathy, clarity, and patient-centredness in both in-person and remote clinical encounters.

There are limited opportunities in undergraduate and postgraduate medical and nurse training curricula to learn effective and compassionate methods of how to communicate over the phone when a patient’s condition changes, or they have died. To aid clinical staff in making challenging telephone conversations, the ISBAR Plus tool and ISBAR Plus training programme were developed. The ISBAR Plus training programme was well received and, in a simulated environment, was associated with large statistically significant improvements in communication content, clarity and overall quality. The ISBAR Plus tool reduced miscommunication, minimising errors and misunderstandings by standardising the communication process. The current study highlights the educational value of the ISBAR Plus tool and training programme and the potential for capacity building in breaking bad news to patients’ families over the phone by using a structured communication tool.

The newly developed ISBAR Plus tool offers exciting and promising possibilities to improve communication beyond the face-to-face inter- and intra-professional communication for which the traditional ISBAR tool was created. This study demonstrates an association between the ISBAR Plus tool and training programme and improved communication performance in an educational context. While acknowledging that this study is exploratory in nature and does not provide definitive evidence of effectiveness in clinical practice, the ISBAR Plus tool nevertheless represents a promising strategy for advancing the standardisation and quality of remote clinical communication at scale. The ISBAR Plus tool provides a structured approach to remote communication. It allows healthcare professionals to present information succinctly while maintaining empathy, ensuring key details are conveyed accurately, and providing a clear plan for next steps. This is a vital step forward in keeping with other modern world advances. This short but effective bespoke educational programme, using the newly developed ISBAR plus tool for phone communication, offers an exciting possibility to inform future training and education programmes in communication by providing a practical and scalable framework for strengthening and harmonising remote clinical communication.
