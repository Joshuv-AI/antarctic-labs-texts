Resting-state EEG default mode network connectivity and suicidal ideation in depressive disorders: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Hyeonjin Kim, Hahyun Lee, Su Mi Park, Da Young Oh, Silvia Kyungjin Lho, So Young Yoo, Jun-Young Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide remains a critical global public health issue, and the ideation-to-action framework suggests that suicidal ideation and attempts involve distinct processes. However, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying the transition from suicidal ideation (SI) to suicide attempts (SA) have not been fully understood, particularly in clinically heterogeneous psychiatric populations. Background

Suicide is a major global public health concern, with approximately 800,000 individuals dying by suicide annually. To prevent suicide effectively, it is crucial to differentiate and understand the factors contributing to the transition from suicidal ideation (SI) to suicide attempt (SA) [1], as the majority of individuals experiencing suicidal thoughts do not ultimately attempt suicide. For example, in a population-based study, only 7.4% of participants reporting SI at baseline went on to attempt suicide within the following two years [2]. While a recent systematic review reported a broader range of 2.6% to 37% [3], the findings still suggest that only around one-third of those with SI eventually take action. Similarly, in Korea, approximately one in ten people experienced suicidal ideation at some point in their lifetime, but only 1.7% made a suicide attempt [4].

The ideation-to-action framework of suicidal behavior postulates that the factors involved in the development of suicidal thoughts are distinct from those involved in the transition from ideation to attempts [1]. This distinction has been emphasized in several theoretical models, including the Interpersonal Theory of Suicide [5], the Three-Step Theory [6], and the Integrated Motivational-Volitional Model [7], which commonly suggest that suicidal ideation and attempts may be associated with distinct neuropsychological processes. Specifically, the Motivational-Volitional Model [7] explains that ideation arises from motivational factors such as feelings of defeat or entrapment, which might be closely related to a depressed psychological state. In contrast, the transition from ideation to attempt is driven by volitional factors, including the capability for suicide, access to lethal means, and other psychological disinhibiting factors like impulsivity. In this context, the heterogeneity between SI and SA has gained increasing attention, leading to a growing body of research aimed at more clearly distinguishing these groups and identifying objective neurobiological correlates relevant to their clinical differentiation [8–10].

Existing literature suggests that altered default mode network (DMN) functioning during the resting state may be relevant to suicidal ideation, particularly in the context of depressive psychopathology. The DMN is implicated in self-referential processing and internally directed thought, and its dysfunction has been linked to depressive rumination and negative self-focused cognition [11, 12]. These cognitive-affective processes are closely related to the motivational phase of suicidal ideation, in which persistent negative self-appraisal, hopelessness, and perceived entrapment may contribute to the emergence or maintenance of suicidal thoughts. Consistent with this view, a meta-analysis reported altered DMN connectivity in major depressive disorder (MDD) [13]. More specifically, decreased functional connectivity within the frontoparietal network and DMN has been observed in MDD patients with SI compared to those without SI [14]. However, the direction of DMN abnormalities has varied across studies, with some findings indicating hyperconnectivity within the DMN [13] and others showing hypoconnectivity between specific DMN subregions [10, 14, 15], including reduced interplay from the anterior to the ventral DMN subsystems in MDD patients [15]. Collectively, these findings suggest that aberrant DMN connectivity may be relevant to SI, particularly in the context of depressive psychopathology.

Regarding impulsivity and SA in the volitional phase, impairments in inhibitory control and related neurobiological processes may contribute to the transition from suicidal ideation to suicidal attempt. Specifically, individuals who have attempted suicide indeed exhibited deficits in the ability to detect and inhibit maladaptive behaviors [16]. Neuroimaging findings indicated that depressed adolescents who have attempted suicide showed reduced activity in the anterior cingulate cortex during tasks involving response inhibition, which may reflect impaired impulse control [17]. Furthermore, patients with major depressive disorder have exhibited altered brain signals during response inhibition tasks, such as reduced Nogo-P3 amplitude or increased activity in brain regions like the inferior frontal gyrus and anterior cingulate cortex, though suicidal behavior was not directly assessed in these studies [18, 19]. These findings suggest that deficits in inhibitory control and associated neural processes may play a critical role in the progression from suicidal ideation to suicidal attempts.

Despite extensive research using structural and functional neuroimaging, it remains unclear which neurobiological features can reliably distinguish SI from SA. Prior studies have often focused either on depression-related alterations in the default mode network or on inhibitory control deficits associated with suicidal behavior, but few have examined these networks simultaneously within the same analytical framework or considered the possibility that their relevance may vary across diagnostic contexts. This gap limits the clinical applicability of existing findings, particularly in settings that require accessible and cost-effective tools.

To address this gap, the present study examined whether resting-state electroencephalography (EEG) can identify dissociable network-level patterns related to SI and SA. EEG offers several advantages compared to other neuroimaging methods [20], including its cost-effectiveness, practicality, and translational potential. Focusing on large-scale networks implicated in prior theoretical models, we investigated whether altered connectivity within the DMN would be associated with SI, whereas changes in the inhibition network would be more closely related to SA. Recent studies in MDD have further emphasized that factors associated with SA may vary across clinical and biological domains, supporting the need to examine SA-related features within specific diagnostic contexts [21–25]. Therefore, we also considered the possibility that such associations may be more readily detectable within diagnostically more homogeneous contexts, particularly depressive disorders. Based on previous findings, we hypothesized that SI would be linked to reduced DMN connectivity, whereas SA would be associated with abnormalities in inhibitory network connectivity.

Methods

Participants

We analyzed data from electronic medical records, psychological assessment batteries, and quantitative EEG at resting state, collected between January 2016 and December 2021 at the Seoul Metropolitan Government-Seoul National University (SMG-SNU) Boramae Medical Center in Seoul, South Korea. Patients with a history of SI and SA were identified through clinical interviews with a psychiatrist and a clinical psychologist. Patients were initially diagnosed by a psychiatrist using the DSM-IV [26] or DSM-5 [27], depending on the time of clinical evaluation. They were also assessed by a clinical psychologist during psychological evaluations. Subsequently, two psychiatrists and two psychologists provided final clinical confirmation of the primary diagnosis, reviewing both the initial diagnoses and the psychological assessments. We included all patients who visited outpatient psychiatric clinics and psychiatric wards, were aged 18 or older, and could use the Korean language fluently. Patients with missing suicidal variables in electronic medical records, key covariates required for the statistical models (age, sex, medication status, and primary psychiatric diagnosis), or QEEG recordings were excluded from the analysis. We also excluded those with a lifetime or current medical history of a neurological disorder, brain injury, or borderline intellectual functioning. All patients with at least one episode of SI or SA were labeled as belonging to the SI or SA group, respectively. Mahalanobis distance matching was used to address demographic imbalances between the groups. The final analyses included 244 subjects: 54 with SA, 69 with only SI without SA, and 121 without SI or SA (Fig. 1). All study procedures were approved by the institutional review board of the SMG-SNU Boramae Medical Center (IRB No. 10-2021-119). Given the retrospective nature of the study design, the requirement for participant consent was waived.

Recording and preprocessing of EEG

Resting-state EEG data were acquired during a 5-minute period in a quiet, controlled setting. To minimize temporal discrepancies between EEG and psychological assessments, we included only participants whose EEG recordings were obtained within two weeks before or after psychological assessment. The median interval between EEG acquisition and psychological assessment was 2 days (interquartile range: 1–5 days). Participants were instructed to remain awake with their eyes closed throughout the recording, which was continuously monitored by an experienced EEG technician to minimize drowsiness or sleep onset. EEG was recorded from 19 scalp electrodes using a Neuroscan system at a sampling rate of 500-1,000 Hz, with online band-pass filtering from 0.1 to 100 Hz and a 60-Hz notch filter to attenuate line noise. Electrode impedances were kept below five kΩ by applying a conductive gel. For the subsequent analysis, the EEG data were downsampled to 128 Hz, and 19 channels were selected based on the international 10–20 system, using a mastoid reference electrode. The ground channel was positioned between the FPz and Fz electrodes.

EEG data were preprocessed using the Neuroguide software to extract sensor-level features (NG Deluxe 3.0.5; Applied Neuroscience, Inc., Largo, FL, USA). Artifacts were removed through visual inspection and an automatic cleaning system. Automatic artifact rejection was carried out in NG Deluxe with the built-in artifact detection and edit-generation functions under the default template settings. Sensitivity was set to high and the amplitude multiplier to 1.00. Artifact-contaminated segments were identified using a root-mean-square template-matching approach and were excluded from subsequent analyses. Using Fast Fourier Transformation, the EEG data were processed with a 2-second epoch, 128 sampling rate, 0.5–40 Hz frequency range, and 0.5 Hz resolution, applying a cosine taper window to minimize leakage. Due to the inherent noise in single epochs, at least 60 s of data were used.

Source localization and connectivity analysis of EEG

Source localization was performed using the standardized weighted low-resolution electromagnetic tomography (swLORETA) method [28] implemented in the NeuroNavigator software (NG Deluxe 3.0.5; Applied Neuroscience, Inc., Largo, FL, USA). Although the EEG data were acquired using a 19-channel montage, prior work has shown that sLORETA outperformed minimum norm estimation across 19- to 256-channel configurations, with only modest reductions in localization error as sensor density increased [29]. Compared with earlier LORETA variants, swLORETA further incorporated the boundary element method (BEM) for realistic head modeling and single-value decomposition (SVD-based lead-field weighting to generate a more homogeneous lead field, which has been proposed to improve source localization performance, particularly for deeper sources [30–32]. In addition, recent developments in simplified EEG technologies—such as dry EEG systems with 2–32 channels—have demonstrated signal quality comparable to traditional wet EEG, while offering substantially improved usability and accessibility in clinical settings [33]. These advancements underscore a broader movement toward practical, lower-channel EEG configurations in clinical neuroscience, supporting the feasibility of using standard 19-channel systems in translational research [34–37].

Once the inverse solution was computed, three-dimensional current vectors were estimated at 12,270 Gy matter voxels registered to the Colin27 MRI template from the Montreal Neurological Institute [38]. These voxel-wise estimates were then grouped by anatomical region using Talairach coordinates and Brodmann area definitions, enabling the extraction of region-specific time series [39, 40]. We delineated the DMN and inhibition network based on established Brodmann area definitions. Functional connectivity between all pairs of regions was computed using lagged coherence, as implemented in NeuroNavigator (NG Deluxe 3.0.5), across six frequency bands: delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (12–25 Hz), high beta (25–30 Hz), and gamma (30–40 Hz). In terms of regions of interest, the DMN comprised the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC; BA 10, 11), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC; BA 23), inferior parietal lobule (IPL; BA 39, 40), and bilateral precuneus (BA 7) [41]. The inhibition network included the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG; BA 44, 45), medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC; BA 9, 10), and temporal regions (BA 20, 21, 22) [42, 43]. This study focused on this source-level functional connectivity (lagged coherence) within the DMN and inhibition network as the key parameters of interest.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.1.3 (https://www.r-project.org). Differences between groups in demographic, clinical, and neuropsychological characteristics were examined using appropriate statistical tests (t-test, one-way ANOVA, or chi-squared test) depending on the type of data. We also analyzed group differences in functional connectivity by using a univariate generalized linear model for each frequency band and network, controlling for the effects of age, sex, medication status, and psychiatric diagnoses. Medication status was coded as four binary variables indicating whether a prescription had been received within the past month for benzodiazepine/hypnotics, antidepressants, antipsychotics, and mood stabilizers/antiepileptics. Primary psychiatric diagnosis was classified into five categories (anxiety disorders, mood disorders, neurodevelopmental disorders, psychotic disorders, and others) and entered as a categorical covariate using dummy coding, with anxiety disorders as the reference category. To address the issue of multiple comparisons, the false discovery rate (FDR) correction was employed [44]. Given that our hypotheses were defined at the level of large-scale functional networks, we defined each network as a separate family of tests and applied correction within each network across frequency bands and connection pairs. We additionally performed a global FDR correction across all tests for completeness. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For non-significant results, Bayes factors (BF) were computed using the lmBF function in R with the same covariates as in the primary analyses. For significant findings, we conducted a post hoc sensitivity analysis after excluding participants with high medication burden, defined as prescriptions for two or more medication classes. We visualized the results using the R package “ggplot2” and the MATLAB function and toolbox “BrainNet Viewer” [45]. Additionally, we repeated the same analysis on only patients diagnosed with depressive disorders.

EEG functional connectivity

First, to identify cortical networks associated with suicidal ideation, we compared connectivity in the DMN and inhibition network between the non-suicidal group and the SI + SA group across EEG frequency bands. This comparison was based on the assumption that suicidal ideation generally precedes suicide attempts. In the overall sample, no significant differences between the SI + SA group and the non-suicidal group survived either the global FDR correction or the network-based FDR correction in either the DMN or the inhibition network.

We next compared the SI-only group and the SA group to identify cortical networks associated with the transition from suicidal ideation to suicide attempt. In the overall sample, no significant differences between the SI-only and SA groups survived either the global FDR correction or the network-based FDR correction in either network. For these non-significant results, Bayes factor analyses indicated that approximately two-thirds of all comparisons provided moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis (Bayes factor for the null vs. alternative hypothesis, BF01 ≥ 3), which was generally consistent with the null pattern observed in the frequentist analyses (Table S3). However, isolated comparisons in the SI-only vs. SA contrast showed BF10 ≥ 3, suggesting that some effects may exist but remain undetected due to limited statistical power.

To further examine whether depressive symptoms may have influenced these findings, we repeated the same analyses in the subgroup of patients with depressive disorders (Table S4). In this subgroup, the comparison between the SI + SA group and the non-suicidal group showed no significant findings under the global FDR correction. However, under the network-based FDR correction, significant group differences were observed in the DMN. Specifically, the SI + SA group showed decreased functional connectivity compared with the non-suicidal group in the theta band, including the following connections: left dmPFC and right IPL (p = 0.028, network-based FDR-corrected), and right dmPFC and right IPL (p = 0.028, network-based FDR-corrected) (Table 2; Fig. 2). In the sensitivity analysis excluding participants with high medication burden, the theta-band DMN connectivity differences remained directionally consistent with the main analysis (Table S5), although they did not survive network-level FDR correction, likely reflecting the reduced sample size (non-suicidal, N = 14; SI + SA, N = 18). The larger partial η² estimates observed in this sensitivity analysis compared with the main analysis therefore warrant cautious interpretation, as effect size estimates from small samples may be upwardly biased and may not necessarily indicate a larger true effect. No significant differences were observed in the inhibition network.

Discussion

The study investigated whether the EEG resting state source-level cortical connectivity (DMN and inhibition network) differentiates SI and SA. Based on the ideation-to-action framework [1] and related neurobiological findings, we hypothesized that altered DMN connectivity would be associated with SI, while deficits in inhibition network connectivity would be linked to SA. In the full sample, no significant group differences survived either global or network-based FDR correction in either network. However, in the depressive disorder subgroup, the suicidal group (SI + SA) showed decreased theta-band lagged coherence within the DMN, particularly between the dmPFC and right IPL, compared with the non-suicidal group. In contrast, we found no significant differences between the SI-only and SA groups, and no significant findings emerged in the inhibition network. Taken together, these findings provide only partial support for our initial hypotheses and suggest that resting-state DMN alterations related to SI may be more detectable within depressive disorders than across a diagnostically heterogeneous psychiatric sample.

In the present study, no significant effects were observed in the overall sample, whereas reduced DMN connectivity emerged in the depressive disorder subgroup among patients with SI, regardless of their history of SA. This pattern was broadly consistent with our hypothesis that suicidal ideation may be associated with disrupted DMN connectivity, at least within a depressive context. This finding was consistent with prior studies reporting diminished connectivity within the DMN in depressed adolescents and adults with SI [14, 46–48]. It was also in line with evidence that the DMN has been implicated in self-referential processing, internally directed thought, and maladaptive rumination [11, 12, 49], all of which are closely related to the phenomenology of depression and suicidal ideation. In this context, reduced connectivity between dorsomedial prefrontal and parietal DMN regions may reflect disrupted integration of self-referential and affective processes in depressed patients with SI. Furthermore, structural alterations such as decreased fractional anisotropy in the ventral cingulum, which connects posterior and temporal DMN areas, have been observed in depressed adults with SI, providing convergent evidence for disrupted DMN-related structural circuitry [50]. Overall, the present finding is consistent with the view that altered DMN may be associated with SI within depressive disorders.

The subgroup-specific finding may be understood in light of the marked clinical heterogeneity of the full sample. Although we statistically adjusted for diagnosis and medication status, the overall sample still comprised patients with diverse clinical presentations and potentially distinct neurobiological mechanisms. In such a transdiagnostic context, neural alterations associated with suicidality may be relatively subtle, vary across disorders, or be partially obscured by disorder-specific pathophysiology. Indeed, previous studies have reported that resting-state EEG patterns differ across psychiatric disorders, although substantial overlap and within-disorder variability have also been noted [34, 51]. In contrast, depression is one of the conditions most strongly associated with suicidal thoughts and behaviors [52–55], and DMN abnormalities have been repeatedly linked to depressive cognition, particularly rumination and negative self-focus [11, 12]. It is therefore plausible that, within depressive disorders, SI may be more tightly coupled to alterations in intrinsic DMN organization. From this perspective, our results do not necessarily suggest that DMN dysfunction is unique to depression, but rather that it may be more readily detectable in a diagnostically more homogeneous context in which depressive psychopathology constitutes a central component of suicidal risk.

Contrary to our hypothesis, we found no significant evidence that resting-state inhibition-network connectivity differentiated the SA group from the SI-only group. This negative finding may have several explanations. First, inhibitory-control deficits associated with suicide attempts may be more robustly captured during task-based paradigms, such as Go/No-Go or Stop-Signal tasks, than during resting-state recordings [16–19], as the transition from ideation to attempt may involve context-dependent failures of inhibitory control that are not fully reflected by static resting-state functional connectivity. Second, the effect size of such differences may be smaller than anticipated, particularly after adjustment for multiple comparisons and relevant clinical covariates. Accordingly, the present findings do not provide support for the view that resting-state inhibition-network lag coherence reliably distinguishes suicide attempters from ideators in this sample.

This study had several limitations. First, the comparisons in the full sample were not statistically significant, and the depressive-disorder subgroup finding should be interpreted cautiously. Although the theta-band DMN connectivity difference was significant under the prespecified network-based FDR correction, it did not survive the more conservative global FDR correction; accordingly, the finding should be regarded as preliminary. This interpretation is further limited by the small non-suicidal depressive group (N = 24), constrained model degrees of freedom, and marked between-group differences in medication use that may affect theta-band EEG measures. Second, the cross-sectional and retrospective design precluded causal inferences, and suicidal ideation and suicide attempt were defined categorically on the basis of clinical history. In particular, suicidal ideation reflected relatively current symptomatology, whereas suicide attempt was defined based on lifetime history, preventing analyses of severity, recency, persistence, or dimensional variation. Future prospective and longitudinal studies with more fine-grained assessments of suicidality will be needed. Third, although diagnosis was modeled using broad clinical categories, some heterogeneity inevitably remained, and diagnostic procedures were based on either DSM-IV or DSM-5 depending on the time of assessment. Nonetheless, these broad categories are largely comparable across the two versions, which may have limited the impact of diagnostic inconsistency on the main analyses. Finally, although swLORETA lagged coherence was used to reduce the influence of zero-phase-lag connectivity and volume conduction, source-level connectivity estimates from low-density EEG should still be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusions

This study suggests that the neural correlates of suicidal ideation and suicide attempt may be influenced by diagnostic context rather than showing a simple one-to-one mapping with specific brain networks. In the full sample, no significant connectivity differences were observed, whereas in the depressive disorder subgroup, the suicidal group (SI + SA) showed reduced theta-band DMN connectivity compared with the non-suicidal group. By contrast, no significant differences were found between the SI-only and SA groups, and no significant findings emerged in the inhibition network. These findings highlight the importance of considering clinical heterogeneity when interpreting resting-state EEG findings in suicide research and suggest that examining more homogeneous diagnostic subgroups may provide a clearer view of relevant neural alterations. Further longitudinal and multimodal studies will be needed to replicate these network-level findings and clarify their relevance to clinical risk assessment.
