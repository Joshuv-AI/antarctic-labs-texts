Integrating temporal morphophysiological and genomic markers for precise classification of flowering time in cannabis
Authors: Mehdi Babaei, Hossein Nemati, Hossein Arouiee, Davoud Torkamaneh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Indigenous Cannabis sativa populations exhibit remarkable diversity in their flowering-time responses to photoperiod cues, reflecting adaptation to varied environments. Understanding the genetic and physiological mechanisms underlying this variation is essential for optimizing cultivation and targeted breeding programs. This study characterizes flowering time diversity among 25 Iranian cannabis landrace populations using longitudinal morphophysiological and genomic approaches. Weekly data on six morphophysiological traits—stem diameter, height, growth rate, node number, internode length, and SPAD chlorophyll index—were collected over 13 weeks for female and 11 weeks for male plants, across 145 accessions genotyped by high-density genotyping-by-sequencing, yielding 233,624 high-quality SNPs. Integrated machine learning analysis of 234,002 features—encompassing SNPs, morphophysiological traits, and environmental variables—identified 53 discriminative features (22 genomic variants and 31 morphophysiological traits) that effectively classify auto, early, and late flowering accessions. Key genomic loci included AutoFlower3 (CsFTL3) on chromosome 08 and CircadianFloweringLocus1 (CsCFL1) on chromosome 09. Auto-flowering accessions showed distinct genetic and phenotypic separation consistent with photoperiod insensitivity, whereas early- and late-flowering groups exhibited overlapping distributions indicative of photoperiod-sensitive responses. These findings advance understanding of the biology of cannabis photoperiodic flowering and provide validated genomic markers for marker-assisted selection and breeding programs targeting specific photoperiod response types.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53686-y.

Introduction

Cannabis (Cannabis sativa L.), commonly known as hemp, marijuana, marihuana, Indian hemp, and industrial hemp, is among the earliest domesticated and cultivated plants in human history with deep cultural, social, and economic significance1–3. Despite its long history, cannabis research has lagged behind other crops due to legal restrictions in most Western countries since the mid-20th century4. With shifting legislation and increasing legalization across countries, including Uruguay, Canada, the United States, the Netherlands, Germany, Thailand, and Morocco, the global cannabis market is projected to reach US$75.09 billion by 20295, creating an urgent need for science-based cultivation and breeding strategies.

Cannabis is a dioecious, short-day plant with a diploid genome (2n = 20), displaying remarkable phenotypic and genetic diversity across cultivars classified by chemotype, ecotype, and biotype6–8. A key agronomic distinction separates fiber-type hemp (THC < 0.3%) from drug-type cannabis, both of which are strongly influenced by flowering time9–11.

Flowering time in cannabis is primarily regulated by photoperiod, with most cultivars requiring 12–14 h of darkness to initiate flowering12–14. However, photoperiod sensitivity represents a complex polygenic trait, with varietal responses ranging from obligate short-day types to nearly day-neutral forms15,16. Plants integrate photoperiod and temperature signals through genetic networks of transcription factors, small RNAs, and epigenetic regulators to fine-tune flowering time2,17–20.

The classification of cannabis phenology has recently undergone a significant transition toward more integrated, photoperiod-based frameworks. Ansari et al.21 proposed a novel classification for industrial hemp into four functional groups: Photoperiod-Sensitive Pioneers (PSPs), with pronounced short-day responsiveness suited to high latitudes; Intermediate Photoperiod Adapters (IPAs), characterized by latitudinal flexibility and broad environmental adaptability; Solstice-Triggered Hybrids (STHs), whose flowering onset is driven more by thermal unit accumulation after the summer solstice than by day length alone; and Biomass Maximisers (BMax), distinguished by reduced photoperiod sensitivity and exceptional vegetative growth capacity. Such systematic categorization offers a practical foundation for aligning genotype selection with specific environmental conditions and production objectives.

Within the broader photoperiod response spectrum of cannabis, three functionally distinct flowering classes are recognized: autoflowering accessions, which exhibit photoperiod-insensitive (day-neutral) flowering behavior independent of light cues; and early- and late-flowering accessions, both of which are photoperiod-sensitive but differ in the duration of their vegetative phase and the degree of short-day responsiveness22,23.

Recent molecular studies have identified key genetic loci controlling flowering time in cannabis. Toth et al.23 identified two significant loci: Autoflower1, associated with photoperiod insensitivity, and Early1, which promotes early flowering. Dowling et al.59 linked the photoperiod insensitivity of the FINOLA cultivar to a duplication of the FLOWERING LOCUS T gene (FT1), termed Autoflower2. Petit et al.25 identified multiple QTLs associated with flowering time, implicating pathways regulating photoperiod sensing, circadian rhythms, and hormone signaling.

Integrating photoperiod-based classification with genomic and morphophysiological data offers a powerful framework for predicting flowering behavior and optimizing variety selection across diverse environments16,21. The strategic deployment of photoperiod-sensitive and insensitive genotypes across agroecological zones enhances the resilience of cropping systems under climatic uncertainty. Machine learning approaches are increasingly used to integrate genomic and phenotypic datasets to classify complex traits and identify key discriminative markers, bridging classification frameworks with practical breeding tools26,27.

This study aimed to characterize flowering-time diversity among 25 Iranian cannabis landrace populations via integrated morphophysiological and genomic analyses. By analyzing 145 accessions through high-density sequencing and weekly phenotypic monitoring, we employed machine learning to identify the primary drivers of flowering-time variation. Our integrated analysis of 234,002 features led to the identification of 53 key discriminative markers that effectively classify auto-, early-, and late-flowering accessions. Importantly, this approach revealed two novel genomic loci, Autoflower3 (CsFTL3) and CircadianFloweringLocus1 (CsCFL1), associated with photoperiod-insensitive flowering. A further complication is that auto-flowering and early-flowering plants are phenotypically indistinguishable during vegetative growth, as both produce solitary flowers at sexual maturity; it is only under long-day conditions that terminal inflorescence development reveals the day-neutral genotype. The findings provide a detailed characterization of photoperiod-based flowering classes and offer effective tools for early selection and targeted breeding under diverse environmental conditions.

Methods

Plant materials

Cannabis seed samples from 25 native populations (Table S1) of Iran were sourced from local markets and native farmers in various regions, ensuring genetic diversity and regional adaptation. The regions from which the seeds were collected encompassed five defined climatic zones (BSh: arid, steppe, hot; BSk: arid, steppe, cold; BWh: arid, desert, hot; BWk: arid, desert, cold; Cfa: temperate, without dry season, sizzling summer) based on the Köppen-Geiger climate classification, with geographic latitudes between 25° and 40° North and longitudes between 45° and 65° East19.

Experimental design and growing conditions

Twenty seeds from each collected population were planted19. Thirty days after sowing (DAS), the seedlings were transferred to growth bags with a mixture of garden soil, leaf mold, sand, and perlite in a proportion of 3:1:1:1. Based on the study by Amaducci et al. (2008), the planting was done 60 days before the photoperiod switch-off in Mashhad, Khorasan, Iran when the days start to shorten (the day length at sowing was 13 h and 17 min) to ensure that the plants would have an adequate vegetative period before entering the reproductive phase. As shown in Fig. S1, on the 60th DAS, the maximum day length (14 h and 29 min) was considered the start of the reproductive phase, with the period before it being regarded as the vegetative phase.

The plants were arranged in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three blocks and five observations, totaling 375 plants, in the greenhouse complex of Ferdowsi University of Mashhad, Iran (36°16’N, 59°36’E, an altitude of 985 m). According to Spatial Analysis (SA), each unit, measuring 60 square meters, was divided into 25 rows and 15 columns, so that each block included 5 columns. The aisles within each unit were arranged so that 16 plants were placed per square meter. At the beginning of the reproductive stage (appearance of solitary flowers), male and female plants were separated and transferred into a unit with similar conditions, thereby reducing the number of plants per square meter by half. The growing conditions and all cultural practices were previously described in Babaei et al.19.

Data collection

Determination of morphophysiological traits

The first data collection was conducted after plant establishment (30 DAS), followed by weekly individual measurements carried out for 13 weeks on female plants and 11 weeks on male plants. Since the auto-flowering populations (IR7385 and IR2845) had a very short growth period, they had a completely distinct growth pattern. For this reason, female plants were harvested in the 10th week of data collection (93 DAS), while male plants were harvested in the 6th week (63 DAS).

The measured traits in this experiment included Stem Diameter (SD), plant Height (H), Growth Rate (GR), the Number of Nodes (NN) on the main stem, Length of Internode in the Middle Third of the Main Stem (LI-MT), and SPAD-based chlorophyll (SPAD). The SD at 5 cm above the soil surface was measured using a caliper with an accuracy of 0.01 mm. The H and LI-MT were measured using a tape with an accuracy of 0.01 m. In addition, the NN on the main stem was counted. The chlorophyll index was measured using a SPAD-502 m (Konica, Minolta, Tokyo), with SPAD readings taken from three leaves (top, middle, and bottom) of each plant, and the average was calculated for each individual. Genotyping

Sampling was performed from healthy and young leaves of all individuals. Samples were then dried using the Freeze Dry System Alpha 2–4 LD plus for 24 h. A total of 145 pooled samples, each sample representing a unique combination of males and females from each population, were ground with metallic beads in a RETSCH MM 400 mixer mill (Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). DNA extraction was carried out using the Qiagen DNeasy® Plant Mini Kit. The extracted DNA quantity was assessed using a Qubit fluorometer with the dsDNA HS assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), and their quality was randomly evaluated using agarose gel electrophoresis. DNA concentrations were adjusted to 10 ng/µl for all samples. Final DNA samples were used to prepare HD-GBS libraries with BfaI as described in Torkamaneh et al.30 at the Institut de biologie intégrative et des systèmes (IBIS), Université Laval, QC, Canada. Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with 150 paired end reads at the Genome Quebec Service and Expertise Center (CESGQ), Montreal, QC, Canada.

SNP calling and filtration

Sequencing data were processed with Fast-GBS v2.0 using the C. sativa cs10 v2 reference genome (GenBank acc. no. GCA_900626175.2)31,32. For variant calling, a minimum of 6 reads is required to call a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP). Raw SNP data were then filtered with VCFtools to remove low-quality SNPs (QUAL < 10 and MQ < 30) and variants with a proportion of missing data exceeding 80%33. Missing data imputation was performed with BEAGLE 4.134, followed by a second round of filtration, retaining only biallelic variants with heterozygosity less than 50% and a minor allele frequency (MAF) of > 0.05. Variants residing on unassembled scaffolds were removed35.

Machine learning (ML) datasets

We employed the ML framework introduced by Abdelwahab et al.36 to identify the most important features for distinguishing the three flowering time clades. This framework is schematically represented in Fig. 1. The populations were categorized into three flowering clades based on the number of DAS to the “Start 10% Flowering Time in Individuals” (10% of bracts formed) (SF10I): auto (60–80 days for females, 40–60 days for males), early (80–115 days for females, 60–95 days for males), and late (115–140 days for females, 95–120 days for males)19. This classification reflects both timing and photoperiod response: auto-flowering accessions-initiated flowering before the photoperiod switch-off, consistent with photoperiod-insensitive behavior, while early- and late-flowering accessions are both photoperiod-sensitive, differing primarily in the length of their vegetative phase22.

The dataset used in this framework included data on six key morphophysiological traits (SD, H, GR, NN, LI-MT, and SPAD) collected over 13 weeks for female plants and 11 weeks for male plants, along with a catalog of 233,624 high-quality SNPs obtained for 145 accessions: 73 male and 72 female plants. This accession panel comprised 12 auto-flowering, 66 early-flowering, and 67 late-flowering accessions. Environmental data, including the average daily temperature based on growing degree-days (GDD °C), described in equation (Eq. 2), and day length over 150 days, were also used. Machine learning (ML) algorithms

In this framework, four ML algorithms were employed: Mutual Information (MI), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), and Random Forest (RF)38–41. The MI examines the relationship between features and classes, as well as the redundancy among features. To address binning, an estimator with K-neighbors (k = 3) was used. In this framework, MI, as a filter-based Feature Selection (FS) technique, initially sorts features based on their importance and value. Features with very low or zero MI were placed at the bottom of the list. Consequently, the top 1,000 features were selected and evaluated in SVM (MI-SVM) to determine the optimal feature set. SVM with a linear kernel was employed to evaluate the classification performance of features selected by MI and RFE.

The RFE model, a wrapper-based FS technique, was used to identify the optimal subset of features that achieve the highest performance by creating a subset of features at each stage and retaining the best subset. Features were then ranked based on their removal. The criterion removal (i.e., SVM with a linear kernel (RFE-SVM)) was applied in the predictive model. In this framework, the algorithm was set to remove 50% of features in each iteration. Specifically, the training data was divided into two equal parts in each iteration, and the features that achieved the highest score in SVM were retained.

The RF model based on the embedded feature selection strategy was used in this framework. Unlike filter-based (MI) and wrapper-based (RFE) methods, feature selection in the RF model was part of the model-building process. This model created multiple decision trees through the Classification and Regression Trees (CART) method, combined with the bagging technique42. Each decision tree resulted in a specific prediction, and in this framework, up to 1,000 trees were used to identify the best subset of features36.

Candidate region identification and gene annotation

To identify genomic regions associated with flowering time variation, linkage disequilibrium (LD) and haplotype blocks (HB) were conducted using PLINK v1.90b5.347. Pairwise LD (r²) values were calculated for all biallelic SNPs across each chromosome using the parameters ‘– r2 –ld-window-r2 0’, enabling full-range LD estimation. HBs were inferred with the ‘–blocks no-pheno-req –ld-window-kb 999’ setting, which defines LD blocks without requiring phenotypic data and allows wide genomic windows for block detection. The results were further processed for visualization using Haploview v4.1, with input formats converted using PLINK’s ‘–recode HV’ function48. Each HB was delimited by its most upstream (5’) and downstream (3’) SNP positions. Genes located within these intervals were considered putative candidates for regulating flowering behavior.

For gene annotation, functional interpretation of candidate regions was performed using SnpEff v5.2e, based on the Cannabis sativa reference genome (cs10 v2; GenBank accession: GCA_900626175.2)31,49. Gene ontology (GO) terms were extracted from the NCBI Cannabis sativa annotation release 100 to characterize the potential biological roles of the candidate genes. The physical distribution of candidate SNP markers across chromosomes was visualized using MG2C (v2.1; http://mg2c.iask.in/mg2c_v2.1/)50,51.

The data were first evaluated using the Outlier Grubbs approach to detect and remove existing outliers52. Subsequently, Minitab® Statistical Software 21 was employed to conduct skewness and kurtosis analyses, assessing the normality of the data and residual errors based on the distribution type53,54. Missing data were imputed using the CART method implemented in the AllInOne package in R software version 4.3.155,56. SA (AR1⊗AR1) was applied to account for potential spatial effects and minimize environmental impact (i.e., 15 columns and 25 rows). The data fitting approach considered population as a fixed factor and block as a random factor. Results

Weekly trends in morphophysiological traits in male and female cannabis plants

Weekly trends of six morphophysiological traits (SD, H, GR, NN, LI-MT, and SPAD) were examined over 13 weeks in female and 11 weeks in male plants across 25 native cannabis populations, starting 30 days after sowing (DAS).

The analytical temporal framework

The six traits exhibited distinct growth patterns, with population variance increasing progressively over the cultivation period, being minimal in early weeks and becoming more pronounced in later stages. Stem diameter (SD) ranged from 0.3 mm in the first week to 3.9 mm in female and 2.3 mm in male plants by the final week, with no single population consistently dominating across all weeks (Fig. 2A and B). Plant height (H) showed a consistent increasing trend, with late-flowering populations displaying notably higher values from week 6 onward in both sexes (Fig. 2C and D). Growth rate (GR) followed a characteristic pattern across all populations, with a distinct peak followed by a decline; female plants reached maximum GR in weeks 6, 7, 8, or 9, while male plants peaked in weeks 5, 7, or 9, enabling population clustering based on the timing of this growth peak (Fig. 2E and F). At three nodes, node number (NN) was uniform at three nodes across all populations in the first week, but it diverged progressively from week 5 onward (Fig. 2G and H). Internode length (LI-MT) showed increasing diversity across weeks, with late-flowering populations (i.e., IR2385 and IR4457) consistently maintaining the highest values from weeks 7–8 onward (Fig. 2I and J). The SPAD index approximately doubled between weeks 2 and 3 and remained relatively stable thereafter (ranging 6–15 in females and 6–18 in males), with late-flowering populations consistently showing the highest values throughout (Fig. 2K and L).

A framework to identify key biological features of auto, early, and late flowering plants in cannabis

Three ML feature selection techniques — MI-SVM, RFE-SVM, and RF — were integrated to identify key features distinguishing auto, early, and late flowering classes across 145 cannabis accessions. The MI model ranked 128,725 out of 234,002 features, with SVM achieving 86.2% weighted accuracy using the top four MI-ranked features; although three features reached the same accuracy, four were retained due to their higher frequency of occurrence as key predictors (Fig. S4A and Table S3). The RFE-SVM approach, starting from 1,000 initial features, achieved a remarkable 96.6% weighted accuracy with only 53 features (Fig. S4B and Table S4). The RF model reached 65.5% accuracy with an optimal configuration of 197 trees (Fig. S4C and Table S5). A comparison of precision, recall, specificity, balanced accuracy, F1-score, and ROC AUC across all three techniques is summarized in Table S6. Confusion matrix heatmaps comparing FS techniques in predicting auto, early, and late flowering accessions are shown in Fig. S5, and ROC AUC curves for FPR and TPR are illustrated in Fig. S6.

In total, 4 features were common across all methods (Fig. S7). Additionally, 24 features were identified by at least two FS techniques. Of the 53 features identified by RFE-SVM, 4 overlapped with MI-SVM and 28 with RF. On the other hand, MI-SVM and RF shared 4 common features (Table S7).

The final SVM classification model was constructed using three feature sets selected based on highest accuracy, performance, and minimal number of features: (a) 4 features common to all methods (LI-MT-RS8, LI-MT-RS9, LI-MT-RS10, and LI-MT-RS11) (Table S7); (b) 28 features identified by at least two methods, including the 4 common features (Table S7); and (c) 53 features selected by RFE-SVM comprising 31 morphophysiological markers and 22 unique SNP sites (Table S4). Feature set (a) achieved an accuracy of 86.2%, and ROC AUC of 0.917; set (b) achieved 86.2%, and ROC AUC of 0.955; and set (c) achieved the highest accuracy and ROC AUC of 96.6% and 0.994, respectively. Discussion

Cannabis landrace populations from diverse geographic origins have adapted to distinct environmental conditions, resulting in considerable genetic diversity underlying flowering time variation. From a photoperiod response perspective, cannabis can be broadly divided into photoperiod-sensitive types, which require specific day-length cues to initiate flowering, and photoperiod-insensitive (autoflowering) types, which flower independently of photoperiod. Within these categories, populations can be further classified into three distinct flowering time classes — auto, early, and late — based on the timing of reproductive transition, with differences spanning several weeks between groups. The classification of accessions as autoflowering is based on their initiation of the main inflorescence prior to the experimental photoperiod switch-off, defined as the appearance of at least 10% of the main inflorescence at the shoot apex in female plants and the accumulation of staminate flowers at the rachis in male plants (SF10I)19. This distinction is biologically meaningful, as commercial pistillate flowering represents an obligate short-day response in photoperiod-sensitive cannabis, whereas autoflowering accessions undergo autonomous reproductive development independent of photoperiod58. The use of SF10I as the classification criterion thus ensures that observed early flowering reflects genuine photoperiod insensitivity rather than accelerated, but still photoperiod-dependent development58. A fundamental challenge in cannabis breeding is that all flowering classes exhibit remarkably similar morphological patterns during early vegetative stages, masking the underlying genetic and physiological differences that ultimately dictate flowering timing. Since this sequential flowering pattern typically emerges only 8–12 weeks after germination, breeders cannot predict flowering class from vegetative characteristics alone, creating a significant bottleneck for early selection.

We addressed this challenge by integrating temporal phenotyping with genomic profiling. Weekly tracking of six morphophysiological traits across 145 accessions throughout their full growth cycle, combined with high-density SNP genotyping, revealed that while flowering classes appear phenotypically uniform, but subtle differences in growth dynamics emerge during the vegetative-to-reproductive transition. Several genetic markers were exclusive to auto-flowering accessions and localized to chromosomal regions harboring autoflowering alleles, including two novel loci — Autoflower3 (CsFTL3) and CircadianFloweringLocus1 (CsCFL1) — identified in this study. These markers enable pre-flowering classification, providing a practical solution for early selection in cannabis breeding programs.

The three flowering classes exhibited distinct developmental trajectories shaped by their responses to photoperiod and internal physiological cues. The clear separation of the auto-flowering group in feature distributions reflects a pronounced developmental divergence driven by genetic adaptations such as photoperiod insensitivity8,23,59, consistent with the day-neutral characteristics of auto-flowering landraces adapted to short-season environments14,29. In contrast, the overlapping feature distributions between early- and late-flowering groups suggest shared genetic ancestry or less pronounced differentiation in photoperiod-sensitive pathways, complicating their classification25,60. While both early- and late-flowering groups are quantitative short-day plants sharing a similar critical photoperiod threshold (~ 12–14 h daylength), they differ primarily in their sensitivity to sub-optimal photoperiods and in the duration of the photoperiod-induced phase (PIP): late-flowering accessions require a more prolonged exposure to progressively shortening days to initiate flowering, whereas early-flowering accessions respond to a less stringent photoperiodic threshold and complete the basic vegetative phase more rapidly15. Nevertheless, these classes remain genetically distinct, with divergence governed by rare alleles and differences in resource allocation — late-flowering types prioritizing an extended vegetative phase with increased stem thickness and plant height. Given the dioecious nature and high heterozygosity of cannabis, the ML framework proved essential for detecting these underlying genetic signatures and resolving complex multi-locus interactions across diverse landrace populations61–63.

Beyond classifying flowering types, our study provides insights into the weekly dynamics of morphophysiological traits and their genetic control. Broad-sense heritability (H²) varied notably across weeks, with pronounced shifts during the vegetative-to-reproductive transition, particularly around the photoperiod switch-off (weeks 5–8)2,64–66. A notable example was the SPAD index, where a 100% increase in chlorophyll content across all genotypes in week 3 coincided with a sharp decline in H², suggesting that an environmental cue triggered synchronized physiological responses independent of genotype during the juvenile growth stage15,67–69. Plant height and stem diameter showed diverse growth patterns reflecting varied vigor, with stem diameter ceasing growth after the switch-off in male plants, while female plants showed a renewed increase in diameter from five weeks post switch-off until harvest, consistent with previous reports70,71.

The stability of the SPAD index from week 3 until the end of the growth period suggests that photosynthesis remained relatively unaffected by the photoperiod switch-off, even as other growth indices were influenced. This refers to plants perceiving day-length change as a physiological cue that regulates the transition to flowering through photosynthetic product partitioning, consistent with photoperiodism’s role in plant development69,72–75. More broadly, temporal analysis revealed that while plants from different populations exhibited similar trait values in early growth, variability increased progressively over time, and this expanding divergence was critical for differentiating flowering-time classes using ML models. Although growth rate ranges between stages differed from those reported by Naim-Feil et al.66 — likely due to our use of genetically diverse seedlings rather than clonal cuttings76–78— the general trend of dynamic trait behavior remained consistent.

The observed divergence in morphophysiological traits across phenological stages is orchestrated by a broad network of genes interacting with environmental factors such as day length and temperature2,79,80, with circadian regulation ensuring synchronization of flowering and other developmental processes with seasonal cycles81,82.

The dynamic interactions among morphophysiological traits revealed by our correlation analyses further support the role of circadian and hormonal coordination in shaping flowering trajectories. Traits such as growth rate, height, and internode length showed interconnected responses across specific weeks, and understanding these interdependencies at critical weekly intervals is key to dissecting the complex physiology underlying different flowering times25,83,84.

The SNPs identified in this study revealed strong genetic signals associated with auto-flowering, offering new insights into the genetic control of photoperiod insensitivity in cannabis. Several markers showed exceptionally high classification performance (ROC AUC 96–100%) and were uniquely present in auto-flowering accessions, suggesting functional relevance. Among them, three closely linked SNPs on chromosome 8 — CsML17, CsML18, and CsML19 — were located within a shared ~ 3 kb haploblock exclusive to auto-flowering landraces. Given its unique structure and strong discriminatory power, we propose this region as a novel auto-flowering locus, designated Autoflower3 (CsFTL3). Both CsFTL3 and CsCFL1 were also identified through genome-wide association analysis (GWAS), where they were further characterized as pleiotropic loci influencing flowering time, morphological traits, and cannabinoid profiles, reinforcing their biological relevance across methodological frameworks8.

In cannabis, the photoperiod pathway is a major determinant of flowering, with genes such as CsFT2, a member of the PEBP gene family (FLOWERING LOCUS T homolog), linked to photoperiod sensitivity and flowering induction22,59,84. Previous studies have identified early-flowering loci in cannabis, such as Autoflower1 (Early1) on chromosome 1 in high-cannabinoid accessions23 and Autoflower2 (FT1) at the distal end of chromosome 8 in hemp cultivars59. In contrast, the markers comprising Autoflower3 (CsFTL3) (CsML17, CsML18, and CsML19), as identified in our study, are located in a different genomic region. This divergence may reflect the influence of structural variation, gene duplication, or population-specific haplotypes that lead to the identification of distinct but functionally related loci across studies85–87. Additional high-confidence markers were also identified on chromosomes X, 1, and 9, including CsML03–07 (two different haploblocks on chromosome X, LOC115711146), CsML09 (chr1), and CsML21 (chr9, LOC115722557, CircadianFloweringLocus1 (CsCFL1)), all contributing to auto-flowering classification. These findings support the hypothesis that autoflowering in cannabis arises from a polygenic and structurally diverse genetic architecture. This complexity is further reinforced by pangenomic studies showing that structural variations (SVs)—such as duplications, inversions, and translocations—and transposable elements (TEs) can modulate gene regulation and contribute to functional diversity across accessions, as also seen in Humulus lupulus24,59,87–90.

Hormonal pathways also contribute significantly to flowering regulation, with gibberellins (GA3) and auxins (IAA) playing central roles in this developmental transition91–94. In cannabis, the interplay between hormonal regulation and morphological traits such as internode length, a key marker identified in this study, reflects the integration of genetic and hormonal signals29,94,95. Under short-day (SD) conditions, reduced GA3 levels in the shoot apex have been shown to correlate with compact inflorescences and shorter internodes, a characteristic also observed in our study83. Furthermore, exogenous application of GA3 has been reported to mimic long-day (LD) conditions, resulting in elongated internodes and disassembly of condensed inflorescences94,96. Similarly, changes in auxin levels under SD conditions may contribute to altered branching patterns and reduced internode elongation, though their precise role requires further investigation83,97. Selective breeding has shaped the hormonal regulation and morphology of cannabis, with traits like internode length directly influencing flowering patterns. Fiber cultivars, driven by higher GA3 activity, develop elongated internodes and exhibit delayed flowering, while seed-propagated cultivars, with reduced GA3 levels, show compact growth and earlier flowering transitions83,97–99.

Developmental timing mechanisms, such as those involving microRNAs like miR156 and miR172, mediate this phase transition by targeting transcription factors in GA3 signaling pathways22,25. These microRNAs have been shown to regulate traits such as internode elongation and flowering in response to environmental and hormonal signals, providing a fine-tuned balance between growth and reproduction22,100. The interplay of CsFTL3 and CsCFL1 with these developmental pathways could help explain the observed diversity in flowering patterns.

Conclusion

A major challenge in cannabis breeding is the inability to distinguish auto, early, and late flowering classes during vegetative stages, when plants appear morphologically similar despite underlying genetic differences in photoperiod sensitivity. This study addressed this challenge through integrated temporal phenotyping and genomic profiling of 25 diverse Iranian landrace populations. Machine learning functioned as the computational framework to integrate these high-dimensional datasets, enabling effective feature selection and reduction from 234,002 variables into a predictive model. By identifying 53 key markers — 31 morphophysiological and 22 genomic — we enable pre-flowering classification with 96.6% accuracy eliminating the 8–12 week waiting period currently required to determine flowering class.

In practice, these markers allow for “smart” selection at the seedling stage to save time and resources. This precision screening is the core of chronoculture in a “smart crop” context for cannabis, as it helps growers align the specific nutritional and environmental needs of each plant with its predicted flowering behavior. Beyond breeding applications, our findings contribute to understanding cannabis flowering biology by demonstrating that variation in flowering time reflects complex interactions among genetic architecture, developmental timing, and environmental cues. The identified loci, including the novel Autoflower3 (CsFTL3) and CircadianFloweringLocus1 (CsCFL1), combining photoperiod-based classification with molecular markers and growth dynamics, provide a foundation for targeted breeding programs that match specific flowering types to appropriate environmental conditions, supporting more efficient and sustainable cannabis cultivation across diverse climates.
