Pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum (PAIVS) and critical pulmonary stenosis are congenital heart diseases with ductal-dependent pulmonary circulation.1, 2, 3 Many patients, especially those with PAIVS, have enormous morphologic diversity of the right ventricle, pulmonary valve, and tricuspid valve.4,5 Such heterogeneity has raised a wide variety of treatment strategies.6 In some cases, a transcatheter approach may be necessary to maintain arterial ductal patency after a successful right ventricular decompression. In clinical practice, coronary stents are used in these newborns. However, the use of drug-eluting stents (DES) is still considered off label in the pediatric population, and their long-term effects are still unknown in small children.Take-Home Message•The case highlights new strategies and materials in interventional pediatric cardiology for the treatment of ductal-dependent pulmonary circulation.

Very recently, iron-based bioresorbable stents (BRS) have been available as a promising alternative to conventional metal stents for ductal stenting. These devices are designed to provide temporary scaffolding to maintain ductal patency, gradually degrading over time as the need for ductal patency diminishes or as the patient transitions to definitive surgical repair. Compared with metallic stents, which often require surgical removal or which may interfere with future interventions, BRS offer the advantage of temporary support without leaving permanent foreign material in situ.7

Case Presentation

A full-term male infant, born via spontaneous delivery after an uneventful pregnancy (gestational age: 37 weeks + 4 days, body weight: 3,170 g), developed profound postnatal desaturation unresponsive to hyperoxia. Initial echocardiography showed critical pulmonary valve stenosis, a hypertrophic and bipartite right ventricle with intact interventricular septum, and a patent ductus arteriosus (Figures 1 and 2). After a multidisciplinary discussion, it was decided to proceed with a percutaneous approach that included pulmonary valvuloplasty and ductal stenting. Cardiac catheterization was performed using 2 femoral accesses (a 5-F femoral vein and a 4-F femoral artery). Angiography showed a hypertrophic right ventricle with very diminutive trabeculated apical portion. The pulmonary valve appeared thickened and dysplastic, with only a tiny antegrade blood flow with a valvular anulus measuring about 5 mm.

Pulmonary valvuloplasty was performed using a Tyshak Mini balloon (7 × 20 mm) (NuMED), with good results; postprocedural angiography showed satisfactory antegrade flow.

Given the marked restrictiveness of the right ventricle, the decision was made to stent the duct with a bioresorbable scaffold. Angiography confirmed the presence of a straight ductus arteriosus, measuring about 2 mm on the pulmonary side and 4 mm on the aortic side (Figure 3).Figure 3Angiography (Lateral Projection) Demonstrates a Patent Ductus Arteriosus of Good Caliber With Mild Distal Narrowing at the Pulmonary End

No procedure-related complications occurred. Antiplatelet therapy with aspirin (15 mg) and clopidogrel (0.6 mg) was started.

Postoperatively, the infant remained hemodynamically stable and was extubated the day after. At the first follow-up after 3 months, the baby was doing well, with oxygen saturation 95%. Discussion

Critical pulmonary stenosis may require ductal patency for additional pulmonary blood flow. In some cases, even after an adequate valvuloplasty, the antegrade flow is not sufficient, either because of poor compliance of the right ventricle or because it is not completely formed.

In the current era, a number of coronary platform stents are available to the pediatric interventional cardiologist, including bare metal stents (BMS), DES, and second-generation DES. In a recent study in infants who underwent patent ductus arteriosus stent placement for ductal-dependent pulmonary blood flow, fluoropolymer-coated everolimus-eluting stents offered promise over BMS, with less intimal proliferation, less need for unplanned reinterventions, and similar pulmonary artery growth to BMS.8

However, some concern remains over the potential immunosuppressive effects of DES. In a study of neonates receiving sirolimus-eluting stents, peak sirolimus levels reached immunosuppressive levels, although no clinically significant adverse outcomes were observed.9

BRS are designed to provide temporary scaffolding to maintain vessel patency and then gradually degrade, reducing the possibility of long-term risks of stent-related complications These stents have recently received the European Union compliance mark and are available for ductal use.

In our case, the intrinsic degradability of the stent, with the consequent possibility of spontaneous ductal closure after a few months, could temporarily compensate for the ductal dependency as the right ventricle develops, which could ultimately allow the achievement of a biventricular circulation without any other reinterventions.

In our early experience, the BRS has shown excellent handling, trackability, and delivery profile. The device is radiolucent, with 2 radiopaque platinum markers that allow easy visualization.

In ductal-dependent congenital heart disease with dual-source pulmonary blood flow, BRS can provide a temporary or permanent therapeutic option, or they can serve as a bridge to more definitive future interventions. This procedure represents a short- to medium-term solution for a carefully selected group of patients. In particular, it may be considered in those with potential recovery of right ventricular diastolic function, thereby allowing deferral or avoidance of a more invasive surgical procedure. Moreover, the iron-based alloy of the stent undergoes gradual resorption within approximately 6 months.

As a matter of fact, the iron undergoes a physiological oxidation in the vascular environment, and the degradation products are subsequently internalized by macrophages and processed through the physiological iron metabolism. As this is the same process of iron metabolization from hemoglobin catabolism, it should cause no adverse effects in the short, medium, or long term.

The main concern for the use of the BRS is the shorter duration of palliation owing to rapid neointimal hyperplasia, as seen in pilot studies.10 However it is too early to draw conclusions on this aspect from our experience.

Conclusions

In this report, we present to our knowledge the first case in Europe regarding the use of a BRS to maintain ductal patency in a patient with critical pulmonary stenosis with dual pulmonary blood supply. BRS offer theoretical advantages over traditional metal stents, including the potential to reduce long-term complications such as vessel distortion, as well as noninterference with future surgical or interventional procedures. Despite these promising features, the clinical efficacy and safety of BRS remain to be fully established, especially in the pediatric population. Further validation through larger, prospective studies and continued technological refinement is essential to define their optimal role in the management of ductal-dependent congenital heart disease.
