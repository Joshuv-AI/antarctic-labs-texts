Adolescents’ health literacy is directly associated with their physical activity but indirectly with their body composition and cardiorespiratory fitness: mediation analysis of the Slovak HBSC study data
Authors: Lenka Hnidková, Peter Bakalár, Rastislav Magda, Peter Kolarčik, Jaroslava Kopčáková, Zuzana Boberová
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth literacy is a core public health issue in relation to children and adolescents associated with multiple health behaviours and health outcomes. The aim of the study is to test the direct associations between health literacy, physical activity behaviour, health outcomes of body composition and cardiorespiratory fitness among Slovak adolescents and possible indirect effect of health literacy on health outcomes of body composition and cardiorespiratory fitness mediated by adolescents’ physical activity behaviour.MethodsData from the Slovak Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) study conducted in 2022 were used. For the purposes of this study, a subsample of the adolescents (n = 508; mean age = 14.50; SD = 0.82; 54.3% boys) which provided HBSC questionnaire data on health literacy, moderate-to-vigorous physical activity and vigorous physical activity and participated in body composition (InBody 230) and cardiorespiratory fitness (20-m shuttle run test) measurements. Data were analysed using linear regression analysis.ResultsThe findings showed that higher health literacy of the adolescents was directly associated with higher frequency of physical activity represented by moderate-to-vigorous physical activity and vigorous physical activity and only with the visceral fat area in the crude model. Furthermore, there was an indirect effect of health literacy on cardiorespiratory fitness and most of the body composition variables (except the Body Mass Index) which was mediated by physical activity of the respondents.ConclusionsHealth literacy is indirectly associated to body composition and cardiorespiratory fitness through higher frequency of physical activity. It seems that health literacy as cognitive and social competencies need behavioural components to be involved in the proposed causal pathway between health literacy and health outcomes. Our findings may contribute to the process of creating a framework for future health literacy interventions in adolescents.Keywords: Health literacy, Health behaviour, Health outcomes, Adolescents, Adiposity, 20-metre shuttle run

Background

Health literacy (HL) as a set of “the cognitive and social competencies which determine the motivation and ability of individuals to gain access to, understand and use information in ways which promote and maintain good health” [1] is also a core public health issue in relation to children and adolescents and should be embedded in health promotion and prevention strategies [2]. HL has been associated with various children’s and adolescents’ health behaviours, including physical activity (PA) [3–5]. However, the evidence of its association with PA among adolescents are scarce and mixed. While some studies conclude positive association [6–8], other studies conclude no association [9–11]. Moreover, there is strong research evidence about the relation between physical activity and its health benefits, especially in relation to body composition outcomes [12, 13]. However, there is no research whether this relationship can be also mediated by the level of HL. Thus, broader research is needed.

HL was also associated with different health outcomes as well, such as overweight and obesity [14], but the evidence are inconsistent too. Some studies so far have identified the negative associations between HL and obesity or higher Body Mass Index (BMI) status [15–17]. Conversely, some studies did not find significant associations between BMI categories and HL [18–21]. However, only few studies have been conducted regarding the relation between obesity and overweight and their factors in adolescents and their HL level [14, 22, 23]. To our knowledge, there is no research regarding the association between adolescents’ HL and device-measured body composition variables which are considered to be more valid indicators of adiposity in adolescents than BMI [24–28]. While BMI is commonly used as a measure of body fatness, to evaluate underweight, overweight and obesity [29], it has serious limitations for characterization of adiposity, particularly in school-aged children [30, 31] where children with the same BMI may have very different fat and fat-free mass distribution [32].

Another health outcome possibly associated with HL is cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) but to our knowledge, there is no study focused on this association among adolescents despite the fact that CRF level of children and adolescents is an important indicator of current and future health [33]. Moreover, similarly to overweight and obesity, low CRF is an important contributor to the economic burden of illness. As it was recently estimated, only in Canada the total annual economic burden of low CRF in Canadian adults was CAD$3.6 billion, representing 2.7% of the overall Canadian burden of illness costs in 2021 [34].

Contrary to mixed evidence regarding the association between HL and overweight and obesity, the evidence about the inverse association between PA and overweight and obesity is consistent [35, 36]. Higher levels of habitual PA are protective against child and adolescent obesity [37] and aerobic PA of moderate-to-high intensity can assist people with obesity to achieve recommended weight loss [38]. At the same time, PA is consistently associated with CRF, which is influenced by several factors, including body fatness, growth, sexual maturation, age, sex, health status and genetics, yet its principal modifiable determinants are moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA) and sedentary behaviour [39].

Based on mentioned above, we would like to answer the question, whether the adolescent’s HL is associated with health behaviour represented by physical activity and health outcomes represented by the body composition and CRF. It is questioned whether the HL is directly related to health outcomes regardless of one’s behaviour or there is an indirect relation through health behaviour.

The aim of our study is therefore to test the direct associations between HL, physical activity behaviour, health outcomes of body composition and CRF among Slovak adolescents, taking into consideration their age and gender, and possible indirect effect of HL on health outcomes of body composition and CRF mediated by adolescents’ physical activity behaviour.

Methods

Sample and procedure

We used data from the Slovak Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) study conducted in 2022 in Slovakia [40]. Three-step sampling was used to obtain a representative sample. First, 195 larger and smaller elementary schools from rural and urban areas were randomly selected from a list of all eligible schools in Slovakia obtained from the Slovak Institute of Information and Prognosis for Education and asked to participate. In the end, 94 schools agreed to participate (response rate was 48%). Second, data from 9697 adolescents from the 5th to 9th grades of elementary schools in Slovakia were obtained using the HBSC survey (mean age 13.4 ± 1.3; 50.9% boys). Last, 1258 of surveyed adolescents (from approx. 13% of the total number of participating schools) participated in body composition and CRF measurements. Final sample consisted of 508 adolescents which provided both questionnaire data on HL and participated in above-mentioned measurements (mean age = 14.50; SD = 0.82; 54.3% boys).

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty at P. J. Šafárik University in Košice (13/N2021). Parents or legal guardian were informed about the study via the school administration and their informed consent was obtained for study participation.

Moreover, participants were also informed about the study in advance by their teachers and explaining the option to refuse to participate at the time of data collection by the HBSC administrator. Participation in the study was fully voluntary and anonymous with no explicit incentives provided for participation.

The body composition and CRF measurements were conducted by the trained HBSC administrators. The measurements were done in the premises of the school gym, the measurements of the body composition in the dressing rooms (boys and girls separately) and CRF in the school gym itself (boys and girls together).

Measures

Health literacy among school aged children (HLSAC)

The purpose of HLSAC measure is to examine adolescents’ perceived knowledge and competencies to make sound health decisions, and to work on and change the factors that constitute their own and others’ health chances [41]. Specifically, HLSAC measures subjective and general HL, it is not a domain-specific measure [42]. HLSAC was internationally validated for 13- and 15-year-olds [7] and was developed for broader international use. Validated Slovak version of the HLSAC was used in our study [43]. HLSAC covers 5 core components: theoretical (health) knowledge, practical (health) knowledge, critical thinking, self-awareness, and citizenship [44], and includes 2 items for each core component. All the items took the form “I am confident that.”, and the response options were (1) not at all true (2), not quite true (3), somewhat true, and (4) absolutely true. A sum-score was generated out of the responses to its 10 items, ranging from 10 to 40. HLSAC was used as a continuous variable without discrete cut-off points, with higher sum scores indicating higher levels of HL.

Moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA)

MVPA was measured by an item adapted for use in the HBSC study from the item developed by Prochaska et al. [45] for the purposes of clinical practice with adolescents. The authors validated it against seven-day continuous measurement using an accelerometer (r = 0.40, p < 0.001) and observed its substantial test–retest stability (intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) = 0.77). The item and its response categories are as follows:

Over the past 7 days, on how many days were you physically active for a total of at least 60 min per day? Please add up all the time you spent in physical activity each day. There were eight response categories: 0 days, 1 day, 2 days, 3 days, 4 days, 5 days, 6 days, 7 days.

Vigorous physical activity (VPA)

VPA explicitly encompasses a dimension of physical activity that taps into recreational pursuits, sports or hobbies in young people’s free time (outside of school) [46]. It was assessed by following item:

Outside school hours: How often do you usually exercise in your free time so much that you get out of breath or sweat? There were seven response categories: every day, four–six times a week, two times a week, three times a week, once a week, less than once a month and never.

Body composition

Body height (cm) was measured using a SECA 213 portable stadiometer. Before the measurement, the schoolchildren assumed an active upright posture, stood barefoot with their backs to the stadiometer, with their heads facing downwards in Frankfurt horizontal plane. The measurement was carried out during inspiration to the nearest 0,1 mm.

Measurement of body weight (kg; BW), and Skeletal Muscle Mass (kg; SMM), and calculations of Body Mass Index (kg/m2; BMI), percentage of body fat (%; PBF), and Visceral Fat Area (cm2; VFA) took place simultaneously during bioimpedance analysis of body composition (BIA) using InBody 230 device (Biospace Co., Ltd.). This BIA device has been shown to be reasonably accurate for prevalence studies in adolescents [47]. The calculation of Body Fat Mass Index (kg/m2; BFMI) and Fat Free Mass Index (kg/m2; FFMI) were calculated separately after using data from BIA. Schoolchildren were instructed to be dressed in training clothes, or to wear a T-shirt, pants, or a skirt at most. The starting weight was set to -0.3 kg to take into account that the schoolchildren were not weighed in their underwear.

Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF)

CRF was assessed by number of laps in 20-m shuttle run test (20mSRT), a progressive aerobic exercise test involving continuous running between 2 lines 20 m apart in time to audio signals. The test has moderate criterion validity against gas-analysed peak VO2 (mL/kg/min), high to very high test-retest reliability, and it is meaningfully associated with a variety of health indicators among children and adolescents [48].

Data analysis

As a first step, we tried to describe the main variables using frequency tables. We calculated means and standard deviations for continuous variables and prevalence for categorical variables. We also tested differences between boys and girls using Mann-Whitney U-test for continuous variables and Pearson chi2 test for categorical variables (Tables 1 and 2 respectively). Pearson’s correlation coefficients for all continuous variables with age are presented in Table 3 and interpreted according to guidelines provided by Boston University School of Public Health [49]. As a second step (see also Fig. 1), we calculated crude effects of HL on MVPA, VPA, body composition and CRF variables using linear regression. Afterwards we tested the effect of a combined regression model with HL and each PA variable separately (MVPA and VPA) onto the body composition and CRF variables. Thus, we obtained a mutually adjusted effect of HL and each PA variable. As a final step, we calculated the indirect effect of HL on body composition and CRF variables mediated with one of the PA variables.

Results

Descriptive statistics show that our respondents reported moderate levels [52] of HL and HL levels between boys and girls did not differ in statistically significant manner (Table 1). However, boys had statistically significantly higher average values of PA (both MVPA and VPA; Tables 1 and 2) and CRF and higher average values of FFMI, SMM and BMI compared to girls. Girls on the other hand had statistically significantly higher average values of BFMI and PBF. Differences in VFA did not reach the threshold for statistical significance (Table 1).

Correlation analysis showed no association between age and HL, and VFA (Table 3). Trivial or very small correlations were found between age and PBF, MVPA and VPA. CRF showed small positive correlation with age in the whole sample, and after stratification for gender we found it to correlate stronger, but still weakly, with age for boys (r = 0.321; p < 0.01) and very weakly or trivially among girls. We could conclude that CRF gets better with higher age among boys, but this association is not so strong among girls. We found a moderate or strong positive correlation of age with FFMI and SMM respectively in the whole sample and after stratification for gender, we noticed an increase in the correlation for both variables among boys and a slight decrease among girls. FFMI and SMM gets larger with higher age in boys and girls however among boys this association is more manifested.

Testing the associations of respondents’ HL with MVPA, VPA, and CRF showed that HL was a statistically significant predictor of MVPA and VPA. Higher HL was associated with higher levels of physical activity represented by those two variables. In addition, no association of HL was found with CRF (Table 4).

On the other hand, we found a statistically significant association between most of the body composition variables and MVPA and VPA. Only in the case of BMI, we did not find an association with MVPA and VPA. In general, we can conclude that more frequent MVPA and VPA are related to better values of body composition variables and CRF (e.g. lower VFA, higher SMM).

As a further step, we tried to test the association of HL to the same body composition variables and CRF but in this case, we adjusted it for the effect of MVPA or VPA respectively (Table 5, combined model). The adjustment did not show any improvement in the HL effect on body composition variables nor CRF. The association of HL and VFA lost its statistical significance and others remained statistically insignificant in all cases of adjusting variables.

As a last step, we tested the possibility that the effect of HL on body composition variables and CRF might be mediated by MVPA or VPA. Our models showed that the indirect effect of HL is statistically significant and is mediated by MVPA and VPA in the case of CRF and almost all body composition variables except the BMI (Table 5, last column).

Discussion

The findings showed that higher HL of the respondents was directly associated with higher frequency of PA represented by MVPA and VPA and only with the smaller VFA in the crude model. There was an indirect effect of HL on CRF and most of the body composition variables except the BMI, which was mediated by PA of the respondents. Our study showed a negative direct association between HL and the VFA value as only one of the body composition variables. Furthermore, we did not find any association, either direct or indirect, between HL and BMI index among our adolescents’ participants. This is in line with several previous studies that similarly did not confirm the relation with the BMI values [18–21]. Such findings might indicate its problematic usage among adolescents in comparison to other body composition variables. Similar observation was found by Kolb et al. [53] that using BMI as a health outcome has some limitations that need to be considered. BMI is an indirect measurement and the diagnostic performance of BMI to identify obesity showed high specificity, but moderate sensitivity in children and adolescents [31]. In case of monitoring the potential causality of HL on health outcomes, it would be meaningful to include other relevant body composition variables to minimise misleading effects. Moreover, it is the VFA value which is considered as more useful than BMI for detecting the risk of cardiovascular disease in the general population, because evidence showed that abdominal obesity and excess visceral adiposity highly correlated with cardiovascular disease [54, 55]. Such findings about the role of VFA also might be useful from the health promotion perspective when health intervention planners decide which overweight and obesity indicator to measure as the result of HL intervention.

From another perspective, the adolescence, especially in the age of 13- and 15-years, is the unique period of individuals’ life characterised by a rapid growth in body size and changes in body composition [56, 57] and this fact might be a reason why we were not able to see the relations of HL with other body composition variables. Age related changes might interfere with HL effect. The HLSAC tool is validated for 13- and 15-years old adolescents [7, 58] and physical maturation might overlay our findings. In future research it is needed to also investigate these relations in younger or older adolescents which are not in such physical body changes to get more complex insight.

We found HL as a statistically significant predictor of MVPA and VPA. This is in line with findings among the adult population [3, 5] and also adolescents [6, 8, 10] in another cultural environment. Moreover, the PA had a mediating role in association between adolescents’ HL and CRF and of most body composition variables except the BMI. This finding might have several explanations. First is that behind the association between levels of PA and health outcomes including BMI and different body composition variables, the HL may play a significant role in establishing PA as health behaviour and subsequently in different health outcomes. In other words, it indicates the role of HL between health behaviour and health outcomes. Some studies [59, 60] suggest a similar observation that HL does not solely influence health outcomes, but their interactions are more multifaceted than just a direct one-way effect. Next explanation is that our study measured general and subjective HL among adolescents [7, 61]. It understands HL as a perceived evaluation of one’s competencies. According to Sheeran et al. [62], particularly the perception of one’s competence is an important factor to promote health behaviour. Thus, this knowledge extends the scope of relations between HL and health outcomes among adolescents, and it emphasises the conative meaning of HL as an ability approaching health information adequately and to derive at health-promoting decisions and actions [44]. Further meaning of such knowledge is its implication for health promotion practice. In planning intervention to influence overweight and obesity and CRF among adolescents by enhancing their PA, it would be beneficial to build it on HL concept.

There are strengths and limitations that need to be considered in this study. To our knowledge, our study is one of the first using such a broader perspective in search for associations between HL and different health outcomes using device-measured body composition and measured CRF and asking about the role of physical activity in these associations. Moreover, study refers to a potential theoretical framework for HL interventions. Generally, most HL interventions lack theoretical embedding [63–65]. The theoretical conceptualization of HL measured in this study was based on the educational context [44] with description of ways to promote learning of individual HL key components [66]. That kind of theoretical educational concept may provide guidelines for the intervention design and materials. On the other hand, the main limitation was the cross-sectional design of this study, which did not allow us to formulate a conclusive statement regarding the causality of our results. Therefore, our findings need to be confirmed in longitudinal studies. Another limitation is that most of the respondents were physically very active and the studied relations may differ for less active respondents. Additionally, the physical activity behaviour was assessed using self-reported questionnaire which could have an impact on the quality of PA data.

Conclusion

HL is indirectly associated with body compositions and CRF through higher frequency of PA which could lead to more favourable body composition and CRF. It seems that HL as a set of cognitive and social competencies needs behavioural components to be involved in the proposed causal pathway between HL and health outcomes. Our findings may contribute to the process of creating a framework for future HL interventions in adolescents.
