Mechanistic insights into the delayed yellowing of broccoli by the combination of ectoine and lactide peptide
Authors: Ke Wu, Xiaozhen Yue, Zeyu Song, Chunjie Zhang, Jieyu Lv, Bangjin Yan, Mina Wang, Shuzhi Yuan, Xiaodi Xu, Jinhua Zuo, Aili Jiang, Qing Chen, Qing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreventing the postharvest yellowing of broccoli florets is a significant challenge. Our study investigated the effect of a combined treatment of ectoine and lactide peptide (ELP, soak treatment for 10 min) on the postharvest physiology and yellowing of broccoli. The treatment effectively reduced yellowing of the broccoli and inhibited the loss of nutrients. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses showed that the treatment with ectoine and lactide peptides reduced the accumulation of linoleic acid by downregulating the expression of LOX2 and EPHX2. Up-regulated the expression of HIS4 and down-regulated the expression of P4HA and PAO3, thereby synergistically promoting the accumulation of histidine and proline. Regarding flavonoid biosynthesis, CHS was upregulated and caffeoylquinic acid exhibited increased abundance. Results of the study enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the regulation of broccoli yellowing and demonstrate the potential use of a combined ectoine and lactide peptide treatment for the postharvest preservation of vegetables. Subject terms: Metabolomics, Biochemistry

Introduction

Lactose peptides, a bioproduct derived from corn sirup, contribute to plant growth, stress tolerance, and nutrient enhancement1. Lactose peptides have been shown to improve stress tolerance in cucumber, tomato, and cabbage by enhancing photosynthetic activity and reducing lipid peroxidation2,3. The ability to produce lactose peptide from corn sirup provides an ecofriendly approach for enhancing crop quality while supporting a value-added use of corn. Importantly, lactose peptides can be used to improve the tolerance of plants to environmental stresses, such as salinity and heavy metal toxicity. While much of the research on lactose peptides has focused on their effect on plant growth, few studies have explored their impact on the postharvest physiology of vegetables. Therefore, our objective was to investigate the potential use of lactose peptides as a postharvest treatment. Our initial findings suggest that combining lactose peptides with tetrahydropyrimidine (ecotoine) effectively inhibits the postharvest yellowing of broccoli.

Ectoine (1,4,5,6-tetrahydro-2-methyl-4-pyrimidinecarboxylic acid) is a natural compound synthesized by extremophilic bacteria for osmotic stress protection, where it maintains cellular hydration and reduces stress-induced damage to biomolecules4,5. Preclinical studies have indicated that the beneficial effects of ectoine are also displayed in animal models, as well as in humans. Ectoine is currently being widely used by cosmetic and pharmaceutical industries due to its efficacy and safety6–10. Notably, the antioxidative and macromolecular stabilizing properties of ectoine are being investigated for agricultural applications, including vegetable preservation. Recent studies indicate that ectoine mitigates oxidative stress in plants and alleviates the impact of abiotic stresses such as salt and heavy metals by preserving chlorophyll content and improving gas exchange properties11,12. Despite these promising properties, research on the use of ectoine in vegetable preservation remains limited and studies investigating ectoine in combination with other bioactive substances have not been conducted.

Broccoli (Brassica oleracea L.), a member of the Brassicaceae, is rich in bioactive compounds such as thioglucosides and phenolic compounds, as well as vitamins and minerals13. Broccoli has been widely recognized for its numerous health benefits, including its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and antimicrobial properties14. However, harvested broccoli is susceptible to yellowing, which negatively impacts consumer choice, nutrient content, and its overall economic value15. Therefore, it is crucial to develop methods that inhibit and delay the yellowing of broccoli.

The stress tolerance-enhancing properties of ectoine, combined with the nutrient-enhancing benefits of lactose peptides, represent a potentially novel approach to mitigating broccoli yellowing and prolonging its shelf life. Therefore, the present study investigated the effect of the combined application of ectoine and lactose peptides on the postharvest quality of broccoli. Physiological, genetic, and metabolomic analyses were used to investigate the mechanisms by which ectoine and lactose peptides impact stress tolerance, and overall postharvest quality. Insights gained from the study can be applied to the development of novel, effective strategies for preserving both the nutritional profile and shelf life of broccoli.

Results

Effect of ELP on the yellowing index, weight loss, and color

Representative photographs of the changes in broccoli appearance over the four-day storage period are presented in Fig. 1A. Little difference in the visual appearance of broccoli was observed between the control and ELP treatment groups on days 1 and 2 of storage. However, broccoli treated with the combination of ELP had a greener appearance, relative to the control group, on days 3 and 4 of storage (Fig. 1A). The yellowing index indicated that broccoli in the control began to show differences from the ELP-treated broccoli in the degree of yellowing as early as the first day of storage, after which the rate of yellowing in the control group increased rapidly (Fig. 1B). The ELP treatment group exhibited a 1-day delay in the onset of yellowing, relative to the control, and yellowing index was significantly lower (P < 0.05) in the ELP treatment group than it was in the control group on days 2–4 of storage. The proportion of weight loss in broccoli heads rose consistently during storage in both groups (Fig. 1C). However, the rate of weight loss in the control group accelerated quickly and was significantly higher (P < 0.01) than that in the ELP treatment group on days 3 and 4 of storage L*, a*, and b* values (Figs. 2D, E, F, respectively) of broccoli heads rose with storage time in both groups. However, the L* values in the ELP treatment group were significantly lower (P < 0.05) than those in the control group starting from day 2 of storage onward (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, a* values in the control and ELP treatment groups rose 3.42 and 2.79, respectively, over the course of storage, with the control group exhibiting higher, although non-significant, values from day 2 onwards (Fig. 2E). The b* values of broccoli heads rose in both groups throughout the storage period (Fig. 2F). However, beginning on day 2 of storage, b* values in the control group increased rapidly and were significantly higher (P < 0.05) than b* values in the ELP treatment group. On the last day of storage, b* values in the control and treatment group were 31.88 and 23.58, respectively.

Effect of ELP on respiration, ethylene generation, and relative conductivity

The respiration rate initially decreased and then rose during the storage period in both broccoli groups (Fig. 1G). No significant differences in respiration rates were found between the two groups on days 0 and 1 of storage. However, the respiration rate was significantly lower (P < 0.05) in the ELP treatment group than it was in the control group beginning on day 2 of storage. The rate of respiration peaked in both groups on day 4 of storage when the respiration rate was 205.32 mg CO2 kg−1 h−1 in the control group and 191.57 mg CO2 kg−1 h−1 in the ELP treatment group.

Changes in ethylene generation during storage in the two groups of broccolis are presented in Fig. 1H. Ethylene levels initially decreased and then increased in both groups. However, after two days of storage the level of ethylene generation dramatically increases in the control group, while ethylene levels in the ELP treatment remained relatively stable from days 1 to 3 of storage before increasing on day 4 of storage. Ethylene concentrations in the treatment group were considerably lower (P < 0.01) than those in the control group from days 2 to 4 of storage.

Electrolyte leakage, measured as relative conductivity, increased in both groups over the course of storage, with a notable surge after day 3 (Fig. 1I). However, the percentage relative conductivity in the ELP treatment group increased more slowly than it did in the control group and was significantly lower (P < 0.05) on days 3 and 4 of storage. By day 4 of storage, the relative conductivity in the control group was 1.96 times higher than in the ELP treatment group.

Effect of ELP on MDA, TSS, total phenol, vitamin C, and chlorophyll content

The joint treatment of broccoli with ELP reduced the rate of MDA buildup compared to the control (Fig. 2A). MDA levels were considerably lower (P < 0.01) in the ELP treatment group compared to the control throughout the entire storage period. MDA content in the control group increased from 0.87 mmol g−1 on day 0 of storage to 3.19 mmol g−1 on day 4 and to 2.29 mmol g−1 in the ELP treatment group.

TSS reduced over the storage period in both groups (Fig. 2B). The sole notable difference between the two groups occurred on day 3 of storage, when the TSS level in the ELP treatment group was significantly lower (P < 0.05) compared to the control. However, no differences in TSS were detected between the two groups on the day of storage when the experiment was terminated.

The total phenolic content in broccoli rose in both groups over the storage period, although it remained significantly higher (P < 0.01) in the ELP treatment group compared to the control throughout the entire storage duration (Fig. 2C). Phenolic content in the ELP treatment group on day 4 of storage was 39.86 g kg−1, compared to 23.83 g kg−1 in the control group.

Vitamin C levels also declined over the storage period in both broccoli groups (Fig. 2D). Nevertheless, vitamin C levels were significantly higher (P < 0.05) in the ELP treatment group compared to the control group on all storage days, except for the last day. Vitamin C content decreased by 41.05% over the course of storage in the ELP treatment group and 44.25% in the control group.

Chlorophyll content also decreased with storage time in both groups (Fig. 2E). However, like the other parameters measured, chlorophyll content in the ELP treatment group was significantly higher (P < 0.05) than in the control group from day 2 to day 4 of storage. Overall, chlorophyll content in the ELP treatment group declined by 52.6% over the course of storage and by 78.09% in the control group, which also displayed a more rapid rate of decline in chlorophyll content.

Effect of ELP on CAT, POD, and APX enzyme activity

CAT enzyme activity augmented with storage time in both groups (Fig. 2F). Both groups demonstrated a modest augmentation during the first two days of storage, with no significant disparity between them. However, significantly higher (P < 0.05) CAT activity was observed in the ELP treatment group than in the control group on days 3 and 4 of storage. Subsequently, CAT activity in the control group remained relatively static with a small increase; CAT activity in the ELP treatment group continued to display a linear increase. Overall, CAT activity surged by 80.7% in the ELP treatment group and by 77.3% in the control group.

POD activity declined with storage time in both groups (Fig. 2G). Remarkably, however, POD activity was consistently and markedly elevated (P < 0.05) in the ELP treatment group compared to the control group. After 4 days of storage, POD activity in the ELP and control treatment groups measured 3.57 and 1.41 units, respectively.

APX enzyme activity showed an initial increase, subsequently followed by a decrease throughout the storage period in both groups (Fig. 2H). APX activity in the ELP treatment group remained consistently and markedly elevated (P < 0.05) compared to the control group throughout the storage period. Both groups exhibited an apex in APX activity on day one of storage, with values of 347.67 and 275.67 U g−1 min−1 in the ELP and control treatment groups, respectively. However, APX activity then decreased rapidly in both groups during the remaining period of storage with values on day 4 of storage with values decreasing to 99.67 and 69.67 U g−1·min−1 in the ELP and control treatment groups, respectively.

Discussion

Harvested broccoli is susceptible to yellowing and senescence in transport and storage, resulting in a rapid loss of nutrients and a significant decrease in its monetary value16. Therefore, considerable research has focused on developing methods to delay broccoli yellowing. For example, Zhang et al.15 reported on the ability of an exogenous application of p-coumaric acid to delay broccoli yellowing, and previously Aiamla-or et al.17 investigated the effect of UV irradiation on the yellowing process. Notably, the application of exogenous substances can have varying effects on broccoli. In the present study, the ability of a combined solution of ELP to inhibit the yellowing process and its underlying mechanism was investigated. Results of the study indicated that broccoli gradually began to yellow with prolonged storage (20 ± 1 °C). However, broccoli heads immersed in an ELP solution prior to storage effectively maintained their organoleptic properties, color, and overall quality, relative to untreated broccoli heads. The ELP solution also inhibited respiration (Fig. 1), reduced nutrient losses, and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity. These findings indicate that the yellowing of broccoli was inhibited by the ELP treatment, potentially due to the protective effects of ectoine and lactic peptides, substances produced by microorganisms to resist extreme environments. Our results indicate that the ELP treatment may mitigate the rate of yellowing by modulating antioxidant capacity by regulating antioxidant enzyme activity, as well as other mechanisms.

Visual appearance and weight loss are general factors that are used to assess changes in broccoli quality. Our findings demonstrated that the extent of yellowing in the untreated broccoli was more pronounced compared to the ELP-treated broccoli, making the untreated broccoli less acceptable to consumers (Fig. 1A). Water plays a crucial biological and structural role in harvested fruits and vegetables18. In this investigation, ELP-treated broccoli exhibited lower percentage weight loss than untreated broccoli, which could be potentially attributed to the protective effects of ectoine and lactide peptides on cellular integrity (Fig. 1C). Notably, the control broccoli also exhibited a higher rate of respiration during storage than the ELP-treated broccoli, which would reflect a higher rate of metabolism and substrate utilization and also accelerated ripening and senescence processes19. Ethylene levels are highly correlated with broccoli senescence and induce floret yellowing in storage20. In our research, the ethylene production rate in the broccoli from the ELP treatment group was markedly lower than in the untreated broccoli, likely contributing to a reduction in the rate of floret yellowing (Fig. 1H).

The level of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in broccoli increased with storage time and led to lipid peroxidation and membrane damage21. MDA and electrolyte leakage (EL) are commonly used markers of stress-induced lipid peroxidation and membrane injury22. We demonstrated that soaking broccoli heads in a combined solution of lactide peptide and ectoine effectively reduced the level of ROS-induced membrane damage (Figs. 1I, 2A). Broccoli encompasses a range of vitamins and other bioactive compounds that augment its color, sensory attributes, and antioxidant potential during storage. Nutrient loss in ELP-treated broccoli during storage was lower than in control broccoli, indicating that the ELP treatment helped preserve the postharvest quality of broccoli during storage (Fig. 1B–E). Antioxidants allow fruits and vegetables to mitigate abiotic stress and slow the rate of senescence23. In this regard, antioxidant enzymes play an integral role in maintaining the green color in harvested broccoli24, with CAT, POD, and APX representing the main antioxidant enzymes25. These enzymes work interactively to reduce oxidative damage and delay senescence. Our results indicated that ELP-treated broccoli florets had elevated levels of POD, APX, and CAT activity compared to the control group (Fig. 2F–H). These results suggest that the exogenous application of ectoine and lactide peptides enhances antioxidant capacity and reduces ROS amassment in broccoli during storage. This may be due to the role of ectoine in maintaining redox balance and lactide peptides serving as nutrients. These properties would enhance the antioxidant capacity of broccoli and compensate for the loss of nutrients, such as vitamin C, during storage. We suggest that lactide peptide and ectoine either enhance the production and efficacy of other antioxidant compounds or directly act as antioxidants. Collectively, the impact of these compounds mitigates oxidative stress-induced membrane damage and delays broccoli senescence.

In addition to functioning as the fundamental constituents of proteins, amino acids perform a variety of other essential physiological roles in higher plants26. These other roles include the regulation of energy metabolism, as well as antioxidant, pigment, and nucleic acid metabolism, and also play a role in phytohormone signaling27. Consequently, amino acids play an integral role in the development and aging of harvested horticultural products. Histidine, a metal-binding amino acid, plays a crucial role in cellular metabolism, antifungal activity, and free radical scavenging28. Our study demonstrated that the ELP treatment enhanced the synthesis of histidine precursors such as imidazole-glycerol-3P and imidazole-acetol-P. This helped to maintain histidine levels in broccoli during storage, contributing to the maintenance of its overall quality and nutritional value. Proline, a diminutive and highly soluble α-imine, constitutes a crucial element of the non-enzymatic antioxidant system in plants and plays a significant role in plant responses to abiotic stress29,30. Arginine, serving as a precursor to proline, assumes a critical function in modulating plant responses to both biotic and abiotic stressors31. Our results suggest that the concurrent administration of ectoine and lactide peptides to broccoli down-regulated P4HA and PAO3, which inhibited proline degradation and maintained proline content in broccoli. The ELP treatment also up-regulated HIS4, resulting in increased histidine synthesis and enhanced free radical scavenging. The ELP treatment also inhibited the catabolism of arginine into compounds such as D-Nopaline and L-Arginine-P, which may have been the result of the enhanced metabolic conversion of arginine to N2-succinyl-glutamate and other precursors of glutamate biosynthesis. This would have resulted in increased glutamate accumulation. The enhanced accumulation of these various amino acids was associated with enhanced resistance of postharvest broccoli to oxidative injury and other stress-related factors32 and delayed senescence, which prolonged its shelf life.

Flavonoids, synthesized in a branch of phenylpropanoid metabolism, shield cells by neutralizing ROS and inhibiting pathogen-induced apoptosis33. Flavonols, flavanones, flavan-3-ols, and dihydroflavonols also function as accessory pigments that enhance and modify the color intensity of flowers34. Chalcone Synthase (CHS) is the primary enzyme in flavonoid biosynthesis and regulates the production of all flavonoids35. Hydroxycinnamoyl coenzyme A manganoyl/quinuclidinic acid hydroxycinnamoyltransferase (HCT) is pivotal in regulating lignin biosynthesis and its composition36. Our study indicated that the ELP treatment enhanced the antioxidant capacity of broccoli by up-regulating CHS, which increased the level of caffeoyl quinic acid and prunin, metabolites that mitigate both biological and environmental stresses37. The ELP treatment also boosted antioxidant capacity by down-regulating HCT and up-regulating E2.1.1.104, which would have inhibited lignin synthesis and promoted flavonoid production38. Anthocyanins, a prevalent class of flavonoids, are compounds that convey orange, pink, purple, and blue hues to plant flowers and other organs39. Tan et al.40 reported that gene expression associated with anthocyanin biosynthesis was correlated with color shifts in Gossypium herbaceum flowers, a species of cotton. Transcriptome data also revealed the downregulation of genes encoding ANS in ELP-treated broccoli, which would have inhibited anthocyanin synthesis in broccoli and delayed yellowing.

Arachidonic acid (ARA) is regarded as a highly conserved defense signaling compound in plants that modulates stress signaling pathways41. ARA has been demonstrated to enhance resilience against Aspergillus mold in lettuce and stimulate the accumulation of secondary metabolites, including polyphenols and anthocyanins42. Our combined transcriptomic and metabolomic data implied that the ELP treatment down-regulated EPHX2 curtailed the abundance of the ARA metabolite 5,6-DHET, and maintained ARA levels in broccoli. Linoleic acid (LA), an unsaturated fatty acid, plays a pivotal role in maintaining membrane fluidity under cold stress43. Lipoxygenase (LOX) governs the initial step in converting LA into other metabolites. High levels of LOX activity are detrimental to the accumulation of α-linolenic acid44. Our data demonstrated that the ELP treatment increased the accumulation of linoleic acid and down-regulated LOX2S, which inhibited the production of linoleic acid metabolites such as γ-linolenate and 9,10-DHOME. Thus, the ELP treatment effectively preserved the probity of the cell membrane and delayed senescence.

Harvested broccoli was immersed in an ELP solution composed of a combination of ectoine and lactic peptide. The ELP treatment reduced the respiration rate, inhibited ethylene production, enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity, and increased phenolic content in stored broccoli. These effects were confirmed in our analysis of physiological and biochemical parameters, as well as by our transcriptomic and metabolomic data. The outcomes of the study illustrated that exposure of harvested broccoli to a combined solution of ectoine and lactide peptides mitigated ROS accumulation and membrane damage. The study also offered revelations into the role of amino acids, flavonoids, arachidonic acid, and linoleic acid in broccoli senescence. The findings of the current study revealed that the engagement of harvested broccoli heads in a combined solution of ectoine and lactide peptides prior to storage reduced nutrient losses, delayed yellowing, and maintained the overall quality of broccoli and prolonged its shelf life. Thus, ELP should be considered as a biopreservative for broccoli.

Methods

Plant material and sample treatments

Broccoli heads (Brassica oleracea var. Italica) were harvested at commercial maturity from a farm located in the Shunyi District, Beijing, China. Only uniformly mature and similarly sized heads with compact, healthy inflorescences were harvested, which were then promptly transported to the laboratory for use in the experiment after field heat had dissipated. The harvested broccoli heads were first sanitized by soaking them in a 5 mL L-1 sodium hypochlorite solution for 10 min, after which they were air dried. The broccoli heads were randomly assigned to two groups. One group was immersed in a solution containing 2 mL L−1 ectoine and 6 mL L−1 lactide peptide complex (ELP group) for 10 min, and the other group was placed in distilled water for an equal period. The treated groups of broccoli heads were then air-dried. Subsequently, three broccoli heads were placed into 0.04 mm-thick polyethylene bags, with the openings folded over without sealing. The packaged broccoli heads were then stored at 20 ± 1 °C and 90% humidity for four days. Assessments of the broccoli heads were conducted daily to monitor changes in appearance, weight reduction, respiration, ethylene generation, and electrolyte leakage. Florets from the broccoli heads in both experimental groups were harvested daily, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis.

The samples for transcriptomics, metabolomics analysis, and other tests were taken from the same treatment bags. In the transcriptomics study, three samples (n = 3) were selected from three different broccoli heads. For the metabolomics study, six samples (n = 6) were extracted from six different broccoli heads. The samples for other analyses were a mixture of samples extracted from multiple broccoli heads (n = 6). On the fourth day of storage (4 d), samples from each treatment group (CK4d and ELP4d), as well as untreated broccoli samples (CK0d), were prepared for transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis.

Respiration, ethylene generation, and electrolyte leakage

The rate of respiration was assessed using a portable emissions analyzer (GXH-3052, Beijing Junfang Institute of Scientific Instruments, China) that measured the level of carbon dioxide. Broccoli heads were enclosed in an airtight chamber, and respiration rates were recorded at 20, 40, and 60-min intervals. Ethylene production was measured using gas chromatography, with samples from each treatment enclosed in a plastic-sealed vessel. After the heads had been in the sealed container for one hour, a 1 mL syringe was used to extract gas from the container, which was then analyzed in a gas chromatograph (7820A, Agilent Technologies, USA). Malondialdehyde (MDA), total soluble solids (TSS), total phenols, flavonoids, vitamin C, and chlorophyll content

The MDA content was determined using the Thio barbituric acid reactive substances method reported by Zhou et al.47. The MDA content in each sample was determined by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm, 532 nm, and 600 nm. The determination of total phenols and flavonoids was performed using the method reported by Zhou et al.48, with the absorbance of the supernatant samples measured at 280 nm and 325 nm for quantification, respectively. The vitamin C content was determined using the ammonium molybdate vanadate spectrophotometry method described by ref. 19. The absorbance of the samples was measured at 760 nm. Chlorophyll content was measured using a modification of the method reported by Sun et al.49. Chlorophyll was extracted using a 2:1 acetone–ethanol solution and absorbance was measured at 664 nm and 645 nm.

Antioxidant enzyme activity

The broccoli tissue extracts for antioxidant enzyme assays were prepared according to the method described by Shi et al.50. The antioxidant enzyme activities of peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) were measured following the protocol reported by Jiang et al.51. The absorbance changes of the reaction mixtures at 470 nm, 240 nm, and 290 nm were recorded for 2 min, respectively.

Transcriptomic analysis

RNA was extracted from 0 d, CK4d, and ELP4d broccoli samples using the RNA prep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). Then, 1 μg of RNA was used for library construction in each sample. The sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. HISAT2 was used to map the reads to the GCA_900416815.2 reference genome. Transcripts were annotated by aligning to relevant databases (http://www.genoscope.cns.fr/externe/plants/chromosomes.html). The threshold for identifying significant differential expressions was set at a minimum fold change (FC) of 1.5 and a maximum p-value of 0.01. Each sample group comprised sequencing reads derived from three separately constructed libraries (n = 3).

Non-targeted metabolomic analysis

The metabolomic assessment of the earlier specified sample groups (0 d, CK 4 d, and ELP 4 d) employed a Waters Acquity I-Class PLUS UPLC connected to a Waters Xevo G2-XS QTOF mass spectrometer, in conjunction with a Waters Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm) for separation. Metabolite identification was determined through the Progenesis QI software’s METLIN database, publicly accessible databases, and Bemac’s exclusive library. In the theoretical fragmentation analysis, the mass tolerance was established at 100 parts per million (ppm) for precursor ions and 50 ppm for product ions. Each sample group comprised six biological replicates (n = 6).

Combined analysis of transcriptomic and metabolomic data

An integrated analysis was performed using the obtained transcriptomic and metabolomic data to examine DEGs and differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) that were enriched in KEGG pathways.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed statistically using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 software. Data were analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance to assess the treatment effect. Duncan’s and LSD tests were used to identify significant differences at P < 0.05 among the treatment groups at each sampled time point. Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard error (SE).
