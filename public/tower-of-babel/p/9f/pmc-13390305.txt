Association Between Living With a Family Member With Dementia and Depressive Symptoms: A Nationwide Study in Korea
Authors: Sun Min Kim, Jun Su Park, Do Hee Kim, Vasuki Rajaguru, Tae Hyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDementia imposes a growing public health burden in Korea, with substantial implications for families living with affected members. Although prior Korean studies have suggested an association between dementia‐related household context and depressive symptoms, population‐based evidence on co‐residence with a family member with dementia remains limited. This study examined the association between co‐residence with a family member with dementia and depressive symptoms among Korean adults aged 50 years and older.MethodsWe conducted a cross‐sectional analysis using data from the 2024 Korea Community Health Survey. Among 231,728 respondents, 120,029 adults aged ≥ 50 years living in non‐single‐person households were included. The exposure was co‐residence with a family member reported to have dementia, rather than a direct measure of caregiving status, intensity, or duration. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9), with a score ≥ 10 indicating clinically significant depressive symptoms. Survey‐weighted multiple logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals.ResultsOverall, 15.8% of individuals reported depressive symptoms. Living with a family member with dementia was highly associated with depressive symptoms (OR = 1.35; 95% CI: 1.29–1.42; p < 0.0001). Among men, significant lower associations showed with good health status (OR = 0.14; 95% CI: 0.13–0.16). Among women, similar patterns were observed, with high income (OR = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.63–0.80) and good health (OR = 0.14; 95% CI: 0.13–0.16) being strongly protective against depression.ConclusionsCo‐residence with a family member with dementia was associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms among Korean adults aged 50 years and older. These findings suggest the need to consider mental health screening and support strategies for families living with dementia, while acknowledging the cross‐sectional and self‐reported nature of the data.

Introduction

Dementia is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that affects memory, cognition, and daily functioning, and it has become one of the most significant public health challenges worldwide. Globally, more than 55 million people are currently living with dementia, and this number is projected to rise to 78 million by 2030 and 139 million by 2050 [1]. In Korea, the prevalence of dementia among adults aged 65 years and older was reported to be approximately 10.3%, with over 900,000 cases in 2022, and these numbers are expected to increase rapidly as the population ages [2, 3]. The burden of dementia extends beyond patients themselves, profoundly affecting families who provide the majority of care.

Family members often act as primary caregivers, especially in countries with strong familial obligations, such as Korea, where multigenerational households are common [4]. Co‐residing with a dementia patient can intensify caregiving demands, as family members experience daily exposure to behavioral disturbances, functional decline, and constant supervision needs [5]. These caregiving responsibilities are well known to be associated with psychological distress, including depression [6]. A meta‐analysis by Pinquart and Sörensen [7] demonstrated that dementia caregivers experience significantly more depressive symptoms compared with non‐caregivers, with co‐residing caregivers reporting the highest burden. Longitudinal studies, such as Arai et al. [8], have also shown that living with a person with dementia increases the risk of clinically significant depression.

Depressive disorders are more prevalent among caregivers of people with dementia than among comparison groups, indicating substantial mental health vulnerability in this population [9]. Prior reviews and population‐based studies suggest that women often experience greater caregiving burden, while socioeconomic disadvantage and poor self‐rated health may increase vulnerability to depressive symptoms [10, 11, 12]. In Korea, cohabitation with a person with dementia has been associated with physician‐diagnosed depression, and the association remained significant among women in sex‐stratified analyses [11, 12].

Despite the growing burden of dementia, population‐based evidence on the relationship between co‐residence with a family member with dementia and depressive symptoms remains limited in Korea [13, 14, 15]. Using data from the 2024 Korea Community Health Survey, this study aimed to examine the association between co‐residence with a family member with dementia and depressive symptoms among adults aged 50 years and older, while adjusting for key sociodemographic, socioeconomic, and health‐related factors. Because the KCHS identifies whether respondents live with a family member diagnosed with dementia, this study focuses on co‐residence rather than directly measured caregiving. Accordingly, the exposure should be interpreted as a household‐level indicator that may include, but does not necessarily confirm, active caregiving involvement.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This was a cross‐sectional study utilizing data from the 2024 Korea Community Health Survey (KCHS), an annual nationwide survey conducted by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) to monitor health behaviors and chronic disease profiles among Korean adults. The KCHS uses a multistage, stratified cluster sampling design to ensure representativeness of the non‐institutionalized population [16].

Study Population

From the original 231,728 participants in the 2024 KCHS, we excluded 44,258 single‐person households to focus on individuals living with family members. We further excluded 164 respondents with missing PHQ‐9 data and 67,149 with missing covariate or dementia status information. An additional 128 participants with incomplete household composition data were removed. The final analytic sample consisted of 120,029 adults aged ≥ 50 years living in non‐single‐person households, including 14,795 who reported living with a family member with dementia and 105,234 who did not.

Measures

Exposure Variable

The exposure of interest was co‐residence with a family member with dementia. This was assessed using household roster information in the KCHS, which identifies whether any cohabiting family member had been diagnosed with dementia. Participants were classified as exposed if at least one family member with dementia was currently living in the same household, and as unexposed otherwise. This variable indicates co‐residence with a family member with dementia and should not be interpreted as a direct measure of active caregiving. Information on caregiving role, intensity, duration, relationship to the person with dementia, dementia severity, and use of formal care services was not available. Dementia status was based on self‐report or household reporting and was not independently validated using medical records [5].

Outcome Variable

The primary outcome was depressive symptoms, assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9). The PHQ‐9 is a validated screening tool for depression, and a score of 10 or higher has been shown to have 88% sensitivity and 88% specificity for major depression [17].

Covariates

Potential confounders were selected based on prior literature and included: age group (50s, 60s, ≥ 70 years), sex (male or female), educational attainment (≥ college, high school, ≤ middle school), residential region (capital vs. non‐capital), household income (high, middle, low), employment status (yes/no), self‐reported health status (good, somewhat good, not good), and presence of any chronic disease (yes/no). Health behaviors (current smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity) were also considered as covariates.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), applying survey weights, strata, and clustering variables to account for the complex design of the KCHS. Weighted descriptive statistics were calculated to compare characteristics between participants with and without living dementia family members. Differences of depression symptoms (Yes/No) were tested using the chi‐square test. Multiple logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between co‐residence with a family member with dementia and depressive symptoms (PHQ‐9 ≥ 10), adjusting for all covariates. Subgroup analyses stratified by sex were conducted to describe whether the association differed between men and women. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Association Within the Depressive Symptoms of the Family Members

Table 2 presents the survey‐weighted multivariable logistic regression results for depressive symptoms. Co‐residence with a family member with dementia was associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms (OR = 1.35; 95% CI: 1.29–1.42; p < 0.0001). Compared with participants in their 50s, those in their 60s and those aged 70 years or older had lower odds of depressive symptoms. Women had higher odds than men, whereas non‐capital residence was associated with lower odds compared with capital residence. Lower educational attainment was associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms. Employment, middle or high income, and better self‐reported health status were associated with lower odds. Chronic disease status was not statistically significant after adjustment.

Multivariate Logistic Regression for Association Between Depressive Symptoms of Family Members Living With Dementia

Table 4 presents the multivariable logistic regression results among participants living with a family member with dementia. Compared with individuals in their 50s, those in their 60s (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.61–0.76) and those aged 70 years or older (OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.77–0.99) had lower odds of depressive symptoms. Women had higher odds of depressive symptoms than men (OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.34–1.56). Participants living in non‐capital regions had lower odds than those living in capital regions (OR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.69–0.82). Compared with participants with college education or higher, those with middle school education or less had higher odds of depressive symptoms (OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.23–1.56), whereas high school graduates did not differ significantly. Employment, higher income, and better self‐reported health status were associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms.

Figure 2 presents sex‐stratified adjusted odds ratios for depressive symptoms. Among men, older age, non‐capital residence, employment, and middle or high income were associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms, whereas lower education, poorer self‐reported health status, and chronic disease were associated with higher odds. Among women, lower odds were observed for those in their 60s, whereas no clear association was observed for those aged 70 years or older. Discussion

In this population‐based cross‐sectional study of Korean adults aged 50 years and older, co‐residence with a family member with dementia was associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms after adjustment for sociodemographic, socioeconomic, and health‐related factors. This association should be interpreted as reflecting the mental health vulnerability of adults living in households affected by dementia, rather than as direct evidence of the effect of caregiving itself. The sex‐stratified analyses further suggested that women living with a family member with dementia may represent a particularly vulnerable group, although the cross‐sectional design does not allow causal interpretation.

Nationally, our findings are consistent with earlier Korean studies that reported high rates of depressive symptoms among family caregivers. For instance, Park et al. [18] found that the prevalence of late‐life depression in Korea was closely related to caregiving roles, low income, and poor health status. Similarly, Jang et al. [11] found that cohabitation with a person with dementia was associated with physician‐diagnosed depression in Korea, with the association remaining significant among women in sex‐stratified analyses. The strong association observed between poor self‐rated health and depressive symptoms is consistent with Kino et al. [19], who found that depressive symptoms among older Koreans were strongly associated with poor self‐rated health and lower household income.

Internationally, our results are supported by numerous studies across different cultural contexts. Pinquart and Sörensen [7] meta‐analysis spanning studies from Europe, North America, and Asia confirmed that dementia caregivers have consistently higher levels of depression and stress compared with non‐caregivers, with co‐residence amplifying this burden. A large US survey by Ory et al. [6] also showed that dementia caregivers were more likely to report emotional distress than caregivers of persons with other chronic illnesses. In Japan, Arai et al. [8] reported that family caregivers living with dementia patients had a twofold increase in clinically significant depression over time, which closely mirrors the elevated odds we found in our study. Systematic reviews have documented an elevated prevalence and incidence of depressive disorders among dementia caregivers, while female caregivers often report greater caregiving burden than male caregivers; however, evidence for sex differences in specific mental health outcomes remains mixed [9, 12].

The observed sex‐specific differences in our subgroup analyses showing higher vulnerability among women and nuanced associations with age and region mirror findings from international studies emphasizing gendered caregiving patterns. For example, a Canadian study by Parmar et al. [20] found that female caregivers reported more depressive symptoms, potentially due to greater caregiving intensity and less access to respite services. Similarly, a systematic review by Chiao et al. [10] across Western and Asian contexts highlighted that lower education and income were consistent risk factors for caregiver depression, which we also observed in both men and women.

Because of the cross‐sectional design, the direction of the association cannot be determined. Depressive symptoms may precede co‐residence with a family member with dementia, arise in response to dementia‐related household stress, or reflect unmeasured household, socioeconomic, or health‐related factors [5, 21]. In addition, depressive symptoms may be related to general household stress rather than caregiving specifically. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal ordering and potential mechanisms.

Cultural context is also critical in interpreting these findings. In Korean society, family caregiving is deeply rooted in Confucian values emphasizing filial piety, which may contribute to stronger emotional burden and reluctance to seek formal support services [22, 23]. Additionally, caregiving is often unevenly distributed among siblings, with one primary caregiver often a daughter or daughter‐in‐law assuming the majority of responsibility [24]. In Korea, the preference for home‐based care over institutional care, due to social stigma and cultural expectations, further compounds the caregiver burden. These cultural nuances may explain the higher prevalence of depression among co‐residing family members in our study and highlight the importance of culturally sensitive policies and interventions.

International literature also points to cultural differences in caregiving expectations and support systems. In many Asian societies, including Korea, caregiving is often considered a family obligation, with limited formal support, which can exacerbate caregiver stress [4]. In contrast, some Western countries have more extensive community‐based respite programs and social services, which have been associated with slightly lower rates of caregiver depression [5, 25]. The comparatively high odds of depression observed in our Korean cohort may therefore reflect both the cultural expectation of family caregiving and the relatively limited formal caregiver support infrastructure.

These findings underscore the urgent need for targeted mental health screening and support programs for family members living with dementia patients. Policy initiatives in Korea could draw on international best practices, such as structured respite care, caregiver training, and financial subsidies, which have been shown to mitigate caregiver depression [4, 25]. Gender‐sensitive interventions are particularly warranted, given the heightened vulnerability observed among female caregivers in both national and international studies.

This study has several strengths, including the use of a large, nationwide dataset, a validated depression screening instrument, and survey‐weighted analyses accounting for the complex sampling design of the KCHS. The study also focused on adults living in non‐single‐person households, allowing examination of household‐level exposure to dementia in a large Korean population.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the exposure variable captured co‐residence with a family member with dementia, not active caregiving itself. Information on caregiving role, intensity, duration, relationship to the person with dementia, dementia severity, and formal service use was unavailable, which may have introduced conceptual heterogeneity and exposure misclassification. Second, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference or determination of temporal ordering. Third, dementia status and depressive symptoms were based on self‐reported information and screening instruments, which may be subject to recall bias or misclassification. Finally, cultural and health system characteristics in Korea may limit the generalizability of these findings to other settings.

Policy Implications

These findings suggest several considerations for dementia‐related family support in Korea. As the number of households affected by dementia increases, mental health screening and psychosocial support for family members living with persons with dementia may be considered within existing dementia care and community health systems. In particular, women and individuals with lower socioeconomic resources may require additional attention. However, because this study examined cross‐sectional associations and did not directly measure caregiving intensity or intervention effects, policy implications should be interpreted as areas for further consideration rather than evidence of causal impact.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this nationwide cross‐sectional study found that co‐residence with a family member with dementia was associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms among Korean adults aged 50 years and older. The association was observed after adjustment for sociodemographic, socioeconomic, and health‐related factors and appeared to differ by sex. Because the exposure reflected co‐residence rather than directly measured caregiving, and because of the cross‐sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as evidence of association rather than causation. Further longitudinal research is needed to clarify causal pathways and to inform support strategies for families living with dementia.
