Neuropsychological profile and clinical benefits of multimodal interventions in a child with ADHD, Generalized Anxiety Disorder, and Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder within the context of intellectual giftedness: a case report
Authors: Andrea Battisti, Cristiana Varuzza, Giulia Brunozzi, Giulia Lazzaro, Pietro De Rossi, Stefano Vicari, Deny Menghini
Published: 2026

Co-occurring neurodevelopmental disorders like Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) and Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder complicate their adaptation in various life contexts. This is the case of twice-exceptional children, who are those presenting intellectual giftedness alongside one or more disorders. ADHD, characterized by inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, poses significant academic and social challenges, making accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment in gifted individuals crucial. Background

In recent years, particular interest has been directed toward the topic of intellectual giftedness, including its characteristics and consequences for psychological well-being.

Despite various interpretations and definitions provided over the years for giftedness [1–3], an intelligence quotient (IQ) higher than the norm has always been the most commonly used and widely recognized criterion [4]. Consistently, children with intellectual giftedness are considered those who exhibit an IQ above 130, typically assessed using standardized cognitive scales such as the Wechsler Intelligence Scales [5]. Notably, intellectual giftedness is not solely defined by high IQ scores; rather, it encompasses a range of cognitive abilities and personal characteristics, including but not limited to analytical thinking, creativity, and motivation for learning [6].

Nevertheless, psychological well-being may not align with high cognitive resources [7–10]. The term “twice-exceptional”, first introduced in 1985 [11], specifically refers to children and adolescents who possess exceptional intellectual abilities alongside one or more disorders [12–14]. In a recent systematic review on the topic [14], involving studies published from 2000 to 2020, emerged that 11 out of 19 studies reported greater emotional and socio-relational difficulties in individuals with intellectual giftedness compared to those without.

Within emotional difficulties, anxiety disorders are particularly described [15–17]. It has been shown that children and adolescents with intellectual giftedness often experience anxiety symptoms due to a tendency toward perfectionism and the pressure to meet high performance standards in all areas of life [18–21]. In some cases, this type of vulnerability can lead to the onset of generalized anxiety symptoms (e.g., Generalized Anxiety Disorder) [22, 23], manifesting as concerns about personal abilities and behaviour adequacy, somatic complaints, worries about the future, performance anxiety, fear of being judged, and frequent requests for reassurance. Emotional difficulties may stem, in part, from a disparity between the robust development of cognitive and neuropsychological capacities and those pertaining to emotional and socio-relational domains. Moreover, they may arise from struggles in establishing common interests with peers, who often engage in recreational activities—as well as school-related tasks—perceived as unstimulating by gifted children.

Considering the aforementioned, identifying and supporting these individuals is crucial, as they may face unique challenges in educational and social contexts. To this end, some international organizations (e.g., the National Association for Gifted Children - https://nagc.org/ and the World Council For Gifted And Talented Children - https://world-gifted.org/) have been developed over the years to advance the understanding of intellectual giftedness and, more critically, to formulate guidelines for fostering the psychological and social well-being of gifted children. For instance, there has been an emphasis on the importance of implementing accelerated educational programs, providing enrichment opportunities, offering psychological interventions to enhance social skills and emotional awareness, and creating support networks that involve both educational institutions and families. These supports could become even more critical for individuals with co-occurring neurodevelopmental conditions, such as Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD).

ADHD is one of the most common neurodevelopmental disorder, marked by persistent patterns of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity that interfere with functioning and development [24]. Of importance, ADHD can lead to significant challenges in academic, occupational, and social settings. Treatment typically includes a combination of behavioural therapies, educational support, and, in some cases, medication. In particular, stimulant medications such as methylphenidate (MPH) have been shown to be effective in reducing ADHD core symptoms [25]. However, comorbid disorders such as learning disabilities, anxiety problems, depression, and conduct disorders are common among individuals with ADHD, which can complicate diagnosis and treatment [26].

For several years, the ADHD diagnosis in individuals with intellectual giftedness has been debated [27]. Specifically, it is believed that certain traits, such as high energy levels, a need for involvement in various stimulating activities, heightened creativity, and a propensity to become bored during routine academic tasks, could be misinterpreted as symptoms of ADHD. However, a systematic review by Rommelse and colleagues [28], in line with the National Society for the Gifted and Talented, suggested that ADHD can occur in intellectually gifted children as well as in those with average intelligence and must be appropriately diagnosed and treated in both groups.

Of note, special consideration should be given when evaluating the presence of ADHD in children with high intellectual potential, given that superior cognitive abilities can often mask clinically relevant symptoms of ADHD [29], resulting in under- or misdiagnosis. Indeed, gifted individuals may rely on cognitive compensatory mechanisms that temporarily reduce the impact of attentional and organizational difficulties. Nonetheless, once ADHD is accurately identified within the context of giftedness, research indicates that interventions (both pharmacological and behavioural) may prove particularly effective [30–33]. This may be due to the enhanced metacognitive awareness and introspective abilities typically observed in intellectually advanced individuals [34, 35], which support the development of more effective strategies for attentional, emotional, and behavioural self-regulation.

In view of the previously discussed, a thorough neuropsychological and emotional-behavioural assessment, along with personalized and holistic therapeutic approaches, are essential for optimal adaptation in all life contexts for children and adolescents with neurodevelopmental and/or neuropsychiatric conditions associated with intellectual giftedness.

The present study aimed to thoroughly characterize the neuropsychological and psychopathological profile of a gifted child to gain a deeper understanding of his overall well-being and to identify the need for a personalized intervention plan.

The study focused on exploring both the cognitive strengths and potential psychological challenges that could impact the child’s development and daily life, ensuring that any intervention is tailored to his unique needs.

Specifically, a 7-year-and-8-month-old child was referred to the Child and Adolescent Neuropsychiatry Unit at the Bambino Gesù Children’s Hospital due to attention difficulties, anxiety issues, socio-relational challenges, and behavioural concerns. To formulate a diagnosis and the therapeutic plan, an extensively detailed assessment was conducted, followed by a 6-months follow-up.

Case presentation

The patient’s medical history was reconstructed through chart review and the collection of anamnestic data.

The patient was born at 40 weeks of gestational age following a normal pregnancy, via caesarean section due to prolonged labor, with a birth weight of 3360 g, a length of 50 cm, and a head circumference of 33.5 cm. APGAR scores were 9 at 1 min and 10 at 5 min. Formula breastfeeding was initiated due to difficulties with breastfeeding. During the neonatal period, the patient was diagnosed with bilateral congenital clubfoot, which was treated with Achilles tendon tenotomy at one month of age. Brain and renal ultrasounds performed at birth were normal.

Regarding psychomotor development, the patient achieved independent walking at 12 months but later showed difficulties with motor coordination. Neurolinguistic development was within normal limits, with first words spoken around 12 months and language development progressing normally for age.

A family history of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) on the maternal side and language disorder on the paternal side was reported. Additionally, the patient’s father has a history of tic disorder.

The first neuropsychiatric evaluation occurred at 2 years and 11 months due to difficulties with rules adherence, which led to a recommendation for a parent-training intervention. At 3 years old, facial and limb motor tics appeared, and a neurological examination in September 2019 found normal age-appropriate neurological status, with a recommendation for re-evaluation if tic symptoms were to worsen. Since then, tic symptoms have fluctuated, with both motor and phonic tics.

At 5 years and 6 months, a neuropsychological evaluation revealed a cognitive profile above the normal range. The Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence–Fourth Edition [36] documented a Verbal IQ of 126, Performance IQ of 140, Processing Speed of 123, and a Full Scale IQ of 140. The adaptive functioning was within normal limits, with scores from the Adaptive Behavior Assessment System-Second Edition (ABAS-II) [37, 38] as follows: General Adaptive Composite (GAC) at the 21st percentile, Conceptual domain at the 33rd percentile, Social domain at the 13th percentile, and Practical domain at the 20th percentile.

Symptoms of emotional-behavioural dysregulation and immature social skills were also observed. Following this evaluation, the patient began a behaviour therapy, and the parents started counselling to manage problematic behaviours.

At 7 years old, the child underwent an outpatient neuropsychiatric visit due to limited improvements observed in therapy and persistent emotional and behavioural difficulties. This visit identified anxious and obsessive traits, such as reluctance to touch chairs and repetitive hand sanitizing, as well as impulsiveness, distractibility, and hyperactivity. Additional behavioural concerns included emotional regulation issues, oppositional traits, poor anger management, adult-like behaviours, inconsistent eye contact, selective eating, and difficulty falling asleep. In the school context (the child was attending the second year of primary school), attention difficulties, incomplete homework, motor restlessness, and overall compromised school functioning were noted. The visit concluded with a recommendation for an extensive neuropsychological and psychopathological assessment to further investigate the overall condition.

Assessment

The neuropsychological and psychopathological assessment was conducted when the child was 7 years and 5 months old.

The assessment included clinical observation, the administration of standardized neuropsychological instruments, emotional-behavioural questionnaires, and individual clinical interviews with both the parents and the patient. All the tools employed provide normative data adjusted for the Italian population.

Intelligence Quotient

To assess cognitive level, the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children-Fourth Edition (WISC-IV) [5, 39] was used.

The WISC-IV is an individually administered standardized tool to measure cognitive ability and intelligence in children aged 6 to 16. It assesses various aspects of intelligence, including verbal comprehension, perceptual reasoning, working memory, and processing speed.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

Adaptive Behavior

Adaptive behaviour was assessed using the ABAS-II [37, 38] and the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales – Second Edition (VABS-II) [40, 41]. The ABAS-II is a caregiver standardized questionnaire that assesses daily living skills in individuals ranging from children and adolescents to adults, spanning from 0 to 89 years of age. The VABS-II is a caregiver standardized semi-structured interview that provides a detailed evaluation of an individual’s practical, social, and conceptual skills necessary for everyday functioning.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

Neuropsychological Measures

Visual-Motor Integration

The Beery-Buktenica Developmental Test of Visual-Motor Integration (VMI) [42] is a standardized test used for assessing visual-motor integration skills in children and adolescents ranging in aged 3 to 18 years.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

Language

The standardized Battery for Language Assessment in Children (BVL 4–12) [43] measures language abilities in children aged 4 to 12 years. It is divided into three sections and specifically evaluates, from both grammatical and lexical perspectives, production, comprehension, and repetition skills.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

Memory

Memory abilities were assessed using the Battery for Memory and Learning Assessment in Developmental Age (PROMEA) [44] and the N-Back task [45, 46]. The PROMEA includes a series of standardized tests that use verbal, visual, or spatial stimuli to evaluate both short-term and long-term memory, encompassing both explicit and implicit components. We included tasks that assessed both short-term memory and long-term memory across each modality: verbal, visual, and spatial, along with semantic memory (CAT).

The N-Back task is a non-standardized test used to assess working memory. Participants monitor a sequence of stimuli, which can be either spatial or auditory, and indicate when the current stimulus matches one presented n steps earlier.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

Attention and Executive Functions

The NEuroPSYchological Assessment-Second Edition (NEPSY-II) [47] is a standardized neuropsychological assessment tool used to evaluate a wide range of cognitive functions, such as attention and executive functions, in children and adolescents aged 3 to 16 years. From the NEPSY-II, we included tasks that evaluated auditory attention, visual attention, inhibitory control, and emotion recognition.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

Academic Skills

Reading Abilities

The MT-3 Clinical Tests (MT-3) [48] are standardized texts designed to assess reading speed, accuracy and comprehension in students ranging from primary school through the third year of first-grade secondary school.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

Writing Abilities

The Writing and Spelling Proficiency Assessment Battery – 2 (BVSCO-2) [49] are standardized tasks to assess various aspects of writing skills in students ranging from primary school through the third year of first-grade secondary school. It comprises 6 tasks that can be categorized into three groups: graphism, spelling proficiency, and written text production.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

Arithmetic Abilities

The AC-MT Test for the Evaluation of Mathematical Competence (ACMT) [50] is a standardized battery designed to evaluate mathematical abilities in students aged 6 to 11 years. It assesses various aspects of mathematical competence, including problem-solving skills and numerical reasoning.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

Child Behaviour Checklist (CBCL)

The CBCL [51] is a standardized caregiver-report questionnaire designed to identify a range of behavioural and emotional issues in children and adolescents aged 1.5 to 18 years. It uses a 3-point Likert scale to rate the frequency of various behaviours and symptoms. The CBCL categorizes scores into three main types of scales: syndrome scales, which assess specific clusters of symptoms; broad band scales, which provide a general overview of behaviour and emotional functioning; and DSM-oriented scales, which align with DSM criteria to facilitate diagnosis.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

Conners’ Parent Rating Scale (CPRS)

The CPRS [52] is a widely used standardized caregiver-report questionnaire designed to assess behaviours related to ADHD and related issues in children and adolescents aged 3 to 17 years. The CPRS uses a 4-point Likert scale to rate the frequency of a range of symptoms, including inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

Sleep Disturbances Scale for Children (SDSC)

The SDSC [53] is a standardized caregiver-report questionnaire designed to assess various sleep-related problems in children and adolescents aged 2 to 18 years. It uses a 5-point Likert scale to evaluate a range of sleep disturbances, including difficulties falling asleep, frequent awakenings, nightmares, and sleep-related breathing issues.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

Swanson, Nolan, and Pelham Rating Scale—Version IV (SNAP-IV)

The SNAP-IV [54] is a standardized caregiver-report questionnaire designed to assess symptoms of ADHD and Oppositional Defiant Disorder in children and adolescents aged 6 to 18 years. It uses a 4-point scale to rate the severity of symptoms, with higher scores reflecting more severe manifestations.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

Socio-Communicative and Relational Assessment

Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule - Second Edition (ADOS-2)

The ADOS-2 [55] is a standardized measure to assess ASD by observing communication skills, social interaction, play quality, and imagination in individuals ranging from 12 months to adulthood.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

The Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised (ADI-R)

The ADI-R [56] is a standardized caregiver-report semi-structured interview designed to assess early developmental history, social interactions, communication, and repetitive behaviours in children and adults with a mental age of at least 18 months, suspected of presenting ASD.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

The Children’s Communication Checklist - Second Edition (CCC-2)

The CCC-2 [57] is a standardized caregiver-report questionnaire designed to assess communication skills in children and adolescents aged 4 to 16 years. It focuses on identifying speech language impairment, ASD, and pragmatic language impairment. The CCC-2 evaluates a range of communication abilities, including speech, syntax, and the use of language in social contexts.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

The Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS)

The SRS [58] is a standardized caregiver-report questionnaire designed to evaluate social deficits in children and adolescents aged 4 to 18 years. It assesses social impairments across five domains: social awareness, social cognition, social communication, social motivation, and restricted interests and repetitive behaviour.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

The Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ)

The SCQ [59] is a behavioural parent report questionnaire used to screen for ASD. It assesses social, communicative, and stereotyped behaviours in children aged 4 years and older.

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia - Present and Lifetime Version (K-SADS-PL DSM-5)

The K-SADS-PL DSM-5 [60] is a semi-structured interview for caregivers and children used to assess psychopathological disorders according to DSM-5 criteria in children and adolescents aged 6 to 18 years. It evaluates a range of symptoms and diagnoses by using a three-point scale to rate symptom severity (absent, subclinical, severe).

For additional information, refer to the Supplementary Materials.

Results

The child was conscious and oriented, cooperative with the examiner, and engaged in interaction with the clinician. Eye contact was possible but not consistently sustained for communicative purposes. Speech was spontaneous and fluent without phonetic or phonological abnormalities. Facial expressions were modulated. There were minimal alterations in prosody. Mood was stable. Symptoms of hyperkinesia were observed. There were no tics observed during the assessment. No focal neurological signs were noted.

Neuropsychological assessment

The results of the neuropsychological evaluation were generally within or above the average range for the child’s chronological age (see Table 1).

Cognitive level and adaptive functioning. The total IQ was significantly above average (i.e., FSIQ: 151). Scores in verbal competence and perceptual reasoning were above average (i.e., VCI: 144 and PRI: 154, respectively), while scores in working memory and processing speed were at the upper limit of the average (i.e., WMI: 127 and PSI: 121, respectively).

In contrast, scores in adaptive functioning were below average or at the lower limit across all components (e.g., ABAS GAC: 65 and VABS Composite score: 77, respectively), indicating that personal autonomy and daily functioning did not align with the child’s excellent intellectual functioning.

Language. Language abilities were fully within the normal range or at the upper limit of the average for both grammatical and lexical components (i.e., BVL 4–12: each task above 1.5 standard deviations).

Visual-Motor Integration. Visual-motor integration abilities were above or at the upper limit of the average in all assessed modalities, including visual-motor integration, visual perception, and motor coordination (i.e., VMI: each task above 1 standard deviations).

Memory. Working memory, short-term memory, and long-term memory were within or above average (i.e., PROMEA: each task above 25° percentile). The N-back task revealed comparable performance across both modalities (verbal and spatial), with slightly higher scores observed in the spatial N-back, and accuracy percentages always above 33%.

Attention and executive functions. Attention and executive functions scores were within or above average across all measures assessed (i.e., NEPSY-II: each task above 18° percentile).

Social perception. In the emotion recognition test the score was above average (i.e., NEPSY-II AR: above 75° percentile).

Academic skills. Scores in reading (speed, accuracy, and comprehension), writing (spelling), and arithmetic (numerical ordering and written calculation), were within or above average for the child’s grade level (i.e., MT-3, BVSCO-2, ACMT: each task always above 0 standard deviations).

Emotional-behavioural assessment

The emotional-behavioural evaluation was conducted through clinical observation and standardized instruments (caregiver-report questionnaires, checklists, and semi-structured clinical interview) (see Table 2). Results from questionnaires indicated widespread emotional-behavioural difficulties in both internalizing and externalizing domains, with clinical or borderline range scores for symptoms as withdrawn, somatic complaints, social problems, thought problems, inattention/hyperactivity, rule-breaking and aggressive behaviours, affective problems, perfectionism, sleep disturbances. Questionnaires and checklists concerning the socio-communicative and relational assessment helped to exclude a diagnosis of ASD. However, some borderline range scores emerged, suggesting the presence of socio-relational and communication difficulties. Specifically, the diagnostic choice of Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder over ASD was based on subthreshold scores on both the ADOS-2 and ADI-R, along with the broader clinical picture. On the ADOS-2, the child obtained a total comparison score of 3, falling well below the ASD threshold. Some difficulties were noted in reciprocal interaction, such as inconsistent eye contact and limited understanding of complex emotions, while the Restricted and Repetitive Behaviour (RRB) score was 0, with no stereotypies or sensory interests observed during the evaluation. The ADI-R also showed subthreshold results: a score of 8 in the Social domain and 6 in Communication, both under the diagnostic cut-offs for ASD. The RRB domain reached the cut-off (3), due to narrow interests and verbal rituals. However, these behaviours were neither pervasive nor functionally impairing. Notably, the child’s restricted interests—focused on topics such as the solar system and planets—appeared developmentally advanced and were judged to reflect high intellectual potential rather than pathological rigidity. The content and expression of these interests were consistent with superior cognitive functioning rather than a marker of ASD.

DSM-5 criteria were operationalized by confirming persistent pragmatic and social communication difficulties (Criterion A of Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder) in the absence of clinically significant RRBs (Criterion B of ASD). Overall, the child’s profile supports a diagnosis of Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder due to the presence of pragmatic deficits and mild social challenges, without the repetitive behavioural patterns necessary for an ASD diagnosis.

The semi-structured interview (i.e., K-SADS-PL DSM-5), conducted separately with the mother and the child, documented the presence of ADHD symptoms (combined presentation), generalized anxiety symptoms, and confirmed the atypical social behaviours. Specifically, child had significant difficulties with attention, a tendency to be easily distracted, and challenges in task organization requiring constant support from adults. Motor restlessness and impulsivity were also observed in several context (e.g., at home, school, and during extracurricular activities, such as judo).

The child reported symptoms of inattention (e.g., “I feel like there are flies in my head distracting me”), distractibility, a continuous need to move, and he referred that at school as is called the “class disruptor”. He often interrupted, showed little respect for speaking turns, and tended to blurt out answers. The child interpreted and reported that his easy distractibility to frequent boredom, particularly during school activities.

Regarding social interaction, the child referred that he enjoys playing with other children at school but preferring the company of a single peer. The mother described some socio-relational difficulties, especially related to interests that peers often do not care. She also noted a degree of behavioural rigidity and adherence to small daily routines, although without functional impairment. Challenges in pragmatic communication skills were described (e.g., difficulties in understanding metaphorical language and irony, in following rules for conversation, in changing communication to match context or the needs of the listener, and rigidity in applying behavioural scripts in social contexts).

Both the mother and the child reported additional concerns included anxiety symptoms as excessive worries about child’s abilities, behaviours, and health, as well as low self-esteem and need for reassurance (“Check if I did it right; I will never be as good as ….“). The mother also reported ritualistic behaviours, with the need to organize belongings in a specific order (e.g., aligning books from smallest to largest), to receive goodnight wishes in a particular way (e.g., “good night and sweet dreams, hug and kiss”), or to arrange school desk according to a rigid pattern (e.g., notebook aligned to the bottom right, pencil case to the bottom left, and water bottle to the top left).

Both in the past and at present, some oppositional-defiant behaviours are reported, including arguing with adults such as teachers and parents, and engaging in rule-breaking activities like blocking bathroom drains and painting walls. There have been no reports of physical or verbal aggression, and there are no episodes of severe loss of control.

In the past, the child exhibited motor tics, such as blinking and shoulder movements, which persisted for about a year. Currently, these tics appear intermittently during periods of increased emotional stress.

Diagnosis and prescriptions

At the conclusion of the assessment, a psychoeducational interview was initially conducted with the parents and subsequently with the child, aimed at presenting the evaluation results, communicating the diagnoses, and sharing the proposed treatment plan. The condition of giftedness was clarified, emphasizing that it does not constitute a diagnosis but rather a developmental characteristic that can represent a strength in the child’s growth. The diagnostic profile was then explained, and the rationale behind the treatment recommendations was provided. The family demonstrated understanding of the information conveyed, showing good awareness of the ongoing difficulties and, in part, expressing relief at having a name for the challenges the child has consistently experienced.

Regarding the attention-deficit/hyperactivity symptomatology, the necessity of a multimodal treatment approach was explained, which was positively received without significant concerns. Concerning the remaining emotional, behavioural, and socio-communicative difficulties, it was illustrated how cognitive-behavioural therapy modulates dysfunctional symptoms and what the parents can do to facilitate the process, including specific examples of parent-training assignments. The need for a comprehensive care plan involving the school was also justified, with full parental awareness and agreement.

Based on the neuropsychological and emotional-behavioural assessment, according to ICD-10 codes and DSM-5 criteria, a diagnosis of Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder - Combined Presentation (ADHD-C), moderate severity (F90.2), Generalized Anxiety Disorder (F41.1), and Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder (F80.89) was established in a child with intellectual giftedness.

According to international guidelines for the treatment of ADHD (www.nice.org.uk), therapeutic interventions were recommended, highlighting the need for both pharmacological and behavioural treatments. The pharmacological approach involved modified-release Methylphenidate, starting with two 5 mg capsules in the morning for one month, followed by three 5 mg capsules. Behavioural therapies included initiating individual cognitive-behavioural therapy (held once a week) aimed at addressing dysfunctional emotional and behavioural symptoms, as well as commencing a parent-training program (with meetings every two weeks) to help parents manage these symptoms effectively.

In the school context, in accordance with national guidelines for students with ADHD and intellectual giftedness, several measures have been suggested. Specifically, it was recommended to apply the Italian ministerial note (Prot. N. 4089-15/6/2010) used in cases of attention difficulties, impulsivity, and hyperactive behaviours (see Supplementary Materials). Moreover, following the issuance of Directive 27/12/2012 and Ministerial Ordinance No. 205 of 03/04/2019, an Individualized Educational Plan (IEP), including specific teaching strategies, has been suggested to place these children in the best conditions for learning. This approach aims to tailor educational practices to meet the unique needs of gifted students effectively (see Supplementary Materials).

Six-month later follow-up

The second evaluation was conducted six months after the first, when the child was 8 years and 2 months old and in the third grade of primary school.

Before the evaluation began, parents reported that they had adhered to the recommendations made at the end of the initial assessment. These included pharmacological treatment with MPH, cognitive-behavioural therapy, and parent training.

The patient was conscious and oriented, cooperative with the examiner, and engaged in interaction with the clinician. There was a marked improvement in the ability to remain seated during the visit compared to the first evaluation. Spontaneous speech was well-organized. Facial expressions were modulated. Mood was stable. Attentional skills showed significant improvement. Vocalizations resembling tics and some other tics involving facial musculature were present.

In the follow-up, only the domains where the child demonstrated mild difficulties were reassessed. In particular, the NEPSY-II Auditory Attention was re-evaluated, along with adaptive functioning using the ABAS-II questionnaire.

The neuropsychological assessments revealed an improvement in the attentional task (i.e., AA: 37.5° percentile vs. 18° percentile).

A significant improvement was also noted in across all indices of the adaptive functioning questionnaire, including the overall composite score (i.e., GAC: 35° percentile vs. < 1° percentile), indicating substantial progress in autonomy and daily life functioning. See Table 3.

The follow-up clinical interview confirmed a substantial improvement in ADHD symptoms, with noticeable effects in both school and home settings. There was reduced distractibility, longer attention spans, decreased motor restlessness, and improved task completion. The patient showed greater ability to follow instructions, increased focus during classroom activities, and less disruptive behaviour. Diagnosis and prescriptions

The diagnosis and prescriptions, both pharmacological and non-pharmacological, remained unchanged compared to the previous ones, both for the family and the school context.

Discussion and conclusions

In the current case report, we presented a child with a complex clinical profile involving neurodevelopmental and psychopathological conditions within a context of intellectual giftedness.

The child was referred to our observation due to attentional, emotional, and behavioural difficulties across primary life contexts, including family and school settings.

The diagnostic formulation was made possible through an extensive neuropsychological and emotional-behavioural assessment. The evaluation of intellectual functioning revealed the presence of intellectual giftedness, further corroborated by remarkable performance across all assessed neuropsychological domains, including visual-motor integration skills, language, memory, attention, executive functions, and academic abilities. The assessment of emotional-behavioural aspects, conducted through clinical observation, questionnaires, and diagnostic interviews, documented that the child met diagnostic criteria for ADHD, Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder, and Generalized Anxiety Disorder.

In the case we presented, the implementation of comprehensive and holistic treatment plan contributed to an overall improvement in the patient’s clinical condition and daily functioning, despite the complexity of multiple diagnoses.

Specifically, the positive evolution of ADHD symptoms and its positive impact on adaptive functioning are consistent with the well-documented efficacy of MPH treatment in conjunction with cognitive-behavioural therapy for ADHD [25]. Although it is widely recognized that clinical diagnoses [26, 30] or emotional-behavioural symptoms [61] comorbid with ADHD can significantly reduce the effectiveness of treatments, the patient showed a favourable treatment response, with improvements evident in questionnaires, semi-structured interviews, and clinical observation. Moreover, this improvement may have been partly influenced by the high cognitive level, as several studies have indicated that elevated IQ levels are associated with a greater likelihood of positive response to MPH [30, 31] and cognitive-behavioural therapy [32, 33].

Improvements, although modest, also emerged in symptoms comorbid with ADHD. Specifically, anxiety symptoms and associated emotional and behavioural difficulties—such as internalizing problems, perfectionism, psychosomatic issues, and emotional lability—appeared reduced, with benefits reported by both parents and teachers in daily management. Evidence suggests that MPH may alleviate comorbid symptoms in ADHD, including emotional symptoms in adults [62], separation anxiety, social phobia, depression, school-related anxiety, suspiciousness [63], and irritability [64]. Additionally, improvements in non-specific ADHD symptoms in children may also be supported by cognitive-behavioural therapy and parent-training programs, which are considered essential treatments for emotional and behavioural difficulties during development [65–69]. Indeed, it can be hypothesized that this type of intervention contributed to enhancing the child’s emotional self-regulation skills, leading to improved management of frustration and dysfunctional behaviours. However, observing a more significant positive shift in anxiety symptoms might require a longer period, partly considering that the effects of cognitive-behavioural therapy may require variable periods of time to become evident [70, 71]. In addition to the interpretations provided, it is also possible that some of the improvements described may be attributable to contextual factors independent of the prescribed treatments, such as increased self-awareness of difficulties on the part of the child, parental expectancy bias associated with the initiation of treatment, maturational factors, or a reduction in school-related stress.

Concerning socio-relational difficulties, it is possible that some of these difficulties are attributable to the discrepancy between his cognitive maturation and that of his peers, resulting in reduced engagement in shared enjoyable activities. Indeed, struggling to connect with same-age peers, gifted children often experience isolation and loneliness due to social stigmas, which in turn heightens their risk of social-emotional challenges [8]. In the case presented, moreover, this type of common difficulty among gifted individuals is accompanied by the clinical symptoms typical of Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder, extending beyond challenges in sharing enjoyment with peers. Indeed, authentic difficulties in pragmatic communication skills have been identified, including challenges in interpreting figurative language and irony, following conversational rules, adapting communication to the context or the needs of the listener, and rigidity in applying social behaviour patterns. Notably, these difficulties still persist at the follow-up, as documented by parental questionnaires and clinical interview. It can be hypothesized that the atypical social behaviours might have benefited only marginally from a standard cognitive-behavioural therapy approach, which had initially been recommended in order to adopt a broad-spectrum intervention targeting the wide range of clinical symptoms identified during the assessment. Specifically, we hypothesize that: (i) given that ADHD symptoms were the most impairing aspect of the overall clinical presentation, the cognitive-behavioural intervention may have primarily focused on enhancing attentional and motor self-regulation strategies, with comparatively less emphasis on components specifically targeting social skills; (ii) high cognitive potential may in part complicate improvements in this domain, as even when gains in social skills occur, marked differences in preferred types of rewarding play activities compared to non-gifted peers may persist, potentially hindering the sharing of leisure time and mutual interests; (iii) as speculated for emotional symptoms, a longer follow-up period beyond six months may be necessary to detect meaningful changes in social functioning.

To date, limited research has been conducted on therapeutic interventions specifically targeting Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder. Treatments involve techniques that enhance social cognition, social interaction, and the structural components of language [72], especially via group-based programs with specific training in social skills [73]. It is therefore plausible that, following stabilization of ADHD-related and anxiety symptoms that most significantly impair the patient’s functioning, a more targeted intervention focusing directly on Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder will be necessary. In line with this interpretation of the case, should a similar pattern emerge at future follow-up—characterized by well-compensated emotional and behavioural aspects alongside only marginal improvements in socio-communicative functioning—a behavioural intervention more specifically focused on enhancing social skills will be recommended.

The follow-up evaluation revealed also a slight increase in tic symptoms compared to the previous assessment. Although evidence remains inconclusive regarding the potential of stimulants (such as MPH) to worsen tics in most individuals with tic disorders, tic exacerbation may nonetheless occur in specific situations [74]. Of note, as described in the case presentation, the patient had a pre-existing tic disorder characterized by a fluctuating course, with periods of increased symptom expression alternating with phases of partial remission. Therefore, it cannot be conclusively stated that the slight increase in tic symptoms observed six months after the initiation of MPH treatment was attributable to the medication itself. Importantly, neither the patient nor the parents reported any specific distress related to this mild tic exacerbation, and the patient’s adaptive functioning remained unaffected. Considering the overall clinical improvement and the absence of direct or clinically significant adverse effects linked to the treatment, there were no ethical grounds to reconsider the continuation of the pharmacological intervention. Nevertheless, careful monitoring of tic symptoms—along with potential other side effects—will be implemented. When necessary, standardized assessment tools [75] will be employed in future follow-up assessments to allow for both qualitative and quantitative evaluation of tic severity.

Moreover, teachers’ reports underlined increased engagement of the child during lessons, reduced hyperactive behaviours, and an enhanced ability to concentrate and complete tasks. In addition to the effects derived from MPH and non-pharmacological therapies, the overall improvement in behaviour observed in the school setting can also be partly attributed to the implementation of the recommended strategies in the educational environment. This highlights the importance of implementing educational suggestions in the school context. Given the significant amount of time children and adolescents spend in school, creating optimal conditions to foster a good adaptation to the school environment is crucial for promoting overall psychological well-being, especially for twice-exceptional children.

Regarding the neuropsychological follow-up, only the subset of tests where the child demonstrated mild difficulties was re-administered. An overall improvement emerged in both adaptive functioning and the measure of attention administered, consistent with the overall clinical improvement described. The marked improvement observed in adaptive functioning represents a particularly significant outcome, as it serves as a cross-sectional measure of the individual’s ability to cope across various life contexts. In our case, this improvement appears to be generalized (i.e., CC, PC, SC, and GAC from ABAS-II), offering a comprehensive picture of the overall positive progression.

The present study has several limitations. As a single-case report, this study lacks a control or comparison group, which limits the generalizability of its findings. Moreover, the assessments, interpretations, and interventions were embedded within the Italian clinical and educational system, which may differ substantially from those in other countries. In particular, the perceived benefits of a comprehensive, multi-contextual approach—including collaboration with the school system—cannot be assumed to apply across educational settings with different structures and levels of support for children with special educational needs. In Italy, gifted students can be recognized as having Special Educational Needs and may receive specific educational plans within an inclusive educational framework. This approach is supported by national regulations, such as the Legislative Decree 66/2017, which emphasize the development of each student’s potential through individualized and inclusive teaching strategies. However, the autonomy granted to schools in implementing tailored interventions requires caution when considering the generalizability of our findings, not only to different countries but also beyond the Italian educational system itself. Additionally, the absence of a detailed account of the cognitive-behavioural intervention limits the depth of analysis regarding the therapeutic strategies employed, and our interpretations remain speculative in this regard. Finally, the six-month follow-up period may be insufficient to assess the durability of treatment effects. Future research could include longer-term follow-up (e.g., one year or more) to better evaluate the persistence and evolution of clinical outcomes over time.

In conclusion, the present study underscores the importance of early and targeted interventions for twice-exceptional children to mitigate potential adverse outcomes. Specifically, our research emphasizes the critical role of multidisciplinary interventions that engage diverse stakeholders across various life contexts, working synergistically to promote optimal adaptation and favourable conditions for growth and development. Considering the limited knowledge available in the literature regarding the management of such complex yet underexplored cases, future studies—employing more robust experimental designs—should investigate the ideal characteristics of a multimodal, multi-setting treatment approach.
