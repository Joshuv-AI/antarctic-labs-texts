A hemangiolymphangioma of the small intestinal mesentery encircled superior mesenteric vessels: case report and review

Published: 2025

Background

Mixed hemangioma-lymphangioma, or hemangiolymphan-gioma[1], is a very rare congenital malformation of both lymphatic and blood vessels[2]. First, on non-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) scans, hemangiolymphangiomas typically present as mesenteric cystic or cystic-solid masses. These lesions are usually hypodense but may demonstrate increased enhancement during the venous and delayed phases. However, enhancement may be absent if the proportion of cystic vascular components is small[3]. Second, on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), the cystic components exhibit hypointense signals on T1-weighted images and appear homogeneously hyperintense on T2-weighted images, often with thin enhancing septations[4]. Finally, the histopathological examination reveals varying admixtures of vascular elements – such as lymphatic and venous endothelium– within the same lesion. These vascular spaces are often filled with red blood cells and proteinaceous fluid resembling lymph[5]. Hemangiolymphangioma often occurs in newborns and infants, but rarely in adults. So far, only a few cases occurring in the small intestine, spleen, rectum, chest wall, mediastinum, adrenal gland, pancreas, duodenal, extremities, or orbit have been reported[6–13]. Nevertheless, only one case of mesenteric hemangiolymphangioma causing massive gastrointestinal bleeding has been described. Eventually, the patient was successfully treated with radiotherapy[14]. Case presentation

A 32-year-old woman presented with a patient-identified periumbilical mass, noted approximately 1 week prior, accompanied only by occasional abdominal discomfort. Her obstetric history included a cesarean section 3 years earlier. Abdominal examination demonstrated a soft, non-tender abdomen with a palpable, mobile 8 × 11 cm mass in the periumbilical region. Routine laboratory investigations yielded no abnormalities.

The abdominal CT scan showed a tumor in the root of the mesenteric vessel. An MRI showed a 6 × 8 × 11 cm mass encircling superior mesenteric vessel located in the mesentery of small intestine, in accordance with a diagnosis of lymphangioma. The neoplasm demonstrated craniocaudal involvement spanning from the third lumbar vertebral body (L3) to the first sacral segment (S1). Comprehensive radiographic evaluation revealed no evidence of peritoneal fluid accumulation (ascites) or secondary metastatic involvement of additional visceral organ systems (Fig. 1A). Surprisingly, US-guided needle biopsy revealed a hemangioma. The patient refused further intervention. After 1 month, she rechecked the abdominal CT, which found no obvious changes in the mass. To make a definite diagnosis, surgical removal of the mass was suggested. Furthermore, based on our comprehensive assessment, the lesion is deemed suitable for radical resection, and thus radiotherapy is not indicated.Figure 1.Radiologic features of the mass.

During the operation, a 10.5 × 8.5 × 4.5 cm brown mass that originated from the mesentery of small bowel was discovered (Fig. 2A). The interior of the mass showed a large dark-red honeycomb mass (Fig. 2B). Given the anatomic configuration in which the superior mesenteric vascular bundle coursed longitudinally through the tumor bed, en bloc resection was accomplished via precise sharp dissection at the mesenteric root (Fig. 2C). This involved systematic ligation of tertiary arterial branches originating from the superior mesenteric artery while maintaining the structural and functional integrity of the primary vascular trunk. Importantly, the implemented surgical strategy ensured complete oncologic clearance without inducing hemodynamic compromise to intestinal perfusion, as confirmed by the absence of perioperative ischemic sequelae.Figure 2.Radiologic features of the mass.

Radiologic features of the mass.

Histopathological examination of the resected specimen revealed that the majority of the neoplasm was composed of diluted vessels with a thin wall, which was characteristic of cavernous hemangioma, and a small part of the mass was full of proliferating, cystically dilated lymphatic spaces (Fig. 3A and B). Immunohistochemistry for blood vessel endothelia biomarker CD31 and lymphatic endothelia biomarker D2-40 were both positive (Fig. 3C and D). Then, the definitive diagnosis was mesenteric hemangiolymphangioma[16–18]. In view of the fact that the pathological results are benign and that we achieved full exposure and precise dissection during the surgical procedure, we do not consider radiotherapy postoperatively. The patient made an uneventful recovery and was discharged home on day 10, postoperatively. During the 6 months of follow-up, she was fit and healthy and completely free of symptoms.Figure 3.Radiologic features of the mass.

Discussion

Hemangioma is one of the most common benign tumors widely found in many organs, with a high occurrence during infancy and childhood. About 250 cases have been reported in the gastrointestinal system in the English papers since 1839 so far[19]. Despite the fact that hemangiomas can be seen in the liver, osseous tissues, mediastinum, soft tissues, and other organs, hemangiomas of mesenteric origin are extremely rare[20–23]. In particular, there are very few reports of hemangiomas originating from the small bowel mesentery. Mesenteric lymphangiomas are rare benign tumors of the lymphatic system with a reported incidence of less than 1 per 100 000 hospital admissions[24,25]. Approximately 95% of lymphangiomas are found in the head, neck, and axillary region. Isolated small bowel lesions are seen in less than 1% of cases[26], but this accounts for 70% of all intraperitoneal lymphatic tumors.

Compared with the hemangioma and lymphangioma in mesentery of small bowel, the mesenteric hemangiolymphangioma is much rarer. Hemangiolymphangioma is a mixed malformation of both lymphatic and blood vessels. Although histologically it is a benign disorder, it has a propensity to invade underlying tissues and to recur locally[27,28]. In adults, hemangiolymphangiomas are very rare and only few cases have been reported[29–31]. Locations of adult hemangiolymphangioma include pulmonary, cervico-mediastinum, gastrointestinal tract, and, rarely, retroperitoneum. In our case, the hemangiolymphangioma was located in mesentery of the small intestine. In the English literature, only one case of mesenteric hemangiolymphangioma was reported. The tumor underwent radiotherapy rather than surgical resection due to its enormous bulk.

The preoperative diagnosis of mesenteric hemangiolymphangioma is reasonably difficult. Despite advanced imaging techniques, mesenteric hemangiolymphangiomas are often misdiagnosed. Clinical differential diagnoses include pseudocyst, lymphangioma, serous and mucinous tumors, sarcoma, enteric duplication cyst, and cystic tumor not otherwise specified[32]. The imaging tests include B-scan ultrasonography, CT, MRI, and so on. Although these tests provide some useful information, they can only demonstrate an abdominal mass rather than diagnose accurately[21,33–35]. The definitive and accurate diagnosis must be established through histopathologic investigation.

Percutaneous fine-needle biopsy was not considered in view of the vascularization of the mesenteric hemangiolymphangioma for a high risk of iatrogenic complications, such as bleeding. In our case, US-guided needle biopsy was performed because the patient refused to surgery and lymphangioma diagnosis by imaging tests. The result of biopsy, which was different from the MRI and CT, diagnosed the mass as hemangioma. Therefore, in order to clarify the diagnosis, surgical excision may be the last and best method. On the other hand, the possibility of hemorrhage due to biopsy could not be excluded. Actually, we saw obsolete bleeding on the puncture site of the resected specimen (Fig. 2A). After surgery, the immunohistochemical stainings of both D2-40 and CD31 of the specimen are positive. However, the immunohistochemistry examination of the percutaneous fine-needle biopsy revealed that CD31 was positive, while D2-40 was negative. This is because fine-needle biopsy may only get the tissue from the hemangioma portion of the mixed tumor containing both hemangioma and lymphangioma.

Surgical resection remains the primary treatment for hemangiolymphangioma based on existing literature. Nonoperative techniques including sclerotherapy, cryosurgery, embolization, and interventional angiography are associated with temporary symptom relief and high recurrence rates. While radiation therapy has been employed in unresectable cases, clinical experience remains limited to a single reported case where palliative irradiation was administered following failed resection due to extensive tumor bulk[14].

The hemangiolymphangioma has the potential to present with life-threatening complications, including traumatic rupture, anemia, intestinal gangrene secondary to volvulus, and intermittent intestinal obstruction depending on its location. Therefore, the radical surgical excision is the best way to treat hemangiolymphangioma even when the patient is asymptomatic. Owing to advances in surgical techniques and materials, the indications for surgery have significantly expanded. Procedures now range from intestinal resection to multi-organ resection and, even in cases of major vascular invasion, vascular reconstruction with prosthetic grafting. Consequently, we posit that there are no longer absolute contraindications to surgery based solely on tumor characteristics. The decision to operate should be guided by a comprehensive evaluation of the patient’s overall condition and the institutional surgical expertise.
