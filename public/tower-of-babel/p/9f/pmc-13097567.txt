ApCtf1β‐Interacting Target Proteins BDPH1 and BDEUL12 Regulate Pathogenicity in Arthrinium phaeospermum
Authors: Qian Chen, Haiyan Luo, Han Zhao, Zhanbo Liu, Shan Han, Chunlin Yang, Long Liu, Hua Yang, Han Liu, Shujiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Arthrinium phaeospermum is the primary pathogen responsible for shoot blight disease in Bambusa pervariabilis × Dendrocalamopsis grandis . Previous research identified ApCtf1β, a key gene involved in pathogenicity encoding a cutinase transcription factor involved in pathogenicity, as essential for infecting hybrid bamboo. However, the interacting target proteins and functions of ApCtf1β within the fungus remain unclear, limiting the comprehensive understanding of A. phaeospermum's pathogenic pathways. Therefore, this study employed yeast two‐hybrid, luciferase protein complementation, and GST pull‐down assays to detect and confirm ApCtf1β‐interacting proteins BDPH1 and BDEUL12. Further bioinformatics analyses of these proteins were conducted. Using Agrobacterium‐mediated fungal genetic transformation, we generated BDPH1 and BDEUL12 overexpression transformants, gene‐silenced transformants, as well as Apctf1β‐BDPH1 and Apctf1β‐BDEUL12 co‐expression transformants. Functional studies of these interacting proteins were performed at different developmental stages, examining gene expression, hyphal growth rate, spore production, chemical susceptibility, pathogenicity and ubiquitination function. The results indicated that BDPH1 and BDEUL12 are closely associated with the virulence of A. phaeospermum, with the BDEUL12 gene exerting a more potent effect on virulence. This study provides a foundation for further elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying the pathogenicity of A. phaeospermum.

Introduction

The cuticle, a unique biopolyester structure in higher plants, performs multiple essential functions that support plant life. Its primary role is as follows: first, it acts as a physical barrier against external stresses, regulating water exchange between plants and their environment while maintaining thermal balance, effectively preventing mechanical damage and pathogen invasion. Thus, it serves as the first line of defence in the plant innate immune system (Li et al. 2002; Wang et al. 2017). More importantly, recent research has shown that the cuticle also functions as a signalling hub during plant development, helping to transmit endogenous developmental signals and mediating transmembrane signal recognition and responses during plant–pathogen interactions. This shift from passive protection to active regulation highlights the indispensable biological value of the cuticle in plant growth, development and stress adaptation. Among plant‐pathogenic fungi, the transcriptional regulation mechanisms of cutinases are notably conserved. Both Fusarium solani and Aspergillus nidulans depend on the cutinase transcription factor CTF1 to activate cutinase gene expression (Li and Kolattukudy 1997; Li et al. 2002). However, the role of cutinase in pathogenicity varies between species: in F. solani f. sp. pisi infecting pea stems, secreted cutinase functions as a key virulence factor, and mutants with disrupted cutinase genes show a significantly reduced capacity to invade hosts (Rogers et al. 1994). Similarly, knockout of the ClCUT7 gene in Curvularia lunata substantially decreases its virulence against maize leaves (Liu et al. 2016). Importantly, studies in other pathogen systems further demonstrate that deletion of cutinase‐encoding genes diminishes pathogen virulence or completely abolishes their infection ability (Davies et al. 2000; Li et al. 2003; Liu et al. 2016; Skamnioti and Gurr 2007; Zhang et al. 2006), confirming that this enzyme plays an irreplaceable role in most pathogen–host interactions.

Pathogenic fungal factors interact with specific proteins to synergistically regulate growth, development and disease processes at multiple levels, forming a complex infection regulatory network (Du et al. 2021; Li et al. 2022; Qin et al. 2025; Shang et al. 2024). For example, in Magnaporthe oryzae, the virulence factor MoMad1 interacts with the kinase MoMps1, forming a key signalling pathway that regulates pathogen development and pathogenicity. Further studies revealed that MoMps1 binds to MoMad1, which is localised to the nuclear envelope, via its N‐terminal domain, and phosphorylates MoMad1 at the conserved Ser‐18 site on its N‐terminus. This interaction system plays a central regulatory role in the pathogenicity process of M. oryzae (Chen et al. 2024). The peroxidase gene BcPRD7 positively regulates the growth, development and virulence of Botrytis cinerea. Experiments confirmed an interaction between BcPRD7 and BcHEX, suggesting that BcPRD7 may influence Woronin body function through this interaction, thereby participating in the regulation of pathogen growth, virulence, and maintenance of cellular integrity (Zhang et al. 2025). In Colletotrichum siamense, the virulence factor CsVosA interacts with the protein CsVelB to form a functional complex that synergistically regulates fungal growth, development and pathogenicity. CsVosA and CsVelB maintain cell wall integrity by regulating chitin metabolism and distribution, while controlling the formation and viability of both asexual and sexual spores. This plays a crucial role in maintaining the integrity of the cell wall and conidial viability (Gao et al. 2023). Furthermore, the pathogenicity factor Sgh1 in Fusarium graminearum influences fungal development and pathogenicity by regulating pre‐mRNA processing. Notably, Sgh1 directly interacts with the SR‐specific kinase Srk1, revealing the critical role of post‐transcriptional regulation in fungal pathogenicity (Wang et al. 2022). However, most studies have focused on the functional analysis of pathogenicity factors without exploring the functions of the identified interacting proteins. This study not only identified interacting proteins of the cutinase transcription factor but also employed multiple research approaches to thoroughly investigate the functions of interacting proteins BDPH1 and BDEUL12 and their roles in Arthrinium phaeospermum.

Bamboo shoot blight in Bambusa pervariabilis × Dendrocalamopsis grandis is a devastating disease caused by A. phaeospermum, leading to widespread mortality of hybrid bamboo and resulting in severe ecological and economic losses (Li et al. 2013). Current research on hybrid bamboo shoot blight primarily focuses on several areas: patterns of disease occurrence, biological characteristics of the pathogen, virulence testing of pathogenic toxin proteins, screening and identification of biocontrol agents and multi‐omics studies of host bamboo under pathogen infection or toxin protein induction (Li et al. 2013, 2020). In contrast, research on identifying pathogenicity genes of this pathogen and analysing their functions remains relatively scarce. Previous studies have combined transcriptomic analyses of A. phaeospermum under different cultivation conditions with its interaction transcriptomics against B. pervariabilis × D. grandis, identifying the key transcription factor gene ApCtf1β involved in hybrid bamboo infection (Fang et al. 2022, 2023). However, screening and functional studies of its interacting target proteins in fungi remain unexplored, limiting a comprehensive understanding of the pathogenic pathway of A. phaeospermum. Therefore, in this study, we screened and identified the interacting target proteins of ApCtf1β in A. phaeospermum and explored its subcellular localisation. Agrobacterium‐mediated fungal genetic transformation was employed to generate overexpression and silenced transformants of BDPH1 and BDEUL12, as well as their co‐expression transformants with ApCtf1β, thereby verifying the functions of the interacting targets. This study lays a solid foundation for the ultimate elucidation of the complete pathogenic signalling pathway mediated by ApCtf1β.

Results

BDPH1 and BDEUL12 Identified as Interacting Proteins of ApCtf1β

Homologous recombination was used to generate the recombinant bait vector pGBKT7‐ApCtf1β (Figure S1A). The bait vector was successfully transformed into yeast cells, and self‐activation assays indicated only mild autonomous transcriptional activity in the yeast cells. The yeast strain harbouring the bait vector showed growth comparable to that of the empty vector control, indicating that the bait protein was not toxic to yeast and satisfied the prerequisite for yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) library screening (Figure S2).

After screening the library using the SOP mating method, 38 positive clones were obtained (Figure S3). PCR analysis using positive strains as templates revealed that the inserted fragments varied in size among the different clones. Sequencing and alignment of single‐band PCR products ultimately identified nine candidate proteins that potentially interact with pGBKT7‐ApCtf1β: BDPH1 (deubiquitination‐protection protein dph1), BDEUL12 (E3 ubiquitin‐protein ligase trip12), BDISA‐1 (importin subunit alpha‐1), BDECM4 (related to ECM4 protein), BDGHE (glycoside hydrolase), BDHPT (hypothetical protein THITE), BDNAD‐SGD (NAD‐specific glutamate dehydrogenase), BDSFH (snf2 family helicase) and BDLCTH (P‐loop containing nucleoside triphosphate hydrolase protein). Results from the one‐to‐one validation assays demonstrated that ApCtf1β interacted with BDPH1, BDEUL12, BDISA‐1 and BDECM4, but no interaction was observed between ApCtf1β and BDSFH, BDHPT, BDNAD‐SGD, BDGHE or BDLCTH (Figure 1a).

(c) In vitro interaction results between ApCtf1β and BDPH1, BDEUL12 by glutathione S‐transferase (GST) pulldown assay.

Subsequently, protein–protein interactions were validated in vivo using luciferase complementation assays (LCA). The constructs cluc‐ApCtf1β, nluc‐BDPH1, nluc‐BDEUL12, nluc‐BDISA‐1 and nluc‐BDECM4 were successfully generated and introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 (Figure S1B,C). Luminescence signals were detected in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves co‐infiltrated with cluc‐ApCtf1β + nluc‐BDPH1 or cluc‐ApCtf1β + nluc‐BDEUL12, whereas no signal was observed in leaves co‐infiltrated with cluc‐ApCtf1β + nluc‐BDISA‐1 or cluc‐ApCtf1β + nluc‐BDECM4, or in any of the corresponding control groups (Figure 1b). These results confirm that the bait protein ApCtf1β interacted with prey proteins BDPH1 and BDEUL12, but not with BDISA‐1 or BDECM4, in plant cells. Therefore, BDPH1 and BDEUL12 were selected for further in vitro validation studies.

To validate these interactions in vitro, glutathione S‐transferase (GST) pull‐down assays were performed. The recombinant vectors pGEX‐6P‐1‐ApCtf1β, pET28a‐BDPH1 and pET28a‐BDEUL12 were constructed using homologous recombination (Figure S1D,E). The predicted molecular weights of the fusion proteins were approximately 49 kDa for GST‐ApCtf1β, 62.03 kDa for His‐BDPH1 and 53.53 kDa for His‐BDEUL12. SDS‐PAGE analysis confirmed the presence of target bands at the expected sizes (Figure S4). GST pull‐down assay results demonstrated that ApCtf1β physically interacted with both BDPH1 and BDEUL12 in vitro (Figure 1c).

Bioinformatics Analysis of BDPH1 and BDEUL12

The BDPH1 ORF spanned 1422 bp, encoding a protein of 473 amino acids (aa) with a relative molecular mass of 47.6 kDa. Its isoelectric point is 5.01, indicating acidity, and its instability index is 42.42, which exceeds 40, classifying BDPH1 as an unstable protein. The protein hydrophobicity index (GRAVY) was < 0, confirming that BDPH1 is a hydrophilic protein. The BDEUL12 ORF spanned 1262 bp, encoding a protein of 409 aa with a relative molecular mass of 44.3 kDa and an isoelectric point of 5.39, indicating its acidic nature. The instability index was 38.11, classifying BDEUL12 as a stable protein. The protein hydrophobicity index (GRAVY) was < 0, indicating that BDEUL12 is a hydrophilic protein. The BDPH1 protein contains two distinct domains: Ubl and UBA (Figure 2a). The BDEUL12 protein contains a characteristic HECT domain (Figure 3a). The phylogenetic tree constructed using BDPH1 protein as a reference indicated that BDPH1 shares a close evolutionary relationship with Apiospora marii (Figure 2b). Similarly, a phylogenetic tree constructed using the BDEUL12 protein as a reference showed that BDEUL12 is closely related to A. marii (Figure 3b). Multiple sequence alignment using MEME software revealed 10 conserved motifs in BDPH1 and BDEUL12 proteins with high sequence conservation: in BDPH1 six motifs (motifs 1–3, motifs 5–7) were identified, with motif 1 scoring the highest confidence (Figure 2d); in BDEUL12 10 motifs (motifs 1–10) were identified, with motif 8 having the highest confidence score (Figure 3d). Neither BDPH1 nor BDEUL12 possesses any transmembrane domains (Figure S5A,B). Both BDPH1 and BDEUL12 proteins have predicted phosphorylation sites for three types of protein kinases: serine, threonine and tyrosine (Figure S5C,D). The PredictNLS online tool predicted cellular localisation, indicating that BDPH1 localises to the plasma membrane (Figure 2c). BDEUL12 also is also predicted to localise to the plasma membrane (Figure 3c).

Secondary structure prediction of the amino acid sequences of BDPH1 and BDEUL12 via the SPOMA website revealed that both proteins consist of three secondary structures: α‐helix, random coil and extended strand (Figure S5E,F). Specifically, BDPH1 contains α‐helix (28.3%), random coil (65.8%) and extended strand (5.9%), whereas BDEUL12 comprises α‐helix (49.0%), random coil (39.6%) and extended strand (11.4%). Tertiary structure homology modelling of BDPH1 and BDEUL12 was performed using SWISS‐MODEL. The comparison results showed that the overall model quality evaluation (GMQE) score for BDPH1 was 0.60 (Figure 2e), whereas that for BDEUL12 was 0.88 (Figure 3e). The interacting proteins for BDPH1 and BDEUL12 were predicted using the protein interaction database STRING, identifying 10 potential interacting proteins for each gene (Figures 2f and 3f).

BDPH1 and BDEUL12 Are Localised to the Cell Membrane

To determine the subcellular localisation of BDPH1 and BDEUL12, expression plasmids pSuper1300‐BDPH1 and pSuper1300‐BDEUL12 were constructed and introduced into A. tumefaciens GV3101 (Figure S6A). N. benthamiana leaves infiltrated with agrobacteria expressing RFP‐BDPH1 or RFP‐BDEUL12 fusion proteins showed EGFP fluorescence at the plasma membrane when observed using laser scanning confocal microscopy (Figure 4). These findings indicate that BDPH1 and BDEUL12 are localised to the plasma membrane.

BDPH1 and BDEUL12 Participate in the Growth and Development of A. phaeospermum and Its Response to Chemical Stress

To elucidate the functions of the BDPH1 and BDEUL12 genes, PCR amplification was performed to obtain fragments of BDPH1, BDEUL12, ApCtf1β and Intron. Overexpression, co‐expression, and silencing vectors for BDPH1 and BDEUL12 were constructed (Figure S6B–D). Using the A. tumefaciens‐mediated transformation (ATMT) method, we obtained overexpression strains OE‐BDPH1 and OE‐BDEUL12, silencing strains KD‐BDPH1 and KD‐BDEUL12, and co‐expression strains COE‐ApCtf1β+BDPH1 and COE‐ApCtf1β+BDEUL12 (Figures S7 and S8).

To determine whether BDPH1 and BDEUL12 affect the vegetative growth of A. phaeospermum, wild‐type (WT) and transformed strains were cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA). The results showed that the OE‐BDPH1 strain grew significantly slower than the WT, COE‐ApCtf1β+BDPH1 and KD‐BDPH1 strains. In contrast, COE‐ApCtf1β+BDEUL12 grew significantly faster than OE‐BDEUL12, WT and KD‐BDEUL12 (Figure 5a,b,e,f). After 21 days of culture on PDA, conidia production was quantified using a haemocytometer. The KD‐BDPH1 strain produced significantly more spores than the WT, COE‐ApCtf1β+BDPH1 and OE‐BDPH1 strains. Conversely, both OE‐BDEUL12 and COE‐ApCtf1β+BDEUL12 exhibited significantly higher spore yields than the WT and KD‐BDEUL12 strains (Figure 5c,d,g,h).

Gene expression levels in overexpression, silencing, and co‐expression strains were assessed at different developmental stages using reverse transcription‐quantitative real‐time PCR (RT‐qPCR). As shown in Figure 5i,j, compared to the WT strain, the expression level of the BDPH1 gene was significantly upregulated in OE‐BDPH1 and significantly downregulated in KD‐BDPH1 at 5, 10 and 15 days of culture. Compared with the WT strain, the expression level of the BDEUL12 gene was significantly upregulated in COE‐BDEUL12+ApCtf1β and OE‐BDEUL12, whereas it was significantly downregulated in KD‐BDEUL12. In comparison with days 5 and 15, the relative expression levels of BDPH1 in OE‐BDPH1 and BDEUL12 in COE‐BDEUL12+ApCtf1β both peaked on day 10 of culture.

To verify whether BDPH1 and BDEUL12 affect the adaptability of A. phaeospermum to chemical stress, we analysed the growth phenotypes of transformants on PDA supplemented with different stress reagents, and the results showed that they varied significantly (Figure 6a,h). First, on PDA containing Congo Red (CR), SDS, H2O2, or dithiothreitol (DTT), OE‐BDPH1 and KD‐BDEUL12 were more sensitive to these four chemical stresses than the WT strain. The mycelial growth diameters of OE‐BDPH1 and KD‐BDEUL12 were significantly smaller than those of the WT. In contrast, the mycelial growth diameters of the KD‐BDPH1 and OE‐BDEUL12 transformants were significantly larger than those of the WT (Figure 6b–e,i–l). To compare the sensitivity differences between transformant strains and the WT strain under osmotic stress, on PDA containing sodium chloride, there was no difference in growth between the transformant strains and the WT strain (Figure 6f,m). On PDA containing calcium chloride, KD‐BDPH1, COE‐ApCtf1β+BDEUL12 and OE‐BDEUL12 showed significantly reduced tolerance to calcium chloride compared with the WT strain. The mycelial growth diameters of KD‐BDPH1, COE‐ApCtf1β+BDEUL12 and OE‐BDEUL12 were significantly smaller than those of the WT (Figure 6g,n). These results indicate that BDPH1 and BDEUL12 are involved in the regulation of cell wall integrity and endoplasmic reticulum function in A. phaeospermum, and participate in the response of A. phaeospermum to heavy metal stress and oxidative stress, but not in its cellular osmotic homeostasis.

BDPH1 and BDEUL12 Regulate the Pathogenicity of A. phaeospermum

To assess the effects of BDPH1 and BDEUL12 on the pathogenicity of A. phaeospermum, assays were performed on young shoots and leaves of B. pervariabilis × D. grandis using mycelial suspensions from WT, overexpressing (OE), co‐expressing (COE), and silenced (KD) strains. The results revealed that symptoms induced by OE‐BDPH1, COE‐ApCtf1β + BDPH1, and KD‐BDEUL12 were significantly milder than those caused by the WT strain (Figure 7a,b). The disease index increased over time for all inoculated strains. Specifically, the disease index of KD‐BDPH1 was higher than that of OE‐BDPH1 and COE‐ApCtf1β+BDPH1, whereas COE‐ApCtf1β+BDEUL12 exhibited a higher disease index than OE‐BDEUL12 and KD‐BDEUL12 (Figure 7c,d). These findings indicate that BDPH1 and BDEUL12 play crucial roles in the virulence of A. phaeospermum. Overexpression of BDEUL12 enhanced fungal virulence, whereas overexpression of BDPH1 attenuated it.

7 days after inoculation with WT and transformed strains on detached bamboo shoots, distinct differences in infection patterns were observed among the experimental groups. Mycelial growth was significantly more extensive in the KD‐BDPH1 group than in the WT, OE‐BDPH1, and COE‐ApCtf1β+BDPH1 groups. Similarly, shoots inoculated with COE‐ApCtf1β+BDEUL12 and OE‐BDEUL12 supported markedly more abundant mycelial growth than those inoculated with WT or KD‐BDEUL12 (Figure 7e,f). Scanning electron microscopy revealed varying degrees of hyphal penetration through the epidermal layer in the WT, KD‐BDPH1, COE‐ApCtf1β+BDPH1, COE‐ApCtf1β+BDEUL12 and OE‐BDEUL12 groups. In contrast, no hyphal penetration was observed in the shoots inoculated with OE‐BDPH1 or KD‐BDEUL12 (Figure 7g,h).

After inoculating hybrid bamboo with the WT, overexpression, co‐expression and knockdown strains, all experimental groups showed significant alterations in the activities of antioxidant enzymes (catalase [CAT], polyphenol oxidase [PPO], peroxidase [POD] and superoxide dismutase [SOD]) compared with the control group (non‐inoculated), and the four antioxidant enzymes showed a similar trend in their activity changes. The antioxidant enzyme activities in the KD‐BDPH1 treatment group were significantly higher than those in the WT and COE‐ApCtf1β+BDPH1 treatment groups, whereas the enzyme activities in the WT treatment group were significantly higher than those in the OE‐BDPH1 treatment group (Figure 8a–d). The antioxidant enzyme activities in the COE‐ApCtf1β+BDEUL12 and OE‐BDEUL12 treatment groups were significantly higher than those in the WT treatment group, and the enzyme activities in the WT treatment group were significantly higher than those in the KD‐BDEUL12 treatment group (Figure 8f–i). In addition, chlorophyll content determination revealed a significant decrease in chlorophyll content in all the experimental groups compared to the control group. The chlorophyll content in the COE‐ApCtf1β+BDEUL12, OE‐BDEUL12 and KD‐BDPH1 treatment groups was significantly lower than that in the WT treatment group, whereas the chlorophyll content in the WT treatment group was significantly lower than that in the KD‐BDEUL12 and OE‐BDPH1 treatment groups (Figure 8e,j).

BDPH1 and BDEUL12 Ubiquitinate ApCtf1β

To investigate whether BDPH1 and BDEUL12 can ubiquitinate ApCtf1β, purified ApCtf1β‐GST, BDPH1‐HIS, and BDEUL12‐HIS proteins obtained from the glutathione S‐transferase (GST) pull‐down assay were used in an in vitro ubiquitination assay. After a 6‐h substrate ubiquitination reaction, western blot analysis was performed. The results showed a diffuse high‐molecular‐weight complex, known as a ubiquitin ladder, in the lane containing E1, E2, ubiquitin, BDPH1‐HIS (E3) and ApCtf1β‐GST proteins (Figure 7i). Similarly, a diffuse high‐molecular‐weight complex was observed in the lane containing E1, E2, ubiquitin, BDEUL12‐HIS (E3) and ApCtf1β‐GST proteins (Figure 7j), whereas no ubiquitin ladder was detected in any of the negative controls. These findings indicate that BDPH1‐HIS and BDEUL12‐HIS can ubiquitinate the substrate ApCtf1β‐GST, forming high‐molecular‐weight (Ubi)n‐ApCtf1β‐GST complexes. These results demonstrate that BDPH1 and BDEUL12 can ubiquitinate ApCtf1β.

To determine whether BDPH1 and BDEUL12 affect the stability of the ApCtf1β protein, knockout and complementation vectors for BDPH1 and BDEUL12 were constructed. Both knockout vectors were simultaneously introduced into A. tumefaciens AGL1, and a double knockout mutant, ∆BDPH1:BDEUL12, was obtained through genetic transformation. The method for obtaining the complemented strain ∆BDPH1:BDEUL12+ was the same as that described above (Figures S9 and [Link], [Link], [Link]). Western blotting results showed that when ApCtf1β‐HIS was co‐incubated with total protein from the WT, the amount of ApCtf1β‐HIS protein continuously decreased over the incubation period. However, when the 26S proteasome inhibitor MG132 was added, the degradation of ApCtf1β‐HIS protein was significantly suppressed (Figure 8m), indicating that ApCtf1β is degraded via the 26S proteasome pathway. When ApCtf1β‐HIS protein was co‐incubated with total protein from the double‐knockout mutant ∆BDPH1:BDEUL12, the amount of ApCtf1β‐HIS protein did not change significantly over the incubation period. In contrast, co‐incubation with total protein from the complemented strain ∆BDPH1:BDEUL12+ resulted in a decrease in ApCtf1β‐HIS protein levels over time (Figure 8n). Together, these results demonstrate that BDPH1 and BDEUL12 mediate ApCtf1β protein degradation (The complete protein electrophoresis image is shown in Figure S14).

To determine whether BDPH1 and BDEUL12 affect the transcriptional activity of ApCtf1β, we constructed recombinant vectors SK62‐ApCtf1β‐BDPH1, SK62‐ApCtf1β, SK62‐ApCtf1β‐BDEUL12 and 0800‐Ap12‐pro (Figure S15). N. benthamiana leaf infiltration was performed after successful transformation into Agrobacterium. The fluorescence imaging system and luciferase activity assays showed that the relative firefly luciferase activity was lowest in the empty vector control group, while the SK62‐ApCtf1β‐BDPH1 and SK62‐ApCtf1β‐BDEUL12 groups exhibited higher relative firefly luciferase activity than the SK62‐ApCtf1β group (Figure 8o). These findings suggest that BDPH1 and BDEUL12 significantly enhanced the regulatory activity of ApCtf1β on the gene promoters.

Discussion

Using ApCtf1β as a bait, we identified two interacting proteins using Y2H, luciferase complementation assay and GST‐pull down. These methods identified and validated two interacting proteins: the deubiquitinating protective protein BDPH1 and HECT‐type E3 ubiquitin ligase BDEUL12. Extensive studies have demonstrated that plant pathogen cutinases and their interacting proteins correlate with virulence, such as the epidermal enzyme of F. solani and the CUT2 enzyme of Magnaporthe grisea (Morid et al. 2009; Skamnioti and Gurr 2007). Cutinase degrades the cuticle layer of tomato leaves, thereby promoting pathogen invasion (Ueda et al. 2018, 2021). The transcription factor FvCtf1α in Fusarium verticillioides not only regulates cutinase expression but also participates in diverse virulence‐related pathways, including toxin biosynthesis (Peng et al. 2024). In addition, certain cutinase genes or cutinase transcription factors may interact with host proteins related to disease resistance. The cutinase transcription factor ApCtf1β interacts with BDUbc and BDSKL1 in B. pervariabilis × D. grandis . The BDUbc and BDSKL1 genes likely contribute to resistance against shoot blight in B. pervariabilis × D. grandis as disease resistance genes, potentially exhibiting synergistic effects (Yan et al. 2024). These studies screened for interacting proteins within the host, and the functions of these proteins are associated with disease resistance. In contrast, the interacting proteins BDPH1 and BDEUL12 in this study were identified from A. phaeospermum. Therefore, BDPH1 and BDEUL12 may function as pathogenicity genes that enhance the ability of A. phaeospermum to invade the host. Notably, this study is the first to report BDPH1 and BDEUL12 proteins as interacting targets of cutinase transcription factors in plant‐pathogenic fungi.

Protein domain analysis of BDPH1 and BDEUL12 revealed fundamentally distinct functional bases. BDPH1 contained a Ubl‐UBA composite domain (Figure 2a), suggesting potential involvement in the ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway (e.g., substrate recognition or ubiquitin chain binding), consistent with the known function of UBA domains in mediating ubiquitin signalling (Heinen et al. 2010, 2011). The single HECT domain of BDEUL12 (Figure 3a) probably indicates its core function as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, catalysing the transfer of ubiquitin molecules to target proteins. Both proteins are acidic and hydrophilic (pI ≈5.0, GRAVY < 0). BDPH1 is unstable (instability index 42.42), potentially reflecting its involvement in dynamically regulated ubiquitination processes. Conversely, BDEUL12's stability (instability index 38.11) may be related to the structural rigidity requirements for its enzymatic function. Phylogenetic analysis indicated that both proteins were most closely related to A. marii (Figures 2b and 3b), suggesting functional conservation across fungi. Multiple sequence alignment further confirmed that the six conserved motifs in BDPH1 (particularly high‐confidence Motif 1) probably correspond to key functional sites within the Ubl‐UBA domain (Figure 2d). The 10 conserved motifs in BDEUL12 (with Motif 8 exhibiting the highest confidence) probably constitute the HECT domain catalytic core (Figure 3d). This high conservation suggests that both proteins fulfil irreplaceable biological roles in ascomycetes. Additionally, both contain serine/threonine/tyrosine phosphorylation sites (Figure S5C,D), indicating that their activity may be dynamically regulated by kinase pathways.

This study demonstrated that the COE‐ApCtf1β+BDEUL12 and OE‐BDEUL12 strains exhibited significantly enhanced growth rates, mycelial biomass, and sporulation on PDA compared to the WT and KD‐BDEUL12 strains. In contrast, the KD‐BDPH1 strain showed markedly increased growth, mycelial yield, and spore production compared to the WT and OE‐BDPH1 strains. Together with previous reports on CsGLOX in Colletotrichum siamense (Xian et al. 2023), these results suggest that BDEUL12 may facilitate host plant infection by promoting the growth and development of A. phaeospermum. Furthermore, BDEUL12 probably enhances sporulation capacity, thereby increasing reproductive potential and resistance to unfavourable conditions. Chemical stress assays were used to evaluate whether the target genes influenced fungal responses to various stressors. This study found that OE‐BDPH1, COE‐ApCtf1β+BDEUL12 and KD‐BDEUL12 transformants exhibited significantly increased sensitivity to CR, SDS, H2O2 and DTT compared to the WT strain. This finding aligns with the altered stress response sensitivity observed following the deletion of the MoPTEN gene in M. grisea (Wang et al. 2021). Therefore, it is speculated that the BDPH1 and BDEUL12 genes may play roles in maintaining cell wall integrity and mediating responses to external oxidative stress in A. phaeospermum. Additionally, deletion of BDPH1 and overexpression of BDEUL12 resulted in decreased calcium ion (Ca2+) resistance in A. phaeospermum, suggesting that Ca2+ may influence the enzymatic activity of BDPH1 and BDEUL12. These findings are consistent with changes in heavy metal stress sensitivity caused by the deletion of ApSDR53C2 and ApSDR548U2 in A. phaeospermum (Liao et al. 2025). The results of this study indicate that BDPH1 and BDEUL12 are involved in regulating cell wall integrity and endoplasmic reticulum function and contribute to the osmotic and oxidative stress responses of A. phaeospermum. These findings align with the existing literature and underscore the significant roles of BDPH1 and BDEUL12 in mediating adaptation to diverse adverse environmental conditions.

The pathogenicity of plant‐pathogenic fungi arises from long‐term coevolution with their hosts, in which key genes, such as those regulating virulence factors, infection structure development and signal transduction, act as molecular switches governing infectious processes. Functional studies of these genes help clarify the mechanisms by which fungi overcome plant immunity (e.g., through the secretion of cell wall‐degrading enzymes and effector‐mediated suppression of host defences) and adapt to the host microenvironment (e.g., via nutrient acquisition and stress tolerance), as well as how plants recognise pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) to initiate immune responses (De Lorenzo et al. 2011; Parker and Köller 1998; Zhang et al. 2014). The results of this study indicate that, compared to the WT strain, the COE‐ApCtf1β+BDEUL12, OE‐BDEUL12 and KD‐BDPH1 strains caused more severe wilting of the leaves and branches of hybrid bamboo following inoculation. Measurements of the disease indices and chlorophyll content further corroborated the observed phenotypic symptoms. These results are consistent with previous reports on F. verticillioides FvCtf1α and the chitinase Bdo_10846 in Botryosphaeria dothidea (Dong et al. 2021; Peng et al. 2024). It can be observed that the BDPH1 and BDEUL12 genes are associated with the virulence of A. phaeospermum. After invasion by pathogenic fungi, plants accumulate reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are toxic to the plants. Antioxidant enzymes can eliminate ROS, and the activity levels of these enzymes can partially reflect the plant's ability to resist fungal invasion, as well as indicate the strength of fungal virulence (Bolwell et al. 2002; Liu et al. 2025; Mandal et al. 2009). In this study, the activities of the antioxidant enzymes POD, CAT, PPO, and SOD were significantly higher in the COE‐ApCtf1β+BDEUL12, OE‐BDEUL12 and KD‐BDPH1 treatment groups than in the WT, whereas the activities in the KD‐BDEUL12 and OE‐BDPH1 treatment groups were significantly lower than those in the WT. These findings indicate that the COE‐ApCtf1β+BDEUL12, OE‐BDEUL12 and KD‐BDPH1 strains exhibit stronger virulence. These results confirm that BDEUL12 and BDPH1 are closely associated with the virulence of A. phaeospermum, with BDEUL12 playing a more prominent role. In experiments involving the inoculation of transformant mycelia on detached young shoots of hybrid bamboo, followed by scanning electron microscopy, it was found that the WT strain KD‐BDPH1 and the OE‐BDEUL12 strain penetrated the surface layer of the hybrid bamboo young shoots. In contrast, OE‐BDPH1 and KD‐BDEUL12 strains did not penetrate the surface layer. These observations align with the reported role of the transcription factor CgHSF1 in the virulence of Colletotrichum gloeosporioides on rubber trees (Gao et al. 2022). Thus, it is speculated that the BDPH1 and BDEUL12 genes are significantly associated with the ability of A. phaeospermum to penetrate the cuticle of hybrid bamboo.

This study demonstrated through in vitro ubiquitination assays that both the deubiquitinating enzyme BDPH1 and the HECT‐type E3 ubiquitin ligase BDEUL12 from A. phaeospermum are capable of ubiquitinating the transcription factor ApCtf1β. This finding implies that BDPH1 and BDEUL12 may contribute to the precise modulation of fungal virulence via ubiquitin‐mediated pathways. The ubiquitin system plays a central role in regulating pathogen–host interactions. Our results are consistent with previously reported ubiquitination‐based regulatory strategies in various plant–microbe systems. For example, the rice E3 ligase SPL11 enhances immunity by ubiquitinating and degrading the pathogenicity factor SPIN6 (Liu et al. 2015), and the Phytophthora sojae effector Avr1d facilitates infection by mimicking E2 activity to inhibit the host E3 ligase GmPUB13 (Lin et al. 2021). In vitro protein degradation experiments confirmed that ApCtf1β is degraded via the 26S proteasome pathway, which depends on the participation of BDPH1 and BDEUL12. This finding reveals a key molecular mechanism regulating the homeostasis of ApCtf1β and provides important clues for understanding the functional regulation of this protein in related physiological and pathological processes. Dual‐luciferase reporter gene assays showed that BDPH1 and BDEUL12 significantly enhanced ApCtf1β's transcriptional activation activity toward the downstream cutinase gene promoter. Together with the previous finding that BDPH1 and BDEUL12 promote the degradation of the ApCtf1β protein, these results outline a dynamic and precise regulatory model in which BDPH1 and BDEUL12 simultaneously promote ApCtf1β's transcriptional activity while limiting its protein accumulation via the ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway. This seemingly paradoxical dual regulation has a profound biological rationale and may constitute a classic “activate‐and‐degrade” feedback control loop (Auwardt et al. 2000; Gantke et al. 2011). Its potential biological significance lies in enabling a rapid and reversible signalling response: BDPH1 and BDEUL12 can quickly enhance the transcriptional function of the existing ApCtf1β protein to promptly initiate downstream gene expression; meanwhile, by promoting the degradation of ApCtf1β, they prevent prolonged transcriptional signalling, allowing A. phaeospermum to swiftly respond to new stimuli and environmental changes. This study reveals a complex regulatory network mediated by BDPH1 and BDEUL12, which coordinately modulates the function and homeostasis of ApCtf1β. Future research may further focus on the specific sites where BDPH1 and BDEUL12 mediate the ubiquitination of ApCtf1β, as well as the specific molecular mechanisms by which BDPH1 and BDEUL12 enhance the transcriptional activity of ApCtf1β.

In summary, this study successfully identified two key interacting proteins of ApCtf1β, namely BDPH1 and BDEUL12. We confirmed that BDPH1 and BDEUL12, as membrane proteins, not only participate in regulating the growth and development of A. phaeospermum but also affect its resistance to extracellular oxidative stress and endoplasmic reticulum stress, and play critical roles in its pathogenic process. More importantly, this study found that BDPH1 and BDEUL12 mediate the ubiquitination of ApCtf1β, enhance its transcriptional activity, and simultaneously limit its protein accumulation via the ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway. Collectively, these findings not only deepen our understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms underlying phytopathogenic fungi but also clarify the functions of the target interacting proteins of ApCtf1β, thus laying a solid foundation for elucidating the molecular mechanisms governing the pathogenicity of A. phaeospermum.

Experimental Procedures

Total RNA Extraction and cDNA Synthesis From A. phaeospermum

Arthrinium phaeospermum was cultured at 25°C for 7 days. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method. The concentration and purity of the RNA were measured using a Thermofisher microvolume spectrophotometer. RNA integrity was further evaluated using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Samples that met the quality criteria for downstream applications were selected for cDNA synthesis. First‐strand cDNA was synthesised using a commercial reverse‐transcription kit.

Screening and Identification of Apctf1β‐Interacting Proteins

The coding sequence of Apctf1β was cloned into the bait vector pGBKT7 to generate the plasmid pGBKT7‐Apctf1β, which was subsequently transformed into the yeast strain Y2H Gold (The sequence information of the pGBKT7 vector is located in Figure S16). After confirming that the construct exhibited no self‐activation activity, a Y2H library screening was conducted (Chandramohan and Gupta 2013).

The pGBKT7‐Apctf1β plasmid was transformed into yeast competent cells. A single colony grown on SD/−Trp medium was selected for PCR verification. The confirmed positive yeast colony was inoculated into 50 mL of SD/−Trp liquid medium and cultured overnight. Then, 1 mL of the A. phaeospermum library culture and 5 mL of the pGBKT7‐Apctf1β yeast culture were transferred into a 2 L sterile conical flask containing 45 mL of 2× YPDA liquid medium. The mixture was incubated at 30°C with shaking at 40 rpm for 24 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 1000 g for 10 min and resuspended in 10 mL of 0.9% NaCl. The suspension was plated onto both SD/−Trp/−Leu/X‐α‐gal/aureobasidin A [AbA] and SD/−Trp/−Leu/−His/X‐α‐gal/AbA media. Blue colonies obtained from the screening were further selected on SD/−Trp/−Leu/−His/−Ade/X‐α‐gal/AbA medium. Colonies that remained viable and turned blue on this quadruple‐dropout medium were subjected to PCR validation and sequencing. The primers used for the Y2H assay are listed in Table S1.

Plasmids were extracted from blue colonies grown on SD/−Trp/−Leu/−His/−Ade/X‐α‐gal/AbA medium using a yeast plasmid extraction kit. The extracted plasmids were transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells and plated onto Luria Bertani (LB) solid medium supplemented with 50 mg/L ampicillin. After incubation at 37°C for 16 h, a single colony was selected for PCR verification. Positive bacterial cultures were sequenced, and plasmids with the correct sequences were extracted and stored for further use (The sequence information of the pGADT7 vector is located in Figure S17). The pGBKT7‐Apctf1β plasmid and candidate positive interaction plasmids were co‐transformed into yeast competent cells. The transformants were incubated on SD/−Trp/−Leu/−His/−Ade/X‐α‐gal/AbA medium for 3–5 days. Candidate interaction proteins that induced blue colouration were considered positive and subjected to subsequent experiments.

To validate protein–protein interactions in vivo, an LCA was performed (Li and Zhang 2014). Vectors pCAMBIA1300‐nluc and pCAMBIA1300‐cluc were digested with KpnI and SalI. The four positive candidate genes identified from Y2H screening were ligated into pCAMBIA1300‐nluc, while Apctf1β was ligated into pCAMBIA1300‐cluc, resulting in five recombinant constructs. Five recombinant vectors, the empty vector pCAMBIA1300‐nluc, and the empty vector pCAMBIA1300‐cluc were individually transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101. Positive clones were selected and cultured for expansion, after which equal volumes of the bacterial suspensions were mixed according to the grouping of control and experimental groups. The mixtures were incubated statically in the dark for 2–3 h prior to agroinfiltration into N. benthamiana leaves. The infiltrated areas were maintained under high humidity in the dark for 24 h, followed by a 24‐h photoperiod. D‐Luciferin, a luciferase substrate, was applied to the injection sites and incubated in darkness for 5 min. Luminescence signals were captured using a multispectral in vivo imaging system (PlantView100) with an exposure time of 3 min. The primers used for LCA are listed in Table S2.

GST pull‐down assays were performed to validate protein–protein interactions in vitro (Smith and Johnson 1988). The vectors pGEX‐6P‐1 and pET28a were digested with BamHI at 37°C overnight and purified. The recovered ApCtf1β fragment was ligated into the linearised pGEX‐6P‐1 vector via homologous recombination, and the target gene fragment was ligated into the linearised pET28a vector, yielding the respective recombinant plasmids. The fusion proteins GST‐ApCtf1β, His‐BDPH1, and His‐BDEUL12 were expressed in E. coli and then purified. GST‐ApCtf1β (experimental group) and GST (control) were separately incubated with His‐BDPH1 or His‐BDEUL12 at 4°C overnight. Proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane for western blot analysis. The primers used for GST pull‐down are listed in Table S3.

Bioinformatics Analysis of Apctf1β‐Interacting Proteins

The physicochemical properties of the genes were analysed using the ExPasy online tool (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/), including protein sequence length (SL), molecular weight (MW), hydrophilicity (GAOH) and isoelectric point (PI). Conserved protein domains were predicted using NCBI CDD (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Amino acid sequence homology and phylogenetic analyses were performed using MEGA 7 software. A neighbour‐joining evolutionary tree was constructed with 1000 repetitions, with all other settings set to default values (Zhang et al. 2020). Conserved protein regions were predicted using MEME (Bailey et al. 2009) (https://meme‐suite.org/meme/tools/meme). Subcellular localisation analysis was performed using the WOLF PSORT (https://www.genscript.com/wolf)‐ Phosphorylation site prediction was conducted using the NetPhos 3.1 Server (http://www.cbs.predictprotein (https://www.predictprotein.org/)) was used for secondary structure prediction of the encoded protein, while SWISS‐MODEL (https://www.swissmodel.expasy.org/) was used for tertiary structure prediction. Protein interaction networks were predicted for BDPH1 and BDEUL12 genes using the STRING protein association network database (https://cn.string‐db.org/). The results were exported to Cytoscape software for further annotation and visualisation.

Subcellular Localisation of Apctf1β‐Interacting Proteins

Based on the sequences of the BDPH1 and BDEUL12 genes, specific primers super1300‐BDPH1‐F/R and super1300‐BDEUL12‐F/R (Table S4) were designed to amplify the insertion fragments. The vector pSuper1300‐GFP was digested with KpnI and incubated overnight at 37°C. The digested vector was purified and subsequently ligated with the BDPH1 and BDEUL12 fragments using a homologous recombination kit to generate recombinant plasmids. Plasmids with correct insertions were extracted and transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101. A single colony carrying the target plasmid was inoculated into 5 mL of LB medium with the appropriate antibiotic and cultured to the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 = 0.5–0.6). The bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 5000 g for 10 min at 25°C, resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES, 150 μM acetosyringone) to an OD600 of 1.0, and incubated at room temperature for 2–3 h. Bacterial suspensions containing different plasmids were mixed in equal volumes (Kokkirala et al. 2010). The mixtures were infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves, and fluorescence was observed under a laser scanning confocal microscope (FV3000; Olympus) after 48 h.

Construction of Overexpression, Co‐Expression and Silencing Vectors for the BDPH1 and BDEUL12 Genes

Primers were designed using Primer Premier v. 5.0 (Premier Biosoft International). The CDS regions of the BDPH1 and BDEUL12 genes were amplified with primers OE‐PH1/EUL12‐F/R to generate fragments for the overexpression vector (Table S5). The plasmid vector pCAMBIA1303 was digested with BamHI. The linearised pCAMBIA1303 was ligated with the overexpression insert using a homologous recombination kit to construct the recombinant plasmid. The recombinant plasmid was digested, and the CDS of the Apctf1β gene was amplified using primers COE‐Apctf1β‐F/R to produce the co‐expression cassette (Table S5). This cassette was ligated into the linearised vector via homologous recombination to generate the co‐expression vector. The forward and reverse sequences of the BDPH1 and BDEUL12 genes were amplified using recombinant primers PH1/EUL12‐Z/FX‐F/R. An intron fragment was amplified with primers PH1/EUL12‐PDK‐intron‐F/R using the pHANNIBAL plasmid as a template to obtain the segment required for the silencing vector (Table S5). The vector plasmid pCAMBIA1303 was digested with XhoI and BamHI. The linearised pCAMBIA1303 was ligated with the forward and reverse fragments and the intron sequence using a homologous recombination kit to assemble the silencing vectors.

Agrobacterium‐Mediated Genetic Transformation of A. phaeospermum

Agrobacterium tumefaciens AGL1 was transformed with the overexpression, co‐expression and silencing vectors. A single colony of AGL1 carrying the target vector was selected and cultured in LB liquid medium supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin for 48 h, followed by subculture for 12 h until the OD600 reached 0.75–1.0. Then, 3–4 mL of the bacterial culture was transferred into liquid induction medium (IM) containing 200 μM acetosyringone (AS) and 40 mM MES (2‐morpholinoethanesulfonic acid) and incubated in the dark for 12 h. Meanwhile, mycelia of A. phaeospermum were harvested and thoroughly ground. The ground mycelia were mixed with an equal volume of Agrobacterium suspension and spread onto IM solid medium supplemented with MES and AS. After incubation at 25°C in the dark for 2–3 days, visible single colonies formed on the IM solid medium were overlaid with PDA containing kanamycin and streptomycin, followed by further incubation at 25°C for 2–3 days. Emerging fungal colonies were promptly transferred onto fresh PDA with kanamycin for continued growth (Hu et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2019). After five rounds of subculture, genomic DNA was extracted from the transformed A. phaeospermum strains in each experimental group. PCR amplification was performed using primers HygR‐F/R to screen for positive transformants (Table S5).

Phenotypic Analysis and Stress Response of Genetically Transformed Lines

To compare differences in growth and sporulation among WT, overexpression (OE), co‐expression (COE) and silenced (KD) strains of A. phaeospermum, each strain was inoculated on PDA and cultured at 25°C under light for 7 days. The colony diameter of each strain was measured daily using the cross method, and growth curves were generated based on these measurements. The experiment was repeated three times with 10 plates per treatment. To assess sporulation capacity, each strain was cultured on PDA for 21 days. Conidia were harvested by eluting the plates with 20 mL of sterile water per plate, and the resulting suspension was filtered through sterile cotton. The conidial concentration was determined using a haemocytometer, and the total spore yield was calculated. The experiment was performed in triplicate (Fang et al. 2021). Data were subjected to ANOVA and multiple comparisons using the least significant difference (LSD) method in DPS software.

To examine the expression levels of BDPH1 and BDEUL12 genes at different developmental stages in both WT and transformed strains of A. phaeospermum, 500 μL of spore suspension from each strain was inoculated into 100 mL of potato dextrose liquid medium and cultured at 28°C with shaking at 200 rpm. Mycelia were collected at 5, 10 and 15 days post‐inoculation, flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until further use. Total RNA was extracted, and cDNA was synthesised. Tubulin from A. phaeospermum was used as an internal reference gene. Primers were designed for each target gene (Table S6) using Primer Premier 3.0 software to detect expression levels. Primer lengths ranged from 20 to 25 bp, and the amplified product sizes were between 100 and 200 bp. Gene expression was quantified using the 2(−ΔΔCt) method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). The quantitative PCR mixture contained 10 μL of mix, 1 μL of forward primer, 1 μL of reverse primer, 1 μL of cDNA and 7 μL of double‐distilled water. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 1 min; 40 cycles of 95°C for 30 s and 60°C for 15 s.

To investigate differences in sensitivity to cell wall stress, osmotic stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, heavy metal stress and extracellular oxidative stress among WT, overexpressing, co‐expressing and silenced strains (Cao et al. 2018; Feng et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2018), the strains were separately inoculated onto PDA supplemented with different concentrations of stress agents: sodium chloride for osmotic stress, H2O2 for oxidative stress, DTT for endoplasmic reticulum stress, SDS and CR for cell wall perturbation, and calcium chloride for heavy metal stress. Cultures were incubated at 25°C. Colony diameters were measured at specified time points to determine strain sensitivity to agents via mycelial growth and growth inhibition (Jiang et al. 2012). Three replicate colonies per strain were analysed in each experiment, with at least three independent replicates. Data were plotted using GraphPad Prism 10 and analysed for significance using SPSS v. 26.0 (p < 0.05).

Identification of Pathogenic Functions in Genetically Transformed Strains

Forty uniformly growing 2‐year‐old green hybrid bamboo plants were selected. Five plants were randomly chosen for inoculation with the WT strain mycelial suspension, which was sprayed onto the upper branches of each plant. The remaining 35 plants were inoculated with OE‐BDPH1, OE‐BDEUL12, COE‐Apctf1β+BDPH1, COE‐Apctf1β+BDEUL12, KD‐BDPH1, KD‐BDEUL12, and sterile water. Twenty‐five days post‐inoculation, the plants were misted every 12 h to maintain humidity. Disease incidence was assessed and graded according to the following criteria: Disease Severity Grading Criteria: Grade 0: No wilting observed. Grade 1: Less than 25% of the branches were wilted. Grade 2: 25%–50% of branches wilted (inclusive). Grade 3: 50%–75% branch wilting (inclusive). Grade 4: Over 75% of branches wilted. The Disease Index was calculated as follows (Martinelli et al. 2015): Disease Index = (∑(Disease Grade × Number of Infected Branches)/(Total Number of Branches × Highest Disease Grade)) × 100.

Tender shoots were selected from uniformly growing annual hybrid bamboo and cut into 5 cm segments. After rinsing with distilled water, both ends of each segment were sealed with paraffin wax to prevent desiccation. The segments were then subjected to scalding. Mycelial plugs (6 mm in diameter) were obtained from solid media cultures of WT, overexpression, co‐expression and silencing strains using a punch and inoculated onto the nodes of the tender shoots. The inoculated shoots were placed in sterile 90 mm Petri dishes, maintained at high humidity by daily spraying with sterile water, and cultured under a 25°C day/night cycle. After 7 days, 1–2 cm sections were excised from the inoculated regions of shoots in each experimental group and immediately fixed in electron microscopy fixative (e.g., 2.5% glutaraldehyde) at room temperature for 2 h. The samples were then rinsed three times with 0.1 M phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) for 15 min each. Next, they were post‐fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4) in the dark at room temperature for 1–2 h, followed by three additional washes with PBS under the same conditions. After dehydration and drying, the samples were observed using scanning electron microscopy (SU8100; HITACHI).

The activities of antioxidant enzymes (POD, CAT, PPO and SOD) were determined using commercial assay kits (Solarbio Science & Technology Co. Ltd.). Chlorophyll content was measured via the UV absorption method with the assay kit G0601W (Grace Biotechnology Co. Ltd.).

In Vitro Ubiquitination Experiments of BDPH1 and BDEUL12 Proteins

Previous studies have confirmed that the UBA and HECT domains are associated with ubiquitination functions (Hartmann‐Petersen et al. 2003; Medina et al. 2012; Sluimer and Distel 2018; Wilkinson et al. 2001). This study identified that the BDPH1 protein possesses a UBA domain, while the BDEUL12 protein contains a HECT domain. Therefore, in vitro ubiquitination experiments were conducted to investigate whether these two proteins can directly ubiquitinate ApCtf1β protein. Following the method described by Ma et al. (2021) with minor modifications, purified proteins from the GST pull‐down experiment were subjected to in vitro ubiquitination using the UbiBio in vitro ubiquitination kit. Negative controls, which lacked E1 and E2, were prepared separately. Mixed samples were added to 200 μL PCR tubes and incubated at 37°C in a metal bath for 6 h. After the reaction, 5 μL of 5× SDS‐PAGE buffer (supplemented with 1 μL of freshly prepared 1 M DTT) was added to each system. Samples were boiled at 95°C for 10 min, followed by loading 10 μL onto SDS‐PAGE gels. Substrate protein ubiquitination was subsequently detected using the corresponding antibodies.

To investigate whether BDPH1 and BDEUL12 affect the stability of ApCtf1β protein, the ApCtf1β gene was first amplified using specific primers, and the vector plasmid pCAMBIA1303 was digested with BamHI. Ligation was performed via homologous recombination to obtain recombinant plasmids. The primers used in this experiment are in Table S7. The recombinant plasmid was transformed into A. tumefaciens AGL1, and the transformant AP‐193 was obtained using an Agrobacterium‐mediated genetic transformation method for A. phaeospermum. The strategy employed for the knockout of BDPH1 and BDEUL12 in this study is shown in Figure S10. For knockout complementation, the BDPH1 and BDEUL12 genes were ligated to the kanMx gene via complementary base pairing and fusion PCR. Subsequently, the fusion fragment was ligated to the upstream and downstream homologous arms of the target gene, as shown in Figure S10. The knockout and complementation vectors were separately transformed into A. tumefaciens AGL1. Using the transformant AP‐193 obtained in the previous step as the experimental strain, the double‐knockout and complementation transformants were generated via the Agrobacterium‐mediated genetic transformation of A. phaeospermum. Total proteins were extracted from the WT, AP‐193, double‐knockout and complementation strains. In a sterile centrifuge tube, 10 mM ATP, 200 μL of total protein from AP‐193, and 100 μL of total protein from the mutant strains were added. For the MG132 treatment group, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 was added to a final concentration of 50 μM, followed by incubation at a constant temperature of 28°C (Qiao et al. 2025). At different time points, 5× SDS‐PAGE loading buffer was added, and the reaction was terminated by denaturation at high temperature for 15 min. Western blot analysis was performed to assess protein degradation status.

To investigate whether BDPH1 and BDEUL12 influence the transcriptional activity of ApCtf1β, the CDS of ApCtf1β and BDPH1 were fused into pGreenII62SK vector via homologous recombination. Similarly, ApCtf1β and BDEUL12 were cloned into pGreenII62SK vector, and the CDS of ApCtf1β was also cloned separately into the same vector. The promoter of Ap‐12 was cloned into the pGreenII0800 vector. The primers used in this experiment are in Table S8 The four recombinant vectors were individually transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101. The bacteria were cultured in liquid medium, collected by centrifugation, and resuspended in infiltration buffer to an OD600 of 1.0, followed by incubation at room temperature for 2–3 h. Two bacterial suspensions containing different plasmids were mixed and infiltrated into the abaxial side of the N. benthamiana leaves. After 48 h, the luciferase substrate D‐luciferin was applied to the infiltration sites, and fluorescence signals were detected using a multispectral dynamic fluorescence microscopy system (PlantView100). At 72 h post‐infiltration, luciferase activity in the experimental groups was measured using a luciferase assay kit.
