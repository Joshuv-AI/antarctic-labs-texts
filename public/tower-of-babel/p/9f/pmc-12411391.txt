Expression patterns of plasma microRNAs in patients with cervical cancer from two teaching hospitals in Ghana
Authors: Helena Quayson, Joseph Humphrey Kofi Bonney, Daniel Sam, Gloria Francisca Nuer-Allornuvor, Roland Osei Saahene, Nicholas Ekow Thomford, Benjamin Amoani, Francis Abrokwah, Karen Pomeyie, Musah Kalaamullah Salisu, Prince Amoah Barnie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimEarly cervical cancer diagnosis is a global challenge that needs to be addressed by the discovery of less invasive diagnostic and prognostic approaches. Circulating miRNAs are stable in plasma and their diagnostic potentials have been elucidated in some cancers. Therefore, in this cross-sectional study, we determined the patterns of expression of 7 selected circulating microRNAs that differ between patients with cervical cancer receiving therapy, patients with cervical not on therapy and healthy females. The goal was to investigate the diagnostic and prognostic potential of these selected miRNAs.MethodsTotal RNA was extracted from plasma samples collected from 53 participants recruited from Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital and the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital, Ghana. Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed, followed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) to amplify and quantify the expression levels of the target microRNAs. Expression levels of seven microRNAs—hsa-miR-146a, hsa-miR-29a, hsa-miR-29b, hsa-miR-34a, hsa-miR-233, hsa-miR-155, and hsa-miR-27a were compared among three groups: healthy controls (n = 27), patients with cervical cancer on therapy (n = 13), and those not on therapy (n = 13).ResultsmiR-155 and miR-27a showed statistically significant differential expression between cancer patients and healthy controls. In addition, miR-29b expression levels differed significantly between stage 4b and stage 4a of patient with cervical cancer undergoing treatment.ConclusionThese findings suggest that circulating plasma miRNAs may serve as non-invasive biomarkers for the early detection of cervical cancer, monitoring disease progression, and evaluating treatment response.

Introduction

The World Health Organization estimated that 604,000 women globally were diagnosed with cervical cancer in 2020 which resulted in an anticipated 342,000 deaths (World Health Organization 2021). The human papillomavirus (HPV) was considered as a risk factor for this fatal sickness. The long-term, high-risk HPV types 16 and 18 infections are the primary risk factors for cervical carcinogenesis. However, only a small percentage of women with morphologically expressed HPV infection have invasive disease, indicating that other factors were likely linked to the occurrence of cervical cancer. The mortality rate for cervical cancer is higher in poorer countries because the disease is not detected in its early stages (Sankaranarayanan and Budukh 2001). Traditional screening methods like Papanicolaou smears and molecular detection of HPV are crucial for early diagnosis, but they are usually associated with a variety of disadvantages, including discomfort when sampling, invasiveness, and logistical challenges. Again, the traditional screening methods may yield false results, leading to unnecessary anxiety, additional testing, or missed diagnoses. This unreliability can erode trust in the screening process (Sharma and Gupta 2020). These factors have led to reduced screening uptake and delayed diagnosis, contributing to higher mortality rates. There is therefore a critical need for non-invasive, trustworthy biomarkers that can improve early identification, monitor treatment responses, and risk-based patient stratification because existing cervical cancer screening techniques, such as Pap smears and HPV testing, might overlook precancerous lesions or early-stage malignancies (Sharma and Gupta 2020).

Recent research has identified microRNAs (miRNAs) which are short, non-coding RNA molecules that regulate gene expression and are involved in cancer progression. MiRNAs serve as potential non-invasive biomarkers, offering a promising alternative for the early detection of cervical cancer and monitoring of treatment in diagnosed patients. Most microRNAs are known to regulate their target mRNA through diverse physiological and pathological mechanisms and have been discovered to be inappropriately expressed in a variety of human cancers (Sohel 2016). It is well known that microRNA binds to the 3’-UTR of target mRNA to impede translation of the target mRNA (O’Brien et al. 2018). This could also result in target mRNA degradation. MicroRNAs have the potential to control about one-third of human genes, which can have up to 1000 targets (O’Brien et al. 2018). About 50% of microRNAs located in the genomic region are closely linked with cancer (Miao et al. 2020; Nagamitsu et al. 2016). These provide compelling evidence to investigate the various microRNA expression profiles for particular cancer types (Miao et al. 2020). Therefore, since cervical cancer is of global concern, it is necessary to identify the expression patterns of miRNA in early cervical cancer cases, since the expression patterns of miRNA may possess the unique ability to identify early cases of cervical cancer and also give substantial indications of cancer progression. Many researchers have identified and reported inconsistent microRNA patterns from the plasma of patients with cervical cancer across the globe (Zeng et al. 2015; Zavesky et al. 2015; Fleischmann et al. 2021) but there’s a paucity of information from Ghana. The chances of genetic variation due to population differences and the potential of the formation of recombinant HPV forms could potentially be associated with a distinct miRNA expression profile in the Ghanaian population.

The present investigation examines the expression profiles of these miRNA molecules in patients with cervical cancer and assesses the diagnostic and prognostic utility of these microRNAs. The findings of this research, being the first in Ghana, provide data that could serve as a push to further advocate for the use of microRNAs from plasma in early diagnosis and prognosis in patients with cervical cancer.

Methods

Study area and population

Cape Coast Teaching Hospital (CCTH), Cape Coast, and Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital (KATH), Kumasi served as locations from which HPV positive participants who were also cervical cancer positive were conveniently sampled. Convenient sampling is a non-probability sampling method where units are selected for inclusion in the sample because they are easier for the researcher to access. Samples were selected based on: the availability of patients with cervical cancer at both Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital and Cape Coast Teaching Hospital, the willingness of patients with cervical cancer at both Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital and Cape Coast Teaching Hospital to participate in the study, and if the health state of the cervical cancer patients at both hospitals were conducive enough for blood sample draw. A total sample size of 26 cervical cancer samples were obtained from the two teaching hospitals; this included 13 cervical cancer positive patients who were undergoing chemotherapy and radiation therapy and 13 patients with cervical cancer who have never been on chemotherapy and radiation therapy. These were patients who were 27 patients without cancer were recruited as normal controls. All these participants were within the age group of 24–60 years.

Blood sample collection

Venous blood samples of patients with cervical cancer and normal healthy control non-cancerous patients were obtained, using a syringe of approximately 5 mL whole blood of each participant was deposited into EDTA tubes. Blood samples were taken by a gynecology specialist.

Cervical cancer blood samples

Cervical cancer blood samples were obtained from patients with HPV infection which is a known risk factor of cervical cancer. These are persons who do not smoke. Using their medical history and personal interviews, it was ensured that persons had no co-infections or non-communicable diseases. Cervical cancer smples were categorised based on their FIGO stages.

Normal healthy control (non-cancerous individuals)

Blood samples from the normal healthy control were obtained from the blood bank of the Cape Cost Teaching Hospital.

Laboratory methods

Plasma collection and storage

Blood samples were brought to the Department of Biomedical Sciences Laboratory, UCC, and centrifuged at 1500 × g at 10 °C for 15 min to obtain plasma. Plasma was collected with a Pasteur pipette into a 1.5 ml disposable cryovial tube (Jiangsu Huida Medical Instruments Co., Ltd.), labeled, well-corked, and stored at − 80 °C, until assayed for immunological analysis at the Noguchi Memorial Institute for Medical Research.

Protocol for the extraction of RNA

A total of 200 µl of pre-treated sample was collected into a 1.5 ml centrifuge tube. Lysis buffer (KSB) of 255 µl containing carrier molecule and 20 µl of proteinase K were added and vortex thoroughly for 10 s to mix. Incubation was done at 56 °C for 10 min. 350 µl of Absolute ethanol was added and vortexed thoroughly for another 10 s to mix. The mixture was spun, dispensed, and centrifuged for one minute at 10,000 rpm. The spin column was then transferred to a new collecting tube, and the tube containing the filtrate was discarded. The spin column was then transferred to a fresh collecting tube, and the tube containing the passing liquid was discarded. Next, 500 µl of wash buffer 1 (KSW1) was dispensed into the spin column, which was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for one minute. The spin column was filled with 500 µl of wash buffer 2 (KSW2), which was then centrifuged for three minutes at a full speed of 13,000 rpm. The passing liquid in the dispensing tube was discarded after centrifugation, and the spin column was transferred to a clean 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tube. The spin column was filled with 70 µl of KSE buffer and left to sit at room temperature for 1 min. The extracted RNA was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 1 min before being used right away.

cDNA synthesis and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

cDNA synthesis was done in a conventional PCR thermocycler before Real Time-qPCR. Each cDNA synthesis reaction vessel was made up of 10 µl of 2X miRNA L-RT solution mix, 1.5 µl of miRNA L-RT enzyme mix, 3.5 µl of RNase free water, 1 µl of stem-loop reverse transcription primer, and a total RNA template of 4 µl, making a total reaction of 20 µl. cDNA amplicons were used as templates for RT-qPCR. Constituents of the Sangon RT-qPCR kit were used to prepare the master mix for the RT-qPCR run which was done with Applied Biosystems Prism 7500 Fast machine. A Ct value of 40 was set and used to determine amplification. All the experiments were repeated two times. The reaction mixtures were incubated initially at 95 °C for 30 s, after which there was denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing at 60 °C, for 30 s for 40 cycles. The specificities of each of the seven microRNAs used in the RT-qPCR experiment were examined using melt curve analysis. Only miRNAs whose melting curves had just one peak were considered for further analysis.

MicroRNA primers

These, (hsa-miR-16-1-5p, hsa-miR-146a, hsa-miR-29a, hsa-miR-29b, hsa-miR-34a, hsa-miR-233, hsa-miR-155, and hsa-miR-27a) are the list of the eight (Zeng et al. 2015) microRNAs used for this study. In assessing the expression levels of miRNA in patients with cervical cancer on therapy and patients with cervical cancer not on therapy relative to non-cancerous patients, all microRNAs were normalized to miR-16-1-5p, which was found in the literature to be one of the most used endogenous control microRNAs for RT-qPCR (Schrauder et al. 2012). Thus, the study assessed the expression patterns of only seven microRNAs in the plasma of all patients with cervical cancer relative to non-cancerous patients as a sign of their diagnostic and prognostic potential.

MiRNA discovery phase

The presence and expression patterns of nine (Zavesky et al. 2015) miRNAs associated with cervical cancer in the works of Santos et al. (2023), Ogechukwu (2015) and Jeong et al. (2024) were assessed using hsa-mir-16-1-5p as endogenous control. This investigation involved RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and RT-qPCR as explained above. The miRNA discovery phase involved nine (Zavesky et al. 2015) samples made up of three (Sharma and Gupta 2020) healthy control samples, three (Sharma and Gupta 2020) cervical cancer positive samples from patients on therapy and three (Sharma and Gupta 2020) cervical cancer positive samples from patients who were not on therapy. Out of the nine miRNAs assessed, seven (O’Brien et al. 2018) miRNAs (hsa-mir-155, hsa-mir-29a, hsa-mir-29b, hsa-mir-34a, hsa-mir-223, hsa-mir-27a and hsa-mir-146a) were differentially expressed in cervical cancer positive patients relative to healthy control. Therefore, these miRNAs were selected for the validation phase which was carried out with 53 samples as described in Fig. 1 below.

Data analysis

MS Excel (version 2016) and GraphPad Prism 9.3.1(471) (GraphPad Software Inc., CA) were used for statistical analysis. The sociodemographic features and additional relevant aspects were described using descriptive statistics. Data was not normally distributed and thus non-parametric. Kruskal–Wallis test was used to analyze the median miRNA expression levels among normal healthy control (non-cancerous patients), cervical cancer-positive patients not on therapy, and cervical cancer-positive patients on therapy. Median miRNA expression was computed together with its interquartile range. When the p-value was less than 0.05 (p-value < 0.05), statistical significance was taken into account.

Diagnostic potential of the expressed miRNAs

The diagnostic potential of the expressed circulating miRNAs was assessed by the use of the area under the curve (AUC) generated from the receiver operating characteristic curve. This assessment was done according to the ratings reported by Çorbacıoğlu and Aksel (2023). According to this rating, in assessing the diagnostic potential of a test or biomarker using the area under the curve, an AUC ≥ 0.9 implies that the test is excellent. A test is regarded to possess considerable discriminatory potential if 0.8 ≤ AUC < 0.9. Additionally, a biomarker or test is referred to as a fair discriminator of positive patients from healthy control if 0.7 ≤ AUC < 0.8.

Diagnostic potential of miRNAs among cervical cancer positive patients on therapy

Using the ratings above, the diagnostic potential of the miRNAs was computed among non-cancerous patients and patients with cervical cancer on therapy. Hsa-mir-27a expression patterns (Fig. 3A) had the highest diagnostic potential (AUC = 0.88 ≈ 0.9), showing that the levels of this miRNA excellently distinguished cervical cancer positive patients from non-cancerous individuals. This miRNA also had the highest sensitivity (88.89%) and specificity (76.19%). Table 2 provides a detailed breakdown of the two miRNAs’ diagnostic potential.

Expression patterns of miRNA among the cervical cancer stages

The differences in the levels of all seven miRNAs among the cervical cancer stages were assessed as a sign of their prognostic potential. Out of the seven miRNAs only mir-29b showed differential expression patterns among some FIGO stages. It was unveiled that the levels of hsa-mir-29b in stage 4b cervical cancer positive patients on therapy were significantly (p < 0.001) elevated relative to stage 4a patients with cervical cancer who were also on therapy by a fold change of 12.15. Hsa-mir-29b was significantly (p < 0.01) increased in stage 4b patients with cervical cancer who were not on therapy relative to stage 4a patients with cervical cancer who were on therapy by a fold change of 5.49. Additionally, there was a significant elevation of hsa-mir-29b in stage 3b cervical cancer positive patients who were not on therapy by a foldchange of 0.89 relative to healthy normal control (non-cancerous individuals) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Cervical cancer is associated with significant rates of mortality and morbidity, which disproportionately impact women that between the ages of 30 and 50. The disease is still considered a public health concern (Shah et al. 2016). Although the most common preventive approach for identifying cervical cancer is the Pap smear test, it has certain drawbacks (Jia et al. 2015). Therefore, novel ways are needed for early detection, prognosis and therapy to prevent this disease. MicroRNAs have been acknowledged as a new class of minimally invasive biomarkers because of their consistency and potential clinical utility in the early identification of the cervical intraepithelial neoplasia stage. The level of miRNA can be altered in either direction when there are genetic and epigenetic changes in malignancies (Chen et al. 2014; Dudea-Simon et al. 2022). Therefore, this study was intended to find out the expression patterns of some selected circulating miRNA in the plasma of cervical cancer patients at some selected teaching hospitals in Ghana.

In this study, the ages of participants ranged from 24 to 60 years and the majority of the participants, (57%) were within the age range of 46–60 years, followed by 30% within the age range of 35–45 years and 18% within the age range of 24–34 years. These were sexually active participants thus highlighting their increased risk of exposure to other HPV variants and a potential dissemination of this infection to uninfected sexual partners. With regards to the marital status, divorced patients had the greatest percentage of 42%, followed by single patients (32%) and married patients (26%). This provides an estimate of individuals who undergo prognosis, and effective treatment. This inference is made because a study by Hewitt et al. (2000) found that married women are more likely to receive diagnosis of their illness and treatment because, married women receive more cervical cancer screenings than single women (Hewitt et al. 2000). Additionally, married women profit from the social support that was advantageously linked to cancer screening, particularly the Pap smear (Suarez et al. 1994). This shows the need to intensify awareness and importance for cervical cancer screenings and create more support platforms for single individuals to also be screened. It is also necessary to identify more less invasive diagnostic and prognostic approaches. This further shows the importance of considering miRNA expression profiles (a less invasive approach) for diagnosis and prognosis.

The expression patterns of seven different microRNAs such as hsa-mir-146a, hsa-mir-155, hsa-mir-29a, and hsa-mir-29b, hsa-mir-34a, hsa-mir-233, and hsa-mir-27a among non-cancerous patients, cervical cancer-positive patients on therapy, and cervical cancer-positive patients not on therapy were investigated. However, only two microRNAs (hsa-mir-155 and hsa-mir-27a) seemed to possess the potential to distinguish patients with cervical cancer patients from non-cancerous individuals. In this study, when comparing the expression patterns of hsa-mir-155 in cervical cancer-positive patients who were not on therapy to the normal healthy control (non-cancerous individuals), there was a substantial difference in the expression pattern of hsa-mir-155 between the groups. Studies by Papaconstantinou et al. (2013) and Wang et al. (2009), have proposed that hsa-mir-155 was overexpressed in tumor tissues and plasma and that tissue expressions were associated with tumor stage and a worse prognosis among patients with cervical cancer undergoing treatment as compared to controls.

In this present study, it was also identified that the expression patterns of hsa-mir-155 in all patients with cervical cancer, was found to be upregulated than the normal healthy control (non-cancerous individuals). This is similar to a study by Fang et al. (2016), who found that the expression of the hsa-mir-155 was substantially higher in cervical cancer tissues than it was in the non-cancerous tissues. In cervical cancer tissues, Wang et al. (2008) also discovered that the expression pattern of hsa-mir-155 was up-regulated. A study by Lei et al. (2012) demonstrated that ectopically overexpressed hsa-mir-155, which has been associated with cervical cancer, acts as a tumor suppressor. It prevents the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and makes cervical cells chemo-sensitive while impeding cell expansion, migration, and invasion. The overexpression of hsa-mir-155 mimics the potential to partially correct the down-regulation of E-cadherin brought on by EGF (epidermal growth factor) therapy in cervical cells. It specifically binds to two binding locations in the Smad2 gene’s 3’ UTR. Overexpression of hsa-mir-155 potentially causes the suppressor of mothers against decapentaplegic (SMAD2) to be downregulated, thus, hsa-mir-155 prevents cervical cancer’s EGF-induced EMT. When EMT is inhibited, hsa-mir-155 increases the cervical cells’ chemo-sensitivity to cisplatin treatment. This may explain why in this present study, hsa-mir-155 was elevated in persons undergoing therapy, thus indicting that, this miRNA may be useful for monitoring treatment outcomes. Lei et al. (2012) also reported that the expression patterns of hsa-mir-155 in patients with cervical cancer in comparison to non-cancerous patients, were generally observed to be elevated in these patients. Ferrajoli et al. (2013) found that monoclonal B-cell lymphocytosis patients’ B cells have greater levels of hsa-mir-155 than B cells from control subjects.

This study also demonstrated that, in contrast to the non-cancerous patients, the expression pattern of hsa-mir-27a was elevated in patients with cervical cancer patients on therapy and downregulated in patients with cervical cancer not on therapy. This is similar to recent studies showing that hsa-mir-27a levels are consistently elevated in a wide range of cancer types, supporting the malignant properties of cancer cells (Su et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2015). Moreover, the expression patterns of hsa-mir-27a promoted cell invasion, migration, and mortality in ovarian cancer by being overexpressed (Li et al. 2019). According to Ben et al. (2020), cervical cancer was found to have an overexpression of hsa-mir-27a, and its elimination significantly decreased the malignant features of cervical cancer cells.

The ROC was done to identify the diagnostic potential of the microRNAs. There were only two miRNAs that were highly expressed with good diagnostic potential among patients with cervical cancer. The hsa-mir-155 showed good potential to distinguish cervical cancer patients not on therapy form non-cancerous individuals (healthy normal control). The levels of hsa-mir-27a also showed excellent diagnostic potential of patients with cervical cancer on therapy. This was indicated by its high AUC, sensitivity (88.89%) and specificity (76.19%) Therefore, based on the ROC ratings by Nahm (2022) these miRNAs may be potential biomarkers for consideration in cervical cancer diagnosis.

Furthermore, it has also been unveiled that the expression patterns of hsa-mir-29b may be a potential biomarker for cervical cancer prognosis. This is inferred because the levels of this miRNA was differentially expressed in stage 4b patients with cervical cancer relative to stage 4a patients who were all on therapy. Despite the fact that the sample size from which this analysis was made is low. It is noteworthy to acknowledge that hsa-mir-29b has been identified as a tumor suppressor that plays a crucial role in regulating cancer (Yan et al. 2015) and its prognostic potential has been previously elucidted. It has also been reported that in various melanoma cell lines, hsa-mir-29b inversely correlated with cancer cell proliferation (Kwon et al. 2019). Evidence of the prognostic potential of the expression patterns of hsa-mir-29b is also reported by Zhang et al. (2019). This previous research reported that hsa-mir-29b increases radiosensitivity and is downregulated in radioresistant cervical cancer cells. This could imply that the expression patterns of hsa-mir-29b is reduced during the progression of cervical cancer dysplasia which potentially occurs in radioresistant cells. Despite the fact that the pattern of expression of hsa-mir-29b in this present study is dissimilar to previous studies, there is evidence of differential expression of this miRNA among Figo stage 4a and 4b. Additionally, despite the fact that the sample size for this current analysis is relatively low, the prognostic potential of hsa-mir-29a discovered is in synchrony with these previous studies.

In addition to the above, the levels of hsa-mir-29b were differentially expressed in non-cancerous individuals relative to stage 3b patients with cervical cancer who were not on therapy. A similar result involving other host miRNA was reported in a study by Azimi et al. (2021) which assessed the possibility of employing hsa-mir-34a and hsa-mir-92a-5p levels found in Pap smears as diagnostic biomarkers for separating patients with LSIL and HSIL from healthy people, using the ROC. The expression of these two molecules could reliably separate healthy individuals from patients with LSIL, just as the differential expression of hsa-mir-29b was able to distinguish stage 3b patients with cervical cancer in this present study from non-cancerous individuals (healthy control). The work of Zhang et al. (2019) also identified differential expression patterns of hsa-mir-29b in patients with cervical cancer relative to healthy control. Additionally, this previous study unveiled that hsa-miR-29b was downregulated in radioresistant subpopulations of cervical cancer cells. This could imply that the levels of this miRNA could be monitored to predict development of resistance to radiosensitivity. It is noteworthy to acknowledge, that since the levels of this miRNA were able to identify stage 3b cervical cancer therapy-naïve patients, who are likely to experience cancer progression due to lack of treatment measures, it may be inferred that hsa-mir-29a may possess the potential to determine cervical cancer progression in patients who are not on therapy. However, this needs to be validated by conducting more research.

This research has unveiled that, the expression patterns of hsa-mir-155 and hsa-mir-27a possess the potential to distinguish patients with cervical cancer from healthy control. Therefore, the expression patterns of these miRNAs may be suitable for the diagnosis of cervical cancer. Furthermore, the promising potential of hsa-mir29b in cancer prognosis has also been elucidated. However, more research must be conducted with a larger sample size to ascertain if the miRNA profile test could be considered in the diagnosis and prognosis of cervical cancer. Additionally, the differential expression patterns of miRNAs identified in this study indicate that beyond the diagnostic and prognostic potential of these miRNAs, there may be some therapeutic properties that could be harnessed in the management or prevention of cervical cancer.

Conclusion

Currently, this study is the first in Ghana to assess the expression patterns of circulating microRNAs in freshly diagnosed patients with cervical cancer not on therapy and patients with cervical cancer on therapy. The expression patterns of hsa-mir-155 and hsa-mir-27a showed substantial differences when compared among cervical cancer-positive and the control groups. This shows the diagnostic utility of the miRNA. It is worth noting that, hsa-mir-155 may be a useful biomarker for assessing treatment outcomes. This is evidenced by its differential expression between patients with cervical cancer on therapy and patients with cervical cancer but are not on therapy. Additionally, the expression patterns of hsa-mir-29b may be considered for monitoring cervical cancer progression since this miRNA was able to distinguish stage 4a and 4b Figo stages as sign of its prognostic utility. There is the need to identify intracellular and extracellular host and viral factors that influence the expression patterns of the selected miRNA. This will provide a better understanding of the biological mechanism underlying miRNA expression patterns and how they can be used. These results provide the basis for the clinical value of the selected serum circulating miRNAs for diagnosis and prognosis. Further longitudinal studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate these findings and determine whether circulating miRNAs can complement or replace existing cervical cancer diagnostics in Ghana.

Limitations

This study was able to assess the diagnostic utility of the expression patterns of the above selected miRNAs in the selected patient samples; however, a larger sample size would have been more generalizable.
