Evaluation of the Prognostic Value of PCR Cycle Threshold and Mortality in Pulmonary Aspergillosis: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Hsien-Po Huang, Po-Hsiu Huang, Ting-Kuang Yeh, Yu-Hui Lin, Che-An Tsai, Chia-Hsin Cheng, Hsiu-Wen Wang, Chun-Mei Ho, Po-Yu Liu, Chia-Wei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary aspergillosis remains highly lethal despite modern therapy. Although polymerase chain reaction (PCR) has enhanced detection, the clinical significance of its cycle threshold (Ct) values is unclear. The key problem is the lack of validation of Ct values for assessing fungal burden or predicting outcomes, limiting their diagnostic and prognostic utility.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 154 adult patients diagnosed with pulmonary aspergillosis at a tertiary medical center in Taiwan (2021–2024). All patients had a positive Aspergillus PCR from a respiratory specimen. We used receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to determine an optimal Ct cutoff for predicting 30-day all-cause mortality and multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors.ResultsAmong 154 patients (mean age 70.5 ± 13.5 years; 48.1% female), the optimal Ct cutoff for predicting 30-day mortality was 30.7 (AUC = 0.65, 95% CI 0.56–0.73). Patients with a Ct value < 30.7 had significantly higher 30-day mortality than those with a Ct ≥ 30.7 (71.2% vs 43.1%, p = 0.002). After adjusting for clinical and laboratory confounders, a Ct value < 30.7 remained the independent predictor of mortality (adjusted OR 3.07; 95% CI 1.27–7.39; p = 0.012).ConclusionA low Aspergillus PCR Ct value is an independent predictor of 30-day mortality in patients with pulmonary aspergillosis. While its discriminative ability is moderate, the Ct value may serve as a valuable predictor to aid in early risk stratification, when interpreted within the overall clinical context.

Introduction

Pulmonary aspergillosis, an opportunistic infection caused predominantly by Aspergillus fumigatus, and to a lesser extent by A. flavus, A. niger, and A. terreus, is a growing global health threat.1 Its incidence is rising not only in classic immunocompromised hosts but also in critically ill patients without traditional risk factors, driven by expanding immunosuppressive therapies and advances in intensive care.2 Traditional risk factors include prolonged neutropenia, hematologic malignancies, and advanced chronic lung diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).3 In contrast, nontraditional risk factors—such as severe COVID-19 pneumonia, liver cirrhosis, diabetes mellitus, and prolonged ICU stays—are increasingly recognized in contemporary practice.4

The disease presents as a wide spectrum of syndromes, from allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) and chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA) to life-threatening invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA). Each carries substantial morbidity, and invasive disease is associated with alarmingly high mortality rates, which can exceed 50% in certain high-risk populations.5,6 This significant clinical burden underscores the urgent need for improved tools for early diagnosis and risk stratification.

However, diagnosing pulmonary aspergillosis is notoriously difficult. Its clinical and radiological signs are often non-specific, and conventional mycological culture of respiratory specimens suffers from low sensitivity, frequently delaying definitive diagnosis.7,8 While non-culture-based diagnostics such as galactomannan and (1→3)-β-D-glucan assays have improved detection, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) has emerged as a particularly valuable tool, offering high sensitivity and specificity for detecting Aspergillus DNA in both respiratory and blood samples.9,10

In this context, the PCR cycle threshold (Ct) value has garnered interest as a quantitative indicator of fungal burden. The Ct value—defined as the number of amplification cycles necessary for the detection of target DNA—exhibits an inverse relationship with the quantity of Aspergillus DNA present in the sample. A lower Ct value thus reflects a higher fungal burden, which could theoretically correlate with disease severity and predict patient outcomes.11 However, the clinical utility of the Ct value as a prognostic marker remains poorly defined. No consensus threshold has been established to define a “high” fungal load, and current diagnostic guidelines primarily use PCR as a qualitative test.9 Consequently, it is unclear whether the quantitative information provided by the Ct value can be reliably used in clinical practice to forecast prognosis.

Therefore, this study aimed to determine the association between Aspergillus PCR Ct values from respiratory specimens and 30-day mortality in patients with pulmonary aspergillosis. We hypothesized that a lower Ct value, indicative of a higher fungal burden, would be an independent predictor of adverse outcomes, thereby providing a valuable and readily available tool for early risk stratification.

Methods

Study Population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary medical center in Taiwan. We identified all adult patients (≥18 years) diagnosed with pulmonary aspergillosis between July 2021 and July 2024. Inclusion criteria were: (1) patients aged ≥18 years (2) a diagnosis of pulmonary aspergillosis established in accordance with the clinical, radiologic, and microbiological criteria of the 2020 European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer/Mycoses Study Group (EORTC/MSG) consensus definitions12 and (3) a positive Aspergillus PCR result from respiratory specimens, including sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL). Patients were excluded if they had incomplete medical records or if 30-day outcome data were unavailable.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Taichung Veterans General Hospital (IRB No. CF24552A). Given the retrospective design and the use of de-identified data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB.

Medical Record Review and Patient Stratification

Comprehensive data were retrieved from electronic medical records. This included demographic characteristics, comorbidities, laboratory values at the time of diagnosis, and microbiological findings. Treatment details and key clinical outcomes were also recorded. Bacterial co-infection was defined as the isolation of a pathogenic bacterium from a respiratory or blood culture within 72 hours of the positive Aspergillus PCR test.

For exploratory analyses assessing the relationship between fungal burden and clinical outcomes, patients were categorized into two groups according to their Aspergillus PCR cycle threshold (Ct) values of respiratory specimens. The Ct value, or cycle threshold value, refers to the PCR cycle number at which the fluorescence signal produced during amplification first exceeds the predefined threshold and becomes clearly distinguishable from background fluorescence. At this point, a sufficient amount of amplicon product has accumulated during the early exponential phase of the reaction to be reliably detected.

PCR Testing and Cycle Threshold (Ct) Value

Real-time PCR for Aspergillus species was performed on respiratory specimens. DNA was extracted using the EZ2 Connect platform (QIAGEN, Germany) and amplified with the AsperGenius® 2.0 Multiplex real-time PCR kit (PathoNostics, Netherlands). The cycling conditions consisted of an initial Taq polymerase activation at 95 °C for 30 seconds, followed by 45 amplification cycles. Each cycle included denaturation at 95 °C for 10 seconds and annealing/extension at 62 °C for 30 seconds, during which fluorescence acquisition was performed. For respiratory specimens, 200µL of BAL fluid or sputum was used for nucleic acid extraction with an elution volume of 50 µL, as recommended by the manufacturer. Each PCR reaction was carried out in a final reaction volume of 25 µL, consisting of 20 µL of PCR master mix and 5 µL of extracted DNA template (including internal control) added per reaction. Ct values were recorded for the positive samples.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using the independent-sample t-test. Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to assess the ability of the Ct value to predict 30-day mortality and to determine an optimal cutoff. The optimal threshold was identified using Youden’s index, which maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity.

To identify factors associated with 30-day mortality, we first performed univariate analyses. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in this initial analysis were then included as candidates in a multivariable logistic regression model. The final model was built to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between the Ct value cutoff and mortality.

A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 10.6.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 154 patients diagnosed with pulmonary aspergillosis were included in the analysis (see Figure 1). The cohort had a mean age of 70.5 ± 13.5 years, and 74 (48.1%) were female. The most prevalent comorbidities included malignancy (n=70, 45.5%), diabetes mellitus (n=52, 33.8%), and chronic lung disease (n=43, 27.9%). Ct Value Cutoff Determination

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal PCR cycle threshold (Ct) values for predicting 30-day mortality. A cutoff point of 30.7 yielded the best balance of sensitivity (46.9%) and specificity (79.5%), with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.65 (95% CI 0.56–0.73; Figure 2). Based on this threshold, the cohort was stratified into a low Ct group (Ct < 30.7; n=52) and a high Ct group (Ct ≥ 30.7; n=102) for subsequent analyses. Multivariable Analysis of Factors Associated with 30-Day Mortality

Factors significantly associated with mortality in the univariate analysis were included in a multivariable logistic regression model (Table 3). After adjusting for potential confounders, a Ct value < 30.7 remained the most significant independent predictor of 30-day mortality (adjusted OR 3.07; 95% CI 1.27–7.39; p = 0.012). Lower percentage of lymphocyte (adjusted OR 0.96; 95% CI 0.93–0.99; p = 0.025) and bacterial co-infection (adjusted OR 2.39; 95% CI 1.03–5.54; p = 0.041) were associated with higher mortality. Other variables, including eosinophil, hemoglobin, platelet count, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, and total bilirubin, lost their statistical significance in the adjusted model. Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we demonstrated that a low Aspergillus PCR cycle threshold (Ct) value is an independent predictor of 30-day mortality in patients with pulmonary aspergillosis. Specifically, a Ct value below 30.7 was associated with a more than threefold increase in the odds of mortality (aOR 3.07), an association that persisted after adjusting for multiple confounding factors. This finding suggests that a high fungal burden, as reflected by a low Ct value, is a distinct driver of poor outcomes. Notably, while most other markers of critical illness—such as anemia, thrombocytopenia, and elevated bilirubin—were associated with mortality in univariate analysis, they lost their independent predictive power once fungal load was accounted for. This highlights the central role of pathogen burden in determining the prognosis of pulmonary aspergillosis.

Our findings align with an emerging body of evidence supporting the use of quantitative PCR as a prognostic tool in invasive fungal infections. Originally used as a diagnostic adjunct, PCR is increasingly recognized for its ability to inform risk stratification and therapeutic management. For instance, Imbert et al found that high Aspergillus DNA copy numbers in serum were independently associated with 90-day mortality in both neutropenic and non-neutropenic patients (threshold ≥ 150 copies/mL; mortality 84% vs 27%)13 and Lamoth et al have underscored the utility of quantitative PCR in distinguishing colonization from invasive infection.14 A recent multicenter study further reported that PCR-based quantification and resistance detection significantly influenced antifungal decision-making and clinical outcomes in invasive aspergillosis.9

However, the literature is not entirely consistent. Some studies have reported a non-significant trend between fungal DNA levels and short-term survival,15 while others found no correlation with clinical outcomes.16 These discrepancies likely stem from variations in study design, including different patient populations, sample types, and a lack of assay standardization across laboratories. Our study contributes to this field by identifying a specific Ct cutoff in respiratory specimens that independently predicts mortality in a mixed patient cohort, reinforcing the concept that quantitative PCR can provide crucial prognostic information when applied in a well-defined clinical context.

There are plausible pathophysiologic reasons why a low PCR Ct would portend higher mortality in aspergillosis. A lower Ct reflects a higher concentration of Aspergillus DNA in the sampled compartment and, by inference, a greater intrapulmonary fungal burden. Quantitative studies utilizing respiratory specimens have demonstrated that elevated Aspergillus DNA loads assist in distinguishing genuine aspergillosis from contamination or clinically insignificant colonization and are correlated with poorer survival outcomes. This supports the notion that fungal burden correlates with disease severity.11 Previous significant research utilizing serum similarly revealed that elevated Aspergillus DNA loads independently forecast 90-day mortality, thereby strengthening the prognostic significance of pathogen burden beyond traditional markers.13 Current consensus frameworks also recognize the clinical challenge of differentiating colonization from invasive disease and have integrated PCR into mycological criteria, reflecting its increasing significance in risk assessment.17 Mechanistically, a higher fungal burden indicates more extensive tissue invasion and impaired gas exchange, potentially signaling host immune failure in containing the infection; these principles are consistent with contemporary reviews of pulmonary aspergillosis pathobiology and diagnostics.14 Collectively, these data furnish a biological and clinical justification for our conclusion that a low Ct threshold (for example, <30.7) identifies patients at an elevated risk of short-term mortality.

The interpretation of Aspergillus PCR quantification is inherently specimen-dependent.18,19 BAL fluid is a relatively standardized lower respiratory tract sample and has been most consistently validated for Aspergillus PCR in invasive aspergillosis,20 including studies indicating that higher fungal burden measured in BAL is associated with worse outcomes.11 In routine clinical practice, however, BAL may not be feasible in all patients, and clinicians frequently rely on sputum or other non-BAL respiratory samples. Sputum-based testing may be limited by variable specimen quality and the potential for airway colonization or contamination, which can complicate the clinical significance of a positive Aspergillus result and reduce the comparability of Ct values across specimen types.21 Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that in our cohort, the majority of respiratory specimens were sputum samples (124 of 154, 80.5%), whereas only 28 specimens were BAL. Despite this predominance of sputum-based testing, the Ct value remained significantly associated with 30-day mortality and retained independent prognostic significance in multivariable analysis. This finding suggests that, although sputum may be a less standardized specimen than BAL, Aspergillus PCR quantification in sputum may still provide clinically meaningful prognostic information when interpreted in the appropriate clinical context. However, it should be applied cautiously across different respiratory matrices, and further prospective studies are needed to validate specimen-specific thresholds.

The Ct value is a readily available metric that can serve as an early, objective tool for risk stratification. A Ct value below our proposed threshold of 30.7 should alert clinicians to a high-risk patient subgroup, prompting more aggressive management. This may include earlier initiation or escalation of antifungal therapy, consideration of combination regimens,22 and closer monitoring or admission to an ICU. Given that timely and appropriate antifungal therapy is strongly correlated with improved outcomes,23 using the Ct value to identify high-risk patients could be a critical step toward improving survival. Furthermore, this quantitative approach may enhance antifungal stewardship by helping to reserve intensive therapies for patients with the highest fungal burden while potentially avoiding unnecessary treatment escalation in those with higher Ct values.9,14,24

Integrating quantitative PCR results into clinical algorithms could thus complement established diagnostic frameworks, such as the 2020 EORTC/MSGERC criteria, which already incorporate PCR as a component of mycological evidence.12 Previous studies have demonstrated that PCR-based quantification of fungal DNA can provide valuable diagnostic information and may serve as a potential tool for monitoring disease progression or treatment response.25,26 Such quantitative monitoring may offer advantage over binary antigen tests such as galactomannan or β-D-glucan, whose values frequently vary independently of disease burden.27

Mortality in intensive care infections is not always determined by microbial resistance patterns alone. Indeed, in the Respiratory ICU study by Uluç et al, which included 261 patients, MDR/XDR Gram-negative resistance profiles were not associated with mortality; rather, APACHE-II and SOFA scores were identified as the predominant determinants.28 This observation aligns with our finding that a Ct value <30.7 serves as an independent prognostic marker, suggesting that pathogen burden and the host response may play a more critical role in mortality than resistance patterns alone. Overall, integrating fungal load quantification into risk assessment models constitutes a pragmatic and evidence-based measure towards personalized management of aspergillosis. Analogous methodologies, employing pathogen burden to inform therapeutic decisions, have already demonstrated their utility in viral and bacterial infections.29,30

Lower lymphocyte has been described as an independent predictor of mortality in previous study.5 An appropriate balance between neutrophils and lymphocytes is crucial for effective pathogen elimination while preventing excessive tissue damage.31 Bacterial co-infection is frequently observed in patients with pulmonary aspergillosis, particularly in ICU settings, and may contribute to increased mortality through additive inflammatory burden, sepsis, and organ dysfunction.32 Similarly, the timing of antifungal therapy initiation may influence outcomes in invasive aspergillosis. Prior studies have suggested that delayed diagnosis and delayed initiation of effective antifungal treatment are associated with poorer prognosis.33 Early recognition and prompt treatment remain clinically important considerations supported by the existing literature. These findings highlight the need for future prospective studies with more granular temporal data to better delineate the impact of co-infections and treatment timing on mortality.

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center, retrospective design may limit the generalizability of our findings. Inter-laboratory variability, differences in nucleic acid extraction efficiency, specimen quality, and other pre-analytical factors may also influence cycle thresholds. Species-level identification using other technologies with good results should not be ruled out for a better approach to the patient with pulmonary aspergillosis. Moreover, although the Ct value demonstrates prognostic utility, its discriminative ability is relatively modest (AUC = 0.65). The modest sensitivity of ROC curve likely reflects the heterogeneity of respiratory specimen types, the semi-quantitative nature of Ct values, and the multifactorial determinants of mortality in pulmonary aspergillosis. Although Aspergillus PCR enables molecular identification, species-level identification alone does not directly dictate antifungal treatment decisions, given the generally broad susceptibility profiles of Aspergillus species, including cryptic species. Therefore, molecular results should be interpreted cautiously and in conjunction with comprehensive clinical assessment and, when available, complementary microbiological and antifungal susceptibility data. Second, as an observational study, we can only establish association, not causation. Unmeasured confounders, such as the timing of antifungal therapy or underlying host immune deficits, could have influenced both fungal burden and mortality. Third, we did not have data on Aspergillus species or antifungal susceptibility profiles. It is possible that low Ct values corresponded with more virulent or resistant strains, which our analysis could not assess. Fourth, owing to reimbursement regulations under the national health insurance system and the retrospective design of the study, most patients had only a single Aspergillus PCR Ct measurement without technical replicates. This may have contributed to variability in Ct values and limited assessment of measurement reproducibility. Finally, our primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality, which does not distinguish deaths directly attributable to aspergillosis from those due to other comorbidities.

Our findings lay the groundwork for several avenues of future research. A prospective, multicenter study is crucial to validate the prognostic utility of a Ct value threshold across different settings and to establish standardized reporting. Future clinical trials could test whether a Ct-guided treatment strategy improves outcomes compared to standard care. Additionally, research is needed to explore the dynamics of fungal load during treatment by monitoring serial Ct values, which may provide a valuable biomarker for therapeutic response. Finally, integrating quantitative PCR with other novel biomarkers, such as those measuring host immune responses, could lead to more comprehensive and accurate prognostic models for pulmonary aspergillosis.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that a low Aspergillus PCR Ct value derived from respiratory specimens is independently associated with increased 30-day mortality in patients with pulmonary aspergillosis. Our findings suggest that quantitative PCR results may provide clinically meaningful prognostic information beyond their diagnostic role. When interpreted in conjunction with the overall clinical context, a low Ct value may help identify high-risk patients who could benefit from closer monitoring and timely therapeutic interventions. These results support the potential integration of fungal burden assessment into risk stratification strategies for pulmonary aspergillosis. Further prospective, multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings, establish standardized Ct thresholds across specimen types, and clarify the role of Ct-guided management in improving clinical outcomes.
