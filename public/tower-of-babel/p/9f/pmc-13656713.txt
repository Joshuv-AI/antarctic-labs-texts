Sequential associations of myelin dysfunction with Aβ and Tau pathology in Alzheimer's disease: a dynamic myelin perturbation
Authors: Jingxiang Wen, Guoyu Lan, Yuan Fu, Weiru Jiang, Bin Xu, Jing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyelin dysfunction has emerged as a potentially pivotal factor in Alzheimer’s disease (AD); however, its temporal dynamics and relationship with hallmark pathological processes remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to investigate dynamic demyelination and its association with the progression of Aβ and Tau pathology in AD.MethodsA total of 1,237 participants from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) with 18F-florbetapir (FBP) PET imaging were included. White matter SUVR (WM SUVR), defined as the adjusted FBP signal in white matter, was used as a proxy measure of myelin integrity. Cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses were conducted to examine associations between WM SUVR and AD progression, as well as AD-related biomarkers. Using cortical Aβ accumulation as a temporal reference, we further evaluated two-year changes in cortical Aβ and cumulative Tau PET signals in individuals with and without reduced myelin integrity (WM − vs. WM + uptake).ResultsWM SUVR demonstrated a gradual decline with advancing AD severity. Lower WM SUVR was associated with changes in Aβ42/40 and pTau181 levels in both cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma. In early-stage AD, the WM − group exhibited a higher two-year rate of cortical Aβ accumulation compared with the WM + uptake group. At comparable levels of cortical amyloid, the WM − group showed significantly higher Tau PET signals in temporal meta-regions of interest and more extensive cortical spread in later stages.ConclusionsProgressive myelin dysfunction is observed across the course of AD as a notable perturbation within neurodegenerative processes. Early alterations in myelin integrity are associated with subsequent Aβ accumulation, whereas later changes coincide with increased Tau aggregation. These findings support a conceptual framework in which myelin degeneration shows temporally structured associations with AD pathology and may serve as a sensitive marker of vulnerability, particularly in early disease stages.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13195-026-02139-9.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), one of the leading causes of dementia in aging populations worldwide, is pathologically characterized by progressive accumulation of neuritic beta-amyloid (Aβ) plaques and hyperphosphorylated Tau neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) in the brain [1]. Despite extensive efforts, most therapeutic interventions targeting Aβ plaques removal have yielded limited to disappointing clinical efficacy [2]. Recent neuroimaging studies have increasingly highlighted the frequent occurrence of macrostructural and microstructural white matter (WM) abnormalities in AD, with the loss of myelin integrity emerging as one of the most prominent pathological features [3, 4]. Multiple neuropathological studies have also shown reduced myelin integrity in the AD brain [5–7], suggesting that myelin degeneration may represent a critical component of AD pathophysiology.

Myelin, a multilayered insulating membrane, ensheathes axons to enable rapid and efficient neural conduction [8]. In healthy adults, myelin demonstrates complex plasticity, undergoing continuous renewal and remodeling through ongoing oligodendrocyte generation and turnover [9]. In contrast, the myelin system in AD is profoundly impaired, showing extensive pathological myelin loss [10]. Cerebral accumulations of Aβ and Tau have been found to correlate with decreased myelin levels, particularly in the tracts rich in late-developing lower myelinated fibers [11–13]. Previous studies have suggested that myelin defects might enhance Aβ production by neurons and oligodendrocytes [14–18], while neuroinflammation triggered by myelinated axonopathy may promote Tau hyperphosphorylation [19–21]. However, due to the limited sample size, the independent and joint contributions of demyelination to Aβ and Tau pathologies remain unclear, particularly across different stages of AD progression.

Recently, WM uptake of [18F] florbetapir (FBP), measured by florbetapir positron emission tomography (FBP-PET), has been proposed as a potential proxy for assessing brain myelin in AD [11, 12, 22]. This interpretation is based on the reported affinity of stilbene derivatives for β-sheet structures present not only in fibrillar Aβ but also in specific proteins such as myelin basic protein (MBP) [23–25], thereby enabling the estimation of myelin loss [26, 27]. However, in the context of AD, Aβ-related signal contributions must be appropriately accounted for [11, 12, 22].

Here, we leveraged a large cohort from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) with cross-sectional and longitudinal data to investigate dynamic alterations in myelin content across the AD spectrum, as assessed by FBP-PET. The present study provides novel perspectives on the dynamic relationships between myelin homeostasis and AD-related pathology, including Aβ deposition and NFTs, and offers integrated insights into myelin-associated processes underlying AD pathology.

Methods

Ethical approval

All research involving human participants adhered to the ethical principles of the Institutional and National Research Committees and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent revisions or equivalent ethical standards. For the ADNI cohort, written informed consent was obtained from all participants, their study partners, or legally authorized representatives prior to the conduct of any study-related procedures. This study involved only secondary analysis of ADNI data; no local patient data were included. Further details on ADNI’s ethical oversight and study protocol are available at http://www.adni-info.org.

ADNI participants

Data used in the preparation of this article were obtained from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database (adni.loni.usc.edu). The ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public-private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD. The primary goal of ADNI has been to test whether serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early Alzheimer's disease (AD). In this study, participants who had FBP-PET, T1-weighted (T1w) MRI and clinical diagnosis were included. Some participants had data from concurrent CSF and plasma examinations (Aβ42, Aβ40, and pTau181), [18F] flortaucipir (FTP) PET, Diffusion-weighted Imaging (DWI), and Fluid Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR) scans. Researchers at each ADNI study site involved in the ADNI project obtained ethical approval. All participants provided written informed consent.

Acquisition and processing of PET imaging data

Details on descriptions of PET imaging procedures were obtained from the processed results provided by UC Berkeley and the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, which are publicly available in the ADNI database. Amyloid PET images using FBP were fully pre-processed by ADNI, including frame realignment and averaging, linear transformation to correct head positioning, standardization of voxel size, and smoothing to a uniform resolution of 6 mm. T1w MRI was first segmented and parcellated with Freesurfer (FS) using the Desikan-Killiany atlas, and then coregistered to the amyloid PET image with SPM. Positive amyloid was defined as a cortical-cerebellar average standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) ≥ 1.11 [28]. Cortical FBP SUVR was defined as integrated values from meta-regions of the frontal, anterior and posterior cingulate, lateral parietal, and lateral temporal regions. The mean cortical SUVR values (mean ± standard error [SE]) were 1.11 ± 0.005 for CN, 1.20 ± 0.013 for MCI, and 1.42 ± 0.007 for dementia. Centiloid (CL) values were derived from cortical SUVR using the equation: CL = 188.22 × cortical SUVR − 189.16. Tau PET images using FTP were also smoothed to a uniform resolution of 6 mm, then coregistered in SPM to the bias-corrected T1w created by FS. The intensity was normalized by the inferior cerebellar gray matter (GM), and the mean Tau uptake (SUVR) within each Desikan-Killiany region and the temporal-meta regions of interest (MetaROI) (including the entorhinal cortex, amygdala, fusiform gyrus, inferior temporal gyrus, and middle temporal gyrus) was calculated. The specific processing methods are available at https://adni.loni.usc.edu/.

Adjustment of FBP-PET SUVR in WM

The primary variable analyzed in this study is the FBP-PET SUVR in WM, serving as a measure of myelination. To reduce the impact of FBP binding to Aβ plaques on FBP binding in WM, we adjusted the raw FBP SUVR in WM as previously described [11, 22]. In brief, in the CN group, which included both Aβ + and Aβ- participants, we used the integrated cortical FBP SUVR as a predictor and FBP SUVR in WM as the dependent variable to fit a linear regression model. We then adjusted the FBP SUVR in WM by subtracting the predicted SUVR (using the estimated linear model) to account for the cortical FBP-PET signal. To quantify the deviation of FBP SUVR in WM in Aβ + participants of the CI group compared to the CN group, we calculated the z-score of adjusted FBP SUVR in WM using the CN group as a reference, resulting in the final myelination measure WM SUVR, also described as Cerebral WM SUVR (Z-score). In this study, the median WM SUVR was used as the cutoff to define WM + and WM − uptake, representing higher and lower white matter uptake (Fig. S1), respectively, rather than a biologically defined positive or negative status.

In sensitivity analysis, the WM calibration procedure was repeated using only Aβ − CN participants as the reference group. The resulting z-score metric was termed Aβ − CN–recalibrated WM SUVR. A two-component Gaussian mixture model was fitted to the distribution of Aβ − CN–recalibrated WM SUVR, and the intersection point between the two fitted Gaussian components was used as a data-driven threshold for WM classification. In the present analysis, this threshold was estimated at − 1.987 (Fig. S2A).

Processing of diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) data

The raw DWI data were preprocessed using PreQual for denoising, artifact correction, and signal dropout imputation. T1w images were processed using SLANT-TICV for GM segmentation and MaCRUISE for cortical surface parcellation. Then these preprocessed DWI data were registered to the Johns Hopkins Eve Type 3 white matter atlas within the brain mask of the T1w image, through a combined use of FSL and ANTs, with diffusion scalar metrics calculated for each region of interest (ROI), including fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD), and axial diffusivity (AxD). The processed data were acquired from publicly available data in ADNI from ADSP (Alzheimer’s Disease Sequencing Project) Phenotype Harmonization Consortium. For each metric (RD, FA, MD, AxD), the median values from the left and right hemispheres were averaged to create four separate composite measures.

Total white matter hyperintensities (WMH) volume data acquisition

The WMH volume data were available in the ADSP shared dataset in ADNI, and were calculated from FLAIR data by FS processing. WMH volume was log-transformed for statistical model due to its skewed distribution.

GM volume data acquisition

The GM volume data were extracted from the ADSP-analyzed T1w MRI data using FS processing, which is publicly available in ADNI. We standardized the data employed in the comparisons by calculating the z-score of the ratio between the volumes of ROIs (grey matter regions defined by the FreeSurfer Desikan–Killiany cortical atlas and subcortical segmentation) and intracranial volume (ICV), a commonly used approach to compensate for head size variability [29, 30]. Neurodegeneration status (N + and N −) were defined according to residualized hippocampal volume (rHCV) adjusted for ICV (Fig. S3), as previously reported [31].

Acquisition of CSF data

CSF Aβ42 and Aβ40 were obtained from the ADNI database, provided by the University of Pennsylvania using 2D-UPLC-tandem mass spectrometry. CSF pTau181 was achieved using the fully automated Roche Elecsys and Cobas e 601 immunoassay analyzer system, with detailed methods available at https://adni.loni.usc.edu/. In this study, CSF pTau181 ≥ 23 pg/mL was used as the cutoff to define T + versus T–, as described previously [31].

Acquisition of plasma data

Plasma Aβ42 and Aβ40 were measured using a technique that combines immunoprecipitation and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) by Bateman Lab. Details of methods are available at https://adni.loni.usc.edu/.

Plasma pTau181 was analyzed using the Single Molecule array (Simoa) technique, with an in-house assay developed by the Clinical Neurochemistry Laboratory at the University of Gothenburg, Sweden, as described in their previous study [32].

Cerebrovascular risk factors

Principal Component 1 of Cerebrovascular Risk Factors (CVR-PC1) and number of MRI infarcts were included as cerebrovascular risk factors in the analyses. CVR-PC1 was computed by the first principal component of heart disease, stroke history, hypertension, diabetes, and BMI in ADNI cohort and obtained from the ADSP dataset. Detailed methods have been described previously [33]. The number of MRI infarcts was derived from imaging data processed by the Charles DeCarli team at UC Davis, using standardized visual ratings of infarcts ≥ 3 mm based on T1- and T2-weighted MRI scans. This method has demonstrated excellent inter-rater reliability (κ = 0.73–0.90). The details are available at https://adni.loni.usc.edu/.

Data analysis

All data processing and analysis were performed using R version 4.4.1. Correlation analysis was generally conducted using linear correlation methods, with results reported as p-values and β-values. For Table 1 and Table S1−4, P-values were calculated using Chi-squared tests for categorical variables and t-tests for continuous variables. General group differences in medians were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. A/T and A/T/N group comparisons used median comparisons via the Kruskal–Wallis test. For multiple comparisons between groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's test was applied, with FDR correction. Multiple spatial comparisons of DTI metrics were performed using linear models with FDR correction. Mediation analysis was conducted using the mediation package to examine whether the association between the independent variable and the outcome variable is statistically mediated by the hypothesized mediator, adjusting for age, sex, APOE4 status, and cerebrovascular risk factors. Results

Demographics

Demographical characteristics were summarized in Table 1. This study included 1,237 participants from the ADNI database with FBP PET scans. Based on a predefined WM SUVR threshold (Fig. S1), 47.2% were classified as WM + uptake, i.e. without myelin damage, and 52.8% as WM- uptake, i.e. with myelin damage, at baseline. WM + uptake individuals were more likely to be cognitively normal (CN: 54.8% vs. 25.9%) and less likely to be cognitively impaired (CI: 45.2% vs. 74.1%) compared to WM- uptake (p < 0.001). The WM + uptake group was slightly younger (mean age = 72.77 ± 7.49 vs. 74.27 ± 7.83 years, p = 0.001). APOE ε4 carriers were more frequent in the WM- uptake group (51.0% vs. 32.4%, p < 0.001). WM + uptake individuals exhibited significantly lower vascular burden, as reflected by a higher CVR-PC1 score (mean 0.02 vs. − 0.52, p < 0.001). No significant difference was found in sex distribution, education years, and number of MRI infarcts (all p > 0.05). Regarding FBP PET, WM + uptake individuals had a significantly longer duration of follow-up (mean 3.42 vs. 2.46 years, p < 0.001) and lower baseline cortical SUVR (mean 1.14 vs. 1.26, p < 0.001). During the FBP-PET follow-up, concurrent data for CSF Aβ42/40, CSF pTau181, plasma Aβ42/40, plasma pTau181, FTP-PET, DWI, FLAIR (WMH volume), and T1w MRI (GM volume) were also comprehensively summarized in Table 1.

WM SUVR as a candidate marker of myelin integrity

DTI is commonly used to assess the WM microstructural abnormalities and is sensitive to myelin-related changes [34]. Here, we found that lower WM SUVR was associated with reduced FA (estimate = 0.222 [95% confidence interval: 0.138 to 0.307], p < 0.001) and increased RD (estimate = −0.265 [95% confidence interval: −0.348 to −0.182], p < 0.001), MD (estimate = −0.262 [95% confidence interval: −0.344 to −0.180, p < 0.001), and AxD (estimate = −0.265 [95% confidence interval: −0.348 to −0.182], p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). While decreased FA and increased MD and RD are consistent with reduced myelin integrity, the observed increase in AxD may reflect axonal degeneration or neuroinflammation, rather than myelin loss per se [35]. Additionally, WM SUVR decreases were associated with increased WMH volume (estimate = −0.349 [95% confidence interval, −0.418 to −0.280], p < 0.001) (Fig. S4), consistent with the hypothesis that WMH indicates demyelinating lesions. Progressive disruption of myelin integrity in AD

A stepwise WM SUVR decline was seen across clinical groups: dementia < MCI < CN, with estimate = 0.776 [95% confidence interval, 0.619 to 0.931], p < 0.001, between dementia and MCI, and estimate = 0.495 [95% confidence interval, 0.380 to 0.613], p < 0.001, between MCI and CN, respectively (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, APOE4 carrier exhibited decreased WM SUVR compared to APOE4 non-carrier (estimate = 0.467 [95% confidence interval, 0.349 to 0.584], p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). Across different A/T profile, WM SUVR was also reduced in A +/T- and A +/T + groups vs. A-/T-, with the lowest in A +/T + (Fig. 2C). Notably, the A–/T+ group showed WM SUVR levels higher than in the A +/T- group (p = 0.007) and comparable to that in the A-/T- group (p = 0.064) (Fig. 2C). Longitudinal mixed-effects models (Model 1, see Methods) monitoring myelin status over 5–10 years confirmed lower WM SUVR in Aβ + than in CN/Aβ- (estimate = −0.346 [95% confidence interval, −0.683 to −0.008], p = 0.046) with a significant group × time interaction indicating a faster decline in Aβ + over time (estimate = −0.063 [95% confidence interval, −0.093 to −0.032], p < 0.001) (Fig. 2D, left). Cross-sectional analysis showed a negative association (Aβ-:estimate = −0.023 [95% confidence interval, −0.036 to −0.009], p = 0.001; Aβ + :estimate = −0.035 [95% confidence interval, −0.060 to −0.010], p = 0.006) between decreased WM SUVR and older ages in CN, suggesting that age remains a significant factor in accelerated myelin degeneration or a loss of the ability of remyelination [36] (Fig. 2D, right). Associations between demyelination and early cortical Aβ accumulation in AD

To evaluate the relationship of demyelination on Aβ deposition, a relatively early event in AD pathogenesis, from a temporal perspective, we analyzed all cases with two years of follow-up data from the ADNI database. Our results (Model 2, see Methods) revealed that lower baseline WM SUVR was associated with faster rates of cortical Aβ accumulation (estimate = −0.014[95% confidence interval, −0.021 to −0.007], p < 0.001) (Fig. 4A). The LOESS-fitted curve also indicated that, prior to the plateau of Aβ accumulation, the WM- uptake group exhibited a higher two-year cumulative slope of cortical PBP SUVR compared with the WM + uptake group (Fig. 4B). The KS test confirmed a significant difference in the distributions of the LOESS-fitted values between the WM- and WM + uptake groups (D = 0.658, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4B), suggesting that lower myelin integrity is associated with earlier cortical Aβ accumulation. Fitting the clinical progression based on the average cortical SUVR values from the CN, MCI, and dementia groups showed that the curves for the WM − and WM + uptake groups differed prior to the clinical diagnosis of dementia [37], particularly during the early stages of AD (Fig. 4B). The brain region differential comparison (Model 3, see Methods) also showed that during the early stages of AD, the WM- uptake group exhibited extensive cortical and subcortical regions with higher 2-year Aβ accumulation compared to the reference (Fig. 4C, Fig. S5A). Mediation analysis incorporating longitudinal outcomes suggested that baseline cortical SUVR did not statistically mediate the association between WM SUVR and subsequent Aβ accumulation, while a significant direct association was observed (Fig. S6A), consistent with a potential association between myelin status and Aβ deposition. Subsequently, we conducted a spatial comparison of WM regions based on DTI metrics, focusing on RD, which is sensitive to white matter microstructural changes and often reflects myelin-related properties [38], to identify the myelin areas most closely associated with cortical Aβ accumulation. As Aβ accumulates, the RD in both superficial and deep white matter shows an increasing trend. Discussion

WM SUVR, which is influenced in part by MBP-related binding and myelin content, has been utilized previously in both multiple sclerosis and AD [22, 39], as a proxy measure of WM integrity for the present analyses. In this study, “myelin integrity” refers specifically to myelin content and sheath status [40], rather than broader microstructural properties inferred from DTI metrics such as FA [41]. Building on prior work [22, 42], we examined the relationship between WM SUVR and FLAIR-based WMH and DTI metrics, and observed consistent patterns across these modalities. We acknowledge that DTI metrics and WMH are influenced by heterogeneous microstructural and vascular processes; however, they may provide convergent, albeit non-specific support for myelin-related alterations within white matter. The association between lower WM SUVR and increased AxD may reflect the combined effects of reduced myelin content and disruption of sheath integrity. Overall, these findings support the use of WM SUVR as a proxy measure, enriched in myelin-related signal, for assessing myelin integrity in the human brain.

Through cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses across the AD spectrum, we observed a progressive decline in myelin integrity with AD progression, consistent with previous studies [12, 22]. While we observed an overall decline in myelin integrity across the AD spectrum, this pattern is not uniform across all subgroups. Within the A/T classification, the A − T + group—often considered representative of non-AD conditions such as suspected non-Alzheimer’s pathology (SNAP) or primary age-related tauopathy (PART)—does not show a clear reduction in WM SUVR. However, when neurodegeneration is taken into account (A/T/N framework), a different pattern emerges. The A − T + N + group exhibits significantly lower WM SUVR compared with the A − T − N − group, and even lower than the A + T − N − group (Fig. S7A). This suggests that in SNAP- or PART-like conditions, myelin alterations become evident primarily when neurodegeneration is present, indicating that myelin dysfunction may reflect a broader vulnerability associated with neurodegenerative processes rather than Tau pathology alone. At the same time, myelin integrity appears to be sensitive to Aβ burden. Across all T/N strata, A + groups consistently show lower WM SUVR than A − groups, with the greatest reduction observed in the A + T + N + group (Fig. S7A–C). Together, these findings suggest a dual pattern: myelin alterations may represent a general marker of neurodegenerative vulnerability, which is further exacerbated in the presence of Aβ-related pathology, particularly when multiple AD-related processes co-occur.

Within this vulnerability framework, we examined how myelin integrity interacts with the temporal dynamics of Aβ and Tau pathology across disease stages. Using a unified temporal framework, we identified vulnerable time windows during which Aβ and Tau are more susceptible to myelin dysfunction, addressing limitations of prior studies that focused on isolated pathological events [11, 12, 22, 43]. During the early Aβ accumulation phase, lower myelin integrity was associated with a higher rate of Aβ accumulation within two years. This pattern may reflect a leftward shift along the Aβ accumulation trajectory, indicating a shorter time interval to reach the plateau phase. In this context, reduced myelin integrity may be linked to accelerated progression of Aβ pathology and earlier attainment of the plateau stage, highlighting inter-individual variability in the timing of Aβ progression.

Importantly, in the context of reduced myelin integrity (WM- uptake), Tau aggregation and propagation were observed prior to the Aβ plateau, whereas under relatively preserved myelin conditions (WM + uptake), Tau spreading typically followed the plateau. These findings suggest that demyelination is associated with an earlier onset of Tau pathology, and may relate to inter-individual variability in the timing of transition from early Aβ positivity to more advanced pathological stages [44]. Notably, increased Tau signal elevations were not evident during the Aβ − stage, even in vulnerable regions. The association between myelin integrity and Tau pathology emerged only with increasing Aβ burden, indicating a stage-dependent relationship. Together, these results support a temporally structured pattern in which reduced myelin integrity is associated with faster Aβ accumulation during the early phase and earlier Tau emergence at higher levels of Aβ. Notably, this overall pattern remained robust across alternative WM calibration strategies. When calibration was restricted to biologically defined Aβ − CN participants, WM-related differences in both amyloid accumulation and Tau pathology became detectable at earlier disease stages while preserving the same sequential pattern. These findings indicate that the principal conclusions are not dependent on calibration strategy and suggest that an amyloid-negative reference may increase sensitivity to early pathological deviations (Fig. S8).

While several experimental studies have suggested that oligodendrocyte abnormalities and myelin dysfunction may contribute to Aβ accumulation [14–18], our data are observational in nature and do not allow inference of a causal relationship between these processes. Nonetheless, lower myelin integrity was associated with faster cortical Aβ accumulation (Fig. 4A), while in Aβ-positive individuals, higher cortical Aβ was in turn associated with a greater rate of WM SUVR decline (Fig. S9), suggesting a potential bidirectional relationship, possibly driven by shared underlying processes. Consistent with this, demyelination was associated with aging and APOE ε4 status (Fig. 2B, D), both of which are linked to oligodendrocyte dysfunction and reduced myelin maintenance [45, 46]. In contrast, the relationship between myelin integrity and Tau pathology was stage-dependent, emerging only in the presence of Aβ and becoming more apparent after the plateau phase. This is consistent with models in which Aβ deposition facilitates Tau propagation from the entorhinal cortex to the neocortex [47]. The Aβ plateau may therefore represent a transition to a later stage of disease, marked by the emergence of myelin–Tau associations and coordinated structural degeneration. Early myelin loss may shift this transition earlier, lowering the threshold for myelin–Tau coupling.

Rather than implying a direct mechanistic coupling, we conceptualize myelin dysfunction as a perturbation of the brain’s stability landscape. From this perspective, AD reflects a progressive loss of brain stability under accumulating Aβ and Tau pathology, rather than a strictly linear cascade driven by either process alone. Within this framework, myelin dysfunction may represent an amplification of underlying structural vulnerability. At the individual level, myelin integrity may serve as an index of resilience to pathological stress, capturing variability in the capacity to maintain structural stability despite comparable Aβ and Tau burden. In this sense, myelin integrity extends the A/T/N framework by introducing a complementary, non-specific dimension of system-level vulnerability, reflecting the brain’s structural robustness in the face of neurodegenerative processes. Consistent with this framework, the WM classification recalibrated using Aβ − CN identified a subgroup with preserved myelin integrity and slower pathological progression (Fig. S8). Importantly, although recalibrated WM classification showed substantial concordance with amyloid status (κ = 0.727), a considerable proportion of Aβ + individuals remained classified as WM + uptake (Fig. S2C). This observation suggests that WM status is not reducible to amyloid positivity alone and may capture inter-individual variability in structural vulnerability or resilience beyond that reflected by amyloid burden.

Given the potential confounding effects of cerebrovascular factors on the assessment of myelin integrity [48], we included cerebrovascular risk factors—CVR-PC1 and the number of MRI-detected infarcts—as covariates in our analyses [33, 49]. Additionally, despite its association with myelin-related change, WMH is also considered to reflect brain vascular injury [49]. Recognizing the reduced FBP uptake in WMH relative to normal-appearing white matter (NAWM) [22], we further adjusted for WMH volume in the models. Importantly, even after controlling for these variables, as well as age, sex, and APOE4 status, the model estimates of WM SUVR remained robust. However, in the longitudinal analyses, WMH and cerebrovascular risk variables were not included because of substantial missing data over the 5–10-year follow-up period, and their omission may result in residual confounding.

A major strength of this study lies in its temporal analysis of how myelin disruption is associated with Aβ and Tau dynamics during AD, supported by large-scale cohort data. Myelin loss has notable associations with both pathologies, with a sequential pattern observed. Nevertheless, important caveats should be noted when interpreting the current findings: (1) The potential impact of Aβ plaque deposition on FBP-PET binding in WM is inevitable. To mitigate this, we employed a previously established method in which WM FBP signals were adjusted based on cortical GM signals [11, 22]. This approach ensures that WM FBP signals are independent of cortical GM FBP signals, thereby minimizing the confounding influence of Aβ-related signals in WM. That said, a small degree of residual Aβ-related signal cannot be entirely excluded. However, as the association with cortical SUVR was markedly attenuated after adjustment, any residual effect is likely limited and unlikely to materially affect the interpretation that the adjusted WM metric primarily reflects white matter integrity. (2) We employed WM SUVR corrected for cortical GM signals, which may introduce some deviation in the linear relationship with the Aβ PET biomarkers. However, despite this correction, the association between myelin alterations and the rate of Aβ accumulation, as well as NFTs signals, remains robust under equivalent cortical signal conditions. Therefore, this does not compromise the validity of our primary conclusions. (3) The imaging data used in this study were obtained from processed data in the ADNI database. Although it cannot be confirmed whether images from different modalities were registered to the same original T1-w MRI, it is confirmed that all original data were collected within a comparable time frame. Due to the inability to perform large-scale spatial registration across modalities, myelin content was treated as a binary measure, mitigating small spatial biases in the large dataset. (4) Residual selection bias due to non-random missingness across imaging modalities cannot be fully excluded (Tables S1–S4). Such missingness may preferentially exclude more severely affected individuals, potentially attenuating the observed associations. Importantly, subsampling-based sensitivity analyses indicate that the key findings in Model 4 remain robust and are unlikely to be driven by sample imbalance (Fig. S10). (5) Future studies incorporating alternative myelin-sensitive imaging techniques (e.g., T1/T2 myelin mapping) will be important for validating and contextualizing WM SUVR. Finally (6), given the observed spatial patterns of demyelination, further work should investigate the network-level interactions among fiber tract demyelination, Aβ deposition, and NFT aggregation across connected brain regions to better characterize the progression of AD pathology.

Conclusions

In summary, despite caveats discussed above, our investigation shows that progressive myelin degeneration occurs throughout the course of AD. Notably, declines in myelin integrity are associated with earlier Aβ deposition in the early stages and greater Tau accumulation in later stages. This sequential, structured pattern suggests that myelin dysfunction is linked to broader system-level perturbations in neurodegenerative processes. Collectively, these results underscore the relevance of myelin in AD and support its potential role as an indicator of structural vulnerability across disease progression.
