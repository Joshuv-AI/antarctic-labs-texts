Pilot Randomized Trial of Dialysis Less Frequently in the Elderly: The DLITE Study
Authors: Christine A. White, Hasitha Welihinda, Andrew G. Day, Daren K. Heyland, Rachel M. Holden
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe elderly represent the most rapidly growing age group with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) world-wide. The hemodialysis (HD) prescription that provides the best outcomes for this distinct population with reduced life expectancy is unknown. The primary objective of the DLITE pilot trial was to determine the feasibility and safety of conducting a trial designed to determine whether twice versus thrice weekly HD improves quality of life (QOL) and important clinical outcomes in elderly patients with HD.MethodsWe conducted a pilot single-center open randomized controlled trial (RCT) of patients aged ≥ 70 years of age who are on incident HD. Subjects were randomized to twice (n = 20) versus thrice (n = 20) weekly HD and followed for 9 months. There was no residual kidney function (RKF) trigger for HD prescription escalation. The primary outcomes were those of feasibility and safety. Secondary outcomes included QOL, hyperkalemia, mortality, and need for emergency HD.ResultsRecruitment rate was 1.1 patients/mo. The consent rate of eligible subjects was high (77%), adherence was ≥ 99%, 96% of QOL surveys were completed, and 78% of randomized subjects completed the study. Pre-HD normokalemia rates for twice versus thrice weekly were 89% and 90%, respectively. Urgent HD treatments for hypervolemia and hyperkalemia, hospitalizations, and death were infrequent in both arms.ConclusionThe results of this pilot RCT of twice versus thrice weekly HD in elderly patients on incident HD reveal that the study intervention was feasible and safe. These results will inform the design of a multicenter RCT with QOL, economic, morbidity, and mortality end points.

The elderly comprise a significant proportion of hemodialysis (HD) patients. In 2023, 30% of the 24,401 Canadians being treated with HD were 75 years or older.1 In most highly resourced jurisdictions, three 4-hour dialysis treatment sessions per week is the standard of care for patients receiving in-center HD. This strategy assumes that all patients require the same minimum dialysis frequency and dose and does not take into consideration important characteristics such as age, size, comorbidity, or life expectancy. The elderly might benefit the most from reconsideration of this “one size fits all strategy” particularly given their much reduced life expectancy on HD (median: 2.6 years for those aged ≥ 75 years),2 their willingness to trade-off life expectancy to decrease the burden of HD,3 and the uncertainty of whether HD confers survival benefit in elderly comorbid frail patients.4,5

There is little data to guide clinicians prescribing HD in general and especially in the elderly. Two landmark historical RCTs examined per-treatment HD dose in patients on prevalent HD receiving thrice weekly dialysis.6,7 One study specifically excluded patients aged > 70 years7 and neither provided subgroup analysis by age. Several small pilot RCTs of incremental HD have recently been published but none included significant numbers of elderly patents (mean age: early 60s) or presented results by age category.8, 9, 10 A systematic review of observational studies comparing twice versus thrice weekly HD revealed a paucity of high quality data with included studies mostly retrospective, lacking in methodological rigor, with substantial selection bias and confounding and no subgroup analysis by age.11

In a Canadian survey of patients, caregivers, physicians, and nurses, the research uncertainty of "how does one determine the optimal length of time and frequency of HD for a particular patient and how can dialysis be tailored so each patient gets effective dialysis in the shortest period of time?" was rated as the first priority by patients and nurses.12 The uncertainty of "how could dialysis delivery be improved to offer patients the best possible quality of life?" was rated as the first priority by caregivers.12

Despite these priorities, patient HD frequency preference and QOL are rarely considered in HD prescription. Further, RCTs in HD prescription have historically not included QOL outcomes.6,7 Current HD prescription guidelines and metrics used to evaluate the quality of delivered HD care generally exclude QOL measures.13, 14, 15 The 2024 report of a National Kidney Foundation sponsored Kidney Dialysis Outcomes Quality Initiative Scientific Workshop Initiative highlights the importance of considering patients’ life goals and priorities in patient management.16 In the 2019 Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes Dialysis initiation, modality choice, access, and prescription controversies conference report, the need to incorporate patients’ goals in decision making around HD prescriptions is emphasized.17 Conducting RCTs comparing less intense HD prescription to current standard of care that include patient-reported outcome measures was highlighted as a crucial step to achieve this.

There exists a significant gap in our understanding of the optimal HD frequency from the perspective of morbidity, mortality, and QOL in elderly patients receiving HD. The primary objective of the DLITE pilot trial was to determine the feasibility and safety of conducting a larger phase 3 multicenter trial designed to determine whether twice-weekly HD compared with thrice-weekly HD, improves clinical outcomes and patient-reported QOL measures in elderly patients on incident HD.

Methods

The DLITE trial is an investigator-initiated, single-center, randomized pilot study. (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03787719). The trial ran between June 2018 and September 2022 in Kingston, Ontario, Canada with a 5-month hiatus in recruitment during the COVID-19 pandemic (March–August 2020) The trial ended when the enrollment target sample size was reached (n = 40). The sample size was targeted to assess the feasibility of a fully powered trial. The study design is illustrated in Figure 1. The Queens University Health Sciences Research Ethics Board approved the trial (6021932), which adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Trial Participants

Adult patients aged ≥ 70 years beginning HD treatment were screened for eligibility. The original protocol had set 75 years as the minimum age; however, it became evident early on in the trial that insufficient patents would be able to be recruited, so eligibility was expanded to include those aged ≥ 70 years in February 2019. Eligible patients were those with incident ESKD receiving in-center HD as their kidney replacement therapy and were anticipated to receive in-center HD as their long-term form of kidney replacement therapy. Given the very high early mortality rate among elderly patients starting HD,2 the possibility of recovery in the setting of acute kidney injury, treatment withdrawal or conversion to home-based therapies, potential participants were approached for enrollment 7 weeks after HD initiation HD. This prevented the unnecessary and expensive early inclusion of patients who do not in fact continue in-center HD. We excluded hospitalized patients without an alternate-level-of-care designation, those with any missed HD treatment sessions due to nonadherence, and those who were already prescribed twice weekly dialysis. Participants were not eligible if there were anticipated significant barriers to ascertainment of patient-reported experience measures or were deemed medically unsuitable for reduced prescription as assessed by primary nephrologists in conjunction with the study investigators. There was no minimum RKF required for study participation. The CONSORT diagram is presented in Figure 2.Figure 2Consort diagram showing patient flow through study. ∗Weeks 4 and 5.

Run-in Period

The first 5 weeks of the study was a run-in period of thrice weekly dialysis to evaluate dry weight and to optimize interdialytic fluid gains and potassium (K) management. During this period, the ultrafiltration rate (UFR) was recorded at each HD treatment and K was measured weekly. In patients with UFRs > 8.5 ml/min/kg, fluid restriction education was provided; and diuretics, which are routinely used post–HD initiation at our center to optimize residual urine output, were reviewed and optimized by study investigators in conjunction with the primary treating nephrologist. Similarly, patients with K above the assay upper limit of normal received dietary counselling, and K bath adjustments. Participants were eligible for randomization if: (i) serum K values were within the normal range of the laboratory on either a K2, K3, or K4 bath for both weeks 4 and week 5; (ii) mean per-treatment UFR was < 8.7 ml/kg/h for weeks 4 and 5; and (iii) the patient was within 1 kg of their prescribed dry weight. A maximum of 8.7 ml/kg/h UFR was chosen to avoid those eventually randomized to twice weekly HD from exceeding a per-treatment UFR of 13 ml/kg/h, which is considered to be the acceptable upper UFR because of increased mortality in observational studies above this threshold.18

Once a participant was eligible for randomization, RKF was assessed by the average of the 48-hour urine urea and creatinine clearance. Plasma samples were collected for creatinine and urea post- and pre-HD flanking the urine collection. Creatinine and urea were measured using the IDMS traceable Abbott Diagnostics enzymatic assay on an Abbott Architech Plus 6000 at Kingston Health Sciences Center.

Randomization

Randomization was implemented through a secure password protected web-based application that maintained a secure audit trail and guaranteed concealment of future allocations by a study coordinator. Randomization was conducted 1:1 using permuted blocks of randomly varying size (2, 4, and 6 but undisclosed during study) and stratified by RKF (< 5 ml/min, 5–10 ml/min, and > 10 ml/min). RKF is a major predictor of outcomes in patients on HD19; therefore, it was critical that the groups were balanced for this.

Intervention

Participants were randomized to either twice-weekly or thrice-weekly HD treatment for 9 months, which was felt to be of sufficient duration to assess the primary outcomes. The new HD prescription took effect on the Monday or Tuesday following randomization. Given the nature of the study, it was not possible to blind treatment assignment or was there an option for a sham HD session for those randomized to the twice-weekly arm. All study participants received standard-of-care otherwise from their attending nephrologist according to local practice informed by guidelines. There was no reflex dose escalation in HD prescription based on RKF or Kt/V.

Data Collection

Subjects were followed-up with from randomization until study completion (9-month period) or death, study withdrawal, or modality change. Baseline demographic data included age, sex assigned at birth, ethnicity, primary cause of ESKD, weight, height, comorbidities, smoking history, body mass index, HD access (site, type, and date of installation). Laboratory results were obtained from routine dialysis care at baseline and as per standard-of-care every 6 to 12 weeks. These measures included hemoglobin, K, bicarbonate, albumin, Kt/V, urea reduction ratio, calcium, phosphate, and parathyroid hormone. RKF was assessed before randomization and at study completion by using the average of the 48-hour urea and creatinine clearances and by urine volume, except for patients randomized to the twice-weekly arm in whom the average of 72-hour urea and creatinine clearances along with urine volume was performed at study completion.

Predialysis K was measured weekly for the duration of the trial. For the twice-weekly arm (Tuesday/Saturday or Monday/Friday), K was measured after the short break to avoid the potential for Friday or Saturday results not having timely review. For the thrice-weekly arm, weekly K was measured according to standard practice in each unit with about half the values being after the long break. HD sessions, treatment times, interdialytic weight gains, and UFRs were reviewed weekly. Any additional or missed treatments and the reasons for them were recorded. Participant death and cause was recorded weekly.

The subjects were reviewed every 6 weeks for hospitalization and reasons for hospitalization and cardiovascular events.

HD prescription (dry weight, medication adjustments, K bath, and additional blood work) was managed by the primary treating nephrologist as per the usual standard of care. In the main in-center HD unit, patients were seen and reviewed by a nephrologist at a minimum weekly basis and comprehensive renal blood work review occurred every 6 weeks. Additional laboratory and clinical assessments occurred on an as needed basis. In satellite units, patients were seen by their nephrologist every 6 weeks at the time of comprehensive blood work review. Otherwise, they were assessed by the nephrology nursing team with remote input from the nephrologist as required. Unless a serious adverse event (SAE) occurred, decisions to escalate HD frequency from twice to thrice weekly HD was initiated by the treating nephrologist with input from the study investigators. There was no study target Kt/V for either arm.

SAEs

SAEs were recorded weekly and were defined as follows: (i) emergency HD dialysis session (unscheduled and needing to occur before the regularly scheduled treatment time) for either volume overload or hyperkalemia, (ii) severe hyperkalemia resulting in hemodynamic instability and or electrocardiogram changes, and (iii) severe volume overload resulting in hypoxia (oxygen saturation < 92%). Extra planned HD sessions for volume management were not considered an SAE. A protocol amendment was introduced midtrial such that extra HD sessions for hyperkalemia or volume overload were not considered SAEs for subjects admitted for a nonhyperkalemic or volume overload diagnosis. Twice-weekly subjects experiencing an SAE were withdrawn. Because this was primarily a feasibility and safety study, we did not wish to contaminate the main safety results by including results from SAE patients randomized to twice weekly after their conversion to thrice weekly in the twice weekly results. Nevertheless, all study clinical and HD events after withdrawal and until original planned study end were collected. Events occurring before withdrawal were included in the main study analyses, whereas events occurring afterward were not and were reported separately. Subjects randomized to thrice weekly experiencing an SAE were not withdrawn.

Patient-Reported Outcome Measures

Patient-reported experience was assessed using the revised Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale (ESAS-r) at randomization, every 6 weeks and at trial end. Although initially developed for patients with advanced cancer, the ESAS-r is recommended for QOL assessment in ESKD,20 has been shown to be simple to administer, is valid for the longitudinal assessment of physical and psychological symptom burden in patients requiring HD and is responsive to change.21,22 QOL was assessed by using the 36-item Short Form survey instrument (SF-36) at randomization, every 12 weeks and at the end of trial.23 These were paper-based surveys and research coordinators were available to assist if required.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this pilot trial were those of feasibility and safety. The 4 feasibility outcomes were as follows: (i) recruitment (> 3 patients/mo over 12 months meeting criteria for randomization and providing informed consent), (ii) proportion of eligible patients enrolled and randomized, (iii) adherence to study protocol (≥ 90% of prescribed HD treatments completed and proportion of QOL surveys completed), and (iv) study completion (≥ 50% of randomized subjects completing the trial). The 2 predefined safety outcomes included K and target weight achievement. We considered this trial safe if ≥ 90% of serum K levels remained within the normal range of the laboratory with standards of practice applied (e.g., dietary intervention and adjustment of dialysis K baths). We considered this trial safe if ≥ 90% of subjects remained < 1.0 kg above their prescribed dry weight after usual standard-of-care assessment and adjustment.

Secondary outcomes included the change in ESAS-r from baseline to the average of all follow-up assessments coded ordinally, as improvement (≥ 1 point average increase), consistent (not > 1-point average improvement or decrease), worsening (a decrease of ≥ 1 point) and finally, death during the 42-week follow-up. ESAS-r, SF-36 mental component summary score and SF-36 physical component summary score at each time point were described by arm. Other end points included dialysis-related outcomes such as urgent unscheduled HD for hyperkalemia or volume overload (SAEs), weekly pre-HD K concentrations > 5.9 mmol/l, treatments with UFR > 13 ml/kg/h, change in RKF and other relevant clinical outcomes such as elective HD withdrawal, mortality, kidney function recovery, non–alternate-level-of-care hospitalization.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical methods for this feasibility pilot trial were primarily descriptive. Feasibility measures are provided as percentages or rates with their targets, where applicable. Clinical outcomes and safety events are described by arm as counts and percentages or means and standard deviations.

ESAS-r scores and the SF-36 mental component summary score and physical component summary score were described using boxplots by arm at each time point. We graphically report the mean change from baseline with 95% confidence intervals as estimated by linear mixed effects models estimated by restricted maximum likelihood with arm, week, and their interaction as fixed effects as well as a random effect for subject and first order autoregressive residuals to account for longitudinal within person correlation.

The change in ESAS-r from baseline to the average of all follow-up assessments was coded ordinally as improvement (≥ 1 point average increase), consistent (not > 1-point average improvement or decrease), worsening (a decrease of ≥ 1 point) and finally death during the 42-week follow-up. A change of ≥1 in the ESAS score has been shown to have the best receiver operating curve characteristics in patients with advanced cancer24 and has been recommended for detecting the minimally clinically important differences in ESAS scores.25 The relative risk of improved versus not improved was provided by arm with 95% confidence intervals.

Considering that the focus of this pilot study was feasibility and safety rather than efficacy, the analysis population is per-protocol, including only participants who received some of their assigned intervention and excluding participants after they had switched from twice weekly to thrice weekly HD, recovered kidney function or converted to peritoneal dialysis.

Otherwise, all available data were used and counted in the assigned arm. All participants contributed to the feasibility assessment and participants receiving any intervention to the safety and outcomes assessment. We did not impute missing values; however, the use of the mixed-effects model and our definition of change in ESAS-r included all participants with any postrandomization assessment.

Results

Subject Flow

A total of 239 subjects were screened for eligibility (Figure 2). Seventy-nine did not meet the inclusion criteria with the most common reason being a planned change in kidney replacement therapy modality (n = 42). Ninety-six of the remaining screened subjects were not eligible for enrollment at week 7, the most common reason being kidney function recovery permitting HD discontinuation (n = 48) followed by HD withdrawal (n = 18) and nonrenal death (n = 12). Ten subjects were not approached because of COVID-19 restrictions, 4 were medically unsuitable as determined by the primary nephrologist and study investigator, 2 were transferred to a satellite HD unit before being approached by the research team, and 2 were unable to provide consent leaving 64 enrollment eligible subjects. Of these, 15 declined to participate and the remaining 49 subjects were enrolled. Nine were excluded before randomization. One withdrew from HD, 1 was considered unsuitable by their primary nephrologist and 7 failed the run-in period. The most common reason for run-in failure was UFR > 8.7 ml/kg/h. Forty subjects were therefore randomized.

Three subjects (1 in the thrice-weekly arm and 2 in the twice-weekly arm) were withdrawn from the study postrandomization but before study intervention because of the unwillingness to participate in their allocated arm (n = 2) or renal recovery (n = 1). The remaining 19 patients in the thrice-weekly arm and 18 patients in the twice-weekly arm were included in the analysis although only 17 and 14 of these, respectively, completed the 9-month study follow-up (Figure 2).

The most common cause of ESKD was diabetes (54%) and there was high prevalence of comorbid cardiovascular diseases. Central venous catheters comprised 84% of HD access with 5 and 1 arteriovenous fistulae in the thrice- and twice-weekly arms, respectfully. Primary Outcomes

Feasibility

Seventy-seven percent of approached eligible patients agreed to participate and 62% were randomized (Table 2). The number of subjects randomized per month was lower than target (1.1 vs. 3–4) despite lowering the age floor to 70 years and very few eligible subjects (n = 2) were missed and enrollment rate was high (77%). Adherence to prescribed HD treatments study protocol was ≥ 99% in both arms (target: 90%) and 96% of QOL surveys were completed. Seventy-eight percent of subjects completed the trial (target: 50%). There was no one dominant reason for study noncompletion. Two subjects, displeased with their treatment allocation, withdrew immediately postrandomization and before intervention initiation, 1 died after palliative HD withdrawal, 1 died from pneumosepsis, 2 recovered kidney function, and 1 switched modalities. One subject in the twice-weekly arm was withdrawn after reaching a hyperkalemia SAE as per protocol and another in the twice-weekly arm, was withdrawn by their primary nephrologist for insufficient solute and volume clearance. Dialysis-Related Outcomes

In Table 3, we show HD-related outcomes by treatment arm. There were only 2 urgent unscheduled HD treatments, 1 in the twice-weekly arm for hyperkalemia and 1 in the thrice-weekly arm for volume overload. Ninety-eight and 99.5% percent of weekly K values were < 6.0 mmol/l for the twice- and thrice-weekly arms, respectively. UFRs > 13 ml/kg/h were similar between groups occurring in 2.1% and 2.8% of HD treatments in the thrice- and twice-weekly arms, respectively. For both groups, these were clustered in a few subjects (n = 5 and n = 4 in the thrice- and twice-weekly arms, respectively). Access complications were similar between arms.

Other Clinical Outcomes

Other clinical outcomes are shown in Table 3. One subject in the thrice-weekly arm died after palliative dialysis withdrawal whereas 1 subject died of pneumosepsis in the twice-weekly arm. One patient recovered kidney function in the twice-weekly arm. There were 8 (0.0015/patient-days) and 8 (0.0017/patient-days) hospitalizations in the thrice- and twice-weekly arms, respectively. The number of hospitalization days per patient-days in study was 0.027 and 0.006 in the thrice- and twice-weekly arms, respectively. The most common admitting diagnoses were non-HD related infections (n = 5), cardiovascular events (n = 5), non-HD related surgical events, and other (n = 3).

SAEs

One subject experienced an SAE (hyperkalemia requiring urgent HD) during an in-hospital admission with critical illness and ischemic colitis. It should be noted that this patient’s HD schedule had been altered during their hospitalization and they were receiving more frequent HD before this acute episode. Another subject in the thrice-weekly arm experienced an SAE requiring urgent unscheduled dialysis for volume overload.

Twice Weekly Subject Results After Study Withdrawal

Two subjects were withdrawn from the study, 1 at 27 weeks (SAE) and the other at 19 weeks (insufficient volume and solute clearance as per their primary nephrologist). Data on clinical outcomes after withdrawal were collected but are not included in the group results. Both subjects were alive and on thrice weekly HD at the time of planned study completion. Postwithdrawal, neither subjects experienced an SAE, a UFR >13 ml/min per 1.73 m2 or required extra unscheduled HD sessions for hyperkalemia or volume overload. One was greater than 1 kg above prescribed dry weight on one occasion. Combined, the 2 subjects had 21 K measurements between withdrawal and original study completion date. Only 1 was > 5.9 mmol/l and 4 were between 5.4 and 5.9 mmol/l. The remainder were within the normal range. One subject was hospitalized for pneumonia.

Discussion

The results of this pilot prospective randomized controlled study of twice versus thrice weekly HD in an elderly incident HD population reveal that the study was both feasible and safe. The consent rate of eligible subjects was high (77%), adherence to HD approached 100% in both arms, 96% of QOL tools were completed while participants remained on study, and 78% of randomized subjects completed study. Pre-HD normokalemia rates (90%) were similar in both arms and fewer than 10% of treatment postweights exceeded target weight by 1.0 kg in the twice-weekly arm. The high consent rate may reflect a high desirability of reduced treatment frequency among this patient population.

Recruitment took longer than anticipated with greater numbers of otherwise eligible patients recovering sufficient kidney function to become HD-independent. Indeed, 30% of patients who met the inclusion criteria were ultimately ineligible because of renal recovery. Other common causes of ineligibility were HD withdrawal, nonrenal death and restrictions to research due to COVID-19. We had examined the historical incidence of HD initiation in patients aged > 75 years at our site during study design but did not sufficiently account for their high HD attrition rate in the first 3 months. We had deliberately decided to include HD patients with acute kidney injury to improve enrollment and this resulted in 27% of study participants initially presenting with acute kidney injury with or without CKD (Table 1). The decision to delay enrollment to 7 weeks post–HD initiation despite this having a negative impact on recruitment was to avoid randomizing patients destined to recover kidney function, die, or convert to alternate therapies or withdraw.

In response to low enrollment, we lowered the age for inclusion from 75 to 70 years early in the study resulting in enrollment rates increasing from 0.75 to 1.23 patients/mo but still falling short of target. Forty-two patients did not meet the inclusion criteria because of planned conversion to home therapies. Retrospective chart review revealed that only 3 of them stayed on in-center HD. It is thus unlikely that continuing to follow-up with patients who have a planned transition to a home therapy would have significantly increased enrollment. Finally, 6 patients exceeded the UFR criteria and were excluded. Three of these had “low” body weights (47–56 kg) and intradialytic weight gains were all < 2.0 kg. Thus, the inclusion of low bodyweight patients was more difficult given the stringent weight-dependent UFR requirement, which was calculated assuming twice/wk HD. Future studies will need to consider this in trial design with consideration given to increasing acceptable UFRs in low bodyweight patients.

Study completion exceeded our predefined target. Death rates were lower than expected and likely the result of delaying randomization to 3 months post–HD initiation. This avoided the period with the highest mortality risk in elderly patients on incident HD, which was deliberately done to avoid excessive loss-to-trial completion.2 In addition, it is possible that the entry criteria (i.e., documented nonadherence, excessive UFR, and requirement for K1 bath) excluded those at highest risk of death. A number of subjects recovered kidney function during the run-in, which reflects the inclusion of patients with acute kidney injury.

There was a single SAE with hyperkalemia requiring urgent unplanned HD in a twice-weekly subject; however, this occurred in the setting of critical illness and hospitalization complicated by ischemic colitis. We subsequently modified the protocol to allow for extra acute treatments in patients hospitalized with non–volume overload or nonhyperkalemia admitting diagnoses. In acute illness settings, HD prescriptions or frequency and timing are often modified to accommodate patients’ clinical status and staffing requirements. Subsequent studies should take this into account in trial design.

Significant hyperkalemia (K > 5.9 mmol/l) occurred on 12 occasions in the twice-weekly arm and 7 of these were in a single patient whose K normalized after dietary intervention and prescription of sodium polystyrene. Only 3 K measures were > 5.9 in the thrice-weekly arm with 1 patient accounting for 2 of these. Only 1 of these hyperkalemia events resulted in the ordering of extra unplanned HD sessions. In future studies, hyperkalemia requiring urgent unplanned HD treatment might be a more suitable study end point. It should be noted that we excluded patients prescribed a K1 bath during run-in. No K1 baths were prescribed in either group after randomization although they were permitted.

Although 7% of postdialysis weights were > 1 kg above the dry weight 2 weeks after full 6 times/wk blood work and clinical review, only 1 unscheduled urgent HD treatment for volume management occurred and this was in a thrice-weekly subject. Target weight attainment is a poor surrogate for “euvolemia” because it is dependent on many factors independent of the dialysis prescription (physician and nursing attention to prescribed dry weight, patient refusal to be weighed or to have dry weight targeted, inaccuracy of weight particularity for bed or lift scales, among many others). This metric does not account for patients with significant RKF in whom pre-HD weights are often not used to guide ultrafiltration, or patients with intercurrent illnesses and altered ad hoc fluid removal prescriptions. Future studies should consider alternate volume status outcome measures such as need for extra urgent HD treatments for volume overload. Similar rates of “high” UFRs were observed in the 2 arms and small numbers of patients accounted for all of these (5 in the thrice-weekly and 4 in the twice-weekly arms). It is interesting that the incidence of “high” UFRs during the study were similar between the 2 arms, suggesting that twice weekly patients may adapt their oral intake according to frequency of treatments.

Observational studies have consistently shown better preservation of RKF in twice weekly.11 In the current study, RKF preservation or improvement was seen in both arms and for both RKF measures. From a protocol perspective, urine volume is less cumbersome and cheaper than 48-hour urine creatinine and urea clearance; and therefore, might serve a reasonable outcome measure in future studies. The increase in urine output from baseline was unexpected and may reflect the attention paid to maximizing urine output during the study with the prescription of high diuretic doses. Although we did stratify randomization by RKF, the numbers are too small to allow for subgroup analysis for outcomes by RKF.

Ungraded recommendations in the 2015 updated Kidney Dialysis Outcomes Quality Initiative Clinical Practice Guideline for Hemodialysis Adequacy allow for reduced frequency HD in the setting of preserved RKF as long as the target weekly standard Kt/V, which includes contributions of ultrafiltration and RKF is ≥ 2.1 volumes/wk.13 The 2019 UK Renal Association Clinical Practice Guidelines on Hemodialysis suggest (grade 2D) that lower HD dose targets may be optimal in patients with significant RKF as long as this is monitored regularly and when QOL and not longevity is the primary treatment goal.15

Thus, though there is gaining institutional acceptability of reduced HD prescriptions in certain scenarios, there is limited data to support its practice. In a systematic review of observational studies comparing twice versus thrice weekly HD, results were highly variable with no clear signal for overwhelming risk or benefit with the exception of RKF, which consistently showed a slower decline in twice-weekly groups.11 No study included QOL measures. A recent incremental HD pilot study in the United States randomized 48 adult patients incident HD with RKF to an incremental HD start (twice weekly for 6 weeks followed by thrice weekly) versus standard thrice weekly and also assessed feasibility. Similar to the current study, they report adequate recruitment and adherence.9 A 6-month incremental RCT of 55 mostly prevalent HD adults with RKF was conducted in the UK.8 HD prescriptions were altered to achieve minimum Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes Kt/V targets. End points were of feasibility, RKF, and frequency of dialysis-related complications with QOL a secondary end point. They had greater difficulty recruiting than the current study and a high drop-out rate in the thrice-weekly arm. There were no safety signals or any differences in QOL measures or RKF preservation. An 8-week pilot cross-over equivalence trial in 26 prevalent HD adults with RKF in the United States compared twice versus thrice weekly HD with QOL, RKF, and HD parameter outcomes.10 HD prescriptions were altered to achieve minimum Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes Kt/V targets with the twice-weekly arm requiring slightly longer per treatment times. There was no difference in QOL or the other outcome measures between the arms except for interdialytic weight gain.

It should be noted that this was not a traditional incremental HD study with a minimum RKF for inclusion and reflex dose escalation as this declines. HD dose escalation was determined by key clinical events rather than loss of RKF. Despite this lack of specific attention to RKF, only 1 patient was withdrawn from the twice-weekly arm by their treating nephrologist because of inadequate clearance confirming the reasonableness of our approach.

The strengths of the study include the simplicity and pragmatism of the study protocol, which is aligned with usual care in well-resourced settings where it could be easily and safely implemented at low cost. This study is unique in its focus on the elderly, an expanding cohort of patients on HD with reduced life expectancy in whom there exists a deficit of data supporting conventional approaches to HD prescription. Finally, the 12-week “settling-in” period before randomization permitted the optimization of clinical status and avoided the enrollment of individuals not destined to continue in-center HD “long” term. Therefore, the study design is streamlined and fiscally responsible.

As with all single-center studies, there is a potential for limited generalization of feasibility and safety results to other sites with different populations, resources, and models of HD care delivery. By design, the study only included patients on HD who survived the first 3 months of HD, were compliant with HD and deemed stable. The results therefore would not be generalizable to all elderly patients on incident HD (survivorship bias). The study does not address dialysis prescription in the first 3 months post–HD initiation. The study is too small to assess treatment effect on QOL or clinical outcomes but does provide a first look at the outcome distributions for the purpose of planning a definitive trial.

In summary, this small feasibility pilot RCT is the first to compare twice versus thrice weekly HD in elderly patients on incident HD. The results suggest that it is feasible to conduct a larger multicenter trial although attention will need to be paid to optimizing recruitment rates. The results provide reassurance of the safety of twice weekly HD in the target population. A larger and longer definitive multicenter study is required to formally compare QOL and clinical outcomes between twice versus thrice weekly HD in elderly patients on incident HD.
