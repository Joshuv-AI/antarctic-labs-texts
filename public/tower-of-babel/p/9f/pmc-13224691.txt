Replication and compartmentalization of Dobrava-Belgrade virus in the central nervous system
Authors: Julia Melchert, Julia Herzig-Nichtweiss, Jenny Meinhardt, Farid Salih, Till Dominik Best, Marie Luisa Schmidt, Lisa Jasmin Müller, Talitha Veith, Jörn Beheim-Schwarzbach, Jörg Hofmann, Terry C. Jones, Eberhard Siebert, Felix Schoenrath, Helena Radbruch, Christian Drosten, Victor Max Corman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA heart-transplant recipient developed fatal neurologic disease. High-throughput sequencing (HTS) identified Dobrava – Belgrade virus in central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral specimens. Genomic analyzes showed distinct viral populations across compartments, suggesting CNS tropism. This case highlights atypical infection presentations in immunosuppressed patients and underscores the diagnostic value of HTS.

Orthohantaviruses are rodent-borne viruses and are among the most common zoonoses in Europe. Transmission to humans occurs mainly through inhalation of aerosolized excreta from infected rodents [1]. In Europe and Asia, orthohantaviruses cause hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS), whereas strains in the Americas cause hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS) [2]. Acute infection typically presents with influenza-like illness, including fever, flank pain, and arthralgias. In severe cases, pleuritic pain, myocardial or pericardial involvement, and oliguria with renal dysfunction may occur [3]. In Europe, human-pathogenic strains include Puumala virus (PUUV), Dobrava-Belgrade virus (DOBV), Tula virus, and Seoul virus (SEOV). Their geographic distribution mirrors the ranges of their rodent hosts, including the bank vole (PUUV) and yellow-necked mouse (DOBV). In northeastern Germany, most infections are caused by DOBV [4–6]. Neurologic manifestations are rare but have been described in PUUV and SEOV infections, mainly in immunocompromised patients. For DOBV, only one patient with severe neurologic symptoms has been reported to date [7–13].

Here, we report persistent CNS infection with DOBV in a patient who was six months post-heart transplantation. High-throughput sequencing of longitudinal samples showed compartmentalization, consistent with viral replication within the CNS.

Results

Six months after heart transplantation, a man in his sixties presented with weakness, dyspnea, dizziness, and exertional heart failure. Echocardiography, coronary angiography, and endomyocardial biopsy showed no evidence of allograft rejection or an alternative cause. During hospitalization, neurologic symptoms developed, beginning with right-arm monoparesis on hospital day 4. Over the next several days, weakness progressed to the left arm and neck, culminating in flaccid tetraplegia. On hospital day 10, the patient was intubated for respiratory failure and transferred to our hospital.

The leading diagnostic considerations included atypical Guillain – Barré syndrome and Miller – Fisher syndrome (acute motor axonal neuropathy). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed mild pleocytosis and moderately elevated lactate and protein levels. Despite intensified immunomodulatory therapy, including intravenous immune globulin and plasmapheresis, the patient deteriorated and developed signs of central nervous system (CNS) involvement. In the final stage, severe encephalopathy progressed to unresponsive coma with a very low-voltage electroencephalogram. The patient died 11 weeks after admission.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed five days after admission to our clinic showed acute polyradiculoneuritis of the spinal motor roots, most prominent at the cauda equina, with involvement along the spinal axis and intracranially, including the oculomotor nerve, with avid contrast enhancement (Figure 1). Findings remained stable over four weeks. Initial brain MRI was otherwise normal except for restricted diffusion consistent with cytotoxic edema in the juxtacortical central white matter, visible only on diffusion-weighted imaging. Although diffusion restriction had resolved on MRI 18 days later, subtle T2-FLAIR hyperintensities emerged in the hemispheric white matter, basal ganglia, thalami, and brain stem and increased markedly two weeks later, when diffusion restriction reappeared in the basal ganglia and thalami (Figure 1). Extensive pathogen and autoimmune diagnostics remained negative, including testing for Cytomegalovirus, Enterovirus, Herpes simplex virus 1&2, JC polyomavirus, Human Herpesvirus 6, Human Parechovirus, Varicella-zoster virus, Escherichia coli K1, Haemophilus influenzae, Listeria monocytogenes, Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus agalactiae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Cryptococcus neoformans/gattii.

Nine weeks after hospitalization, a brain biopsy of the frontal right cortex and meninges was performed. Histopathologic examination showed dura mater, leptomeninges, cortex, and white matter with evidence of marked astrogliosis; activated microglia in the cortex and white matter; and macrophages in the leptomeninges and brain parenchyma. Only single lymphocytes were found in the tissue (Figure S1). Postmortem autopsy was not performed.

Due to the unusual clinical progression in an immunocompromised patient, archived CSF samples were retrospectively sequenced for unbiased pathogen identification, revealing the presence of DOBV. Subsequently, additional archived samples were tested. No other viruses were detected in CSF or brain samples. However, low levels of Torque-Teno virus were detected in two serum samples and HSV-1 was detected in a bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) sample but not in serum or CSF. A DOBV-specific RT-qPCR confirmed viral RNA in CSF for 36 days and in serum for 46 days. DOBV RNA was also detected in a tracheal secretion, BAL, and a formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) brain biopsy (Ct-value 34.7). The highest viral load (4.6×10^6 copies/ml) was detected in the first available CSF sample, obtained seven days after hospitalization (Figure 2(A)), followed by a decline until day 30. Thereafter, viral load increased again in both CSF (after day 30) and serum (after day 40), and high viral loads were also observed in both respiratory samples collected after day 40. Anti-Hantavirus IgM and IgG antibodies were detected in serum samples with a decreasing signal over time. In the last serum sample, taken 53 days after hospitalization, no IgM antibodies were detected. Notably, neither IgM nor IgG antibodies were measurable in CSF (Figure 2(B)). An additional immunoblot was reactive to DOBV- and Hantaan virus- but not PUUV-specific antibodies (Figure S2).

We obtained complete-genome DOBV sequences from CSF, serum, BAL, and a partial genome from a FFPE brain biopsy (accession numbers PZ293686-PZ293696). Phylogenetic analysis determined the viral strain as genotype Kurkino with closest relationship to rodent-borne sequences from Brandenburg and an human case from Mecklenburg-West Pomerania (Figure S3).

To investigate possible viral replication within the CNS, we performed a compartmentalization analysis. A consensus sequence generated from a serum sample (day after hospitalization (D), D7) was used as reference for comparison. Intra-sample diversity, defined as a frequency of a minor variant >10%, was not detected in the D7 sample. In contrast, in a CSF sample from the same day, two minor variants within the coding region were detected (M segment: A110R resulting in E37E/G, L segment: G3445R resulting in V1449I/V) and four single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the non-coding untranslated regions. In the genome sequence of a CSF sample (D7), serum sample (D25), and BAL sample (D50), seven differences to the reference sequence (serum D7) were detected. Eleven differences were detected in a brain biopsy (D63, Figure S4). Both CSF samples had identical SNPs from the reference sequence in three genome locations. Although a mixed population was detectable at these positions in the first CSF sample (D7), one variant had prevailed at two positions in the subsequent sample (D43). This was also observed for one position in comparison to the second serum sample (D25) and the FFPE brain biopsy (S segment UTR: T145Y > T145C). A mixed population was also observed in the second serum sample (D25), while a SNP was detected at the same position in the second CSF sample (D43) (S segment UTR: A105M > A105C). Notably, SNPs within the S segment were mostly observed in the UTR. A total of 18/38 (47.4%) of the SNPs detected were found in the M segment (Figure S4).

Discussion

We present a case of fatal CNS infection caused by DOBV in an immunocompromised male patient six months after heart transplantation. The patient presented with progressive neurologic symptoms, which led to flaccid tetraplegia and respiratory failure, compatible with atypical Guillain Barré and Miller-Fisher overlap syndrome. Imaging findings were non-specific and suggested several differential diagnoses, including postictal changes, mitochondrial diseases, metabolic-toxic encephalopathies, and viral encephalitis.

Extensive targeted diagnostic testing failed to identify a causative pathogen. Finally, DOBV infection was detected by high-throughput sequencing of CSF samples and was confirmed by specific RT-qPCR. The observed viral kinetics, with an initial decline followed by a secondary increase in DOBV RNA levels in serum and CSF after days 30–40, suggest persistent viral replication due to impaired immunity, with insufficient clearance and possible intermittent release from infected and dying cells. The discordant kinetics between serum and CSF further indicate that viral clearance in different body compartments may be influenced by distinct factors and potentially by different replication dynamics.

In line with this, analysis of consensus sequences from serum and CNS samples revealed distinct viral populations in these compartments. Differences were also detected between the brain biopsy and serum. Together, these findings suggest independent viral replication within the CNS. Hantavirus-specific IgM and IgG antibodies were detected in serum samples taken 22 days after hospitalization, but not in CSF. Unfortunately, no samples from the first week after hospitalization or earlier were available for testing, so the approximate time of infection could not be determined. However, as the patient was hospitalized at the end of December, and human infections caused by DOBV are mainly reported in north-eastern Germany from October to December [4], the infection timing corresponds to the peak DOBV season in our geographic region, and the detection of IgM antibodies suggests a recent infection. As the DOBV infection was diagnosed postmortem, it was not possible to investigate possible exposures in detail. The close relationship between the patient-derived sequences and rodent-borne sequences from the same area indicates a locally acquired infection. DOBV was suspected of causing neurologic symptoms in a previous case, though with no viral RNA detected in CSF [14]. However, related viruses, such as PUUV and SEOV, can cause several neurologic diseases, especially in immunocompromised patients [7–10,12,14,15].

Unfortunately, there are currently no specific therapeutic options for the treatment of orthohantavirus CNS infection.

Conclusions

Three major conclusions can be drawn from this case: First, viral infections can present with atypical clinical symptoms, including involvement of organs not typically affected, particularly in immunosuppressed individuals. Second, in these cases, high-throughput sequencing can serve as a valuable diagnostic tool to identify active infections. Third, DOBV can replicate in the CNS, in addition to its usual target organs such as kidneys, liver, spleen, lungs, and heart. Consequently, DOBV might be included in the differential diagnosis of patients with encephalitis of unknown cause or other neurologic symptoms in endemic areas or in patients with a travel history.
