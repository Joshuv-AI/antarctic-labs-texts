A two-step approach to simultaneously correct for selection and misclassification bias in nonprobability samples from hard-to-reach populations
Authors: Christoffer Dharma, Peter Smith, Travis Salway, Dionne Gesink, Michael Escobar, Victoria Landsman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractResearchers studying hard-to-reach or minority populations are increasingly implementing nonprobability sampling strategies that are often prone to selection bias. To address this problem, existing statistical methods suggest integrating data from external probability sample, often collected by government agencies, with the nonprobability sample from the hard-to-reach population. These methods assume that all information collected in the probability sample is recorded without errors. This may not be the case if participants are unwilling to report their minority status, such as sexual orientation, truthfully in large-scale population-based surveys, leading to misclassification bias. In this paper, we propose a novel two-step approach aimed at addressing misclassification bias in the probability sample to improve the performance of the data integration methods aimed at addressing selection bias in the nonprobability sample. By applying the proposed method to simulated data, we demonstrate a significant reduction in bias and validate the proposed bootstrap variance estimator of the estimated mean (prevalence) under low, moderate, and high misclassification rates. This method is particularly beneficial when the misclassification rate is high. Finally, we illustrate the application of the two-step approach to estimate the prevalence of measures of social connectedness among sexual minority men using a real-world nonprobability sample.

Introduction

Data integration methods are increasingly popular in the modern epidemiologic literature.1,2 These methods can be applied to participants from different studies or the same participants nested within two stages of the same study.3 Data integration methods can be potentially useful in applications where the desired prevalence estimates can be obtained only from the data source prone to selection bias, such as a nonprobability sample. These types of samples are common in social epidemiology when studying health and lifestyle characteristics among minority, stigmatized, or other hard-to-reach groups of interest that are often best reached through a nonprobability sample.4 For these situations, the recently proposed Adjusted Logistic Propensity (ALP) method suggests integrating the nonprobability and population-based probability (reference) samples to model participation probabilities in the nonprobability sample.5 The inverses of these participation probabilities, also called pseudo-weights, are used for bias correction by constructing pseudo-weighted prevalence estimates. We illustrated the application of the ALP method by estimating the prevalence of mental health characteristics among sexual minority men (SMM) in a previous publication.6

The ALP and other proposed methods assume that all the information in the probability sample is recorded without errors. However, this may not be the case for minority and other hard-to-reach groups if their participants are unwilling to disclose accurate information when interviewed in a large-scale population-based survey. For example, SMM may feel uncomfortable disclosing their true sexuality when surveyed by a government agency that collects data for probability samples.7,8 As a result, one or more variables in the probability sample may be measured with errors, which invalidates the application of the ALP and similar methods for correcting the selection bias in the nonprobability sample. In this paper, we describe a novel two-step approach, aimed at addressing the misclassification bias in the probability sample and improving the performance of the ALP method. In other words, the proposed method attempts to correct for both types of biases simultaneously and can be applied to a wide range of real-world problems when correcting the selection bias in the nonprobability sample requires data integration with the probability sample prone to misclassification bias.

Variance estimation

The analytic formula for the variance of the mean (prevalence) estimator obtained using the standard ALP method has been developed in previous work.5 Alternatively, the variance can be estimated using a bootstrap variance estimator.9 This approach is particularly useful in situations where the bootstrap weights are provided with the probability reference sample to reflect the information about multistage complex sampling design of the survey, as was the case in the real data application in this study. In this case, the ALP method is applied B times to the b-th (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $b=\mathrm{1,...,}B$\end{document}) resample of the nonprobability sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${s}_c$\end{document} integrated with the probability sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${s}_p$\end{document} and the corresponding b-th set of bootstrap weights. The variance of the estimated mean (prevalence) of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\mu}_1$\end{document} and the 95% CIs around it are approximated from the bootstrap distribution of B mean (prevalence) estimates. The two approaches to variance estimation have been compared in simulated data and produced very close results (data not shown).

In the case of the variance estimation of the mean (prevalence) estimator obtained using the proposed two-step approach, the probability of misclassification should be estimated from each resample of the nonprobability sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${s}_c$\end{document} and the probability sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${s}_p$\end{document}. This probability is further used to generate B copies of the variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\tilde{A}$\end{document}, which in turn define the B copies of the (updated) reference samples. Then, the bootstrap procedure for the ALP method is applied as described above.

and high misclassification rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\sim 55-\mathrm{60}\%$\end{document}) using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${p}_1=\mathrm{0.5,}{p}_2=\mathrm{0.1}$\end{document}. Of note, the TW estimator and the ALP estimator using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${s}_p\mid A=\mathrm{1}$\end{document} cannot be computed in real-world applications. We report them in simulation study for the purpose of comparison.

Evaluation metrics

Each simulation experiment was repeated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $R=\mathrm{200}$\end{document} times for each scenario and each estimator to obtain the r-th estimated value (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $r=\mathrm{1,...,}R$\end{document}) of the population mean. The R software13 function survey::svyglm14 was used to estimate the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document}, which was further used to estimate the participation probabilities, the pseudo-weights and, finally, the pseudo-weighted estimator for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\mu}_1$\end{document}. The bootstrap variance estimator was obtained from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $B=\mathrm{200}$\end{document} replicates for each simulated set. Codes can be found in the first author’s https://github.com/chrisdharma GitHub page.

To evaluate the performance of the point estimators, we computed the relative bias (RB) and the mean squared error (MSE) for each estimator. Relative bias is the average of the difference between the true value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\mu}_1$\end{document} and the r-th estimated value divided by the true value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\mu}_1$\end{document}. The MSE is the average of the squared difference between the r-th estimate and the true value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\mu}_1$\end{document}. The average is taken over R estimated means.

To evaluate the performance of the bootstrap variance estimator, we computed the variance ratio (VR) and the coverage probability (CP). The VR is the ratio of the average of the estimated variance in each simulation (the “estimated” variance) to the variance of the R mean estimates (the “true” variance). The VR close to 1 indicates satisfactory performance of the boostrap variance estimator. The CP was obtained as the proportion of the simulated sets (out of R) in which the 95% CI contained the true value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\mu}_1$\end{document}.

Illustrative example

We illustrate the application of the proposed two-step approach by estimating prevalence of social connectedness among SMM (also known as MSM—Men who have Sex with Men) using data from the community-based survey Sex Now conducted by the Community-Based Research Center (CBRC) in 2019.15 Sex Now is a nonprobability sample of SMM in Canada with participants recruited from social media sites, dating apps, or existing networks of community agencies serving SMM.16 Similar to our previous work,6 the sample from the Canadian Community Health Survey (CCHS) in years 2015-2018 was used as a probability (reference) sample for estimating the pseudo-weights. The CCHS is a multistage complex survey conducted annually by Statistics Canada. The survey sampling weights and the bootstrap weights that reflect the survey design are included with the sample data. We used the Public Use Microdata File (PUMF) version of the CCHS data, available through the Data Liberation Initiative.17 In this analysis, 77 259 participants from the CCHS and 7489 SMM participants from Sex Now were included.

Measures

To illustrate the application of the methods, we seek to estimate the prevalence of loneliness and the strong connection to other SMM using the data from Sex Now. The following covariates, available in both Sex Now and CCHS, were used to model the participation rate: rurality, age group, quadratic term of age group, income, education, race, employment status, immigration status, as well as the interaction between age group and employment.8,18 These variables potentially determine the participant’s decision to participate in Sex Now and are available in both samples.

The variable sexual orientation reported in the CCHS sample was used to define the reference subsample required for the application of the ALP method (this is the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${A}_p$\end{document} variable as defined above under the assumption \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $A={A}_p$\end{document}). For the application of the two-step method, the variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${A}_p$\end{document} in the Sex Now sample was derived from the participant’s response to the question of how likely they are to reveal their sexual orientation in a Statistics Canada survey. This variable was recorded on a 4-point scale.18 Those who responded with “likely” or “very likely” were assigned \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${A}_p=\mathrm{1}$\end{document} (and the rest were assigned \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${A}_p=\mathrm{0}$\end{document}). Discussion

We proposed a two-step estimation method to address the misclassification bias in the probability sample required to improve the performance of estimators obtained from the nonprobability sample prone to selection bias using data integration methods. We evaluated the proposed approach in a simulation study and received very satisfactory results for scenarios with various misclassification rates under correctly specified models. We applied the new method to estimate the prevalence of two outcome measures of social connectedness among SMM. Our results did not show practically important differences between the unweighted estimates, the estimates obtained using the ALP method, and the estimates obtained using the proposed two-step approach for both outcomes. Assuming all the methodological assumptions were met, the findings may suggest that the impact of selection and misclassification biases for these two outcomes in the data sources used in this application is minimal. Our estimates confirm the estimates from previously published findings.19

We validated the two-step approach under a series of assumptions that may not hold in real-world settings. Investigating the robustness of the proposed method to violations of these assumptions is an important direction of future research. The current method assumed there were no other forms of misclassification bias (eg, no misclassification in the outcome) and that all relevant covariates contributing to selection bias were included in the model. Future studies can employ methods from the quantitative bias analysis literature to correct for additional sources of bias.20 In addition, evaluating the performance of this approach in estimating the outcome-exposure association is of great practical interest. One of the lessons learned from our study is the importance of a meticulous design and data collection in the nonprobability sample. For example, collecting information on how likely participants would report their true sexual orientation in a government survey is crucial to implementing the proposed approach in the real-world application presented in this paper. As well, both the original ALP method and the two-step approach assume that the outcome variables are available only in the nonprobability sample. It is important to extend these methods to situations where outcome variables are collected in both the probability and nonprobability samples to fully utilize the available information.

We showed using simulated data that the two-step approach can be a useful method to correct for selection bias in nonprobability samples when the probability (reference) sample has misclassification bias. The approach performs well across all misclassification scenarios and provides the greatest advantage compared to other estimation methods when there is a high misclassification rate in the probability sample. Our illustrative example does not show practically important differences in estimates after applying this method, suggesting that the biases associated with estimating outcomes with these data sources are minimal. At the same time, other outcomes of interest can be more sensitive to the underlying problem of selection and misclassification biases, in which case the observed differences between the prevalence estimates produced by various estimation methods can be more substantial. It cannot be determined a priori whether the discrepancies between the various estimation methods will or will not be practically important for any particular outcome. Therefore, we encourage researchers to apply the proposed methods and present both the unweighted and pseudo-weighted prevalence estimates using the ALP and the two-step method.
