Environmental constraints and their impact on role conflict and ambiguity among ICU nurses providing oral care: a qualitative study using role theory
Authors: Xingru Li, Wenguang Xie, Chao Zhang, Shilin Liu, Shuyuan Huang, Wei Xiao, Wenhao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnvironmental constraints pose significant challenges to oral care provision by intensive care unit (ICU) nurses. However, the specific impact of these constraints on role conflict and role ambiguity in oral care practice remains underexplored. Such role stress can threaten both patient safety and nurses’ psychological well-being. Therefore, this study aimed to address this gap by examining the role stresses of ICU nurses through the lens of role theory.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study design was used. Using purposive sampling, 16 nurses from three ICUs at two tertiary hospitals in China were recruited for semi-structured interviews. Guided by role theory, data analysis was performed using a directed content analysis approach that combined both deductive and inductive strategies.ResultsThrough the lens of role theory, role ambiguity and conflict emerged as core findings, closely intertwined with role overload and underqualification. Role ambiguity was driven by four interrelated factors: failed preparation, informal alliances, responsibility vacuum, and subjective assessment. Meanwhile, role overload, hierarchical culture, and interpersonal risks collectively contributed to ethical trade-offs and silent complicity. The study further identified nuanced variations in these role stresses influenced by seniority levels and specific types of ICU units.ConclusionThe role ambiguity and conflict experienced by ICU nurses are not individual failures, but systemic products of environmental constraints. Effectively mitigating these role stresses requires a fundamental redesign of the role environment, including the establishment of interprofessional protocols to clarify boundaries, the integration of objective tools to define performance, and the cultivation of a psychologically safe culture to empower voice. These systemic reforms are essential to safeguard both patient safety and the well-being of nurses.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04511-4.Keywords: Critical care, Oral care, Role conflict, Role ambiguity, Nurses, Qualitative research

Introduction

Oral care is an integral component of intensive care unit (ICU) practice, for both ventilated and non-ventilated patients [1]. It not only promotes patient comfort but also serves as a key measure for preventing ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), having been shown to reduce the incidence of VAP from 55.26% to 21.05% [2]. Nurses are recognized as the primary providers and guardians of oral care, playing a crucial role in improving patients’ oral health outcomes [3, 4]. However, within the constrained ICU environment, characterized by limitations in time and resources [5–7], nurses often experience conflict between performing oral care and attending to more urgent medical tasks, resulting in oral care being assigned a lower priority in practice [8, 9]. Furthermore, in settings with limited educational resources, ICU nurses may hesitate between providing oral care for high-risk critically ill patients and ensuring their safety [10]. In a study by Su Wen et al. [7], nurses in the ICU environment faced ethical dilemmas when balancing patient dignity with the requirements of oral care procedures, suggesting the presence of an underlying role conflict. Within role theory, role conflict refers to an individual’s perception of existing role expectations as contradictory and mutually exclusive [11]. This may contribute to emotional exhaustion and reduce job satisfaction among nurses [12].

Nurses’ role ambiguity is another type of role stress, which is defined as a divergence in role expectations associated with a lack of clarity regarding these expectations [13]. In recent years, there has been a growing number of guidelines and best practice recommendations on oral care for critically ill patients [1, 10, 14]. However, the increasing proliferation and frequent updates of these guidelines can lead to unclear or delayed role delineation for nurses, thereby contributing to role ambiguity [15]. Such ambiguity impedes effective task performance and can result in frustration and anxiety among nurses [16].

Currently, research on role ambiguity and conflict among ICU nurses has primarily focused on ethical dilemmas [17] and end-of-life care [18]. Few studies have explored the role ambiguity and conflict experienced by ICU nurses in providing oral care under environmental constraints. Zhang et al. [15] employed the role ambiguity subscale of a role stress instrument to assess role ambiguity among ICU nurses and examine its relationship with anxiety and emotional intelligence. However, the five dimensions of this subscale offer limited insight into the nuanced experiences of role ambiguity among ICU nurses. In contrast, qualitative research is better suited to understanding the nuances of subjective experience and context [19]. Although previous studies have identified environmental barriers to oral care delivery by ICU nurses [5, 8], research explicitly investigating role ambiguity and conflict in this specific context remains insufficient.

Role theory can be defined as an overarching framework comprising three concepts: patterned and characteristic social behavior, the roles or identities assumed by social actors, and the scripts or expectations for behavior that are understood and followed by all individuals [11]. A role refers to the expected patterns or set of behaviors associated with a specific position or status [13]. The nursing role refers to the social behavior patterns that nurses should possess, which are consistent with their profession. These include, but are not limited to, communicator, facilitator of recovery, educator, and counselor [20]. Within this professional context, the definition and attributes of oral care include the use of specified care plans, systematic assessment, brushing, cleaning, and moisturizing the oral cavity [21]. In oral care, nurses serve not only as direct caregivers but also as assessors, collaborators, educators, and patient safety advocates [22–25]. Currently, role theory has been applied to identify the role ambiguity of nursing interns [19] and explore the role conflict of pharmacists in opioid risk communication [26]. Given the above background, this study aims to employ role theory as a conceptual lens to explore the role ambiguity and conflict experienced by ICU nurses in providing oral care for critically ill patients under environmental constraints. It is hoped that this research can provide hospital administrators with in-depth insights to optimize management at the systemic level, ensure the quality of oral care, and ultimately enhance patient safety and nurses’ psychological well-being.

Methods

Study design

A theory-based qualitative descriptive design was adopted [27–29], which is particularly suitable for obtaining rich first-hand descriptions of the phenomenon, especially when existing knowledge on the topic is limited. A qualitative descriptive design was selected for its flexible approach, which captures ICU nurses’ factual narratives regarding their perceived role in oral care for critically ill patients by closely aligning with their own language, without seeking to provide an in-depth interpretation of the phenomenon’s essence [30]. When researchers explore the application of a specific theoretical framework in a particular context (such as ICU oral care), a theory-based qualitative descriptive design is particularly suitable [27]. The qualitative descriptive approach can complement the use of role theory by allowing the direct mapping of participants’ descriptive narratives onto the dimensions of the theory, while remaining open to themes outside the theoretical framework [30]. The study reporting adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [31].

Setting and participants

This study was conducted in three ICUs, namely, the General ICU, Surgical ICU, and Medicine ICU, of two tertiary hospitals in Anhui Province, China. These three ICUs were selected because they admit patients with diverse specialties and have high demands for oral care, thereby providing multifaceted perspectives for the study. In the ICUs of the studied hospital, only nurses who hold at least a Bachelor of Nursing degree, have passed the national nurse licensure examination, and completed the hospital’s training program, along with an ICU rotation, are eligible to provide care for ICU patients. All the ICUs employ a primary nursing system, where each designated nurse is responsible for all nursing tasks, including oral care, to their assigned patients. The ICU nurse-to-patient ratio was calculated by dividing the total number of nurses by the total number of patients in the unit. Nursing managers reported the total number of nurses, while the total number of patients was determined as the average count of all patients in the ICU unit during their most recent shift, as reported by each nurse in a monthly survey [32]. In this study, the General ICU had 29 beds staffed by 42 nurses (ratio: 1:1.45); the Surgical ICU had 22 beds staffed by 30 nurses (ratio: 1:1.36); and the Medical ICU had 19 beds staffed by 24 nurses (ratio: 1:1.26). However, the Circular of the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China on the Action for Promoting High-quality Development of Public Hospitals (2021–2025) recommends that the nurse-to-bed ratio in ICU units should be at least 1:(2.5-3.0) [33]. In these three ICUs, nurses worked an average of 40 h per week. During night shifts, each primary nurse was responsible for caring for 3 to 4 patients. Under these circumstances, respiratory therapists were also involved in night care. It should be noted that in other countries, respiratory therapists are formally trained healthcare professionals educated in pulmonary medicine [34]. However, in China, nurses often assume the role of respiratory therapists after completing short-term training programs.

Oral care is seldom offered as an independent course in Chinese nursing education but is instead integrated into fundamental nursing curricula. In the studied hospital, standardized training in oral care is only available for general patients, with no written protocols or specialized training programs targeting critically ill patients. Competency in oral care for critically ill patients is typically acquired during nursing internships or clinical rotations, and the time dedicated to such practice largely depends on the type and number of patients assigned. Theoretical instruction is primarily provided by nursing faculty in academic institutions, while clinical training is delivered by clinical nurse preceptors, who typically hold the qualification of a supervising nurse. In the ICU units of the studied hospital, oral care kits are available; however, their composition varies across different units. Common oral care supplies in the ICU include tongue depressors, forceps, suction swabs, cotton balls, chlorhexidine solution, and suction toothbrushes. Nevertheless, the use of these supplies, particularly suction toothbrushes for intubated patients, is inconsistent and often depends on the nurses’ preferences or the units’ inventory.

All nurses with more than one year of ICU experience who had directly participated in the oral care of critically ill patients were considered eligible participants. The study excluded nursing interns, assistants, and visiting trainees. The purposive sampling was employed to obtain information-rich cases. Participants varied in terms of work experience, professional title, and the type of ICU. All participants had a bachelor’s degree, which was determined by the intensive care staffing policies of the studied hospitals. In these three ICUs, nurses with postgraduate degrees were primarily engaged in nursing research rather than clinical practice; therefore, they were not included in this study.

Data collection

Before the formal interviews, the research team developed an interview guide based on a literature review, clinical experience, and role theory (see Supplementary Material 1). Pilot interviews help optimize interview outlines and upgrade interviewer skills [35–37]. In this study, the guide was adjusted after pilot interviews with three ICU nurses to include subsequent prompts for clarification, such as “what were your thoughts at that time?” and “could you provide an example?” (Data from pilot interviews were not analyzed).

The first author initially communicated with the head nurses of three ICUs. With their assistance, shift schedules and basic information of ICU nurses were obtained. Based on this information and the inclusion criteria, three potential participants (P1, P2, P6) from different ICUs were preliminarily selected. During the off-duty time of these potential participants, they were contacted to schedule a telephone interview. The researchers orally explained the study’s overview and obtained participants’ consent. Interview timetables were arranged in agreement with participants, and semi-structured interviews were conducted in a quiet, undisturbed room. Each interview lasted 30–50 min (mostly around 35 min). All interviews were audio-recorded with the participants’ permission. The interviews were led by the first author (LXR), a female PhD nursing student trained in qualitative research with extensive experience in qualitative data collection. A supervisor was appointed in this study and was assigned to another researcher (XWG). XWG did not participate in asking interview questions. He was primarily responsible for monitoring potential leading questions used by the interviewer and documenting participants’ non-verbal cues, such as tone of voice and pauses. These records enhanced the transparency of the study and the accuracy of the interview data [38–40].

After the first three interviews, the same two researchers (LXR and XWG) conducted a preliminary analysis of the data. Based on emerging concepts and questions, subsequent participants were purposively selected to provide new information or different perspectives. Data collection and analysis then proceeded concurrently until informational saturation was reached, which was defined as the point at which three consecutive interviews yielded no new themes. Saturation was achieved after the 16th participant, at which point recruitment ceased.

Data analysis

Participants’ basic information was anonymized. The recordings were transcribed verbatim within 24 h by a professional transcription service. This study employed a directed content analysis approach, combining deductive and inductive techniques [41]. The directed content analysis approach, also referred to as deductive category development, is commonly employed to validate or extend an existing conceptual framework or theory. It is particularly suitable for research where prior theories or literature exist regarding the phenomenon under investigation [41]. Based on role theory [11, 13], “role ambiguity” (i.e., a divergence in role expectations associated with a lack of clarity regarding these expectations) and “role conflict” (i.e., an individual’s perception of existing role expectations as contradictory and mutually exclusive) were pre-defined as initial coding categories. However, to remain open to themes beyond role theory, inductive coding was applied to data that did not fit the initial coding categories, thereby ensuring that emerging themes were grounded in participants’ narratives [41]. The integration of deductive and inductive approaches allows the research findings to be both theoretically grounded and rooted in the language of ICU nurses, thereby enhancing the practical applicability of the results [30]. Reflexivity and rigor

The research team had no direct conflicts of interest with the participants. All interviews, data collection, and analysis were completed by LXR and XWG. However, the leading interviewer (LXR) had previously worked at one of the hospitals. Some participants were aware that the interviewer was a former employee of the hospital. This relationship helped build trust between the interviewer and the participants, but it could also have led participants to be more reserved when discussing sensitive topics, such as mentioning improper practices by colleagues. To address this, the primary interviewer explicitly stated that the interviews were solely for academic purposes and were strictly confidential, so as to encourage candid responses. To mitigate the impact of personal bias on data interpretation, a supervisor (XWG) was involved in monitoring the interview process. Data analysis was conducted through independent coding by two researchers, followed by regular team discussions. The team acknowledges that the role theory framework may have influenced data interpretation. Therefore, inductive analysis was used to capture emergent themes, and regular team discussions were held to reflect on personal biases, striving to enhance the study’s objectivity.

This study adhered to Lincoln and Guba’s standards [42]. Credibility was ensured by using participants’ original quotes to support the findings, along with regular team discussions to validate the rationality of coding and themes. Dependability was achieved by providing a detailed description of the research methodology and maintaining comprehensive records of data collection and analysis. Confirmability was ensured through dual independent coding, maintaining analytical logs, regular team discussions, and the application of role theory. Transferability was achieved by verbatim transcription of interview records, a comprehensive introduction to the research context, the recruitment process, and participant characteristics.

Ethical considerations

This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any negative consequences. All participants provided electronic written informed consent. The identity of the participants is anonymous at any stage. Only research teams have access to data in password-protected computers. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Approval number: [2025] No. 189, Date: September 9, 2025).

Role ambiguity: operating in a gray zone

Within this theme, when providing oral care, ICU nurses encounter systemic ambiguities in skill training, interdisciplinary collaboration, role boundaries, and quality evaluation. This ambiguity places them in a gray zone characterized by a lack of clear guidance, which is filled with uncertainty and necessitates informal decision-making.

Failed preparation: inadequate role readiness due to ambiguous training

Nurses reported that their pre-service education on oral care primarily focused on general patients, with insufficient training for high-risk critically ill patients, such as those with impaired consciousness or endotracheal intubation. One nurse highlighted the disconnect between academic training and clinical reality:

During training, we practiced on mannequins. But clinical settings are never that straightforward? We have to assess the actual state of the patient’s oral cavity. (P3, ICU)

This training gap left them feeling underprepared and less confident when confronting complex clinical situations, such as bite block dislodgment, trismus, oral mucosal pressure injuries, or bleeding. Moreover, the unit culture prioritized vital sign monitoring and airway management over oral care, leaving practice largely reliant on experiential knowledge passed down by senior nurses.

If the patient has an oropharyngeal airway, you have to maneuver it during insertion or removal. Sometimes I worry the patient might suddenly regain consciousness and bite down or push the tube out with their tongue. (P12, ICU)

There are no specific oral care guidelines for the ICU in our unit. We rarely receive formal training and have no procedural protocol to follow. It’s essentially knowledge passed down from one generation of nurses to the next. (P13, SICU)

Although the experience of senior nurses was invaluable, the absence of evidence-based standardization exacerbated nurses’ safety anxieties when dealing with complex oral care challenges.

Informal alliances: interdisciplinary collaboration triggered by risk perception

Collaboration was often initiated when nurses were unable to manage risks independently. The selection of collaborators was based on various factors, such as shift schedules, personal relationships, individual preferences, and the availability of others. One nurse mentioned that female colleagues were perceived as more attentive, and collaborating with them could improve the quality of oral care. Although collaborating with respiratory therapists and physicians enhanced their sense of security in practice, some nurses reported that non-overlapping shifts with respiratory therapists made colleagues within their own discipline the only available collaborators. Most nurses expressed that doctors were more concerned about VAP or extubation risks than oral care. When instructions from respiratory therapists and physicians were different, nurses reported prioritizing the advice of the more experienced practitioner.

In fact, oral care will basically be concentrated at noon, when respiratory therapists are not available. If you really need help, you will first call the nurses in the group for help. (P10, MICU)

Since all the respiratory therapists in our department are male, and men might not be as meticulous in this aspect, I prefer to collaborate with females. For basic care, women tend to be more careful than men. (P11, MICU)

When opinions differ… if the physician is a department head and is knowledgeable in this area, we would consider following the doctor’s advice. If it’s a physician who has only been practicing for a few years… we are more likely to follow the respiratory therapist’s guidance. (P7, MICU)

The phenomenon of non-standardized collaboration in oral care, characterized by variable collaborators and a seniority-based decision-making model, presents a critical challenge. It offers operational flexibility but simultaneously increases the instability of patient safety and care quality.

Responsibility vacuum: uncertainty between nurses and respiratory therapists

The unstable cooperation system potentially exacerbates the ambiguity of responsibilities. Participants generally reported a lack of written guidelines to clearly delineate responsibilities between nurses and respiratory therapists regarding oral care, particularly for intubated patients. Task allocation often depended not on professional roles but rather on situational factors, such as “who was available” or “who noticed the need first.”

Both respiratory therapists and nurses perform it; for example, if a patient has sputum, whoever notices it will do it. (P1, MICU)

One associate chief nurse recalled the temporal characteristics of respiratory therapist responsibilities. During periods of particularly strained staffing, such as the winter peak or night shifts, respiratory therapists were sometimes requested to assist with the oral care of intubated patients, constituting a strategy to compensate for nursing shortages.

Usually, during winter, when the unit is busy and patient load is high, especially since intubated patients are at higher risk… respiratory therapists were asked to perform oral care for intubated patients. (P9, MICU)

Subjective assessment: being caught in the ambiguity of oral care quality

Ambiguity of training, cooperation, and responsibility potentially threatens the quality of oral care. In addition, nurses primarily rely on subjective “visual or olfactory judgment” to evaluate oral care quality, such as checking for sputum crusts, food debris, or odor, due to the lack of standardized assessment tools. This subjectivity reduces quality assessment to an “impression score.” In this case, nurses judge the quality of oral care differently. Some nurses considered the absence of sputum crusts as indicative of adequate care, while others emphasized proper and secure fixation of the endotracheal tube.

The standard is to check visually for any residue or stains… (P5, MICU)

The quality of care should be judged by whether the tube is securely and properly fixed. (P15, MICU)

Some experienced nurses deeply understood that without objective indicators, it is impossible to establish a definitive link between the occurrence of VAP and the quality of oral care, thereby hindering effective quality improvement. Another supervisor nurse expressed a desire for objective tools:

We need a method, after performing oral care, we want to evaluate the outcome, using certain chemical indicators. If the oral care quality is confirmed to be high, but patients still develop VAP, we can look for other causes. (P14, SICU)

However, such quality improvement initiatives often stall against systemic barriers. The same nurses revealed that although she had proposed introducing objective tools like test strips to nurse leaders, the suggestion was ultimately dropped due to budget constraints and complex procurement processes.

Other hospitals have standardized assessment tools, like test strips, but our hospital doesn’t use them yet. I raised this with leadership, but our hospital requires a formal bidding process, which is quite complicated. The matter was dropped. (P14, SICU)

It is noteworthy that the call for objective assessment tools came primarily from senior nurses, which may reveal that senior nurses are more aware of the importance of objectively evaluating the quality of oral care.

Role conflict: navigating through conflict

Against the backdrop of role ambiguity, nurses in their daily practice face a multitude of conflicting expectations and demands. This forces them to engage in a constant process of “navigating” to make adaptive decisions, which are sometimes filled with ethical strain.

Ethical trade-offs: tensions among task overload, care rationing, and ethical codes

Due to role ambiguity and a limited number of respiratory therapists, nurses are often required to perform tasks beyond their typical scope of practice, such as monitoring endotracheal tube depth and measuring cuff pressure, which are ideally the responsibilities of respiratory therapists. The challenge of working at the limits of their capacity forces nurses to delay oral care. A nurse expressed her helplessness in this situation:

Oral care involves using a manometer to measure and inflate the cuff, then performing oral brushing with a toothbrush, followed by connecting to negative pressure suction for sputum removal. (P5, MICU)

A person’s energy is finite… You cannot balance both roles well. For instance, when there are too many patients, nursing tasks end up being delayed. There’s no way around it. (P10, MICU)

This phenomenon is more prevalent in the MICU. Since patients’ conditions are generally less critical compared to other ICUs, the demand for dedicated respiratory therapists is lower, resulting in nurses assuming dual roles.

The delay, simplification, or omission of oral care during busy shifts (especially night shifts) has become an unspoken norm within the unit. Nurses adaptively adjust the frequency of oral care based on workload, often prioritizing patients who are scheduled for external examinations or likely to interact with family members, to avoid potential questioning.

Compliance is low during the night shift. I remember it was once required, but eventually, everyone stopped doing it… (P12, ICU)

If it’s too busy, I will prioritize… If I notice a patient has excessive oral secretions or a soiled oral fixation device and is about to go for an external examination, I will do their care first. After all, they will be outside where families are present… (P8, SICU)

However, these reality-driven compromises have triggered ethical dilemmas. Some described a sense of helplessness in having to resort to self-consolation, while others engaged in compensatory actions afterward to balance the feelings of guilt and sense of professional integrity, as expressed by one nurse:

If oral care is forgotten, I feel guilty. I’ll make up for it later. It’s part of my job; skipping it makes the care feel incomplete. (P12, ICU)

This theme also reflects dimensions of role overload in role theory [11], encompassing multiple interconnected paradoxes. The conflicts between task implementation and care rationing, as well as between care rationing and ethical principles, collectively place nurses in a double bind.

Silent complicity: selective silence within hierarchical culture and interpersonal risk

Although nurses universally acknowledged the importance of oral care and derived professional fulfillment from it, most participants reported choosing to remain “silent” and “tolerant” when confronted with the perfunctory practices of colleagues, particularly senior nurses.

You really feel that the patient is cleaned, especially in the tracheal intubation patients, their mouth is dirty, and it’s obvious to see their mouth change from white to red (P12, ICU)

It depends on seniority. If she is a junior, I might remind her to perform oral care carefully. If she is a senior, I likely wouldn’t say anything. I don’t feel qualified to instruct them. (P10, MICU)

This silence was also addressed to the role ambiguity with respiratory therapists. Faced with oral care tasks that belong to respiratory therapists, nurses are caught in a dilemma due to the lack of explicit regulations: proactively performing these tasks raises the feeling of unfairness and concerns about potential liability for adverse outcomes, while asserting their stance may damage team harmony. Ultimately, nurses still shoulder the additional workload and potential risks silently.

They (respiratory therapists) are lazy, but I can’t force them to do it. If I end up doing it myself, I feel… (P13, SICU)

Respiratory therapists should be doing this, but they don’t. I wonder if I should just do the oral care myself when it’s due, but I’m also afraid of being held responsible if something goes wrong… (P13, SICU)

There are no written rules. Pushing too hard would offend people. But since some respiratory therapists really don’t do it, it falls back on us nurses. (P16, SICU)

Senior nurses and primary nurses may have different motivations for silence. For junior nurses, silence often originated from deference to authority and insecurity about their own standing. In contrast, silence among senior nurses was more frequently associated with a desire to avoid team conflict.

Under this theme, the perceived high value of the nursing role contrasts sharply with their tacit acceptance of perfunctory practices. This tension, along with the implicit trade-offs nurses make between task completion and accountability risks, reflects their inner role conflict.

Discussion

Through the lens of role theory, this study reveals that the role stress experienced by nurses when providing oral care for critically ill patients is fundamentally a systemic “failure in role construction” under environmental constraints, rather than an issue of individual skill or attitude. Our findings indicate that limitations in resources, culture, and institutional structures collectively shape two core and interrelated forms of role stress: role ambiguity and role conflict, which are intertwined with dimensions of role overload and role underqualification as defined by role theory. This qualitative study deepens the existing understanding of barriers to oral care [5, 8] and provides insights into how the role of ICU nurses in oral care is shaped by the systemic environment.

This study found that role ambiguity permeates multiple aspects of ICU nurses’ oral care practice, stemming fundamentally from a lack of systemic guidance. First, the lack of context-specific oral care training undermines nurses’ confidence and leads to informal collaboration, which reflects role underqualification within role theory [11]. The informal selection of collaborators and the tendency to follow the advice of experienced individuals reflect cognitive biases and the impact of hierarchical structures on nurses’ psychological safety, which have been identified as barriers to interdisciplinary collaboration [34]. An interesting finding in this study is that participants tend to rely on gender stereotypes to ensure safety in the context of role ambiguity. This may be related to the cultural perception of nursing as a female-dominated profession, in which women are often considered to possess greater empathy and cooperativeness [43]. Secondly, quality assessment relies on subjective “visual or olfactory judgments,” reflecting what role theory terms “role performance ambiguity” [11]. Although experienced nurses have called for the use of objective assessment tools, systemic barriers such as budget constraints and procurement processes hinder their implementation. This indicates that ambiguity in quality assessment does not arise from nurses’ unwillingness to evaluate, but rather because the system fails to provide the necessary “metrics” for evaluation.

An emerging issue was identified as an unclear delineation of duties between nurses and respiratory therapists, primarily due to the absence of written protocols. Task allocation driven by situational demands, while temporarily mitigating understaffing, has induced emotional distress among nurses, particularly the feeling of unfairness. These findings align with a prior study indicating that role ambiguity increases stress and reduces job satisfaction [15]. It should be noted that respiratory therapists in other countries are formally trained healthcare professionals educated in pulmonary medicine [34]. However, in China, nurses often assume the role of respiratory therapists after completing short-term training programs. This type of training scheme may potentially exacerbate role ambiguity. Existing literature highlights the value of emotional intelligence training to help ICU nurses cope with role ambiguity [15, 44]. While such individual-level interventions are valuable, they are insufficient to address this structurally rooted issue. Hospital managers should prioritize written protocols, co-developed by nursing and respiratory therapy, to define roles and ensure accountability, thereby mitigating the psychological impact of role ambiguity [15].

Within the context of role ambiguity (e.g., unclear division of responsibilities between nurses and respiratory therapists), nurses are compelled to assume additional care tasks (i.e., role overload). Under such overload conditions, nurses are forced to engage in care rationing (i.e., nurses are unable to uphold oral care activities due to time and resource limitations [45]). This rationing behaviour further conflicts with the internal professional ethical standards of nurses, consequently triggering feelings of guilt and compensatory actions. Moreover, the role overload resulting from the unclear delineation of duties between nurses and respiratory therapists can evoke emotional distress among nurses, such as a sense of unfairness, which further exacerbates interprofessional conflicts. Therefore, in this study, role overload is understood as a mediator linking role ambiguity and conflict, rather than as an independent theme. A key distinction of our findings, however, is that the guilt is not merely a personal emotion but rather a reflection of systemic failures on individual psychology. Although ethical reflection sessions can provide valuable support [46], the fundamental solution lies in clarifying roles and optimising resource allocation to reduce scenarios that provoke such ethical conflicts.

Notably, this study corroborates the findings of Wells [18] regarding the heightened susceptibility of medical ICUs to role ambiguity and conflict, while also providing a novel explanation for this phenomenon. Within the medical ICU, the reduced requirement for dedicated respiratory therapists led to nurses frequently undertaking dual responsibilities, which intensified their role overload and internal conflict. This observation underscores the necessity for refining staffing models and clarifying professional boundaries according to the particular unit’s demands.

Silent complicity is the result of role adaptation by nurses when confronting conflicting expectations, namely, those arising from their professional clinical role versus their social relational role within the team. Organizational culture provides the broader context for the formation of silent behavior. However, attributing silent behavior solely to organizational culture may overlook structural issues and fail to reflect the dynamic psychological motivations of individuals under systemic pressures. Furthermore, our study further reveals a nuanced difference in the motivations behind it: junior nurses primarily remain silent out of deference to authority, whereas their senior counterparts are more motivated by a desire to maintain team harmony. This subtle distinction enriches the understanding of “silence culture” within an international context [47], and indicates that, within the Chinese socio-cultural setting which emphasizes interpersonal harmony [48], strategies to break this silence must be more finely tailored. This silence not only jeopardizes patient safety but can also conceal systemic issues and thus hinder open communication within the team [49]. Therefore, it is essential to foster a psychologically safe environment through structured communication tools (e.g., SBAR) [50] and non-hierarchical debriefing sessions, along with establishing explicit speak-up policies to promote open communication.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations. First, as it was conducted within two tertiary hospitals in China, the transferability of the findings to ICU settings with different cultural and resource contexts may be limited. Second, the relatively small sample size and the educational homogeneity of the participants, all of whom were nurses with bachelor’s degrees, may limit the transferability of the findings. Additionally, the use of telephone interviews prevented the researchers from observing participants’ facial expressions and body language. Finally, the study captured only the perspectives of nurses. Including other stakeholders in future research would provide a more comprehensive understanding of role stress in ICU oral care.

Despite these limitations, to our knowledge, this is the first qualitative study to employ role theory in exploring how environmental constraints contribute to role ambiguity and conflict among critical care nurses providing oral care. These findings offer hospital administrators strategic insights for implementing targeted reforms to improve patient care quality, nurse well-being, and job satisfaction.

Conclusion

The role ambiguity and conflict experienced by ICU nurses during oral care fundamentally stem from systemic design flaws within environmental constraints. Through the lens of role theory, these challenges can be reframed as systemic failures in role preparation, role boundaries, role performance, and role expectations. Consequently, effective interventions should not aim to rectify nurses but must instead commit to redesigning their role environment. This involves clarifying boundaries through interprofessional protocols, defining performance via objective tools, and empowering roles by fostering a culture of safety. Only through such systemic reforms can we safeguard both patient safety and the psychological well-being of ICU nurses.

Clinical implication

Mitigating role stress among ICU nurses cannot rely solely on individual-level interventions such as emotional intelligence training or moral encouragement; it necessitates a systematic role redesign. First, hospital administrators should lead the collaborative development of interprofessional oral care protocols to clearly delineate responsibilities between nurses and respiratory therapists, thereby eliminating the root cause of role ambiguity at the institutional level. Targeted training should integrate role-playing simulations for high-risk scenarios with education on cognitive biases. Second, investment in objective and accessible oral assessment tools (e.g., Oral PH test strips) is essential. Integrating these into routine quality control will shift evaluation from subjective impressions to objective data, thereby ending performance ambiguity. Finally, by implementing structured communication tools and non-hierarchical reporting systems, efforts should focus on cultivating a psychologically safe culture that encourages nurses at all levels to speak up regarding patient safety, thus breaking the cycle of selective silence.
