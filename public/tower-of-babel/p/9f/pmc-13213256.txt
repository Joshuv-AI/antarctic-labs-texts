Clinical and Molecular Genetic Risk Factors for Severe Familial Hypercholesterolemia
Authors: Eun Hoo Rho, Yura Kang, Minjae Yoon, Nak-Hoon Son, Sung Hee Choi, Sang-Hak Lee, Kyong Soo Park, Soo Heon Kwak, on behalf of the Korean Familial Hypercholesterolemia (KFH) Registry Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFamilial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is an autosomal dominant disorder associated with a markedly elevated, yet heterogeneous, risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). To improve identification of highest-risk individuals, the International Atherosclerosis Society (IAS) introduced the concept of “severe FH.” This study investigated clinical and molecular genetic risk factors associated with severe FH in Korean patients.MethodsPatients enrolled in the Korean FH Registry were analyzed. Severe FH was defined according to IAS criteria, including the presence of ASCVD, subclinical atherosclerosis detected by imaging, or markedly elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels together with cardiovascular risk factors. FH-associated genes were analyzed using whole-exome sequencing or targeted exome sequencing. Clinical and genetic characteristics were compared between patients with severe and non-severe FH.ResultsAmong 296 patients, 172 (58%) had severe FH. Compared with patients with non-severe FH, those with severe FH were older, more frequently male, and had higher prevalence of smoking, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus. Triglyceride levels, lipoprotein(a), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein concentrations were also significantly higher in the severe FH group. Although the frequency of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants was numerically higher in severe FH patients (38.3% vs. 30.7%), this difference did not reach statistical significance, likely due to limited statistical power (p=0.170).ConclusionKorean patients with severe FH exhibited distinct clinical characteristics, including older age, male predominance, and a greater burden of cardiometabolic risk factors. However, the presence and type of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in FH-associated genes did not differ significantly between severe and non-severe FH groups.Keywords: Hyperlipoproteinemia type II; Mutation; Asia, Eastern; Coronary artery disease

INTRODUCTION

Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is one of the most common autosomal dominant disorders, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 1 in 500 individuals.1 However, more recent studies have suggested that the true prevalence may be substantially higher, ranging from 1 in 229 to 1 in 350.2,3,4 FH is caused by pathogenic variants in genes involved in low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) metabolism. The most frequently implicated genes include the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), apolipoprotein B (APOB), and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9). To date, more than 1,600 distinct variants have been identified, with LDLR variants accounting for approximately 85%–90% of all FH cases.5,6 A study by Han et al.7 reported a similar genetic distribution in the Korean population, with APOB and PCSK9 variants each accounting for approximately 5%–10% of cases.

FH patients typically exhibit markedly elevated LDL-C levels compared with the general population.8,9 Exposure to elevated LDL-C begins early in life and persists over a prolonged duration, substantially increasing lifetime risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD).10,11,12,13,14 Individuals with FH have up to a 16-fold higher risk of ASCVD than the general population,15 and the incidence of ASCVD is nearly twice that observed in control individuals with normal lipid levels (14.9 vs. 7.1 per 1,000 person-years).16 Despite this markedly increased ASCVD risk, considerable heterogeneity exists among patients with FH. LDL-C concentrations vary substantially across affected individuals,8,9,17 suggesting that ASCVD risk may also differ in magnitude. In addition, concomitant cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension (HTN), diabetes mellitus (DM), smoking, and elevated lipoprotein(a) (Lp[a]) levels, may further modify ASCVD risk in this population.18

In response to the observed variability in ASCVD risk among individuals with FH, the International Atherosclerosis Society (IAS) proposed a clinical definition of severe FH. The primary aim of this proposal was to more accurately identify patients at very high risk for ASCVD. In a study by Funabashi et al.19 involving 380 Japanese patients with FH, those classified as having severe FH demonstrated an approximately 8-fold higher risk of ASCVD events compared with patients with non-severe FH. This increased risk persisted for subsequent ASCVD events. Similarly, in a study by Humphries et al.20 of 2,929 FH patients in the United Kingdom, individuals with severe FH exhibited a 64% higher coronary heart disease mortality rate than those with non-severe FH. These findings have been consistently replicated across multiple studies.19,20,21,22,23 Given this markedly elevated risk, the IAS emphasized the importance of early identification and aggressive treatment strategies for patients with severe FH.

Despite these advances, the phenotypic and genetic characteristics of severe FH remain incompletely defined. Importantly, most previous studies have applied only selected components of the IAS diagnostic criteria, potentially limiting comparability across studies. Accordingly, we sought to comprehensively evaluate clinical and genetic risk factors associated with severe FH in Korean patients by applying the IAS criteria as fully as possible.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study participants

Two datasets were used in this study. The first consisted of data from 97 patients previously reported in 2015,24 who were enrolled between January 2009 and July 2014 at 9 university hospitals. The second dataset comprised 199 patients enrolled between August 2014 and September 2020 at 16 university hospitals.17 The inclusion criteria for the latter cohort were as follows: 1) LDL-C greater than 190 mg/dL with the presence of tendon xanthoma; 2) LDL-C greater than 190 mg/dL with a family history of coronary artery disease (CAD) before 60 years of age or LDL-C greater than 190 mg/dL in a family member; 3) LDL-C greater than 190 mg/dL with incomplete information regarding family history of CAD onset or LDL-C levels; and 4) LDL-C greater than 225 mg/dL. These criteria are similar, though not identical, to the Simon Broome Register diagnostic criteria, as they also include patients with incomplete family histories.

The definition of severe FH was based on the 3 main criteria proposed by the IAS.25 Severe FH was defined as the presence of clinical ASCVD, evidence of subclinical atherosclerosis on computed tomography (CT) imaging, or markedly elevated LDL-C levels in combination with cardiovascular risk factors. Specifically, this included LDL-C levels greater than 400 mg/dL; LDL-C levels greater than 310 mg/dL in the presence of 1 additional risk factor; or LDL-C levels greater than 190 mg/dL in the presence of 2 or more risk factors. Risk factors included age over 40 years without lipid-lowering treatment, smoking, male sex, HTN, DM, and a family history of premature CAD. In this study, the IAS criteria for severe FH were applied as closely as possible. However, because evidence of subclinical atherosclerosis on CT imaging could not be evaluated due to the lack of systematically collected CT data, this component was not incorporated into the definition. In addition, 2 adaptations were made: former smokers were considered to have a risk factor, and family history of CAD was included regardless of age at onset.

2. Data collection

Clinical and laboratory data were collected at each participating hospital at the time of patient enrollment. Demographic variables included age, sex, and body mass index (BMI). Medical history was obtained, including information on HTN, DM, ASCVD, and family history of cardiovascular disease. Laboratory measurements included total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), LDL-C, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), Lp(a), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP). Blood samples were collected after a minimum fasting period of 12 hours and were analyzed within 4 hours at laboratories accredited by the Korean Society of Laboratory Medicine.

3. Molecular analysis

Genomic DNA was isolated from whole blood samples using the QuickGene SP kit (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan). As previously reported by Shin et al.,24 target enrichment for 199 patients was performed using a custom-designed panel covering 3 FH-associated genes (LDLR, APOB, and PCSK9) with a Target Enrichment kit (Celemics, Seoul, Korea), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was carried out on the NextSeq 500 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequence alignment and variant calling were performed using BWA-MEM, Picard (v1.115), Samtools (v1.1), GATK (v4.0.4.0), and Varscan (v2.4.0) to identify single nucleotide variants as well as insertions and deletions. Variants were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic if they met at least 1 of the following criteria: 1) truncating protein mutation; 2) annotation as pathogenic in the ClinVar database (v2020.05.13); or 3) fulfillment of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guideline criteria. For novel variants, pathogenicity was evaluated based on the known deleterious effects of amino acid changes, with particular attention to frameshift insertions or deletions. All pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, including both previously reported and novel variants, were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Copy number variation (CNV) analysis was performed using the Celemics in-house CNV detection tool, which estimates copy number changes by normalizing mean sequencing depth against the on-target median depth of each sample and the regional mean depth of baseline controls. Identified CNVs were validated using multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification or TaqMan assays.

4. Statistical analysis

A total of 296 patients diagnosed with FH were included in the analysis and categorized into severe and non-severe FH groups. Statistical analyses were performed to identify differences in clinical characteristics, genetic features, and biomarker profiles between the 2 groups. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Variables with a normal distribution were summarized as means with standard deviations, whereas non-normally distributed variables were described using medians and interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Univariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the associations between individual variables and FH severity. Multivariable logistic regression models were subsequently constructed to adjust for potential confounding factors and to assess the combined effects of multiple variables on disease severity. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare the incidence of ASCVD across groups stratified by genetic variant status, allowing assessment of trends across these categories. Statistical methods were selected to account for data heterogeneity and variable distribution characteristics, and tests were applied accordingly. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

5. Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of all 16 participating university hospitals (Appendix 1), including Seoul National University Hospital, Seoul, Korea (IRB No. 1512-079-728). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

RESULTS

1. Patient demographics and baseline lipid profiles

A total of 296 patients diagnosed with FH were included in the analysis. Among these, 172 patients (58.1%) were classified as having severe FH (Table 1). The median age of patients in the severe FH group was 53 years, and 65.1% were male. This contrasted markedly with the non-severe FH group, in which only 33.1% of patients were male, indicating a substantial sex-based difference between groups. In addition, comorbid conditions were more prevalent among patients with severe FH. HTN was present in 53.5% of patients with severe FH compared with 8.1% of those with non-severe FH, and DM was also more frequently observed in the severe FH group (12.8% vs. 1.6%).

With respect to lipid profiles and inflammatory markers, patients with severe FH demonstrated a more adverse laboratory profile. TG, Lp(a), and hs-CRP levels were higher in the severe FH group, whereas HDL-C levels were lower than those observed in patients with non-severe FH. Although TC and LDL-C levels were numerically higher in patients with severe FH, these differences did not reach statistical significance.

2. Clinical features by pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant status

Among the overall study population, 104 patients were identified as carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants (Table 2). Of these, 66 patients (63.5%) were classified as having severe FH. Among the 192 patients without pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, 106 patients (55.2%) were diagnosed with severe FH. However, this difference in prevalence was not statistically significant (p=0.170) (Supplementary Table 1). When clinical characteristics were compared according to variant status, similar overall patterns were observed in both groups. Regardless of the presence or absence of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, patients with severe FH exhibited a higher prevalence of DM as well as elevated levels of Lp(a) and hs-CRP compared with those with non-severe FH. In contrast, differences in lipid profiles between severe and non-severe FH appeared to be influenced by genetic variant status. Among patients without pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, lipid differences were attenuated, with identical median LDL-C levels (210 mg/dL) and only minimal differences in TG levels between severity groups. These findings suggest that conventional clinical risk factors and inflammatory markers may contribute more substantially to FH severity, particularly in patients without identifiable pathogenic variants.

Additional analyses were performed to evaluate LDL-C levels and the prevalence of ASCVD according to the type of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant. Among variant carriers, APOB variants were associated with the highest median LDL-C levels (278 mg/dL), whereas PCSK9 variants were associated with the lowest levels (234 mg/dL). Although variation was also observed across LDLR mutation subtypes, none of these differences reached statistical significance. Similarly, the prevalence of ASCVD did not differ significantly across variant categories (Supplementary Table 2).

3. Risk factor analysis for severe FH

Univariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with severe FH (Table 3). This analysis demonstrated that most conventional clinical risk factors for ASCVD were significantly associated with severe FH. Among these, HTN emerged as the strongest risk factor (odds ratio [OR], 13.11; 95% confidence interval [CI], 6.43–26.73; p<0.001), followed by smoking (OR, 10.89; 95% CI, 4.20–28.20; p<0.001) and DM (OR, 8.95; 95% CI, 2.06–38.80; p=0.003). In contrast, TG levels were not significantly associated with severe FH (OR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.99–1.01; p=0.259). Multivariable logistic regression analyses did not identify statistically significant independent associations for these variables. This likely reflects strong correlations among clinical risk factors as well as limited statistical power (data not shown).

4. Clinical characteristics of severe FH patients with and without ASCVD

Among the 172 patients classified as having severe FH, 60 had a documented history of ASCVD. Comparisons between patients with and without ASCVD revealed several notable clinical differences. Patients with ASCVD had a significantly lower proportion of males than those without ASCVD (43.3% vs. 76.8%, p<0.001). In addition, HTN was more prevalent among patients with ASCVD (66.7% vs. 46.4%, p=0.011). By contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed between the 2 groups in the prevalence of DM or smoking. Laboratory findings were largely comparable between patients with and without ASCVD, with the exception of HDL-C levels. Patients without ASCVD had significantly higher HDL-C concentrations (54 mg/dL) than those with ASCVD (44 mg/dL, p<0.001). Other lipid and inflammatory markers, including LDL-C, TG, and hs-CRP, did not differ significantly between groups (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

This study is the first to comprehensively examine the clinical and genetic characteristics of patients with severe FH using data derived from the largest FH registry in Korea. Our findings highlight several key clinical features that distinguish severe FH from non-severe FH, including a substantially higher prevalence of HTN, DM, and smoking, as well as more adverse lipid and inflammatory profiles. Univariable logistic regression identified HTN as the strongest predictor of severe FH, followed by smoking and DM. In contrast, the presence of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants was not significantly associated with severe FH or higher LDL-C levels, a finding that likely reflects limited statistical power due to the relatively small number of variant carriers.

The proportion of patients classified as having severe FH in this study was 58% (172 of 296), which is broadly consistent with the 40%–70% range reported in previous studies.19,20,23 Patients with severe FH were more likely to exhibit traditional cardiovascular risk factors and unfavorable biochemical markers, including elevated TG, Lp(a), and hs-CRP levels, together with reduced HDL-C concentrations. These findings are consistent with prior reports describing a distinct and more adverse clinical profile among patients with severe FH.19,21,22 However, unlike earlier studies that demonstrated significantly higher LDL-C levels in severe FH, our analysis did not identify a statistically significant difference in LDL-C concentrations between severe and non-severe FH groups. This discrepancy may be partially explained by the enrollment criteria of the Korean FH Registry, which selected patients with markedly elevated LDL-C levels at baseline, thereby reducing between-group variability. In addition, a substantial proportion of patients classified as having non-severe FH exhibited very high LDL-C levels but did not meet the IAS definition of severe FH because they lacked accompanying cardiovascular risk factors. Collectively, these findings suggest that although LDL-C remains a fundamental determinant of ASCVD risk in FH, the presence of concomitant risk factors plays a critical role in defining severe phenotypes beyond LDL-C levels alone.

Importantly, the adverse clinical features associated with severe FH were observed consistently among patients with and without pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants. Regardless of genetic status, individuals with severe FH demonstrated similarly elevated prevalences of HTN, DM, and smoking, as well as higher levels of inflammatory markers. Although the proportion of patients carrying pathogenic variants was numerically higher in the severe FH group, this difference did not reach statistical significance. These findings indicate that phenotypic severity in FH cannot be attributed solely to monogenic variant status and underscore the value of the IAS criteria in identifying individuals at high ASCVD risk, even in the absence of confirmatory genetic testing. The consistency of clinical characteristics across genetic subgroups further supports the robustness of the severe FH definition as a clinically meaningful stratification framework.

Among the different types of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, patients with loss-of-function variants in the LDLR gene exhibited the highest LDL-C levels (267 mg/dL), although this difference was not statistically significant when compared with other variant categories. Prior studies have demonstrated that loss-of-function variants are associated with substantially higher LDL-C concentrations and an increased risk of major adverse cardiac events relative to other mutation types.26 The absence of statistical significance in the present analysis is likely attributable to the limited sample size. Accordingly, future studies incorporating larger cohorts, polygenic risk scores, and more detailed LDLR subclassification will be required to better elucidate the relationship between variant type and phenotypic severity in FH.

The principal strengths of this study include the use of the Korean FH Registry, which integrates detailed clinical and molecular data from multiple tertiary care centers, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the findings within this clinical context. Another strength is the comprehensive application of the IAS criteria, which enabled rigorous classification of FH severity using a broad range of clinical and biochemical indicators. Importantly, this study represents the first systematic characterization of severe FH in a Korean population, providing novel region-specific insights into its phenotypic spectrum. In addition, stratified analyses according to genetic variant status and ASCVD history allowed a more nuanced assessment of heterogeneity within severe FH.

Several limitations of this study should also be acknowledged. First, although the overall sample size was relatively large for a monogenic disorder, it was insufficient to support robust subgroup analyses, particularly for comparisons across genetic subtypes. Second, the cross-sectional study design precluded causal inference regarding the relationships between clinical characteristics and ASCVD outcomes. Third, because all participants were recruited from tertiary academic centers, referral bias toward more severe phenotypes may have limited the representativeness of the broader FH population. Fourth, although we sought to apply comprehensive diagnostic criteria for severe FH, evidence of subclinical ASCVD on CT imaging was not incorporated, which may have resulted in under-identification of patients with early, imaging-defined atherosclerosis. Finally, genetic analyses were restricted to 3 major FH-associated genes (LDLR, APOB, and PCSK9), and other contributory variants may therefore have been missed.

In conclusion, this study provides important clinical and molecular insights into severe FH among Korean patients. Severe FH is characterized by the clustering of conventional cardiovascular risk factors and proatherogenic biochemical abnormalities, whereas the presence of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants did not differentiate severe from non-severe phenotypes. These findings reinforce the clinical utility of the IAS definition as a practical and effective tool for identifying individuals at high ASCVD risk. Future research should focus on large, population-based studies beyond tertiary care settings, the establishment of longitudinal cohorts to evaluate incident ASCVD events, and the assessment of treatment responses to support more individualized therapeutic strategies.
