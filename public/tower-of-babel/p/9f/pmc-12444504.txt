Physicochemical Characterization of a Silk Fibroin Film Fabricated from an Antheraea frithi Moore Cocoon for Potential Biomaterial Application
Authors: Sanasam Yaiphabi, Sanjenbam Kunjeshwori Devi, Laishram Rupachandra Singh, Raghumani Singh Ningthoujam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Silk fibroin has numerous biological applications, including wound healing, bone regeneration, corneal regeneration, and tissue engineering. There have been several reports on the fabrication of silk fibroin films using mulberry (Bombyx mori) and other wild nonmulberry silkworms. In our present study, we employed the natural cocoon of Antheraea frithi Moore, a nonmulberry silkworm, to generate a silk fibroin film through a series of stages including demineralization, degumming, dissolution, and casting of the regenerated silk fibroin solution. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) results revealed that the fabricated silk fibroin film exhibits β sheet, α helix, and random coil structures. The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) data revealed that the silk materials, ranging from raw cocoon shell to silk fibroin film, exhibited 5–6 endothermic peaks between 50 and 380 °C, indicating association of water loss, protein structural rearrangement, and thermal degradation during thermal treatment. The trace elemental studies of the possible presence of 15 different elements from ppm to ppb levels were performed. This study revealed that raw cocoon shells are free from toxic heavy metals, such as Hg or Pb. The presence of Ca, Na, and K as a major element is found in the raw cocoon shell. These findings are crucial for the potential application of the fabricated silk fibroin film prepared from the cocoon of A. frithi Moore as a biomaterial for biomedical application.

Introduction

To enhance and deliver superior healthcare in the rapidly advancing domain of medical field and therapeutic applications, novel, naturally occurring, and biologically compatible materials are consistently favored over synthetic alternatives. One of the most commonly studied natural materials is silk, a well-known protein polymer that has been utilized as a natural fiber in the textile industry for millennia. China and India are the largest producers of silk, followed by Japan and Thailand. Among the various organisms, including silkworms, spiders, and mites, the silk obtained from cocoons spun by silkworms has been studied most extensively due to its natural abundance and ease of extraction. Broadly, silk producing silkworms can be categorized into two main types: (i) the domesticated mulberry silkworm (Bombyx mori) and (ii) the wild nonmulberry silkworms such as Antheraea mylitta, Antheraea assamensis, Antheraea pernyi, Antheraea yamamai, and Antheraea proylei, among others. − In the present investigation, the wild nonmulberry silkworm Antheraea frithi was taken as the source of fibroin. It is found in Manipur and adjoining states in the northeastern region of India.

The silkworm cocoon comprises two silk proteins: fibroin and sericin. Fibroin, a homodimeric protein with a molecular weight in the range of 250–500 kDa constitutes around 70–80% of the overall weight of the cocoon. The remaining 20–30% of the cocoon consists of sericin. , The mulberry fibroin consists of a hydrophobic repeating hexameric unit of Gly-Ala-Gly-Ala-Gly-Ser (GAGAGS), whereas the wild nonmulberry silk fibroin consists of the repetitive poly­(alanine) sequence (-Ala-) n . These repeats predominantly determine the highly ordered secondary structure of the silks. , The other silk protein, sericin is a hydrophilic adhesive protein with a molecular weight in the range of 250–350 kDa that help in binding the two fibroin strands (brins) in the silkworm larva spinneret. This gummy gluelike protein, sericin, has to be removed in order to obtain sericin free silk fibroin fiber for preparation of its film. The process employed to remove sericin using sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) or other suitable agent is known as degumming.

Recently, among the two silk proteins, silk fibroin from wild nonmulberry silkworms of the Antheraea species (A. assamensis, A. mylitta, and A. pernyi) has been demonstrated to have significant potential as one of the most robust polymer biomaterials for biomedical applications such as for implants, wound healing, corneal regeneration, and tissue engineering. − In addition to their remarkable biocompatibility, biodegradability, high mechanical strength, nontoxicity, and stability, the wild nonmulberry silkworm’s fibroin is reported to contain an exceptional tripeptide motif called arginine-glycine-aspartate (RGD), which improves its suitability as a biomaterial. Reports indicate that these RGD tripeptides bind with cell integrin receptors, hence promoting cell growth and proliferation. −

To determine biomaterial suitability for biomedical applications, understanding the morphological, structural, thermal, and elemental characteristics is pivotal. In this work, we have, therefore, focused on the systematic studies of physicochemical characterizations such as surface morphology, size distribution of double brins and single brin of fibroin, structures of alpha and beta sheets of proteins, the presence of trace elements, CHNS analysis, and thermal stability of the raw cocoon shell and materials obtained through different processes to the fabricated silk fibroin film using SEM (scanning electron microscopy), EDAX (energy dispersive X-ray analysis), XRD (X-ray diffraction), FTIR (Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy), TGA (thermogravimetric analysis), DSC (differential scanning calorimetry), and CHNS (carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, and sulfur) techniques. The possible presence of elements in trace (ppm) levels is analyzed by different analytical techniques. Our analysis will help in understanding the microstructure, secondary structure, elemental composition, and thermal stability for future advanced biomedical applications. Experimental Section

Materials

Fresh and healthy cocoons of the nonmulberry silkworm A. frithi Moore were procured during April–May and August–September, 2021, from RSRS (Regional Sericultural Research Station), Imphal, Manipur.

EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, HiMedia, purity 99%), Na2CO3 (sodium carbonate, HiMedia, purity 99%), Ca­(NO3)2·4H2O (calcium nitrate tetrahydrate, Merck, purity 99%), and NaOH (sodium hydroxide, HiMedia, purity 99%) were used in this study. Deionized (DI) water (Millipore, USA) was employed. Figure (b) shows the original raw cocoon shell of the silkworm A. frithi Moore collected from RSRS, Imphal, Manipur, India, which is situated at an altitude 790 m above mean sea level (MSL), longitude 93.942567 °N and latitude 24.838818 °E.

Methods for Fabricating the Silk Fibroin Film

The extraction of silk fibroin from the silkworm cocoons and subsequent fabrication of the silk fibroin film were carried out by a procedure involving the following successive steps: (i) washing of the raw cocoon shell with warm DI H2O, (ii) demineralization, (iii) degumming, (iv) dissolution of the degummed silk fibroin fibers, and (v) preparation of the silk fibroin film. Figure shows the schematic diagram illustrating the steps employed for the fabrication of a silk fibroin film from the raw cocoon. Here, the raw cocoon shell represents the cocoon shell obtained after removal of pupa from the cocoon. The average weight of the cocoon was found to be 1.28 g ± 0.1 g. Figure presents (a) the live silkworm of A. frithi, (b) the spinning of the cocoon, and (c) the raw cocoon of A. frithi.

Sequential steps for extraction of silk fibroin from the raw A. frithi Moore cocoon shell for subsequent fabrication of a silk fibroin film.

Washing of the Cocoon with DI H2O

The nonmulberry silk cocoon contains a significant level of impurities as a result of being reared in its natural environment. Consequently, the cocoon shells, obtained after removal of pupa from the raw cocoons, were first washed with warm DI H2O at 35–40 °C (Figure c,d), thoroughly dried, and then cut into small pieces.

Demineralization

Following a slightly modified procedure of Gheysens et al., , 5 g of the cut raw cocoon shell pieces was demineralized by soaking in 200 mL of 1 M EDTA, which helps in removal of minerals, mainly Ca2+, Sr2+, Ba2+, Fe3+, and Mg2+, present in the raw cocoons. The soaked cocoon shell pieces were kept for 72 h at 40 °C with gentle stirring, washed thoroughly with DI H2O at room temperature, and then air-dried overnight using a hot air oven. The resulting cocoon shell pieces were assumed to be free from minerals such as calcium in the form of oxalate or other minerals (Figure e,f.

Degumming

Following the modified protocol reported, , degumming was achieved by boiling 1 g of the demineralized cocoon shell pieces in 100 mL of 0.02 M Na2CO3 for 1 h at 100 °C. After boiling, the degummed fibers were squeezed to remove the excess liquid containing sericin and Na2CO3 and then treated for 20 min in 1 L of warm (60–70 °C) DI H2O, three times in succession, followed by drying in a hot air oven for 12 h. The resulting sericin free degummed silk fibroin fibers were used to obtain the regenerated silk fibroin solution (Figure g,h. The quantitative estimation of degumming efficiency was determined by finding the degumming ratio. The weight of cocoon pieces before and after the degumming treatment was measured, and the degumming ratio was expressed with the help of the following equation Degummingloss(%)=(W1−W2)/W1×100 where W 1 is the initial weight of the silk fiber (g) and W 2 is the weight of the degummed silk fiber (g).

Dissolution of the Degummed Silk Fibroin Fibers

To obtain the regenerated silk fibroin solution, dissolution of the degummed silk fibers was performed following a slight modification of the method reported by Kweon and Park. The degummed silk fibroin fibers were dissolved by heating 1 g of the degummed fibers in 50 mL of 7 M Ca­(NO3)2·4H2O at 120 °C for 5 h. The resulting silk fibroin/Ca­(NO3)2·4H2O mixture solution was dialyzed against DI H2O for 48 h at room temperature using HiMedia dialysis membrane cellulose tubing (molecular weight cut-off between 12 and 14 kDa) to remove the calcium nitrate ions and then centrifuged at 22,000g for 30 min at 4 °C to separate the insoluble fiber materials.

The supernatant obtained was taken as the prepared regenerated silk fibroin solution. The regenerated silk fibroin solution was concentrated by lyophilization at −40 °C, and the lyophilized solution was stored at 4 °C for the fabrication of the silk fibroin film (Figure i–k).

Preparation of the Silk Fibroin Film

For preparation of the silk fibroin film, we followed a slightly modified protocol reported by Acharya et al. The silk films were prepared by gently casting 650 μL of regenerated fibroin solution over the surface of a HiMedia transparent autoclavable plastic sheet (20 × 36 in.2) placed in a 100 mm Petri plate (Figure l) and air-dried under an incubator shaker for 12–14 h at 40 °C to obtain the desired fibroin film. The protein film was peeled off and dried in a desiccator at room temperature for further analysis (Figure m).

Characterization

UV–Vis Absorption Spectroscopy

A UV–visible spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to determine the UV absorption spectra of the regenerated fibroin solution and the fabricated silk fibroin film in the spectral region at 200–900 nm.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

For morphological characterization of the four different samples, viz., raw cocoon shell, demineralized cocoon shell, degummed silk fibers, and silk fibroin film, the corresponding microscopic images were obtained after sputtering with gold followed by scanning using a Sigma field emission scanning electron microscope operated at 20 kV equipped with Oxford energy dispersive spectroscopy for compositional analysis.

Energy Dispersive X-ray Analysis (EDAX)

The elemental composition analyses of the raw cocoon shell, demineralized cocoon shell, degummed silk fibers, and silk fibroin films were carried out using an Oxford energy dispersive spectroscope attached to Sigma field emission scanning electron microscope.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD)

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the four samples, viz., raw cocoon shell, demineralized cocoon shell, degummed silk fibers, and silk fibroin film, were determined by a Rigaku diffractometer using a Cu–Kα (λ = 1.5405 Å) source in the scanning range of 2θ = 10–70° at 40 kV and 30 mA to assess the ordered alpha or β sheet structures of the protein samples.

The interplanar spacing d was calculated by using Bragg’s law: nλ = 2d sin θ, where θ is Bragg’s angle.

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

The secondary structure conformations (alpha, β sheet, or random) of all four samples, viz., raw cocoon shell, demineralized cocoon shell, degummed silk fiber, and silk fibroin film, were studied using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Bruker Vector 22 FTIR spectrometer). To avoid the effect of moisture, the samples were dried overnight in a desiccator. The infrared (IR) spectra were obtained in the spectral region of 400–4000 cm–1.

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)

The mass change percentages of the raw cocoon shell, demineralized cocoon shell, degummed silk fibers, and silk fibroin film with with increasing temperature were determined by thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) using Mettler Toledo TGA/DSC 1 System Stare Software. Each sample weighing about 20 mg was heated from 25 to 700 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C/min in a nitrogen gas atmosphere maintained at a flow of 50 mL/min.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)

To determine the thermal stability of the raw cocoon shell, demineralized cocoon shell, degummed silk fibers, and silk fibroin film, their DSC studies were carried out using a Mettler Toledo DSC1 System Stare System. 3 mg of each of the samples was taken and heated in an inert argon atmosphere from 40 to 50 °C with an increasing heating rate of 10 °C/min.

Trace Element Analyses

The determination of the trace elements, viz., Ca, Mg, Mn, Na, Hg, K, Sr, Mn, Co, Fe, Ni, Cu, Zn, Mo, and Pb, present in the raw cocoon shell of A. frithi (already washed with DI water) was carried out using the techniques of flame atomic absorption spectroscopy (FAAS), graphite furnace atomic absorption spectroscopy (GFAAS), and cold vapor atomic absorption spectroscopy (CVAAS). The concentrations of the trace elements were expressed in parts per million (μg/mL or mg/L) and parts per billion (ng/mL or μg/L). The raw cocoon shell was prepared for the analysis by digesting it completely with nitric acid (HNO3) and H2O2 (added only when required).

CHNS Analyses

Elemental CHNS analyses were performed to find out the composition of carbon (C), hydrogen (H), nitrogen (N), and sulfur (S) of the raw cocoon shell, demineralized cocoon shell, degummed silk fibers, and silk fibroin film using a Vario MACRO cube elemental analyzer (Elementar, Germany).

Raw Cocoon Shell

Figure i shows the surface structure of the untreated raw cocoon shell. The two singular filaments of fibroin brins (indicated by ″a”, ″b”, and ″c″ in Figure i) observed remain in contact with the rough surface. It was reported that the integrity of parallel silk filaments was maintained by sericin coated onto the surface of the filaments. ,

Also, there is an appearance of small granules (indicated by “d” and “e” in Figure i) over the surface and between the silk filaments. There were reports on the presence of similar granules such as calcium oxalate monohydrate in wild nonmulberry silkworm’s cocoon shells of A. mylitta and A. pernyi. ,, Figure ii,iii shows the enlarged microstructure of granules surrounding the two singular filaments of fibroin brins. It is observed that these are cuboids in space.

For fabrication of the silk fibroin biomaterial film from the cocoon of nonwild mulberry silkworms, it is recommended to remove the granular crystals, such as calcium oxalate monohydrate, from the raw cocoon shell. Moreover, studies on the surface morphology of the B. mori raw cocoon shell indicate no report of the presence of calcium oxalate crystals on their outer surface of the cocoon shell, enabling easy softening of the B. mori cocoon. Therefore, treatment of the raw cocoon shell with EDTA was performed to remove the minerals.

Figure v illustrates the SEM image of the degummed silk fibers obtained after Na2CO3 treatment. A separated individual brin is observed, and their brins are represented by “a”, “b”, and “c”. It is suggested that sericin covering over two parallel silk filaments is dissolved in sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) during the heat-treatment process, known as degumming (Section ). Silk Fibroin Film

The surface morphology of the silk fibroin film prepared from regenerated silk fibroin solution after drying at 40 °C and characterized by SEM is shown in Figure vi. The surface of the film shows the formation of small particle structures of different sizes. As a reason for contraction of the silk fibroin particles during drying, formation of small cracks is observed on the film. Similar formation of cracks in the regenerated silk fibroin film of B. mori was reported. Such surface roughness may help in exhibiting the biomaterial property by the A. frithi silk fibroin film fabricated in the present investigation, which is in agreement with the results obtained by Mandal and Kundus, who investigated the nanoroughness of the A. mylitta fibroin film and demonstrated that ethanol-induced nanotopographies (1–12 nm in height) significantly enhanced cytoskeleton organization, cell spreading, and binding of primary rat bone marrow cells.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD) Study

XRD is considered as one of the important techniques for biomaterial physicochemical characterization studies. The order structures of the silk raw cocoons to the fabricated silk fibroin film are analyzed and studied using the XRD technique.

Figure shows the XRD patterns of the (a) raw cocoon shell, (b) demineralized cocoon, (c) degummed silk fiber, and (d) silk fibroin film. It is observed that all the materials show similar diffraction pattern. One major strong sharp diffraction peak at around 2θ = 16°, corresponding to the ordered beta structure of the silk protein, and two minor peaks at 2θ = 13°, which correspond to the alpha helical secondary structure of protein and 2θ = 25°, a less ordered beta structure or random coil structure, are observed for all the materials. Previous studies had reported a similar peak obtained at around 2θ = 16.3–16.8°, representing ordered β sheet secondary structure, which determined the ordered structure of the silk. ,,,,

XRD patterns of the (a) raw cocoon shell, (b) demineralized cocoon, (c) degummed silk fiber, and (d) silk fibroin film.

The major sharp peak at 2θ = 16.52°, 16.72°, 16.76°, and 16.30° and two minor peaks at 2θ = 13.74°, 13.84°, 13.87°, and 13.42° and 2θ = 25.24°, 25.38°, 25.50°, and 24.9° are observed for the (a) raw cocoon shell, (b) demineralized cocoon, (c) degummed silk fiber, and (d) silk fibroin film, respectively, as seen in Figure . The intensity at the β-sheet (2θ ∼16.7) is much higher than that of the α- helix at (2θ ∼13.4). This suggests that the probability of the presence of β-sheet structure of the protein is very high.

From Figure , it is noted that the silk fibroin film exhibits a decrease in intensity as compared to the degummed silk fiber. This decrease could be explained by the regeneration process involving dissolution of the degummed silk fiber and dialysis of the dissolved degummed silk fiber in Ca­(NO3)2·4H2O solution for the preparation of regenerated silk fibroin solution to cast the silk fibroin film (Sections and ). , Therefore, it can be concluded from the XRD analysis that the predominant secondary structure of silk fibroin is found to be β sheet ordered structure coexisting with alpha helical structure and random coil structure.

The three main amides of a protein are classified into Amide I, associated with absorption of light by the vibrational C=O stretching at 1700–1600 cm–1, Amide II (N–H bending), and Amide III (C–N stretching). Out of these three amides, Amide I is considered the most significant in determining the secondary structural conformation of the protein. ,,,

From Figure , the Amide I absorption peak of the raw cocoon shell, demineralized cocoon, and degummed silk fiber are observed at 1620, 1619, and 1624 cm–1, respectively, indicating the presence of the β sheet secondary structure conformation. , Interestingly, the Amide I absorption band of the silk fibroin film is observed at 1645 cm–1, attributed to the random coil conformation of the protein or association of water. , The Amide II regions of the raw cocoon shell, demineralized cocoon, degummed silk fiber, and silk fibroin film are found at 1512, 1514, 1512, and 1537 cm–1, respectively, corresponding to the β sheet secondary structure. The FTIR peaks of Amide III are shown at 1233, 1222, 1233, and 1235 cm–1 for the raw cocoon shell, demineralized cocoon, degummed silk fiber, and silk fibroin film, assigned to the β sheet conformation. ,,

An absorption peak at 1303 cm–1 is only observed in the FTIR spectrum of the silk fibroin film, which is absent in the raw cocoon shell, demineralized cocoon, and degummed silk fiber. This particular absorption band in the silk fibroin film is assumed to be β turns acting as the precursor of the β sheet structure. It was also reported that the RGD tripeptide motif in the silk fibroin of wild silk may contribute to the IR absorption at 1303 cm–1.

The results of the FTIR analysis of the Antheraea silk fibroin film provide important insights into its chemical structure, especially the secondary structures, which are directly related to the biomaterial properties such as biocompatibility, biodegradability, and mechanical strength.

Conclusions

The silk fibroin film has been successfully fabricated from the regenerated silk fibroin solution prepared from the cocoon of A. frithi Moore through different steps. The present study aims to provide a systematic physicochemical characterization of the silk fibroin film. The UV–vis absorption spectroscopy showed the absorption peaks of the silk fibroin film at 280 nm. According to the SEM analysis, distinct surface morphological features were observed from the raw cocoon shell to the fabricated silk fibroin film. The first-time report on the in-depth detailed analyses of the measurement of widths of A. frithi materials provided additional new information on the width of the double brins and single brin of the raw cocoon shell, demineralized cocoon, and single brin of degummed silk fiber. The results of physicochemical techniques such as XRD and FTIR showed that all the materials, including the silk fibroin film, possess β sheet structure along with an α helical structure and a random coil structure. Both results of TGA and DSC indicate the silk fibroin film thermal stability up to 200 °C. The presence of Ca in the raw cocoon shell was supported by both techniques of EDX analysis and trace element analysis. Therefore, the findings from the systematic investigation study of the A. frithi raw cocoon shell to fabricated silk film may pave the way for newer opportunities for its application as a biomaterial in the medical field.
