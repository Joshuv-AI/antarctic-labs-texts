Long‐Term Outcomes With Class 1C Antiarrhythmic Drug Use in Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Aditya Bhonsale, Yisi Wang, Floyd Thoma, Krishna Kancharla, Andrew Voigt, Alaa Shalaby, Aditi Naniwadekar, Virginia Singla, N A Estes, Samir Saba, Suresh Mulukutla, Sandeep Jain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRhythm control with class 1C antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs) is a widely used treatment among patients with atrial fibrillation (AF), but cardiovascular benefits of class 1C pharmacotherapy over rate control strategy are still unclear.MethodsAdult patients with AF (n=100 748; 45% women) evaluated at a multihospital health system between 2010 and 2023 were assessed on clinical characteristics and class 1C AAD use and long‐term outcomes ascertained. Covariate balance across 36 factors was achieved using propensity score matching. Adjusted Cox modeling with competing risk analysis was used to compare patients using rhythm control (class 1C) with those offered a rate control strategy.ResultsPatients receiving a class 1C AAD (n=5239 [5%]) (70% flecainide) were significantly younger with lower cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, heart failure) and less comorbidity burden. Over a mean follow‐up of 5±3 years, matched patients receiving a class 1C AAD had significantly less all‐cause death (14% versus 19%; hazard ratio [HR], 0.65 [95% CI, 0.57–0.74]; P<0.001). Age (P=0.017), CHA2DS2‐VASc score (P=0.0003), and comorbidity burden (P=0.032) modify this relationship, while prior revascularization (P=0.83) or presence of coronary artery disease had no significant (P=0.54) impact. Class 1C recipients also had lower stroke hospitalization (3.6% versus 4.8%; HR, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.8–0.95]; P=0.01), while those aged 65 to 80 years experienced less heart failure (HR, 0.84 [95% CI, 0.71–0.98]; P=0.03) and major bleeding (HR, 0.8 [95% CI, 0.65–0.98]; P=0.039) as well.ConclusionsIn a real‐world contemporary registry of patients with AF from a North American health care system, use of class 1C AADs for rhythm control was associated with improved survival and hospitalization for stroke compared with rate control.Keywords: antiarrhythmic, atrial fibrillation, outcomeSubject Categories: Arrhythmias, Atrial Fibrillation

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

In this study of adult patients with atrial fibrillation, over an average follow‐up of 5 years, use of class 1C antiarrhythmic drugs (flecainide, propafenone) for rhythm control was associated with improved survival and reduced hospitalization for stroke compared with rate control.

In this study of adult patients with atrial fibrillation, over an average follow‐up of 5 years, use of class 1C antiarrhythmic drugs (flecainide, propafenone) for rhythm control was associated with improved survival and reduced hospitalization for stroke compared with rate control.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

The results of this study provide real‐world contemporary data in support of rhythm control with class 1C antiarrhythmic drugs among adult patients with atrial fibrillation.

The results of this study provide real‐world contemporary data in support of rhythm control with class 1C antiarrhythmic drugs among adult patients with atrial fibrillation.

Rhythm control is one of the major pillars of atrial fibrillation (AF) management traditionally used for improving symptoms. 1 , 2 Prior studies 3 , 4 indicate no difference in a rhythm versus rate control strategy in improving all‐cause death or cardiovascular hospitalizations. However, recent data suggest that early rhythm control could improve clinical outcomes, 5 even among asymptomatic patients with AF. 6 As of today, drug therapy remains the corner stone of rhythm control 1 and includes class 1C 7 antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs) like flecainide and propafenone that are increasingly used. 8 , 9 However, real‐world data to choose a drug on the basis of its mortality benefit or improvement in cardiovascular outcomes is extremely limited. 10 Given a shift in expert opinion, 11 demographics, 12 and guidelines, 2 it is important to understand these relative benefits to guide early therapy. Our study uses a large real‐world contemporary cohort of patients with AF to delineate association of such rhythm‐control use with class 1C AADs with regard to all‐cause survival and cardiovascular hospitalizations (heart failure, stroke, and major bleeding) compared with a propensity score (PS)‐matched control group of patients with AF managed with rate control.

Methods

Study Population

This is a retrospective observational cohort study that included patients aged >18 years who had at least 2 outpatient visits with either internal medicine, family medicine, or cardiology clinics of the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center with an associated diagnosis of AF. The diagnosis of AF was based on the presence of International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) and International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes (ICD‐9: 427.31, 427.32; ICD‐10: I48.0, I48.1, I48.2, I48.91, I48.3, I48.4, I48.92). 5 These patients were evaluated between January 4, 2010, and December 15, 2023, and the study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board with no requirement of informed consent given nature of data analysis. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

For this analysis, we excluded patients with a prior history of AF ablation or atrioventricular node ablation as well as surgical mitral valve interventions. We also excluded those with missing follow‐up data and those with <90 days of follow‐up or those who died within 90 days of follow‐up. Rhythm control with a class 1C AAD was defined as use of either propafenone or flecainide at initial visit or at follow‐up. Rate control was defined as absence of any AAD use or AF ablation performance at any point in time. Patients who underwent direct AF ablation without prior initiation of AAD were also excluded. We also excluded those with >1 category of drug use at the same visit (class 1 and class 3 AADs). The first clinic visit with a diagnosis of AF was considered as baseline for clinical characteristics for the cohort.

Patient Characteristics

The University of Pittsburgh Medical Center is an integrated insurance and provider system with >40 academic, community, and specialty hospitals. The hospital system‐wide electronic health record data are aggregated and harmonized with administrative, financial, and other publicly available data in its Clinical Data Warehouse, 12 and the Data and Analytics Program was used to obtain the clinical parameters, including age at presentation; sex; race; cardiovascular risk factors including presence of obesity, smoking history, hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease (CAD), congestive heart failure (HF), mitral valve disease, hyperlipidemia, vascular history (peripheral vascular disease) including prior history of deep vein thrombosis and major bleeding, neurological history including prior ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke and transient ischemic attack, prior ventricular arrhythmias (ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation); and prior cardiac arrest. ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes for the major clinical variables used in the study are as per prior publication. 12 Major comorbidities evaluated included chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, prior history of cancer, obstructive sleep apnea, chronic kidney disease, and liver disease. Current Procedural Terminology codes were used to ascertain prior automatic implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator implantation, permanent pacemaker implantation, pulmonary vein ablation, atrioventricular node ablation, cardioversion, and loop monitor implants along with cardiac interventions, including percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary bypass. Medication use at baseline was evaluated, including use of antiplatelets, anticoagulants, and HF/diabetes medications.

Outcome

The primary outcome of interest was all‐cause death, which was ascertained by review of the Social Security Death Index and enriched by electronic health record data input (hospitalization data or office visit) aggregated in the Clinical Data Warehouse. 12 The last censoring date for mortality outcome was October 15, 2023. The secondary outcomes of interest included hospitalization for HF, stroke, or major bleeding. A hospitalization was attributed to the secondary outcome if the corresponding ICD‐9/10 code was listed as 1 of the top 3 diagnoses (primary, principal, or secondary) during an inpatient admission. 12 Cardiac interventions performed during follow‐up were evaluated including electrophysiology interventions (device therapy, ablation, cardioversions) and PCI. Follow‐up times were calculated from the start of the individual category AAD drug (either started at baseline or during follow‐up). For the rate‐control group, follow‐up time was calculated from baseline to the event of interest or censoring date.

Statistical Analysis

The categorical variables are presented by count and percentage, while continuous variables are reported as mean±SD or median with interquartile range. Normality was assessed by Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Student's t test or the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables when appropriate. When applicable, the χ2 or Fisher exact test was used for categorical variables comparison.

Propensity Score

Logistic regression was used to calculate the PS on the basis of the baseline characteristics (saturated) to reduce selection bias. Each matched set was created by a greedy matching algorithm (1:1 nearest‐neighbor matching without replacement, caliper=0.1 of the SD of the logit PS). After matching, standardized mean difference was calculated for selected variables. A standardized mean difference <15% was considered unbalanced and was adjusted in further analysis. After matching, analysis was accounted for the matched pairs. McNemar's test was used to assess the statistical significance of the risk difference for categorical variables and paired t test or Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for continuous variables when appropriate. For time‐to‐event analysis, a marginal model with robust variance estimation was fit to a Cox regression model that was stratified by the matched pairs used in the PS‐matched cohort. This approach accounted for the within‐pair homogeneity by allowing the baseline hazard function to vary across matched sets. Hospitalization outcomes were assessed using the Fine–Gray model, 13 with death as the competing risk event. Survival analysis was depicted though Kaplan–Meier curves and hospitalization through cumulative incidence curves.

PS matching was performed using the following variables: female sex, race (White, Black, other), active smoking, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, obstructive sleep apnea, HF, hypertension, prior stroke, peripheral vascular disease, malignancy, prior revascularization with PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting, prior cardioversion, direct oral anticoagulant use, diabetes, prior permanent pacemaker implant, prior implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator implant, CAD, history of ventricular tachycardia/ventricular fibrillation, insurance status, prior cardiac resynchronization therapy, prior pulmonary embolism or deep vein thrombosis, year of evaluation, angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitor use, angiotensin receptor blocker use, angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor use, sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitor use, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonist use, aspirin use, age at presentation, estimated income, body mass index, CHAD2S2‐VASc score, Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) score, Elixhauser Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality index, and specialty of initial evaluation (cardiology versus noncardiology).

Sensitivity and Subgroup Analysis

Sensitivity analysis was performed in various subsets, excluding those who switched AAD classes during follow‐up, those who underwent pulmonary vein isolation during follow‐up, those who had atrioventricular node ablation during follow‐up, and (most restrictive) all those in the above models. Additional models excluding those who had cardioversion before presentation were analyzed as well. Two‐way interaction analysis was performed, with sex, diabetes, congestive HF, prior CAD, prior revascularization (with PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting), age category (<65, 65–80, >80 years), CHAD2S2‐VASc score categories (0–1, 2–3, ≥4), and CCI categories (0–1, 2–3, ≥4) to elucidate significant relationship to class 1C use and outcomes. Subgroup hazard ratio (HR) was calculated for the variables defined in the interaction analysis. Furthermore, to ensure a common time origin and avoid immortal time bias, sensitivity analysis using AAD use as a time‐varying covariate (a start–stop [counting process] Cox model) was also performed.

Results

Study Population

The baseline characteristics of the study population (n=100 748; rate‐control arm n=70 177) are noted in Table 1, with 5% (n=5239) of the population initiated on class 1C pharmacotherapy (flecainide, n=3651, 70%). As noted in Table 1, class 1C recipients were significantly younger (65.2±12.4 versus 73.2±12.7 years; P<0.001), more likely women (49% versus 46%; P<0001), more often had commercial insurance, had lower CHAD2S2‐VASc score (2.1 ±1.5 versus 3.0±1.6; P<001), and had less comorbidity (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, stroke, bleeding) compared with the rate‐control cohort. Class 1C AAD recipients were more likely to have had prior cardioversion, with 9% having prior PCI. Table 2 depicts the medication use and comorbidity burden in these patients. While no difference in obstructive sleep apnea or active smoking was noted, comorbidity scores were significantly higher in the rate‐control group. Apixaban was the most common direct oral anticoagulant with similar usage, while warfarin and digoxin were more commonly used in the rate‐control group.

Outcomes

Table 3 details the long‐term outcomes in patients receiving rate control and class 1C AADs. Over a mean follow‐up of 5±3 years, 14% of patients receiving class 1C AADs died, while 11.3% had an HF hospitalization, 3.6% had a stroke hospitalization, and 6% had a major bleeding hospitalization. A switch to non‐amiodarone class 3 agents (dofetilide, sotalol) was seen in 9%, while amiodarone use during follow‐up was seen in 11%. AF ablation was performed in 16% of class 1C recipients, while 18% underwent cardioversion during follow‐up. ICD implantation rates were similar in the class 1C and rhythm‐control groups. All‐Cause Death

Results of the multivariable Cox regression analysis in the PS‐matched population are shown in Table 4. The patients with class 1C AAD use experienced significantly improved survival (14% versus 19%; HR, 0.65 [95% CI, 0.57–0.74]; P<0.001) with survival curves depicted in Figure 1. Results of interaction analysis are shown in Table S2. Age, CHAD2S2‐VASc score, and CCI showed significant interaction with class 1C use and death. Class 1C AAD use in those aged <80 years, CHAD2S2‐VASc score <4, and CCI <3 (HR in subgroups shown in Table S3) was associated with improved outcome, while prior history of CAD or revascularization did not interact significantly. Among patients without prior HF, class 1C use was associated with improved survival, while no significant difference in absolute death was found in those with prior HF (class 1C, 41% versus rate control, 48%; P=0.167).

Hospitalization Outcomes

Competing risk analysis of HF hospitalization shows that the HR of HF hospitalization in the PS‐matched population was 0.91 (95% CI, 0.83–1.07; P=0.598), although statistically not significant, numerically less (11% versus 12%) in favor of the class 1C group (Figure 2). Interaction analysis in Table S2 depicts that prior history of HF (n=444 in matched population) had significant interaction with class IC use and subsequent HF hospitalization with an HR of 1.5 (95% CI, 1.1–1.9; P=0.009) suggesting increased HF hospitalization with class 1C use in those with preexisting HF (Table S4). Sensitivity analysis (Table S5) among those with available echocardiographic left ventricular ejection fraction (EF; n=34 797) data in the matched population suggest no significant difference in those with preserved (EF >50%: 1691 rate versus 1691 class 1C; HR, 1.1 [95% CI, 0.9–1.3]; P=0.22) and mild–moderate left ventricular EF reduction (EF=35%–50%: 305 rate versus 305 class 1C; HR, 1.0 [95% CI, 0.7–1.4]; P=0.892). Additionally, subgroup analysis as reported in Table S4 depicts that among patients aged 65 to 80 years, class 1C use was associated with better outcomes (HR, 0.84 [95% CI, 0.71–0.98]; P = 0.035; Figure S1). Patients with class 1C use also had significantly less stroke hospitalization (HR, 0.81 [95% CI, 0.69–0.95]; P=0.011; Figure 3) compared with those offered rate control. No significant interaction with class 1C therapy for this outcome was noted in Table S2. Among patients with prior stroke (n=591), stroke rehospitalization rates were statistically similar between the 2 groups (8.6% versus 11.6%; P=0.23; Table S6). Major bleeding hospitalization was not significantly different in the class 1C AAD recipients (Table 4), and no significant interaction was noted (Table S2). Subgroup analysis depicting improved outcome in those aged between 65 and 80 years is noted in Table S7 and Figure S2.

Discussion

The incidence and prevalence of AF is increasing in the United States and globally, 1 with an expected prevalence of 12.1 million patients 1 in 2030. Management of the condition has evolved over time, and therapies aimed specifically at restoration and maintenance of sinus rhythm (rhythm control) constitute an important therapeutic strategy, albeit used only in 15% to 25% patients with AF 14 with AAD pharmacotherapy constituting the predominant choice over more invasive options. 9 , 15 , 16 Class 1C AADs received Food and Drug Administration approval in the late 1980s, account for ≈20% of rhythm‐control medications, 17 , 18 and have been shown to be effective in reducing AF recurrence. 2 , 19

Despite the shift in guidelines 1 and peer opinion, 11 the impact of rhythm control on the mortality rate as it pertains specifically to class 1C drug use is not clearly understood given the lack of specific trial data. Understanding the association of class 1C pharmacotherapy with death and hospitalization would enable more appropriate use of these agents, particularly early during the AF course.

The results of our PS‐matched study, using a large contemporary real‐world cohort of patients from a North American health care system point to the fact that use of class 1C AAD for AF is associated with improved survival with an HR consistent with data emanating from both European randomized controlled trials 5 and large Asian observational registries. 9 Although no difference in the mortality rate was observed between the rate‐ versus rhythm‐control arms in the AFFIRM (Atrial Fibrillation Follow‐Up Investigation of Rhythm Management) trial, sparse use of class1C medications 4 preclude any robust extrapolation from those data. Of note, a PS‐matched subset analysis of death reported no difference in death in the minority of patients randomized to rhythm control and receiving flecainide (8%) from that study. At the same time, our results confirm and extend the findings from a prior small (n=689) study 8 that found similar improvement in the composite mortality outcome. The result of our study also suggests that age as well as cardiovascular and noncardiovascular comorbidity burden modifies this relative benefit of class 1C therapy with the traditional paradigm of choosing this medication in younger patients with fewer morbidities providing the best outcomes. Similar registry data with lower hazard with younger age 20 , 21 point to a clinical profile that may best benefit with aggressive class 1C pharmacotherapy.

Among patients without HF, class 1 C use was associated with improved survival and no significant worsening of HF hospitalization. Asian nationwide registries 9 , 21 that have >50% use of class 1C report improved HF hospitalization in a much sicker population (higher CHAD2S2‐VASc and CCI scores) and improved HF outcomes but may require early initiation. 9 Veterans Affairs data that excluded patients with HF suggest improved HF hospitalization compared with class 3 medications. 22 In our study, among patients with a prior HF diagnosis, class 1C use was associated with increased risk of future HF hospitalization compared with rate control, while in a randomized controlled trial post hoc analysis, 8 class 1C use was not associated with worse HF hospitalization but they were numerically small. This may be potentially related to use in those few with significantly depressed left ventricular EF. In our study, sensitivity analysis from those with preserved and mildly depressed EF suggest consistent survival benefit and no HF hospitalization increase like prior analysis. 8 Patients with HF are traditionally contraindicated from a class 1C prescription. 1 Whether those with mild left ventricular dysfunction/HF with preserved EF can be safely treated is a question for future prospective studies, but data from premature ventricular contraction–mediated cardiomyopathy 23 remains encouraging.

The results of our study also show improved hospitalization for all‐cause stroke in those receiving class 1C pharmacotherapy consistent with European and Asian registry data. Less AF burden has recently been associated with less stroke incidence 24 and CV outcomes and may drive some of the improvement seen with rhythm control. Finally, a prior history of cardiac revascularization with coronary artery bypass grafting or PCI or a diagnosis of CAD did not impact the relative benefit of class1C drugs. Food and Drug Administration recommendation labels flecainide use to be contraindicated in all patients with structural heart disease of any pathogenesis. 19 However, patients with (untreated or treated) stable CAD or HF with preserved EF or mildly reduced EF without prior myocardial infarction were not studied in CAST (Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial)25 and recent research on Class 1C in patients with CAD suggest acceptable safety profile. 26

Our data support increasing use of rhythm control in appropriately selected patients. It encourages modern‐day trials of rhythm control with class 1C with rate control as well as continued research in safety and efficacy in those groups previously excluded: prior CAD revascularization and mild left ventricular dysfunction. Sensitivity analysis along with interaction analysis was performed in various subsets, providing robustness to these inferences. Although safety rather than efficacy considerations should primarily guide the choice of AAD, future prospective studies are needed to refine clinical use.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations that are intrinsic to the design of an observational study. Despite the large study population and robust PS matching and sensitivity analysis, unmeasured confounding from clinical features not accounted for in the analysis cannot be excluded and could potentially modify the results. Although our data represent a real‐world cohort with hard clinical outcomes, the reliance on administrative billing data, electronic health record for clinical diagnosis, and subsequent inability to characterize the nature of AF (paroxysmal versus persistent) limits our interpretation. Furthermore, the dose of the class AADs and medication compliance were not known as part of this registry and could potentially modify the outcomes. Additionally, the nature of deaths, whether cardiac or noncardiac, was not ascertainable and limits causal interpretation of the specific cause of death. Additionally, although used widely, Social Security Disability Insurance data may not estimate death accurately. Finally, we do not have data on electrocardiographic changes to estimate PR and QRS widening with medication use.

Conclusions

In a real‐world contemporary registry of patients with AF from a large North American health care system, use of class 1C AADs was associated with improved survival and hospitalization for stroke among patients with AF.
