Liver fibrosis is associated with men’s health care symptoms, especially erectile dysfunction and lower urinary tract symptoms
Authors: Yuka Uesaka, Akira Tsujimura, Riho Kasai, Yukiko Ota, Takashi Kanda, Yuta Anno, Haruhiko Wakita, Keisuke Ishikawa, Ayumu Taniguchi, Taiji Nozaki, Masato Shirai, Kazuhiro Kobayashi, Shigeo Horie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe prevalence of liver dysfunction among men has been steadily increasing in recent decades. Among the various non-invasive assessment tools available, the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) Index has emerged as a particularly valuable and widely adopted scoring system for evaluating liver fibrosis. This study investigated the relationship between liver fibrosis evaluated by the FIB-4 Index and male health care parameters.Materials and MethodsParticipants were assessed using standardized questionnaires, including the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) for lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), the Sexual Health Inventory for Men (SHIM) and EHS (Erection Hardness Score) for erectile function, and the AMS (Aging Males Symptoms rating scale) for late onset hypogonadism. Endocrinological parameters, including dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S), insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), total testosterone and cortisol levels, as well as metabolic factors, including hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and triglyceride level, were evaluated as potential confounders.ResultsThe patient age was 50.62±0.24 years. The analysis revealed significant associations between higher FIB-4 Index quintiles and worsening sexual function and LUTS. Among endocrine factors, DHEA-S and IGF-1 exhibited decreasing trends with higher FIB-4 Index values, whereas cortisol showed an increasing trend. Surprisingly, no significant association was observed between FIB-4 Index and testosterone levels. HbA1c increased, but triglycerides did not correlate with FIB-4. Multiple regression confirmed IPSS and SHIM scores were independently linked to the FIB-4 Index (p<0.05).ConclusionsThese findings highlight the importance of hepatic assessment in men with erectile dysfunction and LUTS, supporting a multidisciplinary approach to care.Keywords: Erectile dysfunction, Hormones, Liver fibrosis, Lower urinary tract symptoms, Metabolic syndrome

INTRODUCTION

We have previously reported a close relationship between metabolic syndrome and late onset hypogonadism (LOH) in middle-aged and older men [1], as well as a significant relationship between serum testosterone levels and arteriosclerosis [2,3]. It is also well known that metabolic syndrome and arteriosclerosis are related to dietary habits as well as to obesity. Recently, liver dysfunction caused by poor dietary habits has attracted attention [4,5]. Indeed, the prevalence of liver dysfunction among men has been steadily increasing in recent decades [6,7], primarily attributed to excessive alcohol consumption and dietary irregularities that impose a significant metabolic burden on the liver function. This alarming trend has necessitated the development of early detection strategies to identify liver impairment before the onset of overt clinical manifestations. The fibrotic process reflects the liver’s response to chronic injury and inflammation, making it an important target for intervention strategies. Among the various non-invasive assessment tools that are available, the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) Index has emerged as a particularly valuable and widely adopted scoring system for evaluating liver fibrosis [8]. This index, calculated using readily available laboratory parameters, including age, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and platelet count, offers a cost-effective and accessible method for assessing the severity of liver fibrosis without the need for invasive liver biopsy procedures. Recent advances in understanding the interconnected nature of organ systems have highlighted potential associations between liver dysfunction and various aspects of male sexual health as well as LOH [9,10]. Similarly, lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), including storage and voiding dysfunction, affect a substantial proportion of aging men and may share common pathophysiological pathways with systemic metabolic disorders [11].

Despite the biological plausibility of associations between liver fibrosis and male specific symptoms, comprehensive investigations examining these relationships in large-scale populations remain limited. Previous studies have primarily focused on individual aspects of male health or have been constrained by small sample sizes, which limits the generalizability of findings and the ability to detect clinically meaningful associations. Therefore, the present study was designed to comprehensively evaluate the relationships between the FIB-4 Index and various parameters of masculinity, including sexual dysfunction, LUTS and LOH parameters, in a large cohort of men.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study included 2,369 men with LOH symptoms, who visited our hospital or affiliated clinic between May 2016 and March 2024. LOH symptoms included lethargy, general fatigue, malaise, depression, insomnia, frustration, reduced concentration, sweating, hot flashes, dizziness, night sweats, sexual dysfunction and LUTS. LUTS, sexual function, and LOH were assessed using the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) and the quality of life index, the Sexual Health Inventory for Men (SHIM) and the Erection Hardness Score (EHS), and the Aging Males Symptoms rating scale (AMS), respectively. Endocrinological data, including dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S), insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), total testosterone and cortisol levels were evaluated. Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and triglyceride levels were selected as variables for analysis because they are dietary-related factors that may have a potential association with liver fibrosis. To accurately determine the FIB-4 Index, age, AST, ALT, and platelet count were also evaluated. The FIB-4 Index, a non-invasive marker of liver fibrosis, was calculated using the following formula: FIB-4=(age [years]×AST [U/L])/(platelet count [109/L]×√ALT [U/L]) [8]. All blood samples were collected between 09:00 and 11:00 to monitor endocrinological variables. This study was conducted retrospectively, and all data were obtained through chart-based extraction from medical records.

First, the calculated FIB-4 Index was stratified into quintiles. Associations between the FIB-4 Index and age, symptom scores, endocrine factors, metabolic parameters, liver enzymes, and platelet count were analyzed. Subsequently, endocrine and metabolic factors that demonstrated significant associations with the FIB-4 Index were included as covariates to evaluate the relationship between the symptom scores and the FIB-4 Index. Finally, for the symptom scores that showed significant associations, the relationship between their severity and the FIB-4 Index was further examined.

1. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables and questionnaire scores are expressed as the mean±standard error (SE). Trend analysis was first conducted to examine the associations between the quintile-based FIB-4 Index and each clinical factor. Subsequently, multiple regression analysis was performed to assess the relationship between the FIB-4 Index and symptom scores, adjusting for endocrine and metabolic factors that showed significant associations in the initial analysis. Given the potential confounding effects of the SHIM and EHS, the SHIM was used to assess sexual function in the multivariable model. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 24.0 (IBM Corp.).

2. Ethics statement

Written informed consent was obtained from all of the patients at the first visit to our institutes. The procedures were approved by the Regional Ethics Committee of Juntendo University (approval number: E24-0197).

RESULTS

The characteristics of the 2,369 patients for whom the FIB-4 Index could be obtained are shown in Table 1. The mean (±SE) patient age, IPSS, SHIM score, and AMS score were 50.62±0.24 years, 9.06±0.12 (indicating moderate symptoms), 11.62±0.14 (indicating moderate erectile dysfunction [ED]), and 40.32±0.25 (indicating moderate symptoms), respectively. All endocrinological and metabolic factors, liver enzymes and platelet count were within normal limit. The FIB-4 Index was also shown to be within the normal limits (1.08±0.01), because a value of <1.3 is considered normal.

As expected, increasing quintiles of the FIB-4 Index were associated with higher age and elevated liver enzyme levels, along with a decreasing trend in platelet counts. Regarding symptom scores, higher FIB-4 Index values were consistently associated with worsening symptoms: IPSS and AMS demonstrated upward trends, while SHIM and EHS showed clear downward trends. Among endocrine factors, DHEA-S and IGF-1 exhibited decreasing trends with higher FIB-4 Index values, whereas cortisol showed an increasing trend. Surprisingly, no significant association was observed between FIB-4 Index and testosterone levels. Similarly, among metabolic parameters, HbA1c showed an increasing trend with higher FIB-4 Index values, while no association was found with triglyceride levels (Table 2). In the multiple regression analysis of symptom scores adjusted for DHEA-S, IGF-1, cortisol, and HbA1c, both the IPSS and SHIM scores were found to be significantly associated with the FIB-4 Index (Table 3). As the FIB-4 Index increased, the SHIM score decreased (Fig. 1A; p for trend <0.001). Conversely, greater severity of ED, as indicated by lower SHIM scores, was associated with higher FIB-4 Index values (Fig. 1B; p for trend <0.001). Similarly, as the FIB-4 Index increased, the IPSS increased (Fig. 2A; p for trend <0.001). Conversely, greater severity of LUTS, as indicated by higher IPSS, was associated with higher FIB-4 Index values (Fig. 2B; p for trend <0.001).

DISCUSSION

Basically, the association between liver dysfunction and ED and LUTS in men is a clinically important finding that deserves attention [9,12]. Liver fibrosis is an important indicator to consider when evaluating early intervention in the progression of the liver from an inflammatory response to chronic damage. Interestingly, a large-scale study utilizing data from 43,881 participants in the UK Biobank quantitatively assessed interstitial fibrosis in the liver, pancreas, heart, and kidneys. This investigation demonstrated a strong statistical and genetic correlation between liver fibrosis and fibrosis in other organs, providing robust evidence for the systemic nature of fibrotic progression [13]. In other words, patients with liver fibrosis may also experience fibrosis progressing in the penis causing ED, as well as affecting the pelvic floor muscles, which is thought to affect sexual function and LUTS.

From a pathophysiological standpoint, a vascular perspective is considered fundamental in the evaluation of ED. It is speculated that liver dysfunction is associated with systemic endothelial dysfunction, which plays a central role in the pathophysiology of ED [14]. This endothelial dysfunction impairs the production of nitric oxide (NO), the most important mediator of penile smooth muscle relaxation and erection [15]. In patients with liver fibrosis, reduced endothelium-dependent vasodilation and increased oxidative stress have been observed, both of which are associated with impaired NO bioavailability [16]. From a hormonal regulation perspective, the liver plays a crucial role in the metabolism of sex hormones, including testosterone, estrogen, and sex hormone-binding globulin. As liver fibrosis progresses, the impaired liver function disrupts the pituitary-gonadal axis. In general, serum testosterone levels have been reported to decrease in patients with liver dysfunction [17,18]. However, in the present study, we observed that as the FIB-4 Index increased, the serum levels of DHEA-S and IGF-1 tended to increase, whereas cortisol levels showed a decreasing trend. In contrast to previous reports, however, no decline in testosterone levels was observed. Although the reason for this discrepancy remains unclear, since the present study targeted men without clinically evident liver dysfunction, it cannot be excluded that the results may differ from the endocrine characteristics previously observed in patients with cirrhosis. Notably, this study was a retrospective analysis of men presenting with LOH symptoms, and it is possible that these individuals were more aware of their testosterone status than the general population. They may have engaged in interventions such as supplementation or resistance training. However, due to the chart-based nature of the study, collecting more detailed information on such factors was not feasible. Factors such as the use of testosterone-related supplements or lifestyle interventions known to increase testosterone levels may also have influenced our results, as these variables were not comprehensively assessed in our survey. Furthermore, among metabolic factors, an increase in the FIB-4 Index was associated with elevated HbA1c levels. This finding is consistent with previous reports that have demonstrated a relationship between liver dysfunction and diabetes [19]. The potential pathway by which liver fibrosis affects erectile function through disturbances in glucose metabolism should also be carefully considered. Furthermore, even in cases of compensatory liver cirrhosis, subtle changes in cognitive function may affect the central pathways regulating sexual function. Common symptoms of liver disease, such as fatigue, depression, and sleep disorders, may further exacerbate sexual dysfunction through psychological pathways. Previous reports have shown that elevated FIB-4 Index values or high stiffness measurements based on transient elastography are associated with a higher incidence of ED [20], even in cases without clinically decompensated liver disease. This suggests that the fibrotic process itself, rather than cirrhosis itself, may play a crucial role in the onset of sexual dysfunction. The results of the present study also clearly demonstrate that an increase in the FIB-4 Index is associated with a decrease in SHIM score and that the severity of SHIM is similarly associated with an increase in FIB-4 Index even in the analysis adjusted for endocrine and metabolic factors that showed significant associations. This suggests a link between liver fibrosis and erectile function. A large-scale survey of 72,068 men aged 50 and older reported that 27.3% had severe symptoms and 51.7% had moderate symptoms related to LUTS [21]. LUTS is closely associated with psychological factors, including anxiety and depression [22]. Recently, the risks and side effects of commonly used medications for benign prostatic hyperplasia have gained increased attention [23]. Regarding LUTS, we clearly demonstrated a close relationship between the FIB-4 Index and IPSS in the present study. Although prostate volume was not measured and uroflowmetry was not performed, resulting in a lack of objective urological data, this limitation must be considered given that the study subjects were patients presenting with LOH symptoms. The association between inflammation of the liver and prostate has already been noted [24]. Furthermore, LUTS observed in patients with liver fibrosis are associated with structural changes in the prostate and the area surrounding the urethra [13]. This pathological change may significantly reduce the flexibility of the urethra, resulting in bladder outlet obstruction (BOO). Specifically, in animal models, it has been reported that collagen accumulation around the urethra causes hardening of the urethral wall and reduces urethral distensibility during urination [25,26], although direct evidence in humans is still lacking. This leads to weak urine flow, difficulty urinating, and ultimately symptoms of overactive bladder. Additionally, in normal prostate tissue, there is a balance between smooth muscle cells and fibroblasts. However, systemic inflammation associated with liver fibrosis may disrupt this balance and cause a predominance of fibrous components [27]. Transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β)-mediated fibrosis in benign prostatic hypoplasia alters the mechanical properties of the prostate and surrounding tissues, and contributes to LUTS [28]. This fibrotic process goes beyond mere mechanical obstruction and affects the physiological function of prostate tissue. Furthermore, the progression of BOO induces adaptive changes in the bladder wall. The increase in bladder pressure caused by BOO leads to compensatory hypertrophy, which promotes bladder wall stiffening [29]. This change reduces bladder compliance and significantly impairs its storage function. As a result, symptoms such as urinary urgency, frequency, and nocturia eventually emerge. Nocturia is also associated with complex changes in fluid dynamics. A decreased liver synthetic function leads to a reduced serum albumin concentration and decreased plasma osmolarity. This change causes fluid to accumulate in the lower limbs and abdominal cavity during the day, which is then redistributed in the vascular space at night when the individual is in the supine position. This fluid redistribution mechanism is thought to increase nocturnal circulating blood volume, leading to increased renal blood flow and a significant increase in nocturnal urine volume, thereby contributing to nocturia [30]. Additionally, similar to the effects on ED, endocrine changes in liver fibrosis also affect LUTS. Estrogen regulates and promotes the proliferation of prostate stromal cells, as well as the development of prostate stromal fibrosis. An increase in stromal components and prostate fibrosis may accelerate the clinical progression of benign prostatic hyperplasia [31]; however, the present study did not include information on estrogen. When our findings are combined with those of other researchers, the importance of a fundamental treatment approach based on pathophysiology for managing urinary symptoms associated with liver fibrosis, in addition to conventional symptomatic therapy, is gaining attention.

While our findings are compelling, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the retrospective nature of the study limited our ability to assess patients’ full clinical histories, including comorbidities, medication use, and lifestyle factors such as alcohol intake and diet. We acknowledge that these factors may affect liver function and fibrosis while simultaneously potentially influencing endocrine parameters, necessitating further investigation in future studies. Second, we lacked objective urological assessments such as prostate volume measurement and uroflowmetry, which could have strengthened the analysis of LUTS. Third, hormonal evaluations were conducted at a single time point, which may not fully capture circadian or longitudinal variations in endocrine status.

CONCLUSIONS

In the present study with a large sample, we clearly demonstrated that men’s health care symptoms, such as ED and LUTS, are closely associated with liver fibrosis as evaluated by the FIB-4 Index. The significant associations observed between the FIB-4 Index and both ED and LUTS underscore the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in evaluating men with these symptoms, including consideration of subclinical liver fibrosis.

Future prospective studies incorporating longitudinal hormonal profiling, imaging, and histological assessments are warranted to further elucidate causal pathways and therapeutic implications.
