New Treatment Strategy and Future Research Direction for BRAF ‐Mutated Cancer
Authors: Masanobu Takahashi, Sakura Hiraide Taniguchi, Yuya Yoshida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT BRAF is a key oncogene in various malignancies including malignant melanoma, thyroid cancer, colorectal cancer, and lung cancer. The protein it encodes is a constituent of the mitogen‐activated protein kinase pathway that regulates cell growth. Mutations, mainly BRAF V600E, that cause the activation of BRAF in turn induce MEK and ERK activation through their phosphorylation, which promotes the growth and survival of cancer cells. Treatment with BRAF inhibitor, and more recently, treatment with BRAF inhibitor plus MEK inhibitor was first developed in malignant melanoma. In 2022, treatment with BRAF inhibitor plus MEK inhibitor was approved tumor‐agnostically for the treatment of BRAF V600E‐mutated cancer, apart from colorectal cancer, in the USA. In colorectal cancer, the combination of a BRAF inhibitor and an anti‐EGFR antibody was developed in a later line setting, and more recently, the combination of a BRAF inhibitor, an anti‐EGFR antibody and chemotherapy was developed. Despite the recent advances of BRAF‐targeted therapies, intrinsic and/or acquired resistance to those therapies still needs to be overcome. We discuss the current status of the clinical development of BRAF‐targeted therapy, clinical findings on the resistance mechanism, and new possible treatment strategies for patients with BRAF‐mutated cancer.

Introduction

The Landscape of Molecular Genetics of BRAF

B‐Raf proto‐oncogene serine/threonine‐protein kinase (BRAF) protein is a key constituent of the mitogen‐activated protein kinase pathway (MAPK) [1, 2]. BRAF activating mutations, the main one of which is BRAF V600E, first activate BRAF, which results in the activation of mitogen‐activated protein kinase kinase (MEK) and extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK) through their phosphorylation, leading to greater growth and survival of cancer cells [3, 4]. Valine at residue 600 is located in an activating site of the BRAF protein and is well‐conserved in other RAF family proteins such as ARAF and CRAF and across various species [1].

In an unstimulated state, inactive RAF is localized to the cytosol in monomeric form [5]. When an extracellular ligand binds and receptor of tyrosine kinases (RTKs) is stimulated, RTK signaling is transduced by adaptor proteins such as SRC homology region 2 domain‐containing phosphatase 2 (SHP2), growth factor receptor‐bound protein 2 (GRB2), and GRB2‐associated binding protein 1 (GAB1), leading to the activation of RAS through activation of SOS family members [5] (Figure 1A). Active GTP‐bound RAS can form a complex with wild‐type BRAF, leading to BRAF dimerization [5, 6]. Activated RAF activates the downstream pathway via the phosphorylation of MEK1/2, and thus the phosphorylation of ERK1/2. ERK activation induces negative feedback upstream of RAF to prevent extreme activation of the signaling pathway [5].

Molecular mechanisms of activation of the ERK pathway through wild type and BRAF V600E, inhibition of the ERK pathway under use of BRAF Class I inhibitor, BRAF inhibition and BRAF/EGFR inhibition of the ERK pathway in colorectal cancer. (A) Under a normal state, an EGF ligand, shown as an inverted triangle, binds to EGFR, leading to activation of receptor tyrosine kinases through phosphorylation, shown as yellow circles. SOS, together with adaptor proteins SHP and GRB2, in turn activates RAS by inducing GDP‐GTP exchange, leading to the activation of downstream pathway including BRAF. Wild‐type BRAF proteins function as dimers, which mediates the pathway signaling through phosphorylating MEK, leading to phosphorylation of ERK. (B) In cells with a BRAF Class I mutation, BRAF V600, the mutant protein act as a monomer and activates ERK signaling in a RAS‐independent manner, leading to promoted the activation of downstream pathway. (C) In colorectal cancer, unlike non‐colorectal cancer, a Class I BRAF inhibitor alone is not enough to inhibit the ERK pathway activated by BRAF Class I mutations, because of the paradoxical activation of the ERK pathway via such as EGFR and CRAF. (D) In colorectal cancer, a combination of a Class I BRAF inhibitor, an anti‐EGFR antibody, and/or a MEK inhibitor can inhibit the ERK pathway activated by BRAF Class I mutations.

Depending on the way in which BRAF is activated, BRAF mutations have been categorized into three functional classes [7]. BRAF V600 mutations, including the most frequently detected mutation BRAF V600E, are classified as Class I mutations. BRAFV600 protein, the alteration of which is similar to the phosphorylation of T599 and S602 in the activation loop of the BRAF kinase domain, is constitutively active and acts as activated monomers [5]. In addition, the activation of BRAF kinase by V600 mutations is independent of RAS‐mediated signaling and insensitive to ERK‐mediated negative feedback of upstream RTKs and RAS adapter proteins [8, 9] (Figure 1B). Meanwhile, Class II mutations, including those mutated at G464, G469, L597, and K601, and some fusions, cause constitutively active protein activity whereby the protein is active in dimeric form [7]. Finally, Class III mutations, including those at G466, N581, D594, F595, and G596, impair kinase activity and activate ERK through tighter binding to RAS‐GTP and CRAF. This activation of signaling is RAS‐dependent and consistent with this, Class III mutations often co‐occur with RAS mutations or neurofibromin 1 (NF1) loss. These mutants have been shown to be sensitive to the inhibition of activated RAS [7].

Clinical Development of BRAF‐Targeted Therapy in Various BRAF ‐Mutated Cancer

BRAF mutations are observed in various malignancies including malignant melanoma (40%–50%), thyroid cancer (10%–70%), colorectal cancer (5%–10%), and lung cancers (1%–5%) [3, 10, 11, 12]. BRAF inhibitors were initially developed in malignant melanoma, as the cancer type in which BRAF mutations are most frequently observed (Table 1). A Class I BRAF inhibitor, vemurafenib, which can inhibit the monomeric form of BRAFV600E, was shown to improve overall survival (OS) and progression‐free survival (PFS) of patients with advanced malignant melanoma harboring the BRAF V600E mutation [13]. In this phase III trial, the vemurafenib arm showed an overall response rate (ORR) of 48%, compared with 5% in the dacarbazine arm [13]. In 2011, vemurafenib was approved by the US FDA for treating patients with unresectable and/or metastatic BRAF V600E‐mutated melanoma. Another phase III trial showed that the combination of BRAF and MEK inhibition by the Class I BRAF inhibitor dabrafenib and the MEK inhibitor trametinib resulted in better efficacy than dabrafenib alone in patients with melanoma (ORR: 76% vs. 54%) [14]. In 2014, dabrafenib plus trametinib was approved by the US FDA for the treatment of patients with unresectable and/or metastatic BRAF V600E/K‐mutated melanoma. The efficacy of adding a MEK inhibitor to a BRAF inhibitor was supported in subsequent phase III studies, namely, coBRIM [15] and COLUMBUS [16]. In the coBRIM trial, the vemurafenib plus a MEK inhibitor cobimetinib arm was found to have superior outcomes relative to the vemurafenib plus placebo arm (median PFS: 9.9 vs. 6.2 months) in patients with previously untreated unresectable or metastatic BRAF V600‐mutated melanoma [15]. Meanwhile, the COLUMBUS trial showed that a combination of a BRAF Class I inhibitor encorafenib and the MEK inhibitor binimetinib exerted better efficacy than vemurafenib alone (median PFS: 14.9 vs. 7.3 months; ORR: 63% vs. 40%) in BRAF V600‐mutated unresectable or metastatic melanoma [16]. These combinations were accordingly approved by the FDA. In 2018, dabrafenib plus trametinib was further approved by the US FDA for adjuvant treatment for resected BRAF‐mutated melanoma with lymph node involvement, according to the results of the COMBI‐AD study [17].

Following its use for malignant melanoma, BRAF inhibitor was next developed for the treatment of lung cancer. Specifically, in an open‐label phase II trial in 2016, Planchard et al. [18] showed that dabrafenib plus trametinib had a high ORR of 63% in previously treated patients with BRAF V600E‐mutated non‐small cell lung cancer. In previously untreated patients with lung cancer who received dabrafenib plus trametinib, an ORR of 64%, including a CR of 6%, was observed [19]. In 2017, the results of these studies led to the approval of dabrafenib and trametinib by the US FDA for the treatment of patients with metastatic BRAF V600E‐mutated non‐small cell lung cancer.

BRAF inhibitor and/or MEK inhibitor has also been developed as a treatment option for thyroid cancer. Brose et al. [20] reported an open‐label phase II study showing that the ORR of patients with BRAF V600E mutation positive papillary thyroid cancer refractory to radioactive iodine treated with vemurafenib was 38.5% (10/26). Moreover, Subbiah et al. [21] reported an open‐label phase II study in which an ORR of 69% (11/16) was observed in previously treated or untreated patients with BRAF V600E‐mutated anaplastic thyroid cancer who received dabrafenib plus trametinib. In 2018, dabrafenib plus trametinib was also approved by the US FDA for locally advanced or metastatic BRAF V600E‐mutated anaplastic thyroid cancer. Encorafenib plus binimetinib was also shown to be effective in a more recent phase II trial analyzing patients with locally advanced or metastatic BRAF V600‐mutated thyroid cancer who were refractory/intolerant to one or more previous vascular endothelial growth factor receptor‐targeted regimens or were considered ineligible for them, with ORR of 54.5% (12/22) [22].

Thereafter, a combination of BRAF and MEK inhibitors was developed as a tumor‐agnostic therapy. In the phase II ROAR trial, dabrafenib plus trametinib was shown to be effective among various BRAF‐mutated cancers including anaplastic thyroid cancer (ORR: 56%, 20/36 by the investigators' assessment), bile duct cancer (53%, 23/43), small intestine cancer (67%, 2/3), low‐grade glioma (54%, 7/13), high‐grade glioma (33%, 15/45), hairy cell leukemia (89%, 49/55), and multiple myeloma (50%, 5/10) [23]. In 2022, dabrafenib plus trametinib was approved by the US FDA for adult and pediatric patients aged ≥ 6 years with previously treated unresectable and/or metastatic BRAF V600E‐mutated solid tumors. The previous lines of evidence and the result of the ROAR study suggest that, whereas the efficacy of BRAF plus MEK inhibition may differ between various cancers to some extent, this treatment has sufficient efficacy clinically in the majority of cancers, with ORR of around 50%.

Clinical Development of BRAF‐Targeted Therapy in Colorectal Cancer

In terms of the successful development of treatments involving BRAF inhibition, colorectal cancer is an exception. In colorectal cancer, the antitumor activity by inhibition of BRAF alone, or even a combination of BRAF and MEK inhibition was limited. Kopetz et al. [24] performed a phase II study that showed RR of only 5% in patients with BRAF V600E‐mutated colorectal cancer who received vemurafenib monotherapy at second line or later. In addition, Prahallad et al. [25] found that epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) blockade had strong synergy with BRAF inhibition through an RNA‐interference‐based genetic screen in BRAF V600E‐mutated colorectal cancer cell lines. Another study showed that rapid ERK reactivation was induced through EGFR‐mediated activation of RAS and CRAF, after transient inhibition of phosphorylated ERK by vemurafenib [26]. Moreover, BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancers had increased expression levels of phosphorylated EGFR than BRAF‐mutated melanomas, supporting the assertion that colorectal cancers are likely to have EGFR‐mediated resistance when BRAF inhibition is applied (Figure 1C). It was also shown that both RAF and EGFR inhibition suppressed the reactivation of MAPK signaling in BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancer cells and markedly improved anti‐tumor efficacy by RAF and EGFR inhibition in vitro and in vivo [26]. Elsewhere, a phase II study by Corcoran et al. showed limited efficacy, with ORR of 12%, in patients with BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancer treated with dabrafenib and trametinib [27]. Conversely, in a phase IB study, Hong et al. showed that combination of vemurafenib, irinotecan, and cetuximab was effective, with ORR of 35% and median PFS of 7.7 months [28]. Moreover, van Cutsem et al. [29] reported their safety lead‐in results of the phase III BEACON colorectal cancer study and showed considerably high ORR of 48% in patients with BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancer treated with a triplet regimen of encorafenib, trametinib and cetuximab as the second or later line. They finally reported the result of the phase III study that showed ORR of 26% and median OS of 9.0 months in patients treated with this triplet regimen, compared with ORR of 2% and median OS of 5.4 months in controls treated with cetuximab and/or irinotecan/FOLFIRI [30]. In patients treated with a doublet regimen with encorafenib and cetuximab, its efficacy of ORR of 20% and median OS of 8.4 months was comparable to that of the triplet regimen [30], suggesting that MEK inhibition is not a key treatment option for inhibiting the MAPK pathway under both BRAF and EGFR inhibition (Figure 1D). In 2025, Elez et al. [31] showed that, in the BREAKWATER study, the combination of encorafenib, cetuximab, and mFOLFOX6 was superior to the standard care including bevacizumab and chemotherapy in untreated patients with BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancer, with ORR of 65.7% versus 37.4%, mPFS of 12.8 versus 7.1 months, mOS of 30.3 versus 15.1 months.

Molecular Characteristics of Cancer Cells Resistance to BRAF‐Targeted Therapy

Although treatment with BRAF inhibitor plus MEK inhibitor appears to be tumor‐agnostically effective for many various malignancies, some patients do not benefit from it because of internal resistance. Moreover, the vast majority of patients treated with BRAF inhibitor and/or MEK inhibitor eventually acquire resistance.

At present, melanoma is the most well‐studied malignancy in terms of the mechanisms by which resistance to BRAF inhibitor and/or MEK inhibitor develops. The molecular mechanism underlying resistance to Class I inhibitors mainly involves alterations leading to RAF dimer formation, which reactivates MAPK pathway signaling. The known acquired resistance alterations include NRAS mutations [32], amplification or overexpression of BRAF [33], alternative splicing of BRAF [34], KRAS mutations [35], overexpression of CRAF [36], and NF1 loss [37]. For example, of 44 patients with melanoma treated with vemurafenib or dabrafenib, alterations that induce reactivation of MAPK pathway including RAS mutations, BRAF amplifications, alternative splicing of BRAF, and MAP2K1 mutations, were observed in 70%, and alterations that induce phosphatidylinositol 3‐kinase (PI3K)‐AKT pathway activation including AKT1/3 mutations, PIK3CA mutations, and PTEN mutations were observed in 22% [38]. Hugo et al. [39] demonstrated that in patients with melanoma treated with BRAF inhibitor alone or BRAF inhibitor plus MEK inhibitor, alterations in the methylome or transcriptome, such as alterations in C‐MET, LEF1, or YAP1 expression, in addition to genetic alterations, can be molecular mechanisms behind the acquisition of resistance to BRAF‐inhibition‐based therapy. Conversely, based on evidence that secondary acquired mutations in BRAF itself such as BRAF L505H or BRAF L514V are quite rare [40, 41], rechallenge of dabrafenib and trametinib was attempted in a phase II study [42]. Among 25 patients with BRAF V600‐mutated melanoma previously treated with BRAF inhibitor and/or MEK inhibitor, rechallenge of the therapy, with an interval of at least 12 weeks, showed clinical benefit with ORR of 32% [42]. The efficacy of rechallenge of BRAF inhibitor plus MEK inhibitor in melanoma was also supported by the finding of a meta‐analysis including this study that ORR of 47% among a total of 238 patients [43]. Moreover, data from the Dutch Melanoma Treatment Registry on the rechallenge efficacy, showed an ORR of 43% among 468 patients with melanoma [44]. A more recent study, the real‐world Spanish Melanoma Registry (GEM‐1801) further supported the clinical benefit of rechallenge in a real‐world setting by showing ORR of 31.6% and survival benefit in patients with ECOG PS of ≤ 1 [45].

Given the lower efficacy, that is, the higher intrinsic resistance rate, from BRAF inhibitor plus MEK inhibitor in colorectal cancer compared with those in other malignancies including melanoma, colorectal cancer is likely to have more complex molecular heterogeneity. Barras et al. classified BRAF V600E mutant type into two molecular subtypes based on gene expression profiles [46]. They performed unsupervised clustering using non‐negative matrix factorization to identify gene expression–based subgroups. BRAF V600E–mutated colorectal cancer was categorized into BM1 and BM2. BM1 is characterized by KRAS/AKT pathway activation, mTOR/4EBP alteration, and epithelial‐mesenchymal transition, while BM2 is characterized by important deregulation of the cell cycle [46]. Compared with BM2, BM1 is associated with better efficacy, with ORR of 38% versus 7%, in patients with colorectal cancer treated with dabrafenib, trametinib, and panitumumab [47]. Elsewhere, Huijberts et al. analyzed 37 patients with colorectal cancer who received BRAF inhibitor, cetuximab and/or MEK inhibitor. They found that among 16 patients with resistance to the BRAF inhibitor‐containing regimen, 6 had acquired alterations of PI3K pathway proteins, while 4 had acquired alterations in RTKs or their ligands such as ERBB or FGF [48].

In a more recent exploratory biomarker analysis of the BEACON trial using whole‐exome, whole‐transcriptome tissue sequencing and ctDNA genomic findings [49], tumors with higher immune signatures showed a trend toward improved benefit in terms of OS with encorafenib, binimetinib plus cetuximab. In this analysis, the benefit to OS with encorafenib plus cetuximab or encorafenib, binimetinib plus cetuximab, compared with chemotherapy, was observed irrespective of the BM subtype [49]. As acquired alterations, RAS, MAP2K1, and MET alterations were most commonly observed, particularly in patients with a high baseline cell‐cycle gene signature [49].

In addition to gene mutation, gene expression, and methylation profile, alterations in noncoding RNA have been shown to be associated with BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancer. MicroRNAs (miRNAs), small noncoding RNAs of 19–25 nucleotides in length, inhibit gene expression by binding to complementary sequences in the 3′‐untranslated region of the target mRNAs. Many miRNAs are involved in tumor growth, migration, and sensitivity to certain anticancer drugs [50, 51]. Some studies have focused on miRNA alterations in BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancer [52, 53]. Through miRNA array analysis, miRNA‐31 was identified as the most upregulated miRNA in BRAF V600E‐mutated colorectal cancer compared with colorectal cancer harboring wild‐type BRAF [52]. Upregulated miRNA‐31 expression was also found to be associated with cancer‐specific mortality [52]. miRNA‐373 expression was significantly downregulated and miRNA‐31 expression was upregulated in BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancer tissues, among 84 miRNAs [53].

In addition, we identified miRNA‐193a‐3p as one of the most altered miRNAs in BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancer and found that miRNA‐193a‐3p acts as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting cell growth and apoptosis [54]. In addition, the tumor suppressor function was further supported by our findings that miRNA‐193a‐3p overexpression altered the phosphorylation levels of many proteins involved in various oncogenic pathways including MAPK‐related ones [55]. Furthermore, miRNA‐193a‐3p overexpression in BRAF‐mutated cells was shown to enhance the cytotoxicity of BRAF/MEK inhibition by inhibiting the reactivation of MAPK signaling and inducing Mcl1 inhibition [55]. Inhibition of Mcl1 by siRNA or by Mcl1 inhibitor enhanced the antitumor effects of BRAF inhibitor, MEK inhibitor, and anti‐EGFR antibody [55].

New BRAF‐Related Therapeutic Strategies in Cancer

New various inhibitors of the proteins involved in the ERK‐related pathway are currently being developed. To overcome the intrinsic and/or acquired resistance by BRAF/MEK and/or EGFR inhibitors in BRAF‐mutated cancer, some of them can be candidates for improving benefit from the current BRAF‐targeted therapy. The candidate inhibitors include ones of RTKs such as insulin‐like growth factor I receptor (IGF1R) [56] or MET [57], RAS‐related proteins of SHP [58, 59] or SOS [60], and MEK/ERK [61].

By the genome‐wide screening using a CRISPR‐CAS9 system, we identified MET, GRB2, SHOC2, PTPN11, FURIN, and GAB1 as genes of which depletion synergized the effect of the BRAF and MEK inhibitors in colorectal cancer cell lines (Numakura R., Takahashi M., Taniguchi S., Yoshida Y., Ishioka C., and Kawakami H., unpublished data). All these genes are involved in RTKs or the ERK pathway, which is in line with the evidence about the currently being developed inhibitors. An inhibitor of SHP2, encoded by PTPN11, SHP099 synergistically enhanced the antiproliferative effect, induced apoptosis, and deactivated the ERK downstream pathways. Johnson et al. [62] have recently evaluated the efficacy and safety of a SHP2 inhibitor migoprotafib in a total of 56 patients with advanced cancer without KRAS, BRAF, or MAP2K1 mutations. Migoprotafib showed modest efficacy with stable disease in 10 patients (18%), which suggests that a combination of a SHP2 inhibitor and other EGFR‐MAPK inhibitors may exert better efficacy. Drilon et al. [63] showed that an SHP2 inhibitor, PF‐07284892, enhanced the antitumor efficacy of encorafenib and binimetinib in a BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancer xenograft mouse model. They also reported that in a BRAF V600E‐mutated colorectal patient previously treated with four lines including a combination of encorafenib and cetuximab, PF‐07284892 monotherapy was ineffective; however, the addition of PF‐07284892 to reuse of encorafenib and cetuximab resulted in 32% tumor regression. Although the result has to be validated in future larger studies, SHP2 inhibition could be one of the promising therapeutic strategies in BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancer [63]. Ayanlaja et al. [64] have shown that combined inhibition of SHP2 and BRAF overcomes resistance to type 1 BRAF inhibitor in BRAF V600E‐mutated high‐grade glioma using in vitro and in vivo model. These findings further support the notion that SHP2 inhibition together with BRAF inhibition is one of the promising strategies in BRAF‐mutated cancer, not just colorectal cancer. In addition, we found that simultaneous silencing of the two candidate genes enhanced the synergized effect more strongly than silencing of a single candidate gene (Numakura R., Takahashi M., Taniguchi S., Yoshida Y., Ishioka C., and Kawakami H., unpublished data). Targeting multiple genes involved in the RTK signaling pathway could be a promising strategy for not just colorectal cancer that is relatively more resistant to BRAF inhibitors, but also BRAF‐mutated other cancers.

Several recent studies have developed a new generation of BRAF inhibitors, and some of them have been tried in early clinical trials. Yaeger et al. [65] have developed PF‐07799933, which is a brain‐penetrant, selective, pan‐mutant BRAF inhibitor. They showed that in first‐in‐human trials analyzing patients with BRAF‐mutated solid tumors refractory to approved RAF inhibitors, PF‐07799933 ± binimetinib was well‐tolerated, and multiple confirmed responses were observed including brain lesions [65]. Manabe et al. [66] have more recently reported that a pan‐RAF inhibitor exarafenib was effective in two patients with BRAF‐class II‐mutant (TMEM106B‐BRAF fusion or BRAF G469S) non‐small cell lung cancer. Vieito et al. have performed a phase Ia/b trial analyzing patients with BRAF V600‐mutated solid tumors and reported that mosperafenib, a next‐generation BRAF inhibitor demonstrating paradox breaking by evading MAPK pathway activation in BRAF‐wildtype cells, exerted a favorable safety profile and meaningful activity, with an overall response rate of 24%, in patients including those who had previously been treated with BRAF inhibitors [67].

In May 2025, a treatment with a RAF/MEK clamp avutometinib and a selective FAK inhibitor defactinib was approved for treating patients with recurrent KRAS‐mutated low grade serous ovarian cancer in FDA [68]. Some patients with BRAF V600E‐mutated melanomas resistant to inhibition of BRAF and MEK show activation of focal adhesion signaling [69]. Lubrano et al. have shown that FAKi plus avutometinib overcomes resistance in multiple models derived from BRAFi plus MEKi‐resistant melanoma patients and immunotherapy‐resistant syngeneic mouse models, which could be one of future promising strategies for treating patients with BRAF‐mutated tumors who are resistant to a classical BRAF inhibitor and/or a MEK inhibitor [69].

As building another strategy for developing new strategies for BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancer, we screened a library of chemical compounds that had been developed in other diseases and identified tretinoin, a retinoid, as a compound that synergistically enhances the cytotoxicity of BRAF and MEK inhibitors with or without EGFR inhibition in BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancer cells in vitro and in vivo [70]. Retinoids including tretinoin, tamibarotene, and bexarotene are currently used for treatments of hematological malignancies such as acute promyelocytic leukemia and cutaneous T cell lymphoma by inducing cellular differentiation [71, 72]. In addition to inducing cellular differentiation, retinoids mediate cell cycle arrest and apoptosis and affect metabolic plasticity, epithelial‐mesenchymal transition, and angiogenesis in malignant cells through binding to members of two nuclear receptor families, the retinoic acid receptors RARα, RARβ, and RARγ, and the retinoid X receptors RXRα, RXRβ, and RXRγ [73, 74]. RARs form heterodimers with RXRs, while RXRs form heterodimers with various nuclear receptor family members. Several clinical studies have explored the therapeutic potential of retinoids as a single agent or a combination with other drugs in solid tumors including colorectal cancer [74, 75, 76], a promising efficacy has not been established yet, and few large randomized studies have not been tried. These evidence suggest that retinoids are not likely to exert clinically meaningful efficacy widely to solid tumors, but possibly do to some specific molecular features of solid tumors.

In our study, we found that the synergized cytotoxicity of retinoids and BRAF/MEK inhibitors was exerted at least in part through retinoic acid RARα and/or RXRα in BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancer cells [70]. Specifically, the increase in cleaved poly(ADP‐ribose) polymerase (PARP) expression by the addition of tretinoin to the BRAF inhibition therapy was canceled when RARα or RXRα was silenced [70]. Although the more detailed molecular mechanisms underlying the synergism of retinoid and BRAF/MEK inhibitors in cytotoxicity against colorectal cancer cells need to be clarified in future studies, our findings provide a rationale for developing retinoids as a new treatment option to overcome resistance to BRAF inhibition‐based therapy for patients with BRAF V600E colorectal cancer and possibly other BRAF V600E malignancies. Based on the results of the BREAKWATER trial, a combination of encorafenib, cetuximab, and chemotherapy is likely to become the standard treatment option in the first‐line setting for patients with BRAF V600E colorectal cancer. We are now preparing to initiate a clinical phase I study to find optimal doses of retinoids in combination with BRAF inhibition‐based therapy in the later‐line setting [31] (Figure 2). In the second line or later therapy, treatment with the BEACON regimen, encorafenib, binimetinib, and cetuximab, can currently be considered as a standard regimen for treating patients with BRAF‐mutated colorectal cancer. We are planning the phase I/II trial to analyze the optimal dose and efficacy for a retinoid with a concurrent use of combination with encorafenib, binimetinib, and cetuximab.

The candidates of new therapeutic molecular targets, such as SOS, GRB2, SHP, RAS, and retinoids, for BRAF‐mutated cancer. The use of retinoids, together with the α inhibitor that targets molecular targets involved in the pathway consisting of SOS, GRB2, SHP2, or RAS, possibly enhances anti‐tumor efficacy resulting from the BRAF/MEK‐inhibition‐based therapy in BRAF‐mutated cancer.

Conclusion

BRAF inhibition‐based therapy has achieved great successes in BRAF V600E‐mutated cancer. The combination of dabrafenib and trametinib is tumor‐agnostically a standard therapy for patients with BRAF V600E‐mutated cancers except for colorectal cancer, and the combination of encorafenib and cetuximab, with or without chemotherapy, is a standard treatment option for patients with BRAF V600E‐mutated colorectal cancer. Despite the considerable effect of the BRAF‐targeted therapy, intrinsic and/or acquired resistance remains a large hurdle clinically to further prolong the survival of patients with BRAF V600E‐mutated cancer. Future studies that clarify the molecular mechanism underlying resistance to BRAF‐targeted therapy and that develop new drug compounds and/or new combination therapies of new and/or pre‐existing drugs could help patients with BRAF‐mutated cancers to benefit more from the targeted therapy.
