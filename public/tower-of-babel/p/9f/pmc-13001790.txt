The effect of perioperative intravenous lidocaine infusion on postoperative sleep of elderly patients with colorectal cancer: A randomized controlled study
Authors: Xiaomei Wang, Liqin Deng, Yanan Wu, Lu Wang, Yuxue Qiu, Haitao Hou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative sleep disturbance (POSD) caused by postoperative pain can impede the enhanced rapid recovery of elderly patients with colorectal cancer. We propose that perioperative intravenous lidocaine infusion may enhance postoperative sleep quality by alleviating postoperative pain in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer.MethodsSixty-three elderly patients were divided into two groups: the lidocaine group (group L) and the placebo group (group C). Patients in group L received intravenous injection of lidocaine 1.5 mg/kg during the induction of anesthesia, followed by lidocaine 1.0 mg kg−1 h−1 during surgery and for 24 h after surgery. Patients in group C received the same volume of normal saline. The primary outcome was postoperative sleep function, which was evaluated using the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS). The secondary outcomes included the pain-at-rest and pain-on-movement of patients at 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after surgery, opioid and propofol consumption, bowel function recovery time, postoperative complications, and lidocaine side effects.ResultsCompared to group C, AIS scores in group L were significantly lower on day 1 (12.9 ± 4.0 vs 10.6 ± 3.9, P < 0.05), day 3 (8.6 ± 4.2 vs 6.2 ± 3.9, P < 0.05), and 1 week (4.3 ± 3.1 vs 2.2 ± 2.5, P < 0.05) after surgery. There were no significant differences in VAS scores between the two groups. The intraoperative consumption of propofol and remifentanil was significantly reduced in group L. Lidocaine infusion significantly shortened the recovery time of bowel function. There were no statistical differences between the two groups regarding the incidences of PONV and postoperative pulmonary complications. No cases of local anesthetic toxicity occurred in either group.ConclusionsPerioperative intravenous administration of lidocaine can facilitate the restoration of postoperative sleep function in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection of colorectal cancer.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is a prevalent malignancy, ranking third and second in terms of incidence and fatality rates globally in 2018,1 and its incidence increases with age.2 Patients with colorectal cancer receive comprehensive treatment that includes radical resection of colorectal cancer. Studies have shown that postoperative pain is an independent risk factor for postoperative sleep disorder.3 In addition, the incidence of sleep disorders increases with age, and the average age of colorectal cancer patients is 67 years old.4 A negative correlation has been demonstrated between age and sleep duration, which gradually decreases with age (especially in REM sleep).5 Therefore, effective management of postoperative pain is crucial for elderly patients undergoing radical resection for colorectal cancer.

Currently, opioids are the main drugs used to manage acute postoperative pain. However, they have been shown to have varying degrees of impact on postoperative sleep function6 and the speed7 of patient recovery. Recently, several studies have confirmed that perioperative intravenous lidocaine can significantly reduce postoperative pain and consumption of perioperative opioid.8, 9, 10, 11 In some cases, perioperative intravenous lidocaine can produce the same analgesic effect as epidural analgesia.12 However, it is still unclear whether perioperative intravenous lidocaine infusion can improve the postoperative sleep of elderly patients by relieving postoperative pain and reducing opioids consumption.

The present study aims to investigate the effect of perioperative intravenous lidocaine infusion on postoperative sleep quality in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer. The findings of this study may provide a reference for the promoting the prompt postoperative recovery of these patients.

Materials and methods

This single-center randomized controlled trial was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (ChiCTR2000038184). The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University (approval number 2020–312), and all patients provided informed consent prior to enrollment.

Patient recruitment

Elderly patients between the ages of 65 and 80, with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status of 2–3 and Body Mass Index (BMI) values of 20–30 kg/m2, who were scheduled for elective laparoscopic colorectal radical resection at our hospital between October 2020 and April 2021, were recruited in this study. Exclusion criteria consisted of allergy to local anesthetics, severe liver disease (aspartate aminotransferase, alanine transaminase, or bilirubin >2.5 times the upper limit of normal), renal impairment (creatinine clearance <60 mL·min−1), cardiac rhythm disorders or systolic heart failure (second- and third-degree heart block and ejection fraction <50 %), neurological or mental disorders, chronic opioid and psychotic use, and cognitive dysfunction.

Randomization, allocation, and concealment

Patients were randomly assigned to either the lidocaine group (group L) or the placebo group (group C) in a 1:1 ratio using computer-generated random numbers. Allocation concealment was achieved by enclosing the assignments in sealed, opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes, which were opened only after the patient arrived in the operating theatre. Drugs and the patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) pump were prepared by an independent researcher not involved in the treatment or assessment of the outcomes according to the group allocation. A 10 mL syringe containing lidocaine solution 2 % based on the patients’ ideal body weight of the patient (1.5 mg·kg−1) or an equal amount of saline 0.9 % was used for the bolus injection. Meanwhile, a 20 mL syringe containing either lidocaine 2 % (1.0 mg·kg−1·h−1) or saline solution 0.9 % was used for continuous infusion. An independent anesthesiologist drew blood (or sham handle) during the operation, and the postoperative blood sample was integrated with routine blood examinations. During intraoperative blood collection, anesthesiologists were requested to step aside for ∼3 min to avoid their awareness of the allocation. Blinding of research personnel was maintained throughout the observation period until adverse events that were evident of local anesthetic systemic toxicity were observed by anesthesiologists or assessors.

The day prior to surgery, the subjects were given instructions on the proper usage of a PCIA pump and how to accurately report their pain intensity.

Study intervention

Group L

The subjects received an intravenous bolus of lidocaine at a dose of 1.5 mg·kg−1·h−1 (calculated of ideal body weight, and infused over a period of 10 min) during the induction of anesthesia, with continuous infusion at 1.0 mg·kg−1·h−1 until 24 h of surgery. Then, PCIA pump should not be used.

Group C

The subjects received equivalent volumes of perioperative saline. Administer PCIA analgesia. The postoperative PCIA pump contained sufentanil (1.5–1.8 µg·kg−1) in saline solution 0.9 % diluted to a total volume of 100 mL. The background infusion rate of the PCIA pump was 2 mL·h−1, a bolus volume of 2 mL for each PCIA press, and a lockout interval of 15 min.

Anesthesia and perioperative treatment

Upon the patient’s arrival in the operating room, the electrocardiogram, arterial blood pressure, heart rate, oxygen saturation (SpO2), and respiratory rate (RR) were monitored, upper limb vein and right internal jugular vein accesses were established, and total intravenous anesthesia was carried out. The induction of anesthesia was performed with 0.02 mg/kg of midazolam, 0.3–0.5 μg/kg of sufentanil, 0.2–0.4 mg/kg of etomidate, and 0.06 mg/kg of rocuronium. Tracheal intubation was performed after 3 min, and mechanical ventilation was carried out after confirming the position of the tracheal tube. Ventilator parameters: tidal volume (VT), 6–8 mL/kg; RR, 12–16 times/min; inspiration: expiration (I: E), 1:2; fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2), 60 %; positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), 5 cmH2O; and carbon dioxide end-tidal partial pressure (PETCO2), 35–45 mmHg. Anesthesia was maintained by continuous infusion of propofol (4–6 mg·kg−1·h−1) and remifentanil (0.05–0.2 μg·kg−1·min−1), and 10–20 mg·h−1 of rocuronium was administered intermittently during surgery to maintain the bispectral index (BIS) value between 40–60. Muscle relaxants were discontinued 30 min before the end of the surgery, and medication for the maintenance of general anesthesia was ceased at the conclusion of the procedure. After the patient regained consciousness and protective reflexes, VT was > 6 mL/kg, RR was 12–20 times/min, and SpO2 was > 95 %. The tracheal tube was removed and the patient was sent to post anesthesia care unit (PACU). Perioperative pain management: Parecoxib sodium 40 mg was intravenously injected 30 min before the operation. Subsequently, 0.75 % ropivacaine was injected around the incision after the operation. Parecoxib sodium was administered every 12 h after surgery for postoperative analgesia. Patients with postoperative pain scores of > 3 points were given an intramuscular injection of diclofenac sodium 75 mg for remedial analgesia and those with moderate to severe nausea or vomiting were given tropisetron 2 mg intravenously. Iatrogenic interference was minimized during the night in the ward to ensure adequate rest.13

Study outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was to assess the postoperative sleep function. The AIS was utilized to evaluate the sleep status of patients in both groups on days 1 and day 3, as well as 1 week after the operation. The AIS scale comprises eight items, namely sleep induction, awakening during the night, final awakening earlier than desired, total sleep duration, overall sleep quality, well-being, functioning capacity, and sleepiness during the day. Each item was recorded on a scale of 0 to 3 based on sleep performance (0: very good, 1: good, 2: poor, 3: very poor). The total AIS score (range 0–24 points) was obtained by adding the scores above. An AIS score of > 6 indicates a sleep disorder, < 4 indicates no sleep disorder, and 4–6 indicates a suspected sleep disorder.14

The secondary outcomes included: (1) VAS score: The VAS was utilized to evaluate the pain-at-rest and pain-on-movement of patients at 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after surgery (0: no pain; 1–3: mild pain; 4–6: moderate pain; 7–10: severe pain). (2) Intraoperative consumption of opioids and propofol, the time to recovery of bowel function, and number of rescued analgesia 24 h after surgery. (3) Postoperative complications, including postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), respiratory and circulatory system complications, and intestinal obstruction. (4) Preoperative mental state, as assessed by the Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA) and Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD). These scales were used to evaluate the changes of anxiety and depression before surgery, with scores of 29 indicating severe anxiety and depression, 21∼29 indicating obvious symptoms of anxiety and depression, 14∼21 indicating anxiety and depression, 7∼14 indicating possible but not serious anxiety and depression, and < 7 indicating good living conditions15; (5) Side effects of lidocaine, such as dizziness, drowsiness, tinnitus, and convulsions.

Statistical analysis

The incidence of POSD was selected as the main observation indicator. Our preliminary experiment showed that intravenous lidocaine infusion improved postoperative sleep function, resulting in a POSD incidence of 71 % and 35 % for group C and group L respectively (α = 0.05, 1-β = 0.8, two-tailed test). Power Analysis & Sample Size (PASS) 15 software was used to calculate the sample size, N1 = N2, with at least 27 cases required for each group. Accounting for an expected dropout rate of 15 %; 31 cases were ultimately included in each group.

SPSS 20.0 software was used for statistical analysis. Normally distributed measurement data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x¯ ± s) and analyzed using the t-test. Skewed measurement data were expressed as the median and interquartile range [M (IQR)] and analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Count data were presented as the number of cases or percentage (%) and analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The results of the present study demonstrated that perioperative intravenous lidocaine was as effective as a postoperative intravenous analgesia pump. This approach improves early postoperative sleep quality and facilitates the restoration of sleep function in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer.

Various factors such as patient characteristics, anesthesia and surgical factors, postoperative pain, environmental factors, and psychological factors can affect the postoperative sleep quality in patients undergoing surgery. Therefore, the present study only included elderly patients, and the general conditions and preoperative comorbidities of patients in both group L and group C were comparable. Patients in the two groups were treated by the same anesthesiologists and surgeons, and anesthesia management and surgical procedures for patients in the two groups were comparable. Patients with severe neurological, mental, and psychological disorders were excluded before surgery. Iatrogenic interference was minimized at night in the ward to ensure adequate rest. The sleep quality of patients was evaluated using AIS, which has been shown to have a reliability and validity of 89 % and 90 % respectively.14

In terms of the dosage of lidocaine, our medication regimen adheres to the international expert consensus on intravenous lidocaine infusion.16 Studies have shown that perioperative continuous infusion of lidocaine has the highest incidence of adverse reactions affecting the nervous system of patients.17 Therefore, we have implemented a continuous infusion of low-dose lidocaine. Additionally, we have closely monitored the plasma concentrations of lidocaine and the levels of plasma lidocaine were lower than the reported toxic dose level of lidocaine (5 μg/mL) which were in the safe dose range, and thus no side effects were observed in patients in group L.

Furthermore, our findings shows that intravenous lidocaine infusion improved the early postoperative sleep quality of elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection for colorectal cancer and facilitates the recovery of postoperative sleep function. The may be attributed to the effective relief of postoperative pain-at-rest and pain-on-movement through perioperative intravenous administration of lidocaine. Previous studies have demonstrated that the postoperative analgesic effect of intravenous lidocaine infusion is satisfactory in patients with abdominal surgery and is consistent with epidural analgesia and intravenous patient-controlled analgesia.12, 18, 19 Pain and sleep are interrelated and can exacerbate each other. The present study also confirms that both pain-at-rest and pain-on-movement were effectively controlled in both patient groups. Intravenous lidocaine infusion improves postoperative sleep by reducing opioids. Recent studies have shown that perioperative intravenous lidocaine infusion significantly reduces the demand for opioids during abdominal surgery.12, 20, 21, 22 Opioids have a wide range of physiological effects, including significant analgesia23 and sleep disturbance,24, 25 suggesting that opioids can affect sleep while reducing pain and even exaggerate POSD.26 In addition, the improvement of postoperative sleep in group L is also related to the anti-inflammatory effect of lidocaine. Studies have demonstrated that increased levels of inflammatory factors are closely related to the occurrence of sleep disturbance.27 Ortiz et al.28 found that intravenous lidocaine infusion reduced interleukin-1 (IL-1), IL-6, interferon-gamma, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, suggesting that lidocaine has a significant anti-inflammatory effect. Therefore, intravenous lidocaine infusion during the perioperative period can also improve postoperative sleep of patients through its anti-inflammatory effect.

In this study, we found that perioperative intravenous lidocaine infusion was equally effective as the postoperative intravenous analgesia pump in reducing intraoperative opioid consumption. However, there were significant differences observed between the two groups in the VAS scores at rest and on movement 2 h after surgery. This difference could be attributed to the possibility that remifentanil may induce hyperalgesia in the control group for postoperative analgesia, and there is no correlation between intraoperative and postoperative analgesia. Studies have shown that pain sensitization caused by remifentanil infusion can be inhibited by intravenous lidocaine,29 which could explain the lower VAS scores at rest and on movement 2 h after surgery in the lidocaine group compared with the placebo group.

Overall, the present study has demonstrated that intravenous lidocaine infusion can significantly improve postoperative sleep in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection of colorectal cancer. However, this study has several limitations. (1) The study protocol was changed after the audit began. One of the inclusion criteria was changed from age 45–65 years old to age 65–80 years old, which differs from the registered information at Chinese clinical trials.gov. (2) Since AIS assessment of sleep quality may be affected by subjective factors, combining it with polysomnography to objectively assess the perioperative sleep quality of the patients may yield more reliable results. However, polysomnography is currently unavailable for postoperative patients in our hospital. (3) This study did not thoroughly explore the specific mechanism by which intravenous lidocaine infusion improves postoperative sleep, warranting further investigation in the future. (4) This is a single-center study with a small sample size; therefore, large-scale multi-center studies should be conducted to validate our findings.

Conclusions

In summary, perioperative intravenous lidocaine infusion enhances the early postoperative sleep quality and facilitates the recovery of postoperative sleep function in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic radical resection of colorectal cancer by providing effective postoperative analgesia and reducing the opioid dosage.
