Chronic hypoxia induces liver injury via PKCδ/ASMase-driven ceramide accumulation and endoplasmic reticulum stress in rats
Authors: Yujie Huang, Yu Ding, Xiaoyu Guo, Lingfang Huang, Xiangqiong Meng, Wenjun Jiang, Zejun Wang, Tian Yang, Minghai Zheng, Xiaoling Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and aimChronic continuous hypoxia imposes a huge physiological burden and impacts human health. The liver is critical for maintaining whole-body homeostasis. Although accumulating evidence has linked hypoxia to liver damage, the underlying mechanisms are poorly understood. This study investigated the effects of chronic continuous hypoxia on the liver and the underlying mechanisms.MethodsRats were exposed to 10% O₂ for 6 weeks. Treatment groups received the acid sphingomyelinase (ASMase) inhibitor desipramine (Des), the protein kinase C delta (PKCδ) inhibitor rottlerin, or the endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) inhibitor 4-Phenylbutyric Acid (4-PBA). The analyses included liver histopathology (Hematoxylin and Eosin staining), ultrastructural observations, serum liver function indicators, serum and hepatic lipidomics, enzyme activities, and ERS markers.ResultsChronic hypoxia altered liver and serum lipid profiles, with hepatic ceramide (Cer) levels showing the most significant increase. Hypoxia increased hepatic Cer levels via ASMase activation, and the ASMase inhibitor Des reduced the serum levels of liver function indicators and ameliorated hepatic histological abnormalities in hypoxic rats. Rottlerin inhibited ASMase activity and ameliorated hypoxia-induced liver injury. Des blocked hypoxia-induced hepatic ERS, and 4-PBA alleviated hypoxic liver injury.ConclusionChronic continuous hypoxia caused liver damage, partially through Cer-mediated ERS via activation of the PKCδ/ASMase axis. Therefore, Cer might be a promising diagnostic biomarker for liver injury in high-altitude residents and patients with cardiopulmonary diseases. Targeting the PKCδ/ASMase/Cer axis represents a potential therapeutic strategy for mitigating hypoxic liver injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02761-w.

Introduction

Oxygen is crucial for survival of aerobic organisms. Hypoxia, which is a reduction in the level of available oxygen to cells, tissues, or organs, poses a significant threat to human health. Hypoxia can be classified as acute or chronic and continuous or intermittent depending on the duration (acute or chronic) and pattern of exposure (continuous or intermittent). Chronic continuous hypoxic exposure can cause multiple organ dysfunction and promote the onset and progression of various diseases. Chronic continuous hypoxia occurs in populations living at high altitudes and in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diffuse interstitial fibrosis, cystic fibrosis, congestive heart failure, or severe anemia [1, 2]. Hence, a detailed understanding of the effects of chronic continuous hypoxia and the underlying mechanisms is important for improving the health of high-altitude residents and patients with cardiopulmonary diseases.

The liver is a hub for metabolism, detoxification, thermogenesis, and synthesis of coagulation factors. These processes require large amounts of oxygen; thus, the liver is extremely susceptible to hypoxia. Previous studies on the effects of chronic hypoxia on the liver have mainly focused on intermittent hypoxia, such as obstructive sleep apnea. Both population-based studies and animal experiments have shown that chronic intermittent hypoxia contributes to the development and progression of liver diseases [3–5]. Although animal studies have shown that chronic continuous hypoxia can also cause liver damage [6–8], the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood.

Recent studies demonstrated that many aspects of lipid metabolism are altered under hypoxia [9–11]. The liver plays a central role in the maintenance of lipid homeostasis. Disruption of lipid homeostasis in hepatocytes leads to alterations in the levels of several lipid classes, including glycerophospholipids, glycerolipids, sphingolipids, fatty acyls, sterol lipids, and prenol lipids. In particular, the accumulation of lipotoxic lipid subclasses, such as saturated fatty acids, ceramide (Cer), diacylglycerol (DG), lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), and lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE), induces lipotoxicity, which can cause cellular damage and cell death [12]. In this study, a comprehensive, untargeted lipidomic analysis was conducted to evaluate the effects of chronic hypoxia on lipid homeostasis in the liver. The analysis of specific lipid classes suggested that Cer levels exhibited the strongest dysregulation in the liver following hypoxic exposure. In contrast, the hepatic levels of other lipotoxic lipids, including DG, LPC, and LPE, remained unaffected. Hypoxia also notably increased serum levels of Cer. Consequently, this study focused on Cer and its metabolism to elucidate its role in hypoxia-induced liver damage. Cer, a bioactive sphingolipid with a sphingosine backbone linked to a fatty acid chain, functions as a key mediator of cellular stress responses [13]. Although accumulating evidence has implicated Cer in lipotoxicity and underscored its significance in the onset and progression of multiple liver diseases [14], its specific role in hypoxia-induced liver damage remains largely unexplored.

Further analysis of the activity of Cer-related metabolic enzymes in this study indicated a key role for acid sphingomyelinase (ASMase) activation in hypoxia-mediated Cer accumulation. Protein kinase c delta (PKCδ) is essential for ASMase activation in breast cancer and myeloma cells [15, 16]. The preliminary experiments of this study demonstrated that hypoxia significantly increased phosphorylated PKCδ (p-PKCδ) levels in the liver. These findings led us to hypothesize that chronic hypoxia induces liver injury via the PKCδ/ASMase pathway-driven Cer accumulation. The results of this study provided the first experimental evidence establishing the PKCδ/ASMase/Cer axis as a novel pathogenic mechanism underlying hypoxia-induced liver injury. These findings highlighted therapeutic targeting of this lipid signaling pathway as a potential new strategy for alleviating liver damage under hypoxic conditions.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

Desipramine (Des), an antidepressant and ASMase inhibitor, was obtained from TargetMol (Boston, USA). Rottlerin, a natural polyphenolic compound and selective PKCδ inhibitor, was purchased from Bioss (Woburn, MA, USA). 4-Phenylbutyric Acid (4-PBA), an aromatic fatty acid and endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) inhibitor, was obtained from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Antibodies against PKCδ (ab182126), sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase 2 (SERCA2) (ab 150435) and protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) (ab229912) were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Antibodies against p-PERK (Thr982) (DF7576), inositol-requiring enzyme-1 (IRE1) (DF7709), p-IRE1 (Ser724) (AF7150), and C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) (AF5280) were purchased from Affinity Biosciences (Jiangsu, China). Antibody against p-PKCδ (Thr505) (93292) was obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA), whereas the antibody against Actin (AA128) was purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China).

Animal studies

Adult male Sprague-Dawley rats weighing 250–270 g were purchased from the animal center of the Third Military Medical University (Chongqing, China). The animal experiments were performed according to institutional guidelines and were accepted by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Third Military Medical University (No. SYXC-2017-0002). The rats were kept in separate cages at 24 ± 0.5 °C, 50%–60% humidity, and a 12-h light/dark cycle. A normal chow diet was provided ad libitum.

In the first study period, rats were randomly classified into two groups (n = 8 per group): normoxic group (N); hypoxic group (H). Rats in the normoxic group were housed in ambient sea-level oxygen level (21% O2) for 6 weeks, while rats in the hypoxic group were exposed to 10% O2 in hypoxic chambers (n = 4 rats/chamber) throughout the 6 week-experimental period. The oxygen levels in the chambers were measured using oxygen sensors. Deviations from the target oxygen levels were actively compensated by precision-controlled nitrogen injection from a pressurized storage vessel. The feedback-controlled system maintained oxygen levels within ≤ ± 0.1% deviation. During the initial nitrogen pressurization, the gas flow rates was set to 10−20 L/min. Upon approaching the critical oxygen threshold of 10% O2, the flow regulation was reduced to 5–10 L/min. The chambers did not restrict the rats’ activities, including feeding, drinking, and locomotion. Brief openings were performed as needed for procedures such as gavage, cage cleaning, and food/water replenishment.

The underlying mechanisms were further investigated in the subsequent study periods. In the second study period, rats were randomly classified into four groups (n = 8 per group): normoxic group (N); normoxic plus Des group (N + Des); hypoxic group (H); hypoxic plus Des group (H + Des). Rats in the N and N + Des groups were housed in ambient sea-level oxygen level (21% O2), while rats in the H and H + Des groups were exposed to 10% O2 in hypoxic chambers throughout the experimental period. ASMase inhibitor Des (15 mg/kg/d) was administered by gavage (once daily, 5 days per week) for 6 weeks. Pharmacological evidence has demonstrated that Des functions as a potent acid sphingomyelinase inhibitor [17]. The dosage of Des administered via oral gavage to rats was determined based on a study by obuchowicz [18]. Des was dissolved in sterile saline solution, and rats in the N and H groups received equivalent volumes of saline as their vehicle. All vehicles were administered using identical gavage protocols.

In the third study period, rats were randomized into two groups (n = 8 per group): hypoxic group (H); hypoxic plus Rottlerin group (H + R). All the rats were housed in hypoxic chambers with 10% O2 for the entire experimental period. Rats in the H + R group were gavaged with Rottlerin (10 mg/kg/d) for 6 weeks (once daily, 5 days per week). In the next study period, rats were randomly distributed into two groups (n = 8 per group): hypoxic group (H); hypoxic plus 4-PBA group (H + 4-PBA). Similarly, all the rats were exposed to 10% O2 for the duration of the experiment. 4-PBA (500 mg/kg/d) was administered by gavage for 6 weeks (once daily, 5 days per week). The oral gavage dosage of rottlerin was established based on previous studies [19–21]. Likewise, the dosage of 4-PBA to be administered via oral gavage was determined based on a previous study [22, 23]. Rottlerin and 4-PBA were dissolved in a solvent mixture of 5% DMSO in corn oil, and the H group received equivalent volumes of this mixture as their vehicle. All vehicles were administered using identical gavage protocols.

At the end of the 42-day environmental exposure in each study period, all the rats were sacrificed under anesthesia with 20% urethane (1.5 g/kg, i.p.). Serum and liver tissues were collected for further analysis. The levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL) and indirect bilirubin (IBIL) in the serum were measured in an automatic analyzer (OLYMPUS AU5400, Japan). For histopathological examination, the tissue sections were cut into slices for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining. Liver tissues were also examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) for ultrastructural observation as previously described [24].

Histological examination

Histopathological analyses were performed in a blinded fashion. The modified histological scoring was determined by the degree of congestion, edema, fat accumulation, sinusoidal dilatation, inflammation and hepatocyte necrosis (0: absent; 1: slight; 2: moderate and 3: severe) according to previous studies [25, 26]. The sum of the individual values represented the histological scores in the liver.

Lipidomic analysis

Liver tissues and serum were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and shipped to Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co. Ltd for metabolomics analysis. Sample preparation and lipid extraction were performed following established protocols [27]. SPLASH® LIPIDOMIX Mass Spec Standard (330707-1EA) was obtained from Avanti (Alabaster, AL, USA). Samples were then subject to liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) assay as previously described [28, 29]. LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted using a Q Exactive plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled to an UHPLC Nexera LC-30A (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Lipid identification and quantification were performed using LipidSearch software (version 4.1, Thermo Fisher Scientific) based on MS/MS spectral matching. The LipidSearch database contains over 30 lipid classes and more than 1,500,000 fragment ions. Both precursor and fragment mass tolerances were set to 5 ppm. The lipids were divided into seven categories: glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, glycerolipids, serol lipids, prenol lipids, fatty acyls, and saccharolipids.

Measurement of the activity of Cer-related metabolizing enzymes

Cer can be produced either via hydrolysis of sphingomyelin (SM), which is catalyzed by ASMase, or through de novo synthesis by serine palmitoyltransferase (SPT) [30]. Cer can be converted back into SM, or metabolized by acid ceramidase (AC) to sphingosine (SPH). SPH, in turn, can be regenerated into Cer via reacylation catalyzed by ceramide synthase (CerS) [30]. The CerS enzyme family (CerS1-6) comprises isoforms that exhibit specificity for distinct acyl chain lengths, thereby catalyzing the formation of Cer species with varying acyl chains. For example, CerS5 and CerS6 preferentially utilize C16-CoA [31]. Measuring these specific enzymes provides mechanistic insights into Cer synthesis and degradation, collectively reflecting Cer metabolic flux.

The activities of SPT, CerS5 and AC were measured using the corresponding assay kits (Mlbio, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The activities of ASMase, CerS6 and AC were measured using the corresponding assay kits (Mmbio, Jiangsu, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, these kits employ specific substrates under optimized reaction conditions to ensure target enzyme detection without cross-reactivity. For sample preparation, 40 mg pieces of hepatic tissue were homogenized in 400 μL ice-cold PBS using a TissueLyser. The resulting homogenates were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Following the incubation of the supernatants with the substrate and reaction mixture, absorbance changes were kinetically monitored using a microplate reader. Enzyme activities were calculated and expressed as relative changes compared to the normoxic or hypoxic group.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay

The content of lipid peroxidation marker MDA was measured using MDA detection kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This assay uses a colorimetric method based on thiobarbituric acid reactivity. Briefly, 50 mg pieces of hepatic tissue were homogenized in 500 μL ice-cold lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) using a TissueLyser. The resulting homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Samples or standards were mixed with 200 μL MDA assay solution and heated at 100 °C for 15 min. After cooling to room temperature in a water bath, the mixture was centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, and its absorbance was measured colorimetrically using a microplate reader. Protein concentrations in the initial lysates were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). MDA levels were normalized to protein content and expressed as nmol/mg protein.

Measurement of SERCA activity

To measure the SERCA activity present in endoplasmic reticulum (ER), the hepatic ER was isolated using the ER extraction kit (Bestbio, Shanghai, China). Pieces of hepatic tissue (100 mg) were homogenized in 500 μL ice-cold Reagent A using a TissueLyser. The mixture was then incubated on ice for 10 min and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube and the pellet was discarded. After two centrifugation steps, 125 μL Reagent B were added to the supernatant and the mixture were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The pellet collected at this stage contained the isolated ER fraction. Then the activity of SERCA was measured using the commercially kit (KeyGen Biotech, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This assay is a classic method based on the quantitative analysis of inorganic phosphate, a product of enzymatic reactions. Briefly, the isolated ER fraction was mixed with ice-cold saline and subjected to an enzymatic reaction. Protein concentrations in the isolated ER pellets were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). The activity of SERCA was expressed as μmol Pi/mg prot/h.

Western blot analysis

Protein samples were prepared as previously described [32]. Briefly, hepatic tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (Beyotime, China) and a PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitor cocktail tablet (Roche Applied Science, Germany). The protein samples were applied to SDS-PAGE gels, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% BSA for 2 h, incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C and then incubated with secondary antibodies. All primary antibodies were diluted 1:1000 except for SERCA2, which was diluted 1:5000. The secondary antibody was diluted 1:7000. The blots were visualized using the enhanced chemiluminescence method. Densitometric analysis was assessed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviations (SD). Differences between the two groups were compared using the Student’s t-test. For comparisons between multiple groups, two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) coupled with Tukey’s post-hoc analysis was conducted. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Chronic hypoxia caused liver damage

Serum levels of ALT, AST, TBIL, DBIL and IBIL, which are well-known biomarkers of liver damage, increased after hypoxic exposure (Fig. 1A-B). Histologically, the hepatic lobule structure was well arranged, and the morphology of the hepatocytes was normal in the normoxic group. Conversely, the livers of the rats in the hypoxic group showed disordered hepatocyte arrangement, congestion, edema, steatosis, hepatic sinusoids dilatation, inflammatory infiltration, and focal necrosis (Fig. 1C-D). Additionally, MDA levels, a well-known oxidative damage marker, were significantly increased in the liver after hypoxic exposure (Fig. 1E). Among the lipid subclasses that were altered under hypoxia in the liver, Cer levels showed the most prominent increase, and Cer levels were approximately 1.25 times higher than those in the normoxic group (Fig. 2A and 2D). Cer is a lipotoxic inducer that can cause organelle dysfunction, cell injury, and cell death. Hepatic levels of other lipotoxic lipid species, such as DG, LPC, and LPE, were not affected by hypoxia (Fig. 2A-C). Serum Cer levels were also significantly increased after hypoxic exposure (Fig. S1C).

ASMase activation-mediated Cer accumulation had an important role in hypoxia-induced liver injury

Lipidomic analyses revealed that hypoxia markedly decreased the levels of SM in the liver but had no obvious effects on SPH content (Fig. 2C). The activity of Cer-related metabolic enzymes was further analyzed to investigate the metabolic pathways by which hypoxia increases Cer levels. As shown in Fig. 3B and 3D, hypoxia significantly increased the activity levels of ASMase and CerS6. However, the activity levels of SPT, AC, and CerS5 were not affected by hypoxia (Fig. 3A, C and E). Moreover, the increases in ASMase activity and Cer levels as well as the decrease in SM content in the liver of hypoxia-exposed rats were reversed by treatment with the ASMase inhibitor Des (Fig. 3F-H). Hypoxia caused liver damage via the PKCδ/ASMase/Cer axis

PKCδ may be the key upstream kinase regulating ASMase activity. The results showed that hypoxia significantly increased p-PKCδ levels in the liver, suggesting that PKCδ was activated by hypoxia (Fig. 5A-B). Under hypoxic conditions, treatment with the PKCδ inhibitor rottlerin suppressed PKCδ phosphorylation in the liver, inhibited ASMase activity, and reduced Cer levels in the liver of hypoxia-exposed rats (Fig. 5C-F). Furthermore, the increased serum levels of ALT, AST, and IBIL, as well as the hepatic histopathological damage in hypoxia-exposed rats, were reversed by rottlerin treatment (Fig. 5E-J). These results suggested that hypoxia caused liver damage by activating the PKCδ/ASMase/Cer axis. Hypoxia-induced liver damage was related to Cer accumulation-mediated ERS

To investigate the mechanisms underlying Cer accumulation-mediated liver injury, the ultrastructure of the liver was analyzed using TEM. Hepatic ultrastructure images showed a disorganized, dilated, and ruptured ER in the hypoxic group (Fig. 6A), suggesting that hypoxia destroyed the structure of the ER. The ER is involved in many metabolic processes, and disturbances in ER structure and function can lead to the accumulation of misfolded or unfolded proteins, a condition referred to as ERS. Subsequently, the protein expression of ERS markers was evaluated. Discussion

In this study, the effects of chronic continuous hypoxia on the liver and the underlying mechanisms were investigated. The results suggested that chronic hypoxia caused liver damage partially through Cer-mediated ERS via activation of the PKCδ/ASMase axis.

Chronic hypoxia induces liver injury and lipid dysregulation

The liver functions as a central organ in numerous physiological and metabolic processes, which make it extremely sensitive to hypoxia. Xiong et al. found that rats exposed to continuous hypoxia (simulating 5500 m altitude) for 10 days developed oxidative damage, inflammatory responses, and pathohistological alterations in the liver [6]. Ibrahim et al. demonstrated that exposing rats to a simulated high-altitude environment (2100 m above sea level) for 8 weeks significantly elevated hepatic MDA levels along with serum ALT and AST concentrations [35]. Das et al. observed that rats exposed to 10% O2 for 21 days exhibited swelling and necrotic foci in some hepatocytes [7]. Liu et al. found that sustained hypoxia (simulating 6000 m altitude) for 30 days induced hepatic mitochondrial damage and increased serum ALT and AST levels in mice [36]. Zhen et al. established that sustained hypoxic conditions (10% O2 for 40 days) induce hepatic oxidative stress and increase serum TG levels in murine models [37]. Consistent with these reports, the present study demonstrates that hypoxia perturbs serum liver function indicators, increases hepatic MDA accumulation, and elevates circulating TG concentrations. Concomitantly with these serum abnormalities, hypoxia induced significant pathological changes in the liver. Collectively, these findings indicate that chronic continuous hypoxia (10% O2 for 6 weeks) causes liver injury.

Chronic continuous hypoxia occurs at high altitudes and in patients with chronic cardiopulmonary disease [1, 2]. Abnormal serum liver function indices are common among residents living at high altitudes. However, previous studies on plateau hypoxia mainly focused on its effects on the lungs, heart, and brain, and rarely involved the liver. Despite previous population-based studies reporting a higher prevalence of liver function abnormalities and a higher frequency of liver disease in patients with COPD or heart failure [38, 39], liver disorders have received less attention in these patients. In recent years, both basic and clinical studies have shown complex interactions between the liver and extra-liver organs [40, 41] and that liver damage can have deleterious effects on multiple organs, such as the heart, lungs, and brain [42–44]. These findings underscore the need to improve liver health in high-altitude residents and patients with cardiopulmonary diseases. Further exploration of the mechanisms underlying chronic continuous hypoxia-induced liver injury, as well as the development of strategies to alleviate liver damage, might be beneficial for facilitating acclimatization to high altitudes or managing chronic cardiopulmonary diseases.

The liver is the central organ in lipid production and utilization. The pivotal role of lipid metabolism disorders in the pathogenesis of liver diseases has been uncovered over the past several years [45]. In this study, histological analysis revealed that chronic hypoxia caused hepatic steatosis. The accumulation of lipid droplets in the liver may limit the blood supply to hepatocytes, leading to aggravated hypoxia, thus creating a vicious cycle of hypoxia and lipid accumulation. Zhen et al. revealed that sustained hypoxic conditions in mice (10% O2 for 40 days) dysregulated genes associated with lipid metabolism pathways [37]. Epidemiological studies have shown that populations living at high altitudes—first observed among native Tibetan residents and Peru Plateau dwellers—exhibit characteristic dyslipidemia, with a high prevalence of hypercholesterolemia and hypertriglyceridemia [46, 47]. Therefore, a lipidomic analysis was conducted to further evaluate the effects of hypoxia on lipid metabolism, which showed that chronic hypoxia altered the serum and liver lipid profiles. Of the hepatic lipid classes that were altered following hypoxia, Cer showed the most obvious change. Hypoxia also notably increased serum Cer levels. Cer is a bioactive sphingolipid that can induce lipotoxicity [30]. Accumulating evidence suggests that Cer plays an important role in the occurrence and development of several liver diseases, cardiometabolic diseases, and pulmonary hypertension [14, 48, 49]. Clinical studies have suggested that circulating Cer levels are predictive biomarkers of cardiovascular diseases and can be used to diagnose high-altitude pulmonary edema [48, 50, 51]. However, limited information is available regarding the role of Cer in hypoxia-induced liver damage.

In this study, hypoxic exposure also markedly increased hepatic Co and ChE levels. Co, an essential component of the mitochondrial electron transport chain and a potent lipid-soluble antioxidant, has demonstrated therapeutic potential in clinical studies for managing steatotic liver disease [52]. The increase in hepatic Co levels following hypoxic exposure likely represents an adaptive compensatory response to hypoxia-mediated hepatocellular injury. Free cholesterol is a lipotoxic molecule that is involved in the occurrence and development of several liver diseases [53]. ChE is synthesized through the esterification of cholesterol with fatty acids and functions as the primary storage form of cholesterol within cells, being the main transport vehicle in circulating lipoproteins [54]. Cholesterol esterification is essential for lipid droplet formation. Thus, while ChE formation partially mitigates the cytotoxicity of free cholesterol, excessive ChE can promote lipid droplet enlargement and pathological accumulation, leading to metabolic dysregulation and cellular stress responses, ultimately exacerbating hypoxia-induced liver injury in this experimental model. Moreover, ChE is subjected to oxidative modifications under pathological conditions [54], and studies indicate that oxidized ChEs contribute to the onset and progression of liver diseases [55]. However, the involvement of oxidized ChEs in the pathogenesis of chronic hypoxia-induced liver injury warrants further investigation.

PKCδ/ASMase/Cer axis

To determine whether Cer accumulation contributes to hypoxic liver injury, the activity of Cer-related metabolic enzymes was initially analyzed. The results showed that hypoxia significantly increased the activities of ASMase and CerS6, with the increase in ASMase activity being the most prominent. ASMase hydrolyses SM into Cer. In parallel with increased ASMase activity, hypoxia markedly decreased SM levels in the liver. SM is the main component of the cell membrane. Over hydrolysis of SM damages the structure of the cell membrane, leading to leakage of liver enzymes into the circulation, which partially accounts for the increased serum levels of ALT and AST after hypoxic exposure. Moreover, treatment with the ASMase inhibitor Des inhibited the hypoxia-induced increase in Cer levels in the liver, which underscored the key role of ASMase activation in hypoxia-mediated Cer accumulation. The hypoxia-mediated increase in hepatic Cer levels was not be completely blocked by Des treatment. As hypoxia increased FA levels in the liver, this increase in substrate for the de novo synthesis pathway of Cer might also contribute to the hypoxia-induced Cer accumulation in the liver. Furthermore, Des treatment ameliorated the alteration in liver function indices in serum and attenuated hepatic histopathological damage. Together, these results indicate the important role of ASMase activation-mediated Cer accumulation in chronic hypoxia-induced liver damage.

PKCδ, a signaling kinase, has been shown to function upstream of ASMase [15, 16]. The results of this study suggested that hypoxia caused liver damage partially via activation of the PKCδ/ASMase/Cer axis. Interestingly, the results also showed that Des administration inhibited hypoxia-induced PKCδ activation. Thus, PKCδ is required for hypoxia-induced generation of Cer, and this Cer could in turn activate PKCδ, thereby amplifying formation of Cer. This reciprocal activation loop represents a previously unrecognized pathogenic amplifier in liver injury models. The reciprocal interaction between PKCδ activation and Cer accumulation might have a key role in the development of chronic hypoxia-induced liver injury. Given the diversity of the PKC family, the potential contributions of other isoforms were also considered. Previous studies have indicated that protein kinase C ζ (PKCζ) acts as a downstream effector of Cer [56] and that Cer can initiate hepatic lipid disorders through PKCζ activation [57]. Whether PKCζ contributes to Cer accumulation-mediated liver injury warrants further investigation. Takahashi et al. recently demonstrated that protein kinase C β (PKCβ) activation inhibits Cer kinase (CerK) [58], the enzyme responsible for phosphorylating Cer to ceramide-1-phosphate. Additionally, Zeng et al. reported that chronic hypoxia activates PKCβ in the left ventricle [59]. These findings suggest a potential role for the PKCβ/CerK pathway in hypoxia-mediated Cer accumulation, though its specific contribution in the liver requires further study. Therefore, further experiments are warranted to explore the potential functional interactions between other PKC isoforms and Cer metabolism and their contributions to hypoxic liver injury.

Cer induces ERS in the liver

The mechanisms by which Cer accumulation causes liver damage in hypoxia-exposed rats were evaluated. Hepatocytes contain abundant ER and are very susceptible to ERS. ERS is an adaptive protective response that helps restore ER homeostasis. However, accumulating evidence has shown that severe or sustained ERS contributes to various liver diseases [60]. The results of this study indicated that chronic hypoxia causes ERS in the liver. IRE1 is the most conserved ERS transducer. Activated IRE1 can promote the transcriptional activation of proinflammatory and proapoptotic signaling pathways [61]. PERK, an ER-resident transmembrane kinase and an ERS sensor, has been increasingly recognized as a critical molecular bridge driving proinflammatory cytokine production [62]. CHOP, a well-characterized pro-apoptotic factor induced linked to ERS, facilitates apoptosis by transcriptionally repressing antiapoptotic proteins and activating caspase-3 [61]. Moreover, studies indicate that CHOP induces mitochondrial dysfunction by directly suppressing peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1 alpha [63], a master regulator critical for mitochondrial biogenesis and functional integrity. ERS also promotes hepatic lipid accumulation by activating transcriptional regulators that upregulate the expression of lipogenic enzymes [64]. The hepatic steatosis observed in this study following hypoxic exposure may therefore be linked to ERS. Additionally, ERS is implicated as a significant trigger for both apoptosis and ferroptosis [61, 65]. The results of this study showed that chronic hypoxia markedly increased the expression of p-PERK, p-IRE1, and CHOP in hepatic tissues, accompanied by pathological manifestations such as inflammatory infiltration and mitochondrial ultrastructural disorganization. Collectively, these findings indicated that hepatic ERS likely contributes to liver injury through multiple pathways, including IRE1- and PERK-mediated inflammatory responses, CHOP-driven mitochondrial damage, dysregulation of lipogenic enzymes, and induction of apoptosis and ferroptosis. Further experiments will be performed to characterize the precise mechanisms underlying ERS-mediated liver damage under hypoxic conditions.

The findings of this study further indicate that the detrimental effects in the liver caused by overproduction of Cer were mediated, at least in part, by induction of sustained ERS. Emerging evidence has underscored the pathophysiological interplay between ERS and oxidative stress [66]. The results of this study indicate that hypoxia causes elicits extensive oxidative injury within the liver. Elucidating the crosstalk between ERS and oxidative stress may facilitate the development of effective strategies to mitigate hypoxia-induced liver injury. Meanwhile, further experiments are warranted to explore the additional mechanisms involved in Cer accumulation-induced liver damage under hypoxic conditions.

Although previous studies have implicated ASMase activation-mediated Cer accumulation in ERS in several liver damage models, the underlying mechanisms remain largely unknown. SERCA, a key factor of cellular calcium homeostasis, is known to provoke ERS in hepatocytes when its function is disrupted [34]. However, few studies have examined the effects of Cer on SERCA in the liver. The findings of this study support a critical role for Cer accumulation in hypoxia-mediated SERCA inhibition in the liver. This establishes a novel pathway through which Cer may trigger ERS via SERCA inhibition under hypoxic conditions. However, the precise molecular mechanisms through which Cer affects SERCA are not completely understood. Specifically, we propose three potential pathways through which Cer inhibits SERCA. First, Cer enrichment within the ER membrane can alter its biophysical properties (e.g., rigidity, fluidity, curvature, permeability) [67], potentially impairing SERCA's conformational dynamics and function. Secondly, given the high hydrophobicity of Cer [67], its acyl chains may directly interact with SERCA's transmembrane domains, potentially modulating its structure and activity. Moreover, SERCA2 is highly sensitive to oxidative damage [68, 69], which can significantly impair its function. In this study, Des treatment significantly attenuated hypoxia-induced elevation in MDA levels and mitigated mitochondrial structural damage. Because mitochondria are both the major sources and primary targets of reactive oxygen species, these data suggest that Cer accumulation plays a key role in exacerbating hypoxia-induced hepatic oxidative stress. Therefore, Cer accumulation may indirectly inhibit SERCA2 by promoting oxidative modifications. Further investigation is required to fully elucidate these mechanisms. Importantly, the specific role and underlying mechanisms of SERCA2 in Cer-mediated ERS in the liver warrant further studies.

Potential diagnostic biomarkers

The liver is the main organ for Cer production, and usually contains much higher levels of Cer than other tissues [70]. Growing evidence suggests that Cer derived from the liver can be exported into the circulation and transported to peripheral tissues [71]. Some large cohort studies have reported that higher circulating levels of specific Cer subspecies, such as Cer (d18:1/16:0) and Cer (d18:1/24:1), are associated with major adverse cardiovascular events [72, 73]. Kim et al. demonstrated that blood Cer (d18:1/24:1) levels were significantly associated with reduced pulmonary function in patients with COPD [74]. However, the effects of hypoxia on hepatic Cer subspecies remain poorly characterized, and the functions of individual subspecies under hypoxic conditions are largely unknown. This study provided the first experimental evidence that hypoxia significantly increases the levels of total Cer, Cer (d18:1/16:0), and Cer (d18:1/24:1) in both the liver and serum (Fig. S2). This observed Cer dysregulation suggests that circulating Cer and its subspecies may be promising diagnostic biomarkers for the early detection of multi-organ impairment, including liver injury, cardiovascular abnormalities, and pulmonary dysfunction under hypoxic conditions. In subsequent research, the characterization of circulating Cer profiles in high-altitude inhabitants and patients with chronic cardiopulmonary conditions, as well as the longitudinal exploration of their correlations with multi-organ functional outcomes, will be performed. This systematic approach will advance the translation of Cer-based biomarkers into clinical practice, enabling the early identification of subclinical organ dysfunction and longitudinal surveillance of disease progression in populations exposed to chronic hypoxia.

Therapeutic implications and translational potential

Emerging evidence highlights the ASMase/Cer axis as a dual pathological mechanism and potential therapeutic target due to its relevance in liver injury, cardiovascular disorders, neurological conditions, pulmonary diseases, and diabetic cardiomyopathy [75–78]. In this study, Des treatment significantly attenuated hypoxia-induced dysregulation of total Cer levels and homeostasis of specific Cer species (Cer d18:1/16:0 and Cer d18:1/24:1) in hepatic tissue and systemic circulation (Fig. S2). These findings imply that targeting the ASMase/Cer axis may be a therapeutic strategy for conferring multi-organ protection under hypoxic conditions. As Cer can accumulate in many tissues and Des was administered by gavage, whether hepatic-derived Cer could be released into the circulation and affect cardiopulmonary function after hypoxic exposure are yet unknown. In the future, targeted intervention against Cer in the liver is warranted to elucidate the role of hepatic-derived Cer in the crosstalk between the liver and other organs under hypoxic conditions.

This study provided compelling evidence that Des and rottlerin exert significant hepatoprotective effects under hypoxic conditions. Des is a Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved antidepressant. Although not yet FDA-approved, rottlerin, a selective PKCδ inhibitor, has multiple pharmacological properties and demonstrated robust preclinical efficacy in models of cancer [79], neurological disorders [80], and viral infections [81]. These findings highlighted the translational potential of repurposing these agents for the clinical management of hypoxic liver dysfunction. Future studies with chronic hypoxic animal models will systematically evaluate the therapeutic potential of pharmacological agents targeting the PKCδ/ASMase axis, including ASMase (e.g., Des), PKCδ (e.g., rottlerin) inhibitors, as well as compounds modulating upstream regulatory mechanisms. These investigations will incorporate a rigorous evaluation of therapeutic efficacy through dose-response studies, pharmacokinetic profiling using mass spectrometry-based assays, and longitudinal safety assessments via histopathological and biochemical monitoring, thereby establishing a translational framework that bridges preclinical research findings with clinical applications. Given that hypoxia triggers multifaceted pathological cascades, combined interventions simultaneously modulating the PKCδ/ASMase/Cer axis and targeting downstream pathways may demonstrate enhanced therapeutic efficacy in mitigating hypoxic liver injury.

Study strengths and limitations

This study revealed novel phenotypic manifestations and mechanistic pathways in hypoxia-induced liver injury, specifically highlighting the critical role of the PKCδ/ASMase/Cer/ERS axis. A reciprocal interaction between PKCδ activation and Cer accumulation was observed, demonstrating the therapeutic potential of concurrently targeting PKCδ and the ASMase/Cer axis to counteract hypoxic liver injury. Furthermore, comprehensive serum lipidomic analysis suggests that targeted modulation of the ASMase/Cer axis is a promising therapeutic approach for ameliorating multi-organ damage and improving health outcomes under hypoxic conditions. These findings provided a novel foundation for the development of targeted interventions against hypoxic organ damage with significant translational potential.

This study has certain limitations. The current investigation was primarily limited to hepatic responses, with no comprehensive multi-organ analysis conducted. The therapeutic efficacy of Des, rottlerin, and 4-PBA was assessed following a 6-week hypoxic exposure protocol. Whether the protective effects of these inhibitors manifest during the early stages or necessitate sustained administration remains unresolved. Although the pharmacological inhibitors used in this study (Des, rottlerin, and 4-PBA) provide initial mechanistic insights, their potential off-target effects underscore the need for future validation using genetic approaches such as knockout models. Notably, as hypoxia orchestrates a spectrum of pathophysiological mechanisms, whether PKCδ activation represents an autonomous oxygen-sensing mechanism or arises secondarily to metabolic perturbations in the liver remains to be elucidated. Further studies will systematically evaluate the involvement of the PKCδ/ASMase/Cer axis in hypoxia-induced multi-organ dysfunction (including that in the cardiac, cerebral, and pulmonary systems), while concurrently investigating the temporal efficacy profiles of axis-targeted therapeutic interventions. Furthermore, comprehensive studies will be conducted to systematically elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms through which hypoxia mediates the activation of the PKCδ/ASMase axis.

Conclusions

Overall, the results of this study demonstrated that chronic continuous hypoxia caused liver damage through Cer-mediated ERS via activation of the PKCδ/ASMase axis (Fig. 9). Targeting the PKCδ/ASMase axis might be an effective approach for preventing Cer accumulation, thereby inhibiting ERS and subsequently mitigating hypoxia-induced liver damage. These results highlight the therapeutic potential of repurposing pharmacological agents, particularly Des and rottlerin, for the clinical management of hypoxic liver dysfunction and provide a foundation for the development of effective preventive and therapeutic approaches.
