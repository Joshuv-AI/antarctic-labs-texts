Association between the triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio and diabetes mellitus likelihood in patients with chronic kidney disease
Authors: Mijie Guan, Shuang Cui, Haiying Song, Haofei Hu, Bo Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) are at an increased risk of diabetes mellitus (DM) and dyslipidemia, yet the specific relationship between lipid profiles, particularly triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (TG/HDL-C ratio), and DM likelihood in this population has not been thoroughly elucidated.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 20,310 unselected patients with CKD enrolled from 2006 to 2015. The relationship between the TG/HDL-C ratio and the likelihood of DM was evaluated using binary logistic regression. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses were performed, and a generalized additive model with smooth curve fitting assessed potential non-linear associations. We also performed the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and decision curve analysis to assess the determination and clinical use, respectively.ResultsAmong the participants (mean age 60.907 ± 10.044 years; 79.580% male), 1,758 (8.656%) had DM. The median TG/HDL-C ratio was 0.655(interquartile range 0.465–0.920). After adjusting for covariates, a significant positive association was found between the TG/HDL-C ratio and DM likelihood (odds ratio [OR], 1.494; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.354–1.648; P < 0.001). A non-linear relationship was observed with an inflection point at a TG/HDL-C ratio of 1.030. The ORs below and above this point were 1.866 (95% CI, 1.472–2.365) and 1.297 (95% CI, 1.094–1.538), respectively. The area under curve (AUC) of the nomogram was of 0.580 (95% CI, 0.566–0.594). Subgroup analyses indicated a stronger association in patients without hypertension, in female and patients with AF.ConclusionThe TG/HDL-C ratio is independently associated with DM likelihood in patients with CKD, exhibiting a non-linear relationship particularly significant when the ratio is below 1.030. The TG/HDL-C ratio may serve as a useful marker for DM likelihood assessment in CKD patients, though prospective studies are needed to determine its role in prevention strategies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04377-9.

Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM), a pervasive chronic condition, has seen a marked increase in type 2 diabetes (T2DM) globally [1]. Forecasts suggest that the prevalence of adult DM will increase from 10.5% in 2021 to 12.2% by 2045 [2]. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) follows a similar trend, representing a significant health challenge, surpassed only by hypertension and diabetes in prevalence [3]. Between 1999 and 2007, the prevalence of CKD increased alarmingly, affecting 10–13% of populations worldwide [4]. Notably, the prevalence of DM among patients with CKD was estimated at 11.85–13.9% [5, 6], highlighting CKD as a critical, independent risk factor for developing diabetes, as evidenced by a study in Taiwan which reported an adjusted hazard ratio (HR) of 1.204 [7]. In a large national cohort study involving 1,337,452 United States veterans without prior diabetes, individuals with blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels exceeding 25 mg/dL were found to have a greater risk (odds ratio [OR] 1.23; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.21–1.25) of developing diabetes compared to those with BUN levels at or below 25 mg/dL [8]. Furthermore, research conducted on a Chinese cohort demonstrated that estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was inversely related to the incidence of diabetes (HR 0.986; 95% CI: 0.984–0.988) and pre-diabetes (HR 0.993; 95% CI: 0.992–0.995) [9, 10].

The risk of DM is multifaceted, encompassing genetic predispositions and lifestyle factors, such as sedentary behavior, inadequate physical activity, smoking, dietary habits, alcohol consumption, and obesity, along with various environmental influences. Significant risk factors for diabetes include irregular blood pressure, lipid profiles, heart rate variability, weight status, and uric acid levels [11, 12]. Notably, dyslipidemia, characterized by an imbalance between triglycerides (TG) and high-density lipoprotein (HDL), is prevalent in renal disorders and heightens cardiovascular risk [13, 14]. In T2DM, dyslipidemia often presents with elevated TG levels and decreased HDL-C [15, 16]. The TG/HDL-C ratio has been identified as a more reliable predictor of diabetes risk than individual lipid metrics [17]. This ratio has increasingly been recognized as a marker for insulin resistance (IR), a key precursor for the development of T2DM [18, 19]. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated significant associations between elevated TG/HDL-C ratios and the risk of developing T2DM in diverse populations, including Korean [20, 21], Chinese [17], Iranians [22], Japanese [23], and Caucasians groups [24].

However, considerable racial disparities exist concerning the diagnostic utility of this biomarker. Knight et al. [25] illustrated that the TG/HDL-C ratio does not effectively predict IR among overweight women of African ancestry, which includes South African, African American, and West African populations. This research suggests that conventional TG/HDL-C cutoff values established in Caucasian populations may not be applicable to other racial groups, underscoring the urgent need for ethnicity-specific validation.

Moreover, He et al. revealed significant sex-specific differences in the diagnostic performance of the TG/HDL-C ratio [26]. Their study indicated that the TG/HDL-C ratio was not an acceptable marker of IR in Chinese men, while it showed predictive power in Chinese women. This finding hints at gender-based discrepancies in lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity, which may affect the clinical efficacy of the TG/HDL-C ratio.

Additionally, age-related variations in TG/HDL-C thresholds have been identified across different populations. Nur Zati Iwani et al. [27] established an optimal cutoff value of 1.11 for detecting IR in obese children aged 10–16 years. This cutoff value is significantly lower than those typically observed in adult populations, where threshold values generally range from 1.5 to 3.0 [18, 26]. These age-dependent disparities emphasize the critical importance of implementing age-stratified reference ranges for accurate biomarker interpretation in clinical practice.

The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), calculated as log (TG/HDL-C), has emerged as a valuable lipid parameter for evaluating cardiovascular and metabolic risk. However, substantial heterogeneity exists in AIP’s predictive capability across diverse demographic and clinical subgroups. AIP has been shown to predict diabetes risk differently among various demographic groups. Particularly, a recent study observed that the strength of the association between AIP and the incidence of prediabetes and DM is notably greater in women compared to men, suggesting a potential gender influence on diabetes risk prediction using AIP [28]. Sun et al. found that the nonlinear association between AIP and T2DM mellitus was particularly pronounced in overweight and obesity patients, with stronger associations observed in specific age groups [29]. AIP’s performance varies significantly across different clinical populations. Yan et al. demonstrated that AIP was significantly associated with diabetic kidney disease risk in patients with T2DM, indicating disease-specific predictive utility [30]. In acute coronary syndrome patients with T2DM undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention, Ma et al. found that AIP had significant prognostic impact for adverse outcomes, suggesting enhanced predictive value in high-risk clinical scenarios [31].

Despite extensive research in general populations, there is a remarkable paucity of data examining TG/HDL-C ratio performance in patients with CKD. CKD represents a unique metabolic environment characterized by altered lipid metabolism, with approximately 40% [32] of patients experiencing dyslipidemia. The complex interplay between uremic toxins, altered lipoprotein composition, and chronic inflammation in CKD may modify the relationship between TG/HDL-C ratios and diabetes risk.

Our study examines the association between TG/HDL-C ratio and diabetes risk in CKD patients using a substantial Chinese cohort (N = 20,310). This investigation addresses two important research needs: (1) the limited availability of population-specific data in Asian CKD cohorts, where genetic and environmental factors may differentially influence lipid-diabetes relationships compared to Western populations; and (2) the clinical requirement for effective diabetes risk stratification tools in patients with CKD, who demonstrate increased diabetes susceptibility relative to the general population.

Subjects and methods

Data source

The primary dataset for this study was derived from the population-based cohort investigation conducted by Zhang et al., which explored the associations between atrial fibrillation and outcomes in adults with chronic kidney disease (CKD). This research is available for public access in PLOS ONE [https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article? id=10.1371/journal.pone.0230189][35]. As a publication under the Creative Commons Attribution License, this article allows for unrestricted use, distribution, and reproduction across various mediums, provided proper attribution is given to the original authors and the source.

Study design

Our analysis was a cross-sectional secondary analysis of data obtained from the Kailuan Study, utilizing data from Zhang et al.‘s retrospective cohort study [33]. The original cohort study was prospective, our research question was addressed using data from a single examination period, without utilizing the longitudinal aspects of the cohort. This study’s target-independent variable was the TG/HDL-c ratio, with DM serving as the outcome of interest (DM, non-DM).

Study population

The Kailuan Study is a prospective cohort investigation conducted in the Kailuan community of Tangshan, China. This community, overseen by the Kailuan Group, encompasses 11 hospitals that deliver healthcare services to local residents. Between June 2006 and October 2007, all employees and retirees aged 18 years or older, totaling 155,418 individuals, were invited to participate. Ultimately, 101,510 participants (81,110 men and 20,400 women, aged 18 to 98 years) provided informed consent and were enrolled, resulting in a participation rate of 65.3%. The collective average age of participants was 51.93 years, aligning closely with the overall employee demographic of 51.92 years, although there was a significant gender imbalance (79.9% male participation compared to 85.9% in the workforce). Initial assessments included questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory tests performed at the participating hospitals. Follow-up evaluations were conducted biennially from 2006 until December 31, 2015, to monitor risk factors and the emergence of new diseases. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the ethics committees of Kailuan General Hospital, Beijing Chaoyang Hospital, and TianTan Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study is registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration number ChiCTR-TNC-11001489) [33–38]. Our secondary analysis complied with the same ethical standards and did not necessitate additional ethical approval.

The original cohort of 101,510 individuals was narrowed down to 21,587 adults aged at least 45 years with CKD after applying inclusion and exclusion criteria from 2006 through December 31, 2015. CKD was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m² and/or a urine dipstick assay indicating 1 + to 4 + proteinuria [39]. Exclusion criteria included: (1) previous kidney transplantation or dialysis (n = 42), (2) an eGFR of 60 mL/min/1.73 m² or greater without proteinuria (n = 56,379), (3) incomplete or invalid baseline data (n = 630), and (4) being younger than 45 years (n = 22,512). In total, 79,563 participants were excluded [33].

In the current analysis, we further excluded individuals with missing TG (N = 288), missing HDL-C (N = 2), missing diabetes-related data (N = 563), and statistical outliers (n = 424), defined as values exceeding ± 3 standard deviations from the mean. This refinement resulted in an analytical sample of 20,310 participants, as depicted in Fig. 1.

Variables

Triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio

TG/HDL-c Ratio: Defined as a continuous variable, computed by dividing TG concentrations by HDL-c.

Diabetes

In this work, DM was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dl, random plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dl, or current usage of glucose-lowering agents [33].

Covariates

We incorporated covariates based on clinical insights and literature [11, 12], including both continuous variables (e.g., diastolic blood pressure (DBP), systolic blood pressure (SBP), age, waist circumference (WC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and C-reactive protein (CRP)) and categorical variables (e.g., gender, education level, clinical history of smoking, alcohol intake, atrial fibrillation (AF), hypertension, myocardial infarction (MI), congestive heart failure, and peripheral artery disease (PAD)).

Baseline data collection

Trained physicians conducted in-person interviews using standardized questionnaires. The comprehensive assessment encompassed participants’ demographics, socioeconomic status, dietary patterns, lifestyle factors, personal and family medical histories, substance use, physical activity, sleep characteristics, financial backgrounds and educational. However, the dataset utilized in this secondary analysis lacked detailed information on socioeconomic status, dietary patterns, lifestyle factors, personal and family medical histories, substance use, physical activity, sleep characteristics, and financial backgrounds, representing a limitation of the available data source. All data collection procedures adhered to rigorous protocols and were executed by a team of specially trained healthcare professionals [33–38, 40–43].

Anthropometric measurements, including height and weight, were recorded with participants in light clothing, barefoot, and without head covering. Height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a portable stadiometer, while weight was assessed to the nearest 0.1 kg with calibrated platform scales. Blood pressure was measured with a calibrated mercury sphygmomanometer while participants were seated; two readings were taken, and the average of these readings was utilized for analysis. Smoking status was classified as ‘never smokers’ versus ‘ever smokers’ (current or former). Alcohol intake was categorized as ‘never drinkers’ versus ‘ever drinkers’ (current or former). Education level was stratified into two categories: middle school or below (≤ 9 years), and high school or above (> 9 years). These classifications align with the standardized protocols established in the Kailuan Study and have been validated in multiple publications from this cohort [40, 41]. Hypertension was defined according to the Joint National Committee 7 (JNC 7) guidelines as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medications. Blood pressure measurements were obtained using standardized protocols with participants seated after at least 5 min of rest [42]. HF cases were identified using ICD-10th revision code I50.x, with consistent application across the Kailuan cohort. Cases were ascertained through linkage with the Municipal Social Insurance Institution database and Hospital Discharge Register, including both inpatients and outpatients [41]. MI was identified using ICD-10 code I21. Diagnosis was determined by clinical symptoms, electrocardiogram findings, and dynamic changes in myocardial enzymes, following WHO’s Multinational Monitoring of Trends and Determinants in Cardiovascular Disease criteria [40]. Stroke events were identified using ICD-10 codes (I63 for ischemic stroke, I60-I61 for hemorrhagic stroke). Diagnosis was based on neurological signs, clinical symptoms, and neuroimaging tests including CT or MRI, in accordance with WHO criteria [40]. Atrial Fibrillation Assessment: All participants underwent standardized 12-lead electrocardiographic examinations to ensure diagnostic accuracy and consistency. Expert cardiologists at Kailuan General Hospital’s interpretation center analyzed the recordings using established Minnesota coding criteria for rhythm classification [33–37, 43]. PAD status was ascertained from participant medical history as recorded in the original Kailuan database. While specific diagnostic criteria for PAD were not explicitly detailed in the primary Kailuan methodology publications, PAD cases were likely identified through standard clinical diagnosis and hospital discharge records, consistent with the general approach used for other cardiovascular comorbidities in this study.

After an overnight fast, venous blood specimens were collected in EDTA-containing vacuum tubes. Samples were promptly centrifuged at 3000 ×g for 10 min at ambient temperature. The resultant plasma was expeditiously aliquoted and cryopreserved at − 80 °C pending analysis. These samples were analyzed at the Kailuan General Hospital’s central laboratory using a Hitachi 747 auto-analyzer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The analysis included serum creatinine, total cholesterol (TC), LDL-C, HDL-C, TG, and C-reactive protein (CRP) [35, 43]. eGFR was derived using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) creatinine formula, with a coefficient adjustment of 1.1 to account for Asian population characteristics ( eGFR = 141 × min(SCr/κ, 1)α × max(SCr/κ, 1)−1.209 × 0.993Age × 1.018 [if female], SCr is serum creatinine, κ is 0.7 for females and 0.9 for males, α is -0.329 for females and − 0.411 for males, min indicates the minimum of SCr/κ or 1, max indicates the maximum of SCr/κ or 1, Age is in years) [33]. Proteinuria was graded from 0 to 4 + using urine dipsticks [33], and an eGFR of less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m² indicated renal dysfunction [44]. Cut-off values for overweight, based on waist circumference, were set at over 94 cm for males and over 80 cm for females [45]. Further details are published previously [33–35].

Missing data processing

Our dataset had missing values, including smoking history for 2 participants (0.0098%), alcohol consumption history for 6 (0.0295%), hypertension for 6 (0.0295%), education for 85 (0.419%), SBP for 55 (0.271%), DBP for 55 (0.271%), WC for 454 (2.24%), LDL-C for 6 (0.0295%), TC for 2 (0.0098%), CRP for 149 (0.734%), and proteinuria for 1630 (8.025%). To address these gaps, we implemented multiple imputation, assuming data were missing at random (MAR) [46, 47], and included a comprehensive set of variables in the imputation model such as age, gender, WC, DBP, SBP, TC, proteinuria, LDL-C, eGFR, history of alcohol intake, smoking, AF, hypertension, MI, congestive heart failure, PAD and prior strokes.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test with Lilliefors correction, which is appropriate for large sample sizes such as ours (n = 20,310). This test provides robust assessment of distributional assumptions necessary for subsequent parametric statistical analyses. Participants were categorized into quartiles based on their TG/HDL-C ratios. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD), while skewed variables were represented as medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. To assess differences among the TG/HDL-C quartiles, we employed the χ² test for categorical data, one-way ANOVA for normally distributed continuous data, and the Kruskal-Wallis H test for skewed continuous data.

Investigation of the linear association between TG/HDL-C ratio and DM

In accordance with the STROBE guidelines [48], we performed both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses to assess the relationship between the TG/HDL-C ratio and DM. We constructed three logistic regression models: an unadjusted model; Model I, which was adjusted for sociodemographic variables including age, gender, WC, DBP, SBP, education level, and history of alcohol intake, smoking, AF, hypertension, MI, heart failure, and PAD; and Model II, which included additional clinical variables outlined in Table 3, such as CRP and LDL-C. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for all models. Potential confounders were identified through expert clinical insights, a thorough literature review [11, 12, 17], and collinearity assessment. Collinearity among variables was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF), with a threshold of VIF > 5 [49] (see Table S1). All variables in final models demonstrated VIF values of 1.0-2.4, confirming absence of significant multicollinearity. Covariate selection was conducted with caution to enhance the robustness of our findings.

Sensitivity analysis

To further validate our results, we employed multiple sensitivity analyses [50]. The TG/HDL-C ratio was categorized into quartiles to evaluate its performance as a continuous predictor and to explore potential non-linear relationships. Given the established risk factors—such as obesity, smoking, and alcohol intake—that elevate the risk of DM [11, 12], we conducted additional sensitivity analyses excluding participants with these characteristics. Furthermore, to evaluate whether extreme values influenced our results, we excluded individuals at the top and bottom 5% of TG and HDL-C distributions, effectively removing potential outliers that might disproportionately drive the observed associations. Additionally, E-values [51] were calculated to quantify the potential effect of unmeasured factors. Together, these analyses assessed the robustness of our results while determining the extent to which unobserved biases could affect the identified relationship.

Exploring the non-linear relationship between TG/HDL-C ratio and DM

To investigate the TG/HDL-C ratio’s non-linear linkage with DM, we utilized generalized additive models (GAM) alongside smooth curve fitting techniques. Through recursive algorithms, inflection points were pinpointed, leading to the construction of a segmented binary logistic regression model for each segment delineated by these points [52]. Log-likelihood ratio tests were then conducted to determine the most suitable model configuration for accurately depicting the TG/HDL-C ratio’s association with DM. This approach allowed for a nuanced understanding of the relationship, highlighting the complexity beyond linear correlations.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup Analysis Stratified binary logistic regression facilitated subgroup analyses by gender, age, hypertension, kidney function, proteinuria, and educational level. Continuous variables were categorized by clinical thresholds, and models were adjusted for relevant covariates: age (< 60, ≥ 60 years); kidney function (< 60, ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73m2); proteinuria (yes, no) [44, 53, 54], as well as AF (yes or no) and PAD (yes or no). Models were adjusted for relevant covariates in addition to the stratification variable itself, including gender, age, WC, education level, alcohol intake and smoking histories, AF, hypertension, MI, congestive heart failure, PAD, SBP, DBP, CRP, and LDL-C. Finally, likelihood ratio tests evaluated interaction terms, shedding light on potential effect modification [55, 56].

Receiver operating characteristic analysis and decision curve analysis

We also conducted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis to evaluate the predictive ability of the TG/HDL-C ratio to DM. We then calculated the area under the curve (AUC) of the ROC and the best cut-off point. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to determine the best cutoff point. Moreover, to assess the clinical utility and decision-making value of the TG/HDL-C ratio model beyond traditional discrimination metrics, we performed decision curve analysis (DCA) using the “rmda” package in R software. DCA quantifies the net benefit of using a predictive model compared to alternative strategies, such as treating all patients or none, across a spectrum of risk threshold probabilities. The net benefit was calculated using the formula: Net Benefit = (True Positives/n) - (False Positives/n) × [pt/(1-pt)], where pt represents the risk threshold probability, and n is the total sample size. This approach incorporates both the benefits of accurately identifying high-risk patients and the relative harm of false-positive classifications, weighted by the threshold probability. The analysis generated decision curves plotting standardized net benefit against risk threshold probabilities, with three reference lines: the prediction model, the “treat all” strategy (assuming all patients receive intervention), and the “treat none” strategy (assuming no patients receive intervention). A model demonstrates clinical utility when its curve lies above both reference strategies, indicating superior net benefit. The range of risk thresholds where the model curve remains above the reference lines defines the clinically useful probability range for decision-making.

Principal component analysis

In this study, we aimed to address the potential biases introduced by highly correlated variables, notably TG, HDL-C, by performing Principal Component Analysis (PCA) following Z-score standardization. PCA can perform principal component analysis after Z-score standardization of highly correlated variables such as TG, HDL-C, etc., and extract representative metabolic factors (such as PC1) as exposure variables to avoid bias caused by repeated counting.

Univariate analysis outcomes via binary logistic regression

Our analysis identified significant positive associations between the incidence of DM and several lipid parameters, including TG/HDL-C ratio (OR = 1.645), TG (OR = 1.269), and TC (OR = 1.124). The likelihood of developing DM also increased with advancing age (OR = 1.013), WC (OR = 1.028), hypertension (OR = 3.780), SBP (OR = 1.009), CRP (OR = 1.016), education level (OR = 1.182), female gender (OR = 1.168), smoking (OR = 1.126), proteinuria (OR = 1.588) and a declining kidney function as indicated by eGFR (OR = 1.004). Conversely, HDL-C demonstrated an inverse relationship with DM incidence (OR = 0.452). All associations reached statistical significance (P < 0.05; Table 2). However, DM did not show a significant correlation with alcohol consumption, MI, congestive heart failure, PAD, AF, DBP, LDL-C in our analysis. Overall, dyslipidemia, abdominal obesity, inflammation, and vascular factors exhibited strong connections to DM risk, while other common conditions and LDL-C did not. These findings highlight potential areas for likelihood stratification and preventive intervention.

Analysis of multivariate data using binary logistic regression

Our binary logistic regression analysis identified a significant association between an elevated TG/HDL-C ratio and DM odds. In the crude model, each one-unit increase in the TG/HDL-C ratio corresponded to a remarkable 64.5% increase in the odds of DM (OR = 1.645, 95% CI: 1.505, 1.799, P < 0.001). This association remained robust after adjusting for various demographic and clinical variables, including age, gender, WC, alcohol intake and smoking history, DM, cardiovascular diseases in Model I. In this adjusted model, each increment in the TG/HDL-C ratio was associated with a 48.4% increase in DM odds (OR = 1.484, 95% CI: 1.345–1.637, P < 0.001). Importantly, this relationship persisted in our fully adjusted model, which incorporated additional factors such as age, gender, WC, education level, history of alcohol intake, smoking, DM, hypertension, MI, congestive heart failure, PAD, DBP, SBP, BMI, CRP and LDL-C. In this comprehensive analysis, every one-unit increase in the TG/HDL-C ratio was linked to a 49.4% increase in DM odds (OR = 1.494, 95% CI: 1.354–1.648, P < 0.001). The consistent confidence intervals across all models reinforce the strength of the regression analyses in illustrating the positive association between the TG/HDL-C ratio and DM odds, as detailed in Table 3.

We further excluded participants with obesity, those with smoking histories, individuals with alcohol intake, and those in the top and bottom 5% of TG and HDL-C values before reassessing the associations. Even within these restricted samples, the relationship between higher TG/HDL-C ratios and increased odds of DM remained significant (OR 1.483, 95% CI 1.297–1.695; OR 1.558, 95% CI 1.370–1.772; OR 1.566, 95% CI 1.384–1.771, and OR 1.663, 95% CI 1.416–1.952 see Table 4).

In our final analytical step, we rigorously assessed the potential impact of unmeasured confounding variables on our results by calculating an E-value. The computed E-value of 2.36 exceeds the relative likelihood for both unidentified confounders and the TG/HDL-C ratio. This significant E-value further emphasized the robustness of the observed association between the TG/HDL-C ratio and DM odds, demonstrating its resilience against potential bias from unaccounted confounding factors.

Nonlinearity addressed by the generalized additive model

Utilizing GAM and smooth curve fitting techniques, we identified a nonlinear association between the TG/HDL-C ratio and the likelihood of DM (Fig. 2). A recursive algorithm identified an inflection point at 1.030. Subsequently, we employed a piecewise binary logistic regression model to calculate odds ratios and establish confidence intervals surrounding this inflection point. For values to the left of the inflection point, the odds ratio for DM was 1.866 (95% CI, 1.472 to 2.365). In contrast, for values to the right of the inflection point, the odds ratio was 1.297 (95% CI, 1.094 to 1.538) (see Table 5).

The results of subgroup analyses

We conducted subgroup analyses to explore potential modifiers influencing the relationship between the TG/HDL-C ratio and DM. The stratification variables included sex, age, hypertension status, presence of proteinuria, education level, eGFR, AF and PAD. Our analysis revealed that factors such as age, presence of proteinuria, education level, eGFR and PAD did not significantly alter the association between TG/HDL-c and DM. Specifically, no significant interaction was found between these variables regarding TG/HDL-c (P > 0.05 for interaction), indicating that they did not substantially impact the relationship between TG/HDL-c and the probability of DM. Conversely, we found a more pronounced association in female participants (OR = 2.235; 95% CI, 1.831 to 2.727; P < 0.001), normotensive individuals (OR = 1.658; 95% CI, 1.456 to 1.888; P < 0.001), and patients with AF (OR = 6.276; 95% CI, 2.450 to 16.074; P = 0.001). In contrast, a weaker association was observed among male participants (OR = 1.323; 95% CI, 1.181 to 1.483; P < 0.001), individuals with hypertension (OR = 1.306; 95% CI, 1.129 to 1.512; P < 0.001), and patients without AF (OR = 1.471; 95% CI, 1.332 to 1.624; P < 0.001; see Table 6). All interactions were statistically significant (P < 0.05). These findings underscore the varying impacts of these factors on the predictive value of the TG/HDL-C ratio for DM odds, highlighting the significance of personalized risk assessment.

Prediction performance of TG/HDL-C ratio in DM odds

To evaluate the diagnostic ability of the TG/HDL-C ratio in predicting diabetes, we performed a ROC curve analysis. This assessment revealed an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.580 (95% CI, 0.566–0.594), indicating moderate discriminatory capacity (see Fig. 3). At the optimal threshold of 0.730, the ROC curve demonstrated the following metrics: sensitivity of 50.7%, specificity of 62.1%, Positive Likelihood Ratio (PLR) of 1.3354, Negative Likelihood Ratio (NLR) of 0.7948, and Diagnostic Odds Ratio of 1.6801. The modest AUC of 0.580 indicates limited discriminatory ability for standalone diabetes prediction, suggesting that the TG/HDL-C ratio should be considered as part of a comprehensive risk assessment rather than an independent diagnostic tool (Table 7).

Decision curve analysis

We conducted DCA to assess the clinical utility of the TG/HDL-C ratio model for predicting DM in patients with CKD. The DCA curve illustrated that the TG/HDL-C ratio model offered a greater net benefit compared to both “treat all” and “treat none” strategies across a broad spectrum of risk thresholds (Fig. 4). Notably, the model demonstrated a positive net benefit when the risk threshold ranged from approximately 0.02 to 0.35, indicating its clinical relevance within these probability ranges. The standardized net benefit peaked at approximately 0.95 at the lowest risk thresholds, gradually decreasing as the threshold probability increased. Beyond a risk threshold of 0.35, the model’s net benefit approached zero, indicating diminished clinical utility at higher thresholds. The persistent positioning of the TG/HDL-C model curve above both reference strategies throughout the clinically relevant threshold range of 0.02 to 0.35 underscores its practical significance for diabetes risk stratification in patients with CKD.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this investigation is the first to specifically explore the association between the TG/HDL-C ratio and the odds of DM in patients with CKD. Our results demonstrating a positive correlation between the TG/HDL-C ratio and DM risk aligned with findings from previous studies in the general population. McLaughlin et al. first highlighted the TG/HDL-C ratio’s effectiveness in identifying IR in a cohort of healthy Caucasian participants [18]. Since then, the TG/HDL-C ratio has emerged as a reliable marker for both IR and β-cell dysfunction across diverse populations [19, 57]. Its predictive capacity for the onset of T2DM has been extensively validated, demonstrating high sensitivity and specificity among groups including Korean [20, 21], Chinese [17], Iranians [22], Japanese [23], Caucasians [24] population. Large-scale studies involving 114,787 Chinese adults have further highlighted the significant association between the TG/HDL-C ratio and various glycemic transitions, including the development of diabetes (HR = 1.159, 95%CI (1.104, 1.215)), the onset and progression of prediabetes (OR = 1.185, 95%CI (1.145–1.226)), and reversion from prediabetes (HR = 0.869, 95%CI (0.842–0.897)) [58–62]. Notably, our analysis revealed a stronger association between the TG/HDL-C ratio and DM risk specifically in patients with CKD, suggesting that this ratio may possess enhanced predictive value in this population.

Moreover, individuals with a TG/HDL-C ratio ≤ 1.030 exhibited more favorable metabolic profiles, characterized by lower WC, SBP, DBP, and TC. This subgroup also had lower proportions of female, smokers, alcohol consumers, and and individuals with lower education levels, and hypertension (Supplementary Table 2)—all factors closely linked to DM risk [11, 12]. Interestingly, when the TG/HDL-c ratio surpasses 1.030, its predictive power for diabetes is diluted due to these prevailing associated factors. Conversely, at ratios ≤ 1.030, where such associated factors are less pronounced, the TG/HDL-c ratio’s predictive strength on diabetes risk is amplified. Uncovering this nonlinear association and risk saturation provides vital insight guiding clinical decision-making around diabetes prevention among higher-risk patients with CKD. This study sets the foundation for future research to determine optimal TG/HDL-c thresholds and targeted interventions for diabetes prevention across the spectrum of kidney disease.

In addition to the overall association, we identified sex, hypertension and AF as significant modifiers in the relationship between the TG/HDL-C ratio and DM odds within specific subgroups. A stronger correlation was observed in women, individuals without hypertension and those with AF, while the association was notably weaker in men, those with hypertension and without AF. These findings suggest that interventions aimed at reducing DM odds related to the TG/HDL-C ratio may be more effective when tailored to women non-hypertensive and AF individuals. For patients with hypertension, a comprehensive strategy addressing both lipid profiles and blood pressure control is critical to reduce the elevated DM risk exacerbated by uncontrolled hypertension. Particularly for women and patients with AF, enhanced lipid management may be essential in minimizing their susceptibility to DM correlated with unfavorable TG/HDL-C ratios. This study underscores the importance of personalized treatment strategies that consider individual factors such as sex, hypertension and AF to more effectively prevent and manage DM in at-risk populations.

The ROC analysis revealed that the TG/HDL-C ratio exhibited modest discriminatory ability for DM detection in patients with CKD, with an AUC of 0.580 (95% CI: 0.566–0.594). While these performance metrics indicated limited standalone diagnostic utility, they align with previous studies demonstrating that single lipid ratios typically exhibit moderate predictive accuracy for diabetes risk [63]. The modest AUC indicates that the TG/HDL-C ratio alone has limited clinical utility for diabetes prediction. This suggests that while statistically significant associations exist, the clinical relevance for individual patient decision-making remains uncertain and requires integration with other risk factors. Notably, the PLR of 1.34 and NLR of 0.79 indicated that the ratio provided incremental diagnostic information, particularly when integrated with other clinical parameters. These findings emphasize the complexity of diabetes prediction in CKD populations, where various metabolic disturbances may affect the performance of traditional biomarkers. Thus, there is a need for developing more sophisticated predictive models that incorporate multiple risk factors to enhance clinical utility.

Numerous mechanisms may account for the association between the TG/HDL-C ratio and the pathogenesis of prediabetes and DM. First, the TG/HDL-C ratio is a well-established surrogate marker IR, a crucial pathophysiological process in the development of T2DM [18]. Elevated TG levels lead to increased circulating free fatty acids (FFAs), which impair insulin sensitivity via several pathways [64]. Prolonged exposure to high FFAs can disrupt AMP-activated protein kinase activity, leading to further triglyceride accumulation. This accumulation alters insulin signaling in pancreatic α-cells, prompting excessive glucagon release and creating a detrimental cycle of IR and elevated TG [65]. These processes contribute to impaired glucose tolerance and set the stage for the development of prediabetes and T2DM. Second, HDL-C normally exerts protective effects against DM through various mechanisms, such as enhancing insulin secretion, facilitating glucose uptake by skeletal muscle, and improving overall insulin sensitivity. When HDL-C levels decrease—reflected in an elevated TG/HDL-C ratio—these protective mechanisms become compromised. Low HDL-C levels correlate with diminished antioxidative and anti-inflammatory capacity, leading to increased oxidative stress and chronic inflammation—both critical contributors to β-cell dysfunction and IR progression [66]. In addition, in vitro studies suggest that LDL-C may inhibit cyclinβ1 expression in pancreatic β-cells, exacerbating IR. Conversely, HDL-C has been shown to mitigate the adverse effects of LDL-C, potentially improving IR and aiding glucose regulation [67, 68]. Third, elevated TG/HDL-C ratios are closely linked to ectopic fat accumulation in metabolically active tissues, particularly within the liver (hepatic steatosis) and pancreas. This ectopic lipid buildup disrupts normal insulin signaling pathways in these tissues, contributing to systemic IR and impaired glucose homeostasis [57]. Forth, in patients with CKD, the relationship between TG/HDL-C ratio and DM may be further amplified by CKD-associated metabolic perturbations. These patients often experience altered lipid metabolism, chronic inflammation, and oxidative stress, which can amplify the diabetogenic effects of dyslipidemia. The uremic environment typical of CKD may additionally impair HDL function beyond mere reductions in HDL-C levels, further compromising the protective mechanisms against diabetes development [69, 70]. Lastly, the non-linear relationship we observed, with an inflection point at TG/HDL-C ratio of 1.030, suggests potential threshold effects in the e related biological pathways. Below this threshold, metabolic perturbations linked to dyslipidemia may reach critical levels where compensatory mechanisms become insufficient, thereby accelerating diabetes risk. This observation aligns with previous reports indicating threshold effects in the association between lipids and diabetes across various populations [58–62]. As such, the intricate interactions among IR, β-cell dysfunction, TG, HDL-C, altered lipid metabolism, chronic inflammation, and oxidative stress in patients with CKD may increase the risk of developing and advancing DM.

Study strengths and limitations

Our cross-sectional study boasts various methodological merits underscoring the robustness and implications of these findings. Firstly, the study benefits from a relatively large sample size when compared to previous research in the field, providing a robust basis for our analyses. Secondly, we have rigorously addressed and explored the issue of nonlinearity within our data. Furthermore, as an observational study, we acknowledge the inherent risk of potential confounding. To mitigate this, we have applied strict statistical adjustments aimed at minimizing the impact of residual confounders. This includes the innovative use of GAM for integrating continuous covariates as curves, enhancing the precision of our analysis. Furthermore, we employed E-value calculations to evaluate the potential impact of unmeasured confounders on our findings, thereby reinforcing the credibility of our conclusions.

However, there are limitations to our study that warrant consideration. Reliance on data exclusively from Hebei Province, China, may limit the generalizability of our findings to other regions or populations. The cross-sectional design prevents establishing conclusive cause-and-effect relationships and limits our ability to establish temporal relationships between TG/HDL-C ratio and diabetes. Our findings represent associations at a specific point in time rather than predictive relationships. The observed associations require validation in properly designed longitudinal studies before causal relationships can be established. Our secondary analysis was limited to variables collected in the original study dataset, which did not include comprehensive assessment of potentially relevant factors such as BMI, FBG, type of diabetes, medication history, socioeconomic status, socioeconomic status, dietary patterns, lifestyle factors, family medical history, physical activity, and sleep characteristics. The absence of these variables may result in residual confounding that could influence the observed associations. In the future, prospective cohort studies with adequate follow-up periods should incorporate comprehensive assessments of BMI, FBG, diabetes subtypes, socioeconomic factors, dietary patterns, sleep quality, and detailed lifestyle characteristics. Such studies would enhance our understanding of potential modifying or mediating factors in the TG/HDL-C ratio-diabetes relationship and establish definitive associations between longitudinal changes in TG/HDL-C ratios and diabetes development among patients with CKD. Moreover, we did not include the 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test or measurements of glycosylated hemoglobin levels, deviating from the 1999 WHO recommendations for diagnosing diabetes. This omission may have led to underdiagnosis of diabetes in our population—though logistical challenges in administering these tests at scale were significant barriers. Additionally, the demographic composition of our cohort—predominantly male and drawn from a specific occupational group in Northern China—may further limit the applicability of our findings. The study focused on a workforce cohort from this region, with a participation rate of 65.31% among the 155,418 employees invited. Notably, the Kailuan Company’s employment records revealed a marked gender imbalance, with males representing 85.93% of the company’s workforce. This disparity was reflected, in our study cohort, where men constitute 79.90% of participants [36, 37]. While focusing on a workforce cohort reduces variability due to socioeconomic factors, it underscores the need for future research to include more diverse populations. Our study population was limited to individuals aged 45 years and older with CKD, reflecting the design of the original study. While this population represents the demographic most commonly affected by both CKD and T2DM, our findings may not be generalizable to younger individuals with CKD. Younger patients may have different metabolic profiles, CKD etiologies, and potentially different relationships between lipid parameters and glucose metabolism. Future studies specifically examining these relationships in younger CKD cohorts would complement our findings and provide a more comprehensive understanding across the age spectrum. The potential influence of unmeasured variables, particularly medication use, remains a concern inherent to observational studies. Future studies should incorporate a broader range of variables, including detailed medication histories, to enhance the analysis and provide more comprehensive insights into the relationships explored. Despite these limitations, the use of E-value assessments provides confidence in the validity of our findings. The derived E-value, at 2.36, surpasses the relative likelihood linked to both unidentified confounders and the TG/HDL-C ratio which underscored the robustness of the observed correlation between the TG/HDL-C ratio and DM odds, affirming its resilience against the distortion by unmeasured confounders.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated a significant and independent association between the TG/HDL-C ratio and DM in patients with CKD. The relationship exhibited a non-linear pattern with an identified inflection point at a TG/HDL-C ratio of 1.030, below which the association is particularly pronounced. These findings suggested that the TG/HDL-C ratio may serve as a valuable marker for DM assessment in patients with CKD, with enhanced predictive utility in specific subgroups, particularly female patients, patients with AF and those without hypertension. Future prospective studies are warranted to establish causal relationships and investigate whether interventions targeting lipid profiles can effectively reduce diabetes incidence in this high-risk population.
