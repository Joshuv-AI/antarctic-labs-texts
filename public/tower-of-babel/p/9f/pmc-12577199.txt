Exposure to pesticides and renal function in agricultural workers in Rafsanjan, Iran: a case-control study
Authors: Faezeh Nosratabadi, Zahra Assadollahi, Alireza Saedi, Mahdieh Jamshidi, Mohammad Amin Lotfi, Hadi Eslami, Sadegh Salehipour, Soudeh Khanamani Falahati-pour, Mahdieh Azin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpraying farmer are at increased risk of exposure to pesticides, which may negatively affect kidney function. Despite worldwide concern, there is limited research on occupational pesticide exposure in spraying farmer on the kidney impacts in Rafsanjan, Iran. This study aimed to investigate the association between occupational pesticide exposure and kidney function among agricultural workers in Rafsanjan, focusing on serum creatinine (Cr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), sodium (Na), potassium (K), and glomerular filtration rate (GFR).MethodsA 10-month case-control study was conducted in 2023, in which 150 participants were divided into three groups: 50 pesticide spraying farmers, 50 residents near pistachio orchards, and 50 urban controls who were not exposed to pesticides. Data collection included demographic information, medical history, and laboratory measurements of kidney function indicators. Pesticide exposure was determined by Intensity of exposure to pesticides, monthly pesticide exposure index and cumulative exposure index. Statistical analyses included MANOVA to adjust for age and assess group differences, and correlation analysis to examine relationships between exposure and kidney function index.ResultsSerum Cr levels were significantly higher and GFR significantly lower in both sprayer farmers and residents near pistachio orchards compared to controls, indicating impaired kidney function in the direct and indirect exposure groups. BUN, Na, and K levels were increased in the at-risk groups, although these increases were not significant. Among sprayers, use of personal protective equipment was associated with lower BUN levels. Positive correlations were observed between cumulative exposure index and spraying years with BUN and negative correlations with GFR, indicating cumulative kidney impairment.ConclusionOccupational and environmental exposure to pesticides is associated with impaired kidney function among agricultural workers and residents near pistachio orchards in Rafsanjan. Use of PPE among spraying farmers was associated with reduced BUN—but not Cr or GFR—suggesting that any potential protective effect of PPE for the kidney may be limited and influenced by the sensitivity of BUN to non-renal factors.

Introduction

Pesticides, essential for pest control in agriculture, pose significant risks beyond their intended targets, affecting non-target organisms, including humans [1, 2]. Their pervasive use has resulted in widespread environmental contamination and serious public health concerns worldwide [3, 4]. Agricultural workers face particularly high exposure levels, with numerous studies reporting adverse health effects linked to pesticide residues in food, water, and soil [5, 6]. From among these chemicals, organophosphorus and carbamates are prominent given their efficacy and short persistence in the environment [7]. Organophosphates, widely used insecticides, are associated with acute poisoning incidents, particularly in regions that heavily reliant on agricultural practices [8, 9]. Exposure typically occurs through multiple routes, including inhalation, ingestion, and dermal contact, resulting in systemic toxicity affecting organs such as the kidneys, liver, and nervous system [10–12]. Pesticides encompass various categories such as insecticides, fungicides, rodenticides, and herbicides, each with distinct chemical compositions and toxicological profiles [13, 14]. Insecticides, for instance, include cholinesterase inhibitors such as carbamates and organophosphates, inhibiting neurotransmitter activity and can cause acute cholinergic syndrome [15]. Chronic exposure to pesticides, even at low levels, remains a concern, particularly for agricultural and greenhouse workers, where long-term health effects are challenging to ascertain [16, 17].

Epidemiological and toxicological studies have linked pesticide exposure to oxidative stress, which impairs antioxidant mechanisms and aggravates inflammatory responses [18, 19]. Oxidative stress, characterized by an imbalance between free radicals and antioxidants, is involved in cellular damage and inflammatory cascades, potentially contributing to chronic kidney disease (CKD) and acute kidney injury (AKI) [20, 21]. AKI, marked by a sudden decline in kidney function, and CKD, characterized by persistent kidney damage over months, are both associated with pesticide-induced nephrotoxicity [22, 23].

Although the pathogenesis of renal injury is not fully understood, multiple mechanisms have been proposed. These include elevated pseudocholinesterase levels in the renal distal convoluted tubule, oxidative stress from high intratubular toxin concentrations, rhabdomyolysis, and dehydration-induced hypovolemia [24]. Likewise, to explore the association between organophosphate exposure and a higher risk of AKI, a retrospective cohort study of over 23 million people in Taiwan was conducted. This study found that the incidence of AKI was higher in patients suffering from organophosphate poisoning than in controls [25]. Organophosphates cause vasoconstriction and changes in vascular tone in the kidneys. They impact the release and activity of vasoactive substances such as nitric oxide and endothelin, causing changes in renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate. These vascular effects can lead to renal hypoperfusion and ischemic injury [24]. Organophosphates can also directly damage renal tubular cells. They disrupt the integrity of the tubular epithelium, impair tubular transport functions, and induce tubular cell death. Loss of tubular cell function results in impaired reabsorption and secretion processes, ultimately causing changes in fluid and electrolyte balance and the development of AKI [26].

Overall, a significant research gap exists given the ambiguity surrounding the primary mechanisms by which pesticides affect renal function, and the notable lack of studies examining kidney function among agricultural workers exposed to pesticides in Rafsanjan, Iran. This study seeks to address this gap by investigating the prevalence and impact of kidney disorders arising from occupational pesticide exposure, with the goal of informing evidence-based strategies to protect the health of agricultural workers.

Materials and methods

Study design and sampling

This 10-month case-control study was conducted in 2023 in Rafsanjan city, involving the health centers for participant selection as well as the laboratory of the Medical School at Rafsanjan University of Medical Sciences for biochemical analyses. A total of 150 participants were selected, comprising 50 spraying farmer, 50 residents near pistachio orchards, and 50 people living in an urban area and working in a non-farming occupation with no exposure to pesticides. The method of selecting agricultural sprayers was snowball sampling. Two other groups were included in the study by announcing a call for participation and via convenient sampling.

The inclusion criteria for farmers required spraying agricultural orchards at least twice a year. For those living near agricultural orchards and those residing in Rafsanjan city, eligibility required employment outside of agriculture and spraying, as along with completion of an informed consent form. Exclusion criteria included employment at the Sarcheshmeh Copper Complex, a BMI greater than 30, AKI, CKD, diabetes, chronic hypertension, cancers, a history of any kidney or urinary tract surgery, and prior exposure to harmful chemical compounds other than pesticides. All participants were asked to continue their routine diet without any restrictions on water or food along the study.

The study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Rafsanjan University of Medical Sciences (IR.RUMS.REC.1399.210). Also, all methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with informed consent obtained from all participants.

Data collection

Participants’ information, including demographic characteristics, history of drug and supplement use, and disease history, was recorded in the researcher’s checklist. A pesticide exposure checklist [27] was completed, and laboratory examinations were performed on the participants. Kidney function was evaluated through laboratory indicators such as BUN and Cr [28, 29]. Blood samples were collected from each group in order to measure levels of blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr), and electrolytes (sodium (Na), and potassium (K)) using spectrophotometry and an autoanalyzer. The glomerular filtration rate (GFR) was calculated using the Cockcroft-Gault formula. The most common measure of kidney activity was calculated using the Cockcroft-Gault formula: GFR = (140 − age) ×weight​/ plasma creatinine×72 × (0.85 in women) [30].

For the study, 5 ml of venous blood was drawn from each participant and placed in tubes without anticoagulant for BUN, Cr, Na, and K measurements. The samples were transferred to the laboratory within two hours, centrifuged, with the serum separated and stored at -70 degrees Celsius. The levels of BUN, Cr, Na, and K were then measured using spectrophotometry (Spect GENESYS 20) and an autoanalyzer (DIRUI, CS-400, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Pesticide exposure calculation

To examine pesticide exposure, several factors were considered: the processes of transportation, mixing, using pesticides, as well as repairing or cleaning spraying equipment. Exposure levels were influenced by the type of activity (e.g., using poisons, mixing), method of use (e.g., backpack sprayer, hand sprayer, speed sprayer), usage of personal protective equipment (PPE) (PPE such as gloves, masks, boots, clothing), along with work habits and personal hygiene practices (e.g., changing clothes or bathing after work). The intensity of pesticide exposure was calculated as follows:

Intensity of exposure to pesticides = (mixing mode + method of use + equipment repair or container washing) ×use of PPE.

The mixing mode was categorized into three levels: Never mixing was represented by a value of 0, less than 50% of mixing time involved was denoted by a value of 3 and, greater than 50% of mixing time involved was assigned a value of 9. The application method comprised seven levels: No spraying corresponding to a value of 0, aerial application and tablet distribution both represented by a value of 1, hole application valued at 2, tractor application assigned a value of 3, backpack application corresponding to a value of 8 and, hand spray application assigned a value of 9. The status of equipment repair or washing was a two-level variable: No repair or washing represented by 0 and equipment repair or washing corresponding to a value of 2.

There were four groups for Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) use, categorized as follows: PPE-0: 0% protection, no PPE used, PPE-1: 20% protection, (one of the following is observed: Face shield or glasses, Leather or cloth gloves, and boots), PPE-2: 30% protection, (one of the following is used: Special respirator, disposable clothing), PPE-3: 40% protection, special rubber gloves resistant to chemicals used. The PPE usage variable had eight levels: 1 = PPE-0, 0.8 = PPE-1, 0.7 = PPE-2, 0.6 = PPE-3, simultaneous use of: 0.5 = PPE-2 and PPE-1, 0.4 = PPE-3 and PPE-1, 0.3 = PPE-3 and PPE-2, and 0.1 = PPE-4 (combined protection of PPE-3 and PPE-2).

The monthly pesticide exposure index was determined by:

Monthly pesticide exposure index = years of spraying× number of days spraying per year​/30 days.

The cumulative exposure index was calculated as:

Cumulative exposure index = intensity of exposure×years of spraying×number of days spraying per year [31, 32].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22 software. Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. The normality of quantitative variable distributions was first ascertained. Next, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test were employed to assess the homogeneity of the three groups regarding confounding variables.

Given the lack of age homogeneity among the groups, MANOVA was employed to compare other quantitative variables and to adjust for the effect of age. The assumptions of MANOVA, including the homogeneity of variances of dependent variables across groups, were evaluated using Levene’s test. Relationships between two quantitative variables were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient when parametric assumptions were fulfilled, or Spearman’s correlation coefficient when they were not. The significance level for all statistical tests was set at 0.05.

Results

Demographic and clinical information

The demographic information of the studied groups is reported in Table 1. The most common level of education among participants was a diploma (39.3%), while postgraduate degrees were the least common, with only two individuals (1.3%) holding such qualifications. There was no statistically significant difference in education level among the three groups studied (p = 0.128), demonstrating that the groups were largely homogeneous in terms of educational attainment. Considering medical history, 110 participants (73.3%) had no history of illness. There was a statistically significant difference between the groups in terms of disease history (p = 0.011), with a higher prevalence among those living in near pistachio orchards. The study found no statistically significant difference among the three groups regarding herbal medicine and alcohol use (p > 0.05). Nevertheless, there were statistically significant differences in the history of using certain drugs, smoking, and consumption of coffee and tea (p < 0.05). Further, there were no statistically significant differences in clinical variables such as urine color, urine volume, presence of blood in urine, and reduction in pressure or urine flow (p > 0.05).

Comparison of kidney function indicators in the three groups

Because of the lack of age homogeneity among the groups, MANOVA was utilized to compare serum Cr, BUN, Na, K, and GFR as well as to adjust for the effect of age (Table 2). It was found that the three groups have a significant difference in terms of Cr serum level after adjusting for age (P < 0.0001). Post hoc results revealed that the amount of Cr was significantly higher in the spraying group than in the control group (p = 0.001), as well as in the group living in near pistachio orchards compared to the control group (p < 0.0001). A significant difference in GFR was also seen (P < 0.0001), with the GFR being significantly lower in the spraying group compared to the control group (P < 0.0001) and in the group living in near pistachio orchards compared to the control group (P < 0.0001). Although urea levels were higher in pesticide spraying farmers and residents near pistachio orchards when compared to the control group, there was no significant difference between the three groups (p = 0.197). Further, the levels of Na (p = 0.066) and K (p = 0.941) rose in the two groups of farmers and residents near pistachio orchards compared to the control group, but there was no significant difference.

Pesticide exposure of sprayer farmers

Among pesticide spraying farmers, individuals with 50 years of experience comprised the largest group (3 persons, 6%), while those with four years of experience constituted the smallest group (1 person, 4%). Regarding spraying history, the majority had 50 years of experience (2 people, 4%), while the minority had four years (1 person, 2%). All participants in the spraying group (100%) utilized organophosphorus pesticides, with 2% spraying several times a week and 90% spraying only a few times a year. The primary method of pesticide application (100%) was by tractor. Concerning equipment maintenance, 70% of the spraying group engaged in cleaning and repairing, mainly using water. Furthermore, 90% of the group mixed pesticides independently. Usage of personal protective equipment (PPE) varied: PPE-0 (0%) used no protection, PPE-1 (20%) applied face shields, goggles, or gloves, PPE-2 (30%) utilized respirators or disposable clothing, and PPE-3 (40%) made use of chemical-resistant rubber gloves. Most participants (72%) disposed of pesticide containers in the environment, while 26% kept them and 2% sold them.

There was no significant difference in serum Cr levels and electrolytes Na and K, as well as GFR between pesticide sprayers who used PPE and those who did not. In the pesticide spraying farmer group, those using PPE had significantly lower BUN levels than those who did not utilize PPE (p < 0.05)) Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study explored the influence of pesticide exposure on kidney function among farmers, highlighting significant health concerns in agricultural settings where pesticide use is prevalent. The kidney is one of the main organs in the human body that detoxifies, removes, and eliminates toxic chemicals. The results of present study revealed that the serum Cr level in the spraying farmer and residents near pistachio orchards were significantly higher than in the control group. Also, In the spraying farmer and residents near pistachio orchards, GFR was significantly lower than in the control group. Although the serum BUN, Na and K level increased sequentially from the control group to the people living near the agricultural orchards and then the spraying farmer, this rise was not significant.

The current findings regarding serum Cr levels align with an Indian study which reported significantly elevated Cr levels in pesticide-exposed groups. Both studies confirm pesticide exposure heightens serum Cr, demonstrating possible renal impact. However, the non-significant rise in Na levels among pesticide-spraying farmers in our study contrasts with the Indian research, which observed significant Na elevation [33]. Our non-significant Na results diverge from the Indian study’s findings, suggesting that contextual factors (e.g., pesticide types and exposure duration) may influence electrolyte changes. Javeres et at, found that exposure to organophosphorus pesticides caused a significant rise in BUN and Cr levels in Pakistani farmers. This study revealed that chronic exposure to a mixture of organophosphates may contribute to the development of metabolic syndrome and other chronic diseases, including CKD [34]. A significant increase in urea and creatinine in the pesticide exposure group compared to the control group has also been observed in other studies. It is concluded that exposure to pesticides causes renal dysfunction in male agricultural workers [35]. The current study is in line with these study in terms of increased Cr, but differs regarding BUN levels. This discrepancy highlights the need for further investigation into variables influencing biomarker expression across populations. There are conflicting results on short-term and long-term exposure to pesticides in studies. For instance, in a study on greenhouse workers exposed to pesticides, Cr levels were reduced compared to the control group in the short-term exposure period. However, this parameter increased along the long-term exposure period [36]. Farmers with long-term exposure (more than ten years of work experience) may develop hepatic-toxicity and/or nephrotoxicity [37]. This highlights the potential long-term health risks associated with pesticide exposure and underscores the significance of protective measures as well as monitoring for those regularly handling these chemicals.

In the current research, sprayer farmers who used PPE had significantly lower BUN levels compared to those who did not. However, the remaining variables did not differ significantly between the two groups. The selective association of PPE with BUN may be because BUN is sensitive to a number of non-renal variables, such as protein intake, body hydration status, tissue catabolism, and liver function. These factors can influence BUN levels independently of kidney function, thereby making BUN more susceptible to environmental or occupational interventions like PPE, as compared to Cr or GFR. Cr and GFR are considered more stable markers of kidney function and are less affected by extrarenal factors [38, 39]. The lack of association with Cr and GFR suggests that the protective effect of PPE on renal function may be limited or marker-dependent and should be interpreted with caution. Proper training of sprayer farmers in the use of PPE could be effective in this regard. On the other hand, Hardt et al. found that PPE might promote contamination, as it can absorb chemicals and some can migrate through the material within ten minutes. Thus, while PPE offers protection, its effectiveness varies based on type and proper usage [40].

The results of the present study revealed that spraying and farming years positively correlated with BUN levels, indicating that with increasing years of farming and pesticide spraying, the level of BUN in the blood rose. Spraying and farming years negatively correlated with Na levels and GFR. This correlation between increasing years of exposure to elevated urea and diminishing GFR suggested that kidney damage grows with increasing pesticide exposure. Nevertheless, in the case of Na, we expected a positive correlation, and more studies with a larger number of samples are needed in this area. In this regard, the results of a study in East Java, regarding serum Na levels before and after pesticide application, showed that there was a significant increase in Na after spraying, probably indicating accumulation of pesticides in the kidneys and development of nephrotoxicity [41].

The cumulative exposure index also correlated positively with BUN and negatively with Cr. Meanwhile, we expected a positive correlation with Cr, and future studies with large sample size are needed. Altogether, these correlation findings emphasize the potential harmful influence of long-term pesticide exposure on kidney health.

The majority of participants had an educational level of diploma, while those with postgraduate degrees were notably scarce. The lack of statistically significant differences in education levels across the groups indicates homogeneity regarding educational attainment. This homogeneity in education suggests that any observed differences in other variables are less likely to be confounded by educational disparities.

A significant finding was related to the medical history of participants. A large majority, 110 individuals, reported no history of illness. Nevertheless, there was a statistically significant difference in disease history among the groups, notably with higher prevalence in those living near pistachio orchards. This may indicate an environmental factor associated with proximity to specific agricultural activities. People living in pistachio orchards, though not engaged in agricultural work, seem to be indirectly exposed to pesticides because of their proximity to the pistachio orchards.

The study also delved into the use of certain substances. While no differences were observed in the use of herbal medicine or alcohol consumption, there were significant differences regarding smoking as well as the consumption of coffee and tea. Specifically, smoking rates were higher in the sprayer farmer group (28%) compared to others, while coffee and tea consumption was markedly different, suggesting lifestyle and consumption patterns linked to occupational exposure or environmental factors. According to Kong Tip et al., smoking prevalence among Thai farmers was 19.2%, whereas alcohol consumption was 66.7% (39). These findings reflect the importance of comprehensive lifestyle assessments in developing effective health interventions for farming communities.

The limitations of this study included its cross-sectional design and the limited duration of the survey of farmer. Although cross-sectional studies cannot prove causality, they are suitable for identifying initial associations and can pave the way for future research. In such a study design we could not investigate causality. No pre- and post-spraying surveys were conducted on farmers. Further, we could not measure other kidney function parameters (such as chloride, bicarbonate and …) and pesticide metabolites. Also, in the present study, due to financial limitations, we were unable to measure more sensitive renal markers such as cystatin C and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin. In this research, attempts were made to consider confounding factors such as concurrent exposure to other pollutants, underlying diseases (such as high blood pressure or diabetes), and dehydration, which could affect kidney function, as exclusion criteria, but lifestyle factors (such as diet, etc.) were not inspected in detail. In addition, potential environmental exposures through water and food intake were not examined in this study. Due to the limited sample size in some study groups, we were unable to simultaneously adjust for all confounding variables (e.g., smoking, coffee/tea consumption, and herbal medicine use) using multivariate regression models, as this could lead to model overfitting or reduced statistical power. Also, since the PPE scoring system is subjective, it should be considered as one of the limitations of the study.

Conclusion

Overall, individuals involved in pesticide spraying and those residing near pistachio orchards exhibited significantly higher serum Cr levels and lower GFR compared to unexposed controls, indicating impaired kidney function. While elevations of serum BUN, Na, and K were observed in exposed groups, these changes did not reach statistical significance, probably highlighting the complexity of biomarker responses to pesticide exposure as well as the potential influence of contextual factors such as pesticide type, exposure duration, and individual susceptibility. The observed positive correlation between years of pesticide exposure and BUN levels, together with the negative correlation with GFR, maybe underscores the potential for cumulative renal damage associated with prolonged occupational contact with pesticides. The use of PPE among sprayer farmers was associated with reduced BUN levels, possibly due to the sensitivity of BUN to non-renal factors. While no significant effect was observed on more stable kidney markers such as Cr and GFR, suggesting that the kidney protective effects of PPE may be limited and dependent on correct use.
