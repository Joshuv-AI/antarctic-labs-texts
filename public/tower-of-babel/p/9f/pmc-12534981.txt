Evaluating the performance of a novel double-threaded dynamic stabilization system: a finite element study
Authors: Mehmet Yigit Akgun, Melihcan Savasci, Nazenin Durmus, Caner Gunerbuyuk, Tunc Oktenoglu, Ozkan Ates, Ali Fahir Ozer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the biomechanical performance of a novel dual-cord and dual-spacer posterior dynamic stabilization system compared to a conventional single-threaded construct.MethodsA validated finite element (FE) model of the L1–S1 lumbar spine was developed. Posterior dynamic stabilization was simulated at the L4–L5 segment using two systems: a traditional polyethylene terephthalate (PET) cord with polycarbonate urethane (PCU) spacer (single-threaded), and a dual PET cord–spacer construct. Both systems were analyzed under full range of motion (ROM) loading and physiological loads using Abaqus software to simulate stress distribution and motion.ResultsThe dual-cord system enhanced segmental stability at L4–5 by approximately 22% while preserving adjacent level mobility within normal physiological limits. Peak stress levels on implant components increased marginally but remained within safe thresholds.ConclusionThe dual-cord dynamic stabilization system demonstrates improved biomechanical stability with minimal adjacent segment compromise. These results support its potential for reducing long-term mechanical failure risks in lumbar stabilization.

Introduction

Dynamic stabilization systems have emerged as an alternative to rigid spinal instrumentation, aiming to maintain controlled mobility while reducing the risk of adjacent segment degeneration. Recent studies have evaluated the comparative effectiveness of these systems in both lumbar and cervical applications [1–3]. Li et al. [1] compared interspinous dynamic stabilization to endoscopic decompression in lumbar stenosis; Song et al. [2] assessed postoperative muscle degeneration in dynamic vs. fusion systems; and Chung et al. [3] performed a biomechanical comparison of dynamic and static cervical plates in terms of implant behavior and tissue impact.

Spinal disorders associated with chronic back pain can be managed by using rigid fixation systems [4]. These systems are designed to provide immediate postoperative stability. However, they have been linked to several drawbacks, including the imposition of high mechanical stress on adjacent segments due to hypermobility. This often results in long-term degenerative changes, necessitating further fusion surgeries [5, 6]. Additionally, complications such as pedicle screw loosening or failure have been reported with rigid pedicle screw-based stabilization systems [6–9]. Clinically, while these devices achieve fusion of the involved segment, many patients continue to experience persistent pain, suggesting that rigid fixations are not entirely successful.

Dynamic stabilization systems have emerged as alternatives to rigid systems for spinal surgeries [10, 11]. These systems aim to improve load sharing while ensuring sufficient segmental stability and pain relief [12–14]. Unlike rigid systems, these devices can potentially increase the load on the intervertebral disc of the treated segment, promoting disc regeneration in the early stages of the disease [15–18]. Approaches like the Dynesys system aim to address the limitations associated with traditional fusion surgeries, offering patients a surgical option that alleviates pain while maintaining physiological spinal movement. However, challenges arise in long-segment deformities where thread/spacer failures have been observed [19, 20].

The innovation of the "Double Hole" transpedicular screw marks a significant advancement in dynamic stabilization technology. This specially designed screw supports two tensioned Polyethylene terephthalate (PET) cords and dual Polycarbonate urethane (PCU) spacers, enhancing mechanical redundancy and durability in long-segment constructs. By incorporating two thread tensions, this novel screw system aims to enhance the overall reliability of dynamic stabilization constructs, potentially addressing a critical limitation of existing rope systems.

To address the durability limitations observed in existing posterior dynamic systems—especially in long-segment applications—we propose a novel double-threaded stabilization construct incorporating dual PET cords and PCU spacers. This biomechanical design aims to improve load-sharing and reduce peak implant stress while preserving physiological motion. In this study, we perform a detailed finite element (FE) analysis comparing the proposed system to a conventional single-threaded model, focusing on range of motion (ROM), implant stress distribution, and adjacent segment mechanics.

Methods

Lumbar finite element model

The geometry of the lumbar spine was obtained from computed tomography (CT) scans of a healthy 35-year-old male. The CT data were processed using medical image processing software (Mimics® Version 14.1; Materialise, Inc., Leuven, Belgium) to create a three-dimensional surface model of the vertebrae from L1 to the sacrum (Fig. 1). Material properties, loading and boundary conditions, and simulations were performed using Abaqus software (Abaqus®, Version 6.10–2; Abaqus, Inc., Providence, RI, USA).Fig. 1Finite element mesh of the lumbar spine (L1–sacrum) created from CT data of a healthy adult male. The model includes cortical and cancellous structures and serves as the base for biomechanical simulation

Finite element mesh of the lumbar spine (L1–sacrum) created from CT data of a healthy adult male. The model includes cortical and cancellous structures and serves as the base for biomechanical simulation

The intact L1–L5 lumbar spine model was validated across three motion planes using data from previously published in vitro studies [21]. Segmental rotation was assessed and compared to values reported in the literature [22]. To simulate physiological loading, a pure moment of 10 Newton-meters (Nm) was applied along with a 400 Newtons (N) follower load during flexion–extension and axial rotation tests. The resulting motion response was then evaluated against in vitro findings reported in the literature, serving as a basis for validation [18, 21–23]. Figure 2 illustrates the comparison between the ROM predicted by our intact FE model and experimental ROM values reported in the literature. The FE model predictions fall within the experimental range in all loading directions, supporting the model's validity. The validated model has also been used in our previous studies to support further biomechanical analyses [24–26]. Fig. 2Predicted ROM compared with experimental data from prior in vitro studies [15, 18]

Predicted ROM compared with experimental data from prior in vitro studies [15, 18]

PCU mechanical properties were defined based on previous studies [27, 28], and PET cord elasticity and strength were obtained from [29].

The vertebrae mesh was divided into three regions: the cortical layer, cancellous core, and posterior elements. An elastic isotropic constitutive model was used to simulate the material properties of these regions.

A mesh convergence study was conducted at the L4–L5 segment using three mesh densities (coarse, medium, and fine). The model was subjected to flexion loading, and ROM was measured. The difference in ROM between medium and fine mesh models was less than 5%, indicating mesh independence. Therefore, the medium mesh was selected for all simulations to balance accuracy and computational cost.

The intervertebral disc, composed of the annulus fibrosus and nucleus pulposus, was modeled as a composite structure. The annulus was depicted using a circular mesh pattern to replicate its layered structure. The Neo‑Hookean hyperelastic model was applied to the annulus ground substance, defined by the strain energy density:

W = C1 (Ī1—3) + D1 (J-1)^2, where Ī1 is the first deviatoric strain invariant, J is the determinant of the deformation gradient, and C1, C2, D1 are material constants. The nucleus pulposus, constituting approximately 40% of the disc, was simulated with low stiffness [1 megapascal (MPa)] and near-incompressibility (Poisson’s ratio, ν = 0.4999). The fiber behavior in the annulus was reinforced using the rebar option in Abaqus, oriented at ± 25° relative to the horizontal, and modeled with the “no compression” option to emulate tensile behavior.

In this model, C1 and D1 govern the shear and bulk response of the annulus matrix, respectively. The C2 term, often used in extended hyperelastic formulations, was not included here as it was not required for the simplified Neo-Hookean model. Surgery stimulation

Posterior fixation at the L4-L5 lumbar vertebrae was achieved using a Dynesys implant (Fig. 5). While Dynesys implants typically use screws to hold a single PCU spacer, we modified the design to accommodate two PCU spacers. The Computer Aided Design (CAD) model for the modified Dynesys implant was created. The surgical constructs, including the titanium screws, PCU spacers, and PET cords, were modeled using SolidWorks based on actual dimensions from implant datasheets. The components were assembled and aligned with the native vertebrae in Abaqus to replicate surgical positioning. Fig. 5Posterior dynamic fixation at L4–L5 with the Dynesys system. The model was tested under flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation

Posterior dynamic fixation at L4–L5 with the Dynesys system. The model was tested under flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation

No separate mesh sensitivity study was conducted for the implant components, as their relative stiffness and simpler geometry rendered them less sensitive to mesh density in the context of global ROM outputs. A uniform mesh size of was applied to all implant models, consistent with prior studies.

Following the insertion of the Dynesys implant at the L4-L5 levels, a pretension of 300N was applied to each thread in the system. The bone-screw interface was modeled using a "TIE" constraint, and the contact between the PCU spacer and the screw head was defined using surface-to-surface interaction.

Loads and boundary conditions

A pure moment of 7.5 Nm was applied to the L1 vertebra in all directions. A follower load of 400 N was applied on each side of the vertebra using connector elements between each set of adjacent vertebrae. The nodes of the sacrum were constrained in all directions.

Discussion

Summary of findings

In this study, we conducted a finite element analysis comparing the biomechanical performance of a novel double-threaded dynamic stabilization system with the widely used single-threaded Dynesys system. Our results show that the double-threaded system provides greater segmental control while maintaining flexibility. Although the system demonstrates increased stiffness, it did not lead to increased adjacent segment degeneration—a finding that challenges earlier assumptions [30, 31]. Load distribution analysis further revealed that the second thread in the double-threaded system bears a larger portion of the load, thereby reducing stress on the first thread and potentially improving implant longevity.

Segmental ROM at the instrumented level (L4–L5) was significantly reduced—up to 98.04% compared to the intact model—yet adjacent segment mobility remained largely unchanged. These results indicate that the double-threaded system does not excessively restrict physiological motion at non-instrumented levels.

Clinical implications

Clinically, the findings suggest that double-threaded systems may be especially useful in managing long-segment deformities where implant failure risk is higher. The load-sharing mechanism may help mitigate early hardware failure and reduce the need for revision surgery [32]. Furthermore, the ability to maintain physiological motion at adjacent segments without inducing degeneration may translate into better long-term outcomes. Additionally, in patient groups where instrumentation failure is highly likely to be predicted, consider two-stage surgery strategies, especially for patients with complex deformities [33, 34].

Biomechanical insights

The enhanced segmental control in the double-threaded system is due to the distributed constraint mechanism provided by the second thread and dual spacer. While this increases stress transfer to the screws, our FE model confirmed that these loads remain within safe thresholds [25]. Von Mises stress was higher than in the conventional Dynesys system, but screw integrity was not compromised. In fact, controlled stress transmission may have positive biological implications, potentially stimulating disc metabolism. Even in the event of partial breakage, motion preservation helps prevent the rigid failure modes seen in fusion systems [18].

Mesh convergence testing was performed on the L4–L5 segment, and no separate convergence test was conducted for implants. However, implants were meshed consistently with prior validated models. Although hybrid loading was not applied in this study, as we noted previously [26], this limits full interpretation of adjacent segment biomechanics. Future validation using hybrid test protocols is recommended.

Comparison with literature

Our findings are consistent with prior work emphasizing the benefits of motion-preserving stabilization in reducing adjacent segment disease [18, 21]. The dual-threaded system’s unique configuration optimizes load sharing and may outperform previous designs with respect to fatigue resistance and biomechanical behavior [30–32]. The absence of adverse adjacent-level effects despite increased construct stiffness may initially appear contradictory, but is likely explained by the device’s dynamic compliance and distributed load pathway.

Recent literature supports our simulation methodology. TPMS-based models under high strain rates [35], in vitro-validated lattice implants [36], and biomimetic simulations of viscoelastic systems 37 reinforce the need for detailed material modeling, as used in our study.

Limitations

This study used a simplified finite element model that did not incorporate patient-specific anatomy, bone quality variations, or hybrid loading conditions. Fatigue and wear behavior of PCU spacers and PET cords under repetitive loading were also not simulated. The addition of a second cord and spacer increases implant complexity, which may raise concerns regarding hardware fatigue, implant–bone micromotion, and surgical handling.

Future research

Patient-specific models based on CT/MRI data and bone density mapping are recommended to improve anatomical realism. Future work should also include fatigue simulations and experimental validation of long-term mechanical behavior. In vivo or cadaveric studies under hybrid loading will further clarify clinical performance.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that a novel double-threaded posterior dynamic stabilization system reduced ROM at the index segment (L4–L5) by up to 98%, while maintaining adjacent segment mobility within 5% of physiological levels. Although the implant components experienced higher von Mises stress due to increased construct rigidity, all values remained within safe mechanical limits. The dual-cord and spacer configuration enhanced load distribution and may improve long-term implant durability.

Future in vivo and clinical studies are warranted to validate these findings and assess long-term outcomes in patients with lumbar degenerative instability or multi-level pathology.
