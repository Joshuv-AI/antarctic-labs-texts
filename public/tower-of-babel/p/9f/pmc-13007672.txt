Gut-Lung Axis in COPD: Investigating the Impact of Dietary Fiber Intake on Systemic Inflammation and Lung Function Decline
Authors: Lu Lu, Juan Xu, Jian Wang, Yue-Lin Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe gut-lung axis represents a promising therapeutic target in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). This study investigated whether dietary fiber intake differs between COPD patients and healthy controls, and examined its association with systemic inflammation and lung function.MethodsA case-control study was conducted including 100 COPD patients (cases) and 100 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Dietary fiber intake was assessed using a validated food frequency questionnaire. Systemic inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6) were measured by ELISA. Lung function parameters (FEV1, FEV1/FVC, DLCO) were evaluated according to ATS/ERS guidelines. Logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the association between dietary fiber intake and COPD risk.ResultsCOPD patients had significantly lower dietary fiber intake (18.30 ± 6.20 g/day) compared to controls (28.70 ± 8.10 g/day, P < 0.001). Inflammatory markers were significantly elevated in COPD patients: CRP (5.80 ± 3.20 vs. 1.20 ± 0.80 mg/L), IL-6 (8.40 ± 4.10 vs. 2.10 ± 1.30 pg/mL) (all P < 0.001). In COPD patients, dietary fiber intake was inversely correlated with CRP (r = −0.52), IL-6 (r = −0.48), and positively correlated with FEV1 (r = 0.41) and DLCO (r = 0.38) (all P < 0.001). After adjusting for confounders, low dietary fiber intake (<20 g/day) was associated with 3.2-fold increased odds of COPD (OR = 3.24, 95% CI: 1.86–5.65, P < 0.001).ConclusionLow dietary fiber intake is significantly associated with COPD and correlates with increased systemic inflammation and reduced lung function. These findings support the potential role of the gut-lung axis in COPD pathophysiology. However, causality cannot be established due to the limitations of the cross-sectional case-control design. Prospective interventional studies are warranted to confirm these associations and evaluate whether dietary fiber modification can improve clinical outcomes in COPD patients.Keywords: COPD, gut-lung axis, dietary fiber, systemic inflammation, lung function, case-control study

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, affecting over 391 million individuals and causing approximately 3.23 million deaths annually.1 Characterized by progressive airflow limitation and persistent respiratory symptoms, COPD imposes substantial economic burdens on healthcare systems globally. While cigarette smoking remains the primary risk factor, emerging evidence suggests that COPD pathogenesis extends beyond direct airway injury to involve complex systemic inflammatory processes.2,3

Recent advances in microbiome research have unveiled the gut-lung axis as a bidirectional communication network linking intestinal and pulmonary health.4,5 This axis operates through multiple mechanisms, including microbial metabolite circulation, immune cell trafficking, and shared mucosal immunity pathways.6 Gut-derived signals reach lung tissue via blood and lymphatic circulation, influencing respiratory immune responses and inflammatory tone.7 Conversely, respiratory insults can alter gut microbiome composition, creating a feedback loop that may perpetuate chronic inflammation.8

Notably, COPD patients tend to have suboptimal dietary patterns, with studies reporting that a substantial proportion fails to meet recommended fiber intake levels. Data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) indicated that the average dietary fiber intake among adults with obstructive lung disease was approximately 15–17 g/day, considerably below the recommended 25–30 g/day.9,10 This dietary inadequacy may be attributable to disease-related factors including dyspnea-induced eating difficulties, medication-associated appetite changes, fatigue limiting meal preparation, and reduced physical activity affecting energy expenditure and dietary variety. Shaheen et al observed that individuals with COPD consumed significantly fewer fruits, vegetables, and whole grains compared to healthy individuals, suggesting a broader pattern of nutritional compromise.11 Understanding the typical fiber intake patterns in COPD populations is essential for contextualizing the potential impact of dietary modification strategies.

Dietary fiber plays a crucial role in this gut-lung crosstalk through its fermentation by intestinal microbiota into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), particularly acetate, propionate, and butyrate.12,13 These microbial metabolites exert immunomodulatory effects by activating G-protein coupled receptors (GPR41/43) and inhibiting histone deacetylases (HDACs), thereby suppressing pro-inflammatory pathways and enhancing regulatory T cell function.14 Recent experimental evidence demonstrates that dietary fiber-derived butyrate suppresses ILC2-dependent airway inflammation in COPD models.15

Epidemiological studies have consistently linked higher dietary fiber intake with reduced COPD risk and improved lung function. A 2023 meta-analysis by Valisoltani et al revealed that each 10 g/day increase in fiber consumption was associated with a 26% reduction in COPD risk.16 Population-based studies have demonstrated that individuals in the highest fiber intake quintile exhibited 60–90 mL higher FEV1 compared to those in the lowest quintile.17,18 However, the mechanistic relationships between fiber intake, systemic inflammation, and lung function in COPD patients remain incompletely characterized.

Systemic inflammation, characterized by elevated circulating inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of COPD and predictor of disease progression.19,20 Persistent systemic inflammation associates with increased exacerbation frequency, accelerated lung function decline, and higher mortality in COPD cohorts.21 Despite this recognition, the dietary determinants of systemic inflammation in COPD remain understudied.

To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a case-control study to: (1) compare dietary fiber intake between COPD patients and healthy controls; (2) examine associations between fiber intake and systemic inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6); (3) investigate correlations between fiber consumption and lung function parameters (FEV1, DLCO); and (4) determine whether low fiber intake independently associates with COPD risk after adjusting for confounders. Given the inherent limitations of observational designs in establishing causality, our study aimed to provide hypothesis-generating evidence regarding the fiber-inflammation-lung function relationship in COPD, with the goal of informing future prospective and interventional research. We hypothesized that COPD patients would exhibit lower fiber intake, that fiber consumption would inversely correlate with inflammatory markers, and that low fiber intake would independently increase COPD odds.

Participants and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This case-control study was conducted between January 2024 and December 2024 at a tertiary care hospital. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Approval Number: 2023-IRB-001, Approval Date: December 15, 2023) and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Cases comprised 100 patients with spirometry-confirmed COPD, defined as post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio <0.70 according to Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) criteria.22 Inclusion criteria for cases were: (1) age 40–80 years; (2) clinically stable disease without exacerbation or hospitalization in the preceding 4 weeks; (3) no recent changes in COPD medication regimen (≥3 months). Exclusion criteria included: (1) other chronic respiratory diseases (asthma, interstitial lung disease, bronchiectasis); (2) inflammatory bowel disease or other gastrointestinal disorders affecting nutrient absorption; (3) autoimmune conditions; (4) active infection or systemic corticosteroid use within 4 weeks; (5) malignancy or chronic kidney disease; (6) inability to complete questionnaires.

Controls comprised 100 healthy individuals recruited from the same geographic region, matched to cases by age (±5 years) and sex in a 1:1 ratio. Controls had no history of chronic respiratory disease, normal spirometry (FEV1/FVC ≥0.70), and met the same exclusion criteria as cases. Sample size was calculated based on expected 8 g/day difference in fiber intake between groups (SD=10 g/day), requiring 88 participants per group to achieve 80% power at α=0.05, with 100 per group accounting for potential data loss.23

Dietary Assessment

Dietary fiber intake was assessed using a validated semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) covering the previous 12 months.24,25 The FFQ comprised 131 food items with specified portion sizes, including comprehensive coverage of fiber-rich foods (whole grains, fruits, vegetables, legumes, nuts). Trained dietitians administered the FFQ through structured interviews. Total dietary fiber intake (g/day) was calculated using a standardized food composition database. Quality control included duplicate entry of 10% of questionnaires to ensure data accuracy (concordance rate >95%).

Inflammatory Marker Measurement

Fasting venous blood samples were collected between 07:00–09:00 after an overnight fast. Serum was separated by centrifugation (3000 rpm, 10 minutes, 4°C) within 2 hours and stored at −80°C until batch analysis. CRP concentrations were measured using immunoturbidimetric assay on an Aristo specific protein analyzer (Shenzhen Guosai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. IL-6 concentrations were quantified using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) according to manufacturer protocols.26 Assay sensitivities were 0.01 mg/L for CRP and 0.70 pg/mL for IL-6. Intra-assay coefficients of variation were <4.5% for CRP and <5.2% for IL-6. All samples were analyzed in duplicate by laboratory personnel blinded to participant group assignment.

Lung Function Testing

Spirometry was performed according to American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) 2019 guidelines using a calibrated body plethysmograph (MasterScreen Body, JAEGER, Würzburg, Germany).27 Participants performed forced expiratory maneuvers in triplicate, with the best of three reproducible measurements (within-session variability <5% or 150 mL) recorded. FEV1 and forced vital capacity (FVC) were measured, with results expressed as percentages of predicted values based on reference equations. Bronchodilator administration (400 μg salbutamol) was performed for COPD cases, with post-bronchodilator measurements recorded 15 minutes later.

Diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) was assessed using the single-breath technique according to ATS/ERS standards.28 DLCO values were corrected for hemoglobin concentration and expressed as percentage of predicted. Quality control included daily calibration verification and weekly quality control measurements.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were tested for normality using Shapiro–Wilk tests and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

Independent samples t-tests were used to compare continuous variables between groups. Chi-square tests assessed categorical variable differences. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) quantified relationships between dietary fiber intake and inflammatory markers or lung function parameters, with correlation strength interpreted as weak (|r|<0.30), moderate (0.30≤|r|<0.50), or strong (|r|≥0.50).29

Multivariable logistic regression analysis estimated odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between low dietary fiber intake (<20 g/day, based on median split) and COPD, adjusting for potential confounders including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status (never/former/current), and pack-years. Model fit was assessed using Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This case-control study provides observational evidence of an association between reduced dietary fiber intake and elevated systemic inflammation and impaired lung function in COPD patients. Our principal findings were: (1) COPD patients consumed 36% less dietary fiber than matched controls (18.30 vs. 28.70 g/day); (2) COPD patients exhibited markedly elevated inflammatory markers (CRP and IL-6); (3) fiber intake inversely correlated with inflammatory markers (r = −0.48 to −0.52) and positively correlated with lung function parameters (r = 0.38 to 0.41); and (4) low fiber intake (<20 g/day) independently associated with 3.2-fold increased COPD odds after adjusting for confounders. These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that dietary fiber may be associated with COPD risk through gut-lung axis mechanisms, although direct evidence for this pathway was not obtained in the present study.

Notably, the association between low dietary fiber intake and COPD was substantially attenuated after adjustment for confounders, with the OR decreasing from 5.23 (unadjusted) to 3.24 (adjusted), representing an approximately 38% reduction. This attenuation indicates that a considerable portion of the crude association was attributable to confounding, particularly by smoking status and cumulative pack-years, which were strongly imbalanced between groups (42% vs. 12% current smokers; 38.45 vs. 8.32 mean pack-years). This finding is expected, as smoking is both the strongest established risk factor for COPD and a recognized determinant of dietary patterns, with smokers tending to consume fewer fiber-rich foods such as fruits and vegetables. Nevertheless, the persistence of a statistically significant and clinically meaningful 3.2-fold increased odds after comprehensive adjustment suggests that the fiber-COPD association is at least partially independent of smoking exposure. Similar patterns of effect attenuation have been reported in prior studies; Varraso et al observed that the protective association between fiber intake and COPD risk was reduced but remained statistically significant after adjusting for smoking and other lifestyle factors.18 The magnitude of attenuation also underscores the importance of adequate confounder adjustment in observational studies of dietary exposures and respiratory outcomes.

It should be acknowledged that not all prior studies have reported consistent findings regarding the fiber-COPD relationship. Kaluza et al, in a prospective cohort of Swedish men, found that while total dietary fiber was associated with reduced COPD risk, the association was primarily driven by cereal and fruit fiber, with vegetable fiber showing no significant association, suggesting that fiber source may modify the observed relationship.30 Additionally, some studies have reported that the protective association between fiber and respiratory outcomes was more pronounced among current smokers than never-smokers, suggesting potential effect modification by smoking status that may limit generalizability across populations.18,31 Furthermore, Butland et al reported that dietary patterns characterized by high fruit and vegetable consumption, rather than fiber intake per se, were more strongly associated with lung function outcomes, raising the possibility that our observed fiber-COPD association may partly reflect broader dietary quality differences between groups.31 These discrepancies highlight the complexity of diet-disease relationships and underscore the need for future studies examining specific fiber types, food sources, and overall dietary patterns in relation to COPD risk and progression.

Our observation of substantially reduced fiber intake in COPD patients aligns with prior population-based studies. Valisoltani et al’s 2023 meta-analysis of eight epidemiological studies demonstrated that each 10 g/day fiber increment conferred 26% COPD risk reduction (pooled RR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.67–0.82).13 Similarly, Lin et al analyzed NHANES 2013–2018 data (n=8,246 adults) and found that individuals in the highest fiber intake quartile had 44% lower COPD prevalence compared to the lowest quartile.32 Our findings of 10.4 g/day lower fiber consumption in COPD patients translate to an estimated 2.7-fold (26% reduction per 10 g × 1.04 increments) risk increase based on meta-analytic dose-response relationships, closely matching our observed 3.2-fold adjusted OR. This concordance validates the magnitude of our effect estimate.

The hypothesized mechanistic basis for fiber’s potential protective effects may involve the gut-lung axis via short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs).9–12 Colonic fermentation of dietary fiber by anaerobic bacteria (Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes) generates acetate, propionate, and butyrate, which enter systemic circulation and reach lung tissue.11 These SCFAs exert pleiotropic anti-inflammatory effects through G-protein coupled receptor (GPR41/43) activation and histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition, resulting in suppression of NF-κB signaling, reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine production (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β), and enhanced regulatory T cell differentiation.33,34 Jiang et al recently demonstrated that butyrate supplementation suppressed ILC2-dependent airway inflammation in COPD models by reducing IL-13 and IL-5 production. Our finding of strong inverse correlations (r = −0.48 to −0.52) between fiber intake and systemic inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6) is consistent, but does not directly confirm, this mechanistic framework.

The clinical significance of our inflammatory marker findings merits emphasis. CRP elevation in our COPD cohort (mean 5.80 mg/L) falls within the range identified by Agustí et al as defining the “persistent systemic inflammation” COPD phenotype (CRP >3 mg/L), which associates with doubled mortality risk (13% vs. 2% over 3 years).18 Similarly, IL-6 levels in our COPD patients (mean 8.40 pg/mL) approach the 13 pg/mL threshold that Fahyim et al identified as predictive of acute exacerbations.35 The inverse correlations between fiber intake and these prognostically important biomarkers raise the possibility that dietary modification could influence the inflammatory phenotype, a hypothesis that warrants testing in interventional studies.

Our lung function findings extend prior observations from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) Study, which reported 60.2 mL higher FEV1 in individuals in the highest versus lowest fiber intake quintile.14 The moderate positive correlations we observed between fiber intake and FEV1 (r = 0.41) and DLCO (r = 0.38) suggest that fiber intake may be associated with lung function through potentially inflammation-related pathways. Chronic systemic inflammation promotes lung parenchymal destruction, airway remodeling, and pulmonary vascular dysfunction, manifesting as reduced FEV1 and DLCO.36,37 If confirmed in longitudinal studies, the attenuation of inflammatory burden through adequate fiber intake could potentially slow these pathological processes.

From a clinical perspective, dietary fiber represents a modifiable factor worthy of further investigation given its safety profile, low cost, and wide availability. Current COPD management guidelines emphasize pharmacological interventions (bronchodilators, inhaled corticosteroids), smoking cessation, and pulmonary rehabilitation, but dietary modifications receive minimal attention despite growing evidence supporting their efficacy.38,39 Recent systematic reviews have documented benefits of dietary interventions on respiratory function, exercise capacity, and quality of life in COPD cohorts.40,41 Our findings raise the hypothesis that increasing fiber intake may warrant evaluation as a component of comprehensive COPD management, pending confirmation from interventional studies.

Strengths and Limitations

Study strengths include the matched case-control design minimizing confounding by age and sex, use of validated dietary assessment instruments, objective inflammatory biomarker and spirometry measurements performed according to international standards, comprehensive statistical analysis adjusting for key confounders, and adequate sample size providing 80% power. The consistent direction of correlations between fiber intake and both inflammatory markers and lung function parameters provides internal coherence, although causal inference cannot be drawn from this observational design.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional case-control design precludes definitive causal inference regarding temporal relationships between fiber intake, inflammation, and COPD development. Reverse causation remains a substantial concern: COPD patients may alter their dietary patterns as a consequence of disease symptoms, including dyspnea-related eating difficulties resulting in preference for energy-dense but fiber-poor foods, medication-induced appetite suppression or taste alterations, fatigue limiting meal preparation and grocery shopping, and reduced physical activity leading to decreased energy expenditure and dietary variety. These disease-driven dietary changes could explain, at least in part, the observed lower fiber intake in the COPD group. Prospective longitudinal studies tracking dietary fiber intake before COPD diagnosis, along with serial inflammatory marker and lung function measurements, are essential to establish the temporal sequence and directionality of these associations. Second, dietary assessment via FFQ involves recall bias despite instrument validation. More rigorous dietary assessment methods, including weighed food records, 24-hour dietary recalls, or objective biomarkers of fiber intake (eg., plasma alkylresorcinols for cereal fiber), would strengthen exposure measurement and reduce potential misclassification in future studies. Third, we did not directly quantify circulating SCFA concentrations or characterize gut microbiome composition, limiting mechanistic inference regarding the gut-lung axis. Future studies should integrate metabolomics and metagenomics to map fiber-microbiome-SCFA-inflammation pathways. Fourth, residual confounding by unmeasured factors (physical activity, socioeconomic status, other dietary components) may persist despite statistical adjustment. Fifth, single-center recruitment may limit generalizability, although our sample characteristics align with multicenter COPD cohorts. Finally, we did not differentiate between soluble and insoluble fiber or fiber food sources (cereals, fruits, vegetables, legumes), which may have differential effects on gut microbiota composition, SCFA production profiles, and downstream inflammatory modulation. Experimental evidence suggests that soluble fibers (eg., pectins, β-glucans) are more readily fermented to SCFAs than insoluble fibers (eg., cellulose), potentially conferring differential anti-inflammatory benefits. Kaluza et al demonstrated that cereal and fruit fiber, but not vegetable fiber, were significantly associated with reduced COPD risk, further supporting the importance of fiber source characterization in future studies.

These findings open several research avenues. Prospective cohort studies should examine whether baseline fiber intake predicts COPD incidence, exacerbation frequency, and lung function decline trajectories. Interventional trials testing fiber supplementation effects on inflammatory markers, lung function, and patient-reported outcomes would provide definitive evidence regarding therapeutic potential. Mechanistic studies integrating gut microbiome sequencing, fecal and plasma SCFA quantification, and immune profiling would elucidate gut-lung axis pathways. Dose-response investigations could identify optimal fiber intake thresholds for respiratory health benefits. Comparative effectiveness research examining different fiber types (soluble/insoluble) and sources (whole grains, fruits, vegetables, legumes, supplements) would inform dietary recommendations. Finally, implementation science approaches addressing barriers to dietary modification in COPD populations could facilitate translation of evidence into clinical practice.

Additionally, the dichotomization of dietary fiber intake based on median split (<20 g/day) warrants discussion as a methodological limitation. While this approach facilitates clinical interpretation by identifying a practical threshold, it has recognized drawbacks: (1) dichotomization of continuous data discards within-group variation information, potentially reducing statistical power to detect true associations; (2) the study results may be sensitive to the chosen cutoff point, as a slightly different median value (eg., 19 or 21 g/day) could alter the magnitude of the estimated odds ratio, affecting the stability and generalizability of findings; and (3) this approach cannot address dose-response questions, such as the incremental risk reduction per 10 g/day increase in fiber intake, which would be more instructive for developing specific dietary recommendations. Future studies should consider analyzing fiber intake as a continuous variable or using quartile-based categorization to better capture the dose-response relationship and reduce dependency on a single arbitrary cutoff point.

Our study examined only two inflammatory markers (CRP and IL-6). While these represent the most extensively validated and clinically established systemic inflammatory biomarkers in COPD research, as demonstrated in the ECLIPSE cohort and multiple meta-analyses, a broader panel encompassing TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-8, IL-17, IL-10, and fibrinogen would provide a more comprehensive characterization of the inflammatory milieu and better elucidate the specific inflammatory pathways potentially modulated by dietary fiber through the gut-lung axis. Future studies should incorporate an expanded biomarker panel, including markers specifically implicated in gut-lung crosstalk. Multiple correlation analyses were conducted without formal correction for multiple comparisons (eg., Bonferroni correction), which may increase the risk of Type I errors. However, all four primary correlations (fiber intake with CRP, IL-6, FEV1, and DLCO) achieved significance at P < 0.001, well below the Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.0125 (0.05/4 comparisons), providing reassurance that the observed associations are unlikely to represent false positives attributable to multiple testing.

Although our sample size of 100 per group met a priori power calculations for the primary outcome (detecting an 8 g/day difference in fiber intake), it is relatively modest for an observational study involving multiple confounder adjustments. This may limit the precision of adjusted estimates, the ability to detect smaller effect sizes, and the feasibility of meaningful subgroup analyses (eg., by COPD severity or smoking status). Larger multicenter studies are warranted to validate our findings with greater statistical precision.

The substantial imbalance in smoking prevalence between groups (42% vs. 12%) raises concerns about residual confounding. Despite statistical adjustment for current smoking status and cumulative pack-years, unmeasured aspects of smoking exposure—including secondhand smoke exposure, smoking duration independent of pack-years, time since cessation, and smoking-associated behavioral and socioeconomic patterns—may contribute to residual confounding that cannot be fully eliminated through statistical modeling alone. Disease duration, GOLD stage distribution, and exacerbation history were not systematically incorporated into our analyses, precluding assessment of whether dietary fiber intake is differentially associated with disease severity, progression rate, or clinical phenotype. Future studies should examine fiber intake across different COPD stages and clinical trajectories.

The linearity assumption for continuous predictors (age, BMI, pack-years) in the logistic regression model was not formally tested using methods such as restricted cubic splines or fractional polynomials. While these variables are commonly modeled as linear predictors in comparable epidemiological studies, formal verification of linearity and consideration of potential non-linear relationships should be incorporated in future analyses. Although scatter plots presented in Figure 2 visually support approximate linear relationships between fiber intake and study variables, formal residual diagnostics and tests for non-linearity (eg., quadratic terms, LOESS smoothing) were not systematically performed. Future analyses should include comprehensive model diagnostics to verify the appropriateness of the assumed linear functional forms.

Conclusion

This case-control study provides preliminary evidence that low dietary fiber intake is associated with COPD, elevated systemic inflammation, and reduced lung function. COPD patients consumed 36% less fiber than age- and sex-matched controls, exhibited substantially elevated CRP and IL-6 levels, and demonstrated inverse correlations between fiber intake and inflammatory markers as well as positive correlations with lung function parameters. After adjusting for age, sex, BMI, and smoking variables, low dietary fiber intake (<20 g/day) remained independently associated with 3.2-fold increased COPD odds, although this effect was notably attenuated from the unadjusted estimate (OR = 5.23), indicating substantial confounding by smoking and other factors. Importantly, while the adjusted association between fiber intake and COPD was statistically significant and clinically meaningful, smoking exposure (OR = 1.09 per pack-year) remained the dominant risk factor, and dietary fiber should be viewed as one potentially modifiable component within a multifactorial disease framework rather than a primary intervention target.

These findings are consistent with the hypothesized gut-lung axis framework, whereby dietary fiber-derived short-chain fatty acids may exert systemic anti-inflammatory effects. However, our study did not directly measure gut microbiota composition, circulating SCFAs, or gut-lung axis immune mediators, and the mechanistic interpretation remains speculative based on the current data. Furthermore, the cross-sectional case-control design cannot establish causality, and reverse causation—whereby COPD symptoms lead to reduced dietary quality—cannot be excluded.

Whether increasing dietary fiber intake through dietary counseling or supplementation can reduce systemic inflammation, slow lung function decline, or improve clinical outcomes in COPD patients remains to be determined. Well-designed prospective cohort studies are needed to establish the temporal relationship between fiber intake and COPD development, and randomized controlled trials are essential to evaluate the efficacy of fiber-based dietary interventions. Such studies should incorporate comprehensive inflammatory biomarker panels, gut microbiome profiling, and fecal and circulating SCFA measurements to elucidate the proposed mechanistic pathways. Comparative investigations of different fiber types (soluble vs. insoluble) and food sources would inform specific dietary recommendations. If the observed associations are confirmed in longitudinal and interventional settings, dietary fiber modification could warrant consideration as a low-cost, safe, and accessible complementary approach within comprehensive COPD management strategies.
