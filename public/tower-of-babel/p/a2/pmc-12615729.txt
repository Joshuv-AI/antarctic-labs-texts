Global research trends and therapeutic potential of fibrinolytic enzymes
Authors: Chinmay Hazare, Prashant Bhagwat, Suren Singh, Santhosh Pillai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Fibrinolytic enzymes are a type of proteolytic enzymes that degrade fibrin clots and are commonly known as “clot-busting enzymes” 1. Blood clots primarily consist of platelets and red blood cells (RBCs), along with other components, and are held together by a network of the protein, fibrin 2. Fibrinolytic enzymes act on fibrin and lyse the protein scaffold, leading to degradation of the clot. Consequently, they play a crucial role in managing thrombosis, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), stroke, and related pathological conditions 3. For centuries, researchers worldwide have been fascinated by the mechanisms of blood clot formation as well as its lysis. Notably, the study of blood clot lysis began in the 18th century, when the Italian anatomist and physician Giovanni Battista Morgagni observed that blood sometimes did not coagulate after sudden death 4. A century later, in 1861, Denis documented the spontaneous dissolution of clots after death, particularly in trauma patients 5. Further advancements were made in the 19th century, as it became apparent that the dissolved fibrin exhibited resistance to re-coagulation, implying an enzymatic basis for fibrin clot breakdown. For instance, in 1889, Denys and De Marbaix proposed the existence of a latent enzyme responsible for dissolving blood clots 6. The term “fibrinolysis” was first introduced by Dastre in 1893, establishing the conceptual framework for enzymatic fibrin clot breakdown 7.

Fibrinolytic enzymes are categorized into two distinct categories based on their mechanism of action: direct and indirect fibrinolytic enzymes. For instance, the direct-acting enzymes operate by directly cleaving fibrin. Nattokinase and Lumbrokinase are examples of direct fibrinolytic enzymes, and they share structural similarities with the physiological enzyme plasmin 8, 9. This direct mechanism facilitates the rapid and complete degradation of thrombi. On the other hand, the indirect fibrinolytic enzymes function as plasminogen activators that initiate a downstream cascade leading to the conversion of the inactive plasminogen to the active form plasmin, which then degrades the fibrin clot 10. Some well-known examples of indirect fibrinolytic enzymes include tissue-type plasminogen activator (t-PA) and urokinase. Currently, therapeutic interventions like antiplatelet medications, anticoagulants, and various enzymes are utilized in the treatment of thrombosis 11. However, they cause several undesirable side effects such as bleeding, haemorrhage, allergic reactions and so on. These drawbacks underscore the need for a thorough investigation into fibrinolytic enzymes with reduced side effects and enhanced efficacy. Furthermore, understanding the mechanisms and applications of these enzymes is critical to advancing therapeutic strategies to mitigate thrombosis and other hemostatic disorders.

The first publication on fibrinolytic enzyme research, as captured in the Scopus database, dates back to 1945. Over the subsequent decades, a substantial volume of research publications on fibrinolytic enzymes has emerged. However, to dissect such large numbers of scientific research datasets more efficiently, employing an analytical methodology such as bibliometric analysis could be explored 12. Moreover, this analytical approach serves as a valuable tool for readers, particularly researchers, facilitating a better understanding of past research undertakings, current trends, and the existing research gaps within the scholarly literature 13. With current advancements in bibliometric software, including but not limited to VOSviewer, SciMAT, Bibliometrix, and Biblioshiny 14, along with access to comprehensive databases such as Web of Science (WoS) and Scopus 15, the landscape for data retrieval and presentation has significantly evolved. This integration of sophisticated software and expansive databases not only streamlined data accessibility but also enhanced the efficacy of data presentation. Despite the wealth of literature on ‘fibrinolytic enzymes research’, a systematic bibliometric analysis has not been performed to date. Recognizing this research gap, the authors have attempted to provide a comprehensive reference for emerging researchers embarking on this field. This work aims to establish a foundational basis for researchers, thereby contributing to the advancement of scholarly discourse and comprehension of fibrinolytic enzymes.

Bibliographic methods

Data retrieval

The Scopus database was selected as the primary source of bibliographic data to identify global research trends in fibrinolytic enzyme studies. A comprehensive keyword search was performed using the terms “fibrinolytic enzymes”, “fibrinolytic enzyme”, “thrombolytic enzyme”, and “thrombolytic enzymes”. These search terms were applied to the article title, abstract, and keywords sections. Publications were specifically selected based on the following criteria: 1) Publications released between 1945 and 2024, 2) written in English, and 3) documents classified as articles and reviews.

Data analyses

Scopus’s built-in “Analyze results” tool was used for the preliminary data analysis to examine publication trends 16. This feature offered a comprehensive overview of key metrics, including temporal publication patterns, document types, authorship trends, institutional affiliations, and country-specific contributions. The retrieved data were then exported to CSV format and analyzed using VOSviewer (version 1.6.15). VOSviewer is a specialized software tool widely used for bibliometric mapping and text mining. It enabled the construction of visual networks, including co-authorship patterns, keyword co-occurrence clusters, and collaborations between institutions 17. Furthermore, the combination of Scopus’s comprehensive indexing 18 and VOSviewer’s advanced visualization capabilities provided a strong analytical foundation. This approach facilitated the identification of gaps in research, influential researchers, key publication sources, and collaborative research clusters within the field of fibrinolytic enzymes 19.

Results and discussion

Publication outcomes

A basic search of the Scopus database yielded 1752 documents. Out of these documents, several constraints were applied to exclude irrelevant documents, ensuring the reliability and relevance of the remaining dataset. These included retracted documents, which are deemed unreliable due to errors or misconduct; editorials, which are opinion pieces lacking original research data; data papers, which present raw data sets without analysis or conclusions pertinent to the research topic; conference reviews, which summarize conferences rather than contribute original research; erratum, which are corrections to previously published articles; notes and short surveys, which are often too brief to provide substantial data or analysis; and letter categories, which generally consist of correspondence or brief comments on previously published work rather than original research. This resulted in a refined dataset of 1728 documents. Further refinement involved the exclusion of 36 book chapters and 37 conference papers. Book chapters were excluded as they are not peer-reviewed as rigorously as journal articles and vary widely in scope. Conference papers were excluded because they often represent preliminary findings and often face the same fate as book chapters in the peer-review process. Thus, the resulting dataset comprised only peer-reviewed journal articles and reviews, providing a robust foundation for subsequent bibliometric studies.

Furthermore, language restrictions were applied to limit the dataset to English-language publications, which reduced the count to 1364 documents. Additionally, temporal constraints commencing from 1945, the earliest year available in the Scopus database for fibrinolytic enzyme research, to 2024 were applied. To ensure analytical consistency, data integrity, and reproducibility of results, the analysis window was set to end in 2024. This avoided the biases that may have arisen if partial data from 2025 had been included. Consequently, the final dataset comprised 1334 documents, ensuring an extensive and methodologically sound bibliometric analysis.

Trends in annual publications related to fibrinolytic enzymes

Overall, 1334 documents were recovered, which were primarily research articles (n = 1229, 92.1 %) followed by reviews (n = 105, 7.9 %). Within the examined dataset, the earliest publication dates back to 1945, entitled “A fibrinolytic enzyme in menstruation and late pregnancy toxemia”, authored by Smith and Smith 20. During the early period, especially from 1945 to 1969, the number of publications was relatively low, with annual counts typically in single digits (Fig. S1). This phase represents the nascent stages of fibrinolytic enzyme research, during which foundational studies were conducted. A noticeable upward trend began in the early 1970s, with the number of publications increasing steadily, from 10 per year in 1975 to 39 per year in 2024. The surge in research activity around fibrinolytic enzymes from the 1970s onward reflects broader developments in molecular biology and biotechnology. Most notably, the emergence of recombinant DNA technology in the early 1970s and the breakthrough in monoclonal antibody production in 1975. These advancements opened the doors for more precise and detailed exploration of enzymatic functions, including those involved in fibrin degradation.

Moreover, the increase in publications observed since the 1980s can be attributed to several key moments. For example, the article “Molecular mechanism of physiological fibrinolysis” published in 1979, highlights the scientific community's eagerness to uncover the molecular mechanisms underlying fibrinolysis 21. This pivotal work laid the groundwork for further exploration and understanding of the physiological processes involved in fibrinolysis. Furthermore, the first highly purified form of human t-PA was obtained from uterine tissue (t-PA) in 1982, which triggered further research on the inherent molecular mechanisms of fibrinolysis and blood coagulation 22. The development of recombinant DNA technology in the early 1970s led to the successful cloning and production of t-PA in 1983, marking a major milestone that enabled its mass production for clinical use 23. Furthermore, this period also saw the discovery of recombinant streptokinase in the early 1990s 24. The development of monoclonal antibody production in 1975, along with improved chromatography techniques such as Fast Protein Liquid Chromatography (FPLC) in the 1980s, also facilitated the study of fibrinolytic enzymes in later years.

Even though there has been a steady increase in publications on fibrinolytic enzymes, the overall volume of literature in this area remains relatively small compared to other fields of Life sciences. This is due to the field's specialized nature, which requires sophisticated equipment and specific expertise. Furthermore, its clinical focus, primarily in cardiovascular and thrombotic therapy, restricts wider interdisciplinary involvement. It is important to note that fibrinolytic enzymes are a subset of the broader category of proteolytic enzymes. While extensive research has been conducted on proteolytic enzymes across various biological contexts, fibrinolytic enzymes remain a narrow area of study. This distinction highlights the unique scientific and translational challenges in fibrinolytic enzyme research, as well as its strategic importance in certain therapeutic areas. However, the ongoing increase in publications reflects a significant and specialized interest within the scientific community, demonstrating researchers' continued dedication to advancing this field. For instance, the years 2019, 2020, and 2022 saw consistent publication numbers of 36, 36, and 38, respectively, indicating a sustained focus on fibrinolytic enzyme research, unaffected by external factors like the global COVID-19 pandemic. This trend highlights the crucial role of fibrinolytic enzymes in both basic and clinical research contexts.

Interdisciplinary categories of fibrinolytic enzyme research

Fibrinolytic enzyme research spans a wide array of disciplines, with significant contributions from diverse fields such as medicine, biochemistry, genetics and molecular biology, immunology and microbiology, pharmacology, toxicology and pharmaceutics, agricultural and biological sciences, chemical engineering, chemistry, environmental science, and engineering. Among these, ‘medicine’ and ‘biochemistry, genetics and molecular biology’ were the two major subject categories. Medicine accounted for 591 publications, while biochemistry, genetics and molecular biology contributed 549 publications. Together, these two categories accounted for a staggering 51.9 % of all publications, with only a 1.9 % difference between them. Notably, the field of medicine included studies on the effects of conventional fibrinolytic drugs, such as streptokinase 25 and urokinase 26 on patients. For example, the study titled “Intravenous and intracoronary fibrinolytic therapy in acute myocardial infarction: Overview of results on mortality, reinfarction and side-effects from 33 randomized controlled trials” 27. Wherein 33 randomized trials on the effect of streptokinase and urokinase on approximately 6000 patients were evaluated. The findings indicated a significant reduction in the odds of death and reinfarction with intravenous treatment, demonstrating the clinical benefits. Contrastingly, the use of these conventional fibrinolytic agents is not without risks. Studies have also highlighted several side effects associated with streptokinase and urokinase. For example, streptokinase is also known to cause minor to severe bleeding, allergic reactions, and hypotension 28. Urokinase, on the other hand, can lead to complications such as bleeding and severe hypotension 29. These side effects necessitate careful patient monitoring and judicious use of these therapies, balancing their benefits against potential risks. Likewise, the fields of ‘biochemistry, genetics and molecular biology’ included studies on purification 30, characterization 31, cloning 32, codon optimization 33, and improving the catalytic efficiency of fibrinolytic enzymes 34, among others. Moreover, other categories such as immunology and microbiology (10.8 %), included research on the immune response to fibrinolytic enzymes 35, substrate specificity 36 and food-derived microbial fibrinolytic enzymes 37. Pharmacology, toxicology, and pharmaceutics (6.9 %) included studies on in vivo models 38, toxicity studies 39, and others. The agricultural and biological sciences (7.1 %) category included studies on cyanobacteria as a source of fibrinolytic enzymes 40, the isolation of fibrinolytic enzymes from plants 41, and the use of agro-industrial waste for the production of fibrinolytic enzymes 42, among others. The field of chemical engineering (5.8 %) included kinetic modelling 43, process optimization 44, and other related studies. Moreover, other categories included are chemistry (3.3 %), environmental science (2.2 %), engineering (2.2 %), and other fields, suggesting that fibrinolytic enzyme research spans multidisciplinary areas (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Distribution of fibrinolytic enzyme-related publications by subject area (in percentage).

3.5 Keyword analysis.

Understanding the thematic landscape of fibrinolytic enzyme research is crucial in discerning emerging trends, identifying prevalent topics, and tracking scientific advancements, thereby aiding in recognizing research gaps. Among the most effective methods to achieve this goal is to examine keyword co-occurrences 45. In this regard, a comprehensive analysis of 78 years of fibrinolysis research (1945–––2023) was conducted and 8225 unique keywords were identified. Notably, 160 keywords met the threshold of appearing at least 30 times or more. Additionally, to enhance the interpretation of the results, outlier terms, such as those used in the literature search, were deliberately omitted 46. The subsequent analysis yielded four distinct clusters from the entire array of keywords (Fig. 3). Noteworthy associations were observed among various ‘terms’ such as fibrinolytic agents, fibrinolysis, human, plasmin, and thrombosis, exemplifying the dense interconnections among the thematic clusters, with 156 linkages. Laboratory-based research (Cluster 1)

The keyword analysis yielded four distinct clusters from the entire array of keywords. Within the four clusters, two major clusters were observed (Fig. 3), with cluster 1, represented by red, encompassing keywords such as enzyme activity, enzymology, metabolism, molecular weight, non-human, and purification. This suggests that Cluster 1 primarily focuses on non-human experiments, including purification, characterization and metabolic aspects of enzymes. Moreover, it emphasizes the significance of biochemical and molecular characterization of fibrinolytic enzymes, highlighting the importance of foundational research to understand their mechanisms and functions.

Translational and clinical research (Cluster 2)

In contrast, Cluster 2 indicates work predominantly associated with animal and human trials (Fig. 3, denoted by the green color). Keywords such as animals, fibrinolysis, and humans are prominent within this cluster, pointing towards research endeavours involving fibrinolytic processes and trials conducted on animals and humans. This cluster underscores the translational and clinical aspects of fibrinolytic research, emphasizing studies aimed at understanding the therapeutic potential and clinical applications of fibrinolytic enzymes. A deeper dive into this cluster reveals that human-related studies and controlled trials are among the most prominent themes, reflecting the ongoing efforts to translate basic enzymology into clinical practice. 10.13039/100014337Furthermore, the high frequency of keywords such as 'tissue plasminogen activator,' 'plasminogen,' and 'urokinase' indicates sustained interest in clinically relevant fibrinolytic agents.

Venom-Based fibrinolytic enzymes (Cluster 3)

The blue Cluster (Cluster 3, Fig. 3) is distinguished by its focus on venom-based fibrinolytic enzymes, predominantly consisting of snake venom fibrinolytic enzymes. This cluster, although smaller in size, is indicated by keywords such as “snake venom,” “crotalid venoms,” and “metalloproteinase,” pointing towards the exploration of alternative natural sources for fibrinolytic agents 47. Additionally, it is observed that the snake venom node is linked to nodes such as the fibrinolytic agent within the cluster, but is also linked to nodes in cluster 2, including fibrinogen and animal. This indicates an interdisciplinary linkage and suggests that research on snake venom-derived fibrinolytic enzymes is not only focused on their biochemical properties (Cluster 3) but also explores their interactions with biological systems (Cluster 2). Notably, while snake venom-derived fibrinolytic enzymes can be potent, they often lack specificity, thereby limiting their clinical applications 47. This could be one of the reasons cluster 3 appears weaker than the other clusters in fibrinolytic enzyme research, suggesting that snake venom is not as extensively studied for therapeutic use. Future research should focus on engineering or discovering enzymes that maintain efficacy while minimizing adverse effects.

Peptide hydrolases (Cluster 4)

Lastly, the yellow cluster (Fig. 3) features the keyword “peptide hydrolase”. Peptide hydrolases are a class of enzymes which cleave peptide bonds in proteins. They are also known as peptidases, proteases, or proteinases. This broad category encompasses various enzyme groups, including caseinolytic, gelatinolytic, collagenolytic, and fibrinolytic enzymes. Due to the broad nature of this enzyme class, the term “peptide hydrolase” is not commonly used as a keyword in many publications, as evidenced by the size of this cluster. However, it is essential to note that using a general keyword, such as “proteolytic enzyme” or “peptide hydrolase,” can increase the publication's visibility compared to using overly specific terms, which are typically included in the abstract and title anyway.

Emerging trends and themes

The keyword analysis reveals a balanced research focus that spans fundamental biochemical studies, translational research, and clinical applications, as evident from previous discussions. The thematic structure observed across the identified keyword clusters highlights the complex and interdisciplinary nature of research on fibrinolytic enzymes. Each cluster reflects a distinct facet of the field, ranging from fundamental biochemical studies to applied clinical investigations. In particular, the interplay between laboratory-scale research (as seen in Cluster 1) and clinical-focused studies (Cluster 2) illustrates a translational trajectory; one that connects mechanistic understanding with therapeutic potential. Yet, despite notable advances in enzyme purification and characterization, translating these findings into viable clinical therapies remains a challenge. [46]. Key obstacles, including immunogenic responses, instability under physiological conditions, and undesirable side effects, continue to limit the broader application of many fibrinolytic agents. These barriers underscore the need for more targeted research to enhance enzyme formulations, delivery systems, and patient safety. An emerging but less emphasized theme within the dataset involves the exploration of microbial fibrinolytic enzymes, particularly those derived from fermented food sources. Although not dominant in the co-occurrence network, this area represents a growing frontier with the potential to yield novel, low-cost therapeutic candidates. Its relative novelty, compared to the more established clinical or molecular research streams, suggests untapped opportunities for future investigation. This elucidates the critical topics and prevalent themes within the discourse on fibrinolytic enzymes, offering valuable insights for researchers and the medical fraternity.

Analysis of sources for fibrinolytic enzymes

Additionally, the keyword analysis also sheds light on the diverse origins of fibrinolytic enzymes. The sources of fibrinolytic enzymes include a wide array of organisms, including microorganisms, plants, and animal venoms. Some of the well-known microbial fibrinolytic enzymes include streptokinase from beta-hemolytic Streptococci, staphylokinase from Staphylococcus aureus, and nattokinase from Bacillus subtilis natto 48. Furthermore, fibrinolytic enzymes have even been purified from plant sources; for example, from latex of the plants Calotropis gigantea 49, and Tabernaemontana divaricata 50, and even seeds of Gliricidia sepium 51. Comparatively, snake venom-derived fibrinolytic enzymes are potent but lack specificity, which limits their direct clinical applications 47. “Earthworm” is one of the keywords in the cluster. However, fibrinolytic enzymes isolated from earthworms often lack specificity. This underscores the importance of careful source selection as it has an influence on the characteristics of the enzyme such as substrate specificity, activity, and immunogenicity 52. Overall, the analysis of keyword co-occurrence has uncovered thematic clusters that provide a clear picture of trends in fibrinolytic enzyme research. This includes a significant focus on the biochemical characterization of enzymes, their clinical applications, and the exploration of diverse natural sources, all of which contribute to advancing this critical field.

The most cited publications

The citation count is considered an important index of the quality and relevance of a publication in the research fraternity 53. In this case, the top 20 most cited publications on fibrinolytic enzymes were scrutinized. It is noteworthy that the topmost cited (1,211) publication was of a seminal work on the standard plate assay for fibrinolytic activity entitled “The fibrin plate method for estimating fibrinolytic activity” (Table 1), which indicates that this method has been widely utilized for screening fibrinolytic enzymes in subsequent research. Surprisingly, the analysis revealed a disconnect between publication year and citation count, defying the conventional notion that older publications might garner more citations over time. However, in this analysis, the second most cited publication emerged in 2020, possibly due to its association with COVID-19 endotheliopathy, which may have increased its visibility and, consequently, the number of citations. This underscores the potential for newer perspectives and relevance to drive citation patterns, regardless of publication date. Remarkably, the fourth most cited publication, titled “A novel fibrinolytic enzyme (nattokinase) in the vegetable cheese Natto; a typical and popular soybean food in the Japanese diet”, stands out as a classical contribution in the field. This seminal work unveils the discovery of the first fibrinolytic enzyme, nattokinase. This enzyme was purified from the microorganism Bacillus subtilis natto, which was isolated from traditional Japanese fermented food, Natto. Furthermore, the most cited papers also include fibrinolytic enzymes purified from other fermented foods, such as Chungkook-Jang and Douchi, as well as non-food sources, including earthworm (Lumbricus rubellus), Snake venom, and Vampire bat saliva (Table 1).

The diversity of topics covered in these highly cited publications highlights several key trends and commonalities. Notably, there is a significant focus on fibrinolytic enzymes derived from various natural sources, ranging from traditional fermented foods to unique animal sources such as snake venom and vampire bat saliva. The variety of sources explored for fibrinolytic enzymes highlights the scientific community's keen interest in discovering new and effective fibrinolytic agents. A notable trend is the focus on purifying and characterizing these enzymes, as seen in numerous publications from different sources. This consistent focus highlights the fundamental role of these processes in understanding the properties and potential applications of fibrinolytic enzymes.

Interestingly, among a collection largely composed of original research articles, a single review on the composition and use of Okara (soybean residue) stands out. Although it primarily addresses Okara's applications in the food industry, it also touches on its role in producing fibrinolytic enzymes. This review, while not exclusively centred on fibrinolytic enzymes, merits attention due to its inclusion in bibliometric analyses. Despite its broader scope, the mere mention of fibrinolytic enzymes in the abstract led to its selection by Scopus indexing and subsequent inclusion by software tools. This highlights a potential drawback of bibliometric analysis when not conducted meticulously. Patents on fibrinolytic enzymes

The patent activity serves as a crucial indicator of innovation and commercial interest in fibrinolytic enzyme research. A comprehensive search yielded 25,334 patent records related to fibrinolytic enzymes. Despite the large number of patents filed, it is essential to consider the finite lifespan of patents in various jurisdictions, such as the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan, and others, where patents typically endure for a maximum of 20 years from their filing date, contingent upon timely payment of maintenance fees. For instance, a patent on a fibrinolytic assay filed by Elcatech Inc. in 1989 (EP0373908A2) expired in 2009, demonstrating how older patents can lapse and, in turn, be a limitation, further opening opportunities for new advancements and patent filings in the field.

It is essential to note that patenting native microorganisms that produce biocatalysts is generally prohibited, as naturally occurring organisms are not considered inventions eligible for patenting. Additionally, patenting native microorganisms can raise ethical and environmental concerns, particularly regarding biopiracy, biodiversity preservation, and the implications of exploiting traditional knowledge. The most critical section of the patent lies within its claims, typically found on the final pages of the patent document. These claims delineate the scope of the invention. In the realm of biotechnology, inventors often claim ownership of genes, amino acid sequences, and variant sequences (including protein-engineered or chimeric forms) of fibrinolytic enzymes. Many patents in this domain focus on safeguarding gene-protein sequences. Upon obtaining patent validation, the creators of the gene patent exert control over the commercial utilization of the gene, as well as the enzyme, thereby yielding significant influence over its commercial applications.

The correlation between the number of patent applications and the number of patents granted is particularly notable in the field of fibrinolytic enzymes. The number of patent applications consistently surpassed the number of patents granted, from 2001 onwards (Fig. 4a). This discrepancy suggests a high level of interest and innovation in the field, with many proposed ideas being submitted for patenting, although not all are granted. The significant uptick in patent numbers observed after 1980 coincides with notable discoveries in the field of inherent fibrinolysis mechanisms. For instance, in the early 1970s, Ed Reich's group in New York revealed that malignant tumours often secrete plasminogen activator activity 54. Furthermore, key developments such as the acquisition of melanoma cell lines from patient Bowes in 1975, initially by Grant Barlow from Abbott and later by Daniel Rifkin from New York University Medical School, laid the groundwork for subsequent studies. Additionally, key developments such as the introduction of a physiological model for fibrinolysis in 1978, the isolation of natural human tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA) in 1979 21, and the successful cloning of t-PA in 1983 23, which led to its first use in treating acute myocardial infarction (AMI) patients that same year 55. Future prospects

The search for a fibrinolytic enzyme with high specificity with minimal side effects is still ongoing. Future initiatives should focus on creating enzymes that can specifically target fibrin clots while avoiding the cleavage of other physiological proteins, thereby minimizing complications like excessive bleeding. Advances in areas like protein engineering, directed evolution, omics, and high-throughput screening offer valuable tools for discovering next-generation fibrinolytic agents with better safety as well as efficacy. Given the complexity of this area, a multidisciplinary approach combining expertise from biochemistry, molecular biology, clinical medicine, pharmacology, and bioengineering will establish a more comprehensive framework for innovation. Collaborative research initiatives and cross-disciplinary networks play a crucial role in sharing knowledge and infrastructure, thereby facilitating more efficient progress. A key objective must be the translation of laboratory findings into clinical applications. Although many purified enzymes have shown significant promise in experimental settings, their integration into clinical practice necessitates the development of robust preclinical models and well-structured clinical trials to confirm both safety and therapeutic efficacy. Emerging biotechnological advancements are set to revolutionize the exploration and design of fibrinolytic enzymes. For example, tools such as CRISPR-Cas9 for gene editing, omics techniques, and computational models for enzyme behavior offer exciting prospects for future research. Currently, much of the research in this field is concentrated within a limited number of institutions and countries. To overcome this, strategic partnerships among academia, the pharmaceutical industry, and clinical research organizations are required to bridge the gap from bench to bedside. Expanding global participation, particularly by encouraging contributions from underrepresented regions, can introduce fresh perspectives and broaden the scientific base. For example, regions rich in biological diversity, such as Africa, Latin America, and parts of Asia, must be collaborated with for untapped potential. Investigating novel sources of fibrinolytic enzymes, including extremophiles, marine organisms, and traditional fermented foods, could lead to the discovery of an enzyme with clinical significance. It may uncover new mechanisms of fibrinolysis and significantly contribute to clinical innovation. These emerging directions not only enhance the therapeutic landscape of fibrinolytic enzymes but also support the broader goal of effectively managing thrombotic and hemostatic disorders.

Conclusion

This review provides a detailed overview of the current progress in the field of fibrinolytic enzyme research, capturing its historical development, ongoing trends, existing challenges and gaps in the literature. It stands as the first in-depth analysis of this kind, adding substantial value to the scientific literature. Fibrinolytic enzymes play a crucial role in thrombolysis and hemostasis, particularly in their potential to impact treatment strategies for clot-related and cardiovascular conditions. Most of the global output is concentrated in a few countries hence there is a need for more inclusive international collaborations, particularly those involving the African, South American, and Latin American regions. Moreover, the research shows a strong interdisciplinary network with contributions from medicine, genetics, molecular biology, immunology, microbiology, and biochemistry. Fibrinolytic enzymes are sourced from a wide range of sources including fermented foods, animal venoms, and other natural origins, which points to the diverse and expanding interest in this field. Patent trends, especially the surge in filings after 1980, further demonstrate growing commercial and scientific interest. These correlate with major technological breakthroughs such as recombinant DNA technology and the cloning of tissue plasminogen activators. In summary, this bibliometric analysis confirms the significant and evolving role of fibrinolytic enzymes in both foundational and applied research. Continued international collaboration, coupled with innovative research strategies, will be crucial to advancing this field and translating its findings into therapeutics. With technological support and a global perspective, fibrinolytic enzyme research is well-positioned for significant discoveries and therapeutic breakthroughs. This work serves as a reference point for emerging researchers, encouraging ongoing exploration in this promising area of biomedical science.
