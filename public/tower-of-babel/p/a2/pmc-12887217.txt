Chronic Mitral Valve Fibrosis in Rheumatic Heart Disease: From Immune Trigger to Inflammatory and Mechanical Progression
Authors: Zhou Liu, Ruifei Liu, Jiajun Liang, Maozhou Wang, Meili Wang, Hongjia Zhang, Wenjian Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRheumatic heart disease is a chronic valvular disorder resulting from acute rheumatic fever, predominantly affecting the mitral valve and characterized by progressive fibrotic thickening. Although the initial insult is driven by autoimmune responses triggered by Group A Streptococcus infection, the sustained progression of valvular fibrosis involves a complex interplay of persistent immune activation, chronic inflammation, and, critically, long‐term mechanical stress. Mechanical forces arising from altered hemodynamics and structural valve deformation perpetuate valvular interstitial cell activation, endothelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition, and extracellular matrix remodeling, thereby driving fibrosis even after acute inflammation resolves. Current therapeutic options to halt or reverse mitral valve fibrosis are limited, with surgical repair or replacement remaining the primary interventions for advanced disease. Advancing mechanistic understanding through physiologically relevant models, coupled with early diagnostic strategies and multitargeted antifibrotic approaches, holds promise for preserving valve function. Integrating molecular insights with scalable public health measures may ultimately mitigate the global burden of rheumatic heart disease and improve outcomes for affected populations.Keywords: chronic inflammation, immune response, mechanical stress, mitral valve fibrosis, rheumatic mitral valve diseaseSubject Categories: Basic Science Research, Fibrosis, Inflammation, Mechanisms

Rheumatic heart disease (RHD), caused by acute rheumatic fever (ARF), mainly affects the mitral valve. It remains a leading cause of cardiovascular disease in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Despite a decline in high‐income regions, RHD still affects >40 million people and causes an estimated 320 000 deaths each year—making it the leading cause of cardiovascular death in young populations worldwide. 1 , 2 , 3 RHD results from an autoimmune response triggered by Group A Streptococcus (GAS) infection. The molecular mimicry between GAS antigens and cardiac valve proteins leads to inflammation and damage to the mitral valve. Although primary and secondary prevention strategies, like antibiotic prophylaxis, have reduced ARF and RHD incidence, challenges remain. Limited access to health care and difficulties in early diagnosis hinder effective prevention. 4 , 5 Epidemiological data indicate that ∼60% of individuals with an initial ARF episode progress to clinically significant RHD within 10 years. 6 In mitral‐dominant RHD, the mechanisms driving progressive valvular degeneration remain poorly understood. No effective pharmacological therapies currently exist to halt or reverse fibrosis or functional decline in the mitral valve. As a result, valve dysfunction continues to advance, often culminating in heart failure. Alarmingly, up to 27% of patients diagnosed with RHD develop heart failure within 5 years, 6 underscoring the urgent need to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and develop disease‐modifying interventions.

As the RHD progresses, mitral valve leaflets thicken, the commissures fuse, and subvalvular chordae shorten, reflecting extensive fibrotic remodeling. 7 , 8 , 9 Collagen accumulation and, in later stages, calcification, stiffen the valve and reduce its mobility, leading to severe stenosis (Figures 1, 2, 3). These changes often require surgical intervention. Although research has focused on the autoimmune and inflammatory aspects, recent findings suggest that immune dysregulation may not be the main cause of long‐term mitral valve fibrosis in patients with RHD. 2 , 10 This highlights the need to explore other factors contributing to chronic fibrotic progression. In this review, we critically examine the current understanding of mitral valve fibrosis in RHD, with an emphasis on emerging molecular and cellular pathways implicated in the sustained fibrotic remodeling of the valve. By integrating recent insights into valvular interstitial cell activation, extracellular matrix (ECM) dynamics, and potential nonimmune mediators (Figure 4), we aim to provide a conceptual framework for identifying future therapeutic targets that may interrupt the progression of mitral valve disease in RHD.

PATHOGENIC DRIVERS OF PROGRESSIVE MITRAL VALVE FIBROSIS IN RHEUMATIC HEART DISEASE

Immune Response

The autoimmune response elicited during ARF is well established as the initiating factor in rheumatic mitral valve injury. However, the extent to which immune mechanisms contribute to the long‐term progression of mitral valve fibrosis in RHD remains controversial. During the acute phase of ARF, molecular mimicry between GAS antigens and host valve proteins triggers both humoral and cell‐mediated immune responses. The resultant antistreptococcal antibodies cross‐react with valvular components, particularly ECM proteins, initiating inflammatory injury to the valve. 11 , 12 In a subset of patients, recurrent streptococcal infections following ARF recovery can lead to repeated immune activation, potentially serving as a driving force for ongoing valve injury and progressive fibrosis. Type I collagen, a major structural component of the valvular matrix, becomes exposed during acute valvular damage and has been identified as a key autoantigen in multiple autoimmune conditions. Its sustained antigenicity may perpetuate immune‐mediated injury and contribute to the architectural distortion of the valve in the chronic phase of RHD. 2 , 11 , 13 , 14 Recent studies have also implicated specific T‐cell subsets—particularly Th17 cells—and their associated proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐17 (interleukin‐17) and IL‐6, in the pathogenesis of RHD. These cytokines are closely associated with chronic inflammation and fibrotic remodeling of the valve. 15 Dysregulation of immune homeostasis may establish a persistent inflammatory milieu within valvular tissue, further accelerating the fibrotic process. These findings suggest that immune dysfunction, even in the absence of active infection, may contribute to chronic valvular deterioration in RHD. Accordingly, immunomodulatory therapies—such as targeted immunosuppression—warrant investigation as potential strategies for mitigating the progression of mitral valve fibrosis in RHD. 11 , 16

Inflammatory Response

Chronic inflammation is a well‐recognized driver of tissue fibrosis across organ systems and plays a critical role in the progression of rheumatic mitral valve disease. 17 The immune dysregulation initiated by ARF elicits an intense early inflammatory response, resulting in pathological alterations of the heart valves, particularly the mitral valve. Even after the resolution of overt immune activation, the structurally compromised valve continues to be subjected to aberrant mechanical and biochemical stimuli, sustaining a chronic inflammatory state that perpetuates tissue injury and promotes progressive valvular dysfunction—manifesting clinically as worsening mitral stenosis or regurgitation. 18 Histopathological and molecular studies have consistently demonstrated infiltration of inflammatory cells—primarily macrophages and lymphocytes—within the valve tissue during the chronic phase of RHD. 19 , 20 , 21 These immune cells secrete a range of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF‐α (tumor necrosis factor‐alpha), IL‐1, IL‐1β, IL‐6, and IL‐18, all of which contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of mitral valve fibrosis. 9 , 22 , 23 , 24 IL‐1, in particular, has been implicated in mediating inflammatory injury during the acute phase of RHD, 25 , 26 and genetic polymorphisms in the IL‐1 receptor antagonist gene have been associated with disease severity. 27 IL‐1β further amplifies fibrotic responses by upregulating MMPs (matrix metalloproteinases), TGF‐β (transforming growth factor‐beta), and IL‐6, thereby promoting myofibroblast proliferation and ECM deposition. 28 , 29 Beyond cytokine‐mediated signaling, chronic inflammation may also facilitate the activation and phenotypic transformation of valvular interstitial cells (VICs) into myofibroblasts, which are key effectors of fibrogenesis. 30 Concurrently, oxidative stress—a downstream consequence of persistent inflammation—emerges as a critical amplifying factor. Accumulation of reactive oxygen species can directly injure valvular cells and ECM components, while also enhancing fibroblast activation and promoting maladaptive ECM remodeling. 31 , 32 Clinical studies lend further support to this paradigm: patients with RHD exhibit significantly elevated plasma levels of hs‐CRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein) and increased neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio compared with healthy controls. 33 , 34 Notably, individuals with heightened systemic inflammation experience more rapid deterioration of valvular function, reinforcing the hypothesis that chronic, subclinical inflammation within the valve tissue actively contributes to disease progression.

Mechanical Stress

As the disease progresses, chronic mechanical stress plays a pivotal role, particularly after the acute inflammatory phase subsides. The mitral valve is a biomechanically dynamic, multilayered structure composed of fibroblast‐like cells and VICs embedded within an ECM scaffold. These cells are constantly subjected to diverse mechanical forces during each cardiac cycle, including bending stresses, shear stress from blood flow, and tensile forces during valve closure. 35 , 36 , 37 In rheumatic mitral valve disease, initial immune‐mediated injury, triggered by GAS infection, leads to structural changes and abnormal hemodynamic conditions. These changes significantly alter local mechanical stress distributions, potentially disrupting mechanosensory signaling in VICs. Aberrant cellular responses to altered mechanical cues may, in turn, exacerbate ECM remodeling and fibrosis, thereby creating a vicious cycle that sustains valvular disease progression. 35 This cycle can persist even after the acute inflammatory phase has resolved, leading to progressive valvular stiffening and stenosis. The principle behind surgical repair for rheumatic mitral valve disease is to correct the abnormal structural changes in the mitral valve. The goal is to restore the anatomical structure and hemodynamic function of the valve as much as possible. 38 This, in turn, helps to interrupt the vicious cycle of fibrosis and prevents further deterioration of valve function. Mechanical strain has been shown to activate fibrogenic signaling cascades—including the TGF‐β pathway—in various cell types, including VICs. In the rheumatic mitral valve, excessive mechanical load is thought to trigger TGF‐β overactivation, leading to downstream activation of SMAD‐dependent pathways, VIC activation, and transdifferentiation into myofibroblasts. 36 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 This cellular transformation promotes pathological collagen deposition and accelerates fibrotic thickening of the valve. Furthermore, structural deformities in the rheumatic mitral valve may result in heterogeneous distribution of mechanical forces, particularly in terms of localized shear stress. However, the spatial relationship between regional mechanical abnormalities—especially shear stress—and the severity of fibrosis across different anatomical regions of the valve remains poorly characterized. To address this knowledge gap, ex vivo models that recapitulate the hemodynamic and pathophysiological features of human rheumatic mitral valve disease may prove valuable. 43 Integration of these models with advanced cardiac imaging modalities—such as multiphase cardiac computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging—can facilitate precise reconstruction of valve anatomy and dynamic flow patterns. Correlating regional shear stress distribution with histopathological changes across the diseased valve would offer critical insights into the mechanobiological underpinnings of progressive mitral valve fibrosis in RHD. Such an approach may help identify novel therapeutic targets aimed at disrupting the pathological mechanical signaling that sustains valvular remodeling.

CELLULAR AND MOLECULAR MECHANISMS UNDERLYING THE PROGRESSIVE FIBROSIS OF THE RHEUMATIC MITRAL VALVE

Activation and Heterogeneity of Valve Interstitial Cells

In normal mitral valve tissue, VICs comprise >75% of the total cellular population. Under physiological conditions, these cells predominantly exhibit a quiescent phenotype and are responsible for maintaining ECM homeostasis and structural integrity. However, under pathological conditions or upon external stimuli, VICs undergo phenotypic transformation into activated myofibroblasts. This process is marked by upregulation of key cellular markers, including vimentin, α‐SMA (alpha‐smooth muscle actin), and embryonic nonmuscle myosin heavy chain. 30 , 37 , 44 Myofibroblasts exhibit enhanced proliferative and synthetic capacities, and their activation plays a crucial role in tissue repair by promoting collagen synthesis and stabilizing damaged areas. However, chronic or excessive activation leads to pathological ECM accumulation, resulting in progressive fibrosis. 45 , 46 , 47 Proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐α and IL‐1β have been shown to induce this fibroblast‐to‐myofibroblast transition, accompanied by increased expression of α‐SMA and collagen. 30 In addition, mechanical stress has been implicated as a potent stimulus for VIC activation. Specifically, in human mitral VICs, cyclic mechanical strain activates fibrogenic phenotypes via RhoC/ROCK/MRTF‐A (Ras homolog gene family, member C/Rho–Rho‐kinase/myocardin‐related transcription factor A) and Erk1/2 (extracellular signal‐regulated kinase 1/2) signaling pathways. 36 Multiple intracellular signaling cascades regulate VIC activation and fibrogenesis, including the canonical NF‐κB (nuclear factor kappa B), TGF‐β, and Wnt pathways. 30 , 40 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 These pathways not only enhance VIC proliferation and migration but also promote the synthesis and deposition of ECM components, thus perpetuating fibrosis. Recent advances in single‐cell RNA sequencing have revealed substantial heterogeneity within the VIC population. 44 , 53 Beyond the traditional dichotomy of quiescent and activated phenotypes, emerging studies have identified additional subpopulations, including inflammatory‐like VICs, osteogenic/calcifying VICs, immune‐like VICs, and endothelial‐like VICs. 54 This phenotypic diversity appears to influence not only cellular function and fibrotic potential but also contributes to other pathological processes such as calcification and immune activation. Recognizing and characterizing VIC heterogeneity offers a new perspective on the complexity of mitral valve pathology, including that seen in RHD. A deeper understanding of these subpopulations and their regulatory networks may uncover novel therapeutic targets and inform the development of precision medicine strategies for treating valvular fibrosis and its associated complications.

Endothelial‐to‐Mesenchymal Transition

Endothelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition (EndMT) is a phenotypic conversion in which endothelial cells, under specific physiological or pathological stimuli, lose endothelial‐specific markers—such as CD31 (platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule), von Willebrand factor, and vascular endothelial‐cadherin—and acquire mesenchymal traits characterized by the expression of α‐SMA, N‐cadherin, vimentin, FSP‐1 (fibroblast‐specific protein‐1), and various forms of collagen. 55 , 56 This cellular reprogramming contributes significantly to fibrotic remodeling in cardiovascular diseases and has been increasingly implicated in the progression of mitral valve fibrosis. 41 , 49 , 55 , 57 , 58 , 59 TGF‐β is recognized as a central inducer of EndMT. It exerts its effects primarily through the activation of SMAD‐dependent signaling pathways, which drive transcriptional reprogramming toward a mesenchymal phenotype. 55 , 60 , 61 , 62 In addition to TGF‐β, dysregulation of the Notch signaling pathway has also been linked to EndMT. Altered expression of Notch receptors and ligands can modulate downstream transcriptional networks associated with mesenchymal transformation. Experimental studies demonstrate that loss of Notch signaling accelerates EndMT and leads to enhanced fibrotic remodeling and scar tissue formation in valve tissue. 55 , 63 , 64 Noncoding RNAs—particularly microRNAs and long noncoding RNAs—have emerged as important regulators of EndMT. These epigenetic modulators fine‐tune the transition by targeting key endothelial markers and mesenchymal‐associated transcription factors. 65 Several microRNAs have been shown to participate in EndMT, promoting profibrotic signaling in EndMT‐derived fibroblast‐like cells. 66 , 67 In a notable study using a Lewis rat model of RHD, robust EndMT was observed in mitral valve tissue, accompanied by inflammatory infiltration and fibrotic damage. Pharmacologic inhibition of ACVR2A (Activin receptor type IIA) significantly suppressed EndMT, suggesting that ACVR2A may represent a promising therapeutic target for attenuating valvular injury in RHD. 58 To date, most EndMT‐related studies have focused on nonrheumatic valvular diseases, such as fibrotic and calcific aortic or degenerative mitral valve conditions. However, the potential role of EndMT in the pathogenesis of rheumatic mitral valve disease remains underexplored. 60 , 68 Further research is urgently needed to elucidate the unique or shared mechanisms of EndMT‐mediated fibrosis in rheumatic versus nonrheumatic valve pathologies. Such comparative insights may offer valuable perspectives for disease‐specific therapeutic interventions.

Dynamic Regulation of Extracellular Matrix Remodeling

The ECM of the heart valves is organized into stratified layers enriched with elastin, proteoglycans, and fibrillar collagens, each conferring distinct biomechanical properties to the valve leaflets and supporting structures. 69 , 70 The composition and turnover of ECM are primarily governed by the synthetic activity of VICs, which orchestrate the expression of genes encoding fibrillar collagens, chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans, and elastin—components essential for maintaining the layered ECM architecture of healthy valves. 71 , 72 During valve development and remodeling, matrix‐modifying enzymes such as MMPs, their endogenous inhibitors (TIMPs [tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases]), and cysteine proteases (eg, cathepsins) are dynamically regulated and play crucial roles in ECM maturation. 71 , 73 Disruptions in ECM composition are also implicated in several connective tissue disorders with prominent valvular phenotypes, including Marfan syndrome, Williams syndrome, and Ehlers–‐Danlos syndrome, all of which are associated with ECM abnormalities leading to valvular malformations and progressive dysfunction. 37 In fibrotic processes more broadly, ECM involvement shows stage‐dependent features, and acute phases are typically dominated by inflammatory mediators and immune cell recruitment. 9 , 21 , 74 In the chronic progression of rheumatic mitral valve disease, substantial ECM remodeling occurs, marked by excessive collagen deposition and dysregulation of MMP expression. These changes result in leaflet thickening and stiffening, which compromise the valve’s biomechanical integrity and contribute to clinically significant functional impairment. 40 , 75 , 76 Rheumatic ECM remodeling is thought to be driven by persistent inflammatory stimuli and aberrant mechanical stress, with activated fibroblasts and myofibroblasts serving as the predominant cellular source of altered matrix components. Although these pathological changes have been well documented histologically and molecularly, the specific signaling pathways and regulatory mechanisms underlying ECM remodeling in RHD remain incompletely understood. Nevertheless, emerging evidence supports the therapeutic potential of targeting ECM remodeling. Strategies such as MMP inhibition, modulation of fibroblast activation, or suppression of key fibrogenic signaling cascades may offer novel approaches to slow or reverse disease progression in rheumatic mitral valve pathology.

DEVELOPMENT AND OPTIMIZATION OF DISEASE MODELS

The development of robust and reproducible models is critical for elucidating disease mechanisms; however, to date, there remains a lack of reliable models that accurately recapitulate the pathophysiological features of RHD. This limitation has significantly hindered mechanistic investigations into disease progression. Among the few available strategies, repeated immunization with heat‐inactivated GAS or purified streptococcal M protein in Lewis rats has been reported to induce autoimmune valvulitis, mimicking the inflammatory manifestations observed in patients with ARF and RHD. 77 , 78 , 79 This remains the only experimental model widely used for studying the immunopathogenesis of ARF/RHD in a laboratory setting. The classical induction protocol involves initial and booster injections in 6‐ to 8‐week‐old female Lewis rats. Injections are administered into the hind footpad using a 1:1 volume mixture of 0.5 mg heat‐inactivated GAS antigen and complete Freund’s adjuvant. After a 21‐day sensitization period, rats typically exhibit clinical signs of valvulitis and myocarditis. Histological analysis—using hematoxylin and eosin staining—demonstrates hallmark features of autoimmune valvulitis, characterized predominantly by mononuclear cell infiltration. 78 To enhance the reproducibility and robustness of this model, extensive efforts have been directed at optimizing the induction protocol. These include modifications to the injection site, 79 , 80 adjustments in antigen composition, 81 , 82 and refinements in booster immunization strategies. 83 , 84 Recent attempts have also focused on improving the pathological fidelity of these models. Extending the induction period from the conventional 21 days to 12 to 24 weeks has yielded more stable phenotypes, with better representation of chronic fibrotic remodeling. 85 , 86

Nevertheless, the limitations of rodent models must be explicitly acknowledged, particularly their inability to replicate the slow, progressive fibrosis and calcification that are characteristic of human mitral valve disease. The translational relevance of these models is limited by incomplete understanding of the human disease’s molecular drivers and by variability in immune responses across species. 82 , 87 , 88 Emerging organoid technologies may provide a promising avenue to bridge these gaps. Patient‐derived, 3‐dimensional valvular organoids, which integrate endothelial and interstitial cell populations with ECM scaffolds, could more faithfully replicate the native valve structure and enable the investigation of how to induce valve pathology in vitro that mimics the changes observed in RHD. Such systems would overcome species‐specific limitations, allow precision studies of patient‐specific pathways, and offer a translational platform for therapeutic testing. 89 , 90 Although still in early development, organoid‐based models represent an exciting future direction for improving the pathological fidelity and clinical relevance of RHD research.

SURGICAL STRATEGIES AND FUTURE MANAGEMENT DIRECTIONS IN RHD

Currently, there are no effective pharmacological treatments to halt the progressive fibrosis of the mitral valve in RHD. 21 , 91 As a result, patients typically undergo surgical intervention only when severe hemodynamic abnormalities occur due to advanced mitral valve disease, with the main options being surgical valve repair or replacement. 92 , 93 Previous researches have demonstrated that surgical repair yields better long‐term outcomes than valve replacement. 38 , 94 , 95 However, successful surgical repair relies on early assessment and intervention in mitral valve disease. In cases of late‐stage severe fibrosis or calcification (Figure 1), valve replacement becomes the only viable option. Understanding the histopathologic patterns of mitral valve disease, such as diffuse fibrosis and focal calcification, is critical in guiding clinical decisions. Diffuse fibrosis is often associated with progressive mitral stenosis, leading to gradual stiffening and thickening of the valve leaflets (Figures 2 and 3), resulting in restrictive mitral inflow and worsening heart failure symptoms. In contrast, calcification, commonly seen in the later stages of the disease, leads to localized calcium deposits that impair leaflet mobility and further narrow the valve. 96 These patterns help cardiologists assess the stage of fibrosis and determine whether surgical repair or replacement is appropriate. A better understanding of the molecular mechanisms of rheumatic mitral valve fibrosis, along with the identification of effective molecular biomarkers for different stages of mitral valve fibrosis, could enable regular monitoring of patients, facilitating early detection of fibrosis. This would help clinicians understand the stage of fibrosis and prevent interventions when valve dysfunction has reached an irreparable level, ultimately improving the chances of preserving valve function. Moreover, exploring effective antifibrotic therapies for the mitral valve could become an invaluable adjunct to surgical interventions in the future. 21 Although antifibrotic drugs such as nintedanib and pirfenidone are already approved for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and many other investigational agents are in clinical trials (Table 1), these cannot be directly applied to halt rheumatic mitral valve fibrosis. 98 Valvular tissue is uniquely characterized by poor vascularity and limited drug penetration, which poses major challenges for systemic drug delivery. In addition, most immunomodulatory or antifibrotic interventions target acute inflammatory or single fibrogenic pathways, whereas chronic RHD involves established and heterogeneous drivers—including immune cells, valvular interstitial cells, and endothelial‐derived myofibroblasts—making single‐target strategies insufficient. 9 Furthermore, the absence of robust preclinical models that faithfully recapitulate the chronic, progressive fibrosis of human mitral valves has hampered translational progress.

Overall, therapeutic strategies to directly intervene in and suppress the persistent fibrotic remodeling of the mitral valve in chronic RHD remain underdeveloped, underscoring the urgent need for dedicated research. Future directions should emphasize the development of valve‐specific drug delivery systems, multitargeted antifibrotic approaches, and next‐generation organoid or ex vivo models that better mimic human valve pathology. 17 , 21 Importantly, because RHD disproportionately affects populations in low‐ and middle‐income countries, the real‐world impact of mechanistic advances will depend on integration with scalable public health measures, including primary and secondary prophylaxis, broader echocardiographic screening, and affordable access to therapies. This perspective has also been recognized at the international level. The World Health Organization adopted a landmark resolution calling for intensified global action against RHD in 2018. 4 The American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology have issued statements highlighting the urgent need to accelerate both prevention and treatment strategies. 2 , 10 Bridging mechanistic discoveries with these global health priorities is essential to translate scientific progress into meaningful clinical benefit for the populations most affected by RHD.

CONCLUSIONS

Rheumatic mitral valve fibrosis arises from a dynamic interplay of immune activation, chronic inflammation, and, critically, long‐term mechanical stress. Although acute rheumatic fever initiates autoimmune‐mediated injury, sustained hemodynamic alterations and abnormal mechanical forces exerted on the valve appear to dominate the progression of fibrosis during the chronic phase. Long‐term valvular deterioration involves multifaceted mechanisms, including activation of valvular interstitial cells, EndMT, and dynamic ECM remodeling. Despite advances in elucidating these pathways, therapeutic options remain limited, and surgical intervention is still the primary recourse for advanced disease. Moving forward, the development of physiologically relevant models—including organoid and ex vivo systems—will be critical to dissecting the chronic fibrotic processes and enabling the identification of novel molecular targets. Concurrently, efforts to design valve‐specific antifibrotic strategies, multitargeted therapies, and early diagnostic biomarkers may offer the potential to preserve valve function before irreversible damage occurs. Importantly, translating mechanistic insights into clinical practice must be coupled with scalable public health initiatives, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income regions disproportionately burdened by RHD. Integrating molecular research with preventive and therapeutic strategies offers the most promising path toward mitigating the global impact of rheumatic mitral valve disease and improving outcomes for affected populations.
