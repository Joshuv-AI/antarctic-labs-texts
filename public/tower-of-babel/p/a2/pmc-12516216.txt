Real World Effectiveness and Tolerability of Novel Monoclonal Antibodies and Rituximab for NMOSD
Authors: Zarmina Javed, Mengke Du, Mary Rensel, Le H Hua, Carrie M Hersh, Justin R Abbatemarco, Devon S Conway, Daniel Ontaneda, Jeffrey A Cohen, Amy Kunchok
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this multicenter retrospective cohort study of 135 people with aquaporin‐4 IgG+ neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), some of whom were exposed to multiple therapies, we evaluated the effectiveness and tolerability of rituximab (n = 111) and novel monoclonal antibodies (nMAbs): eculizumab (n = 9), inebilizumab (n = 23), and satralizumab (n = 14). Over a median follow‐up of 3.92 years, 21/111 rituximab‐treated patients relapsed. In contrast, relapse occurred in only 1/23 inebilizumab‐treated patients (median follow‐up 1.27 years), with no relapses observed in those receiving eculizumab or satralizumab. Twelve‐month relapse‐free probabilities were 92% (rituximab), 94% (inebilizumab), and 100% (eculizumab and satralizumab). Among those with available MRI data, 10/73 rituximab and 1/14 inebilizumab developed new lesions. There were no serious adverse events. These findings support nMAbs as effective and well‐tolerated first‐line therapies for NMOSD.Keywords: effectiveness, monoclonal antibodies, NMOSD, rituximab, tolerability

Introduction

Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is an inflammatory disease of the central nervous system (CNS) mediated by autoantibodies against aquaporin‐4 (AQP4‐IgG). Relapse prevention is crucial to reduce disability accrual [1].

Randomized controlled trials demonstrated the efficacy of rituximab and novel monoclonal antibodies (nMAbs); eculizumab, inebilizumab, satralizumab, and ravalizumab for AQP4‐IgG+ NMOSD compared to placebo [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Although there are observational studies demonstrating the effectiveness of rituximab in AQP4‐IgG+ NMOSD, there are only a few real‐world studies reporting the effectiveness and safety of nMAbs [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Real‐world data can be used to demonstrate effectiveness and tolerability in more generalizable and representative populations than clinical trials [13, 14]. Therefore, we aimed to examine disease activity (relapse and MRI activity) and tolerability of rituximab and nMAbs in a clinical population of people with AQP4‐IgG+ NMOSD.

Methods

Participants and Study Design

This multicenter retrospective cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Cleveland Clinic, and participant consent was waived. The study population was derived from Cleveland Clinic centers in Ohio, Florida, and Nevada. We identified AQP4‐IgG+ NMOSD patients through a search of electronic medical records (1990–2023). The cohort included patients currently treated with rituximab or nMAbs, as well as those who switched from rituximab to a nMAb or from one nMAb to another at any point (ever exposed). We did not include patients never exposed to monoclonal therapies.

Outcomes

A relapse was defined as new or worsening of existing neurological symptoms lasting for ≥ 24 h. MRI brain and/or spinal cord activity (gadolinium‐enhancing or new T2‐weighted lesions) after > 6 months of therapy commencement or after relapse was evaluated by treating neurologists and neuroradiologists. Tolerability outcomes included leukopenia (white cell count < 10 k/uL), infusion reactions, gastrointestinal intolerance, headaches, and infections (pulmonary and urinary). Serious adverse effects were defined as events requiring hospitalization and/or adverse effects associated with treatment discontinuation.

Statistical Analysis

We report demographics at the time of initiation of the current therapy. As some patients switched therapies, we examined relapses, MRI activity, and tolerability in all patients ever exposed to the therapies. Thus, some patients are represented in > 1 treatment group. Relapses within the first 30 days of treatment start were not included (due to therapeutic lag). Relapses were examined up until the last infusion plus an estimated duration of therapeutic effect (180 days after last infusion of rituximab and inebilizumab, 14 days for eculizumab, and 30 days for satralizumab). If the patients last clinical follow‐up date was earlier than this estimated duration of effect, then the last follow‐up was truncated to this date. Standard dosing for therapies is in Table S1.

Nonparametric Kaplan–Meier estimators were applied to assess the relapse‐free probability of rituximab and each nMAb separately. Each patient was included as a unique case for the specific treatment period they underwent. Start dates were aligned with the initiation of treatment, and patients were censored at the time of treatment discontinuation or at their last follow‐up if treatment was ongoing. The event was defined as the first relapse within the predefined treatment period. Relapses outside this window or subsequent relapses were excluded. R version 4.3.1 was utilized for all analyses [15].

Results

Study Cohort

The cohort included 135 AQP4‐IgG+ NMOSD patients currently treated with rituximab (92) and nMAbs (eculizumab 8, inebilizumab 22 and satralizumab 13). The median age was 55 (IQR = 40–65), 66% were female, 56% were White, and 36% were African American. The median disease duration and EDSS are reported in Table 1. The main reasons for switching to nMAbs were relapse (39%), intolerance (50%), and insurance coverage (11%) (Table 1). Treatment histories are represented in Figure 1.

Effectiveness: Clinical Relapses of nMabs and Rituximab

We examined all patients ever exposed to a therapy (rituximab n = 111, eculizumab n = 9, inebilizumab n = 23 and satralizumab n = 14). Among patients treated with rituximab (median follow‐up of 3.92 (IQR = 1.52–6.41) years), 21 (19%) experienced a relapse. Of these, 5/21 relapses occurred within the first 6 months, with a mean time of 3.6 (range = 2–6 months) after the first infusion. Sixteen relapses occurred > 6 months but within the therapeutic window. There were 6 patients with CD19 measured; 4 (67%) showed no evidence of B‐cell repopulation at the time of relapse and prior to their most recent infusion (Table 1).

Among patients treated with inebilizumab (median follow‐up of 1.27 [IQR = 0.78–2.24] years), 1 (4%) patient had a clinical relapse at 7 months from treatment start (1 month after second infusion).

The probability of relapse‐free survival at 1 year was 92% for rituximab and 94% for inebilizumab. No relapses were observed on eculizumab over a median of 2.04 (IQR = 0.42–3.72) years or on satralizumab over a median of 1.34 (IQR = 0.34–1.55) years of follow‐up (Figure 2).

Effectiveness: MRI Activity on nMabs and Rituximab

The average interval between MRI's and number of scans is reported in Table S2. Among the rituximab group, brain MRI was available for n = 79, and 3 (3.8%) had T2 lesions with gadolinium enhancement. Cervical spine MRI was available for n = 73, and 3 (4.1%) had lesions (1 T2 lesion without gadolinium‐enhancement, and 2 T2 lesions with gadolinium‐enhancement). Thoracic spine MRI was available for n = 79, and 4 (5.1%) had a T2 lesion with gadolinium enhancement (Table 1). All enhancing lesions were at new locations and were symptomatic. One patient had a new cervical spine non‐enhancing T2 lesion that was asymptomatic.

Among the inebilizumab group, brain MRI was available for n = 14, and one patient had a new symptomatic optic nerve T2 and gadolinium‐enhancing lesion at 7 months after starting treatment. No new MRI lesions were observed in eculizumab and satralizumab groups during the MRI follow‐up period (Table 1).

Safety and Tolerability

Side effects are summarized in Table 1. Among ever exposed patients, infections occurred in 29% on rituximab, 11% on eculizumab, 22% on inebilizumab, and 7% on satralizumab. Infusion reactions were reported in 9/111 (8%) rituximab, 2/23 (9%) inebilizumab, and 2/14 (14%) satralizumab.

Discussion

This real‐world study examined disease activity (relapses and MRI new lesions) and tolerability of AQP4‐IgG+ NMOSD patients treated with rituximab and nMAbs (eculizumab, inebilizumab, and satralizumab). We found minimal or no relapses for all three nMAbs. MRI activity was low for nMAbs‐treated patients, and tolerability was excellent.

Compared to the randomized controlled trials of the nMAbs, our cohort included older patients (median age 55), particularly in the satralizumab group (median age 62) [1, 2, 3, 4]. Additionally, at baseline, our cohort was more disabled than the inebilizumab and satralizumab trials. We had a greater proportion of African American patients in our cohort (36%) compared to clinical trials [1, 2, 3, 4]. These demographic and clinical features of our cohort provide data from an older and more diverse population.

There was a high probability of relapse‐free survival with all three nMAbs. Specifically, the probability of relapse‐free survival at 1 year was 94% for inebilizumab, 100% for eculizumab, and 100% for satralizumab. These observations confirm findings from the clinical trials (RCTs) and meta‐analyses of high relapse‐free probability for all therapies compared to placebo [16, 17]. Our findings are also similar to a recent eculizumab observational cohort study, which found that eculizumab reduces annualized attack rates and new MRI lesions in AQP4‐IgG+ NMOSD [11].

Notably, 65% of patients treated with nMAb were switched from other immunosuppressive therapies (39% due to relapse and 50% due to intolerance). In our cohort, the probability of relapse‐free survival at 1 year was 92% for rituximab. Although multiple studies have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of rituximab in the majority of NMOSD patients, there are a few patients who fail treatment, possibly due to genotypic variability or the development of immunogenicity, who may benefit from nMAb treatment [8, 18, 19].

In addition to low relapse rate, there was minimal MRI activity on nMAbs, further corroborating their effectiveness. There are few observational studies and clinical trials to date that have examined MRI activity as an efficacy outcome. A recent eculizumab observational cohort study showed a reduction in MRI activity [11]. A post hoc subgroup analysis of the N‐MOmentum study showed reduced MRI lesion activity on inebilizumab in comparison to the placebo group [20].

The most common adverse events, including pulmonary, urinary tract infections, and headaches, were not sufficiently serious to lead to nMAb discontinuation. This is similar to the recent clinical trials in NMOSD, where adverse effects from nMAbs were comparable to those in the placebo group, and suggest that these therapies are well tolerated by patients [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6].

Our study was limited by a small sample size, which precluded direct comparisons between nMabs or with rituximab. Further longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm the low relapse rate of nMAbs, given their shorter follow‐up duration compared to rituximab. This difference in follow‐up time may partially account for the higher number of relapses we observed with rituximab. Additionally, B‐cell repopulation data was limited for most relapses. Strengths of our study include the real‐world population, making our results more generalizable than trials. We also included imaging outcomes, which have rarely been reported in NMOSD cohorts to date.

This study demonstrated that nMAbs prevented relapses and were associated with minimal MRI activity in NMOSD patients in real‐world clinical practice. Additionally, the tolerability of all three nMAbs was excellent, and minimal infections were reported. These findings support utilizing nMAbs as first‐line therapy for relapse prevention for NMOSD.
