Construction of a potential ecological network based on public datasets: a case study in Tuscany
Authors: Andrea Gini, Simona Re, Angelo Facchini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article describes the building process behind an in silico ecological network of the species of Tuscany (Italy) and their different interactions as an OPEN (Open Potential Ecological Network). All the data sources consist of already open and available databases from experts and citizen science projects on species presence and interactions. The result is a multi-interaction metaweb which is uncommon on public repositories. Both the final dataset and the pre-built potential ecological network are completely open and ready to use. The methods and framework pipeline used for collecting, cleaning and joining the original databases are described and summarised. The network building process is generalised to facilitate adaptation and reproducibility for other geographical locations. This pipeline can be used to generate broader metaweb from existing data for the purpose of performing baseline ecological network analysis, as well as serving as an educational tool for the public.

Background

Ecological networks are complex systems that try to account for the plethora of interactions between different entities concisely, maintaining the intrinsic complexity of ecosystems [[1], [2], [3]]. Distinct ecological networks could be used for different scopes with theoretical and practical applications [[4], [5], [6]]. Open databases containing different types of ecological networks already exist, but most datasets are limited in terms of the number of entities or the type of habitat studied [7]. Building an ecological network is a time-consuming and difficult process that involves many researchers from different fields. Due to this complication, there is a lack of network data compared to other well-established ecological techniques [7,8] (see also the Supplementary Table from Brose et al [9]). As new methods [10,11] emerge to predict interactions, there isn't a consensus [12] or a protocol to standardise and accept ecological networks derived only by computations. Here we build and propose an in silico ecological network that portrays the potential interactions between the species of Tuscany from open data (OPEN – Open Potential Ecological Network).

This in silico potential ecological network is completely open and based on open data and citizen science projects. The species list is based on the GBIF (Global Biodiversity Information Facility) meta-database collection of 71 single data sources [13], including scientific surveys and national checklists available for the public and citizen science smartphone applications (e.g. iNaturalist). The interaction data is provided by a subset of the GLOBI (Global Biotic Interactions) database [14]. This potential interaction is real, established and physically recorded at a specific moment and space between the species. The concept of 'potential interaction' in the field of ecological network is also summarised as a metaweb [15], but in this project we wanted to focus not on graph embedding or machine learning techniques, but on the building process of a large network from open data and for a geopolitical boundary that is rational in the Italian context (Italian regions are often used as a unit for species distribution atlases).

GLOBI data could be accessed via query or geographical filter, but this standard process may hinder retrieval of certain data, excluding it. This is because some species entries may not be geolocated and, if they are, it is possible that a species is only recorded in the database for a nearby location. The GLOBI database captures information on interacting entities, so it is possible that some species present in Italy and interacting with each other are geolocated outside the country, because the original evidence of interactions is an article or a photograph not physically located in Italy. Another case could be a generic article that focuses on the interactions themselves and not on the location of the species. Our database exploits the GLOBI database at the node level for all species known in Tuscany (from public domain sources) without the inherent limitation of the quality of the data entered or geolocation.

We believe that many research questions, analyses, policy and social communication issues could benefit from an integrated regional-scale view [16] of potential species interactions. Thus, we offer the compiled database and the pre-built network [17] to facilitate dissemination, analysis and teaching activities that require a large set of data on species interactions. All data, techniques used and necessary software are open sources and can be downloaded and used free of charge. In addition to the release of the Tuscany metaweb, the same repository also hosts commented code snippets which are useful to recreate a similar metaweb or to adapt the same data cleaning pipeline to other locations of interest. In the specific case of the pre-built metaweb, some data were manually entered and checked for typos based on the specific species of interest and ecological knowledge of the authors and collaborators in the field of ecology.

Columns general naming scheme:

The input_output directory inside the repository contains the raw, cleaned and final output files. Raw files already include the metadata and naming scheme inside them or as a reference to their original documentation (e.g., GLOBI).

In the case of the partially cleaned, cleaned and final output files for our pipeline a common scheme was used: SourceTaxon[rank]Name and TargetTaxon[rank]Name. Source and Target refer to the source and target nodes that establish the relationship (e.g., a pollinator insect is the source which targets a plant species). The [rank] indicate the information about the taxonomy and it can be used to aggregated nodes (e.g., a pollinator with SourceTaxonSpeciesName Apis mellifera may be grouped together as Hymenoptera at the SourceTaxonOrderName).

As an example, this is the specific naming scheme that can be found in the dataset used for the metaweb building process: source = 'sourceTaxonSpeciesName', target = 'target', edge_key = 'interactionTypeName'. The source node for the interaction is sourceTaxonSpeciesName, an entity at the resolution of the species. The target of the interaction is simply the target column, since it contains taxa at different ranks. The interactionTypeName column contains the interaction label (the interactions naming scheme can be found in the methods section).

For a complete description of the files contents and the descriptions of all the tables please refer to the Zenodo repository. The html and Jupyter files explain the pipeline and the cleaning process from the raw to cleaned columns.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Data retrieval

The list of species present in Tuscany can be retrieved from GBIF (snapshot at April 26, 2021). The species list was downloaded using a filter based on the administrative subdivision of Italy (gadm.org) [13]. This dataset was cleaned of duplicates, thus obtaining all the entities present in Tuscany from all the 71 single sub-dataset within the GBIF collection.

The database of interactions was downloaded in its entirety from GLOBI (snapshot at 26 April 2021).

The GLOBI dataset snapshot is provided in a compressed way in the Zenodo repository associated with this article (input_output/interactions_stable.csv.gz) [17].

Data cleaning

Pandas (v. 1.3.3) was used in a Python environment (v. 3.8) to perform the data cleaning process . The GBIF database was split several times to obtain separate Python lists of species, genera, families, orders, and classes. The whole GBIF species list was also used as a standard to uniform the taxonomy.

The GLOBI database was set as a categorical data type to ease access and optimise memory usage. Then, it was cleaned up by removing unnecessary columns and information to retain only the source and target nodes (with their respective taxonomical hierarchy and IDs) combined by type of interaction. Subsequently, the database was filtered into source and target nodes with the GBIF checklist of Tuscan species in order to retain only data of the area of interest.

Simplified theoretical example 1:

Once the data from GBIF and GLOBI for the area of interest are obtained, Podarcis muralis Laurenti, 1768 turns out to be the only species of the genus Podarcis in the database. If so, all interactions for P. muralis are represented at the species level, making those reported at the Genus level duplicate information. Therefore, in cases where genus and species overlap, interaction data at the Genus level are not added to the database.

Taxonomical resolution at the species level is optimal for many common analyses in network science. But, since GLOBI records are not completely exhaustive for Italian fauna and flora, the species level of information has not always been possible. Thus, in order to obtain the most complete data possible, when an entity that was present in GBIF was not found in GLOBI, we included it even if with a different taxonomical resolution. Proceeding in this way, starting from the Phylum level, all entities were found to be already entered into the databases with some degree of taxonomical accuracy. This suggests how well the GLOBI data may already be a good representation of an actual regional biodiversity pool. In the rare case where an interaction was only present at a higher taxonomical level than the GBIF data, then the interaction was included in the database at the lower taxonomical level, that is, according to the GBIF presence data. This means that, in rare cases, our analyses may have enriched information about the interaction at a more specific level than that found in GLOBI.

Simplified theoretical example 2:

The GBIF data for the area of interest include Podarcis muralis and Podarcis siculus. However, while the genus Podarcis and the species P. muralis are present in the GLOBI database, P. siculus is not. As interactions at Genus level complement data at the species level, the unspecified interactions of P. siculus are added.

This also applies in cases where we have information only about the interactions for its genus but not about its species, thus including only Genus-level information, which translates into considering the genus as a node for interactions.

The finished database was checked for duplicates, taxonomical errors (especially at the Genus level), and impossible interactions. The GLOBI database is a powerful and deep source of information, but it can be subject to errors or mismatches. Some of these errors could be fixed en masse with a few cross-checks, but many can only be resolved manually.

Simplified theoretical example 3:

Vulpes vulpes Linnaeus, 1758 predated by Ricinus communis L. is considered an impossible interaction. In general, we treated this kind of data as taxonomical errors or typos kind of errors which cumulated in the original dataset due its particular data gathering process. In this case, R. communis may be an error of data input towards Ixodes ricinus (Linnaeus, 1758), or even a redundant name at the Genus level between animals and plants.

A complete change-log was not recorded at the time of the dataset cleaning since it was not considered important as this type of punctiform actions are common even in field notes. Every entity which exists inside the metaweb at upper than species level is to be automatically considered simplified. If inside the Tuscan metaweb dataset a node is recorded only at its Genus name, then the species was not present in the GLOBI dataset and so was collected at the Genus level.

Our approach to manually introduce as many species as possible does not directly affect the work pipeline. The in-silico approach can be used as it is, and if the researcher deems appropriate to have as much data as possible for a specific group of interest, it is needed to manually check if a species is present and then input at an upper rank. However, this remains an option as the quicker way to produce an in-silico metaweb remains to follow this pipeline without caring for missing species. The punctiform actions on typos and species with an updated name can also be performed by students during IT labs in order to learn coding applications, what is described in the paper is the general methodology to build a broader regional metaweb from already existing data which also retains multi-interactions information.

To summarise, most of the interactions present at the species level were originally already present at that level in the raw data, a minority of these interactions were created in the case of similar congeneric species (see example 2) based on expert knowledge. The interactions present at the genus level follow a similar pattern, if species of these genera are known to interact, the interaction was inserted at the genus level. This is also due the fact that many regional relationships can be represented at the genus level lower resolution without losing too much information or analysis power [18]. Punctiform actions represent both a limitation on the strict reproducibility of experimental design and an important step towards ensuring that human input and sector-specific expertise remain an integral part of the analysis of biodiversity data – including local data – and towards ensuring that data on focal species are as comprehensive as possible.

The latest cleaning process aimed at simplifying the interactions. Many interactions are detailed and redundant, so we aggregated some interactions under a common alias. Also, we dealt with interactions that involve direction (e.g., trophic, pollination, etc.) and those that are undirected (positioning and co-presence).

Network building and interactive visualisations

To optimise analysis and visualisation, we built the network in two different ways. The first was built in a Python environment (v. 3.8) with the NetworkX library (v. 2.6.1). The cleaned and filtered databases can be used as an unweighted edge list with node and edge properties (the taxonomy for each node and the interaction type for the edges). This network could be analysed differently or exported in a different format. It is already split into directed and undirected to ease analysis in NetworkX and to better capture the diversity of interactions. It is essential to use a fast and efficient tool to navigate a network of this size. We chose Cytoscape (v. 3.8.2) as our visualisation tool and rebuilt the directed network with its import tool for nodes and edges (see Table 1 for a summary description of the networks built for visualisation with the two major entities). Limitations

The interaction database was checked manually for any impossible interactions resulting from shared Genera names across animals and plants (e.g., a plant that eats a fox, as in theoretical example 3). In such cases, the database was searched for the same genus name but with the correct interaction to edit the wrong ones. If no compatible genus was found, the incorrect interaction was deleted.

Working with datasets this large and generated from different sources (from paper to citizen science projects) often means to inherit typos. However, species with the wrong name do not exclude those with the right name, and usually the majority of interactions reach the species with the correct name anyway, making the presence of punctiform errors in the totality of the nodes negligible.

The list of taxa at different hierarchical levels was checked for missing data and completed accordingly. For rank without clear taxonomical information, we decided to use the immediate lower or higher infra-rank (e.g., if a taxon did not have an official Family, we used the Superfamily or Subfamily). An exact and absolute accurate taxonomy was not our main goal, because taxonomy is very subject to change over time. Still, we tried to be as comprehensive as possible in order to have more information for grouping taxa in the future. Even with limitations, we want to highlight how a project like GLOBI is helpful in gathering interaction data, and how further improvements by the open community may be even more impactful in the future of metaweb building and ecological network studies. These groups of manual interventions can be really poorly automated and are mostly based on the expertise and knowledge of both authors, local experts, and domain specific collaborators (i.e., zoologists, ecologists, evolutionary biologists).

Finally, some of the previously corrected entries were selected in order to assess the accuracy and completeness of the information. The database, by its nature, remains updatable in the future for revisions and additions.
