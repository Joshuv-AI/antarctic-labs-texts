The hidden burden of sleep in adolescents with idiopathic generalized epilepsy
Authors: Carlo Alberto Quaranta, Martina Paola Zanaboni, Elena Ripamonti, Gaia Garghetti, Massimiliano Celario, Serena Grumi, Helene Vitali, Valentina De Giorgis, Ludovica Pasca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdiopathic Generalized Epilepsies (IGEs) are electroclinical syndromes characterized by distinct seizure types, EEG patterns, and presumed polygenic inheritance. While seizure prognosis is often favorable, sleep disturbances, behavioral and emotional difficulties, and executive function impairment are common, especially during adolescence.ObjectiveTo investigate the relationship between subjective sleep quality, emotional–behavioral functioning, and executive functions in adolescents with IGE.MethodsIn this single‐center observational study, 28 adolescents (mean age 15.0 ± 2.4 years) with IGE were evaluated with the following standardized measures: PSQI (sleep), BRIEF‐2 (executive functions), CBCL and YSR (emotional–behavioral profile), SAFA‐A/‐D/‐S (internalizing and somatic symptoms). Dream‐related experiences were also collected. Pearson correlations tested behavioral and emotional associations with sleep quality.ResultsPoor sleep quality (PSQI ≥5) occurred in 28.6% of patients, although only 14.8% reported sleep problems in their clinical history. Poor sleepers showed significantly higher anxiety, depression, somatic complaints, and stress‐related symptoms (p < .05). Executive function impairment, particularly in emotional control and cognitive flexibility, was more frequent in this group.SignificanceSleep quality is an under‐recognized but clinically relevant dimension in patients with IGE, closely linked to emotional vulnerability and executive function difficulties. Routine sleep screening and targeted sleep‐focused management should be integrated into pediatric IGE care to support psychological well‐being.Keywords: adolescents, emotional–behavioral symptoms, executive function EFs, idiopathic generalized epilepsy IGE, sleep quality

INTRODUCTION

Although formally subsumed under the umbrella of Genetic Generalized Epilepsies (GGEs) in the 2017 ILAE classification, the Idiopathic Generalized Epilepsies (IGEs) remain a clinically meaningful entity due to their distinctive features. 1 , 2 , 3 Traditionally, including four core syndromes, Childhood Absence Epilepsy (CAE), Juvenile Absence Epilepsy (JAE), Juvenile Myoclonic Epilepsy (JME), and Generalized Tonic–Clonic Seizures Alone (GTCS), IGEs account for 15%–20% of all epilepsies, with typical onset between childhood and early adulthood. 4 Patients generally show a favorable response to antiseizure medications (ASMs), yet up to one quarter of patients remain drug‐resistant. 5

IGEs do not appear to significantly impair global cognitive functioning, which generally remains within the normal range; however, growing evidence suggests the presence of subtle deficits in executive functions (EFs) and learning abilities, negatively affecting social functioning. 6 Psychiatric comorbidities—particularly mood and anxiety disorders as well as impulsivity and certain personality traits—along with adverse psychosocial outcomes, have been considered not merely consequences of a chronic disease but integral components of the IGE endophenotype. 7 , 8 , 9

Within this complex clinical landscape, sleep has emerged as a critical yet under‐recognized factor influencing disease expression. The epilepsy–sleep relationship is bidirectional: on one side, interictal epileptic discharges and ASMs can lead to impaired sleep quality, indeed IGEs typically present an increased spike–wave activity during sleep and transition to sleep 1 ; on the other side, sleep fragmentation and deprivation lower seizure threshold and trigger seizures and interictal epileptic activity. 10 , 11 , 12 Polysomnographic studies in adults with IGEs consistently demonstrate reduced sleep efficiency, increased arousals, and abnormal distribution of slow‐wave and REM sleep, changes possibly linked to cognitive dysfunction. 11 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 A recent meta‐analysis further highlights lower sleep efficiency and total sleep time, as well as prolonged REM latency, in IGEs compared to controls. 17 Pediatric data, though more limited, indicate similar alterations with associated attentional and behavioral disturbances. 14 , 15

Beyond seizures, sleep disruption impacts learning and memory processes, as well as mood and self‐regulation. 18 In clinical practice, the systematic assessment of sleep quality and the promotion of sleep hygiene in epileptic patients are strongly recommended, 19 not only to optimize seizure control, but also as integral components of overall health care. A deeper understanding of the interplay between sleep quality, cognition, and emotional–behavioral symptoms in IGEs is crucial to guide individualized management.

The present study investigates sleep quality, cognitive performance, EFs, and emotional–behavioral profiles in a pediatric IGE cohort, with the aim of disentangling their interrelationships to inform targeted diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This was a single‐center observational study primarily aimed at evaluating sleep quality in adolescents with IGEs. Secondary objectives were to explore the associations between sleep quality, cognitive functioning, EFs, and emotional‐behavioral profiles in this population.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committees of the IRCCS Mondino Foundation (Comitato Etico della Lombardia 6, Approval Number: 2023‐3.11/169) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06782074. Participants were consecutively recruited from outpatient clinics during routine clinical activities. All eligible patients who agreed to participate and provided written informed consent were included. Data collection began in May 2023 and ended in October 2024.

Participants

Adolescents (11–18 years) with IGE, diagnosed according to ILAE criteria by a trained epileptologist, were recruited.

Inclusion criteria were (1) a diagnosis of IGE, (2) age between 11 and 18 years, and (3) adequate compliance with study procedures.

Exclusion criteria were (1) a diagnosis of structural or cryptogenic epilepsy or any epilepsy other than IGE; (2) age below 11 or above 18 years old; (3) visual or motor impairments of such severity as to invalidate test administration; (4) a history of significant neurological or psychiatric conditions (e.g., brain malformations, stroke, head injury, schizophrenia, etc.); (5) intellectual disability (ID); and (6) inadequate compliance with study procedures.

Demographic and clinical data

Demographic and clinical data were collected at the time of enrollment through direct interviews and review of medical records. The collected information included sex, age at seizure onset, age at last seizure, time from seizure onset to evaluation, epileptic syndrome diagnosis, seizure type and frequency, seizure freedom, concomitant ASMs, sleep disorders, and family history of epilepsy or psychiatric disorders.

Measures and procedures

Sleep quality

All participants underwent subjective sleep quality assessment using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a validated self‐report questionnaire that evaluates sleep quality and disturbances across seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. 20

To explore dream activity and experience, three self‐report instruments were used: the Dream Recall Frequency Scale, the Van Dream Anxiety Scale, and the Manheim Dream Questionnaire (MADRE). 21 , 22

Cognition and executive function

Cognitive functioning was assessed using the Full‐Scale Intelligence Quotient (FSIQ) obtained from either the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children–Fourth Edition (WISC‐IV) 23 or the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale–Fourth Edition (WAIS‐IV) 24 according to patient's age.

Furthermore, each participant completed the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function–Self Report Version (BRIEF–SR), 25 for executive functioning assessment. Caregivers also completed the parent version of the BRIEF‐2. The BRIEF‐2 25 includes 7 scales: (1) Inhibition as ability to control impulses and stop inappropriate behavior; (2) Shift as cognitive flexibility and ability to transition between tasks; (3) Emotional Control as regulation of emotional responses; (4) Initiation as ability to begin tasks and generate ideas; (5) Working Memory as holding and manipulating information in mind; (6) Planning/Organization as managing complex tasks and setting goals; and (7) Monitoring as tracking performance and self‐correction. In addition, there are 4 Indices: Behavioral Regulation Index (BRI) includes Inhibition and Shift; Emotional Regulation Index (ERI) includes Shift and Emotional Control; Cognitive Regulation Index (CRI) includes Initiation, Working Memory, Planning/Organization, Monitoring; Global Executive Composite (GEC) as an overall measure of executive functioning.

Behavior, emotion, and affectivity

To assess behavior and mental health, parents completed the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL/6–18), 26 , 27 a standardized questionnaire widely used to identify behavioral/emotional problems and assess social competencies in children and adolescents. The CBCL consists of two main sections: one addressing social competence/adaptive functioning, and the other focusing on behavior problems. Based on responses, scores are computed for eight syndrome scales: Anxious/Depressed, Withdrawn/Depressed, Somatic Complaints, Social Problems, Thought Problems, Attention Problems, Rule‐Breaking Behavior, and Aggressive Behavior. These are further aggregated into three broadband scales: Internalizing Problems, Externalizing Problems, and Total Problems. In parallel, participants also completed the Youth Self‐Report (YSR), providing a self‐reported measure of emotional and behavioral functioning.

To dive deeper into anxiety and depression symptoms, the Psychiatric Scales for Self‐Administration for Youths and Adolescents (SAFA) 28 were used. This psychometric tool assesses a wide range of psychological symptoms and conditions in children and adolescents; it consists of six self‐report scales that can be administered either individually or collectively. For the purposes of this study, we used the following three SAFA subscales: SAFA‐A (Anxiety), SAFA‐D (Depression), and SAFA‐S (Somatic Symptoms and Hypochondria).

Statistical approach

Descriptive statistics were computed to characterize the sample in terms of demographic (gender and age at testing) and epilepsy‐related variables. For continuous variables, means and standard deviations were calculated.

Pearson's correlation coefficients were used to assess the relationship between epilepsy‐related variables, emotional profile, cognitive profile, and subjective sleep quality.

The association between sleep quality (assessed with PSQI) and executive functioning (BRIEF scores) and emotional–behavioral problems (CBCL, YSR, SAFA scores) was examined using two approaches: (i) Pearson's correlation analysis, treating the PSQI global score as a continuous variable, significance levels were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure (Benjamini–Hochberg, q = .05); (ii) participants were divided into two groups based on their PSQI total score (0–30), following established clinical criteria: a score of ≤5 indicated good sleep quality (group 1), while a score >5 indicated poor sleep quality (group 2). This dichotomization allowed for a clearer distinction between participants with good versus poor sleep, providing a more homogeneous and clinically meaningful representation of the sample. At this stage, independent samples t‐tests were conducted, comparing participants with poor sleep quality (PSQI >5) and good sleep quality (PSQI ≤5) on all other outcome measures.

Dream activity

Dream activity questionnaires (explorative analyses) were completed by 25 patients, of whom 14 (56%) reported habitually remembering their dreams. Most patients did not report issues related to nightmares, while approximately half (48%) expressed an interest in dreams. In depth, at least 20% of patients highlighted sleep difficulty due to nightmares.

Neuropsychological data

Cognitive profile

Regarding cognitive functioning, our sample performed within an average range FSIQ (mean 99.83, SD 11.3; minimum 84 – maximum 132). Subscales scores were as follows: Verbal Comprehension Index (VCI; mean 105, SD 12.05), Perceptual Reasoning Index (PRI; mean = 103.78, SD = 11.45), Working Memory Index (WMI; mean = 89.74, SD = 13.03), and Processing Speed Index (PSI; mean = 95.13, SD = 13.46).

Executive functions

EFs were assessed using both the parent and self‐report versions of the BRIEF‐2. According to parent reports, the most frequently affected domains included Working Memory (15.4%), Task Monitoring (19.2%), and Planning/Organization (19.2%). Self‐reports also indicated difficulties, particularly in Emotional Regulation (14.3%), Shift (10.7%), Self‐Monitoring (10.7%), and Working Memory. Full details are reported in the Supplementary results section.

Emotional and behavioral features

Emotional and behavioral functioning was assessed using the CBCL, YSR, and SAFA‐A, ‐D, and ‐S.

Among the clinically relevant domains of CBCL, the highest frequencies of clinical scores were observed for internalizing problems (20%), somatic complaints (19.2%), anxiety problems (26.9%), depressive problems (16%), and stress‐related problems (16%).

As far as YSR, the most affected domains included anxious/depressed (19.2%), stress‐related problems (19.2%), internalizing problems (15.4%), and withdrawn/depressed (15.4%).

At SAFA, the most affected domains were feelings of inadequacy and low self‐esteem (21.4%), generalized anxiety (17.9%), depression (14.3%), and simulation (14.3%). Somatic symptom scales were only mildly affected, with scores above the cut‐off in 3.6% of patients.

Dichotomized PSQI and clinically related variables

Significant differences (p < .05) from the t‐test between the two PSQI groups and clinical‐related variables were found.

Individuals with higher PSQI scores, indicating worse sleep quality, showed a significantly older age at last seizure (mean ± SD = 16.09 ± 2.08 years) compared to those with better sleep quality (mean ± SD = 12.43 ± 3.13 years). A trend toward a later age at seizure onset was also observed in the poorer sleep quality group (13.21 ± 2.31 years vs. 10.01 ± 4.83 years; p = .072). No significant difference emerged for the number of ASMs taken at the time of evaluation.

Dichotomized PSQI and executive functions in self‐reports

Significant differences (p < .05) in self‐reported executive function assessments between PSQI groups were observed in Shift, Emotion Regulation, ERI, CRI, and GEC, as illustrated in Figure 2. Participants with poorer sleep quality showed greater impairments in emotional control (t(26) = 2.49, p = .019), cognitive flexibility (Shift: t(26) = 2.35, p = .027), and both cognitive (t(26) = 2.12, p = .044) and emotional regulation indexes (t(26) = 2.72, p = .012), as well as in the Global Executive Composite (t(26) = 2.36, p = .026). Overall, poorer sleep quality was associated with broader executive functions impairment, encompassing deficits in both cognitive control and emotional self‐regulation.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to jointly assess sleep quality, emotional–behavioral symptoms, and executive functions within a single IGE pediatric cohort. While previous work has typically addressed these domains mainly in adult patients or in isolation, our integrated approach highlights that poor sleep quality may underlie broader behavioral and emotional difficulties in IGE.

Sleep disturbance emerged as a relevant clinical issue. Although only 14.8% of participants spontaneously reported sleep problems, standardized PSQI scores revealed poor sleep quality in 28.6%, with frequent complaints of prolonged latency, reduced sleep time, nocturnal awakenings, and daytime dysfunction. These results align with previous evidence on sleep quality measured by the PSQI in IGE adults 17 and in part mirror the physiological sleep changes typical of adolescence, a developmental phase particularly vulnerable to sleep–wake dysregulation. 18 The well‐known bidirectional link between sleep and epilepsy reinforces this susceptibility: seizures and ASMs disrupt sleep, whereas sleep loss facilitates epileptiform activity. 11 , 29 , 30 , 31 Subjects with a more recent seizure occurrence and a later age at epilepsy onset were more likely to exhibit impaired sleep quality; this finding is consistent with previous evidence showing a higher subjective disease burden close to diagnosis and in cases of uncontrolled seizures, particularly in patients with drug‐resistant forms of IGEs. 13

Poor sleep was significantly associated with lower EF performance according to self‐report BRIEF scores, showing lower scores in working memory and emotional control. Given that sleep disruption adversely affects daytime behavior and cognition, this link may reflect shared neurodevelopmental mechanisms. Frontal slow‐wave activity (SWA) undergoes major reorganization during adolescence, paralleling prefrontal cerebral maturation, 32 and its disruption may mechanistically contribute to EF deficits. 33 Consistent with meta‐analytic findings, 34 IGE patients generally perform worse than controls in verbal, attentional, and memory domains, independent of seizure control or medication. 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 These impairments, attributed to fronto‐thalamo‐cortical dysconnectivity, 7 may contribute to inefficient cognitive control. EF alteration also relates to behavioral problems frequently observed in pediatric epilepsy, 38 as reduced inhibitory and monitoring capacities compromise social cognition and adaptive behavior. 39 , 40

Specific EF processes have been associated with emotional disorders, and it has been suggested that depressive mood and anxiety are linked to difficulties in maintaining task goals and that EF impairments contribute to the maintenance of these emotional disorders. 41 Emotional regulation difficulties, such as blunted conflict responses, reduced empathy, and impaired recognition of negative emotions, likely reflect fronto‐limbic dysregulation and increase vulnerability to internalizing symptoms. 42 Our findings confirm a substantial emotional burden, particularly anxiety and depression symptoms, in agreement with previous pediatric IGE reports 9 ; Varesio et al., 43 for example, found anxiety symptoms in 40% and depression symptoms in 22.5% of adolescents with IGE using CBCL, YSR, and SAFA measures, rates closely matching those observed in our cohort. These symptoms often manifest through somatic complaints (e.g., fatigue, irritability, sleep problems), delaying recognition and appropriate referral. Sleep plays a crucial role in emotional regulation, 44 and greater sleep difficulties may exacerbate challenges in affective control and emotion recognition, further amplifying anxiety and depressive symptoms in youth with IGEs. Therefore, routine emotional and sleep screening should be integral to epilepsy care.

Dream‐related findings further illustrate the subjective impact of sleep disturbance: 20% of patients highlighted sleep difficulty due to nightmares and mood alterations related to dream content, with an overall high dream recall. Although the neurobiology of dreaming in epilepsy remains incompletely understood, frequent nocturnal awakenings, common in our cohort, likely facilitate dream recall and enhance emotional salience, 45 thereby compounding daytime distress and contributing to the broader sleep–emotion–cognition triad. In the absence of objective PSG data, speculations regarding REM‐specific mechanisms should be drawn with caution.

The finding that significant associations emerged between sleep quality and self‐reported measures of behavior and emotions, but not parent proxy reports, may reflect differences in the ability to recognize and report emotional and cognitive distress but can also suggest that sleep disturbances are more closely linked to subjective experiences of emotional and cognitive distress that subjects may be able to perceive and report better than caregivers. This interpretation may reflect informant‐specific effects that clinicians should be aware of and account for to achieve a comprehensive assessment. 46

The limitations of this study include the lack of an objective sleep assessment and the absence of a report on EEG features; nevertheless, to date, IGE epileptiform activity has not been found to be associated with the behavioral phenotype. 47

Although the PSQI is a well‐validated and widely adopted instrument, self‐report inherently captures the perceived experience of sleep rather than its objective features. This gap between subjective perception and objective sleep outcome represents an important caveat when interpreting the observed sleep–cognition–emotion associations and underscores the need for future studies incorporating polysomnography, actigraphy, or EEG spectral analyses to provide a neurophysiological basis driving these relationships and behavioral outcomes in pediatric IGEs.

Similarly, the absence of EEG data, including interictal discharge density and spike burden, could limit the results, and their contribution to the observed sleep–cognition–emotion associations cannot be excluded.

Furthermore, utilized scores were derived from patients' self‐reported psychiatric validated screening tools and not psychiatric diagnoses; as the chosen screening tools were validated and widely applied, a more detailed psychiatric assessment would probably not have affected the overall outcome. A subset of patients was receiving psychological support at the time of evaluation. Formal psychiatric comorbidities were excluded per protocol; however, a subset of patients was receiving psychological support, which in this context does not necessarily reflect underlying psychopathology. Given the small size of this subgroup, no formal statistical comparison of sleep quality between supported and non‐supported patients was performed. Future studies should incorporate standardized psychological interventions as a covariate, and longitudinal designs are warranted to explore the potential preventive role of psychotherapy in this population.

The cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the directionality of the observed associations. Finally, the study's sample size constitutes an additional limitation, reducing statistical power and limiting the generalizability of the findings. Replication in larger, multicenter, and syndrome‐stratified cohorts is required to extend these results. Our cohort comprised four distinct IGE syndromes pooled together, although this reflects that the shared genetic and neurophysiological substrate of the IGE spectrum, syndrome‐specific differences in sleep, including seizure timing distribution and semeiology, could not be explored given the insufficient sample size per subgroup. Syndrome‐stratified analyses in future larger cohorts are warranted. Although no significant difference in the number of concomitant ASMs was found between sleep quality groups, qualitative effects of specific agents on sleep architecture and neurocognitive functioning cannot be excluded.

Taken together, our results support a model in which poor sleep amplifies emotional dysregulation and executive impairment in pediatric IGEs. While the subjective nature of the sleep measures employed warrants interpretive caution, the consistency of associations across multiple validated instruments supports the plausibility of the proposed relationships and their clinical relevance.

Beyond its role as a physiological regulator, sleep quality may also act as a proxy for unmet emotional needs and internalizing traits. Clinically, poor sleep may serve as a sensitive early marker of hidden affective distress, highlighting the value of routine sleep screening in epilepsy care alongside targeted therapeutic strategies to improve sleep quality and hygiene. Neurobiologically, alterations in sleep architecture, particularly reduced restorative slow‐wave sleep, likely interact with limbic and prefrontal circuits, undermining emotion regulation and reinforcing internalizing symptomatology. Recognizing and addressing sleep impairment may therefore represent a critical avenue for improving not only seizure control but also the broader neuropsychiatric outcomes of children and adolescents living with IGEs.
