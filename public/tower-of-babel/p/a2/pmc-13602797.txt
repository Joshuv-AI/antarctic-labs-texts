Sevoflurane blood concentrations in patients undergoing mild and moderate hypothermic cardiac surgery: a prospective observational study
Authors: Takahiro Tamura, Atsushi Mori, Takahiro Imaizumi, Tatsuro Yokoyama, Kimitoshi Nishiwaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe aimed to clarify the changes in sevoflurane blood concentrations in cardiovascular surgeries with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) under mild to moderate hypothermia.MethodsIn this single-center, prospective observational study, we included patients undergoing elective cardiac surgery with CPB under mild and moderate hypothermia using four types of oxygenators. Radial artery blood samples were collected at repeated time points, both before and during CPB. Simultaneously, bladder temperature was recorded. Sevoflurane blood concentration was measured using gas chromatography.ResultsSevoflurane blood concentrations gradually increased during general anesthesia induction via native lung ventilation. When using CPB under mild hypothermia, the concentration dropped sharply after switching to an oxygenator but then gradually increased and reached equilibrium. These changes were observed in all four oxygenators, with no significant differences among them. In moderate hypothermic surgeries, the bladder temperature gradually decreased during the pre-circulatory-arrest phase, and the sevoflurane blood concentration increased accordingly. As rewarming began after the distal aortic anastomosis, the bladder temperature gradually increased, and the sevoflurane blood concentration returned to levels observed during native lung ventilation. In total, 85 patients undergoing mild hypothermic cardiac surgery (SE, n = 21; LI, n = 21; TE, n = 22; ME, n = 21) and 25 patients undergoing moderate hypothermic cardiac surgery were analyzed. Using a linear mixed-effects model, bladder temperature was significantly associated with sevoflurane blood concentration in both mild hypothermia (β = − 5.90 μg/mL per °C; n = 73 patients, 1,123 observations; p < 0.001) and moderate hypothermia (β = − 2.11 μg/mL per °C; n = 25 patients, 378 observations; p < 0.001) cardiac surgery. No significant differences in sevoflurane blood concentrations were observed among the four oxygenator types.ConclusionsWe demonstrated chronological changes in sevoflurane blood concentrations during mild and moderate hypothermic cardiac surgery, showing an inverse correlation with bladder temperature.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04053-x.

Background

In laboratory studies, volatile anesthetics (VAs), including widely used sevoflurane, have demonstrated remarkable cardioprotective effects, particularly through preconditioning mechanisms and during reperfusion, making them useful agents in surgeries involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) [1–4]. Some small-scale clinical trials have shown that VAs improve postoperative myocardial function and reduce markers of myocardial injury [5–8]. However, the large-scale MYRIAD study has yet to prove the superiority of VAs over total intravenous anesthesia in terms of 1-year mortality following coronary artery bypass graft surgery [9], and the myocardial protective effect of VAs remains inconclusive. Zaugg et al. emphasized that the timing and duration of VA administration are critical to maximizing cardioprotective benefits [10]. While the debate regarding the cardioprotective effects of VAs continues, an important prerequisite is the accurate characterization of VA pharmacokinetics during CPB, which is essential for both optimizing anesthetic management and designing future clinical trials investigating VA-mediated organ protection.

During CPB, assessment of anesthetic delivery differs fundamentally from that during native lung ventilation. Membrane oxygenators vary in design characteristics, including membrane material, surface area, and flow configuration, which may influence the efficiency of volatile anesthetic transfer from the sweep gas to the blood. Owing to the structural characteristics of membrane oxygenators, measurement and interpretation of end-tidal VA concentrations are more complex compared to conventional pulmonary ventilation. As a result, anesthesiologists cannot rely solely on end-tidal VA concentrations to estimate anesthetic exposure during CPB. Direct characterization of blood concentrations may therefore provide important information regarding actual anesthetic delivery under hypothermic CPB conditions. Moreover, patients undergoing CPB experience non-physiological temperature changes that differ markedly from those observed during standard general anesthesia. Under these conditions, temperature-induced changes in anesthetic solubility may alter anesthetic exposure and potentially influence anesthetic depth, potentially affecting intraoperative awareness prevention, cardiovascular stability, and postoperative recovery. Despite the widespread use of CPB and hypothermia in cardiac surgery, systematic measurement of sevoflurane blood concentration changes throughout the cooling and rewarming phases remains limited, representing an important knowledge gap that may affect clinical decision-making regarding vaporizer settings, anesthetic depth monitoring, and anesthetic management.

According to Henry’s law, the solubility of a gas in a liquid increases as temperature decreases. This well-established physical principle suggests that hypothermia during CPB may increase the blood solubility of sevoflurane, potentially leading to higher blood concentrations at unchanged vaporizer settings. Because anesthetic gas transfer across biological membranes is governed by partial-pressure gradients rather than by blood concentration, the interpretation of blood concentration data under hypothermic conditions should be made with awareness of this physicochemical principle. However, the magnitude of this temperature-dependent change in blood concentration in the clinical setting—particularly across different oxygenator types and varying degrees of hypothermia—has not been systematically quantified. In addition, hemodilution resulting from the priming volume of the CPB circuit may influence measured anesthetic blood concentrations at the onset of CPB. In our observations, arterial blood sevoflurane concentration transiently decreased immediately after initiation of CPB but subsequently stabilized despite the continued hemodilution. This pattern suggests that equilibration of sevoflurane delivery through the oxygenator and systemic circulation also contributes to determining arterial blood anesthetic concentration during CPB.

The blood concentration changes over time during CPB remain poorly understood, except for findings from studies on isoflurane and sevoflurane [11–13]. In our previous pilot study using four types of oxygenators, we demonstrated that the sevoflurane blood concentrations at 20 min after the initiation of CPB were equivalent to those at 20 min after its introduction into the native lung ventilation in patients who were scheduled for elective cardiac or aortic surgery with CPB [14]. However, the blood concentrations rapidly decreased to approximately 25% of the equilibrium concentration achieved during native lung ventilation at 5 min after switching to CPB [15]. To date, no studies have specifically addressed sevoflurane blood concentration changes throughout hypothermic cardiac surgery involving CPB, highlighting a notable gap in the literature. We hypothesized that 1) sevoflurane blood concentrations would increase with decreasing body temperature during hypothermic CPB, consistent with temperature-dependent increases in gas solubility, and 2) the four polypropylene membrane oxygenator types would deliver equivalent sevoflurane blood concentrations under standardized conditions. To address this gap, we aimed to measure sevoflurane blood concentration during mild and moderate hypothermic cardiac surgery in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass graft (mild hypothermic cardiac surgery; bladder temperature ranging from 35–32 °C) and complex aortic surgeries, such as total arch replacement (TAR) (moderate hypothermic cardiac surgery; bladder temperature drops to approximately 25 °C), providing a deeper understanding of temperature-related effects on sevoflurane pharmacokinetics. The findings may provide quantitative information relevant to anesthetic management during hypothermic cardiac surgery.

Methods

Patients

This prospective study was registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN ID: UMIN000042084) and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Nagoya University Hospital (IRB #2020–0422). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to participation. Patients aged 20–90 years were scheduled for elective cardiac surgery with CPB or elective TAR surgery. Those having contraindications for VA use, including malignant hyperthermia and notable renal or hepatic impairment, were excluded. Patients were assigned to one of four oxygenator groups based on body surface area (BSA): Senko (SE group; HPO-21FHP EXELUNG, Senko Medical Instrument, Tokyo, Japan; BSA 1.4–1.8 m2), LivaNova (LI group; INSPIRE 6 F, LivaNova, Tokyo, Japan; BSA 1.4–1.8 m2), Terumo (TE group; CAPIOX FX, Terumo, Tokyo, Japan; BSA < 1.4 m2), and Medtronic (ME group; Fusion, Medtronic, Tokyo, Japan; BSA > 1.8 m2).

Patient treatment

During native lung ventilation prior to CPB, patients were mechanically ventilated with a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg predicted body weight, a respiratory rate of 10–14 breaths/min, and a positive end-expiratory pressure of 5 cmH₂O. Fresh gas flow was maintained at 3.0 L/min with an oxygen/air mixture (FiO₂ 60%) and sevoflurane at 1.7 vol% delivered via the anesthesia machine vaporizer.

Intraoperative general anesthesia procedures, including the anesthesia machine and monitors such as electroencephalography, were the same as described for sevoflurane in our previous report [14]. During CPB, pump flow was maintained at approximately 2.6 L/min/m2 (± 10%) according to institutional protocols. Temperature management was performed using an integrated heat exchanger incorporated in the oxygenator, allowing controlled cooling and rewarming during CPB. In both types of surgery, once the target pump flow rate was achieved, ventilation was stopped, and a constant mixture of oxygen and air (FiO2 50–60%) was supplied at 0.5–6.0 L/min sweep gas flow with 1.7 vol% sevoflurane through the oxygenator vaporizer (D-Vaper 2000, Dräger Medical Japan Co., Tokyo, Japan). This single model of oxygenator-mounted vaporizer was used for sevoflurane delivery in all cases. The anesthesia machine used in this study was the Perseus A500 (Dräger Medical Japan Co., Tokyo, Japan), and the anesthesia machine and vaporizers underwent monthly maintenance and calibration by clinical engineers to ensure accurate anesthetic delivery under controlled sweep gas flow conditions. Alpha-stat blood gas management was used for acid–base control during mild hypothermic surgeries. In the moderate hypothermic surgeries, after aortic cross-clamping and administration of cold cardioplegia, hypothermic CPB was started with the goal of moderate hypothermia defined as a bladder temperature of 25 °C while maintaining stable perfusion pressure, in preparation for circulatory arrest during TAR. Hypothermia was classified according to the consensus definition proposed for aortic arch surgery [16]. Upon reaching a bladder temperature of 25 °C, the pH–stat blood gas management protocol was implemented. A selective cerebral perfusion catheter was inserted to enable circulatory arrest for TAR. After the distal aortic anastomosis, the peripheral circulation was resumed via CPB, rewarming was initiated, and proximal anastomosis was performed, followed by cervical branch anastomosis. CPB was conducted according to institutional protocols using roller or centrifugal pumps with integrated heat exchangers. Postoperatively, patients were transferred intubated to the intensive care unit.

Blood sample collection

Blood samples (0.5 mL) were obtained through a radial artery cannula connected to a closed blood sampling circuit (Tru Wave with VAMP system; Edwards Life Sciences, Irvine, CA, USA) and immediately transferred into sealed headspace vials containing 5 mL of saline at the bedside. Samples were stored at room temperature and analyzed on the same day to minimize volatilization. Gas chromatographic measurements were performed by trained personnel experienced in volatile anesthetic analysis using a standardized analytical protocol. Native lung ventilation samples, representing blood collected under sevoflurane anesthesia without CPB, were taken every 5 min for the first 30 min after anesthesia induction. In mild hypothermic surgeries, additional samples were collected every 2.5 min for 20 min, followed by 5-min intervals until 60 min after the start of delivery of 1.7 vol% sevoflurane via the oxygenator. During the pre-circulatory-arrest phase of moderate hypothermic surgeries, samples were collected every 2.5 min for 20 min, followed by 5-min intervals until 30 min. After completion of the anastomosis and resumption of lower-limb circulation, blood samples were collected at 2.5-min intervals for 20 min and every 5 min up to 60 min. Other variables, such as sweep gas flow rate, bladder temperature, electroencephalography data, patient demographics, and surgical details (including anesthesia, operation, and CPB times), were recorded from the electronic medical charts (ORSYS and ACSYS, Philips, Amsterdam, Netherlands). In this study, bladder temperature was used as body temperature data based on the evidence that bladder temperature provides a reliable estimate of core temperature when adequate urine output is maintained [17, 18].

Measurement of sevoflurane blood concentration

Sevoflurane blood concentrations were measured as previously described with minor modifications [14]. The modifications from our previous method included optimization of headspace equilibration conditions and extension of the calibration range to accommodate the wider blood concentration range observed during moderate hypothermia. Sevoflurane and its internal standard, isoflurane, were purchased from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd. (Osaka, Japan). The headspace analysis was performed on a Shimadzu GC-2010 Plus (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) with a flame ionization detector equipped with a capillary column SUPELCOWAX 10 (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25-μm film thickness; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The oven and detector temperatures were set to 60 °C and 120 °C, respectively. The Shimadzu HS-20 headspace autosampler was used with an inlet pressure of 90 kPa, with helium as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 36.5 mL/min. Data acquisition and processing, analyte identification, and quantification were performed using LabSolutions Insight™ software (ver. 5.99, Shimadzu) and analyzed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Blood concentrations were calculated in μg/mL and corrected for blood sample weight. The correlation coefficient of the linear calibration curve was r2 > 0.99, and linearity was observed across the entire blood concentration range (0–152.6 μg/mL) with a limit of detection at 7.63 μg/mL. Intra- and inter-day validation result ranges were 0–15% for accuracy (% error) and 1.9–5.1% for precision (%RSD).

Sample size determination

Sample size calculations of both types of surgery on sevoflurane blood concentration were based on a previous study [14]. The chronological changes in the blood concentrations during mild hypothermic cardiac surgeries were compared among four types of oxygenators. We designed a non-inferiority trial with two groups showing the greatest mean difference. A sample size calculation with a common standard deviation of 6 μg/mL, derived from our previous pilot study [14], and a mean difference of 3 μg/mL required at least 18 cases in each group, with a clinically meaningful difference of 2 μg/mL, α error = 5%, and 80% power. To accommodate potential dropouts, the target number of cases increased to 20 per group. In moderate hypothermic cardiac surgery, the initial target was set at 20 cases per oxygenator, totaling 80 patients. However, we changed to the total target number of 20 cases regardless of the type of oxygenator, assuming the equivalent performance of the four oxygenators in delivering sevoflurane based on the mild hypothermic data.

Statistical analyses

The primary analysis of this study was performed using a linear mixed-effects (LME) model to evaluate the relationship between bladder temperature and sevoflurane blood concentration based on individual patient-level data. The LME model was selected because it accounts for the correlated nature of repeated measurements within participants. In the model, patient identity was included as a random intercept, and bladder temperature was included as a fixed effect. This model constituted the main analytical framework for assessing the temperature–blood concentration relationship in this study. Data are presented as means ± standard deviations. In mild hypothermic cardiac surgeries, demographics and surgery-related times, such as anesthesia time and CPB time, were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For supplementary comparison of temporal profiles among oxygenator groups in the mild hypothermic cohort, blood concentrations were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA. Blood concentrations between oxygenators at specific time points were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s test. Blood concentrations between time points in each oxygenator were compared using a two-tailed t-test, with the Holm–Bonferroni sequential correction applied to control for inflation of Type I error due to multiple comparisons. Correlation analyses using individual patient-level measurements were performed as supplementary analyses to demonstrate trends between bladder temperature and sevoflurane blood concentration, and they were not used as the primary basis for statistical inference. Descriptive statistics were employed for electroencephalography-related figures. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant (two-tailed). Stata 17 MP software (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA), Python version 3.10 (https://www.python.org/), or R version 4.2.1 (https://www.r-project.org/) was used for statistical analyses.

Results

Patients were recruited between March 1, 2021, and March 31, 2023. The number of cases included in each analysis is described below. Other outcomes assessed included complications related to oxygenators, such as plasma leaks, fissures, denaturation, blood clots, hypoxia, and other unexpected complications. No oxygenator-related complications were observed in this study. There were no cases of intraoperative awareness reported by patients postoperatively, and no adverse events attributable to sevoflurane administration via CPB were identified.

Three patients in the SE group, three in the LI group, four in the TE group, and six in the ME group were excluded owing to gas chromatography measurement failure, including gas leaks from sample vials. No patients withdrew from the study. Finally, 21 patients in the SE, LI, and ME groups and 22 patients in the TE group were included. For the LME model analysis in the mild hypothermic cohort, only measurements obtained at bladder temperatures ≥ 32 °C were included, consistent with the defined mild hypothermic range (35–32 °C). After applying this temperature filter, 73 of the 85 patients contributed 1,123 observations to the model. Bladder temperature was found to be significantly correlated with sevoflurane blood concentration during mild hypothermic cardiac surgery (β = − 5.90 μg/mL per °C, 95% CI [− 6.26, − 5.53], p < 0.001), indicating an increase in blood concentration with decreasing temperature. No significant interaction between oxygenator type and bladder temperature was observed (likelihood ratio test: χ2 = 0.66, df = 3, p = 0.882), supporting comparable performance among the four oxygenators. In the LME model for the moderate hypothermic cohort (n = 25 patients, 378 observations), with patient identity as a random intercept and bladder temperature as a fixed effect, bladder temperature was significantly associated with sevoflurane blood concentration (β = − 2.11 μg/mL per °C, 95% CI [− 2.29, − 1.93], p < 0.001). For visualization of the relationship at the individual patient level, scatter plots of bladder temperature versus sevoflurane blood concentration for all measurements obtained during moderate hypothermia are shown in Fig. 3b. Given the limited sample size and the comparable performance of oxygenators demonstrated in the mild hypothermic cohort, data from all oxygenator types were analyzed together. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for descriptive purposes only and were not used as the primary basis for statistical inference.

When data from both mild and moderate hypothermic cohorts were combined, the overall LME model yielded β = − 2.60 μg/mL per °C (95% CI [− 2.76, − 2.44], p < 0.001), confirming an inverse association between bladder temperature and sevoflurane blood concentration across the entire temperature range studied (19.3–37.3 °C). The combined dataset comprised 98 patients (73 mild-hypothermic and 25 moderate-hypothermic patients) contributing 1,528 observations (1,150 and 378 observations, respectively). The 73 mild-hypothermic patients were the same individuals included in the cohort-specific mild-hypothermia LME model. However, whereas the cohort-specific model included only measurements obtained at bladder temperatures ≥ 32 °C (1,123 observations), the combined analysis additionally retained 27 sub-32 °C measurements from 6 of these patients to cover the full temperature range, resulting in 1,150 mild-cohort observations. A likelihood ratio test revealed that the temperature–blood concentration slopes differed significantly between the two cohorts (χ2 = 173.5, p < 0.001), indicating that the magnitude of blood concentration change per degree Celsius varies across the temperature ranges represented by the two cohorts.

Discussion

We present three key findings: 1) chronological changes in sevoflurane blood concentrations during mild and moderate hypothermic cardiac surgery, 2) similar sevoflurane blood concentrations achieved by the four oxygenators at the same doses during CPB, and 3) an inverse correlation between sevoflurane blood concentrations and bladder temperature in patients with hypothermia.

In the pilot study, the experimental data were only from three cases/groups; however, we displayed that the sevoflurane blood concentrations in the four oxygenators used in this study, at 20 min after switching from native lung ventilation to oxygenator, were equivalent to those at 20 min after initiating administration to the native lung ventilation. Moreover, the 20-min values during CPB did not significantly differ from the 40-min values for each oxygenator [14], suggesting no functional differences among the four oxygenators. In the present study, we measured sevoflurane blood concentrations and bladder temperatures over time after initiating native lung ventilation administration and switching to CPB. The sevoflurane blood concentrations did not differ among the four oxygenators, even with a decrease in bladder temperature. Furthermore, post hoc pairwise comparisons using Tukey's test showed that sevoflurane blood concentrations did not significantly differ among the oxygenators at 20, 40, and 60 min after switching to CPB, with no bladder temperature differences (see Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). The sevoflurane blood concentrations at 20 min after native lung ventilation administration and after switching to CPB, or between 20 and 40 min following CPB, did not differ significantly. In addition, the relationship between bladder temperature and sevoflurane blood concentration was assessed using individual patient-level repeated measurements, demonstrating a consistent inverse association under hypothermic conditions. Hence, the results of the previous study can be replicated using a larger sample size, supporting the similar functional quality of the four oxygenators used.

Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies investigating volatile anesthetic blood concentrations during CPB. Wiesenack et al. [11] demonstrated differential isoflurane uptake and elimination rates among different membrane oxygenators, and Alston et al. [12] reported comparable arterial blood isoflurane concentrations between two polypropylene oxygenators during CPB. Nitzschke et al. [13] documented changes in sevoflurane blood concentrations during on-pump cardiac surgery and similarly observed temperature-dependent effects on anesthetic blood concentrations. The present study extends these observations by systematically comparing four commercially available oxygenators and by characterizing sevoflurane blood concentration changes across a wider range of hypothermic conditions, including moderate hypothermia with circulatory arrest.

As described previously [14], we used the sevoflurane blood concentration at 20 min after initiating native lung ventilation as the standard for comparison because it is the equilibrium point based on data from the sevoflurane interview form and package insert. In this study, each oxygenator group did not significantly differ (21–22 cases), when comparing 20 and 30 min after initiating native lung ventilation. However, the two time points across all 85 patients significantly differed (p = 0.012, two-tailed t-test). Therefore, when comparing sevoflurane blood concentrations under conditions without CPB and within the physiological temperature range typically encountered during general anesthesia, sevoflurane blood concentrations achieved via native lung ventilation can be considered equivalent among the four oxygenators. In contrast, under conditions involving CPB with progressive hypothermia, blood concentrations may be influenced by temperature-dependent changes in solubility, and fixed time-based equilibrium points may no longer be appropriate.

Mild hypothermia was associated with changes in bladder temperature after switching from native lung ventilation to CPB. The four groups showed an average decrease of 1.56–1.82 °C at 20 min after initiating CPB. Sevoflurane blood concentration at 20 min after switching to CPB was not significantly different from that at 20 min after starting native lung ventilation. This similarity likely reflects the combined effect of ongoing anesthetic administration and early temperature-related increases in solubility rather than attainment of a fixed equilibrium time point. However, it was nearly identical to the blood concentration at 30 min after initiation, probably owing to the increased solubility of sevoflurane in blood resulting from the decrease in body temperature. Further reductions in bladder temperature and increases in sevoflurane blood concentrations were observed at 20–40 and 60 min after switching to CPB. The 20- and 60-min periods significantly differed (Holm–Bonferroni corrected), revealing that sevoflurane blood concentration increases in mild hypothermia (see Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Overall, during mild hypothermia, sevoflurane blood concentrations showed only modest increases over the 60-min observation period, consistent with temperature-dependent changes in solubility, and they remained within a range commonly encountered in routine anesthetic practice.

In moderate hypothermic cardiovascular surgery, the sevoflurane blood concentration reached approximately 1.4 times the equilibrium blood concentration achieved by native lung ventilation administration after switching to CPB. Based on the information in the interview form, a blood concentration of 70 μg/mL corresponds to 3% sevoflurane administered to an adult [19]. Although this blood concentration is within a range that has been reported in routine clinical practice, the pharmacodynamic implications cannot be inferred from blood concentration alone, since anesthetic effect is determined by partial pressure, which was not directly measured in this study. Accordingly, care must be taken when interpreting this finding for clinical decision-making during hypothermia. In addition, during the rewarming phase following circulatory arrest in the moderate hypothermic cohort, sevoflurane blood concentration gradually decreased toward levels observed under normothermic conditions.

The temperature coefficient differed between the mild and moderate hypothermic cohorts (β = − 5.90 vs. − 2.11 μg/mL per °C), and a likelihood ratio test confirmed that these slopes were significantly different (χ2 = 173.5, p < 0.001). This difference likely reflects the non-linear nature of the temperature–solubility relationship. During mild hypothermia, the relatively small temperature decrease (1–5 °C below normothermia) occurs along a steeper portion of the solubility curve, whereas during moderate hypothermia additional physiological factors — including circulatory arrest, altered tissue perfusion during rewarming, and changes in the blood–gas partition coefficient at lower temperatures — may attenuate the apparent coefficient. Formal model comparison using maximum likelihood estimation confirmed that a cohort-specific slopes model (AIC = 9927.1) provided a substantially better fit than a single unified slope model (AIC = 10,127.0; likelihood ratio test: χ2 = 203.8, df = 1, p < 0.001). A quadratic temperature term also significantly improved model fit (AIC = 9942.4; χ2 = 186.6, df = 1, p < 0.001), consistent with the non-linear solubility–temperature relationship. When all data were combined, the overall temperature coefficient was β = − 2.60 μg/mL per °C (95% CI [− 2.76, − 2.44]; n = 98 patients, 1,528 observations), yielding a single overall slope across the full temperature range studied (19.3–37.3 °C); this combined estimate should be regarded only as a descriptive summary of the pooled data and not as a clinically actionable parameter. The cohort-specific values likewise describe the temperature–blood concentration relationship observed in each clinical setting. Because anesthetic effect is determined by partial pressure rather than by blood concentration — and partial pressure was not measured in this study — neither the combined nor the cohort-specific coefficients should be used to infer pharmacodynamic effects or to guide vaporizer adjustment.

Hypothermia itself reduces cerebral metabolic rate and suppresses central nervous system activity, resulting in decreased anesthetic requirements as body temperature falls. At sufficiently low temperatures, patients may become unconscious even in the absence of anesthetic agents. Therefore, the higher sevoflurane blood concentrations observed during hypothermia should be interpreted in the context of reduced anesthetic requirements at lower temperatures. Because anesthetic pharmacodynamics depend on partial pressure rather than on blood concentration, the clinical meaning of these temperature-associated increases in blood concentration cannot be inferred directly from the present data. This interaction between temperature-dependent anesthetic pharmacokinetics and reduced anesthetic requirements may be relevant when interpreting anesthetic pharmacokinetics during hypothermic CPB.

At the onset of CPB, acute hemodilution resulting from the circuit priming volume may contribute to changes in measured sevoflurane blood concentration. However, the initial decline may also reflect a transient reduction in effective sevoflurane delivery during the transition from native lung ventilation to oxygenator-based administration before full CPB flow is established [15]. In this study, a fixed vaporizer setting was used throughout both the native lung ventilation and CPB phases to allow standardized comparison of sevoflurane delivery across different conditions. We acknowledge that this approach differs from routine clinical practice, in which anesthesiologists often apply an initial overpressure to rapidly achieve the desired anesthetic partial pressure, particularly during the early phase of CPB. The use of a fixed vaporizer setting in this study was intended to minimize variability in anesthetic input and to isolate the effects of temperature and oxygenator characteristics on measured blood concentrations. However, this simplified experimental condition may limit the direct applicability of our findings to dynamic clinical settings where anesthetic delivery is continuously adjusted.

From a clinical and anesthesiological perspective, our observations provide descriptive information about sevoflurane pharmacokinetics during hypothermic cardiac surgery. Under hypothermic conditions, sevoflurane blood concentration increases despite unchanged vaporizer settings, reflecting increased solubility of VAs at lower body temperatures. This pharmacokinetic behavior should be recognized when interpreting blood concentration data during CPB, particularly during moderate hypothermia associated with circulatory arrest. Because anesthetic effect is governed by partial pressure — which was not directly measured here — the present blood concentration data alone are not sufficient to guide vaporizer adjustments, and any clinical application should be made cautiously and in conjunction with established monitoring and clinical judgment. During hypothermic circulatory arrest, electroencephalographic indices such as the bispectral index or SedLine typically decrease to near-zero values, limiting their utility for anesthetic depth monitoring. In contemporary clinical practice, selective cerebral perfusion is frequently maintained during circulatory arrest, allowing sevoflurane delivered via CPB to continue reaching the cerebral circulation. These observations may therefore contribute, together with established monitoring modalities, to a broader understanding of anesthetic behavior when conventional depth monitoring becomes unreliable; however, prospective studies that integrate direct partial-pressure measurements and clinical outcomes are required before these observations can inform specific vaporizer-adjustment strategies.

The challenge of monitoring anesthetic depth during hypothermic CPB warrants further consideration. Processed electroencephalographic indices, such as the bispectral index and SedLine patient state index, become unreliable during moderate to deep hypothermia because electroencephalographic activity itself is suppressed by hypothermia. In such circumstances, alternative monitoring strategies may be valuable. Raw electroencephalographic interpretation, including the assessment of burst suppression patterns, can provide information about cortical activity that is not captured by processed indices. Furthermore, a multimodal neuromonitoring approach—integrating raw electroencephalographic data, somatosensory evoked potentials, and pharmacokinetic parameters such as anesthetic blood concentrations—may offer a more comprehensive assessment of anesthetic depth. Although our temperature–concentration observations may be considered alongside such established monitoring modalities in future investigations, blood concentration data alone do not provide a direct measure of cerebral anesthetic exposure, and any role within a multimodal framework would require prospective validation against direct partial-pressure measurements before any clinical utility could be claimed. Although processed electroencephalographic data were recorded in this study (Supplementary Fig. S1), a formal analysis of the relationship between sevoflurane blood concentrations and processed EEG indices during hypothermia was beyond the scope of this investigation. Because hypothermia itself can substantially influence EEG activity and processed EEG indices, interpretation of anesthetic depth under these conditions remains complex. Future studies integrating anesthetic blood concentrations, temperature, and electrophysiological monitoring may provide further insight into anesthetic depth during CPB.

The observed increase in sevoflurane blood concentration with decreasing bladder temperature is consistent with the well-established physicochemical relationship between gas solubility and temperature. According to Henry’s law, the solubility of VAs in blood increases as temperature decreases, resulting in a larger quantity of anesthetic dissolved in blood at lower temperatures for a given partial pressure. During hypothermic CPB, this temperature-dependent increase in solubility may therefore contribute to higher measured arterial blood concentrations of sevoflurane even when the vaporizer concentration remains unchanged. Importantly, the pharmacodynamic effects of VAs are determined primarily by their partial pressure rather than by blood concentration. Because the present study quantified arterial blood concentrations but did not directly measure anesthetic partial pressure in blood or in the oxygenator gas phase, it is not possible to determine whether the observed increase reflects a rise in anesthetic partial pressure or increased solubility at lower temperatures.

Another important finding is the absence of significant differences in sevoflurane blood concentrations among the four oxygenators examined. Although our institution uses four types of oxygenators, many centers use only one or two devices. The oxygenators evaluated in this study account for approximately 85% of the CPB oxygenator market share in Japan. Therefore, these findings suggest that sevoflurane can be administered via CPB in a consistent manner regardless of oxygenator type in most clinical settings. This equivalence also has implications for future clinical and translational research. In studies investigating myocardial preconditioning or organ-protective effects of VAs during cardiac surgery, provided that polypropylene membrane oxygenators are used, differences in oxygenator type are unlikely to confound outcomes. Therefore, the present findings provide a methodological foundation for future investigations evaluating VA–based cardioprotection during CPB. Regarding statistical considerations, the primary outcome of this study was sevoflurane blood concentration, a continuous variable measured repeatedly within individual patients. A LME model was therefore used as the primary analytical approach to appropriately account for within-subject correlation and interindividual variability. Correlation analyses were performed as supplementary analyses for descriptive purposes and were not intended to establish causal relationships. By including patient identity as a random effect in the LME model, the relationship between bladder temperature and sevoflurane blood concentration was evaluated while accounting for repeated measurements within individuals and interindividual variability, allowing estimation of the temperature effect independent of baseline differences among patients. We acknowledge that alternative approaches to analyzing repeated measurements, such as summarizing individual patient data using the area-under-the-curve, could provide complementary evidence. However, such summary measures reduce the temporal resolution of the data and would limit evaluation of the dynamic relationship between temperature and sevoflurane blood concentrations during CPB.

Limitations

The varying membrane areas of the oxygenators, which define the perfusion volumes, were a limitation in this study. Oxygenator selection was based on the BSA of the patient: < 1.4 m2 in TE, > 1.8 m2 in ME, and 1.4–1.8 m2 in SE and LI, determined by the clinical engineer and surgeon. However, the choice of oxygenator did not influence the sevoflurane blood concentration. We used bladder temperature as a surrogate for core body temperature because it is widely used in cardiac surgery and has been shown to provide a reliable estimate of core temperature when adequate urine output is maintained [17, 18]. However, other monitoring sites, such as nasopharyngeal temperature or mixed venous blood temperature, may reflect different physiological aspects of temperature distribution during CPB and could influence the observed relationship between temperature and anesthetic blood concentration. This was a single-center observational study, and the number of patients undergoing moderate hypothermic cardiac surgery was relatively limited. Furthermore, we focused on pharmacokinetic behavior, and we did not evaluate clinical outcomes such as myocardial injury markers or neurological complications.

This observational study is subject to the inherent limitations of a non-randomized design. Oxygenator allocation was based on BSA, reflecting routine clinical practice, and resulted in systematic differences in patient body size among groups (Table 1). In addition, CPB management parameters, including pump flow rate and sweep gas flow, were adjusted according to patient size and clinical requirements. These factors may introduce variability in anesthetic transfer and should be considered when interpreting the equivalence of sevoflurane delivery among oxygenator types. Most importantly, the present study quantified arterial blood sevoflurane concentrations rather than anesthetic partial pressure. Because the pharmacodynamic effects of VAs are governed primarily by partial pressure, the absence of direct partial-pressure measurements represents an important methodological limitation that constrains the clinical interpretation of our blood-concentration data. Although anesthetic gas transfer is governed by partial pressure gradients, measurement of VA blood concentrations using gas chromatography remains a well-established approach for pharmacokinetic investigations. In the present study, analysis of sevoflurane blood concentrations allowed quantitative comparison of anesthetic exposure across different temperature ranges and oxygenator conditions. Furthermore, several samples were excluded because of leakage during headspace sampling, which may have contributed to partial data attrition. Although careful handling procedures were applied, such losses can occur in volatile anesthetic measurements and represent a technical limitation of the sampling process rather than a systematic measurement error. Finally, although this study characterized the pharmacokinetic behavior of sevoflurane during hypothermic CPB, it did not evaluate clinical outcomes such as anesthetic depth, awareness, or postoperative organ injury. Future investigations integrating anesthetic partial pressure measurements, temperature dynamics, and clinical outcomes may provide further insight into anesthetic management during hypothermic cardiac surgery.

Conclusions

We demonstrated chronological changes in sevoflurane blood concentrations during mild and moderate hypothermic cardiac surgery and showed an inverse correlation between these concentrations and bladder temperature.
