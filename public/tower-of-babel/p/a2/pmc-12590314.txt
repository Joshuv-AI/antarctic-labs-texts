Using the Theoretical Domains Framework and Behavior Change Wheel Framework within the world of nutrition support
Authors: Sharon Carey, Sophie Hogan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAttempts at behavior change have been shown to be more effective when interventions are based on evidence linked to an implementation framework. The Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) and Behavior Change Wheel (BCW) provide a systematic way to map effective implementation strategies to address barriers and enablers to behavior change. This paper aims to outline the steps involved in designing and evaluating an intervention using the TDF and BCW, including (1) identifying barriers and enablers (TDF); (2) mapping barriers and enablers to the Capabilities, Opportunities, Motivation hub; and (3) identifying key interventions and specific strategies that are known to bring about behavior change. Real world gaps in the implementation of malnutrition screening and implementation of a preoperative prehabilitation clinic are used as examples to highlight the functionality of the framework. Given the TDF and BCW have been designed specifically to change clinician behavior, it is an ideal framework for addressing evidence‐practice gaps in nutrition support.Keywords: nutrition support practice, nutrition support teams, outcomes research/quality

HISTORY OF THE BEHAVIOR CHANGE WHEEL AND IMPLEMENTATION SCIENCE

Implementation science (IS) has existed since as far back as the 1940s, 1 and in the decades following, the concept expanded to include classic theories related to promoting evidence‐based clinical change. 2 , 3 Statistics during the last two decades have indicated that only 20% of research ever make it to implementation within a clinical setting. 4 For this reason there has been a revitalized interest in the use of IS, with the development of theoretical frameworks to aid development, implementation, and evaluation of change. 5 One such theoretical framework was designed by Miche et al. 6 in 2011, which encompasses the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) 7 and the Behavior Change Wheel (BCW). 6 Although these two frameworks can be used independently, together they provide a process for systematically mapping effective implementation strategies to address barriers and enablers to behavior change. 8

The TDF was developed initially as an aide to help identify and develop interventions aimed at changing behavior in a healthcare setting. 7 The development of the TDF merged multiple behavior theories, consensus processes, piloting, and validation to produce the final framework. The final TDF identified 14 key domains and 84 theoretical constructs, which allows researchers and/or clinicians to detect possible barriers and enablers in leveraging behavior change. 9 The TDF domains can be mapped to the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation‐Behavior (COM‐B) component of the BCW to guide types of interventions and strategies that are more likely to prompt behavior modification. 10

Nutrition support is an essential component in the clinical management of acute and chronic illness and includes the use of oral, enteral, and parenteral modalities. Nutrition support has the ability to reduce complications and improve immune function, response to treatments, quality of life, and survival. 11 , 12 Yet ensuring implementation of evidence‐based nutrition practice relies on agreement from the whole healthcare team, including physicians, nurses, dietitians, and other members of the multidisciplinary healthcare team. Stamp and Davis 13 demonstrated only 37% of dietitian recommendations were actioned within the hospital setting, often because of non–evidence‐based beliefs of physicians. These findings mean many patients are denied optimal nutrition care.

Malnutrition screening within the inpatient setting is an example of a practice recognized to have major benefits for patient care but that is not always implemented in hospitals. 14 International agreement supports the implementation of nutrition screening to identify those at risk of malnutrition to provide early interventions to prevent further decline and commence early nutrition support where needed. 11 Many cross‐sectional studies have shown that the practice of nutrition screening is not universal across healthcare setting in multiple countries. 15 , 16 Barriers have been well‐documented, including lack of resourcing, knowledge, ownership, and competing priorities. 16 , 17 A second example of an emerging evidence‐practice gap is the use of preoperative prehabilitation. Emerging evidence supports this multidisciplinary approach to physical, nutrition, and psychological optimization before surgery. 18 Benefits include improved functional capacity, improved surgical recovery, reduced complication rates, and improved healthcare costs. 19 , 20 , 21 Prehabilitation is now considered best evidence‐based care. 18 The nutrition component of prehabilitation in itself has strong positive clinical, patient‐reported, and economic evidence to support it becoming standard care, 22 particularly for high‐risk groups such as malnourished 23 and frail 24 populations. Despite this growing evidence, the majority of hospitals across developed countries have not implemented prehabilitation. 25 Studies exploring impediments to implementation of prehabilitation have shown multiple barriers, including resources, complex clinical pathways, declining medical condition of patients, and individual motivation. 26 , 27 , 28

The implementation of evidence‐based practice within the realm of nutrition support continues to be a struggle as the role of nutrition competes with surgical, medical, nursing, and economic priorities. Hence the development of the TDF and BCW, specifically designed to guide the design of implementation strategies and aimed at changing behavior in the healthcare setting can be of huge benefit. It provides a stepped process to (1) identify barriers and enablers (TDF); (2) map barriers and enablers to COM‐B components; and (3) identify key strategies that are known to bring about behavior change (Figure 1). 8

USING THE TDF AND BCW FOR IMPLEMENTING CHANGE

Identify barriers and enablers using the TDF

The first step in addressing an evidence‐practice gap is to identify key barriers and enablers to changing behavior. Although barriers and enablers can be explored using different methodologies, qualitative interviews with key stakeholders are an ideal way of doing this. Key stakeholders need to include all those people involved in clinical, administrative, and financial decision‐making within the topic area. Clinician stakeholder engagement often ranges from junior clinicians up to senior and consultant level staff including medical and surgical, nursing, and allied health professionals. Creating qualitative questions designed to explore the 14 domains of the TDF (Table 1) will ensure a wide scope of barriers and enablers are explored without bias or preconceived assumptions. Qualitative interviews need to ensure rigor through credibility, transferability, dependability and/or consistency, and neutrality. 29 This will ensure that findings are not biased from any overt or inherent beliefs the research team may have. Analysis of qualitative interviews will identify domain/s seen as barriers and also those that may be seen as enablers. (Tables 2 and 3).

Map domains to the COM‐B

Once key domains are identified using the TDF, these can be mapped to the COM‐B, to determine which strategy is required. Each component of the COM‐B has two possible components that can influence behavior change (Figure 2). Capabilities focus on the individual's ability to influence change through physical means (eg, skills) or psychological means (eg, beliefs or knowledge). 6 , 8 Opportunity focuses on factors external to the individual that may influence change such as social factors (eg, culture or social norms) or physical factors (eg, equipment or organizational structure). 6 , 8 Lastly, motivations focus on factors that may influence a person's decision to change behavior such as automatic (eg, incentives or feelings) or reflective (eg, understanding of consequences or professional identity) components. 6 , 8

EVIDENCE OF BCW AND TDF USE IN NUTRITION SUPPORT

When searching the nutrition literature using keywords and mesh terms related to BCW and TDF, such as “health knowledge, attitudes, practice,” motivation, qualitative research, and “attitude of health personnel,” it is clear there are minimal interventions that use the TDF and BCW frameworks, and those that do have a strong focus on community and population health. Studies conducted in these settings have focused on changing consumer behavior as opposed to clinician behavior (for example, identifying parental factors associated with increasing children's fruit and vegetable intake 31 or assessing how to best support elite athletes to meet their daily energy intake). 32 The barriers and enablers associated with these targeted behaviors are often well researched and understood. Therefore, the focus of these studies is more on the BCW to identify interventions. Within the clinical nutrition setting, the TDF and COM‐B have been used to identify interventions aimed at promoting healthy eating behaviors (for example, preventing gestational diabetes during pregnancy). 33

Use of the TDF and BCW within the acute hospital setting is minimal, yet studies that have used this theoretical framework have proven beneficial. One of the few studies that has used TDF and BCW investigated the barriers to optimizing early neonatal nutrition by conducting semistructured interviews with medical and nursing professionals and then linked the outcomes to the BCW to identify interventions. 34 This systematic approach resulted in a group of interventions for implementation embedded within an implementation framework. This highlights the importance of providing a consistent approach to the entire strategy of IS in the clinical setting for nutrition support. A step‐by‐step toolkit is essential to support time‐poor researchers to implement targeted and meaningful interventions that bring about positive change.

BENEFITS OF THE TDF AND BCW FRAMEWORK

The benefits of using the TDF and BCW are embedded in using a framework that has collated large amounts of studies to provide strategies that have the highest likelihood of bringing about behavior change in the healthcare setting 9 and, in particular, changing individual clinician behavior. As highlighted above, reducing the evidence‐practice gap in nutrition support is reliant on changing individual clinician knowledge, experiences, and practices, making the TDF and BCW an ideal toolkit in this clinical field.

TDF and BCW provides a well‐structured process for clinicians without extensive experience in IS to map out service delivery, quality improvement, or research to address evidence‐practice gaps in their own facility.

Most challenges in nutrition support often have multiple barriers to change. IS literature supports the use of interventions with multifaceted strategies, in which multifaceted strategies are more likely to affect behavior change compared with single strategies alone. 30 The TDF and BCW allow for the mapping of multiple strategies to address multiple barriers, with enough flexibility in strategy design to allow for the nuances of each service or hospital.

LIMITATIONS OF THE TDF AND BCW FRAMEWORK

For those unfamiliar with the TDF and BCW processes, getting started and progressing can seem overwhelming with the multiple layers of mapping required. It is also an extensive process to undertake all steps to thoroughly explore barriers and enablers and plan what is likely a multifaceted intervention. Significant time needs to be allocated for these processes. It is likely that some people will be tempted to skip steps or make assumptions, which can lead to a less effective outcome. Given the complexity of implementing behavior change to address evidence‐practice gaps in healthcare, the time required to thoroughly and accurately plan an IS intervention should be viewed positively.

When using the TDF, it is very easy to focus on barriers to change. A limitation is the lack of emphasis on possible enablers and the positive impact they may have. A focus on enablers can provide motivation for clinicians to change just as much as addressing barriers. A simple example of this is the use of audit and feedback. Regular feedback on performance, especially when compared with peers, is one of the most powerful strategies to motivate behavior change in surgeons. 35 , 36

Although the design of this framework to address individual clinician behavior can be seen as a benefit, it does mean that this framework may not take into account larger societal and financial barriers. Equally, it may not be as effective when looking at wider societal issues, such as increasing incidence of poor dental health or rising prevalence of obesity.

TDF and BCW are not explicit in providing guidance on evaluation of the designed intervention and strategies. The project team needs to ensure that there is a clear process for the evaluation of fidelity, reach, and effectiveness of each strategy to determine which strategies are beneficial and which are not.

EXAMPLE 1: ADDRESSING THE EVIDENCE‐PRACTICE GAP IN INPATIENT MALNUTRITION SCREENING

For a team that wishes to address gaps in inpatient malnutrition screening within their service or hospital using the TDF and BCW framework, the first step is to interview key stakeholders. Key stakeholders may include (but are not exclusive to) junior and senior clinical staff, ward dietitians, ward administrative staff, nursing, medical and dietetics managers and executives, as well as consumers. Interviews need to incorporate questions related to the 14 TDF domains. For example, a question aimed to explore issues around role delineation may ask, “what is your understanding about who is currently responsible for malnutrition screening in your service?” Once analyzed, key domains will emerge to indicate barriers to behavior change. Using the example of role delineation, if key stakeholders all identify someone else as responsible for malnutrition screening, with no one identifying this as their responsibility, then a key barrier is related to professional role and identity within the TDF.

Key domains identified as barriers are then mapped to the COM‐B to determine what type of behavior change is required. Role delineation is mapped to motivation (reflective) in the COM‐B. The motivation (reflective) component of the COM‐B is then further mapped to the best type of intervention to bring about behavior change, indicating that an intervention focused on education, persuasion, incentivization, or coercion is most effective. Further mapping shows possible examples of strategies could be (1) a guideline or regulation stating roles and responsibilities related to malnutrition screening, (2) role modelling, or (3) incentives for increased malnutrition screening rates.

EXAMPLE 2: ADDRESSING THE EVIDENCE‐PRACTICE GAP IN PROVIDING A PREOPERATIVE PREHABILITATION OUTPATIENT NUTRITION CLINIC

To address the evidence‐practice gap in the outpatient setting using the TDF and BCW, identification of key stakeholders is required. This needs to include clinicians and administrative and executive staff as well as consumers. Interviewing a range of stakeholders using questions mapped to the 14 domains of the TDF will identify key barriers and enablers. In this example, we may ask questions such as, “Do you feel current staffing for prehabilitation is adequate to meet the needs of the patient group?,” “Do you feel there is adequate understanding within the service and by individual patients about the benefits of prehabilitation,” and “How willing would you be to engage in a prehabilitation program?” to assess domains related to environmental context, knowledge, and optimism, respectively. In this example, resourcing and patient gaps in understanding of prehabilitation benefits may be found to be barriers, but patient willingness to engage may emerge as an enabler. These key domains are then mapped to the COM‐B to determine what type of behavior change is required. Environmental context is mapped to the opportunity (physical) component, knowledge is mapped to capabilities (psychological), and optimism is mapped to the motivation (reflective) component.

These components of the COM‐B are then used to determine the best type of strategy to bring about behavior change. To address opportunity (physical) factors, interventions should include restriction, environmental restructuring, or enablement; to address capability (psychological) factors, interventions should include education, training, or enablement; and to use motivation (reflective) as an enabler, the intervention should include education, persuasion, incentivization, or coercion. Hence, a multifaceted intervention could include (1) business case to executive leadership to pilot increased resourcing for prehabilitation, with fiscal monitoring; (2) patient‐facing education sessions on benefits of prehabilitation before enrollment into the program; and (iii) patient advocacy through hospital consumer engagement committees that have direct access to executive leadership to guide decision‐making.

CONCLUSION

Implementation of research into practice within health continues to be a slow process. There are many IS frameworks available to guide interventions, but the TDF and BCW are specifically designed to change individual behavior within the healthcare setting. Hence, TDF and BCW represent an ideal framework for novice and experienced clinicians to use. The stepped approach to identifying barriers and mapping interventions and strategies to inform service changes, quality improvement, or IS research ensures that strategies are not random. Interventions and strategies within the BCW have been selected based on extensive review of the literature to ensure they are more likely to address the identified barrier and promote behavior change. Simple examples used in this paper demonstrate the functionality of the TDF and BCW in addressing the common nutrition support evidence‐practice gaps of malnutrition screening and implementation of preoperative prehabilitation clinic.
