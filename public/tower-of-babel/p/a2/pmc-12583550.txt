Intensive post-war migrations and mtDNA variation based on Poland case
Authors: Barbara Wasowicz, Grzegorz Chrobak, Marianna Soroka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn Poland, the population of Western Pomerania stands out due to the near-total displacement of its inhabitants following World War II. The German population was displaced (1.6 million people), and these lands were subsequently resettled by Poles arriving from various regions of Poland and Europe. Currently, the population of the West Pomeranian Voivodeship is 1.63 million, with 298 unrelated individuals studied. The research aimed to assess the variability of the entire mitochondrial control region (16024..16569, 1..576 bp in rCRS) among the inhabitants of the West Pomeranian Voivodeship and to compare the data with those from European and Polish populations. The study revealed a high level of genetic diversity in mtDNA among the inhabitants of the West Pomeranian Voivodeship, making it one of the most diverse populations in Poland and Europe. A total of 243 haplotypes were identified, with the most common haplogroups being HV, U, T, J, and R0. Within this population, the probability of random matching was 1:145. The molecular parameters obtained indicate a high degree of heterogeneity in the population of the West Pomeranian Voivodeship compared to the neighbouring German population from Fore Pomerania.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22219-4.Keywords: mtDNA, Genetic variation, Post-war migration, Forensic genetics, EMPOP databaseSubject terms: Genetics, Molecular biology

Introduction

Intensive migrations or forced resettlement can significantly increase or decrease the diversity of the populations studied, depending on whether we examine the source or target population. An increase in diversity through the exchange of individuals from various regions or through immigration presents statistical challenges in forensic genetics, affecting the interpretation of genetic evidence. While a high level of diversity enhances the ability to distinguish individuals, it also indicates a larger and more significant number of distinct mtDNA lineages within a population, which requires accurate identification and careful monitoring of their frequencies. This is crucial for estimating the power of discrimination (PD) in court cases.

After the end of World War II, Poland encountered radical territorial changes resulting from the Yalta and Potsdam Conferences, both held in 1945. The country’s borders were shifted westward, resulting in the loss of its eastern territories. In return, Poland received lands in the west and north that had previously belonged to Germany. These changes resulted in the mass migration westward of millions of displaced Poles and Germans1. In the east, Poles were forced to move from the territories annexed by the former Soviet Union and resettled to the so-called recovered territories, i.e., areas that had previously been inhabited by the German population (Fig. 1). The newly settled areas were also inhabited by repatriates from other parts of Europe, including Poles from France and Romania, as well as national minorities such as Ukrainians, Lemkos and Boykos, who were also resettled (as a part of the “Vistula” operation)1.

One of the largest migration waves in Europe in the 20th century must have had a lasting impact on the genetic structure of Poland’s population. Over the past 80 years, this country has experienced the migration of over 11 million individuals of Polish and non-Polish ancestry, either into or out of the country2. This phenomenon provides an essential context for research on the contemporary genetic diversity of the Polish population, which has not been taken into account in research so far, especially for court purposes. In the current mtDNA studies conducted in forensic sciences, there has been no comprehensive research on a highly variable population, such as the West Pomeranian Voivodeship in Poland. The areas of the so-called recovered territories, which include the aforementioned Voivodeship, have become a melting pot, where various population groups of different geographical and cultural origins have mixed. This mass resettlement made it a unique object of genetic research.

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is an excellent tool for tracing ancient human migration due to its unique characteristics, including uniparental maternal inheritance and the absence of recombination. Therefore, mtDNA is passed down almost unchanged from generation to generation, and analysing it helps reconstruct lineages and assess historical gene flow. However, its small size (ca. 16.5 kb) and relatively low mutation rate make it less effective for detailed studies of modern migration patterns3. The mitochondrial genome is most effective when combined with nuclear markers and in contexts involving the study of broad ancestry patterns and admixture. However, mtDNA can also be used to infer historical demographic processes, the effects of which are reflected in this genome’s variability4. Most mtDNA population studies focus on ancient human migrations, medical applications, or assessing current diversity levels, rarely considering post-war migrations and their implications for forensic genetics, such as the creation of databases. The topic of post-war migrations has been exclusively addressed in Y-chromosome analyses. One of the few remarkable phenomena in Y chromosome diversity in Europe is the Polish population, which demonstrates the geographical homogeneity of Y chromosome lineages despite the relatively large geographical area occupied by the Polish state5. Furthermore, a clear genetic boundary has been identified between the paternal lineages of Poland and Germany, which closely corresponds to the political border between the two countries. It has been suggested that the widespread relocation of people after World War II, involving millions of Poles and Germans, clearly explains the observed phenomenon5.

Accurate interpretation of mtDNA results for forensic purposes necessitates robust population databases that capture the full spectrum of mtDNA diversity in the target population. Without comprehensive databases, the rarity of a specific mtDNA haplotype or haplogroup may be either overestimated or underestimated, potentially leading to incorrect conclusions6.

Current analyses of the mtDNA of the Polish population do not consider historical events or demographic changes, but only briefly mention them7. Others have been conducted solely within the context of the origin of Slavic groups4. The main goal of this paper is to detail the characteristics of the population established post-war in the West Pomeranian Voivodeship, as well as to compare the obtained results with those from other European populations, including various regions of Poland.

Results

Among the 298 sequences obtained, 199 polymorphic sites were identified, with most mutations occurring within the HVSI region (108 mutations). The most frequent haplotype among the 258 identified in the studied population of West Pomerania, with mutations A263G, 315.1 C, and T16519C, was observed in 12 individuals, and these mutations define haplogroup R0. The next most frequent haplotype, which was assigned to haplogroup H1c, occurred in 5 individuals, while the subsequent haplotype assigned to haplogroup HV0 was found in 4 individuals. The remaining 18 haplotypes were repeated less frequently, appearing in up to 4 individuals among the 41 samples. All haplotypes are listed in Supplementary file 1.

In the analysis of point and length heteroplasmies, only those present on both mtDNA strands were included. Out of 298 sequences obtained, seven sequences (2.3%) displayed 7-point heteroplasmies, all of which were transitions (Table 1). All heteroplasmies were observed only in single samples. Length heteroplasmy occurred within HVSII, between positions 303 and 315, which, combined with a transition at position 310 (C/T), made it difficult to determine the exact number of cytosine bases at this site; therefore, they were not included in the analyses of genetic diversity. However, length heteroplasmies in the 16183 C stretch were not observed among the 298 sequences analysed.

Using the Haplogrep software (Haplogrep 3 3.2.1 https://haplogrep.i-med.ac.at/) and based on the mtDNA phylogenetic tree (Phylotree build 17), the samples were assigned to 55 different haplogroups (Table 2). The most common haplogroup was U5 (10.1%), followed by haplogroups H1 (9.1%), J1 (8.7%), T2 (8.1%), and HV* (7.7%). Together, they make up 43.7% of the entire examined population of the West Pomeranian Voivodeship. Twenty-one haplogroups were observed in single individuals, representing 7% of the population with unique haplogroups and haplotypes (Table 2). At a broader haplogroup level (referred to here as macrohaplogroups), the most common was HV (37.9%), followed by U (23.5%), T (12.1%), J (9.4%), and R0 (7.7%) (Table 2). Notably, the mtDNA haplogroup composition of the West Pomeranian population sample set was similar to that of other European populations at this level of resolution.

Interpopulation analyses

The analysis of basic genetic parameters based on the entire mtDNA control region indicates that the number of identified haplotypes correlates with the number of examined samples from a given population (Table 3). The highest number of haplotypes (Nh = 421) was observed in the population of the Netherlands, followed by Sweden (362) and Finland (289), which are the largest sets of populations. The fewest haplotypes (33–75) were found in populations with the smallest number of tested individuals (39–91), specifically in those from Ukraine and Cyprus, but not in Italy (40). Despite the larger sample size (243), the number of identified haplotypes in Italians was lower than in Cyprus, which had 152 fewer individuals analysed. In the West Pomeranian population and Northern Greece, 243 haplotypes were identified, despite the difference in sample size (Table 3). In West Pomerania, 97 out of 243 haplotypes (40.0%) are shared with at least one other population, while 60.0% are private to this subpopulation. In Kuyavian–Pomeranian, 56.0% of haplotypes are shared and 44.0% are private. Thus, the two Polish subpopulations differ in the balance of shared versus private haplotypes: West Pomerania shows a comparatively higher private component, whereas Kuyavian–Pomeranian has more shared haplotypes. Among the 25 European populations in our dataset, the proportion of shared haplotypes ranges from 7.9% to 63.6% (minimum Estonia 7.9% and maximum Ukraine 63.6%). Complete data for these 25 European populations are provided in Supplementary file 2.

Among all the analysed populations, the highest values for the number of polymorphic sites were recorded in the largest populations, like the Netherlands (228), Sweden (219), and Northern Greece (203), followed by West Pomerania (199), even though Northern Greece is not the largest population in the dataset (Table 3).

The highest nucleotide diversity (π) values were found in the populations of Ireland, Cyprus, and Serbia, although these are not the most populous groups (Table 3). The next populations with similarly high π values include two Polish populations from Kuyavian–Pomeranian and West Pomerania Voivodeships, as well as a Greek population. The lowest π values were observed in Italy and the Basques (Table 3).

The highest haplotype diversity (Hd) values were observed in the Polish populations from Kuyavian–Pomeranian Voivodeship, Serbia, and Polish West Pomerania, which are not the largest populations in the dataset. Notably, the Kuyavian-Pomeranian Voivodeship population, despite having the fewest tested individuals among the three, achieved the highest diversity value.

The comparison of pairwise FST distances and their P-values was conducted across all European populations, with significance assessed using 10,000 permutations (Supplementary file 3). The resulting significance matrix is presented as a heatmap (Fig. 2). Statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) were identified in 450 out of 575 possible cases (78.3%). Significant genetic differences were found for the entire West Pomeranian Voivodeship when compared to 15 European populations, but the highest FST value was observed in comparisons with Estonia, Italy, and Ireland.

Matrix of significant FST P-values for the control region dataset.

The greatest number of statistically significant differences (among 25 populations) was observed between Basques, Cyprus, Estonia, Greece, Ireland, and Italy. The fewest significant differences were recorded for Ukraine (6), the German city of Munich (9), Ulm (12), and the Polish population of Kuyavian–Pomeranian Voivodeship (12). The FST-based distance matrix shows that the populations of Estonia, Italy, and Ireland differ significantly from the other groups, even though they do not have the highest levels of intrapopulation genetic diversity, as measured by haplotype diversity (Hd), nucleotide diversity (π), number of haplotypes (Nh), and the number of polymorphic sites (S).

Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to visualise the potential grouping of the 25 populations examined in this study based on the FST matrix (Fig. 3). A graphical distribution of dependencies was produced, with the first principal component (PC1) explaining 89.83% of the variability, while the second principal component (PC2) accounts for 7.59%. Together, both components account for 97.42% of the total variability. This indicates that the two-dimensional PCA plot provides a highly accurate representation of the overall genetic structure. The PCA reveals a clear separation of certain populations from the main cluster. Notably, Estonia and Italy emerge as distinct outliers along PC1 and PC2, respectively. Most of the populations, such as Austria, Bulgaria, the Czech Republic, Finland, the German city of Munich, the Netherlands, North Macedonia, Northern Greece, West Pomerania, Romania, Russia, Slovakia, and Sweden, form a very tight cluster near the origin of the plot, indicating low genetic distances among them in terms of mtDNA variation. Interestingly, Ireland is positioned apart from the core cluster along the negative PC1 axis, possibly reflecting subtle western maternal influences.

An Analysis of Molecular Variance (AMOVA) was performed to evaluate significant variation in mtDNA distributions among European populations. A variance of 2.91% was observed across the 25 populations, while 97.09% can be attributed to differences within populations (Table 4). According to the Wright scale, the FST fixation index was 0.02907, indicating low genetic differentiation between subpopulations. Additionally, the probability value P = 0.00000 ± 0.00000 suggests that the difference in mtDNA distribution, although small, is statistically significant.

The separation of mutations in HVSI and HVSII from the entire control region resulted in decreased values of all examined genetic parameters for the West Pomeranian Voivodeship population. An analysis of the basic molecular parameters of HVSI and HVSII sequences indicates that the number of identified haplotypes increases with a larger number of samples examined from a specific population (Table 5). The highest number of haplotypes (277) was found in the Kuyavian–Pomeranian population, followed by the entire West Pomeranian Voivodeship population (229) and German Fore Pomerania (194). The fewest haplotypes (fewer than 100) were identified in populations with the smallest number of examined individuals (73 and 87), specifically the Suwałki region and Upper Silesia.

Discussion

Intra-population analysis of sequences from the entire mtDNA control region revealed high genetic diversity within the population of the West Pomeranian Voivodeship, as evident in various genetic parameters (Table 3). However, these values are typical for Central European populations. Of the 243 identified haplotypes, only 97 (approximately 44.0%) were found in 24 other European populations, placing this population slightly below the median but still within the normal European range (Supplementary file 2).

In the case of the separated subpopulations of the West Pomeranian Voivodeship, despite having a larger number of rural representations, the same number of haplotypes was observed (Tables 3 and 5). Greater migration to cities contributed to increased diversity in the urban population. Consequently, the value of the calculated random match parameter (RMP) is lower for the urban subpopulation compared to the rural one (Tables 3 and 5). A lower RMP indicates a greater number of unique DNA profiles within a population, which increases confidence that a specific DNA sample originates from a particular individual. Compared to other European populations, the entire West Pomerania region achieved the second-lowest value of this parameter in the dataset for the whole control region. This confirms a higher number of unique DNA profiles within the population. The discriminatory power of the studied population was greater when analysing the entire mtDNA control region compared to just the HVSI and HVSII regions, which is significant for forensic purposes. The probability of a random match between two unrelated individuals in this population for the CR was 1:145, whereas for HVSI and HVSII, it was 1:79. In the case of HVSI and HVSII, West Pomerania exhibits the highest discrimination power compared to other Polish populations, with only German Fore Pomerania reaching a higher value in the entire dataset.

The frequencies of East Asian lineages in Europe are relatively low and may, according to some sources, originate from migrations that occurred during the Neolithic or medieval periods27,33. In our studies, we identified nine African and Asian macrohaplogroups, four of which (D, F, L, and Z) were not present in the analysis of this province as part of the 2019 Poland study7. Haplotypes from macrohaplogroup L and D are generally found at low frequencies in most European populations, including Slavic groups27.

Nucleotide diversity (π) indicates the variety of nucleotide sequences in a population. Unlike haplotype diversity (Hd), π is based on differences in individual nucleotides rather than the presence of different combinations of nucleotide sequences, as seen in haplotypes. The number of samples tested influences both mtDNA nucleotide diversity and haplotype diversity. Theoretically, a larger sample size should increase the number of genetic differences, potentially leading to an increase in the π and Hd indices. However, this effect, like haplotype diversity, can be modified by various historical, genetic, and random population processes. Analysing genetic indices reveals that the neighbouring to West Pomerania inhabitants of German Fore Pomerania, exhibit the lowest level of nucleotide diversity among all compared populations, despite the analysis involving 300 individuals, comparable to the number of West Pomeranian Voivodeship or Kashubians (Table 5). Such significantly different results from the neighbouring population confirm the tumultuous demographic history in western Poland. This highlights that the population is more heterogeneous compared to the population from Fore Pomerania. Across the entire mtDNA control region, the West Pomerania Voivodeship population generally shows moderate values for molecular parameters, with haplotype diversity being an exception, ranking third highest. Further subdivision of the tested population did not result in a significant increase in molecular variation. Notably, only the Kuyavian-Pomeranian Voivodeship displays a greater number of haplotypes and polymorphic sites (Table 3). Slightly higher values for the studied population were observed for the HVSI and HVSII regions, but only in comparison to other Polish populations (Table 5).

Based on the haplotype diversity values for the HVSI and HVSII datasets, it can be concluded that the population of the West Pomerania Voivodeship, together with German Fore Pomerania, represents the most variable populations. When analysing the entire control region, the West Pomeranian population — among groups of comparable sample sizes (242–319 individuals) — exhibits one of the highest haplotype diversity (Hd) values, ranking just after the Kuyavian–Pomeranian and Serbian populations (Table 3). The highest Hd value among all 25 populations was observed in the Kuyavian-Pomeranian Voivodeship. However, this is not confirmed by the comparison limited to the HVSI and HVSII regions.

The highest nucleotide diversity (π) was observed in populations from Ireland, Cyprus, and Serbia, despite these not being among the largest samples — particularly Cyprus, which, after Ukraine, has the smallest sample size in the dataset (Table 3). Along with the population from German Mecklenburg, West Pomerania also exhibits one of the highest values of this parameter in the HVSI and HVSII datasets. Elevated nucleotide diversity in these populations may indicate a high level of maternal lineage heterogeneity, potentially resulting from historic migration events. For instance, Cyprus’s central position in the eastern Mediterranean might have facilitated continuous gene flow from multiple directions over time.

The initial results of mtDNA studies on the entire Polish population were published in 2019 by Jarczak et al., and the only comparative data available for West Pomerania come from this article7. This study included 269 participants from West Pomerania and 5,852 individuals from all voivodeships in Poland, but mtDNA variability was assessed only at the intra-population (national) level. A comparison of the population in the West Pomeranian Voivodeship from two independent studies7 and the studies presented here) indicates that, despite a very similar number of individuals examined, there are differences in the frequencies of individual mtDNA lineages. The absence of assignment of samples exclusively to the macrohaplogroups U and I in our studies, along with significantly higher frequencies of lineages H and T in the 2019 studies, may suggest that the microarray method used by Jarczak’s team is less accurate than sequencing the entire mtDNA control region for identifying haplogroups and their lineages. A detailed comparison of Eurasian haplogroup frequencies allowed us to identify 11 haplogroups in this study that were not recorded in the West Pomeranian Voivodeship, as noted by Jarczak’s team. These haplogroups include H103, H14, H27, H28, H36, H39, H57, HV1, HV6, HV9, and W5. Conversely, the 2019 study identified eight haplogroups that were absent in our studies7.

The mtDNA Population Database (EMPOP), created by the European Group on DNA Profiling (EDNAP), is the result of international collaboration among mtDNA laboratories and the DNA laboratory at the Institute of Forensic Medicine (GMI) in Innsbruck, Austria. This database compiles mitochondrial DNA population data used in routine forensic work and assists in verifying the accuracy of sequences, reducing errors that may arise from potential sample mix-ups or misinterpretation of data. For these studies, 300 sequences of the entire control region were submitted to the database; 298 of these achieved full confidence and were included in the EMPOP database, assigned the number EMP00884. Unfortunately, this essential resource, the EMPOP Database, has not been updated from 2019 to 2025 (R14). This update is critical for the current phylogenetic tree concerning mtDNA, which includes identifying haplogroups and estimating their frequencies within populations.

The analysis of human mitochondrial genome variability still faces certain challenges. These include difficulties in estimating identified haplogroups, especially subhaplogroups. Most publicly available data on complete mitochondrial genomes and the discovery of new haplogroups originate from commercial sources. Although it may appear that the topic of human population diversity regarding mtDNA is well documented, it remains difficult to find studies offering a comprehensive comparative analysis of all European populations examined.

Previous studies have shown that the distribution of mtDNA haplogroups is quite similar among European populations; however, some differences still exist between them34–36. Unfortunately, not all populations are adequately represented; some countries lack data, which can hinder the identification or assignment of mtDNA to a specific geographic region or population. Such data are crucial for archaeogenetic studies and commercial inquiries into biogeographic origins. While identifying mtDNA haplogroups has limited usefulness for forensic purposes, it remains intriguing in archaeology and research on our species’ past37. However, studies published in recent decades seldom focus on parameters that measure levels of differentiation and rarely consider the historical context of the populations under study8,12. Typically, researchers employ a division into geographical regions, which does not necessarily align with past migration processes24. In the articles published to date, the most frequently identified haplogroups are highlighted, yet they are the least informative regarding population variability. The most studied population in Europe, within the control region of mtDNA, from the Netherlands, consists solely of 680 men, with these studies conducted to establish a national forensic mtDNA reference database23. As a result, we end up with unrealistic representations of individual countries, especially those with a high percentage of immigrants, like France and Sweden. Even a large country like Germany lacks comprehensive studies, considering its division into federal states, geographic regions, and national minorities, such as the Turkish and Polish populations.

Currently, there are no studies in the literature that analyse the entire population of Poland while considering the country’s history, including the partitions and mass migrations following World War II. The only research addressing the complete Polish population involves mitochondrial DNA analysis of currently separated voivodeships and counties based on samples collected for medical purposes7. There is a significant need to ground mitochondrial genome studies and those of the male Y chromosome in autosomal data, especially in a historical and cultural context38. Only a comprehensive analysis of the population’s genetic structure, based on diverse genetic markers and informed by historical knowledge, will yield a reliable result and reveal demographic or random phenomena that occurred in the past.

Materials and methods

Sample collections and ethics statement

Buccal swabs were collected from 298 unrelated individuals (170 women and 128 men) across 101 urban and rural communities and three cities with county rights in the West Pomeranian Voivodeship, all of whom provided informed consent (Fig. 6). All samples were collected anonymously; the only available information was the individuals’ places of residence. The Bioethical Commission at the District Medical Chamber in Szczecin, Poland, approved the study (Approval No. 14/KB/VI/2017). The number of individuals enrolled from each community was determined by the ratio of residents in that specific community to the total number of residents in the province. Communities with very few residents, where this ratio was less than 0.5, were excluded from the study, resulting in the identification of 10 such communities out of a total of 114 communities and cities. However, when this ratio ranged from 0.5 to 1, a sample was collected from only one person.

Map of Poland and surrounding German regions. Dark blue areas show voivodeships (in Poland) or federal states (in Germany) where entire populations were sampled. Stars indicate locations where only a specific subregion or cultural area within a voivodeship was included (e.g., Gdańsk, Kashubia, Suwałki, Podhale, Upper Silesia). In the West Pomeranian Voivodeship, each county from which samples were taken is outlined in yellow.

To test the hypothesis of higher levels of migration within urban areas, the population of the West Pomeranian Voivodeship was divided into two subpopulations. The first includes residents of cities with county rights as well as urban communes. Due to significant migration from county cities to neighbouring communes, this group also encompasses those communes that have experienced a considerable population increase over the last 25 years (approximately one generation). The second subpopulation consists of all other samples from residents of rural communes, excluding those associated with the agglomeration of county cities, specifically Szczecin and Koszalin.

DNA isolation, D-loop amplification, and sequencing

The total DNA was extracted from the swabs using the column method according to the procedure recommended by the manufacturer (NucleoSpin Tissue, Macherey Nagel, Germany). The entire control region (CR, 16024–576 bp) of the mitochondrial genome was amplified and sequenced based on the EMPOP database, which has been a forensic database since 200639,40. PCR amplification and sequencing (using the ABI Prism 3130XL Analyser and BigDye Terminator v3.1) were also carried according to EMPOP requirements (Table 7). The EMPOP database confirmed the quality of the obtained sequences.

Data analysis and haplogroup assignment

The obtained DNA sequences were aligned with the reference sequence (rCRS)41 and evaluated using Sequencher v5.5.6 software (Gene Codes Corporation, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). The quality of each sequence was evaluated independently by two researchers, and the nomenclature was conferred according to the guidelines of the EMPOP database42. Haplogroups were assigned using Haplogrep software43 and, for some samples, manually using PhyloTree Build 1744, and verified in the EMPOP Database Search: SAM245.

Statistical analysis

Arlequin software v. 3.5.2.246 was employed to determine the level of diversity within a studied population by analysing molecular diversity indices, haplotype frequencies, molecular variance (AMOVA), and FST values. Random Match Probability (RMP) was also calculated with Arlequin software. The population of West Pomerania was compared to 24 European populations for which complete mtDNA control region data are available in EMPOP. To supplement this dataset, additional control region sequences were extracted from complete mitochondrial genomes available in peer-reviewed population genetics studies, as well as from GenBank entries with a commercial source (Family Tree DNA). The populations, their studied individuals, and references are listed in Table 3.

A PCA plot was constructed based on the FST matrix to visualise the genetic distances among the 25 European populations using the scikit-learn library47. The graphical visualisation was performed using Tableau software (Tableau Desktop v2025.2 https://www.tableau.com/support/releases/desktop/2025.2). Moreover, data on basic molecular indices were compiled to compare the variability of comprehensive populations. The values of these parameters were calculated using Arlequin software46.

A detailed analysis was conducted, adjusting the data related to the studied population by extracting mutations located exclusively within HVSI and HVSII with the same range used in available published data. The results obtained from these two regions were compared with previously published data on Polish populations, which included information solely from these two mtDNA fragments. The populations, individuals studied, and references are listed in Table 4. Additionally, the populations from northeastern Germany (300 and 213 individuals), adjacent to the West Pomeranian Voivodeship, were used for comparative analyses31,32. FST pairwise, AMOVA, and PCA analyses were performed to compare these populations. The Random Match Probability (RMP) was also calculated for the extracted HVSI and HVSII fragments.

The Python program (v. 3.13) was used to identify shared haplotypes, and the results are available in Supplementary file 248.

For the analyses of genetic parameters and genetic distances, unstable mutations at positions 309, 315, 515, 524, 574, 16,182, 16,183, and 16,193 were excluded due to their high variability and potential to bias the results.
