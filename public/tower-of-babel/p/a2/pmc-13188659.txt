Influence of memory functioning on quality of life in stroke patients: the moderating role of social support
Authors: Robertson Mensah-Diawuo, Mark Owusu Amponsah, Matthew Tobechukwu Nwanamulu, Bismark Asare
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke survivors commonly experience memory impairments that negatively affect daily functioning and overall quality of life (QoL). Although social support is often considered beneficial in stroke recovery, its role in influencing cognitive outcomes and QoL remains inconclusive. This study examined the influence of memory functioning on QoL among stroke patients and investigated whether perceived social support moderates this relationship in the Cape Coast Metropolis of Ghana. The study also explored the effects of selected sociodemographic characteristics on memory functioning and QoL.MethodsA quantitative correlational design was employed. Using simple random sampling, 132 stroke patients were selected from a population of 189 at Cape Coast Teaching Hospital. Standardized instruments assessed memory functioning, stroke-specific quality of life (SS-QoL), and perceived social support. Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations) determined levels of the main variables, while frequencies and percentages summarized sociodemographic characteristics. Pearson correlation analysis examined associations among variables. Simple and multiple linear regression analyses tested predictive relationships. Moderation analysis was conducted using Hayes’ PROCESS macro. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) and between-subject analyses assessed the effects of age, sex, education, and marital status on memory functioning and QoL.ResultsParticipants demonstrated generally high levels of memory functioning and perceived social support, while overall SS-QoL was moderate, with domain scores ranging from low to moderate levels. Memory functioning showed a moderate positive correlation with QoL and significantly predicted QoL (β = 0.523, p = .021). Social support was not significantly correlated with QoL, did not predict QoL (β = 0.331, p = .150), and did not moderate the relationship between memory functioning and QoL (interaction term p = .15). Sociodemographic analyses revealed no significant differences in memory functioning or QoL across sex, education, or marital status. However, age demonstrated a near-significant trend with a moderate effect size for memory functioning and QoL, suggesting possible age-related variation.ConclusionMemory functioning is a significant determinant of quality of life among stroke survivors in the Cape Coast Metropolis, whereas perceived social support did not demonstrate direct or moderating effects in this sample. The findings underscore the importance of incorporating cognitive assessment and memory-focused rehabilitation into post-stroke care, with particular attention to age-sensitive interventions to improve long-term quality of life outcomes.Keywords: Memory Functioning, Quality of Life, Social Support, Stroke

Introduction

Stroke remains a major complex condition that affects not only the patients, but also families at various levels, physically, psychologically, socially, and economically [1], . Across the world, stroke still ranks as the second cause of death after cardiac diseases and the third leading cause of disability [2]. According to the American Heart Association [3], a stroke is a sudden neurological disorder impeding the arteries supplying the brain due to either a blockage (ischemic stroke) or a rupture (hemorrhagic stroke) of a blood vessel resulting in brain cells death by the lack of blood and oxygen supply [4]. World Health Organization [5] defines stroke as the sudden onset of a focal neurological deficit that lasts for more than 24 h or results in death due to no other identifiable cause, while a transient ischemic attack (TIA) lasts less than 24 h.

Recent global figures show that stroke is still one of the main causes of death and long-term disabilities worldwide. The data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023 indicate that every year more than 12 million people worldwide suffer a stroke, and around 6.6 million dies from it [4]. Stroke is among the top killers worldwide, and it is a significant cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), especially in low- and middle-income countries.

The stroke burden in sub-Saharan Africa has increased drastically over the last 30 years, and most of the stroke deaths worldwide are now in low- and middle-income settings [4]. Population growth, population ageing, and the increasing occurrence of vascular risk factors, especially hypertension, have led to higher stroke incidence and mortality in the whole region.

Stroke is still one of the main causes of death and disability among adults in Ghana. The national health facility data indicate that confirmed stroke cases rose from 10, 732 in 2016 to 23, 009 in 2021, which is more than a two-fold increase within six years [8]. This increasing trend was seen in various regions and age groups. Previously, community-based and hospital-based research studies in Ghana have consistently documented stroke as a significant cause of death and long-term neurological disability (Sarfo et al., 2019). The latest figures highlight the increasing public health problem of stroke in Ghana and the necessity of timely implementation of effective preventive measures and provision of comprehensive rehabilitation services.

Even though the advances in hospital care have brought about the reduction of mortality rates, most of the survivors are faced with cognitive, functional, and emotional problems that are hard to ignore, thus making quality of life (QoL) one of the main goals in the post-stroke recovery process [6]. From its definition, the QoL of an individual is their subjective evaluation of their health status, their mental state, their social ties, and their degree of autonomy in the framework of their environment. It has been shown that stroke survivors who suffer from depressed quality of life have lower functional levels, more depend on caregivers, and use more healthcare services [7]. This shows the utmost importance of investigating the long-term adaptation factors rather than survival ones.

The consequence of stroke is far more devastating in the less-privileged economies. Sub-Saharan Africa has witnessed a huge epidemic of non-communicable diseases among which stroke has been identified as the main neurological disorder [7]. The prevalence of stroke in Ghana is estimated to be about 8% and the rate of new cases is still climbing among clinical populations [8]. Neglect of post-stroke psychological, cognitive, and social management, which largely determines the patient’s long-term wellness is evident, since the dominant approach to handling stroke aftermath is still focused on physiological stabilization.

One of the most significant, yet oftentimes ignored, consequences of stroke is the cognitive dysfunction, particularly the memory impairment. Since memory functioning is a basic constituent of daily living, people with memory problems may have difficulty with medication adherence, problem-solving, communication, and independence in routine activities [9, 10]. The vascular lesion as a result of stroke can damage the neural circuits required for encoding, storing, and retrieval of information and thus lead to functional dependence. The data gathered from the studies prove that, of all the stroke survivors, one-third experience a noticeable decline in their memory during the first year after the stroke which has a negative impact on their recovery paths as well as overall quality of life [10]. The studies done in the African continent also provide evidence that stroke survivors with memory impairments can expect to have compromised quality of life and additionally, the research conducted in Ghana shows that memory deficits correlate with lower life satisfaction and problems encountered in social and work reintegration [11].

That said, stroke recovery varies from one person to another as it also depends on sociodemographic variables that partly condition the outcomes. Age, sex, level of education, and marital status can all affect cognitive recovery and quality of life through factors such as cognitive reserve, resource availability, and provision of emotional support. Aging individuals are assumed to have slower neurological recovery due to reduced neural plasticity, on the other hand, higher educational attainment has been related to stronger cognitive reserve that may shield aging persons from post-stroke cognitive deterioration [12]. Likewise, being married provides the ground for caregiving support while female or male experiences in rehabilitation and psychosocial adjustment may differ quite significantly. It is, therefore, crucial to identify the influence of such sociodemographic variables on memory functioning and quality of life so as to spot the most vulnerable groups and to provide the needed rehabilitation therapies to the less fortunate.

Social support, apart from the individual factors, is another key element to consider in post-stroke recovery. It covers the emotional, information, and actual help received from family members, friends, and other significant individuals, which have been proven to be a protective factor in managing chronic illnesses [13]. People within supportive social networks can cope more easily, be more compliant to the treatment plan, and take part in rehabilitation activities thus their health-related quality of life improves. Theories go further and state that social support can serve as a predictor of wellbeing directly or as a moderator that influences how cognitive deficits turn into functional problems. Stroke survivors furnished with good support may efficiently handle memory deficits through reminders, joint budgeting, and assistance with daily activities whereas those who hardly get any help may encounter serious functional discontinuity.

In the Ghanaian setting, the focus of stroke care has been on the biomedical aspect; thus, psychosocial factors of recovery have always been overshadowed. New evidences show that most of the survivors are discharged to the communities where they get little or no structured support from the healthcare system hence becoming prone to negative cognitive and psychosocial outcomes [14]. Despite the acknowledgment of memory functioning and social support being major players in post-stroke adjustment, very few empirical studies in the Cape Coast Metropolis and surrounding areas have played in the same pool or even alluded to the impact of these variables on quality of life. Such endeavor is extremely valuable in making clinical decisions, enhancing rehabilitation undertakings, and forming public policies towards elevating the outcomes of stroke survivors over a long period.

Given this background, the present study was designed to assess the levels of memory functioning, social support, and quality of life of stroke patients and determine the relationship between these variables. More specifically, this study examined the effect of memory functioning on quality of life, whether social support is a predictor of quality of life, the effect of sociodemographic characteristics such as age, gender, educational level, and marital status on memory functioning and quality of life, and whether social support moderates the relationship between memory functioning and quality of life among stroke patients.

Materials and methods

The study employed correlational design. The population was all the one hundred and eighty-nine (189) stroke patients that had been medically diagnosed and were receiving treatment at Cape Coast Teaching Hospital (CCTH) [15]. The sample size of 132 was determine based on the Cohen, Manion, and Morrison [16] tabulated formula. Participants were selected using a simple random sampling procedure.

The inclusion criteria for the selection of the study participants comprised: stroke patients admitted and receiving treatment at CCTH, had minimum age of 18 years, and had a brain imaging diagnosis of ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke that matches the clinical presentation of stroke. The exclusion criteria comprised: stroke patients who were unable to communicate to consent to taking part in the study, had no comorbidity conditions at the end-stage other than diabetes, hypertension, and heart failure, and unwillingness to participate in the study.

We sought ethical approval from the University of Cape Coast’s Ethical Review Board (CES/ERB/UCC/edu/06–22/55) to address issues such as confidentiality, anonymity, consent, and debriefing. Additional ethical approval was sought from the Ethical Review Committee of the CCTH (CCTHERC/EC/2023/009). The questionnaires were administered to stroke patients individually by trained research assistants at the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital. Participants were approached in their wards or during outpatient visits. The study objective, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and data use were explained verbally. For patients with difficulty reading or writing, the research assistant read the questions aloud and recorded responses. Completion of the questionnaire took approximately 20–30 min per participant. Data were collected between March 1 and May 31, 2023. Eligible stroke patients were randomly selected from hospital records using a computer-generated random number list, ensuring that each patient had an equal probability of selection.

Instruments

The data collection was done using the following demographic questionnaire and an adopted scale. Although the instruments employed were extensive, the full scales were used to ensure comprehensive assessment of memory functioning, quality of life, and social support. Participants were given sufficient time to complete the questionnaires, and research assistants provided clarification when needed to minimize fatigue or distraction. The demographic characteristics assessed included gender, age, marital status, level of education, and employment status.

Memory functioning questionnaire (MFQ)

This questionnaire is a 64-item self-report scale developed by Gilewski et al. [17] to examine individuals’ self-assessment of everyday memory functioning. It measures 5 subscales: Frequency of Forgetting, which assesses how often forgetting occurs in 28 specific situations (items 2 to 29); Memory Performance in General that contain 5 items (item 1, 30, 31, 32 and 33); Seriousness of Forgetting, which rates memory failures from 18 different situations (item 34 to 51); Retrospective Functioning, which rates change in current memory ability relative to 5 points earlier in life (item 52 to 56); and Mnemonics Usage, which assesses the frequency with which 8 specific mnemonics are used (item 57 to 64). The responses are scored on a 7-point Likert scale (1–7 scores). Overall scores ranged from 64 to 448, whereby higher scores indicated higher levels of perceived memory functioning, with fewer forgetting occurrences, fewer serious incidents, improved current memory ability compared to earlier in life, and less use of mnemonics [17]. A score ranging from 64 to 128 implied low memory functioning, 129–256 implied moderate memory functioning and 25–448 implied high memory functioning [18]. Regarding the subscales, score ranging from 28 to 56, 57 to 112, and 113 to 196 implies low, moderate, and high frequency of forgetting respectively; score ranging from 5 to 10, 11 to 20, and 21 to 35 implies low, moderate, and high Memory performance in general respectively; score ranging from 18 to 36, 37 to 72, 73 to 126 implies low, moderate, and high Seriousness of forgetting respectively; the score ranging from 5 to 10, 11 to 20, and 21 to 35 implies low, moderate, and high retrospective functioning; the score ranging from 8 to 16, 17–32, and 33–56 implies low, moderate, and high mnemonics usage. The MFQ was developed and validated in 778 cognitively healthy older adults. The factor scores have a high degree of internal consistency, with alpha values ranging from 0.94 to 0.83. Cronbach’s alpha for the subscales Frequency of Forgetting, Memory Performance in General, Seriousness of Forgetting, Retrospective Functioning, and Mnemonics Usage reported Cronbach α for internal consistency of each factor was 0.94, 0.94, 0.89, 0.88, and 0.83, respectively [17]. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale and the sub-scales (Memory Performance in General, Retrospective Functioning, Mnemonics Usage, Seriousness of Forgetting, and Frequency of Forgetting) were 0.969, 0.613, 0.957, 0.920, 0.949, and 0.965 respectively.

Stroke specific quality of life scale (SS-QOL)

The Stroke Specific Quality of Life Scale (SS-QOL) is a 49-item self-report scale developed by Williams et al. [18] to assess health-related quality of life in stroke patients. This scale ask patients to respond to each SS-QOL question about the previous week on a 5-point Likert-type scale (1–5 scores). The scale measures 12 subscale: (a) energy, which comprised items 1, 2, and 3. (b) family roles, which comprised items 4, 5, and 6. (c) Language, which comprised items 7, 8, 9, 10, and 11. (d) Mobility, which comprised items 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, and 17. (e) Mood, which comprised items 18, 19, 20, 21, and 22. (f) Personality, which comprised items 23, 24, and 25. (g) Self-care, which comprised items 26, 27, 28, 29, and 30. (h) Social roles, which comprised items 31, 32, 33, 34, and 35. (i) Thinking, which comprised items 36, 37, and 38. (j) Upper extremity function, which comprised items 39, 40, 41, 42, & 43. (k) Vision, which comprised items 44, 45, and 46. (l) work/productivity, which comprised items 47, 48, and 49 [18]. Overall scores ranged from 49 to 245, whereby higher scores imply better performance. The score ranging from 49 to 114, 155 to 180, and 181 to 245 implies low, moderate, and high quality of life respectively [18]. Regarding the subscales, the score ranging from 3 to 6, 7 to 10, and 11 to 15 implies low, moderate, and high energy respectively; the score ranging from 3 to 6, 7 to 10, and 11 to 15 implies low, moderate, and high family roles respectively; the score ranging from 5 to 11, 12 to 18, and 19 to 25 implies low, moderate, and high language respectively; the score ranging from 6 to 13, 14 to 21, and 22 to 30 implies low, moderate, and high mobility respectively; the score ranging from 5 to 11, 12 to 18, and 19 to 25 implies low, moderate, and high mood respectively; the score ranging from 3 to 6, 7 to 10, and 11 to 15 implies low, moderate, and high personality respectively; the scores ranging from 5 to 11, 12 to 18, and 19 to 25 implies low, moderate, and high self-care respectively; the score ranging from 5 to 11, 12 to 18, and 19 to 25 implies low, moderate, and high social roles respectively; the score ranging from 3 to 6, 7 to 10, and 11 to 15 implies low, moderate, and high thinking respectively; the score ranging from 5 to 11, 12 to 18, and 19 to 25 implies low, moderate, and high upper extremity function respectively; the score ranging from 3 to 6, 7 to 10, and 11 to 15 implies low, moderate, and high vision respectively; the score ranging from 3 to 6, 7 to 10, and 11 to 15 implies low, moderate, and high work/productivity respectively. The SS-QOL produces subscales scores as well as an overall SS-QOL summary score. Items 4, 19, 22, 31, and 33 were reversed. SS-QOL demonstrates excellent internal consistency with Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding 0.73 across all 12 subscales, including energy (α = 0.88), family roles (α = 0.79), language (α = 0.85), mobility (α = 0.86), mood (α = 0.80), personality (α = 0.77), self-care (α = 0.89), social roles (α = 0.85), thinking (α = 0.73), upper extremity function (α = 0.83), vision (α = 0.81), and work/productivity (α = 0.75) [18]. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale and the sub-scales (Energy, Family Roles, Thinking, Work/Productivity, Vision, Personality, Self-Care, Social Roles, Language, Upper Extremity Function, Mood, and Mobility) were 0.961, 0.950, 0.604, 0.885, 0.986, 0.984, 0.920, 0.881, 0.814, 0.960, 0.928, 0.892, and 0.937 respectively.

Multidimensional scale of perceived social support (MSPSS)

The study focuses on social support as a moderating variable. The researcher considered support from friends, family, and significant others when determining what types of social support contribute to the study. Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) is a 12-item self-report scale developed by Zimet et al. [19] to assess the perceived sufficiency of support. It measures three subscales: (a) Family, which include items 3, 4, 8, and 11. (b) Friends, which comprised items 6, 7, 9, and 12. (c) Significant others, which include items 1, 2, 5 and 10. This scale ask individuals to respond to each MSPSS items on a 7-point Likert-type scale (1–7 scores). Overall scores ranged from 12 to 84, whereby higher scores denotes a high degree of perceived social support. The overall scale has a cut-off point of 42. Each of the sub-scales has a cut-off point of 14 [18]. The overall score has a test-retest reliability of 0.85 [19]. The MSPSS has a strong psychometric quality; the coefficient alpha for subscales is as follows: overall score (0.88), family (0.87), friends (0.85), and significant other (0.91). In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale and the sub-scales (family, friends, and significant others) were 0.871, 0.916, 0.968, and 0.991 respectively.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS statistics version 23. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages, were computed to summarize participants’ characteristics and the levels of memory functioning, quality of life, and perceived social support among stroke patients. Pearson product–moment correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships among memory functioning, quality of life, perceived social support, and their respective dimensions. Variables demonstrating significant bivariate associations were subsequently entered into regression models to determine their predictive contributions. Regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the role of perceived social support in predicting quality of life. To avoid conceptual overlap and potential multicollinearity, two separate models were estimated. The first model examined the predictive effect of overall perceived social support (MSPSS total score) on quality of life using simple linear regression. The second model employed multiple regression to assess the unique contributions of the three social support dimensions: family, friends, and significant others to quality of life. Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Model 1) was used to test the moderating role of perceived social support in the relationship between memory functioning and quality of life. A multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) was conducted to examine differences in memory functioning and quality of life across sociodemographic characteristics. The multivariate approach was selected because the dependent variables were conceptually related and significantly correlated, allowing for simultaneous testing while reducing the risk of Type I error. Prior to inferential analyses, assumptions of normality, linearity, homogeneity of variance-covariance matrices, independence of errors, and absence of multicollinearity were assessed and found to be satisfactory. Statistical significance was evaluated at an alpha level of 0.05.

The results showed that, on average, the participants reported quite high levels of memory functioning (M = 361.09, SD = 93.37). Among the different memory domains, frequency of forgetting had the highest average score, followed by seriousness of forgetting and mnemonics usage, indicating that the patients did have memory lapses, but they often used compensatory strategies. Retrospective functioning and general memory performance had relatively lower average scores, which indicates that there were only minor differences between the various cognitive domains rather than a consistent impairment.

The overall stroke-specific quality of life was reported to be moderate (M = 133.28, SD = 52.29), which, in a way, represents the multidimensional impact of stroke on the survivors. The scores for mood, self-care, upper extremity functioning, social roles, and language were quite high, which suggested that the patients were relatively better in their emotional wellbeing and had some degree of basic functional independence. On the other hand, the low scores for mobility and energy are indicative of the patients' physical limitations, which are likely to interfere with their complete recovery and return to their everyday activities. All these patterns, taken together, depict the inconsistency in the post-stroke recovery across the different functional domains.

The participants perceived social support was well above the scale midpoint (M = 72.13, SD = 16.71), which implies that the majority of the patients reported that they had access to individuals who were supportive. The most significant source of support was a significant other, followed by family and friends. Such a distribution could be an indication of the typical close relational ties in caregiving within the Ghanaian sociocultural context.

Most importantly, these descriptive results offer a crucial backdrop for a better appreciation of the following multivariate analyses as they depict the cognitive, functional, and psychosocial conditions within which stroke survivors attempt to recover.

This set of descriptive results directly respond to the study objective that deals with the characterization of stroke survivors' cognitive, functional, and social conditions as well as serving as a basis for the subsequent inferential analyses. After having identified the baseline levels of the primary study variables, inferential analyses were subsequently conducted to test the hypotheses of the study.

Preliminary assumption testing revealed a significant Box's test of equality of covariance matrices (Box's M = 82.25, p =.005). In behavioral datasets, Box's test is very sensitive, hence, Pillai's Trace was taken as the main multivariate test because of its resilience to violations of assumption. Although memory functioning (p =.003) and quality of life (p =.027) indicated the heterogeneity of variance by Levene's tests, MANCOVA can be considered robust against moderate violations of homogeneity when group size is reasonably equal.

Controlling for age, sex had a statistically significant multivariate effect on memory functioning and quality of life together, Pillai's Trace =.077, F(2, 92) = 3.86, p =.025, partial η² =.077 (sex accounted for 7.7% of the variance between the two variables).

On the other hand, educational level (Pillai's Trace =.023, F(6, 186) = 0.36, p =.904, partial η² =.011) and marital status (Pillai's Trace =.063, F(6, 186) = 1.00, p =.426, partial η² =.031) were not significantly related to the combined dependent variables.

Follow-up univariate analyses

Follow-up univariate analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) were performed to identify the dependent variable(s) responsible for the significant multivariate effect of sex (Table 6). It was found that sex had a significant effect on memory functioning, F(1, 93) = 7.76, p =.006, partial η² =.077, which means a moderate effect size. Discussion

Our study investigated the influence of memory functioning on stroke patients’ quality of life, using social support as a moderator in the Cape Coast Metropolis. The study found that stroke patients demonstrated relatively high levels of memory functioning. Although assessing the determinants of memory recovery was not an objective of the present study, this finding provides important contextual insight into the cognitive profile of the sample and supports subsequent analyses examining its association with quality of life. The observed pattern may reflect variations in recovery trajectories commonly reported in stroke populations; however, caution is warranted in attributing these outcomes to specific mechanisms such as rehabilitation intensity or cognitive reserve, as these factors were not directly measured in this study. These results have been supported by empirical research, which indicates that intensive rehabilitation and cognitive reserve can greatly improve memory recovery following a stroke. Everts et al. [21] investigated memory functioning in childhood stroke patients. They found that verbal functions exhibited a higher storage level than visuospatial functions, highlighting a functional advantage in verbal abilities during recovery. The study supported the significant improvement in memory functioning among stroke patients and highlighted the significance of understanding the relationships between different factors influencing rehabilitation outcomes. Similarly, Wang et al. [22] examined long-term memory functioning in a large population both before and after a stroke. They discovered that memory decline was faster in people with stroke compared to those who had not experienced a stroke, suggesting that memory loss may begin years before the stroke event. Despite this decline, focusing on cognitive interventions and better cognitive reserve can lead to significant improvements in memory after stroke, according to the current findings. In contrast, Douiri et al. [23] found that memory loss remains prevalent long-term among patients with stroke, largely influenced by sociodemographic factors. Their research highlighted the need for preventive strategies and post-stroke surveillance to manage memory loss, implying that not all stroke patients experience significant memory recovery, contrary to the significant improvements observed in the current research. Furthermore, Tang et al. [24]. found gaps in the care provided to stroke patients who had memory problems, indicating that in stroke, physical impairments frequently take precedence over cognitive problems. These disparities in care priorities can impede memory recovery. These disparities may be caused by differences in rehabilitation programs, changes in the severity of the initial brain damage, and variations in study populations. Everts et al. [21] and Wang et al. [22]. emphasize the significance of cognitive reserve and specific interventions, while Douiri et al. [23] and Tang et al. [24]. highlight complex problems and demographic factors that might limit recovery.

Our study further examined whether memory functioning among stroke patients varied across key sociodemographic characteristics, including age, educational level, gender, and marital status. Although none of these variables reached statistical significance, age demonstrated a near-significant trend with a moderate effect size, suggesting that memory functioning may vary meaningfully across age groups even when statistical thresholds are not met. The study found that stroke patients demonstrated relatively high levels of memory functioning. Although assessing the determinants of memory recovery was not an objective of the present study, this finding provides important contextual insight into the cognitive profile of the sample and supports subsequent analyses examining its association with quality of life. The observed pattern may reflect variations in recovery trajectories commonly reported in stroke populations; however, caution is warranted in attributing these outcomes to specific mechanisms such as rehabilitation intensity or cognitive reserve, as these factors were not directly measured in this study. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that ageing is associated with differential cognitive recovery trajectories following stroke, particularly in memory-related domains, due to age-related reductions in neural plasticity and cognitive reserve [12, 25]. The absence of statistically significant differences across educational levels contrasts with studies that report higher educational attainment as protective against post-stroke cognitive decline through enhanced cognitive reserve [26, 27]. However, this inconsistency may reflect contextual factors such as limited variability in educational exposure among participants or the overriding influence of stroke severity and rehabilitation access, which have been shown to attenuate the protective effects of education in clinical samples [28]. Similarly, the lack of gender differences in memory functioning supports prior findings that cognitive impairment following stroke is more strongly influenced by neurological and rehabilitation-related factors than by biological sex alone [29]. Marital status also did not significantly influence memory functioning in this study, which may appear inconsistent with literature emphasizing the role of close relationships in cognitive recovery [30, 31]. However, marital status may not accurately capture the quality or functional availability of support, suggesting that structural indicators of social ties may be less predictive of cognitive outcomes than perceived or functional social support. Collectively, these findings reinforce the view that post-stroke memory functioning is shaped by a complex interplay of biological recovery processes and psychosocial resources, with sociodemographic characteristics exerting indirect or context-dependent effects rather than direct influences on cognitive outcomes.

The study found that stroke patients reported a moderate level of quality of life. While some stroke patients can retain a moderate quality of life, many of them encounter significant challenges that negatively affect their overall well-being. These observations are consistent with the results of other empirical studies like the study of Naess et al. [32] who examined the HRQoL of young people who suffered from ischemic stroke in Norway. They discovered that young people with stroke had significantly lower HRQoL compared to control groups and the general population, especially in physical functioning. The study also highlights the significance of early detection and intervention for depression, fatigue, and physical handicaps to enhance HRQoL in patients with stroke. This finding supports the moderate quality of life observed in the current study, highlighting the physical and psychological challenges faced by stroke patients. Similarly, Kong and Yang [33] examined HRQoL among chronic stroke patients in Singapore and found that a significant majority of stroke patients still had physical limitations and depressive symptoms, which affected their total HRQoL. The outcome of this current study that stroke patients have a moderate quality of life because they continue to face psychological and physical difficulties is supported by study of Kong and Yang [33]. In contrast, Owolabi and Ogunniyi [34] discovered that individuals with more severe strokes have a lower quality of life since the severity of the stroke had a substantial impact on HRQoL. This contrasts with the moderate quality of life reported in the current study, which may reflect differences in the study populations or assessment methods. Furthermore, Baumann et al. [35]. found that stroke patients and the family carers who cared for them had reduced emotional well-being and life satisfaction, mostly because of sensory, motor, and memory impairments. Their study sheds insight into the enduring effects of stroke on the quality of life and the difficulties encountered by caretakers, which may deviate from the moderate quality of life observed in the current study. The inconsistencies in the results could be attributed to variations in study populations, the severity of strokes, the presence of comorbidities, and the availability and effectiveness of rehabilitation and support services.

The finding also revealed that stroke patients received above-average social support, predominantly from significant others. The results indicate that higher levels of social support, especially from significant others contribute positively to various aspects of stroke rehabilitation and overall well-being. The research by Tsouna-Hadjis et al. [36]. is one study that supports the conclusion that stroke patients receive above-average social support from significant others. Their study focused on social support and its impact on stroke rehabilitation outcomes over six months. Also, they found that a high level of social support from significant others was associated with increased functional abilities and improved psychosocial status among stroke patients. This supports the idea that supportive relationships with significant others are essential to the recovery process of stroke patients. Kruithof et al. [37], support the result of this study regarding the positive correlation between social support and health-related quality of life among patients with stroke. This shows that stroke patients’ well-being is highly influenced by their perceived level of social support rather than by particular sources of care. However, some research findings contradict the above-average social support for stroke patients. Northcott and Hilari [38], for example, discovered that social relationships usually weaken after a stroke, and that depression is associated with friendship loss. This contradicts the above-average social support and shows that stroke patients may lose social relationships, which might have a negative influence on their quality of life. Similarly, Adriaansen et al. [39] observed a progressive decrease in social support for the spouses of stroke patients over time. This indicates that as stroke patients progress in their rehabilitation, the level of social support they get may decline, which goes against the results of the study. These contradictory findings may stem from differences in study populations, methodologies, and the specific aspects of social support examined. Different studies may indicate varying levels of social support due to factors like cultural differences, variations in social networks, and the severity of stroke and related illnesses.

The finding of our study further revealed that memory functioning was a significant predictor of quality of life. This observed influence suggests that the memory functioning of stroke patients from the CCTH contributes positively to their overall quality of life. The study by Nichols-Larsen et al. [40]. supports this notion by highlighting the correlation between lower HRQOL in the physical aspect and factors such as comorbidities and decreased upper-extremity functionality in stroke survivors. Notably, stroke survivors with a higher number of comorbidities experienced lower HRQOL specifically in the domain of memory and thinking. This suggests that memory functioning plays a crucial role in the overall perception of quality of life after stroke. Saxena et al. [41] also provide support for this idea by emphasizing the importance of cognitive status in functional recovery post-stroke. Their findings suggest that cognitive impairment can hinder the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions aimed at improving physical functionality, indirectly implying a link between cognitive abilities such as memory and overall quality of life in stroke patients. Contrastingly, Melkas et al. [42]. focus on the impact of post-stroke dementia on long-term survival outcomes, highlighting dementia as a predictor of unfavorable survival outcomes. Their findings suggest that cognitive impairments, including memory deficits associated with dementia, can significantly affect post-stroke outcomes, which may indirectly impact quality of life. Similarly, Mellon et al. [43]. concentrate on cognitive impairment following stroke, identifying factors such as being female and having a history of cerebrovascular disease as associated with cognitive impairment six months post-stroke. Their findings imply that cognitive impairments, including memory issues, are prevalent post-stroke, which could impact overall quality of life. The discrepancies between these studies may stem from differences in their methodologies, sample characteristics, assessment tools, and follow-up durations.

Our finding additionally revealed that social support was not a significant predictor of quality of life. This finding was in line with previous research findings like Lynch et al. [44] and [45], which reported a lack of significant association between social support and quality of life in stroke patients. Their findings underscored the importance of social support in enhancing QoL. Factors such as social ties, coping strategies, communication, and physical independence were identified as significant contributors to overall quality of life after stroke. In contrast, Pallesen [46] and Martinsen et al. [47] present findings that challenge the consensus on the influence of social support QoL in stroke patients. Pallesen observed that stroke patients often experienced social stigma and isolation, where acquaintances and former friends avoided them due to perceived incapacity. This social withdrawal and perceived stigma could diminish the impact of social support networks on QoL, suggesting that negative social interactions may offset potential benefits. Also, Martinsen et al. [47]. highlighted how some stroke patients hide their symptoms to avoid social repercussions, indicating a complex relationship between social interactions and perceived support. Such findings imply that the quality and nature of social support received may vary significantly, potentially limiting its overall influence on QoL outcomes in stroke patients. The discrepancies in findings across these studies could be attributed to several factors. Methodological differences, such as varying measurement tools and sample characteristics may lead to contrasting conclusions regarding the impact of social support. Cultural differences and societal norms regarding illness and disability also play a role in shaping the social interactions and support networks available to stroke survivors, influencing their perceived QoL.

Additionally, although the present study did not identify a statistically significant moderating effect of social support on the relationship between memory functioning and quality of life among stroke patients, recent empirical evidence suggests that the role of social support in post-stroke outcomes is complex and often indirect rather than linear. Research published in BMC Psychology and related Springer Nature journals indicates that perceived social support frequently exerts its influence through psychological and behavioral pathways rather than acting as a direct moderator of cognitive–quality-of-life relationships. For example, Upasen et al. [48] found that among older adults, perceived social support was associated with resilience and mental health both directly and indirectly via resilience, highlighting the importance of internal psychological resources in shaping wellbeing. Similarly, Oh et al. [48] reported that social support played a role in cognitive and daily activity outcomes in older adults, with subjective cognition and resilience being central components of these pathways. These findings suggest that social support often influences outcomes via intermediary psychological resources such as resilience and coping capacity, consistent with the current study’s results, where perceived social support did not significantly moderate the relationship between memory functioning and quality of life. Moreover, Li et al. [49] demonstrated that perceived social support was significantly associated with psychological resilience among stroke survivors, with hope mediating this relationship. Their findings suggest that social support enhances internal psychological resources, which in turn contribute to better psychosocial adjustment following stroke. While memory functioning was not explicitly modeled in their study, resilience and hope are closely linked to cognitive engagement and adaptive functioning, implying that social support may influence quality of life indirectly rather than through direct moderation of memory outcomes. This interpretation aligns with the current findings, where social support did not significantly alter the strength of the association between memory functioning and quality of life. Similarly, emerging intervention-based evidence from BMC Psychology highlights that psychosocial and cognitive improvements in stroke survivors often depend on structured therapeutic contexts rather than passive availability of social support alone. This may explain why general perceived social support, as measured in the present study, did not demonstrate a moderating effect. Further support for this explanation comes from gerontological research published in BMC Geriatrics, which found that social support influenced cognitive health primarily by promoting participation in cognitively stimulating activities rather than directly buffering cognitive decline [50]. In this study, older adults with higher perceived social support were more likely to engage in mentally stimulating behaviors, which subsequently reduced cognitive decline risk. Applied to stroke populations, this suggests that social support may require behavioral activation mechanisms to exert measurable effects on cognition and quality of life. In addition, evidence from broader psychosocial models in stroke recovery demonstrates that social support often functions within conditional or moderated mediation frameworks rather than as a standalone moderator. A recent study using a moderated mediation model showed that social support strengthened the indirect effect of family functioning on psychological distress via hope among stroke survivors, but did not independently predict outcomes in isolation [51]. This finding reinforces the notion that social support operates synergistically with internal psychological processes, which may account for its non-significant moderating role in the present study. Systematic reviews further corroborate these observations by showing inconsistent associations between social support and health-related quality of life after stroke. While some studies report positive relationships, others find weak or non-significant effects, particularly when social support is measured broadly without accounting for quality, type, or functional relevance [52, 53]. Such variability supports the present findings and suggests that perceived social support alone may be insufficient to modify the impact of memory functioning on quality of life without targeted cognitive or psychosocial interventions. Taken together, evidence from BMC journals and related literature indicates that while social support remains an important component of post-stroke care, its influence on cognitive outcomes and quality of life is likely indirect, conditional, and context-dependent. This helps explain why social support did not moderate the memory-quality-of-life relationship in the current study and underscores the need for future research to examine more nuanced models incorporating mediators such as resilience, hope, cognitive engagement, and rehabilitation intensity.

Strengths and limitations

The study follows a correlational design with no causal inference or interpretations. The study was conducted at a single point in time; therefore, it could not help to assess the extent to which social support influences the memory functioning and quality of life of stroke patients over a long period. Further studies could adopt longitudinal design to address this challenge. Also, the study only included stroke patients at the CCTH. As a result, the findings were limited in their generalizability.

Notwithstanding, the strengths of the study are worth acknowledging. Although the current study did not directly examine the impact of social support on psychological outcomes such as stress, depression, or anxiety, the observed association between social support and quality of life highlights the potential importance of considering patients’ social networks. Healthcare providers may therefore benefit from integrating assessments of social support into patient-centered care to identify opportunities for enhancing well-being. Rehabilitation specialists, physiotherapists, speech and language therapists, clinical health psychologists, and neurologists can use the findings of the study to develop and implement more effective and comprehensive rehabilitation programs. For the research community, this study adds valuable knowledge to the growing body of literature on the interplay between memory functioning, social support, and quality of life in stroke patients. Focusing on the Ghanaian population, it addresses a gap in the current research, providing context-specific insights that are crucial for understanding the unique challenges faced by stroke patients. The findings of the study can serve as a foundation for future stroke researchers by exploring similar variables in different cultures and geographical areas, thereby contributing to the global understanding of stroke rehabilitation. Additionally, it sets a new direction for stroke research in Ghana, advocating for a shift from focusing solely on physiopathology to exploring the social and psychological experiences of stroke patients.

Conclusion

The study showed that most stroke patients have a high memory functioning. While the overall quality of life among stroke patients was moderate, the analysis of specific SS-QOL dimensions revealed scores ranging from low to moderate levels. This descriptive analysis provides context for understanding the patterns of quality-of-life outcomes and complements the main analyses, which focused on the predictive roles of memory functioning and social support. These results are consistent with prior stroke research indicating variable recovery across different functional domains (e.g., mobility, self-care, social roles). The study shows that stroke patients reported above-average levels of social support from significant others, family, and friends. However, the study did not find that these sources of support were significantly associated with quality of life, indicating that perceived social support, while generally high, did not directly correspond to changes in quality of life among participants One of the key findings drawn from this study was the significant influence of memory functioning on the quality of life in stroke patients. Specifically, the frequency of forgetting dimension had a significant impact, emphasizing the need to address memory-related issues in rehabilitation and support interventions. On the other hand, the study found that neither social support nor any dimension of it had a statistically significant influence on the quality of life in stroke patients, indicating that traditional forms of support may not have a significant beneficial impact on the overall well-being of stroke patients. This unexpected result calls for additional investigation as it poses fascinating concerns regarding the function of social support in this situation. Although memory functioning showed a negative influence but statistically insignificant influence in predicting quality of life in stroke patients, the introduction of social support appeared to amplify this weak relationship. Notably, the interaction effect between social support and memory functioning was discovered to be insignificant, indicating that social support had no significant moderation effect on the influence of memory functioning on quality of life. These findings highlight the diverse nature of stroke patients’ experiences, in which memory functioning and social support interact in complex ways to influence their quality of life. As the medical and caregiving communities attempt to provide comprehensive care for stroke patients, addressing memory-related issues and creating effective support systems remain critical for enhancing the overall well-being and rehabilitation outcomes of these patients. Further research and intervention initiatives in this area have the potential to improve the lives of stroke patients and contribute to a more holistic approach to post-stroke care. With respect to socio-demographic differences, the study found no statistically significant variations in memory functioning across age, educational level, gender, or marital status. However, age demonstrated a near-significant trend with a moderate effect size, indicating that memory functioning may vary meaningfully across age groups despite not reaching conventional levels of statistical significance. This pattern suggests that age-related cognitive vulnerability and recovery trajectories may influence post-stroke memory outcomes in subtle but clinically relevant ways. The absence of significant differences across other socio-demographic variables implies that memory impairment following stroke may be driven more strongly by neurological and rehabilitation-related factors than by demographic characteristics alone.
