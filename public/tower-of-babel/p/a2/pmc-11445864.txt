Giant pituitary macroadenoma with apoplexy presenting with isolated bilateral hypoglossal nerve palsy: illustrative case
Authors: Mazen Zaher, Devin W Kolmetzky, Zein Al-Atrache, Swar Vimawala, Nadeem R Kolia, Saniya S Godil
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDGiant pituitary prolactinomas are rare entities that can present with pituitary apoplexy and acute neurological decline. Isolated acute hypoglossal nerve palsy is an extremely rare presentation that is not well described in the literature. The patient underwent urgent endoscopic transsphenoidal decompression and debulking of her tumor and was started on medical therapy with immediate, significant improvement in the bilateral hypoglossal nerve palsies.LESSONSProlactinomas presenting with acute neurological decline, including bilateral hypoglossal nerve palsy, can benefit from urgent endoscopic transsphenoidal surgical decompression and safe debulking. Giant prolactinomas rarely present with apoplexy along with rapid neurological decline.1 Most commonly, these cases present with visual symptoms due to suprasellar and cavernous sinus extension with involvement of the upper cranial nerves (CNs).2–6 In rare situations, the posteroinferior growth pattern of adenomas can present with lower CN dysfunction.7–9 There has been one case report in the literature of an isolated unilateral hypoglossal nerve palsy that was managed nonsurgically.10 This report describes the first case of a giant pituitary prolactinoma with apoplexy that presented with isolated, bilateral hypoglossal nerve palsies and rapidly improved after endoscopic transsphenoidal decompression.

Illustrative Case

A 62-year-old female with a history of 2 months of worsening neck pain presented to the hospital after a syncopal episode with associated sudden-onset dysarthria and glossoplegia. Given her initial sudden-onset presenting symptoms and neurological deficits, there was concern that she had had a stroke. However, head computed tomography (CT) revealed a large sellar and suprasellar tumor with extensive bony invasion and anteroposterior extension. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a 7 × 5 × 6–cm heterogeneously enhancing pituitary adenoma with extensive parasellar extension (Fig. 1). The neurological examination was remarkable for bilateral CN XII palsy causing severe dysarthria and difficulty swallowing due to tongue hypomotility (Fig. 2A). The patient had no deficits in visual fields or acuity with normal vocal cord mobility and no other CN dysfunction.

Endocrine laboratory values were significant for a prolactin level of 4700 ng/mL and a dilutional prolactin level of 32,660 ng/mL. The patient was started on high-dose decadron and bromocriptine within 24 hours of presentation to the hospital. Given the patient’s sudden-onset neurological decline and significant CN XII palsy, she was likely symptomatic from the mass effect of acute apoplexy rather than long-term tumor invasion of the skull base and hypoglossal canal. Therefore, she was taken for an urgent endoscopic transsphenoidal decompression and debulking of her pituitary macroadenoma within 48 hours of presentation. The goal of surgery was to reduce the mass effect from the recent apoplexy and achieve maximal safe debulking of her tumor.

Our surgical approach and exposure revealed both sphenoid and sellar components of the tumor (Fig. 3A). Intraoperatively, after the sellar dura was opened, a large component of hemorrhagic tumor and mixed-density blood products were evacuated under high pressure (Fig. 3B). This was followed by safe debulking of the sphenoid, sellar, and clival components of the tumor (Fig. 3C). No cerebrospinal fluid was encountered during the procedure. Meticulous hemostasis and copious irrigation followed, and the anterior skull base defect was filled with a large piece of Duragen and covered by a free mucosal graft. The patient was placed in a cervical collar postoperatively because of concerns of craniocervical instability given the extensive bilateral condylar invasion and her history of neck pain. Histological and prolactin staining confirmed the diagnosis of giant pituitary prolactinoma (Fig. 4). The patient did well postoperatively. She was extubated on postoperative day 1, with slow improvement in tongue mobility and dysarthria in the week following her surgery (Fig. 2B). She had immediate improvement of her CN XII dysfunction. She passed a swallow test on postoperative day 9 and was discharged home on a pureed diet with thick liquids on postoperative day 11. The patient continued to have improvement in her tongue mobility at her 3-week outpatient follow-up (Fig. 2C). Postoperative brain MRI showed adequate decompression of the sellar and suprasellar components of the tumor with expected residual tumor in the bilateral cavernous sinuses, clivus, and bilateral occipital condyles (Fig. 5). She was switched to oral cabergoline, which she is expected to remain on for life. Her dilutional prolactin level decreased to 11,233 ng/mL 6 weeks postsurgery. She had no neck pain by her 2-month outpatient follow-up. Flexion-extension radiographs of the cervical spine showed no signs of instability; therefore, her cervical collar was discontinued. She continues to be followed clinically at this time and has residual right hypoglossal weakness and tongue wasting with no dietary restrictions (Fig. 2D).

Discussion

Observations

Apoplexy of giant prolactinomas can have different presentations, most commonly with headaches, endocrinological dysfunction, and neurological deficits due to local mass effect.11 These deficits are usually dependent on the predominant direction of tumor growth. The posteroinferior growth pattern of macroprolactinomas has been described in patients with multidirectional expansion who presented with multiple CN deficits.7–10 Invasion of occipital condyles and obliteration of the hypoglossal canal is thought to be the etiology explaining CN XII dysfunction in these patients. One case reported isolated chronic unilateral right hypoglossal dysfunction in a patient with a giant prolactinoma without apoplexy who underwent an endonasal transsphenoidal biopsy only.10 The authors documented neurological improvement and resolution of hypoglossal nerve palsy 2 months after starting bromocriptine and dexamethasone. To our knowledge, our patient is the first reported in the literature who presented with isolated bilateral acute hypoglossal nerve palsies that improved after endonasal surgical decompression.

Cases of giant prolactinomas causing occipitocervical instability through bony destruction of the occipital condyles have been reported in the literature. Chugh et al. in 2021 reported the case of a 39-year-old male who had presented with a giant prolactinoma with bilateral condylar invasion and clinical signs of craniocervical instability, including mechanical neck pain with arm and occipital dysesthesias.12 The patient underwent an occiput to C5 posterior fusion to address the instability and was started on cabergoline to address the prolactinoma. The authors further concluded that bony erosion of the condyles, even without ligamentous involvement, was an indication for surgical fixation. We chose a different approach with our patient. Our main priority was the endonasal transsphenoidal decompression and hypoglossal nerve recovery. As a precaution, we placed the patient in a cervical collar in the perioperative period. Two months postsurgery, she had no neck or radicular pain, and flexion-extension radiographs of her cervical spine showed no abnormal subluxation; thus, her collar was discontinued. Tumor shrinkage after medical therapy initiation can lead to craniocervical instability in the future;12 therefore, our patient will continue to be followed up clinically and radiographically for any signs of instability.

Lessons

Isolated acute bilateral hypoglossal nerve palsy after apoplexy of giant prolactinomas is a very rare presentation that can be addressed with urgent endoscopic transsphenoidal decompression and safe debulking of the tumor. Patients with bilateral condylar tumor invasion and neck pain can be managed with a cervical collar in the immediate perioperative period and subsequently followed up clinically and radiographically for signs of craniocervical instability.
