Toward precision stroke rehabilitation: an integrated causal machine learning and clinician feedback approach
Authors: Aaron Baird, Houping Xiao, Yusen Xia, Chloe Sellers, Katheryn Metzger, Karen Patterson, Liv Nyankori, Raeda Anderson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo identify causal mechanisms driving variations in the impact of rehabilitation treatments on stroke survivors’ independence improvement during rehabilitation inpatient stays.Materials and MethodsIterative cycles of clinical input and causal machine learning (causal ML) were employed toward the goal of identifying relevant heterogeneous treatment effects. Data were from stroke patients (n = 484) seeking to improve independence during inpatient rehabilitation, where treatments provided included sessions (eg, physical therapy) and medication administration.ResultsWe find heterogeneity in rehabilitation treatment effects for a number of patient subgroups. Patient subgroups found to have the most heterogeneity in treatment effects were those with a bilateral involvement stroke location and those with diabetes. In a small minority of cases, we also observe heterogeneous treatment effects for those of older age, males versus females, and stroke location on either the right or left side of the brain. In regard to therapies, those related to mental health (ie, psychotherapy and spiritual/chaplaincy) had the most positive uplift in independence outcomes by the end of inpatient rehabilitation stays.DiscussionStroke survivors have varying responses to stroke rehabilitation treatments. We show that heterogeneous treatment effects are indeed present in rehabilitation. Identification of specific mechanisms, such as stroke location and provisioning of mental health services, is made possible through the use of causal ML applied to observational data in stroke rehabilitation.ConclusionsCausal ML can help to identify the mechanisms driving independence outcome variation. However, the large number of effects discovered and the small size of many effects make clinician feedback of paramount importance. Use of causal ML with clinician feedback throughout the process improves identification of appropriate measures and selection of relevant results.Keywords: stroke, rehabilitation, independence, machine learning, clinician feedback

Background and significance

Precision health is “the tailoring of medical treatment to the individual characteristics of each patient… [and] the ability to classify individuals into subpopulations that differ in their… response to a specific treatment.”1,2 Precision rehabilitation takes into account characteristics of patients when determining which rehabilitation therapies and medications are most appropriate.3,4 We specifically consider here the application of rehabilitation toward improving stroke recovery. Prior research has found rehabilitation of stroke patients to be more effective when interventions are tailored to individual characteristics. For instance, it has been found that high-intensity speech and language therapy can be more effective for middle-aged individuals while low-intensity can be more effective for older individuals.5 However, identification of which treatments work best for whom is not trivial.

Given the challenge in defining which treatments will work best for patient subgroups, one of the frontiers of research in precision rehabilitation is the application of machine learning (ML). ML uses computational techniques to identify patterns in data, select models, and find variable (feature) associations toward a better understanding of which treatments (interventions) have the strongest effect on outcomes.6–12 For instance, emerging research has begun to use ML to predict motor function in stroke patients using clinical data and magnetic resonance images (MRI).7 Further research in this area has found that automated exercise assessment, which applies ML toward classifying exercise execution accuracy, can help patients and therapists better understand if patients are engaging in exercise effectively.9

While research is beginning to report on the use of ML in healthcare,3,5 our knowledge in regard to heterogeneous treatment effects (HTEs)—ie, identification of the difference in average treatment effects (ATEs) between treatment and control groups when conditioned by subgroup variable(s)—in stroke rehabilitation remains limited (please see the Supplementary Appendix for more details on the current state of literature in this space). Causal ML is emerging as especially useful in increasing our understanding of heterogeneity in treatment effects and causal mechanisms in medicine,12–14 especially when using observational data.12,15 Casual ML can be especially effective at identification of HTEs as it can apply nonparametric methods, flexibly select models, and handle high dimensional data.16,17 We suggest that stroke rehabilitation could become more precise and effective if causal ML was applied toward more in-depth understanding of rehabilitation HTEs.

However, while the estimation of HTEs with causal ML can result in findings that are statistically significant, such estimations may not be actionable, generalizable, or substantive enough to make a notable difference in practice.18 Thus, this study reports how results were generated and refined through a collaboration between data scientists and clinicians. Clinician feedback was specifically solicited through a focus group series leveraged throughout the phases of the ML pipeline as well as by the clinicians who are co-authors on this study. The composition of the focus groups was based on inpatient specialization with inclusion of clinicians from a myriad of positions: nurses, physical therapists, occupational therapists, speech therapists, therapy managers, and physicians. Results were then assessed. Actionable items were selected for integration into clinical decision making. More details on clinician feedback are available in the Supplementary Appendix.

Research aims and objectives

The objective of this study is to identify causal mechanisms driving variations in the effect of rehabilitation treatments on stroke survivors’ independence improvement during rehabilitation inpatient stays. An iterative approach of generating results and obtaining clinician feedback to understand which results were most translatable to practice, or needed further examination, was employed.

Methods

Research setting

The data for this project originate from the Shepherd Center, a 152-bed neurological rehabilitation hospital and facility, located in Atlanta, Georgia. Shepherd Center provides inpatient and outpatient services to patients with acquired and traumatic brain injuries, spinal cord injuries, and patients with other neurological conditions, including multiple sclerosis. This project focused on the stroke recovery treatments (eg, therapy sessions, medication management) provided by Shepherd Center to patients recovering from stroke during their post-stroke rehabilitation inpatient stay at Shepherd Center.

Data collection

For this study, patient record data were extracted from EPIC, the electronic medical record used by Shepherd Center, and other electronic medical record systems at Shepherd Center. All data were de-identified across all 18 HIPPA private health information (PHI) indicators (https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/special-topics/de-identification/index.html). IRB approval was obtained (IRB Date and Reference Number: June 11, 2024, #380423). Data were patient-centered and included demographics, stroke location, length of rehabilitation inpatient stay, types and frequency of sessions, medication prescription and use, outcomes from National Outcome Measurement Systems (NOMS) (https://spars.samhsa.gov/sites/default/files/2022-09/CMHSNOMSToolEnglish.pdf) including measures for cognition, communication, and swallowing, and CMS Inpatient Rehabilitation Facility (IRF) Quality Indicator codes for self-care and mobility (https://www.cms.gov/medicare/quality/inpatient-rehabilitation-facility). Data extraction was restricted to inpatient stroke patients at Shepherd Center from April 2019 to December 2023 and excluded any patients without full inpatient journeys within that window (ie, admitted prior to April 2019, discharged after December 2023) so as to have full inpatient journeys on all included patients. More informatics details related to data extraction and transformation are available in the Supplementary Appendix.

Data analysis

Following available methodological guidelines for estimating treatment effects with ML,12,16 we applied the causal forest method.19 Specifically, we used the causal forest method in the Python EconML package (Version 0.15.0) using the double machine learning (DML) module (https://econml.azurewebsites.net/_autosummary/econml.dml.CausalForestDML.html). (All hyperparameters were left as default except for 2. We set min_samples_leaf = 39 to apply an 8% cutoff (ie, min of 8% of patients out of 484 patients total in our sample must be in a leaf). We set random_state = 44 to maintain stability between runs of the model.) DML can achieve doubly robust estimation of treatment effects that can overcome both regularization and overfitting biases.20 The causal forest was selected as it has been used in multiple relevant studies,13,16,21,22 can identify both linear and nonlinear relationships,23 and has been identified as a robust method for health analytics.24,25 The causal forest is tree (forest) based, which results in interpretable and explainable output, and can support both discrete and continuous treatment variables. The estimates are also convergent and unbiased, to the extent that confounders are included as covariates and that the common support (overlap) assumption is met (ie, the propensity scores are bounded away from 0 and 1).26 Furthermore, the causal forest method provides point estimates for treatment effects and also confidence intervals.16

Application of the causal forest method requires identification of an outcome variable, a treatment variable, and covariates (Table 1). In this study, the outcome variable, as fully described in Table 2, is a binary indicator of More Independence Improvement (1) or Less Independence Improvement (0) of post-stroke patients during a rehabilitation inpatient admission. The binary target was generated by evaluating independence improvement for multiple measures per patient (cognition, self-care, mobility, and swallowing). The outcomes for most measures feeding into the binary target are calculated as relative improvement percentages, in order to fairly compare independence changes when admission functional independence scores vary and patients have different treatment needs.27–30 The treatment variables are the frequency of each session type per week and frequency of administration of each medication per week of rehabilitation inpatient stay. We note that we divided the session and medication frequencies by the number of weeks in the inpatient stay to adjust for the fact that patients who stay longer are likely to receive more treatments. Two exceptions to dividing by number of weeks were sessions and medications that occurred 3 times or less during the stay, such as wheelchair fitting and Botox administration. For these less frequently offered sessions and medications, the treatment was binary, where 1 = received and 0 = not received. Covariates used to address confoundedness include sex, age, race, education, stroke location, married, time since stroke, BMI, comorbidities, and admission scores for cognition, swallowing, self-care, and mobility.

When applying the causal forest, the HTEs (aka, conditional average treatment effects, CATEs) are defined as the difference in outcomes between the treated and control groups, conditional on a set of covariates, for specific patient subgroups (eg, patients with neglect syndrome). To calculate these CATEs, DML is a framework designed to adjust for confounding variables when estimating treatment effects. The algorithm models the treatment assignment and the outcome given provided covariates, using an ML method (random forests, in our case). Then the algorithm calculates the residuals for both the outcome and treatment models, by calculating the deviation between the real value and the expectation conditional on the covariates. Finally, a causal forest is run using the residualized data, which partitions the data by maximizing difference in treatment effects between subgroups. Then the effect is estimated and is averaged over the population of covariates. For determining heterogeneity, we first select samples in each patient subgroup identified as exhibiting heterogeneity, then retrain the model for the best fit, by retraining the outcome and treatment models, and then re-generate the treatment effect for each sample within the subgroup, which are averaged to generate the CATEs.

To address non-random treatment assignment, as more severe patients are more likely to receive treatments designed for more complex issues, we included the admissions scores for all variables used to construct our outcome variable (cognition, swallowing, self-care, and mobility) as covariates. We also used quintiles of length of inpatient rehabilitation stay (LOS)31 as a clustering variable.32 LOS is shorter for patients with less rehabilitation needs and longer for those with greater rehabilitation needs. Furthermore, needs can develop further during the stay, thus making LOS a good proxy for understanding complexity over the entire stay. One additional possibility, however, for having a shorter LOS would be lack of ability to pay, such as insurance limiting LOS or self-pay limitations. We note that this rehabilitation hospital uses donor funds to cover LOS when financial constraints are present. Therefore, LOS represents how much time was required to sufficiently recover.

We also note that causal inference methods, including the method applied here, are sensitive to sample size. Prior research has found that sample sizes of 250 to 500 are typically sufficient.33 Thus, given our sample size of 484 patients, we propose our sample is of sufficient size. Furthermore, in regard to minimum subgroup size, one study34 chose 8% as their sample size cutoff when interpreting final results (ie, subgroups had to be at least 200 individuals in size given their sample of just under 2500 patients). Therefore, to increase the practical application of our results, reduce result complexity, and align with prior work,34 we report the HTEs for subgroups that are 8% or larger of our total sample size (ie, at least 39 patients in a subgroup, given the final sample of 484 patients).

We further note that, in some cases in our results, the common support assumption was found to be violated as the propensity score distribution for some treatments included values at or close to 0 (ie, never treated) and close to 1 (ie, always treated). Following well-known guidance in this area, we trimmed our sample in each case to include only those with propensity scores between 0.01 and 0.99,26,35 to retain those in the area of common support. This also means that our effects are local average treatment effects, in these cases.

ML pipeline + clinician feedback

We integrated causal ML and clinician assessment throughout our analyses process (Figure 1). For clinician feedback, iterative semi-structured recorded focus groups were conducted with clinicians specializing in acquired brain injury (ABI). Analysis of focus group recordings and transcriptions was done via thematic analysis, led and analyzed by a data scientist with extensive mixed methods training. In the initial focus groups, clinicians were asked to identify the key metrics of interest for their patient rehabilitation goals and which factors they felt drove those goals in a substantive manner. Further details are in the Supplementary Appendix. After ML analyses were conducted, clinicians were engaged in email exchange and meetings to provide feedback on findings, provide clinical insights, and identify any findings that were surprising and/or counterintuitive to their experiences with inpatient stroke recovery patterns.

Results

Data description

Stroke rehabilitation patient records (n = 560) were extracted, which represented a census of patients treated during the defined time period. Data related to the target variable was missing for 53 patients and these patients were dropped from the analyses due to not being able to assess impacts on outcomes. There were also 23 patients with a stroke type of “other,” which includes strokes from blood clots in amputations and other rare situations. These patients were also dropped from the analyses due to the unique nature of their stroke rehabilitation needs. We describe in Table 1 the characteristics of the sample of included patients (n = 484).

Data translation

The primary outcome (aka, target; dependent variable) in this study is whether the patient achieved more or less independence improvement (with respect to local data) at the time of discharge from their post-stroke rehabilitation inpatient stay (see Table 2 for details). We made the determination of “more” or “less” independence improvement using multiple measures (cognition, self-care, mobility, and swallowing), where for each measure we determined if the patient had improved more than the median relative to other patients in the same dataset for that measure by discharge or not. Then, we aggregated the binary median splits per measure into one final overall binary measure, where if the patient was above the median for half of the measures or more, they were classified as having achieved more independence improvement. Another formulation of the target is a majority vote, and robustness checks based on this alternative formulation are available in the Supplementary Appendix.

The quantity of different types of sessions (number of distinct session types = 193) and medications (number of distinct medications = 658) would generate too much complexity if every session type and medication were considered individually in our analyses. To address this, we made 2 decisions. First, we grouped the therapy sessions (sessions, henceforth) into eight different categories: physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech language pathology, recreation therapy, family training, chaplaincy services, wheelchair services (fitting), and other. Second, we consulted with clinicians as to which medications to examine in-depth for this patient population. The clinicians identified three medications for analysis via the reasoning that the three medications are known to impact spasticity and tone: baclofen, Botox (Onabotulinumtoxina), and dantrolene. We analyzed the HTEs of the clinician recommended medications and also identified the top and bottom largest HTEs for the other types of medications given to patients. We also ran a number of additional analyses and the results of these are available in the Supplementary Appendix.

The HTE estimates can be interpreted as the treatment effect of the session type (eg, occupational therapy, OT) on independence improvement during the rehabilitation stay, conditional on the covariate (eg, comorbidity count: high). The results in Figure 2 show that many HTEs for sessions conducted are positive. There are a few HTEs that are negative or near zero at the top of the confidence interval, including WHEELCHAIR, OT, and SLP if the stroke location is bilateral and OT for those with high comorbidity count category. Positive HTEs are found for chaplaincy (not specific to any one religion) services (SPIRITUAL) and psychotherapy sessions (PSYCH) for those with diabetes, wheelchair fitting (WHEELCHAIR) for those with a high number of comorbidities, and with neglect syndrome; SPIRITUAL for those with right stroke left body hemisphere stroke location (Stroke Loc) or with bilateral involvement of stroke location or male; as well as wheelchair fitting (WHEELCHAIR) for those with high number of comorbidities. We also find that recreation (REC) therapy has a positive HTE for those with a bilateral stroke location. We note that additional analyses related to the specific type of PT offered and the timing of PT are available in the Supplementary Appendix.

We ran 2 sensitivity tests. First, we changed the target variable to only require 1 of the 4 measures of independence to be 1 (More Independence Improvement), in order to test the sensitivity of the results to a different target variable specification. Second, we ran another sensitivity test where the relative independence improvement was compared only to those within the same age group (ie, younger, older, middle). Effects that were consistent between the main results and 1 of the 2 sensitivity tests are marked with a single star (*).

None of the HTEs for clinical recommended medications were significant (Figure 3). One medication that had a consistent HTE in all sensitivity tests was Baclofen for those of older age, but the confidence interval includes zero. No other significant effects were found for any of the medications and covariates considered. We note that Botox exhibits potentially more negative HTEs for those who are older, male, have diabetes, or a higher number of comorbidities. Botox also appears at the other end of the continuum as having potentially more positive HTEs for females, those with less comorbidities, those with neglect syndrome, and those with right stroke left body hemisphere strokes. However, all the confidence intervals for these HTEs of Botox include zero, and thus are not significant. We also note that the confidence intervals are wider for Botox than for other medications tested, meaning that uncertainty of effect estimation of Botox is higher. This perhaps indicates that Botox has a wider range of impact, or perhaps that the timing of Botox treatment is important in relation to other treatments (eg, most effective when administered before PT). Thus, this would be an interesting area of future study. We further note that we ran the same sensitivity tests mentioned prior for these medication treatment effects. Baclofen for those who are of older age was found to be consistent with both sensitivity checks (and is marked with **).

Negative HTEs on independence improvement are found for a number of medications and covariates including aluminum—magnesium hydroxide—simethicone for those with diabetes, alprazolam for those with right stroke left body hemisphere stroke location, and methocarbamol for those who are not White. Higher positive HTEs include lactated ringers for those who are older, levofloxacin and albuterol sulfate for those who are older, and methyl salicylate—menthol for those with hypertension. We note that 2 of these HTEs were consistent with one of the sensitivity checks, although for the full list of medications (655), more consistency was found. Even so, this indicates that HTEs for specific medications are sensitive to target variable specification.

Deployment for decision making

After completing the casual ML analyses, clinicians reviewed the results and were asked how to best incorporate the final findings into clinical decision-making processes. Clinicians identified 2 key clinical committees to disseminate findings, interpret results into actionable results, and guide future analysis as the rehabilitation outcome committee and the stroke committee. Furthermore, the process represented in Figure 5 was recommended to incorporate treatment effect heterogeneity into clinical decision making.

Process for incorporating heterogeneity in treatment effects into clinical decision making.

Specific feedback received from clinicians is summarized here as follows. First, it was stated that PT, OT, SLP, and wheelchair fittings are things that all patients receive as part of their standard care, while recreation therapy (REC) and chaplaincy services (SPIRITUAL) are things that patients seek out in addition to their standard care. Thus, the positive impacts of REC and SPIRITUAL for some patient subgroups were not surprising as they reflect internal motivation and often have a lift in spirits. Thus, mental health appears to play a big role in recovery. Second, in regard to medications, it was suggested that if clinicians providing therapy services (eg, PT, OT, SLP) had a list of medications and their likely impacts on factors relevant to the therapy session (eg, spasticity, tone, or swallowing impact), even if atypical, then they could time treatments for when the medication was at its peak for the patient, toward the goal of maximizing the effectiveness of the therapy session (eg, when spasms are at a minimum). For instance, perhaps the outcomes after Botox are more variable than for some other medications and require careful monitoring while perhaps Baclofen could have a beneficial impact for older adults. These types of findings would be good to first research further and, if treatment effects can be accurately estimated after randomized controlled trials, then communicated to prescribing and practicing clinicians for consideration. Furthermore, as a result of this study, independence is now measured by Shepherd Center using a standardized format and is being incorporated into quality monitoring, including dashboards. Finally, as a result of this study and the data-driven efforts being undertaken by Shepherd Center, patient outcomes are now being included in clinician performance evaluations. Thus, demand for results such as these, which identify areas that could be improved and also areas that may have a larger effect on improving outcomes than others, are likely to be in even more demand by clinicians in the future.

Discussion

This study has evaluated variation in rehabilitation treatment effects, for sessions and medications, provided to post-stroke patients during rehabilitation inpatient stays.

Principal findings and implications

Our findings support the need to address heterogeneity in treatment effects in stroke rehabilitation. Subgroups found to have the most heterogeneity in treatment effects were patients specifically with a bilateral stroke location and with diabetes. In a smaller minority of cases, we also observe heterogeneity in treatment effects for those of older age, males versus females, as well as in stroke location on either the right or left side of the brain. We find that for sessions (eg, therapies), therapies related to mental health (ie, psychotherapy and spiritual/chaplaincy) had the most positive uplift in independence outcomes by the end of inpatient rehabilitation stays.

We also find that clinician assessment of ML-based results is essential, given the volume and variety of results. We further note that hypothesis generation36,37 and causal decision making38 are emerging areas of inquiry associated with causal ML and we believe our findings are consistent with these approaches. Under a causal decision-making framework, the primary question of interest is treatment assignment, rather than exact treatment effect estimates.38 We believe our findings are especially valuable for making treatment assignment decisions and for generating new hypotheses that can be further tested in randomized designs.

Limitations

This study is limited to patients who received inpatient stroke treatment from one rehabilitation hospital in the United States. Further we note that, as with all causal analyses, assumptions were made. Causal inference methods assume consistency in treatment and non-interference (stable unit value treatment assumption, SUTVA), unconfoundedness, and common support.

Regarding SUTVA, we addressed this assumption by testing many different treatments and by including a clustering variable (LOS quintiles), but treatment variation could still be present, such as when different physical therapists treat patients differently. Therefore, future research could address such treatment variations and explore ways to further cluster patients.

In regard to unconfoundedness, we sought to include as many potential confounding factors as possible in our analyses, but overlap issues will still be present. In particular, we do not have full information on prior medical history per patient, patient behaviors such as diet and exercise, the degree of social and family support that will be provided during rehabilitation and post-rehabilitation, or social determinants of health that may impact both expected and actual recovery. Future research with access to such data could further test using additional variables. We also note that we did not consider the medications the patient was on prior to rehabilitation admission or the specific dosage of each medication administered during rehabilitation admission, as effective dosages are often in a range and determination of level of effectiveness was beyond the scope of this study. However, we note that this is a possible confound and more research should be done into the effects of medications at different dosage and frequency levels. Future research could also consider HTEs by severity of condition over time (eg, improvement or degradation as time progresses) as well as stroke severity beyond consideration of stroke on one side of the brain or both. Further insights could also be generated with consideration of more clinical metrics, such as patient reported outcomes associated with process metrics (eg, pain and spasm management) and overall outcomes (eg, self-perception of independence improvement).

Regarding common support, we note that our relative improvement approach is relative to this specific patient population (local) and is not based on a national population or sample. Consequently, one way to improve the accuracy of estimates in analyses like the ones in this paper would be larger, pooled datasets aggregated from multiple stroke rehabilitation programs.

We also note that our target variable could weigh each of the 4 measures differently, and even have weights adjusted by patient characteristics such as age. We discussed this issue with the clinicians and they suggested not weighing each measure differently in this study as the appropriate weights are debatable. This could be an interesting area of future research.

An additional area of future research would be the impact of orthosis on how therapies are provided to patients using assistive devices as well as the subsequent impacts on independence post-rehabilitation stay.

Finally, we note that this method, the causal forest method with DML, is a newer method that is not as mature as other traditional methods (eg, logistic regression). Therefore, we are also limited by the newness of this method and lack of consensus on how to best report results.

Conclusions

We find treatment effects for sessions (ie, therapy sessions) and certain medications to indeed vary by some stroke rehabilitation patient characteristics (ie, sex, age, race, stroke location, BMI, comorbidities, and admissions scores for outcome variables). We further found clinician involvement to be invaluable, as identifying actionable results requires more than just quantitative analyses. We also find that sessions (therapies) related to mental health, including psychotherapy and chaplaincy services, are associated with the highest uplift in independence by the end of inpatient rehabilitation stays. Thus, we suggest that (1) use of causal ML can provide an excellent basis for identifying mechanisms driving variations in the effects of rehabilitations treatments offered, and (2) including clinician assessment throughout the ML pipeline is essential for focusing on the results that have the most applicability and relevance.
