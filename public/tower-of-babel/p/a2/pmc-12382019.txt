Factors influencing willingness to perform cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and use an automated external defibrillator (AED) among non-healthcare community participants in a CPR fun run
Authors: Nur Izzati Mohd Hashim, Amsyar Daud, Azmawati Mohammed Nawi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOut-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) remains a leading cause of sudden cardiac death globally. Early bystander intervention using CPR and an AED significantly improves survival outcomes. This study aimed to assess willingness to perform CPR and use an AED among non-healthcare community participants in a CPR fun run, and to identify influencing factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in Melaka, Malaysia, between November 2021 and October 2022, involving 217 randomly selected participants who had previously taken part in the Melaka CPR Fun Run from 2018 to 2020. Data were collected using the validated FIXED questionnaire, covering sociodemographic, knowledge of CPR and AED, training status, perceptions regarding CPR and the use of an AED, attitudes, self-efficacy, subjective norms, and barriers.ResultsThe mean willingness score to perform CPR and use an AED was 33.14 ± 7.87 out of 40. Only three factors remained significantly associated with willingness: self-efficacy (β = 0.950, p < 0.001), perception (β = 0.569, p < 0.001), and barriers (β = −0.403, p < 0.001). Notably, for the subgroup analysis showed that participants who attended more CPR training sessions had better perception (r = 0.155, p = 0.022) and self-efficacy (r = 0.246, p < 0.001.ConclusionsParticipants demonstrated a high willingness to perform CPR and use an AED. Willingness was significantly influenced by self-efficacy, perception, and perceived barriers. Although CPR training did not directly affect willingness, it showed a weak but significant correlation with improved perception and self-efficacy. These findings highlight the need for interventions that enhance psychological readiness, beyond just increasing training frequency, to strengthen community response in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest situations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24412-6.Keywords: Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), Automated external defibrillator (AED), Willingness, Self-efficacy, Barriers, Community members

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of mortality globally, responsible for approximately 17.9 million deaths each year, accounting for about 32% of all global deaths [1]. Among the various forms of CVD, ischemic heart disease (IHD) is the most prevalent and prominent subtype. It contributes significantly to the global burden of disease, recording the highest age-standardised disability-adjusted life years (DALY) among cardiovascular conditions [2]. IHD affects males more than females (57.8% vs. 42.2%), and its increasing prevalence continues to place a public health concern on healthcare systems worldwide [2, 3].

In Malaysia, the situation is equally concerning. According to the Department of Statistics Malaysia, IHD has remained the leading cause of medically certified deaths, accounting for 16.1% of all mortalities in 2022 [4]. The National Cardiovascular Disease Database - Acute Coronary Syndrome (NCVD - ACS) Registry highlights that Malaysians experience myocardial infarction at a younger age, with an average of 58.6 years, compared to 63.4–68 years in high-income countries [4]. Even more worrying is the rising trend of premature cardiac deaths, particularly among individuals aged 41–59 years, who made up 20% of sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) deaths in 2022, higher than the 16.7% seen in those aged 60 and above [5]. These statistics underscore the urgent need for preventive public health interventions and greater awareness of early cardiovascular risk especially in the younger age.

One of the most devastating and crucial manifestations of CVD is Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest (OHCA), which contributes significantly to sudden cardiac deaths worldwide [6]. OHCA is defined as the sudden cessation of mechanical heart activity that occurs outside the hospital, leading to a lack of circulation and loss of signs of life. It often presents without warning and, unless treated immediately, results in death within minutes. According to the American Heart Association (AHA), more than 356,000 cases of OHCA occur annually in the United States alone, with survival rates as low as 5–12%, depending heavily on the speed of emergency response and public access to life saving measures [7, 8]. The most common cardiac rhythms in OHCA are ventricular fibrillation (VF) and pulseless ventricular tachycardia (VT), both of which are potentially reversible if prompt cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and defibrillation using an automated external defibrillator (AED) are administered [8].

Unfortunately, in many low- and middle-income countries, including Malaysia, survival rates from OCHA remain alarmingly low, largely due to delayed recognition, limited public awareness, and inadequate access to emergency services and AED [9–11]. OHCA is a time sensitive emergency, and without intervention, the chance of survival decreases by 7–10% for every minute that passes after collapse [8]. Studies have consistently shown that early bystander-initiated CPR can double or even quadruple survival chances, and early defibrillation with an AED can increase survival by up to 70% in shockable rhythms [12, 13]. Despite the well-established benefits of early intervention, global studies consistently show that public willingness to perform CPR or use an AED during out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) remains suboptimal across diverse populations and settings. A scoping review in Japan revealed that fewer than 50% of OHCA victims receive bystander CPR, with willingness hindered by emotional barriers, lack of training, and legal concerns [14]. Similarly, in China found that only 53.2% of residents were willing to perform CPR on strangers, largely due to fear of causing harm or being sued [15]. In Hong Kong, a study reported that only 44.8% were willing to perform CPR, and 47.8% to use an AED, with the lowest willingness seen in young adults [16]. Meanwhile, in Saudi Arabia highlighted limited CPR awareness and training among university students, impacting their willingness to act [17]. Furthermore, a study in Netherland emphasized that socioeconomic disparities contribute to reduced AED access and lower bystander response rates in low and middle-income communities [18]. These findings underscore the global need for broader public CPR and use of an AED education, legislative protection, and equitable AED distribution. While these studies provide valuable insights, most focus on general populations, and few explore the perspectives of trained non-healthcare community members.

Moreover, some factors can act as both facilitators and barriers; therefore, barriers were included as a key factor to better understand the participants’ willingness to perform CPR and use an AED. Addressing these sociodemographic, psychological factors and barriers are critical to improving OHCA survival in Malaysia. In light of these issues, this study aims to examine factors influencing willingness to perform CPR and use an AED among non-healthcare community participants in a CPR Fun Run.

Methodology

This study was conducted in the state of Melaka, located in southern Malaysia, which is administratively divided into three districts: Melaka Tengah, Alor Gajah, and Jasin. Melaka was selected as the study site due to its high and increasing incidence of OCHA and the critically low rate of bystander CPR [11]. Hospital Melaka, the only government specialist hospital in the state, serves as the main referral centre, with its Emergency and Trauma Department operating 24/7 as the front line of acute care. To raise public awareness about CPR and AED, and to promote healthy living, the department has organized the Melaka CPR Fun Run annually since 2018. This initiative was launched in Melaka as a central state-level effort to educate and engage the community in emergency response awareness.

A cross-sectional design was done between 1st November 2021 up to 31st October 2022 to assess the willingness to perform CPR and use an AED among non-healthcare community participants in a CPR Fun Run and to explore the influencing factors including knowledge, training status, attitude, subjective norms, self-efficacy and barriers. The study targeted adults who had participated in the Melaka CPR Fun Run held between 2018 and 2020. The study population included citizens aged 18 years and above residing in Melaka, who had previously participated in the Melaka CPR Fun Run during the years 2018 to 2020. Participants were identified from the official registration database of the event. Inclusion criteria included Malaysian nationality and the ability to understand Malay. Exclusion criteria were residence in Melaka for less than 6 months and healthcare workers.

A simple random sampling method was used, using a computer-generated random selection based on the official participant list of the CPR Fun Run event. The sample size was calculated using the Daniel Soper online calculator for multiple linear regression, because this study aimed to find out what factors influence people’s willingness to perform CPR and use an AED. Using a small-to-moderate effect size (f² = 0.10), a significance level of 0.05, power of 80%, and 20 influencing factors, the minimum required sample size was 153 participants. To allow for a possible dropout rate of up to 20%, the final recommended sample size was increased to 185 participants.

Two theoretical models were adapted in this study to understand the factors influencing the public’s willingness to perform CPR and use an AED. The first was Ajzen’s Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB), which is widely used to study health-related behaviours [19, 20]. TPB posits that a person’s intention to perform a behaviour is the strongest predictor of that behaviour. According to TPB, this intention is shaped by three main components: attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioural control, which in this study was represented as perception regarding CPR and the use of an AED. To complement this, the Intention-Focused Model for Bystander CPR Performance was also applied [21]. This model emphasizes behaviour change through targeted interventions such as education and awareness, suggesting that early CPR training can positively influence both attitude and self-efficacy.

A self-administered questionnaire, known as FIXED (Factors Influencing Community Willingness to Perform Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Use AED) [22] was used for data collection. The validated tool included various sections that applied different Likert scale formats to assess participants’ perceptions, attitudes, and confidence levels. A 4-point Likert scale was used for perception and barriers, a 7-point scale for attitude, subjective norms, and self-efficacy, and a 10-point scale for willingness to perform CPR and use an AED. Knowledge was assessed using Yes/No and multiple-response questions. The instrument was originally developed in English and translated into Malay using a standard forward—backward translation method, with content adapted to local cultural context. Validity and reliability testing were carried out. The overall Cronbach’s alpha of the validated Malay version was 0.875, indicating excellent internal consistency.

The questionnaire used in this study comprised five major sections. Section A focused on the participants’ sociodemographic and medical background, collecting data on age, gender, ethnicity, education level, occupation, marital status, residency, the presence of personal comorbidities and any family history of chronic illnesses among household members and also previous experience related to cardiac arrest events. Sections B, C, and E of the questionnaire were designed to assess various factors influencing participants’ willingness to perform CPR and use an AED. Section B assessed knowledge, Section C focused on training status and psychological factors, including perception regarding CPR and AED use, attitude, subjective norms, and self-efficacy, while Section E addressed the barriers. Section D, on the other hand, measured the participants’ willingness to perform CPR and use an AED. All the sections were summarized into the Table 1.

Data collection was conducted through both online platforms (via Google Forms) and in-person distribution. Participants were allowed to complete the questionnaire independently. Clear instructions were provided, and clarification was offered when needed, without influencing the responses. Completion time ranged from 15 to 30 min. All responses were anonymized, coded, and securely stored for analysis. Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant’s characteristics and study variables. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations. Normality of all continuous variables was assessed using the (Shapiro-Wilk test, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, histogram, and with skewness-kurtosis), and all were found to be approximately normally distributed. Therefore, the results were presented consistently using mean ± standard deviation (SD). Bivariable analysis was conducted using simple linear regression to explore the crude association between each independent variable and the willingness to perform CPR and use an AED. A p-value threshold of < 0.25 was used to select candidate variables for multivariable analysis. Variables that met these criteria were then entered into a multiple linear regression model. Backward elimination was applied to identify significant predictors and assess for confounding. The final model was evaluated at a significance level of p < 0.05. Results were reported as adjusted beta coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CI). No multicollinearity was detected (VIF < 10), and all model assumptions are met.

Results

A total of 217 non-healthcare community members participated in this study. Table 2 presents their sociodemographic characteristics, medical background, CPR training experience, and other related factors including knowledge, perception, attitudes, subjective norms, self-efficacy, and barriers that influencing the willingness to perform CPR and use an AED. The mean age was 35.37 ± 9.46 years, with a near-equal distribution of males (48.4%) and females (51.6%). The majority were Malay (83.9%), had tertiary education (71.9%), and were employed (77.4%). Most respondents lived in urban areas (60.8%) and had no comorbidities (91.2%). Only 7.4% had ever performed CPR, and 15.7% had witnessed a heart attack event. A family history of heart attack was reported in 24.9% of participants.

Knowledge regarding CPR and AED was poor among most respondents (81.6%). Based on the analysis of knowledge items, several questions were found to be associated with poor knowledge among respondents (see Supplementary File 1). Item 5, which assessed when an individual should perform CPR through multiple correct responses, revealed that only 2.8% of respondents identified all six correct answers, with most scoring three or fewer. For Item 6, which asked about the correct location to perform chest compressions, 70.5% answered correctly, leaving nearly 30% uncertain or incorrect. Item 11, which inquired whether respondents knew the location of an AED near their home or workplace, showed that only 48.4% responded “Yes,” indicating a substantial knowledge gap in AED accessibility. Lastly, Item 13, which asked whether respondents knew that AEDs provide voice instructions, found that only 75.3% answered correctly, while 24.7% were unaware of this basic AED feature.

The average number of CPR trainings attended was 2.26 ± 1.74, with the last session attended a mean of 24.17 months ago. The results also showed that participants had a high mean perception score regarding CPR and AED (36.49 ± 4.29 out of a maximum of 40). The attitude score was moderately high (39.29 ± 8.36, range 23–56). Meanwhile, the subjective norm score (21.91 ± 3.85 out of 28) and self-efficacy score (27.96 ± 4.47 out of 35) were also relatively high. However, the barriers score averaged 18.00 ± 6.19 (range 7–28), indicating a moderate level. Importantly, the mean willingness score was 33.14 ± 7.87 out of 40, indicating that most participants were quite willing to perform CPR and use an AED if needed.

Table 3 presents the bivariable analysis of factors associated with willingness to perform CPR and use an AED. Several factors met the threshold for inclusion in the multiple linear regression model (p < 0.25), including total number of CPR trainings sessions attended, perception regarding CPR and the use of an AED score, self-efficacy, subjective norm, and barriers score. While five variables were significant in the simple linear regression, only three factors; self-efficacy, perception, and barriers remained significant in the multiple linear regression after controlling the confounders.

In the final multivariable analysis (Table 4), three associated factors were significantly associated with willingness. Firstly, the perception regarding CPR and the use of an AED was significantly associated with willingness (β = 0.569, p < 0.001). This indicates that for every 1-point increase in the perception score, the willingness score increases by 0.569 points. In other words, the more individuals believe in the importance and usefulness of CPR and AED, the more willing they are to intervene during emergencies. Secondly, the self-efficacy score was significantly associated with willingness (β = 0.950, p < 0.001). For each 1-point increase in the self-efficacy score, the willingness score increases by 0.950 points. This indicates that the more confident individuals feel in their ability to perform CPR or use an AED correctly, the more likely they are to take action. Lastly, the barrier score in this study was negatively associated with willingness to perform CPR and use an AED (β = − 0.403, p < 0.001), indicating that for every 1-point increase in perceived barriers, the willingness score decreased by 0.403 points. This suggests that concerns such as fear of making mistakes or facing negative consequences may discourage individuals from taking action, even if they have the adequate knowledge or training. The most significant barrier identified was the fear of contractinginfectious diseases during CPR (mean = 2.88 ± 1.06), followed by worries about injuring the victim (2.77 ± 1.04) and legal consequences (2.65 ± 1.14), highlighting the importance of addressing these specific concerns to improve bystanders response in emergency situations (see Supplementary File 1). Following the multiple linear regression analysis, a sub-analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between the number of CPR training sessions and two final factors: perception and self-efficacy. Although the number of training sessions did not show a direct association with the willingness to perform CPR and use an AED, a weak positive correlation was found between training and both perception and self-efficacy. This suggests that CPR training may indirectly influence willingness by positively affecting these factors. As shown in Figs. 1 and 2, there was a statistically significant positive correlation between the number of CPR training sessions attended and perception score (r = 0.155, p = 0.022), as well as self-efficacy score (r = 0.246, p < 0.001). These results suggest that individuals who attended more CPR training sessions tended to have higher perception and self-efficacy scores related to CPR and the use of an AED.

Discussion

This study found that the mean willingness score to perform CPR and use an AED was 33.14 ± 7.87 on a 5–40 scale, indicating a generally high level of willingness among participants. This high score aligns with findings from a Taiwanese nationwide survey, where 86.7% of respondents reported willingness to perform CPR on strangers, provided they had the necessary skills [24]. The elevated willingness in the Taiwanese population may be attributed to several key factors, including high public awareness (90% had heard of CPR), a positive societal attitude toward the importance of CPR training (96.8% believed the public should learn CPR), and strong legal and educational motivators [24]. Similarly, in a Poland study involving medical students, a strong willingness was observed toward performing CPR for family members and known individuals, even though the willingness dropped when it came to strangers and children [25]. The high willingness in this study linked to their recent CPR training (over half had participated in training within the past year) and their hands-on experience, with more than 94% having practiced CPR on adult manikins [25].

However, a study in Hong Kong, only 44.8% were willing to perform CPR and 47.8% to use an AED, with the lowest willingness among young adults [16]. And a study in China found a slightly higher willingness rate at 61.3%, despite only 26.5% having prior CPR training [26]. Across both studies, willingness was significantly influenced not by knowledge levels alone, but by positive attitudes, perceived behavioural control, and social norms. Barriers such as fear of causing harm, fear of legal repercussions, and lack of confidence were consistently identified as major obstacles [16, 26].

Among the various factors investigated, only three were found to be statistically significant in this study. A strong positive perception toward CPR and AED use, with a mean perception score of 36.49 ± 4.29 out of a maximum of 40, indicating strong agreement on the importance, accessibility, and visibility of these CPR and AED. Specifically, a 1-point increase in perception score was associated with a 0.569-point increase in willingness score (β = 0.569, 95% CI: 0.334 to 0.803, p < 0.001), indicating that more high perceptions toward CPR and AED use significantly enhanced willingness to act. Similarly, a study in Perak among people without a medical background found a mean perception score of 20.41 ± 2.60 out of 25, showing a generally positive view toward CPR and AED use [27]. This is similar to a study in Lahore, Pakistan, where participants had a high perception score of 26.13 ± 3.84 out of 30 after receiving CPR training [28]. Although the scoring scales differ, the studies show a high score. The slightly higher perception in this study may be attributed to participants’ prior exposure to CPR awareness campaigns or community initiatives such as the CPR Fun Run. These findings suggest that the public in both settings recognizes the value of CPR and AED in emergency situations, supporting the need to further enhance this perception through sustained public education, improved AED visibility, and wider access to CPR training.

This study found that participants had a high level of confidence in their ability to help during a cardiac emergency, with a mean self-efficacy score of 27.96 ± 4.47 out of 35. Self-efficacy was the strongest influencing factors to willingness to perform CPR and use an AED (adjusted β = 0.950, p < 0.001). This means that the more confident a person felt, the more likely they were to take action in an emergency. A similar finding was reported in a study at China, who found that people with higher confidence in their CPR skills were more willing to help others during a cardiac arrest [29]. These results show that increasing public confidence through regular hands-on training and simple, clear instructions can make a big difference in encouraging people to act when it matters most.

Meanwhile, this study found that barriers score had a significant negative impact on willingness to perform CPR and use an AED, with an adjusted β of – 0.403 (p < 0.001). The mean barrier score was 18.00 ± 6.19, indicating a notable level of concern among participants. Common fears included legal consequences, disease transmission, and accidentally harming the victim were issues that often discourage bystanders from intervening during emergencies. These findings are consistent with other studies [19, 25, 27]. In Poland, many participants reported similar fears, particularly concerns about legal liability and lack of confidence in their ability to help effectively [25]. Likewise, in the Perak study, about 87.6% feared being sued, 82.4% were afraid of causing harm, and 67.4% worried about contracting disease, highlighting how these barriers are common across different populations [27]. Together, these results suggest that targeted education efforts and public reassurance such as promoting Good Samaritan laws and emphasizing safety in CPR delivery are crucial to reduce hesitation and empower more people to respond in emergencies.

Interestingly, this study found that the number of CPR training sessions attended had a statistically significant but weak positive correlation with both perception and self-efficacy. This suggests that while training does contribute to improved perception and confidence, the impact was relatively small. A possible explanation is that many participants had only attended one training session in the past, often more than two years ago, which may have limited their skill retention and confidence in performing CPR. Similarly, the Perak study reported that 69% of respondents had no formal Basic Life Support (BLS) training, and 79.7% did not know where to get CPR training, highlighting poor training coverage [27]. Another study also indicated that lack of recent or repeated training was linked with lower confidence and knowledge in CPR [19]. The finding also aligns with international evidence demonstrating that not only the presence of training, but also its recency and repetition, play critical roles. For instance, a study at South Korea found that individuals who had received CPR training within the previous two years were nearly twice as likely to express willingness to use an AED, underscoring the importance of recent exposure to training content [28]. In Saudi Arabia, reported that both the number of previous BLS trainings and the recency of training significantly give the positive attitudes toward CPR and AED use [30]. Furthermore, in Japan, the study mentioned with willingness to perform CPR increasing progressively with each additional session attended; notably, shorter intervals between trainings (< 1 year) were associated with significantly higher confidence levels even before the next training began [31]. These findings collectively suggest that one-off training is insufficient, and that ongoing training ideally annually is needed to maintain both competence and confidence over time.

In light of this, future CPR education strategies should also emphasize longitudinal training models, such as community-based refresher sessions, digital simulations, or school and workplace integrated programs, to enhance retention and practical readiness.

Addition, in this study, several participant’s characteristics were not significantly associated with the willingness to perform CPR and use an AED, including age, gender, marital status, education level, occupation, and previous exposure to cardiac arrest. These findings differ from several previous studies. For example, a study in Slovenian found that younger age, male gender, and higher education were significantly associated with greater willingness to act [19, 32]. A study in China also supported these findings, where younger age (mean 28.9 years, p = 0.014), male gender (p = 0.019), and tertiary education (p < 0.001) were all linked to higher willingness [34]. On the other hand, a study in Perak and one of international study found that gender and other demographic factors were not significantly associated with willingness, which is consistent with the present findings [19, 27]. These mixed results suggest that in this study population, many of whom had prior CPR exposure through community events like the CPR Fun Run had the influencing factors such as self-efficacy, perception, and barriers may play a more dominant role than demographic characteristics in determining willingness to perform CPR and use an AED.

Only 18.4% demonstrating good knowledge in this study. This gap in knowledge reflects similar findings in other Malaysian studies, where the overall mean knowledge score was rather low (7.9 ± 3.0) and 17.9% were showing good knowledge respectively [27, 34]. These findings suggesting that knowledge regarding CPR remains underemphasized in the general public despite its critical importance during cardiac emergencies. One possible reason for this persistent gap is the lack of consistent and practical CPR training opportunities, especially in non-healthcare settings [33, 34]. Public CPR campaigns may increase awareness but often do not provide hands-on practice or structured learning, leading to limited retention [27]. Additionally, training is often a one-time event, with few refresher sessions, causing knowledge to fade over time [20, 25, 35]. Cultural barriers, limited access to training centres, and low prioritization of first aid education in schools and workplaces may further contribute to poor CPR knowledge among the public [25, 36].

The importance of these findings is underscored when examined against global and national OHCA outcomes. Despite numerous training initiatives, actual CPR and AED engagement by the public remains low, contributing to poor survival outcomes. One of the studies analysing 40 years of global data found that among OHCA patients who received CPR, the overall rates for return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), survival to admission, and survival to discharge were 29.7%, 22.0%, and 8.8%, respectively [37]. Survival outcomes were significantly better with bystander CPR and showed improvement over time, though rates remained lower in Asian countries [37]. Globally, less than 40% of OHCA cases receive bystander CPR, and AED use before emergency medical services (EMS) arrive occurs in fewer than 12% of cases [38].

Meanwhile, in Malaysia, the statistics related to OHCA are concerning. Survival to admission rates in various Malaysian regions range from 1.3 to 6.7%, which are notably lower than in several other Asian countries. According to the Pan-Asian Resuscitation Outcomes Study (PAROS), only 22.6% of OHCA victims in Malaysia received CPR from bystanders, and public use of AED was nearly negligible [39]. Earlier PAROS findings reported even lower rates, with only 16–22% of OHCAs in Malaysia receiving bystander CPR [39]. While recent initiatives, such as national CPR training programs, have led to some improvement, with bystander CPR rates rising to an average of 38%, Malaysia still falls short compared to developed Asian nations like Singapore (50%) and South Korea (47%) [39]. Despite these gains, AED usage by the public remains exceedingly rare, estimated at just 1–2% of OHCA cases, indicating that even when AEDs are available, most bystanders do not retrieve or apply them [39]. The combined impact of low CPR and AED engagement results in poor survival outcomes with only a small amount of OHCA victims surviving to hospital discharge in Malaysia [40]. In contrast, Japan has achieved significant success in public-access defibrillation (PAD) through nationwide efforts involving AED deployment in schools and public spaces, supported by widespread training. In Japan, PAD programs have been associated with markedly higher survival and favourable neurological outcomes in public settings, as demonstrated by data showing that by 2015, over 87% of paediatric OHCA cases in schools involved AED use, resulting in a four-fold increase in survival with good neurological outcomes [41, 42]. As the implementation of PAD programmes in Malaysia differs significantly from that in other countries such as Japan particularly in terms of AED accessibility, public training, and infrastructure, the generalizability of the findings to other settings may be limited. These findings make it clear that training alone is not sufficient. While increasing CPR skills is important, greater emphasis must be placed on empowering the public with confidence and willingness to act, particularly in stressful emergency situations.

The use of self-reported data may also affect accuracy due to possible recall and information biases. This study focused on non-healthcare community participants who attended a CPR fun run event and were exposed to CPR training during the session. However, the absence of a comparison group comprising untrained individuals limits the ability to evaluate the true impact of training on willingness. A systematic review by a study found that trained individuals had significantly higher willingness scores than untrained individuals [43]. Additionally, a study among university students reported significant improvements in perception and confidence following structured CPR and AED training (p < 0.001) [44]. These findings underscore the value of including an untrained comparison group in future studies to better assessing the impact of CPR training on community willingness to perform CPR and use an AED.

Lastly, the study was conducted during the pre and pandemic recovery period of Covid-19, potentially affecting participant engagement and accessibility to training. A study in Poland reported that public access to AED devices and training was hindered due to movement restrictions and the closure of facilities during lockdowns, limiting the public’s opportunity to gain or refresh essential life-saving skills [45]. A cross-sectional survey in Toronto observed reduced public confidence and a decline in willingness to perform CPR, likely influenced by fewer recent training opportunities [46]. While a global systematic review and meta-analysis assessing OHCA incidence also noted that pre-hospital care practices were impacted during the pandemic, with altered response dynamics potentially reflecting reduced public preparedness and training exposure [47]. And supporting these findings, a study in Vietnam also reported that consistent, increase frequency in CPR training during the pandemic significantly improved knowledge, skills, and willingness to act during the emergency [48]. Collectively, these studies highlight the need for focus, adaptive training for CPR and use an AED to maintain the readiness during public health emergencies.

The urban focused setting may also restrict the generalizability of these findings to more rural or underserved populations. Nonetheless, this study adds to the growing evidence supporting the importance of self-efficacy and perception in predicting willingness to perform CPR and use an AED.

Despite its limitations, this study offers important insights into the psychosocial determinants of willingness to perform CPR and use an AED among non-healthcare community members. While overall willingness scores were moderately high, the most influential factors were perception, self-efficacy, and perceived barriers. Notably, the number of previous CPR trainings had only a weak association with these outcomes, suggesting that training alone may not be sufficient to enhance the willingness.

The findings underscore the need for comprehensive public health strategies that move beyond traditional training and incorporate confidence-building, risk communication, and behavioural reinforcement. Addressing psychological barriers and reinforcing public belief in their ability to intervene during emergencies are critical components of such interventions. Therefore, future efforts should focus on developing targeted programs that enhance self-efficacy and reduce fear-based barriers, particularly in urban working populations who are more likely to witness OCHA. Further research is also warranted to examine long term behavioural outcomes and the effectiveness of repeated, structured training in improving real world CPR response among laypersons.

Conclusion

This study identified self-efficacy, perception, and perceived barriers as the key psychosocial determinants influencing the willingness to perform CPR and use an AED among non-healthcare community members. The findings suggest that strengthen the psychological readiness may be more critical than simply increasing the frequency of CPR training. These insights support the development of targeted interventions to improve community responses during out-of-hospital cardiac arrest.
