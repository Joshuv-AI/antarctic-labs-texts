On the Overlooked Effect of Counter‐Cations From Borohydrides for the Gold‐ and Metal‐ Mediated Catalytic Reduction of 4‐Nitrophenol
Authors: Aleksandra Smolska, Nina Lock, Jonathan Quinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe catalytic reduction of 4‐nitrophenol to 4‐aminophenol is a widely used model reaction for water treatment and to evaluate the catalytic activity of nanomaterials. Here, we report on the strong, yet overlooked, effect of cations in the borohydride‐mediated reduction of 4‐nitrophenol using gold as a catalyst. Catalytic activity consistently increases in the order Li+ < Na+ << K+, with KBH4 leading to the highest turnovers compared to LiBH4 or NaBH4, both with pre‐formed gold nanoparticles but also using HAuCl4, AgNO3, or Pd(NO3)2.

Introduction

The catalytic reduction of 4‐nitrophenol (4‐NP) to 4‐aminophenol (4‐AP) is a well‐studied model reaction for water treatment, and selected worldwide to assess the catalytic performance of metal nanoparticles (NPs) [1, 2]. In particular, gold (Au) NPs are broadly used in heterogeneous catalysis, generally exhibit strong size‐dependent catalytic activity, and are frequently employed as catalysts for the 4‐NP reduction [3].

Extensive research has been conducted to understand the kinetics and mechanisms driving the catalytic reduction process of 4‐NP mediated by NaBH4 on various materials, and Au NPs in particular [4, 5, 6, 7]. These investigations have often employed the Langmuir‐Hinshelwood model to describe surface‐mediated reactions on metal NPs [3, 8], although the Eley‐Rideal mechanism has also been reported [9]. A recent study challenges this classical view, suggesting that the catalytic 4‐NP reduction follows an interfacial structural water‐dominated electron and proton transfer mechanism [10]. To optimize catalysts beyond active sites [11] and given the wide use of the 4‐NP reaction to benchmark Au NP activity, various factors influencing the reaction kinetics have also been explored along the years, including the catalyst concentration [12] composition [13, 14] size and morphology of the NPs [15], reactant molar ratio (NaBH4/4‐NP) [16], pH of the reaction medium [17], reaction temperature [18], the use of alternative reducing agents [19], or type of water [20], to name only a few examples.

Given the popularity of the 4‐NP reduction reaction in the scientific literature, a surprisingly non‐investigated variable is the counter‐cation X from XBH4. Cations are known to play a key role in catalysis. For instance, it is well established, and supported by both theoretical and experimental studies in electrocatalysis, that different cations in the electrolyte lead to different metal‐cation non‐covalent interactions that increase in strength in the order K+ < Na+ < Li+. In other words, the availability of the metal surface in the presence of cations is expected to increase in the order: Li+ < Na+ < K+. A higher surface availability is expected to lead to less energy‐demanding adsorption and desorption phenomena and thus to lead to higher catalytic activity in the increasing order: Li+ < Na+ < K+. This explains, for instance, the preferred use of KOH as electrolyte for a range of reactions in alkaline media [21]. This effect is anticipated to lead to favored adsorption/desorption of other molecules on metal surfaces and thus is a phenomenon expected to be relevant to consider in a range of catalytic reactions.

NaBH4 is the most commonly used and preferred reducing agent for the 4‐NP reduction, if not the exclusive borohydride‐based reducing agent used to date when it comes to Au‐catalysed reduction [21, 22]. We could only find rare examples where KBH4 was used as an alternative reducing agent and only for Ag‐based materials [23], and no examples with LiBH4, although it is expected to be a stronger reducing agent. Surprisingly, we could not find any unified and systematic investigation comparing the effect of using different alkali borohydrides, despite their expected differences in solubility, stability, and hydrolysis kinetics, all of which may significantly influence the overall reaction rate and mechanism. Possible cation effects for the 4‐NP reduction catalysed by Au have only been touched upon in two previous works, to the best of our knowledge. Ding et al. found that alkali metal cations (Li+, Na+, K+, Rb+, Cs+) influence the 4‐NP reduction rates by stabilizing catalytic intermediates [10]; Shirin et al. showed that Ca2+ outperforms Mg2+ due to stronger coordination interactions between the cation and 4‐NP molecules [24]. Both studies added different cations to NaBH4 that still acted as the main reducing agent for the 4‐NP.

Here, we investigate the effects of various borohydrides, namely LiBH4, NaBH4, and KBH4, on the reaction kinetics of the Au NPs‐catalysed reduction of 4‐NP. This work shows a strong influence of the cations on the performance of the Au‐mediated 4‐NP reduction and the potential benefits of tuning the cations to improve the catalytic performance.

Experimental Section

Chemicals

All chemicals were used as received: HAuCl4∙3H2O (99.9% Sigma–Aldrich or 99% BLD Pharmatech), ultrapure water (mQ, Milli‐Q, Millipore, resistivity of >18.2 MΩ cm), 4‐nitrophenol (4‐NP, ReagentPlus, ≥99%, Sigma–Aldrich), NaBH4 (ReagentPlus, 99%, Sigma–Aldrich), KBH4 (99.9%, Sigma–Aldrich), LiBH4 (95%, thermoscientific), nitrogen gas (3N, 99.9% purity), AgNO3 (99.9%, Sigma–Aldrich), Pd(NO3)2∙2H2O (>99.0%, Merck).

Au NP Synthesis

Au NPs were synthesised by adapting a previously reported protocol by Astruc and co‐workers [25], as a promising surfactant‐free colloidal synthesis approach relevant for catalysis [26]. Tetrachloroauric acid (HAuCl4) was reduced with sodium borohydride (NaBH4) in polystyrene UV–vis cuvettes (340–900 nm, VWR, 634–0675) at room temperature. The molar ratio of NaBH4/HAuCl4 varied from 4 to 15 to control particle size, and the Au NPs obtained varied from 5.5 ± 1.0 to 16.1 ± 5.2 nm. In all cases, the concentration of HAuCl4 used was 0.5 mm, and the final volume was 3 mL. Further details on the synthesis and size control are provided in recent work [27].

NP Characterization

Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) micrographs of Au NPs were acquired using FEI Talos F200X operated at 200 keV using a bright field detector. Au NP size distribution was determined by measuring 100 individual NPs with the ImageJ software.

4‐Nitrophenol Reduction

The reduction of 4‐NP to 4‐AP was followed in situ by UV–vis spectroscopy (Go Direct spectrophotometer) at room temperature. Each sample (2 mL total volume) contained 0.05 mm 4‐NP, 5 mm XBH4 (X = Li, Na, K; XBH4/4‐NP molar ratio of 100), and 0.00125 mm Au NPs (Au/4‐NP molar ratio of 2.5%). The reaction mixture was stirred at 550 rpm in sealed polystyrene cuvettes inside an LED‐lit photobox, and spectra were recorded every 40 s, starting after catalyst addition. The decrease of the 400 nm peak was fitted to pseudo‐first‐order kinetics to obtain rate constants (kapp), and turnover frequencies (TOF) were calculated. Full experimental details are provided in the Supporting Information and further details are given in previous literature [28].

Cation‐Dependent Catalytic Trends

The impact of the reducing agent was evaluated across a range of Au NP sizes. As expected from previous literature, the activity decreases with an increasing size [28, 29, 30, 31]. For each Au NP size, the 4‐NP reduction was carried out using three different borohydrides (XBH4, where X = Li, Na, K), and TOF values followed the increasing trend: Li+ < Na+ < K+. These results demonstrate a cation effect across various NP sizes, Figure 2, and the effect is more pronounced for the smallest NPs. This confirms our hypothesis that the reducing agent strongly influences the reaction kinetics. The reason why the smallest NPs show a more pronounced dependence on the cation is attributed to the generally higher catalytic activity due to a higher surface area for a given mass, which makes those smaller NPs more sensitive to the cation. It could also be that the smallest NPs show different structures that make the effect of the cation more pronounced. In any case, the results show that a cation effect is observed and that higher catalytic activity is obtained using KBH4 compared to NaBH4 or LiBH4.

Thus, the effect of the cation is more significant for the smallest NPs. To confirm further a cation effect on the Au‐mediated reduction of 4‐NP, a different strategy to perform the reaction was also investigated.

Catalysis Without Preformed Au NPs

Since borohydrides are used both for the synthesis of Au NPs from HAuCl4 and as reducing agents in the reduction of 4‐NP, we also investigated the catalytic reduction of 4‐NP performed directly from the gold precursor, without using pre‐synthesised Au NPs [25]. Irrespective of the chosen borohydride, the reaction proceeded significantly faster than in all experiments involving pre‐synthesized Au NPs and the highest turnover was obtained using KBH4. Figure 3 shows the TOF values obtained using different XBH4 (X = Li, Na, K) in the presence of HAuCl4 (the same amount of Au was used as in the previous experiments with pre‐formed NPs). Given the substantial excess of borohydride (100‐fold relative to 4‐NP), it is likely that Au NPs formed in situ during the reduction of HAuCl4, with sufficient XBH4 remaining to drive the subsequent reduction of 4‐NP [32]. This is consistent with established NP synthesis methods, where borohydrides, such as NaBH4, reduce gold precursors to form Au NPs [25]. The superior performance observed using KBH4 as a reducing agent in 4‐NP degradation is to be attributed to the nature of the counter‐cation, influencing the formation and/or catalytic activity of the resulting Au species, as per the case detailed in Figure 2, where pre‐formed NPs were used.

TOF values obtained for 4‐NP reduction with different borohydrides using HAuCl4 instead of pre‐formed Au NPs. Notably, TOF values are strongly dependent on the choice of HAuCl4 supplier (see Table S2 for details), but not on the trend that KBH4 leads to higher activity. Error bars represent standard deviations calculated from triplicates (n = 3).

Note that in this approach, the amount of Au NPs formed is too small to perform any further characterization. Based on our previous study [27], it would be expected that using LiBH4 would lead to smaller NPs compared to the use of NaBH4 or KBH4, especially at higher XBH4/Au molar ratios, as it is the case here. Given that smaller NPs are expected to be more active due to larger surface area, using LiBH4 would lead to larger TOF values. The fact that LiBH4 leads to the smallest TOF values is here to be related to a cation effect during the catalytic processes rather than a possible size effect on the NPs formed in situ. It is interesting to note that LiBH4 can be expected to be a stronger reducing agent, and yet, due to a stronger metal‐cation interaction, it leads here to lower catalytic activity.

A similar cation effect was observed using Ag and Pd precursors, where the catalytic activity increases with Li+ < Na+ < K+, Figure S4. These results suggest the broader relevance of the findings for different metal catalysts and possibly different catalytic reactions.

Discussion

The present results illustrate and confirm the effect of the cations. It is important to note that others showed that using NaBH4 and adding LiCl led to higher activity for the 4‐NP reduction compared to adding NaCl or KCl [10, 24]. However, different experimental conditions can make a direct comparison of the results challenging, as detailed in Table S4. In particular, in the direction to study cations effects, reports to date have preferred functionalized or supported NPs, mixed cations, and the strategy to introduce the cation leads to the addition of chloride anions. We here preferred a surfactant‐free approach and used XBH4 as the sole source of cations.

The results presented shine light on a new strategy to approach cations and anions effects and on the need for considering further the role of cations in future attempts to discriminate between the Langmuir‐Hinshelwood [3, 8], or Eley‐Rideal mechanism [9] for the gold‐mediated reduction of 4‐NP. A water‐assisted synergistic proton‐ and electron‐enhanced reaction kinetics was also proposed by Zhang's group [10, 33, 34]. In this approach, chloride anions play a key role to synergistically stabilise the transition state by acting as an anchoring point for the hydrated cations, functioning as a “water‐relay station” for activated proton transport. Without the anionic stabilisation in the present XBH4 system, the relay effect is likely not activated, allowing non‐covalent interactions (an active site blocking effect) to become the likely reason for the catalytic activity observed here.

A last possible interpretation of the results could be that LiBH4 undergoes hydrolysis much faster than NaBH4 or KBH4, which could lead to a lower effective concentration of LiBH4 that could then account for the lower activity observed. However, LiBH4 is here used in large excess, and hydrolysis alone would not explain why KBH4 leads to higher activity than NaBH4. Therefore, those observations tend to confirm a cation effect in the catalysed 4‐nitrophenol reduction.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the critical role of the alkali metal cation in the catalytic performance of the Au‐mediated reduction of 4‐NP. By performing the systematic comparison of three different borohydrides used as reducing agents (LiBH4, NaBH4, or KBH4) in the 4‐NP degradation, using a range of 7 different sizes of spherical Au NPs (from 5.5 ± 1.0 to 16.1 ± 5.2 nm) or directly using HAuCl4, AgNO3 or Pd(NO3)2, we show a consistent increased activity trend in the order Li+ < Na+ << K+. The choice of the cation leads to a significant change in TOF values for the smallest NPs in the reactions involving preformed Au NPs as well as in the case where metal precursors were used directly. For instance, switching from Li+ to K+ increases the average TOF value by ca. 60% to ca. 100% (for Au NPs, 5.5 ± 1.0 nm, and for HAuCl4‐mediated reaction, respectively), switching from Na+ to K+ increases the average TOF by ca. 55 or 50% (for Au NPs, 5.5 ± 1.0 nm, and for HAuCl4‐mediated reduction, respectively).

The results shine light on the overlooked influence of the counter‐cation from the borohydride for the model reaction that is the 4‐NP reduction. It is expected that similar effects will be observed for other borohydride‐mediated reactions. Tuning the cation has the potential to be a simple and widely applicable strategy for optimizing catalytic performance in NP‐driven catalysis for environmental purposes and beyond.
