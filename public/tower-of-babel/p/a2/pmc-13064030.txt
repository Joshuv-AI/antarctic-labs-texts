“Because this is my body”: patients’, families’ and health professionals’ perspectives on family-oriented informed consent in China
Authors: Jing-Ru Li, Simon Walker, Jing-Bao Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn China’s clinical settings, it is common for families to have the final say in informed consent when the patient has a terminal or critical illness, even when the patients are fully competent. This practice is different from the individual-based pattern in the majority of Western countries, which is grounded in respect for individual autonomy. One common justification of family-oriented informed consent (FOIC) is that Chinese patients prefer to have their families make decisions on their behalf, and this aligns with China’s familial culture, which emphasises relational autonomy. However, recent empirical findings show that FOIC is not as widely supported by Chinese patients as is often assumed. MethodsIn this study, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 13 Chinese patients, 14 family caregivers, and 9 health professionals in two megacities in northern China to explore their perceptions and attitudes of FOIC. Their experiences of and attitudes towards FOIC were explored. ResultsMany family and patient participants believed FOIC is a good way to preserve hope for patients and therefore supported the approach. However, when imagining themselves as patients, many of them preferred individual-based informed consent, citing a patient’s right and autonomy as the reasons. Healthcare professionals were conflicted on FOIC. They acknowledge the impact of familial culture but also mentioned practical factors that may equally contribute to FOIC. ConclusionThe interviews of this study and the literature indicate there are similarities between China and the West when it comes to family involvement in decision making which may have been overlooked or downplayed. The qualitative findings with participants reveal that there are other equally significant social-economic factors such as medical violence contributing to the prevalence of FOIC. Although FOIC is widespread in China, familial culture neither fully explains the practice nor provides an adequate justification for it.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01422-6.Keywords: Family-oriented informed consent, Familial culture, Individual-based informed consent, China, Thematic analysis

Background

In China’s clinical settings, families commonly play an active and authoritative role in informed consent. When the diagnosis of some critical or terminal illness is made, doctors normally will first inform the family members and discuss the treatment options with them. The family then will decide if they want the patient to be informed and part of the treatment discussion, and if so, in what way [1]. If family members have decided on what the best treatment or care plan is, they do not have to get specific consent from patients [1]. According to the first author’s personal and fieldwork experience, it is very common that if the family suspects the patient would reject the decision, they will exaggerate the benefits or downplay the risks of a particular treatment, so that they could manipulate the patient into accepting the treatment plan they choose. Nonetheless, partly due to the sensitivity of the topic, little empirical—qualitative or quantitative—literature exists to support this observation. This approach is variously referred to as family-oriented informed consent (FOIC), familial consent, and family consent in the literature [2–4]. It contrasts with what may be termed ‘individual informed consent’, whereby patients have the final say in informed consent. FOIC is practised in clinical settings in many Asian cultures [5–7]. It is different from shared decision making. In shared decision-making making patients are given control of decision-making process [8], whereas with FOIC, families have the final say.

The prevalence of FOIC in China has been indicated in several empirical studies. Two studies found that the majority (50.3%-80%) of surveyed Chinese cancer patients were not fully informed of the nature of their illness [9, 10]. Another found that when patients were informed, less than half (39.4%) were informed once the diagnosis was made, with most being informed much later [11]. A study of families’ preferences found a significant proportion (66%) of family caregivers prefer to hide information from patients [12]. Similarly, a study of Chinese doctors found that many (40.7%-65%) believe that truth-telling is not appropriate if patients have advanced-stage cancer, citing better compliance with treatment and mitigation of psychological distress as the main reasons [11, 13]. However, it has also been found that despite efforts made to prevent patients from knowing the truth about their condition and treatment options, a significant percentage (56.3–61.9%) discover it on their own [14, 15]. Studies have also revealed that 49%-70% of informed consent sheets are signed by families, whereas only 23%-30% are signed by patients, and 8.4% are co-signed by families and patients [16–18]. To the best of our knowledge, there is no study investigating who (the patient or the family) is the actual decision-maker in clinical practice. However, given the high number of patients who are not appropriately informed, it is reasonable to infer that the family-oriented approach of decision making is more common than the individual approach in practice, as a valid decision can only be made with full disclosure.

One common reason for FOIC, as explained by some scholars, is that Chinese culture is fundamentally different from Western culture, and that FOIC is compatible with Chinese culture. According to this view, Western culture prioritises individual autonomy because it has an ‘atomistic’ or individualistic understanding of personhood [6, 19, 20]. By contrast, in a Confucian society, humanity (仁, Ren) lies at the core of the moral system, which requires people to love each other [21]. This duty to humanity generates a set of regulations that rule the five basic human relations, which are called the five cardinal relationships (五伦, Wulun). They include the relations between father and son, ruler and minister, husband and wife, elder and younger, and friend and friend [22, 23]. On the basis of this, the concept of person is said to be constituted by relations to other people in Confucian culture [24], and this in turn leads to a relational or collective understanding of ‘autonomy’ [25]. Among the five relations, kinships are the most important ones [20, 26, 27], because in China, family “always carries a greater weight than individuals”. This, it is argued, means that within Chinese families, patients view a medical decision as a family matter that should be made together with the other family members, and family members recognise an obligation to take care of each other. Consequently, Chinese patients are willing to let their families make the decision on their behalf, and the family’s decision can override the patient’s when their interests are in conflict [19, 20, 25]. Ruiping Fan [20] argues that Chinese patients “take it for granted that it is the whole family, rather than the patient alone, that should make medical decisions”. In the same article, it is continued with the following statement that “the [Chinese] patient often does not want to engage in a serious conversation with [the] physician”, and “it is generally accepted that they should not be bothered by discussing issues with physicians directly”. In addition, some scholars argue that death is a taboo topic in China, and as such, telling the truth about a serious diagnosis or prognosis is discouraged or forbidden, as familial duty requires family members not to cause mental distress of any kind to the loved ones [28, 29].

However, in recent years, empirical studies have shown that FOIC is not as widely accepted by Chinese patients as previously assumed. In one study, the majority of the patients surveyed supported disclosure to patients [17]. Similarly, many patients (36.8%-42.4%) believe patients have the individual right to informed consent; 75.2% specify that they prefer to know the truth about their condition and treatment; and 58.2%-62.0% believe patients should be informed by doctors [11, 17, 18]. When it comes to who should be informed first, the patient group is divided: 49.3% prefer to be informed before families, while 47.6% expect families to be informed first [17]. Many patient participants in these studies disapprove of their family intervening in their medical decisions [12, 17, 18]. These findings suggest that FOIC may not be the most compatible approach with China’s social context, and that the desire for individual informed consent may have been largely overlooked under the current ethical and legal framework. In particular, there is a gap in the literature comparing the perspectives of Chinese patients, families, and health professionals on FOIC. Due to the sensitivity of this topic, patients’ voices are often absent from academic discourse—despite the fact that they should be central to it.

In this study, we interviewed Chinese patients, families, and health professionals to explore their perspectives and experiences of FOIC and to understand the reasons behind their views. The aim of this study is threefold. First, we aim to gather firsthand experiences and opinions from patients to provide a more comprehensive picture of FOIC in China. Second, by comparing the perspectives of patients, families, and health professionals, the study identifies the unmet needs of both laypeople and professionals. This leads to the third aim: building an empirical foundation for future ethical, legal, and policy research in this area.

Methods

This study reports data collected for a PhD thesis that integrated empirical methods and normative enquiry, exploring the practice, perceptions, and attitudes of FOIC and examining the main justifications for FOIC.

Eligible participants must be 18 and older and competent to provide consent. Eligible patient participants needed to meet the following criteria:

They were either consumers of health services when the interviews took place or had received health services before the interviews.

They were either consumers of health services when the interviews took place or had received health services before the interviews.

They were diagnosed with a critical or terminal illness.

They were diagnosed with a critical or terminal illness.

They had either experienced FOIC, were currently experiencing it, or had heard about others' experiences with it.

They had either experienced FOIC, were currently experiencing it, or had heard about others' experiences with it.

Family caregiver participants were eligible if they had provided care for a relative diagnosed with a terminal or critical illness. Health professional participants were eligible if they had experienced or implemented FOIC in their practice. Our aim was to only recruit patients with personal experience of FOIC. However, recruiting these participants was difficult, partly due to the sensitivity of the topic, and partly because, in some cases, it would require us to refrain from sharing medical details with patients during the interviews (such as the diagnosis). To avoid this problem, we recruited only patients who were initially unaware of their condition but had subsequently been informed about it before the interviews took place. We then determined that expanding the inclusion criteria to include patients who had heard of others’ experiences with FOIC could still contribute to achieving the study’s aims. While priority was given to patients and family caregivers from the same family, we also included those who were not related. Similarly, we preferred health professionals who provided care to the patient participants; however, this was not always possible.

Participants were recruited from Tianjin and Beijing between August and December 2018 by Jing-Ru Li (JRL). Tianjin and Beijing are heavily populated cities with well-resourced health care services. Selecting these two cities as the fieldwork site ensured participants from different cultural backgrounds and socio-economic statuses were recruited, thereby increasing the diversity of the sample. The snowball sampling method was employed, beginning with participants recruited through the first author’s professional networks, initially involving two healthcare professionals. These participants subsequently referred patients, who then introduced other patients and family members. When recruiting patients, we consulted the individuals who introduced them to us to confirm that the patients had already been informed of their diagnosis, thereby avoiding potential harm. All the interviews were conducted in Mandarin Chinese, the predominant language in China, by JRL. She subsequently translated and transcribed the quotes relevant to the topic into English.

The thematic analysis method was employed to examine the findings using NVivo 12. Thematic analysis is “a method for systematically identifying, organising, and offering insight into patterns of meaning (themes) across a data set”, which “allows the researcher to see and make sense of collective or shared meanings and experiences” [30]. Thematic analysis was selected for this study as it is suitable for revealing detailed and nuanced experiences and perspectives. We followed the six-step analysis proposed by Braun and Clarke [31]. Both semantic and latent meanings within participants’ responses were explored. This not only identified participants’ practices and the reasons behind them on a surface level but also revealed the underlying perceptions. We combined inductive and deductive methods in the data analysis. Inductive analysis is a data-driven method that is focused on discovering data from the findings rather than the pre-existing theories [31]. A deductive method, by contrast, is based on the “researcher’s theoretical or analytic interest in the area” [31]. During the literature review, we found that there is a limited number of qualitative studies on FOIC with Chinese patients. Therefore, inductive analysis facilitated the discovery of new qualitative findings on this topic. Deductive analysis was driven by existing theoretical discussions on FOIC, including various aspects of cultural difference arguments. The frequency of the themes was counted to identify key themes. When analysing the data, researchers were mindful of the potential inconsistencies and incoherence of the themes, mismatches between the data and themes, and excessive overlaps between themes, among other issues that may affect the quality of the analysis. The coding was repeatedly checked and reviewed by JRL. The codes and themes were reviewed by Simon Walker (SW) and Jing-Bao Nie (JBN), the other two authors, to mitigate inaccurate interpretation.

To mitigate any undue influence on participants’ perspectives, the interviewer used open-ended questions and allowed participants to express their views freely without prompting or leading. The thematic analysis was led by JRL but discussed with SW and JBN, whose diverse perspectives enabled a balanced interpretation.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the University of Otago (reference number H18/098). All the participants gave their oral informed consent prior to being enrolled to the study.

A total number of 13 patients, 14 family caregivers, and 9 health care professionals were recruited (see Table 1). Participant recruitment ended when there was no new themes were reported, and there was sufficient information to address the study aim. Patient participants are aged between 30 and 92, and the majority are female (11 out of 13). Three patients had not been truthfully informed initially but were truthfully informed prior to the interviews. The educational background of the 13 patients varied from primary school to a master’s degree. The 14 family caregivers were aged between 26 and 80, with 7 females and 7 males. The nine health care professionals (6 males and 3 females) were recruited from different specialties, ranging from nursing to anaesthesiology.

Results

The following themes are identified. They are low psychological resilience as the primary reason for not informing; some reasons for informing patients; Patient and family participants’ personal views about truth-telling; conflicting perspectives among health professionals; participants referred to individual rights; and cancer and death as taboo topics. In reporting the quotes, “P” is for “patient participant”, “F” is for “family caregiver participant”, and “H” is for “health care professional participant”.

Low psychological resilience as the primary reason for not informing

When asking about the attitudes towards FOIC as a caregiver, most of the patient and family participants advocated for FOIC. They explained this is because the majority of patients lack psychological resilience (心理承受能力, meaning the mental capacity to bear bad news). They were concerned that without psychological resilience bad news would cause psychological stress to patients and lead to adverse health outcomes.

I do not recommend truthfully informing the patients, regardless of the condition of the illness. If the patient cannot bear the bad news, withholding the truth is not a malicious act. (P3)

Some lay participants promoted using a case-by-case approach, depending on the patient’s level of psychological reliance. For example:

I believe it depends on the circumstances. One size doesn’t fit all. If the patient has strong psychological resilience and the capability to confront the things that follow, I think they should be informed. (P10)

A staged disclosure strategy was preferred and used by a few family participants. On this strategy, families revealed a bit of information to the patient at different stages of the treatment course, in order to minimise any potential psychological harm of truth telling.

Each time when she asked what illness she exactly had, we would reveal a bit information. Just like this, through these occasions, we gradually informed her. (F3)

Some reasons for informing patients

While most patients and family caregivers said patients should not be informed, a few specifically advocated for truthfully informing the patient. The common reasons included “respecting the ownership of one’s body”, “achieving better therapeutic benefits”, and “patients would find out anyway”, so it is better that they are properly prepared. For example:

It is good to know the truth. If you do not know you will not be able to handle subsequent events, you may not consent to the operation so easily. (P2)

I think when patients know the truth, they will get better treatment. Patients are not fools. They will know if their illness is severe after a period of time. (F6)

Patient and family participants’ personal views about truth-telling

Though the majority of patient and family participants were generally positive about FOIC when it applied to others, most had a different view when it was applied to themselves. Most said that if they were the patient, they would prefer to be informed and objected to the idea of FOIC. In explaining their reasons, the right to self-determination was commonly mentioned, along with therapeutic benefits, and psychological resilience.

I would like to know the truth. If [that happens to me] … if I don’t know I only have one week left to live, I probably will live my life as usual, my ordinary life. If I know [the truth], and I am very strong, I think I will try to finish the things I want to do. (P1)

If I am going to be the sick one, I hope that my family will give me more information. I hope they will not withhold too much information. I think I can take the bad news. (F3)

There were some apparent exceptions. A few patient participants indicated that they wish not to know the truth about their illness. They believed the bad news would be psychologically distressing and that this would negatively impact the effect of treatments.

No matter how curious I am about (the information regarding my health), if my husband believes I cannot handle it, he should not inform me. What is point of informing me of news that I cannot handle? (P3)

However, when asked further about these concerns, some of these patients revealed more nuanced positions. For example, P11 stated that she did not totally oppose being informed; instead, she just hoped to be informed “slowly, very slowly”.

In addition, P11 also believed the attitude the attending doctor takes when informing the patient is critical.

A few family participants appeared to be hesitant and stated it was difficult to give a certain answer as they could not predict what mood they would have when they were ill. F7 explained as follows:

It is not happening to me, so I cannot image it. Maybe I would have a breakdown. The most unpredictable thing is our future. No one can predict this. (F7)

I don’t know if I would be strong enough [to confront the bad news]. You don’t know what kind of mindset you will have, if that happens one day. (F14)

Two family participants consistently preferred non-disclosure, either as the family caregiver or as the patient. However, this view is complicated by the fact that they believed that patients would sense when their illness was incurable, or they were dying, and hence that explicit and formal disclosure would be unnecessary.

I would prefer to stay ignorant. I prefer not to know the truth. [Whether I know or not], I am going to die. If they [doctors] can treat you, why don’t they? [If they refuse to treat you, ] that must mean your illness is incurable. (F1)

Conflicting perspectives among health professionals

Many health care professional participants have a conflicted view on FOIC. Most health care professionals strongly supported individual informed consent, citing the benefits of truthfully informing patients and respecting a patient’s right to self-determination (See findings in Section “Participants referred to individual rights”). However, they also admitted that individual informed consent would face resistance from the family. Several interviewees reported that when a dispute over a treatment decision occurred between the patient and the family, most Chinese doctors let the patient and the family talk through their disputes by themselves, according to their observations and experiences. Some doctors considered these disagreements family affairs, which should be resolved within the family. A lot of medical decisions would not only affect the patient’s health and wellbeing, but also change individual family members’ future lives, and the future plans of the whole family. In light of this, the family’s opinions should be taken into account:

I think that community, clan, family, and broader social connections are important to Chinese people. We play diverse and complicated roles in society. I have a strong experience that when a person is sick, it is not just their personal business. When a person is sick, it becomes the family’s business. So, doctors cannot simply disregard all the recommendations and opinions of the family. (H4)

I asked the patient to sign the informed consent document for anaesthesia. The patient said: ‘I … You’d better ask my family to sign the document.’ The patient felt he could not take the responsibility for the decision. For example, if the patient is going to have a gynaecological operation, the patient will ask me to inform her husband [about the risks of the operation], because the decision might concern their fertility plan. (H7)

Some health care professionals believed that Chinese laws and legal regulations granted families ultimate legal power in informed consent, which contributes to the prevalence of FOIC. For example, one said:

After all we are just health care practitioners. The final say is in the family’s hand. For example, in the Chaoyang Hospital case, doctors did not perform [the procedure] in the end. Because they believed the family had the right to decision making which was authorised by the law. (H9)

The participant was referring to a famous case. In 2007, a pregnant woman named Liyun Li was admitted to Chaoyang Hospital in Beijing by Zhijun Xiao, who claimed to be her husband. Following a medical examination, doctors recommended an emergency Caesarean section to save Li’s life. Although they attempted to obtain consent from Li, she quickly fell into a coma. Xiao refused to authorise the procedure, believing it was unnecessary. The doctors were told by the former Beijing Municipal Bureau of Health that, without the family’s consent, they should not legally proceed with the surgery, which ultimately resulted in Li’s death.

Another frequently mentioned factor is medical disputes and violence. Many health care professionals explained that the growing mistrust between patients and doctors has led to numerous medical disputes and cases of perpetuating violence against medical staff. Health care professionals often feel the need to protect themselves and avoid conflicts or disputes with the patient’s family. This concern for their own safety means doctors defer to the family’s decisions.

I will never directly inform the patient. Even if I have the intention of informing the patient, I will first get to know the family and see what they think. I will think, if I inform the family, what risks I will undertake. The family may create trouble for me if I inform the patient without their consent. I can’t bear that. Sometimes the family has already had arguments with the patient, and so they will vent their anger towards me, not the patient. (H6)

Financial loss for hospital departments was reported as a further common practical factor driving FOIC. In most Chinese hospitals, departments take full responsibility for their own profits and losses. Revenue generated from treatment and medication was given out to the doctors and nurses in the department as a bonus. As many Chinese patients do not have medical insurance, their family is required to pay for the treatment. Consequently, doctors feel they must obtain the family’s consent to the treatment, because if they do not, the family would not pay the bill. If the family did not pay the bill, expenses incurred from the treatment would be paid by the department, and any bonuses for the doctors and nurses would be reduced as a whole. This consideration pushed many doctors to prioritise the interests of the department by following the family’s decisions. H3 explained the situation as follows:

The family’s consent is equally important in China’s current conditions. Many patients don’t have medical insurance. They have to rely on their family to pay the bill. [Also], when disputes occur, the family caregivers are the ones we have to confront. So, it is important to obtain their consent. (H3)

Similarly, H2 remarks:

Even if doctors can provide emergency treatment, the law does not say who should pay the expenses. This situation can lead to the department having to pay the expenses. If this situation becomes a custom, this discourages doctors from [providing treatments to patients without their family’s consent]. You cannot do a good deed and then let the other [colleagues in the department] share the expenses with you. This collective pressure will push you not to do good deeds. (H2)

However, there were also reservations among health professionals regarding whether FOIC is truly beneficial to patients, and whether families consistently prioritise the patient’s interests. H6 commented on how some family members are unwilling to take responsibility for payment:

This is a very common phenomenon in rural areas. The children of the patient pass the buck, especially in a family with three or four sons, over who should pay for the treatment. They all think the others should pay. In the end, no one wants to pay for the costs, and the elderly patient dies. The money in dispute can be just around 1,000 CNY (equivalent to 137.01 USD). (H6)

H8 expressed doubts about the impact of informing patients:

I feel like, for this patient, if he had not been informed at the beginning, what would have happened to him? If I had not informed him, would his illness have progressed slower? If I tell patients the truth, and their lifespan gets shorter [due to psychological distress], there will be many things left undone. … I am conflicted about this. (H8)

On the other hand, H5 observed that some patients can be overwhelmed by news of a terminal diagnosis:

For those patients who have a low education level and do not have the capability to understand the information delivered to them, if you tell them they will die within three months, they will be very fearful and worried. The fear and the worries will linger in their mind. (H5)

Participants referred to individual rights

A majority in the patient and health professional groups used rights-based language when explaining their perspectives on medical truth-telling and informed consent. Many patient participants indicated clear support for the notion of self-determination, and some viewed this as a right in the self-regarding sense. Accordingly, they believed patients had the right to be truthfully informed and to make medical decisions. For example:

Patients can decide for themselves. Why shouldn’t they decide for themselves? [Patients] have the right to [decide for themselves]. They surely have that right. (P9)

I am the patient. I have the right to be informed. (P6)

Patients have the right to make their own decisions. (P11)

Of course, I would want to know. Everyone has different psychological resilience. I would [prefer to] make my own decisions. Don’t let others decide your fate. (F05)

Of course, patients have the right to informed consent. (H9)

Some patients understood the right to informed consent as reflecting a person’s ownership of their own body and life. They considered this ownership to grant a person authority over decisions pertaining to them. P10 explained that she did not let her husband intervene in her medical decisions because she is her own master, and she knows herself better than anyone else. She did not want to rely on anyone, even her family. Even though P10 valued her marriage, she did not believe her husband should make medical decisions for her. She believed that ultimately people should be independent agents who set their own course in life, and hence that patients should make their medical decisions for themselves.

Some patients emphasised the consequences of medical decisions and explained that since patients would suffer the consequences of any treatment, they should have the final say in decisions that would affect their own health and future life. For example, P1 asked the following rhetorical question:

Because the [surgical] knife would be used on my body, why shouldn’t I make the decision? (P1)

Similarly, P5 remarked:

Patients should have the final say. No matter if [the operation] goes well or not, we will bear the consequences, right? (P5)

Several health care professionals expressed strong support for the idea of respecting patients’ rights to self-determination. For example, H5 explained why patients should have this right with a short statement, as if it is a matter of fact: “Because that’s his life”. H4 expressed a similar view using the method of delivery in childbirth as an example:

If the pregnant woman insists on having a natural birth, and the woman has the decision-making capacity, the woman’s right should be respected. (H4)

H2 put the same point in general terms, saying “It’s best to let the person govern their right to make decisions”.

One medical professional noted that the importance of respecting a patient’s choices extends to not informing them, if that is what they decide:

I don’t think it is possible for the patient not to know what illness they have, unless they were being deliberately obtuse. They should be crystal clear about their illness. They are just avoiding (the truth). If that is the case, we should not inform them. (H3)

There was also a belief that China’s informed consent laws actually protected patients’ best interests. This means that families do not always have the authority in making medical decisions, and if the doctor believes the family’s decision could cause harm to the patient, doctors should intervene and perform the procedures without obtaining the family’s consent. H2 expressed this belief in the following:

China has a legal stipulation that doctors have the right to decide whether to provide medical intervention. If the doctor believes the intervention was more beneficial [than the family’s treatment choice], the doctor should use that right to protect the patient’s interests to the fullest extent. (H2)

Compared with patient and health care participants, the notion of individual rights was mentioned much less often by family participants in the other-regarding sense. However, as noted in the previous section, when answering as if they were the patients, the majority of family participants stated it was important to respect the patient’s self-determination. In other words, if they were the patients, they would prefer to have full control over the information and decision-making (even though this was not necessarily the case when they are the family members of patients). F5 said:

First of all, we should decide for ourselves. You should not let other people determine your destiny. (F5)

F6 had a similar view:

As the person concerned, I hope I have the most knowledge of my illness. I don’t want my family to know more about my illness than me. (F6)

Two family participants believed the context and details were significant in determining whether a patient should be informed; however, they said that when the decisions are a matter of life and death, patients should be able to decide. F11 and F14 said:

Because the patient is the party involved, they have the right to decide whether to get treated or not, and to choose life or death. (F11)

Patients have the right to choose treatment when it is a matter of life and death. (F14)

Interestingly, when asking several family participants if they would be upset when patients decided to keep the truth to themselves, they all said they would accept it. They understood the decision as the patient’s personal matter and respected the patient’s choice. F9 explained this as follows:

It was very normal that [she did not tell me about her condition]. If it had not been very serious, and I had not wanted to worry her, I would not have told her. Or maybe if it had been very serious, [and even if I had told her, ] it would not have changed anything. I would not tell her either. (F9)

There were only a few family participants who maintained that they had the right to be informed. Moreover, even within this group, they did not believe this right was absolute, and that priority should be given to safeguarding the patient’s well-being and welfare. F5 said:

Usually, the family cares about the patient and hopes to get involved in [the decision making]. They have the right to be informed. However, I think we should not take an absolute view on a lot of things. The primary obligation of a hospital is to treat the illness and save the patient’s life. They should not refuse to provide treatment to the patient because the family does not show up. This will lead to a delay in treatment. (F5)

Cancer and death as taboo topics

On the topic of death and bad news as taboos, cancer patients in this study commented that they would avoid discussing their illness unless necessary, such as when talking about treatment and care plans. Even when they discussed the treatment plans, the family and the patient would avoid using the word “cancer”. For example, they might use “tumour” to replace the word, as F9 described in the following:

We would rather say tumour than cancer. Ever since we were young, we have known cancer means death and no cure. This notion is deeply rooted among the people. People avoid using the word. (F9)

One patient participant (P1), whose mother had cancer a few years ago, explained in this way:

Cancer is taboo to my mom. My mom had cancer before, and her teacher had died from cancer. When some of her friends got cancer, she did not even want to visit them in the hospital. I don’t know how to describe that feeling. When we had to discuss the treatment options, we would talk about these things. But as soon as the operation was done, we talked no more about it. It feels like, if you don’t talk about it, it never happened. (P1)

The fear of death and the shame of illness were two other reasons why many patients did not want to talk about their illness. For example, P8 did not like talking about her illness when she was unwell. To her, those conversations brought thoughts of death to mind. P9 felt that when others brought up the topic, they must have talked about her behind her back:

I didn’t feel like talking about my illness [when I was sick]. The pain got worse and worse as the treatment went on. The operation was okay, but the side effects of chemotherapy… When your body was not well, your mood went bad too. When you were unwell, [the conversations about the illness] were essentially about death. (P8)

Even if someone knew about my illness, they never talked about it with me, not even my family. It is okay if I bring it up, but other people [should not bring it up]… I felt like people talked about me behind my back. Many people had this feeling. (P9)

Two family participants’ experiences of Chinese-Western cultural differences

A few participants reported perceived cultural differences between Chinese and Western clinical practices regarding informed consent. Some family members had received informed consent as caregivers in both China and the West. These participants described feeling included in the informed consent process and respected by the doctors in the West. Their experiences did not fit with the view that ‘individual’ informed consent as supposedly practiced in the West excludes families. The main difference they noticed between Chinese and Western practice was that patients in the Western contexts were given priority and authority in the informed consent process, whereas in China, families mostly made decisions in the patients’ absence.

One family participant reported that when they received the initial diagnostic report at the Chinese hospital, the doctor called her daughter out for a private talk and broke the bad news only to the family. However, after they returned to New Zealand for another clinical consultation, the doctor in the New Zealand hospital straightforwardly told the daughter that the patient had a right to informed consent. When the daughter proposed withholding the truth from the patient for the moment, the doctor rejected the proposal, as non-disclosure would be against the law. Nonetheless, they also reported that in their experience in New Zealand, families were still involved in the informed consent process, noting that the New Zealand doctor spent a lot of time addressing the daughter’s concerns as the family caregiver. She was grateful that she was included in the decision-making process.

A similar story was shared by another family participant (F2) whose adult daughter had obstetric examinations at an American hospital years ago. The attending doctor arranged a private meeting with the grandmother to explain the situation and talk through her questions:

Her obstetric doctor was a very attentive and warm person who made you feel at ease. We had great talks. There was one time, she called me into her office after my daughter’s obstetric examination. I thought she discovered something bad from the examination. I told her she can be upfront with me. She said, everything was fine. She knew it was my daughter’s first pregnancy, and she guessed I must have a lot of worries as a mother. She wanted to know if I had any worries, so that she could explain to me. I was so flattered. She smiled and said: “I understand your worries as a mom. That is why the most important thing is to let you be relieved.” (F2).

Discussion

When asking patients and families about their opinions as caregivers, the majority advocated for a case-by-case approach, assuming that non-disclosure would be suitable in most situations, and in this respect, they supported FOIC. Most of these participants referred to ‘psychological resilience’ as their main reason for supporting FOIC. Some qualitative studies have also reported that Chinese families withhold medical information from patients in order to prevent potential psychological distress [32, 33]. It appears that ‘keeping hope’ is widely believed to be important for achieving better therapeutic outcomes, and that patients who are more hopeful about their illness and prognosis have a better chance of recovery, or recover more quickly. This is why some believe that doctors should disclose bad news only to families. The participants in this study also described another approach to reducing psychological distress: the method of ‘staged disclosure’. In this approach, families would reveal only the information that they believed patients could handle at the time. When they felt that patients were ready for more stressful news, they would share a bit more information with them. In doing so, patients would not be overwhelmed by all the bad news at once. This approach has also been called “step-by-step” method in other studies [32, 34].

However, when considering the same question with themselves in the role of patients, most participants—both patients and families—expressed a strong preference for individual informed consent, citing personal rights as the reason. Patient and family participants’ perceptions of personal rights and associated notions were not straightforward. These findings are consistent with the quantitative studies, which indicate a strong belief in self-determination among Chinese patients [18, 35], but provide insight into the nature of this belief and the reasoning behind it. They challenge the claims that Chinese people are unfamiliar with the concept of individual autonomy and do not care about the right to informed consent. Further, the ways that lay participants expressed this belief are compatible with the common meanings of autonomy in the West. Beauchamp and Childress [36] explain that ‘autonomy’ can have a range of meanings, including “self-governance, liberty, rights, privacy, individual choice, freedom of the will, ‘causing one’s behaviour’, and ‘being one’s own person’”. Some of these phrases, such as ‘rights’, ‘my body’, and ‘one’s own life’, were used by lay participants.

Although China is often considered an authoritarian, collectivist and familistic country, some scholars point out that Chinese society is witnessing a rise of individualism and a decline in familism [37, 38]. The traditional familial culture is being challenged by the emergence of a trend towards individualisation. Yan [39] observes that more and more young Chinese people are acquiring more freedom than before, and their relationships with their parents are changing accordingly. As a result, the pivotal aspect of “self-sacrifice” in traditional filial piety is fading, which symbolises the decline of familism. Commenting on the place of personal rights, including the right to be informed, in contemporary China, Nie [40] has argued that concepts of human rights are deeply rooted in China’s intellectual life, political society and medical practice, going back to the late Qing dynasty. Discussions on the relevant concepts can be found in Meng Zi’s works. Nie concludes that the claim that “a patients’ rights approach is culturally incompatible to China has significantly oversimplified the great richness and future potential of indigenous Chinese cultural traditions”. This suggests that the value placed on autonomy, rights, and informed consent by participants of this study is not simply acquired from Western thinking and practice but has origins in Chinese culture.

The reported experiences in this study and scholarly findings suggest there are similarities between China and the West when it comes to family involvement in decision making, which may have been overlooked or downplayed. While in the West the final decision legally rests with the patient, a preference for family involvement is shared by many Western patients and their families. It has been reported that Western patients often take their family’s interests into consideration when making medical decisions [41], and that Western families also expect to take part in the discussion about a patients’ future medical and care planning [42]. Studies involving Western patients prefer to make medical decisions in conjunction with their family [43]. Gilbar’s [44] interview study with patients and relatives in England found that some families take an active role in decision-making. They engaged in direct communication with doctors, asked critical questions, and weighed the pros and cons of treatment options. In some cases, relatives’ opinions had a substantial influence on patients’ decisions. One study reports that 70% (35 out of 50) of surveyed German patients believed families should have a say in medical decision-making [45]. The clinicians in a qualitative study in the United States said they struggled to ensure the level of family participation matches the patient’s preference, and the patient-family disagreements were often challenging for them to navigate, which echoes Chinese health care professionals’ perceptions and experiences in this study [46]. A survey study involving 2,031 participants across seven East-West countries reveals that the stereotyped culture differences do not have as significant an impact on people’s preferences for family involvement as is often assumed; rather, individual differences appear to be more influential [5]. The authors conclude that assuming individuals from certain cultures prefer FOIC “could obscure important individual differences within each culture” [5].

The health professional participants in this study conveyed considerable ambivalence towards the morality of FOIC. While many admitted that they supported patients’ right to make their own decisions, citing the benefits of informing patients and one’s moral and legal right to self-determination, but they also explained the practical challenges when practising this approach, as families may not approve, potentially leading them into trouble. The absence of trust has been identified as the most serious problem facing doctor-patient relations in China [38]. The level of trust between patients and doctors has rapidly declined over the past two decades. In recent years, the number of cases involving medical violence and medical malpractice lawsuits has increased dramatically. There have been growing numbers of what are called yi nao shijian. Yi nao (医闹) or “medical mobs” are people who use illicit acts, such as intimidating health professionals or damaging property in hospitals, in order to pressure the hospital into giving compensation during medical malpractice disputes. A 2014 study showed that 60% of the health care professionals surveyed have suffered verbal abuse from patients and patients’ families, and 13% have experienced physical assault [47]. As a matter of fact, because of the increasing cases of yi nao and medical violence, the government had to issue a law to stipulate yi nao as a criminal act [48].

Some professional participants mentioned the law as another factor in their ambivalence towards FOIC. The literature shows a major gap in China’s legislation on informed consent. Article 1219 of the Civil Code (民法典) allows doctors to inform the family instead of the patient if they believe it is not appropriate to inform patients. However, the law does not clearly define when it is not “appropriate” to inform a patient. This lack of clarity provides doctors with a way to bypass the ordinary requirements of informed consent. Furthermore, it does not state what should happen when there is a disagreement between a competent patient’s decision and their family’s wishes, leaving professionals uncertain about whose choice should prevail. Together with the other practical factors mentioned in the interviews, it is easy to understand why health care professionals may feel reluctant to promote FOIC in practice. The participants concern about mistrust and medical violence suggests that reasons other than familial culture play an important, if not primary, role in driving FOIC.

Health professionals also expressed reservations about the benefits of truth-telling, explaining that some patients may not be fit to be fully informed. Lay participants similarly expressed concerns about the harms of individual informed consent, especially on the patient’s psychological health. On this question, several studies in Hong Kong and Taiwan, albeit limited in number, have been conducted to explore the benefits of individual informed consent and truth telling, and they consistently find that truthful disclosure is beneficial to participants [49–51]. Further research is needed to understand the benefits of individual informed consent on Chinese patients.

The perceived cultural differences in informed consent between China and the West was an important finding. While participants did describe differences between Chinese and Western practices, they also noted that these did not follow the typical patterns, and in some respects they overlap. These perceptions indicate that cultural differences may not be as radical as they are often portrayed in the literature (see examples in the Background). Rather, while culture (including the social, political and historical context) certainly influences practice, there are variations in how a model of practice such as ‘individual informed consent’ can develop and be expressed. As expounded by Nie (41, 53), the radical contrast between collective and familial Chinese culture and individualistic Western culture is largely mythical, but it is a myth widely believed in China and elsewhere. In light of this, the references to taboo culture should not be taken as a general endorsement of FOIC. Rather, the finding can be taken as indicating the importance of recognising the diverse preferences and needs regarding the practice of informed consent among patients. As commented by a participant, “one size does not fit all”.

The findings have informed our theoretical understanding of relational versus individual autonomy, which we intend to develop further in a separate publication. This holds significant relevance for other cross-cultural discussions in bioethics. It is important that any examination of cultural differences be evidence-based, meaning that it should draw on empirical findings. Likewise, it is essential to look beyond apparent cultural differences and explore their underlying causes, as well as identify voices that may have been overlooked.

Strengths and limitations of this study

Most participants are from northern China. Patients and families from Southern China and provinces with unique minority cultures are less represented. The majority of the patient participants are female (9 out of 10), which means males’ voices are less represented. However, as this is a qualitative study, our aim is not to show how Chinese people in general perceive FOIC. Instead, our aim is to uncover and discuss the nuances and underlying details behind the common understanding and arguments about the FOIC practice. We believe this aim has been achieved in this study.

The fieldwork was conducted in 2018, and the literature review for this study has been kept up to date. We have not identified any significant changes in the practice of FOIC in the recent literature, and therefore believe our empirical findings remain both relevant and innovative in this area. Future studies should further explore the influencing factors on participants’ attitudes towards FOIC, such as China’s medical education on informed consent.

Conclusion

It has been widely held that Chinese patients prefer FOIC and do not value informed consent, i.e. being provided with information about their condition or treatment, and being the final authority in treatment decisions. This study shows that the values of autonomy and individual rights are recognised by some patient and family participants. In explaining these values, one participant vividly stated, “Because this is my body.” Our normative analysis suggests that the values of individual rights and autonomy, and the norms relating to them, have cultural and social roots in China, and that they are becoming increasingly important in contemporary Chinese society. The varied preferences of informed consent expressed by patients in this study and the literature suggest that FOIC may not always be supported by most Chinese patients, and therefore, it is questionable to claim FOIC as the most compatible consent approach in China’s clinical practice. The claim that there are radical cultural differences in respect of informed consent between China and the West is likely mistaken. Though FOIC is prevalent in China, the findings of this study revealed that practical factors are often neglected in the discussions of FOIC, which suggests cultural factors may not be the predominant factor as often assumed. Future studies should focus on comparing the benefits and harms of FOIC and individual informed consent, among other issues.

More generally, for theories and methodologies of transcultural medical ethics, this empirically-based ethical study demonstrates that the deeply rooted and popular ways of dichotomising cultures as radical others are empirically dubious, normatively questionable, and socio-politically problematic. Moreover, it underscores the need and contributes to developing more nuanced approaches to cultural issues in health care, approaches that adequately appreciate the great complexity and internal diversity of each culture and recognise not only cross-cultural differences but also transcultural similarities, and the potential to find common ground.
