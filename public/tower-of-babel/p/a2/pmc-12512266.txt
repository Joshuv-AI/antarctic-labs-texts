Isolation and genetic characterization of parvovirus from Bengal tiger in China
Authors: Ye Wang, Shuyi Han, Yi Li, Guohui Yuan, Yanan Xing, Wanqian Chen, Mengyan Cheng, Hongxuan He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCanine parvovirus (CPV) poses an emerging threat to endangered felids, yet its epidemiology and evolutionary dynamics in tigers remain poorly characterized.MethodsPathogen detection was performed through necropsy, colloidal gold assays, and PCR on a deceased Bengal tiger, followed by genetic and molecular characterization of the identified CPV.ResultsThis study isolated a CPV-2c strain (HNSY-031) from captive Bengal tigers. Genetic analysis revealed its closest relationship to Vietnamese canine parvoviruses, with 100% identity to CPV strains circulating in China and Vietnam since 2020. These findings highlight the necessity of implementing comprehensive biosecurity protocols around wildlife facilities, expanding vaccination programs for animals in surrounding regions, and establishing ongoing surveillance systems to monitor potential sources of pathogen exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-025-02952-y.Keywords: CPV, Bengal tiger, VP2, Mutation, Isolation, PCR, Phylogeny

Introduction

Tigers play a vital role in maintaining ecological balance and biodiversity. However, tigers face severe population threats including habitat fragmentation, illegal poaching, and human-wildlife conflicts. Recent surveys indicate that the wild tiger population is approximately 5,575 (https://www.worldwildlife.org/species/tiger). Strikingly, the number of tigers in captivity has reached 15,000–20,000 [1]. Captive breeding programs have aided population recovery, but tigers remain highly vulnerable to infectious diseases. For example, encephalomyocarditis virus [2], influenza virus [3–5], canine distemper virus (CDV) [5] and canine parvovirus (CPV) [6].

CPV, a member of the family Parvoviridae, subfamily Parvovirinae, genus Protoparvovirus, and classified under the species Carnivore protoparvovirus 1, is a significant pathogen that causes hemorrhagic enteritis and myocarditis in canines. Its genome consists of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA), which exhibits high genetic variability and remarkable host adaptability. VP2 is the primary protein determining the host range of parvoviruses and mediating their interactions with host cells. Since its initial identification in domestic dogs during the 1970 s, CPV-2 has undergone continuous evolution, leading to the emergence of major variants including CPV-2a, CPV-2b, and CPV-2c. In addition to cat and dog [7], CPV-2 can also infect wildlife, such as pangolins, foxes, giant pandas, and tigers [6, 8–11]. The expanding host range of CPV-2 complicates its spatiotemporal epidemiology while increasing spillover risks to conservation-dependent felids. Furthermore, disease outcomes in these novel hosts are often complex and may be modulated by co-infections with other pathogens. CPV has been frequently reported in co-infections with viruses such as canine circovirus (CanineCV) [12]. These interactions can potentially enhance viral pathogenicity, complicate clinical presentation.

Currently, the epidemiological characteristics and evolutionary dynamics of CPV-2 and other pathogens in large felids, such as tigers, remain poorly characterized. To address this knowledge gap, we investigated viral pathogens in Bengal tigers that died of unknown causes, with a focus on characterizing CPV-2 strains through genomic and phylogenetic approaches. This study provides critical data to elucidate the adaptive evolution of CPV-2 in large felids and its potential role in cross-species transmission within endangered ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The Bengal tiger breeding facility was located in a mountainous area, featuring a semi-open environment. The tigers were housed in enclosures perimetered by iron railings. The population was primarily fed a diet of chicken meat and had not received any vaccinations.

The Bengal tiger individual died in August 2018, its carcass was immediately frozen and stored at ≤ −40 °C until the autopsy in July 2024. Tissue samples including heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, intestine, and mesenteric lymph nodes were concurrently collected. Subsequently, these samples were transported to the laboratory using dry ice and stored in an ultra-low temperature refrigerator at −80 ℃ until analysis.

Screening of potential pathogens

Clinical and necropsy findings suggested viral infection as the probable cause of death. Consequently, PCR assays and Colloidal gold rapid test strips were employed to screen for documented viral pathogens in tigers, including: Feline panleukopenia virus (FPV), CPV, CDV, Feline calicivirus (FCV), Influenza A virus (IAV), Canine parainfluenza virus (CPIV), Canine coronavirus (CCoV), and Feline alphaherpesvirus 1 (FHV-1).

Tissue was homogenized in liquid nitrogen first, and then it was mixed with phosphate-buffered saline (1 × PBS, pH = 7.4) at 4 ℃. Genomic DNA/RNA were extracted from tissue suspensions using DNA extraction kit (TIANGEN, Beijing) or TRIzol Reagent (Thermo, USA). Primers used for pathogen screening are shown in Table 1. All screening assays included negative controls. The PCR products were confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and then, the positive PCR products were ligated to the pCE2 TA/Blunt-Zero Vector and sequenced by Sanger sequencing (Qingke Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Nucleotide sequence alignment was performed using BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi).

Virus isolation

The isolation of CPV was conducted using F81 cells. Specifically, the tissue suspensions were centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm filter membrane. Subsequently, F81 cells were inoculated with the filtrate at a ratio of 1:10 (filtrate to culture medium), and continuous passages were performed until cytopathic effects (CPE) were observed. Following DNA extraction, the full-length VP2 gene was amplified using primers CPV-F and CPV-R with the sequences 5’-ATGAGTGATGGAGGAGTTCAACC-3’ and 5’-TTAATATAATTTTCTAGGTGCTAG-3’, respectively.

Phylogenetic analysis

The sequence with the highest nucleotide homology to the HNSY-031 was compared using the blastn tool in NCBI. CPV-2 vaccine strains and other parvovirus subtypes sequences were downloaded. All sequences were aligned using ClustalW in MEGA-X software, and duplicate sequences were removed. Subsequently, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the maximum likelihood method with the General Time Reversible (GTR) model, employing 1,000 bootstrap replicates.

Analysis of VP2 mutations

Reference sequences with GenBank accession numbers M38245 were retrieved from the NCBI database for comparative analysis with the viral sequence isolated in this study. Amino acid mutation sites were identified using BioEdit software version 7.7.1.0, employing a sliding window analysis with a step size of 10 residues and a display window of 50 residues per line to systematically detect substitution patterns and visualize mutation distribution across the viral protein.

Results

Physical examination and autopsy

The tiger was a female weighing 33 kg. Absence of antemortem clinical records precluded historical analysis. Gross examination revealed widespread punctate white granular deposits on visceral surfaces, which manifested a distinct gritty texture when scraped with a scalpel blade, particularly prominent on renal and hepatic surfaces (Fig. 1).

Pathogen screening and isolation identification

The screening results were negative for all screened pathogens except CPV. PCR results are shown in Figure S1.

Following three successive blind passages, significant CPE were observed in the cell culture system, characterized by cellular rounding, loose adherence, aggregation into clusters, and progressive detachment (Fig. 2). Subsequently, the culture supernatant was collected and confirmed to remain positive by PCR amplification, thereby designating this isolate as HNSY-031.

Phylogenetic analyses

Phylogenetic reconstruction demonstrated that the VP2 gene of HNSY-031 clusters within the Parvovirus clade, forming a monophyletic group with CPV-2c variants (Fig. 3). This strain exhibits closest phylogenetic affinity to CPV-2c subtypes circulating in dog hosts from China and Vietnam, suggesting recent cross-border transmission dynamics.

Maximum likelihood tree based on the nucleotide sequences of the VP2 gene. The isolate from this study is indicated by a red square, while the isolates with the closest phylogenetic relationship to it are labeled in blue font

Genetic variation and mutations

The HNSY-031 strains generated in this study shared 100.0% nucleotide identity with other strains in NCBI blast (OK094444.1-Vietnam-2020, ON322797.1-China: Henan-2020, PP035122.1-China: Jilin-2023, PP035124.1-China: Jilin-2022, MW728965.1- China: Beijing-2020). Compared to the reference CPV-2 strain (original CPV2, GenBank no. M38245), 12 mutations A5G, M87L, I101T, F267Y, S297A, A300G, D305Y, Y324I, Q370R, N375D, N426E, and I447M were found in the VP2 protein in this study.

Discussion

Captive wildlife face heightened pathogen risks due to dense populations and frequent contacts, which promote pathogen transmission and spillover from humans and domestic animals. This study reports the isolation of a CPV strain, HNSY-031, from deceased Bengal tigers in China. Phylogenetic analysis positioned HNSY-031 within the CPV-2c clade, specifically clustering with dog-derived isolates from Vietnam. BLAST analysis showed 100% nucleotide identity between the VP2 gene sequence of HNSY-031 and CPV-2c strains from dogs in China and Vietnam since 2020. Notably, the tiger originated from a semi-open captive facility and had no vaccination history. Although no direct contact with domestic animals was reported, the possibility of indirect exposure through contaminated feed, breeder, wildlife, or fomites cannot be excluded. In 2020, a giant panda in Wolong Nature Reserve, Sichuan, China, died due to suspected CPV infection linked to dogs in nearby urban areas [19].

CPV-2 is widely distributed in dogs, cats, rodents, bats, insectivores, and zoo animals [20]. It has become a major pathogen threatening both wildlife and captive animal populations. Since its initial identification, various genetic subtypes of CPV-2 have continuously emerged. As of 2021, CPV-2c has become the predominant variant, accounting for 67.7% of all detected CPV-2 strains [20]. Notably, co-infections involving different CPV-2 variants, such as CPV-2a and CPV-2b, or CPV-2b and CPV-2c, have been documented [21]. Furthermore, CPV may also cause co-infections with other pathogens; for instance, giant pandas have shown concurrent infections involving CPV-2, CDV, or giant panda rotavirus (GPRV) [22], while dogs have been co-infected with CanineCV and CPV-2 [12]. Studies indicate that in cases of CanineCV and CPV-2 co-infection, CanineCV may enhance CPV-2 replication by inducing immunosuppression [23]. Such viral interactions can potentially enhance pathogenicity and complicate clinical presentations. Therefore, it is recommended that future monitoring programs incorporate broad-spectrum pathogen screening to comprehensively assess health risks in captive wildlife, including tigers, and to elucidate the synergistic mechanisms underlying CPV cross-species transmission.

The VP2 protein serves as the primary determinant of CPV host range and virus-host interactions. Recent continuous VP2 variations have generated multiple viral variants while expanding host tropism [8, 10, 11, 24]. In HNSY-031, residue 426 of VP2 is E, a site critical for antigenic divergence among CPV-2 subtypes: 426 N in CPV-2a, 426D in CPV-2b, and 426E acid in CPV-2c. This mutation directly modulates the major antigenic region of the viral capsid [25]. The transferrin receptor represents the core cellular receptor for parvovirus entry. Mutations S297A, A300G, and D305Y in HNSY-031 localize to the TfR-binding domain and antibody recognition sites. Residue 300 constitutes a key determinant for cross-species evolution among carnivores, where single-site substitutions alter host infection susceptibility [26–28].

Notably, TfR homology among CPV-2-infected hosts ranges from 77.2% to 99.0%, with porcine TfR sharing 80.7%, 80.4%, and 77.2% identity to feline, canine, and human orthologs, respectively [20]. For captive tigers housed near human settlements, this conserved receptor biology—combined with frequent exposure to domestic carnivore excreta—creates sustained spillover risk beyond natural ecological interfaces. Previous studies implicate VP2 mutations at residues 297 and 562 in modulating raccoon dog parvovirus pathogenicity, where A297S and L562V significantly reduce virulence [29]. Furthermore, the A5G and Q370R substitutions observed in HNSY-031 represent predominant mutations across Asia [30, 31]. The A5G variant frequently co-occurs with F267Y, Y324I, and Q370R [32, 33]. A recent global study on CPV-2 suggests that residues such as 426E or 426D, 305 H or 305D, and 297 S in VP2, along with sites 87 L, 93 N, 232I, and 305Y in the receptor-binding loop, may be associated with cross-species transmission of CPV-2 [20]. It is therefore imperative to maintain ongoing surveillance of amino acid mutations in the VP2 protein of CPV-2.

In conclusion, this study underscores the critical need to implement robust pathogen surveillance and enhance biosecurity management protocols within captive wildlife facilities. Furthermore, integrating comprehensive vaccination programs into routine health management plans for tigers and other captive wild animals is strongly recommended to mitigate the risks of cross-species transmission.

Conclusion

In summary, this study identifies a CPV-2c strain in a captive Bengal tiger that may be linked to multiple environmental or animal sources. The results underscore the importance of integrated strategies for protecting endangered felids, including strengthened biosecurity measures, widespread vaccination of proximate animal populations, and systematic surveillance to better understand and mitigate possible routes of viral transmission.
