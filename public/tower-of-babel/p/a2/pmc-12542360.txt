Axillary tumor deposits in primary breast carcinoma: a case report
Authors: Wafa Mokni, Zeineb Nfikha, Nihed Abdessayed, Sarra Yacoub, Moncef Mokni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTumor deposits are irregular clusters of tumor cells in adipose tissue, discontinuous from the primary tumor. While their prognostic relevance has been well established in colorectal and gastric cancers, their significance in breast cancer remains poorly understood. This case highlights the presence of tumor deposits in breast cancer, with focus on their potential prognostic implications as well as the need for clearer diagnostic criteria.Case presentationWe report the case of a 58-year-old Tunisian woman who presented with a 2-cm breast nodule. Clinically, the lesion was staged as T4bN0Mx, and imaging classified it as American College of Radiology category 5. Fine needle biopsy revealed an invasive grade III breast carcinoma according to the Scarff–Bloom–Richardson grading scale. The patient received hormone therapy, radiotherapy, and neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by mastectomy with axillary dissection. Histopathological examination of axillary adipose revealed 14 tumor deposits and five metastatic lymph nodes with extracapsular extension. The pathological response to chemotherapy was poor, with more than 50% residual tumor. The patient is currently in good condition and is receiving hormone therapy.ConclusionAlthough tumor deposits are correlated with poor prognosis in other cancers, particularly in colorectal and gastric cancer, limited data are available for breast cancer. Owing to the unclear diagnostic criteria, they are often considered as lymph node metastases. To address this issue, we recommend reporting tumor deposits, and we encourage further studies on larger cohorts to better define their prognostic value and eventually, discuss their inclusion in the tumor, node, metastasis staging system.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13256-025-05452-3.Keywords: Tumor deposits, Breast cancer, Lymph node metastases, Case report, Prognosis

Introduction

Tumor deposits (TDs) are small, irregular tumor clusters located in adipose tissue, distinct from the primary tumor. They have been described in various cancers, especially in gastrointestinal malignancies. Multiple studies have identified TDs as independent predictors of early recurrence and poor prognosis in colon and gastric cancers [1]. Their therapeutic input has also been demonstrated in colorectal cancer, where the presence of TDs influences the N classification and consequently can be decisive regarding the type and duration of adjuvant therapy [2]. In breast cancer, extra nodal tumor deposits (ETDs) have been observed in 10% of breast cancer cases and have been shown to predict the presence of four or more additional positive nonsentinel lymph node metastases [3]. The studies of this emerging entity have led some authors to propose their inclusion in the tumor, node, metastasis (TNM) staging of breast cancer [3]. The actual version of TNM classification of breast cancer does not include TDs in the N staging, which can sometimes lead to an underestimation of the tumor stage and subsequently, undertreatment. The aim of our study is to focus on TDs in breast cancer and their potential impact on the prognosis and the TNM classification through a case report and a review of available literature.

Case presentation

A 58-year-old Tunisian female patient, with no personal or family medical history, presented with a palpable nodule in her left breast. Physical examination revealed a firm, poorly defined mass located in the outer quadrants of the left breast associated with skin changes suggestive of “peau d’orange.” The mass measured 2 cm in diameter. No palpable axillary lymph nodes were noted. Clinically, the nodule was staged as cT4bN0Mx.

Imaging and pathological examinations

Breast ultrasound and mammography classified the lesion as American College of Radiology category 5 (ACR5). A core needle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of invasive breast carcinoma of no special type, Scarff–Bloom–Richardson (SBR) grade III, and luminal B, HER2-positive subtype (Figs. 1, 2). Further imaging did not reveal any distant metastases at the time of diagnosis.

Treatment

The patient received four cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy consisting of epirubicin–cyclophosphamide followed by paclitaxel during a period of 6 months. Chemotherapy was combined with hormone therapy and radiotherapy. No treatment-related complications were noted. Radiological assessment after chemotherapy using ultrasound and mammography revealed a complete response. Later on, the patient underwent a left mastectomy with ipsilateral axillary lymph node dissection (ALND).

Macroscopic examination

Gross examination of the mastectomy specimen revealed a fibrous, whitish plaque measuring 15 cm × 9 cm × 4 cm with areas of necrosis. It was located in the outer quadrants and distant from the surgical margins. The axillary dissection specimen measured 10 cm × 8 cm × 2 cm and included 28 nodules, ranging in size from 0.3 cm to 1 cm.

Histological findings

Extensive sampling from the fibrous area showed diffuse scar fibrosis, associated with elastosis and a few inflammatory cells, indicating a partial response to chemotherapy. Residual viable tumor was evaluated as more than 50%. The tumor cells were medium-to-large in size, with eosinophilic or pale cytoplasm and prominent nucleoli (Fig. 1). Vascular embolism was noted, but no perineural invasion or Paget’s disease was found. All surgical margins were free of disease. Among the 28 retrieved nodules, 5 were identified as metastatic lymph nodes (Fig. 3), with extra capsular extension in 2 of them. The remaining 14 nodules were identified as TDs. They were independent tumor clusters, without a lymphocytic peripheral rim nor a residual vascular or neural wall (Fig. 4). The tumor was staged as ypT1aN2aM0. The patient continued to receive hormone therapy and was started on trastuzumab. Follow-up showed no complications. The patient remains in a good clinical condition. No recurrence has been detected.

Discussion

In this case, we reported the presence of 14 TDs associated with five lymph node metastases in the axillary area of a patient who underwent mastectomy and axillary dissection for a high-grade luminal B, HER2-positive invasive breast carcinoma. The identification of TDs in our case was performed on the basis of the criteria used in colorectal cancer (CRC). These criteria include the absence of a lymphocytic peripheral rim and a capsule, as well as the absence of a residual vascular wall or a neural structure within the tumor cluster. Although the presence of TDs was not included in the N classification, their presence and number were explicitly documented in our final pathological report.

TDs are an emerging entity in breast cancer. Research papers that discuss TDs in breast carcinoma are very limited. In fact, it is a controversial entity with recent publications and data. Their frequency varied between 10% and 29% [4, 5]. However, TDs were described initially in colorectal adenocarcinomas, then in other carcinomas, including head and neck, pancreatic, biliary, stomach, and cholangiocarcinoma [4]. Their prognostic impact was especially proven in gastrointestinal cancer. In CRC, Goldstein et al. [6] demonstrated that, among patients with a similar number of positive lymph nodes in CRC, those with tumor deposits had worse outcomes. In addition, TD presence was correlated with lymph node metastases and extramural vascular invasion. Several other retrospective cohort studies have shown a decrease in 5-year overall survival when TDs are present alongside lymph node metastases [6]. According to Yabata et al., the effect of tumor deposits on overall survival lies between that of lymph node metastases and distant metastases. They are also associated with an increased risk of local recurrence, especially in rectal cancer cases, as confirmed by Landau et al. [6, 7]. Furthermore, in gastric cancer, TDs were linked to increased risk of recurrence, reduced survival, and other poor prognosis factors, including vascular invasion, perineural invasion, advanced stage, and synchronous distant metastasis [7–9].

In breast cancer, data are very limited, and the prognostic impact of TDs is beginning to be explored. A univariate study of 110 cases conducted by Durak et al. [4] revealed a significant correlation between TDs and distant metastases. In fact, this risk is 3.3 times higher in patients with TDs. Moreover, the 10-year disease-free survival rate and overall survival were 67.2% and 73.7%, respectively, and these associations were documented in both univariate and multivariate analysis.

In our case, residual tumor was evaluated as more than 50%. This partial response is a common finding in T4 tumors with studies reporting that more than 50% of T4 tumors achieved partial response regardless of the presence or absence of TDs [10, 11]. However, data on the correlation between TDs and response to chemotherapy remain scarce and very limited.

Other studies have explored the potential association between TDs and sentinel lymph node positivity. According to Mamtani et al. [5], in a study involving 1114 cases, in T1–T2cN0 patients with sentinel lymph node metastases, TDs were strongly associated with four or more positive nonsentinel lymph nodes on ALND. These findings have significant therapeutic implications. Indeed, ALND may be considered even in patients not meeting the criteria for it.

Despite the increasing focus on TDs’ prognostic impact in breast cancer, their diagnosis remains challenging. In some cases, it may be difficult to distinguish TDs from lymph node metastases. Diagnostic criteria favor lymph nodes; a round shape, a peripheral rim of lymphocytes or lymphoid follicles, a possible subcapsular sinus, a residual lymph node in the surrounding fibro-adipose tissue, and a thick capsule have been suggested as the most useful features in favor of a lymph node structure [6]. Moreover, neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy can also complicate evaluation owing to the presence of fibrosis and chronic inflammatory changes, complicating histological interpretation.

The distinction between a TD and a lymph node becomes even more delicate in the presence of extracapsular extension of metastatic lymph nodes. This occurs in 22–55% of metastatic sentinel lymph nodes and in 21.7–57.5% of nonsentinel lymph nodes [12]. A completely distorted architecture can mimic tumor deposits. This distinction is crucial given the significant prognostic impact of extracapsular extension. Indeed, it is associated with a higher rate of metastases in nonsentinel lymph nodes, a greater lymph node tumor burden, a higher frequency of pN2 stage, and a poorer prognosis, with a negative effect on both overall survival and recurrence-free survival [12, 13].

Given their prognostic value, TDs were formally incorporated into the 7th and 8th editions of the CRC TNM classification, designated as pN1c in the absence of lymph node metastases. This inclusion was based on the prognostic and therapeutic impact. In stage III CRC, the duration of adjuvant treatment is not only guided by the pT stage but also by the pN stage. In this context, excluding TDs in the presence of lymph node metastases risks underestimating the prognosis and undertreatment [11]. Some researchers proposed the inclusion of the number of TDs in the pN category, regardless of whether nodal metastasis are present. They advocate that TDs’ prognostic effect is either additive or more significant than that of lymph node metastases [12, 13]. The Japanese Society of Colon and Rectal Cancer recommended including TDs in the pN category, with the addition of the symbol V+ or Pn+ if there is evidence of venous or perineural invasion [14]. However, in the UK, pathologists still use the TNM 5 system, classifying TDs as metastatic lymph nodes, given the better reproducibility [15, 16].

In gastric cancer, the prognostic relevance of TDs is still controversial. They were not formally introduced into the TNM classification. Sun et al. [14], in their study of 2998 cases, found no significant prognostic difference between patients classified as T4a with and without tumor deposits. Therefore, they suggested that tumor deposits should be considered a form of serosal infiltration (T4a). Others have suggested that they should be integrated into the N category. However, the Japanese Gastric Cancer Treatment Guidelines (JGCT) recommend that each deposit should be included in the N category as a metastatic lymph node [7]. In addition, Wang et al. [17] found a correlation between the number of tumor deposits and prognosis, and they propose incorporating them into the N3 or M1 categories depending on their number.

In breast cancer, the discussion about including TDs in the TNM staging or the modalities of doing it have not been considered yet. However the number of studies concerning TDs in breast cancer is increasing, with lager cohorts and more significant results [4, 5]. Through our case, we underline the importance of identifying TDs in breast cancer and of mentioning their presence and number in the pathological report.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, data regarding the frequency of axillary TDs in breast carcinomas are still limited. This entity represents a diagnostic challenge, given the potential of confusion with axillary lymph node metastases with extracapsular extension. Through our case, we are discussing the challenges in the diagnosis of TDs in breast carcinoma. As for now, criteria for TD diagnosis are the ones used for CRC [4]. The main objective of these criteria is to differentiate TDs from a metastatic lymph node. They include the absence of a lymphocytic peripheral rim and a capsule, as well as the absence of a residual vascular wall or a neural structure within the tumor cluster [6]. The prognostic value of TDs in breast cancer is emerging but remains limited. This case highlights the need for further large-scale studies on TDs in breast cancer and on larger cohorts of patients. Demonstrating their prognostic impact may support their inclusion in the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification, and ultimately, lead to more accurate staging and more optimized therapeutic strategies.
