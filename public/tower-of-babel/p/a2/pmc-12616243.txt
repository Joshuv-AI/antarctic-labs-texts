Phase I Clinical Trial of the Bifunctional EGFR/TGF-β Fusion Protein Ficerafusp Alfa (BCA101) Alone and in Combination with Pembrolizumab for Advanced Solid Tumors
Authors: Alberto Hernando-Calvo, Richard D. Carvajal, Paul K. Paik, Van Karlyle Morris, Dan P. Zandberg, John M. Kaczmar, David N. Bohr, Ralf D. Reiners, Elham Gharakhani, Rachel L. Salazar, Patrick Hall Lizotte, Elena V. Ivanova, Philippe L. Bedard, Glenn J. Hanna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:To evaluate the safety, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and efficacy of ficerafusp alfa (BCA101), a first-in-class bifunctional protein targeting EGFR and TGF-β, as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab in patients with advanced solid tumors.Patients and Methods:At escalating doses in a parallel 3 + 3 design, patients with EGFR-driven advanced solid tumors received weekly intravenous ficerafusp alfa as monotherapy (64–1,500 mg) or in combination (240–1,500 mg) with pembrolizumab (200 mg i.v. every 3 weeks). The primary objective was to determine safety/tolerability. Secondary objectives included assessment of pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity, and preliminary efficacy per investigator-assessed response. Exploratory analyses included pharmacodynamic biomarkers.Results:Among 61 patients (monotherapy, n = 46; combination, n = 15), the most common treatment-related adverse events included acneiform dermatitis (46%) and fatigue (20%) with monotherapy and acneiform dermatitis (73%), fatigue (53%), pruritus (40%), epistaxis (40%), and maculopapular rash (40%) with combination therapy. One patient had a dose-limiting toxicity with 1,250-mg monotherapy (grade 3 anemia and hematuria). The MTD was not reached in either cohort. With monotherapy, objective response was observed in one of 42 evaluable patients and 16 (38%) achieved stable disease. With combination therapy, four of 13 evaluable patients (31%) had a confirmed response, including one with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma refractory to anti–PD-1 therapy and cetuximab. Prolonged neutralization of plasma TGF-β1 was observed at doses ≥500 mg.Conclusions:Ficerafusp alfa exhibited a manageable safety profile and clinical activity as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab, with exposure increasing proportionally at anticipated therapeutic doses. See related commentary by Choudhury et al., p. 4617

Translational Relevance

Although immunotherapy has improved outcomes for patients with many solid tumors, only a fraction of patients derive long-term clinical benefit, partly because of the presence of competing immunosuppressive pathways. Activation of the EGFR pathway and TGF-β has been associated separately with immune resistance. Ficerafusp alfa (BCA101) is a first-in-class bifunctional protein targeting EGFR and TGF-β. This first-in-human, phase I dose-escalation study demonstrated proof of target engagement, biological activity, and a tolerable safety profile for ficerafusp alfa, alone and in combination with pembrolizumab. Activity was observed in patients refractory to both immunotherapy and EGFR inhibitors, suggesting that ficerafusp alfa may address intrinsic or acquired mechanisms of immune resistance. Ficerafusp alfa with pembrolizumab is being tested in expansion cohorts for patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma of the anal canal, squamous non–small cell lung cancer, and squamous cell carcinoma of the skin.

Introduction

Immunotherapy has improved outcomes for patients with many solid tumors. However, only a fraction of patients derive prolonged clinical benefit (1–4), which may be indicative of the presence of competing or preexisting immunosuppressive pathways (5). Activation of the EGFR pathway has been associated with immune resistance manifested by upregulation of PD-L1, PD-1, and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated protein 4 (6). EGFR is overexpressed in a variety of tumors such as head and neck and lung cancers, and EGFR alterations have been associated with hyperprogression to immune checkpoint blockade (7, 8). Recent data suggest that the combination of EGFR-directed monoclonal antibodies (such as cetuximab) with an immune checkpoint inhibitor is tolerable and may provide synergistic tumor control (9–11).

EGFR is intimately linked with the TGF-β pathway, and both cooperate to promote tumorigenesis (12). Epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a cellular process that causes epithelial cells to acquire mesenchymal characteristics, leading to cellular proliferation (13). Emerging data have linked EMT with EGFR inhibitor and immunotherapy resistance (14, 15), with the EMT being partly mediated by TGF-β (16). TGF-β facilitates immune escape within the tumor microenvironment by promoting downregulation of both innate and adaptive immunity and is also negatively correlated with infiltration by tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (17–19). Furthermore, TGF-β has been shown to polarize tumor-associated macrophages and enhance immunosuppression of tumor-associated fibroblasts and myeloid-derived suppressor cells within the tumor microenvironment (20, 21). Thus, blocking or reversing EMT by targeting TGF-β in combination with immune checkpoint blockade is an attractive strategy and an active area of research (22).

Ficerafusp alfa (BCA101) is a first-in-class bifunctional antibody that fuses a TGF-β immune-modulating payload or trap to an EGFR tumor antigen–directed antibody for selective delivery to the tumor microenvironment. Ficerafusp alfa aims to target immunosuppressive mechanisms in the tumor microenvironment mediated by TGF-β1. Ficerafusp alfa exhibits superior antitumor efficacy compared with anti-EGFR alone in human tumor cell line xenografts, patient-derived xenografts, and co-implantation models (23). In coculture assays, ficerafusp alfa has been shown to rescue TGF-β1–induced immunosuppression by decreasing regulatory T cells, increasing T helper 1–associated cytokines such as IFNγ and TNF, and enhancing the cytolytic activity of peripheral blood mononuclear cells and NK cells against tumor cells (23, 24). Furthermore, when combined with pembrolizumab in syngeneic mouse models, ficerafusp alfa enhanced antitumor effects, leading to more robust immune activation and prolonged tumor regression, supporting its clinical evaluation in combination immunotherapy strategies (23). We hypothesized that targeting EGFR and TGF-β with ficerafusp alfa as monotherapy and in combination with the anti–PD-1 inhibitor pembrolizumab would favorably modulate the tumor microenvironment by decreasing immunosuppressive signaling.

Here, we present the results of the dose-escalation phase of a first-in-human, phase I clinical trial investigating ficerafusp alfa as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab in patients with select advanced solid tumors.

Patients and Methods

Participants

Eligible patients were ≥18 years of age with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 or 1 and a pathologically confirmed diagnosis of prespecified EGFR-driven advanced solid tumor types that were treatment refractory, treatment intolerant, or ineligible for standard treatment. Specifically, for the cohort receiving ficerafusp alfa monotherapy, EGFR-driven eligible tumor types included squamous non–small cell lung cancer (SqNSCLC), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), microsatellite-stable RAS wild-type colorectal cancer, and squamous cell carcinoma of the anal canal (SCAC); a list of all eligible tumor types is presented in Supplementary Text S1. Patients were eligible regardless of the number of previous lines of systemic therapy. Patients may have received prior treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors and may have received prior treatment with cetuximab or other anti-EGFR therapies, but this was not required. EGFR tumor tissue expression testing was not required for participation. For the dose-escalation cohort receiving ficerafusp alfa in combination with pembrolizumab, tumor types included recurrent unresectable or metastatic (R/M) HNSCC and SCAC.

Eligible patients were required to have adequate organ function, measurable disease by RECIST version 1.1 (25), and available archival tumor tissue or fresh tumor biopsy material for biomarker profiling. Patients were ineligible if they had a history of gastrointestinal bleeding 4 weeks prior to enrollment, a history of clinically significant cardiac valvular disease, previous treatment with anti–TGF-β therapy, or a prior history of grade ≥2 intolerance or hypersensitivity reaction to cetuximab or other anti-EGFR therapy (full eligibility criteria are included in Supplementary Text S1).

Study design

Dose escalation in this first-in-human phase I clinical trial (BCA101X1101) followed a parallel 3 + 3 design for both ficerafusp alfa monotherapy and ficerafusp alfa in combination with pembrolizumab (Supplementary Fig. S1). Backfilling of up to six additional patients was allowed once acceptable safety had been demonstrated for the respective dose level. Once the first two dose levels of ficerafusp alfa monotherapy were determined to be safe, enrollment in the combination started. The tumor-selected dose-expansion phase of the study is ongoing and will be reported separately.

Objectives

The primary objective of this study was to assess safety and tolerability and to determine the MTD of ficerafusp alfa as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab. The primary endpoint was the incidence of dose-limiting toxicities (DLT). DLTs were defined as an adverse event or abnormal laboratory value assessed as unrelated to disease, disease progression, inter-current illness, or concomitant medications occurring within the first 21 days of treatment with ficerafusp alfa and meeting any of the criteria specified in Supplementary Text S2, including grade 2 eye pain or reduction of visual acuity not responsive to topical therapy, grade 2 pneumonitis if not resolved to grade ≤1 within 7 days of corticosteroid treatment, grade 2 myocarditis, grade 2 colitis persisting >7 days despite corticosteroid treatment, grade 2 alanine aminotransferase or aspartate aminotransferase elevation accompanied by elevation of bilirubin (>1.5× upper limit of normal), and grade 2 bullous disease that does not resolve to grade ≤1 within 7 days of corticosteroid treatment.

Secondary objectives included the characterization of the pharmacokinetic (PK) and immunogenicity profile of ficerafusp alfa and preliminary antitumor activity of ficerafusp alfa as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab as assessed by the investigator by RECIST version 1.1 (25). Moreover, pharmacodynamic biomarkers were assessed as an exploratory objective.

This study was designed, implemented, executed, and reported in accordance with the International Council for Harmonization Harmonized Tripartite Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice, applicable local regulations, and the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol and all amendments were approved by the ethics committee or institutional review board at each study site. All patients provided written informed consent before participating in the trial. This nonrandomized clinical trial was conducted and its findings were reported according to the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Nonrandomized Designs statement. The trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04429542).

Treatment and assessments

The starting dosage of ficerafusp alfa in monotherapy for patients enrolled in the study was set at 64 mg i.v. administered continuously once weekly. The selected starting dosage followed the International Council for Harmonization S9 guidelines. Ficerafusp alfa was administered as an infusion over 120 minutes. The starting dosage of ficerafusp alfa in combination with pembrolizumab was 240 mg i.v. once weekly. Pembrolizumab 200 mg i.v. was administered after ficerafusp alfa as a 30-minute infusion every 3 weeks (cycle length, 21 days). Patients received treatment until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, withdrawal of consent, loss to follow-up, or death, whichever occurred first.

Imaging assessments by CT and/or MRI were performed every 6 weeks, until cycle 13, then every 9 weeks during the treatment period, and every 12 weeks in post-treatment follow-up. Evaluation of cardiac function was done with serial echocardiograms and electrocardiograms. Safety was additionally assessed by physical examination and laboratory testing at each study visit. Adverse events (assessed according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0) were recorded continuously until 30 days after the last dose of investigational therapy. Treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAE) and treatment-related adverse events (TRAE) were listed by preferred term and system organ class using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities terminology and categorized by grade.

PK and immunogenicity analyses

Serum samples were collected at days 1 to 4, 8, 15 to 18, and 21 of cycle 1 for PK analyses and days 1, 8, 15, and 21 of cycle 1 for antidrug antibody analyses, as well as day 1 of cycles 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 16, 24, and 30, and at the end of treatment for both analyses. All patients enrolled to receive ficerafusp alfa monotherapy or in combination with pembrolizumab had dense PK sampling to allow for the determination of PK parameters by noncompartmental analysis. The incidence and titer of antidrug antibodies were analyzed to evaluate the immunogenicity of ficerafusp alfa as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab.

Biomarker analysis

Biomarker studies were exploratory. The inhibition of TGF-β isoforms 1, 2, and 3 in plasma was used as a pharmacodynamic biomarker for ficerafusp alfa. Plasma was activated with 1N HCl for 10 minutes and neutralized with 1.2N NaOH/0.5 mol/L HEPES. Isoforms of TGF-β were quantified in plasma using the U-PLEX multiplex platform (Meso Scale Discovery, #K15067M-1) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples collected from patients who missed the previous dose of ficerafusp alfa were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize all patient characteristics, treatment administration and compliance, efficacy endpoints, and safety parameters. The safety set included all patients who received at least one dose of study treatment, ficerafusp alfa or pembrolizumab. The dose-determining set included all patients in the safety set who enrolled during dose escalation and either experienced a DLT at any time during the DLT evaluation period (first 21 days) or had all required safety assessments without experiencing a DLT within the DLT evaluation period. The efficacy-evaluable set included all patients who received at least one dose of study treatment and had at least one post-baseline tumor assessment as defined in the protocol at or beyond the first post-baseline visit for tumor assessment. If a patient had progressive disease before the first post–baseline assessment visit, they were also included in the efficacy-evaluable set. The PK analysis set consisted of all patients who had at least one blood sample providing evaluable PK data. The duration of response analysis was performed using Kaplan–Meier methods. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4.

The data cutoff for the data reported was April 17, 2024.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between June 25, 2020, and June 9, 2022, 61 patients were enrolled; 46 received ficerafusp alfa monotherapy at dosages ranging from 64 to 1,500 mg once weekly, and 15 patients received ficerafusp alfa at dosages 240 to 1,500 mg once weekly in combination with 200 mg pembrolizumab every 3 weeks. Patients treated with ficerafusp alfa monotherapy had a median age of 61 (range, 43–82) years and a median of four (range, 1–7) prior antineoplastic therapies. Patients treated with ficerafusp alfa in combination with pembrolizumab had a similar age range and had received a median of two (range, 1–8) prior lines of antineoplastic therapies. Prior anti-EGFR and prior anti–PD-(L)1 exposure was reported for 28% (13 of 46) and 46% (21 of 46) of patients treated with ficerafusp alfa monotherapy and 33% (five of 15) and 60% (nine of 15) of patients treated with ficerafusp alfa in combination with pembrolizumab, respectively (Table 1). Patients included in this study were representative of patients with advanced solid tumors across broader populations in clinical practice (Supplementary Table S1).

Safety

TRAEs were reported for 87% (40 of 46) and 87% (13 of 15) of patients treated with ficerafusp alfa monotherapy and ficerafusp alfa in combination with pembrolizumab, respectively. For ficerafusp alfa monotherapy, the most common TRAEs included acneiform dermatitis (46%; 21 of 46), fatigue (20%; nine of 46), hypomagnesemia (17%; eight of 46), pruritus (17%; eight of 46), and maculopapular rash (17%; eight of 46; Table 2). For ficerafusp alfa in combination with pembrolizumab, the most common TRAEs were acneiform dermatitis (73%; 11 of 15), fatigue (53%; eight of 15), epistaxis (40%; six of 15), pruritus (40%; six of 15), and maculopapular rash (40%; six of 15). The most common TEAEs are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Serious TRAEs were reported for 7% (three of 46) monotherapy patients and 20% (three of 15) combination therapy patients, including one DLT at the ficerafusp alfa monotherapy 1250-mg once weekly dosage level (Supplementary Table S3), consisting of grade 3 hematuria and grade 3 anemia (in one patient), resulting in discontinuation of study treatment. Notably, the patient had existing ureteral stents for tumor-related urinary obstruction prior to enrollment. The other two serious TRAEs included a grade 3 upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage, which required transfusion but no endoscopic intervention, and a grade 1 creatinine phosphokinase increase with a grade 1 troponin-T increase, which was asymptomatic and without ischemic findings on electrocardiography. For ficerafusp alfa in combination with pembrolizumab, serious TRAEs included grade 3 anemia (n = 1), grade 3 stomatitis with grade 3 atrial fibrillation (n = 1), and grade 3 stomatitis with grade 3 pneumonitis (n = 1). All tested dose levels were deemed safe by the safety review committee, and an MTD was not reached.

Efficacy

Of the 46 patients treated with ficerafusp alfa monotherapy, four patients did not have a post-baseline assessment and were excluded from the efficacy-evaluable population. Among the efficacy-evaluable population (n = 42; Fig. 1A), tumor regression was observed in patients treated at higher doses. Notably, one patient with EGFR-amplified SqNSCLC refractory to first-line chemotherapy with anti–PD-1 combination therapy treated with ficerafusp alfa 1,250 mg once weekly monotherapy achieved a partial response (PR) with a duration of response of approximately 9 months (Fig. 1B). Sixteen patients achieved a best response of stable disease (SD) for a clinical benefit rate of 41% [95% confidence interval (CI), 26%–57%], including two patients with SD for more than 6 months. Among these 16 patients with best response of SD, the median duration of SD was 4.2 (95% CI, 2.9–5.8) months. Of the 17 patients with a PR or SD, four had received prior anti-EGFR therapy.

Among the 15 patients treated with ficerafusp alfa in combination with pembrolizumab, 13 patients were RECIST 1.1 evaluable, and two patients discontinued treatment because of adverse events before the first post-baseline efficacy assessment (Fig. 1C). Four patients (HNSCC, n = 2; SCAC, n = 2) achieved a PR (Fig. 1D), with three of the four demonstrating a duration of response exceeding 2 years (range, 10.1–36.7 months), including one response that was ongoing at data cutoff. Three of the responses occurred in patients who were naïve to anti-EGFR and/or anti–PD-1 therapies, and a PR was observed in a patient with R/M HNSCC of the hypopharynx refractory to pembrolizumab with an investigational immunotherapy agent in combination, followed by cetuximab (Fig. 1E). Notably, this patient had achieved progressive disease as best response to both prior treatment regimens. Five patients achieved SD, resulting in an objective response rate of 31% (95% CI, 9%–61%) and a clinical benefit rate of 69% (95% CI, 39%–91%) for the combination. Of the nine patients with a PR or SD, three had received prior anti-EGFR therapy.

PK and immunogenicity analyses

The PK-evaluable population included 46 patients who received ficerafusp alfa monotherapy and 15 patients who received ficerafusp alfa in combination with pembrolizumab. Dosage levels ranged from 64 to 1,500 mg once weekly as monotherapy and 240 to 1,500 mg once weekly as combination therapy. Mean serum concentration–time profiles are shown in Fig. 2A and B.

Mean serum concentration of ficerafusp alfa over time. A, Concentration–time plot after the first dose of ficerafusp alfa. B, Concentration–time plot after the third dose of ficerafusp alfa. BLQ, below lower limit of quantitation; SD, standard deviation.

Maximal concentrations (Cmax) were generally reached between 2 and 5 hours after the start of infusion on cycle 1 day 1. Thereafter, the concentration–time profiles declined in a biexponential manner at higher doses, with geometric mean half-life ranging from 14 hours for lower doses associated with target-mediated drug disposition to 84 hours for higher doses. Cmax and area under the plasma concentration versus time curve from 0 to 168 hours (AUC0–168h) increased in a slightly more than dose-proportional manner between 64 and 500 mg. However, a nearly dose-proportional increase in Cmax and AUC0–168h was observed between 500 and 1,500 mg. These data suggest that nonlinear clearance is negligible and linear clearance dominates at doses above 500 mg as EGFR is likely fully saturated. No significant difference in exposure was observed between ficerafusp alfa as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab across 240 to 1,500 mg dosing.

Antidrug antibody incidence (as defined by Shankar and colleagues; ref. 26) ranged between 18% and 27% across all ficerafusp alfa doses in monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab.

Pharmacodynamic analysis

Samples were available at day 1, day 8, and day 28 for patients treated with ficerafusp alfa at doses of 64, 240, 500, 750, 800, 1,000, 1,250, and 1,500 mg. Patients treated with ficerafusp alfa achieved complete and prolonged neutralization of TGF-β1 in plasma at weekly doses of ≥500 mg (Fig. 3A). Minimal neutralization of TGF-β2 was observed at doses of ≥500 mg (Fig. 3B). TGF-β3 levels were close to the lower limit of quantification and therefore not analyzed.

Discussion

Here, we demonstrate the safety and preliminary activity of ficerafusp alfa, a first-in-class EGFR/TGF-β bifunctional antibody, as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab in patients with select EGFR-driven, advanced solid tumors. Importantly, the toxicity profile was tolerable for both ficerafusp alfa as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab, with few patients experiencing serious TRAEs leading to discontinuation of study treatment, and antitumor activity was observed in a treatment-refractory patient population. The MTD was not reached and the only DLT observed was in a patient treated with ficerafusp alfa 1,250-mg monotherapy who experienced grade 3 hematuria and resulting anemia that was considered possibly related to study treatment. Notably, this patient also had previous anemia and hematuria and a recent prior ureteral stent insertion to relieve disease-related urinary obstruction.

An increased risk of mucosal bleeding events has been reported for previously investigated TGF-β inhibitors. Bintrafusp alfa (M7824), a bifunctional antibody targeting both TGF-β and PD-L1, reported drug-related grade 3/4 bleeding events in 10.2% of patients treated (27, 28). Moreover, the TGF-β inhibitor galunisertib also induced grade 3/4 bleeding among 8.3% of patients treated (29). One patient in the current study reported a grade 3 TRAE of upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage. Of note, this patient received prior palliative radiation to the primary tumor in the pancreas and was under treatment with rivaroxaban, and endoscopic evaluation showed findings of post-radiation gastropathy. Taken together, we hypothesize that the localization of TGF-β neutralization observed with ficerafusp alfa may in some way mitigate systemic bleeding risk.

Rash is a well-recognized on-target toxicity of EGFR inhibition related to widespread expression of EGFR in the dermis. Pivotal trials with approved EGFR inhibitors in a variety of cancers have reported treatment-related grade 3/4 skin rash in 5% to 17% of patients treated (30, 31). In the dose-escalation portion of our study in monotherapy patients, there were no severe rash events that were suspected to be related to study treatment. Lower-grade acneiform dermatitis was frequently observed in both the cohorts receiving ficerafusp alfa as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab with similar frequency. Comparable with experience with other EGFR inhibitors, skin toxicities occurred early after initiating ficerafusp alfa treatment. Skin toxicity was generally manageable across all dose levels with topical corticosteroids and oral antibiotics. Furthermore, newer prophylactic and mitigating agents for rash associated with EGFR inhibitors are under investigation, such as EGF-containing ointments, which may further reduce the impact of skin toxicity (32). The nature and frequency of TRAEs that may be considered immune mediated are consistent with the known safety profile of pembrolizumab (e.g., pneumonitis). Based on the data available to date, there have been no synergistic toxicities with respect to immune-mediated AEs observed with ficerafusp alfa in combination with pembrolizumab.

From a PK perspective, ficerafusp alfa exhibited nonlinear PK behavior that was likely due to specific target binding, which affects the clearance mechanisms of ficerafusp alfa and subsequently affects ficerafusp alfa exposure and half-life. However, a dose-proportional increase in Cmax and AUC0–168h observed at doses ≥500 mg suggested that EGFR is likely fully saturated at this dose range. Of note, ficerafusp alfa showed a nonlinear PK profile consistent with target-mediated drug disposition at doses <500 mg. Specifically, nonoverlapping exposures were achieved at 750 and 1,500 mg once weekly dosages. Of the five responses observed, four were observed at dosages >1,000 mg once weekly. Dosages of ficerafusp alfa ≥500 mg once weekly induced full sequestration of TGF-β1 and minimal neutralization of TGF-β2 in plasma. The selectivity of ficerafusp alfa may help minimize toxicities associated with pan–TGF-β inhibitors. As a result, ficerafusp alfa 1,500 mg once weekly was selected as the recommended dosage based on the PK, pharmacodynamic, and preliminary antitumor activity observed at this dosage level and will continue to be assessed for safety and efficacy during dose expansion. Ficerafusp alfa 750 mg once weekly is also being explored further as part of dose optimization, along with an every-2-week dosing schedule to augment quality of life by reducing clinic visits and mitigating costs. Based on available PK results from 1,500 to 1,750-mg every-2-week cohorts, the every-2-week dosing regimen is expected to reach non–clinically derived target concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S2A and S2B).

Ficerafusp alfa demonstrated clinical activity as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab. There was monotherapy activity with tumor reductions in two EGFR-amplified (per next-generation sequencing) patients with SqNSCLC refractory to prior therapy including chemotherapy and immunotherapy, with one patient having prolonged SD for 14.2 months and the other having a confirmed PR. Tumor reduction was not observed in one patient with SqNSCLC who did not have EGFR amplification. Historically, EGFR inhibitors have not been proven successful for the treatment of SqNSCLC (33, 34). Moreover, there are few effective systemic therapies for progressive SqNSCLC refractory to standard chemotherapy and immunotherapy combinations in the absence of actionable driver mutations, highlighting the relevance of this activity in SqNSCLC. PRs were also observed in four patients with HNSCC and SCAC receiving ficerafusp alfa in combination with pembrolizumab, including a patient with immunotherapy-refractory and cetuximab-resistant human papillomavirus–negative HNSCC. Collectively, this preliminary activity highlights the distinct mechanism of action of ficerafusp alfa and deserves further investigation.

The findings presented here should be interpreted with recognition of the limited sample size and the heterogeneity of the population under study in this phase I clinical trial. The preliminary observations of ficerafusp alfa antitumor activity in patients with EGFR-amplified tumors by multi-gene panel testing will need to be validated in larger cohorts. Given the demonstrated activity and safety with ficerafusp alfa monotherapy, an expansion cohort is currently testing the role of ficerafusp alfa in anti–PD-1–refractory cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, in which there is a large unmet need, and EGFR inhibition has strong rationale (35, 36). Moreover, given the promising responses observed in patients with refractory SCAC and HNSCC, ficerafusp alfa in combination with pembrolizumab is currently being investigated in expansion cohorts as first-line treatment for patients with R/M PD-L1–positive HNSCC and SCAC.

In conclusion, ficerafusp alfa seems well tolerated and exhibited preliminary clinical activity both as monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab. Enrollment among tumor-selected expansion cohorts is ongoing.
