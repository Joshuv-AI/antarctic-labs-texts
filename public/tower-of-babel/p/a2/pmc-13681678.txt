Intranasal gold nanoparticle-antigen induces mucosal and systemic immunity against β-coronaviruses
Authors: Muzhuo Wang, Weijie Wang, Wei Xu, Zhikai Chen, Junlan Zhang, Zeya Li, Jiadong Li, Shibo Jiang, Lu Lu, Zezhong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The continued emergence of diverse beta-coronaviruses (β-CoVs) from animal reservoirs underscores the urgent need for broad-spectrum mucosal vaccines, yet their development is hindered by physiological barriers, rapid antigen clearance, and suboptimal multivalent display strategies. To address this, we intranasally immunized BALB/c mice (n = 3 or 4 per group) with 5 or 15 nm gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) displaying the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) receptor-binding domain (RBD, 5 μg per mouse) and adjuvanted with the STING agonist cGAMP (20 μg per mouse). Guided by the serotyping of STING agonist-elicited neutralizing responses, we generated AuNP-Mix co-displaying six representative β-CoV RBDs for comparative immunological evaluation against single-RBD AuNP formulations. Antigen delivery, pulmonary retention, mucosal and systemic immune responses were evaluated via in vivo imaging, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, pseudovirus neutralization assays, flow cytometry, immunofluorescence, and challenge studies. We demonstrated that 5 nm AuNP-RBD adjuvanted with cGAMP exhibited superior mucosal penetration and lung retention, induced robust local and systemic immunity, and elicited cross-neutralizing antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 variants, SARS-CoV, and SARS-related coronaviruses. Intranasal immunization with AuNP-Mix elicited broader mucosal/systemic immune responses than those achieved with simple RBD mixtures, as demonstrated by its potent neutralizing activity against SARS-CoV-2 variants, SARS-CoV, Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV), and divergent MERS-like viruses. These findings establish AuNP-based mosaic display as a versatile platform for next-generation mucosal vaccines.

Introduction

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) continues to evolve, and despite the availability of multiple vaccines, effective control of its transmission remains challenging. Of particular concern, the extensive genetic diversity of SARS-CoV-like and Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV)-like coronaviruses poses a persistent threat to global public health. Some of these viruses use human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) as a functional receptor and display broad host tropism, posing a direct threat to human health [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. These findings highlight the need for broad-spectrum vaccines against beta-coronaviruses (β-CoVs), not only to control SARS-CoV-2 variants but also to prepare against future spillovers of SARS-related and MERS-related coronaviruses (SARSr-CoV and MERSr-CoV).

Respiratory mucosal vaccines, delivered intranasally or via aerosol inhalation, can induce secretory IgA (sIgA) antibodies locally in the respiratory tract [5]. These sIgA antibodies are secreted into the mucus layer, where they exhibit better resistance to degradation than IgG, directly neutralize viruses, and block viral entry into host cells, thereby effectively reducing virus transmission in the population [5]. In addition, mucosal immunization generates tissue-resident memory T (TRM) cells that can directly kill infected cells at the frontline of viral invasion [6,7]. Importantly, these robust sIgA and lung TRM cell responses in the respiratory mucosa are difficult to achieve with conventional intramuscular immunization [8,9]. The primary function of intramuscular vaccines is to induce systemic humoral and cellular immunity to confer protection against severe disease. However, intramuscular vaccines have limited capacity to block viral infection and transmission at the mucosal portal of entry [8]. This limitation underscores the critical need for developing mucosal vaccines as a next-generation strategy for controlling respiratory viruses.

Currently approved respiratory mucosal vaccines predominantly rely on viral vector platforms [10]. This is largely because the respiratory tract presents multiple structural barriers to vaccine delivery, including the mucus layer, ciliated epithelial cells, and immune cells distributed beneath the epithelium in the lamina propria [9,11]. Viral vector-based vaccines take advantage of the natural tropism of respiratory viruses, which enables them to efficiently penetrate the mucus layer, overcome mucociliary clearance, and reach dendritic cells (DCs) and other antigen-presenting cells (APCs) residing in the submucosal tissue, thereby initiating robust adaptive immune responses [12]. In contrast, no subunit-based respiratory mucosal vaccine has been approved to date. The major hurdle lies in the inherent properties of protein antigens, which exhibit limited capacity to penetrate the viscous mucus layer and are readily cleared by mucociliary movements before reaching the underlying immune cells [13]. Consequently, subunit antigens often fail to access DCs and other APCs localized beneath the epithelium; yet, these cells are critical for triggering effective mucosal immunity [14]. This limitation underscores the need for innovative delivery strategies to enable protein-based mucosal vaccines to overcome these physiological barriers.

Several protein nanoparticles have demonstrated remarkable potential for multivalent antigen display, inducing potent and broad-spectrum immune protection [15]. However, they may elicit scaffold-specific B cells that could theoretically limit the expansion of broadly neutralizing antibody (bnAb) precursors in germinal centers (GCs) [16]. Moreover, their fixed particle size may not be well-suited for navigating the physiological barriers of the respiratory tract. Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) are particularly attractive for vaccine delivery due to their biocompatibility, low toxicity, and ease of synthesis with precisely controlled sizes and surface modifications [[17], [18], [19]]. Their large surface area enables efficient co-display of multiple antigens, while their intrinsic adjuvant activity enhances antigen uptake by DCs and triggers innate immunity [20]. Nevertheless, the potential of AuNP-displayed coronavirus antigens to overcome the respiratory barrier and induce broad and potent mucosal immunity remains to be explored.

To optimize AuNP size for mucosal delivery and antigen display density, we leveraged a biotin-streptavidin conjugation strategy to display SARS-CoV-2 receptor-binding domain (RBD) on AuNPs and identified 5 nm AuNPs as the optimal size for intranasal immunization. The AuNP-displayed antigens markedly improved DC uptake and maturation in vitro and in vivo, while substantially prolonging antigen retention in the respiratory tract. When formulated with the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) agonist cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate (cGAMP) as an adjuvant, intranasal delivery of AuNPs displaying the SARS-CoV-2 Delta RBD unlocked potent and dual mucosal and systemic T- and B-cell responses, outperforming free RBD in both magnitude and breadth against sarbecoviruses. Notably, this vaccine conferred effective heterologous protection against BA.2.2 challenge in mice. To engineer a pan-β-CoV mucosal vaccine, we systematically evaluated each representative β-CoV RBD for its induced neutralizing antibody (nAb) breadth in combination with a STING agonist adjuvant. This profiling enabled us to select six antigenically distinct RBDs for mosaic display on AuNP surfaces. Intranasal administration of this multi-target AuNP vaccine, in contrast to simple mixtures of these RBD antigens, elicited potent and broad-spectrum mucosal and systemic immunity across β-CoVs. This work establishes AuNP-based mosaic antigen display as a versatile platform for developing next-generation mucosal vaccines against emerging respiratory viruses.

Results

Characterization of AuNP-RBD Conjugates

Mucosal vaccine application faces significant hurdles from mucus-mediated capture and nasal mucosal ciliary clearance, both of which restrict antigen bioavailability [5]. Optimizing particle size and stability is therefore essential for achieving effective nasal permeation [21,22]. Following the fabrication of 5 nm and 15 nm AuNP-streptavidin, transmission electron microscopy analysis demonstrated their spherical, monodisperse morphology (Figures 1A and S1A–S1B). The efficacy of RBD (Delta)-biotin loading was corroborated through comparative analysis of optical and hydrodynamic parameters. Specifically, the characteristic localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) peaks of streptavidin-functionalized 5 nm and 15 nm AuNPs (516 nm and 520 nm, respectively) underwent a 6–10 nm redshift following RBD incubation, indicating the local refractive index shift induced by the protein layer (Figure 1B–1C, Table S1). Subsequent dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements quantitatively confirmed this surface modification, as evidenced by expansion of the hydrodynamic diameters from 5 nm and 15 nm particles to 15.69 nm and 24.36 nm, respectively, from their initial states (Figure 1D–1E, Table S2). The combination of residual citrate ions and the streptavidin coating afforded the purified AuNPs a negative zeta potential (ζ) of approximately –30 mV. However, upon conjugation with the RBD protein, a conspicuous increase in zeta potential (i.e., a decrease in absolute negative charge) was observed for both platforms. Specifically, the ζ value shifted to approximately –23 mV for the 5 nm AuNP-RBD and –14 mV for the 15 nm AuNP-RBD (Figure 1F–1G, Table S3). This attenuation of surface negative charge can be attributed to the formation of the streptavidin-biotinylated RBD complex, which effectively masks the underlying carboxyl groups of the citrate layer and alters the electrical double layer of the nanoparticles. The resulting moderate negative potential, complemented by the steric hindrance of the protein shell, ensures the colloidal stability of the vaccine candidates in physiological buffers. Beyond fundamental nanoparticle stability, an effective mucosal antigen must successfully navigate the complex mucus barrier. To evaluate this, we utilized multiple particle tracking via confocal microscopy to record the real-time motion of the nanovaccines in an artificial mucin gel network (Figure S1C, Table S4). The mean squared displacement (MSD) curve for the 15 nm AuNP-RBD rapidly plateaued, indicating significant steric hindrance or mucoadhesion within the mucus (Figure S1C). In contrast, the 5 nm AuNP-RBD is sufficiently small to permeate the mucin gel network, maintaining continuous diffusion despite minor resistance (Figure S1C). Size-dependent Immunogenicity of Intranasal AuNP-RBD Vaccines

To assess how AuNP size influences adaptive immune responses, we intranasally immunized BALB/c mice with 5 nm or 15 nm AuNPs conjugated to the SARS-CoV-2 Delta RBD, either alone or adjuvanted with a STING agonist (Figure 1H). Analysis of Delta-RBD-specific IgG titers by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) showed that all AuNP-RBD formulations without cGAMP induced higher mean antibody titers compared to the RBD control (Figure 1I, Table S5). Pseudovirus neutralization assays demonstrated that 5 nm AuNP-RBD elicited more potent nAbs than the potency attained by either 15 nm AuNP-RBD or free RBD (Figure 1J, Table S6). When the cGAMP adjuvant was added, the binding and neutralizing antibodies induced by 5 nm AuNP-RBD were superior to those in the 15 nm AuNP-RBD and free RBD groups (Figure 1K–1L, Table S7). Specifically, in the presence of the cGAMP adjuvant, the 5 nm platform induced IgG titers approximately 12-fold higher and nAb titers 14-fold higher than those achieved by the 15 nm counterpart (Figure 1K–1L). These results indicate that nanoparticle size markedly affects the efficacy of mucosal vaccines. Therefore, 5 nm AuNPs were used for further in-depth studies.

Enhanced DC Uptake, Maturation, and Lung Retention of AuNP-RBD

The particle size of nanocarriers significantly influences both lymph node targeting and antigen uptake by APCs [21]. For mucosal vaccine antigens to elicit adaptive immunity, they need to be efficiently internalized by APCs, particularly DCs. To evaluate whether AuNP-displayed antigens are more readily taken up by DCs than free antigens, we first validated in vitro using the bone marrow-derived dendritic cell (BMDC) model. Specifically, RBD was labeled with Cy5 fluorescence; subsequently, free RBD-Cy5 and AuNP-RBD-Cy5 were incubated with BMDCs, respectively. Cellular internalization was quantitatively assessed using confocal microscopy and flow cytometry (Figure 2A). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) revealed distinctly higher intracellular accumulation of AuNP-RBD in BMDCs after only 15 min of incubation compared to that of free RBD and untreated control groups (Figure 2B, Table S8). Effective vaccine platforms must couple antigen delivery with potent immunostimulatory signals to drive DC maturation [23]. Previous studies have suggested that ultrasmall AuNPs (∼5 nm) primarily activate the inflammasome pathway rather than the NF-κB signaling [20]. In contrast, we investigated the intrinsic adjuvant-like properties of the AuNP platform using both in vitro and in vivo models (Figure 2A and 2D). The results indicated that AuNP-RBD effectively promoted BMDC maturation, as evidenced by the pronounced upregulation of costimulatory markers CD80 and CD86 (Figure S2A‒S2D, Table S10), alongside the increased mRNA level of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including Il12, Tnfa, Il1b, and Il6 (Figure S2E‒S2H, Table S11). This enhanced cytokine profile may be attributed to the display of RBD antigens on the AuNP surface, which facilitates receptor clustering and potent downstream signaling. Consistently, AuNP-RBD treatment increased the expression of MHC-II, CD80, and CD86 on DCs within the mediastinal lymph nodes (mLNs), whereas RBD alone failed to elicit a similar response (Figure 2E–2H, Table S12). Furthermore, elevated mRNA levels of Cxcl10, Cxcl9, Ifng, Tnfa, Cd86, and Il1b were detected in the lung at 24 h post stimulation in AuNP-RBD-treated mice, further confirming potent local immune activation (Figure S2I‒S2N, Table S13). While ultrasmall AuNPs are known to be potent inflammasome activators, the concomitant increase in Cd86 and Cxcl9/10 in our study suggests that the AuNP-RBD complex functions as more than a simple carrier. By combining the intrinsic adjuvanticity of the 5 nm gold core with the structured display of RBD, our formulation effectively bridges the gap between innate sensing and the orchestration of a potent local immune environment in the mLNs.

The duration of antigen exposure within the respiratory tract is critical for inducing robust mucosal immunity since antigens can be rapidly cleared by ciliated cells. Thus, we assessed the pulmonary delivery and retention kinetics of our platform following intranasal administration of AuNP-RBD-Cy5 or RBD-Cy5 in mice. We monitored fluorescence signals in lungs harvested at multiple intervals following intranasal instillation. Ex vivo imaging performed with the in vivo imaging system (IVIS) revealed that conjugation with AuNPs prolongs antigen retention in the lungs at late time points. At 48 h post administration, the AuNP-RBD group exhibited markedly stronger residual RBD-Cy5 fluorescence than the soluble RBD group, resulting in extended local antigen exposure (Figure 2I–2J, Table S14). These findings demonstrate that the AuNP-based platform significantly potentiates the adaptive immune priming phase by accelerating antigen uptake, promoting DC maturation and inflammatory signaling, and extending the residence time of the antigen within the lung tissue.

Mucosal and Systemic Humoral Immunity Elicited by Intranasal AuNP-RBD/cGAMP

To further investigate the efficacy of the AuNP-RBD (Delta) mucosal vaccine, we performed a new round of mouse immunization experiments. Three groups of mice were intranasally immunized with 5 nm AuNP-RBD/cGAMP, RBD/cGAMP, or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (control) three times at 21 d intervals (Figure 3A). Sera and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were collected at 7 and 10 d after the final immunization, respectively, to evaluate humoral immune responses. Mice receiving AuNP-RBD/cGAMP treatment displayed significantly elevated serum titers of anti-Delta RBD IgG, IgG1, IgG2a, and IgA by day 49, representing 31-fold, 3-fold, 4-fold, and 105-fold increases, respectively, compared to the RBD/cGAMP group (Figure 3B, Table S15). Furthermore, we determined the titers of cross-binding antibodies against other sarbecoviruses in serum. The AuNP-based platform induced significantly higher cross-reactive IgG titers against SARS-CoV-2 XBB and bat SARSr-CoV WIV1 compared with the RBD/cGAMP group (Figure 3C, Table S16). In contrast, the IgG levels against BA.5 and SARS-CoV were comparable between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences observed (Figure 3C). In addition to systemic antibody responses, we found that BALF from AuNP-RBD/cGAMP-immunized mice exhibited noticeable elevations in cross-reactive antibodies compared with the RBD/cGAMP group (Figure 3D–3E, Table S17). Specifically, BALF IgG titers against SARS-CoV-2 (Delta and XBB), SARS-CoV, and SARSr-CoV WIV1 showed a significant 2- to 2.9-fold increase, while those against BA.5 remained comparable between the two groups (Figure 3D, Table S17). Furthermore, a marked 63- to 178-fold enhancement in sIgA was observed specifically against SARS-CoV-2 (Delta and XBB) and SARS-CoV (Figure 3E, Table S17). We further determined nAb titers in sera and BALF to evaluate the breadth of protection. Sera from the AuNP-RBD/cGAMP group demonstrated potent neutralization against a broad spectrum of coronaviruses (Figure 3F, Table S18). Specifically, the 50% neutralization titers (NT50) against SARS-CoV-2 Delta and BA.5 variants reached 113,566 and 12,141, respectively, and the AuNP-based vaccine notably maintained substantial potency against the highly immune-evasive XBB variant with a titer of 219 (Figure 3F). Beyond the SARS-CoV-2 lineage, the platform exhibited remarkable cross-neutralizing breadth across the Sarbecovirus subgenus, effectively neutralizing pseudotyped SARS-CoV and SARSr-CoVs (WIV1 and Rs3367) with NT50 values of 4469, 17,448, and 87,247, which marked 3-, 9-, and 6-fold increases over the RBD/cGAMP group, respectively (Figure 3F). Neutralization activity in BALF closely correlated with systemic antibody responses (Figure 3G, Table S19). Parallel to the serum profiles, the AuNP-RBD/cGAMP vaccine established a formidable immunological barrier within the respiratory mucosa. In the BALF, NT50 titers against the SARS-CoV-2 Delta, BA.5, and XBB variants were 6668, 1151, and 202, respectively, representing 9-, 4-, and 9-fold increases relative to the free RBD group (Figure 3G). The localized cross-neutralization against divergent sarbecoviruses was even more pronounced. Specifically, NT50 values in the BALF for SARS-CoV, SARSr-CoV WIV1, and SARSr-CoV Rs3367 reached 142, 140, and 379, respectively (Figure 3G). Notably, these localized responses represented remarkable 27-fold and 16-fold increases for SARS-CoV and SARSr-CoV Rs3367 compared to the RBD/cGAMP group (Figure 3G). To further rule out the effect of cGAMP alone, we conducted an independent immunization experiment, which confirmed that the observed robust responses were strictly antigen-specific. Mice receiving only the cGAMP adjuvant showed baseline levels of IgG comparable to the PBS group, exhibiting a slight background immunity in neutralizing assays but no specific anti-Delta RBD binding activity (Figure S3A‒S3C, Table S20).

The induction of robust GC reactions is fundamental for the generation of high-affinity antibodies and long-lived humoral immunity. Flow cytometry analysis of mLNs at 10 d post final immunization confirmed a robust GC B cell response in the AuNP-RBD/cGAMP group (Figures 3H and S3D, Table S21). Specifically, the percentage of GC B cells (B220+GL7+) in this group reached 7.30% ± 2.55%, which was significantly higher than that of the RBD/cGAMP group (3.71% ± 0.21%) (Figure 3H). In line with the enhanced GC activity, mice immunized with AuNP-RBD/cGAMP exhibited the highest frequency of follicular helper T (Tfh) cells in the mLNs (Figures 3I and S3E, Table S22). These Tfh cells accounted for 3.63% ± 0.51% of total CD4+ T cells, markedly surpassing the levels observed in both the RBD/cGAMP group (1.32% ± 0.22%) and the PBS control (Figure 3I). Correlating with these findings, the AuNP-RBD/cGAMP group elicited a superior SARS-CoV-2 RBD-specific memory B cell response, achieving a substantially elevated frequency compared to the RBD/cGAMP group (Figures 3J and S3F, Table S23).

At the end of the study (4 d post infection), mice were euthanized to assess viral replication in the lungs and brains. Lung tissue analysis revealed that viral loads were notably reduced in the AuNP-RBD/cGAMP-treated mice compared to the RBD/cGAMP-treated mice (Figure 5E, Table S30). Specifically, compared to the PBS control group, immunization with RBD/cGAMP reduced viral RNA by approximately 41-fold. Notably, the AuNP-RBD/cGAMP group maintained an even lower viral load, achieving an approximately 11-fold further reduction compared to the free RBD/cGAMP formulation (Figure 5E). In the brain, although no significant difference was detected between the AuNP-RBD/cGAMP and RBD/cGAMP groups, both formulations exhibited comparably low viral loads that were significantly reduced compared to the PBS control group (Figure 5F, Table S31). Our data suggested that AuNP-based vaccination significantly reduced viral loads and alleviated lung inflammatory damage in hACE2-Tg mice by mitigating the excessive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines.

Serological Profiling of Immune Responses Induced by RBD-Fc Adjuvanted with STING Agonist

A further advantage of AuNPs is their ability to simultaneously display multiple coronavirus RBD antigens on their surface to achieve broad-spectrum protection [26,27]. While our single-antigen AuNP-RBD/cGAMP vaccine conferred strong protection against sarbecoviruses, it likely lacks broad cross-efficacy across the wider β-CoVs genus, particularly against merbecoviruses, due to the substantial genetic and structural divergence highlighted in our phylogenetic analysis (Figure 6A). To select which coronavirus RBDs to display on AuNPs for maximizing the coverage of induced nAbs against both sarbecoviruses and merbecoviruses, we constructed a phylogenetic tree of representative β-CoVs (Figure 6A) and individually expressed RBDs from representative β-CoVs, including SARS-CoV-2 D614G strain and its variants Delta, BA.2, BA.5, BQ.1, XBB, EG.5, BA.2.86, and JN.1, as well as SARS-CoV, SARSr-CoV WIV1, MERS-CoV, NeoCoV-T510F, and MjHKU4r-CoV-1. We further evaluated the induction of TRM precursor cells in the lung parenchyma. Unexpectedly, we found early CD4+ and CD8+ TRM frequencies reached 78.9% ± 3.3% and 41.4% ± 10.2%, representing higher proportions than the Mix/cGAMP group (64.5% ± 6.3% and 22.9% ± 2.8%, respectively), with the CD8+ compartment showing a nearly 2-fold expansion (Figure 8D–8E, Table S43). Complementing this localized protection, the vaccine further expanded the early systemic TEM cell pool in the spleen (Figure 8F–8G, Table S44). AuNP-Mix/cGAMP increased the CD4+ TEM precursor cell frequency to 46.3% ± 5.6%, compared to 36.7% ± 3.1% in the Mix/cGAMP group (Figure 8F–8G). Additionally, early CD8+ TEM precursor cell frequencies remained comparable between the groups at 15.3% ± 4.4% and 14.7% ± 4.2%, respectively (Figure 8F–8G). To further evaluate cellular immunity, ELISPOT assays were employed to assess antigen-specific IFN-γ responses (Figure 8H–8J, Table S45). The results showed that AuNP-Mix/cGAMP immunization significantly boosted Th1-biased responses, as evidenced by the elevated frequencies of IFN-γ+ splenocytes specific to SARS-CoV-2 (Delta), SARS-CoV, and MERS-CoV RBDs, representing a 4- to 24-fold increase over Th1-biased responses of the Mix/cGAMP control group (Figure 8H–8J). This robust cytokine production underscores the superior capacity of the multivalent AuNP display to prime potent, cross-reactive T cell effectors.

Discussion

The development of respiratory mucosal vaccines has long posed a significant challenge in vaccinology. Recent advancements in SARS-CoV-2 formulations—such as intranasally or aerosol-delivered viral vectors and alveolar-targeting dry powders—have demonstrated promising efficacy in eliciting robust mucosal immunity [[28], [29], [30]]. However, it remains difficult to develop mucosal delivery strategies that effectively overcome respiratory barriers, while simultaneously expanding the breadth of coronavirus vaccine protection. In this study, we demonstrate that 5 nm AuNPs represent a versatile mucosal delivery platform. When displaying single or multiple coronavirus RBD antigens together with the STING agonist cGAMP, intranasal administration enabled efficient respiratory barrier penetration, prolonged antigen retention, improved DC uptake, while eliciting potent, broad-spectrum mucosal and systemic T- and B-cell responses against diverse β-CoVs. These findings establish size-optimized AuNPs as a promising platform for next-generation mucosal vaccines.

Nanoparticle-based mosaic display has emerged as an effective strategy for inducing broad-spectrum immune protection against SARS-CoV-2. For instance, ferritin and Mi3 nanoparticles have been employed to co-display multiple SARS-CoV-2 RBDs on their surfaces via the SpyCatcher-SpyTag system, thereby stimulating B cell receptors (BCRs) that can crosslink using both antigen-binding Fabs between adjacent, nonidentical RBDs [31,32]. This approach potently activates B cells and induces high levels of nAbs against diverse coronaviruses. However, these protein-based nanoparticles may elicit immune responses directed against the nanocarrier itself. Furthermore, most of these protein nanoparticles have been primarily applied to intramuscular immunization. Although an RBD-displaying vaccine based on Helicobacter pylori ferritin (HPF) has been shown to induce broad mucosal immunity via intranasal delivery, its fixed particle size limits further optimization for mucosal applications [33]. This underscores the need for novel and flexible nanocarriers tailored for mucosal delivery. Inorganic nanoparticles, such as AuNPs, offer advantages in tunable particle size and minimal carrier immunogenicity. While nanoparticles exceeding 100 nm display limited mucus penetration across the respiratory air–liquid interface [21], our findings expand this concept to smaller sub-100 nm particles and confirm particle diameter as a dominant determinant of intranasal vaccine performance. Specifically, 5 nm AuNP-RBD efficiently permeates respiratory mucus to access submucosal immune compartments, while most 15 nm AuNPs become trapped inside the mucus barrier and fail to achieve effective tissue delivery. Though soluble free RBD readily crosses mucus, unformulated antigen tends to be rapidly cleared locally after penetration, which may limit durable antigen presentation and robust immune priming. Beyond mucus-trafficking efficiency dictated by particle size, nanoparticle surface antigen density constitutes a second independent layer governing immunogenicity. Recent evidence highlights that antigen density on nanoparticle surfaces, rather than absolute antigen quantity, is critical for immune potency [16]. Our streptavidin-conjugated AuNP design enables anchoring of up to four biotinylated RBD molecules per surface streptavidin unit, precisely elevating surface antigen density. Such optimized antigen packing might strengthen BCR cross-linking signaling, facilitate productive GC maturation, and preferentially drive generation of high-affinity nAb responses.

Beyond these optimized biophysical properties for mucosal trafficking and high-density display, the AuNP platform offers essential translational and immunological benefits. From a manufacturing perspective, AuNPs are straightforward to synthesize and highly scalable [34]. As delivery vehicles, they effectively penetrate the respiratory barrier and resist mucociliary clearance, prolonging antigen retention. Their viral-like size and geometry facilitate efficient uptake by DCs, and beyond their carrier function, AuNPs possess intrinsic adjuvant properties that promote cytokine secretion [17]. Crucially, inorganic AuNPs serve as scaffolds with low carrier-specific immunogenicity that circumvent the carrier-induced epitope suppression typical of protein-based vehicles, ensuring that immune responses remain focused on target antigens. Leveraging the dual-adjuvant effects of AuNPs and cGAMP, combined with efficient mucosal delivery, intranasal immunization with cGAMP-adjuvanted AuNPs displaying only the SARS-CoV-2 Delta RBD elicited potent nAbs in both BALF and serum against a broad panel of viruses, including immune-evasive variants such as XBB, as well as SARS-CoV and the SARSr-CoV WIV1. This response significantly exceeded that induced by free RBD with cGAMP. Additionally, vaccination promoted the early formation of robust CD8+ TRM cells in the lungs and systemic CD8+ TEM cells, both critical for viral clearance. Heterologous challenge studies in mice further demonstrated that the AuNP-RBD formulation conferred superior protection against SARS-CoV-2 BA.2.2 variant challenge compared to free RBD, underscoring the broad protective efficacy of this platform.

Rational antigen selection is critical for designing broadly protective vaccines. Previous serotyping studies using mRNA vaccines classified 27 SARS-CoV-2 variants into six serotypes based on cross-neutralization profiles [35,36]. However, because our subunit vaccine platform employs a STING agonist adjuvant that may alter immunogenicity profiles, we systematically evaluated the cross-neutralizing activity of individual RBDs from diverse β-CoVs, including SARS-CoV, SARSr-CoV, MERS-CoV, and MERSr-CoV. Our analysis revealed that among SARS-CoV-2 variants, BA.5 RBD induced the broadest neutralization against both pre-Omicron and Omicron lineages. Nevertheless, we selected Delta and XBB RBDs for co-display to achieve even broader antigenic coverage. Notably, RBDs from MERSr-CoV elicited weak but detectable cross-neutralization, suggesting the presence of conserved epitopes that could inform future pan-sarbecovirus or pan-merbecovirus vaccine design. This preliminary serological grouping was implemented via intramuscular immunization of RBD-Fc combined with CF501, a STING agonist. It is well recognized that identical antigens can trigger divergent immune hierarchies under distinct tissue microenvironments or alternative adjuvant formulations when delivered via intramuscular vs intranasal routes. Although our final intranasal vaccine replaces CF501 with cGAMP and switches from intramuscular to mucosal administration, both compounds act as canonical STING agonists targeting the same downstream signaling cascade. Encouragingly, our neutralization data confirm that the six selected RBD combinations still retain broad protective breadth after nasal delivery with cGAMP. Co-display of six representative RBDs on AuNP surfaces elicited broad-spectrum immune responses. Compared to simple mixtures of the six RBD antigens, the mosaic AuNP vaccine induced significantly more potent nAb responses against both sarbecoviruses and merbecoviruses in BALF and serum. Notably, AuNP-displayed antigens substantially boosted nAb titers against MERS-CoV and MERSr-CoV, including NeoCoV-T510F and MjHKU4r-CoV-1. These findings suggest that this mosaic vaccine platform may play an important role in protecting against future emerging merbecoviruses.

Despite the promising efficacy of our AuNP-based mucosal vaccine platform, certain aspects warrant further investigation. Although a substantial portion of administered AuNPs can be cleared from the body, their potential long-term retention in certain organs remains a concern that requires in-depth safety assessment [37]. Notably, however, AuNP-based vaccines have already entered clinical trials, with encouraging safety and immunogenicity profiles reported, highlighting their potential as a vaccine delivery platform [38]. Additionally, while our study demonstrated protective efficacy against BA.2.2 challenge in vivo, challenge experiments against merbecoviruses were not performed. Nevertheless, the potent nAb titers induced by our mosaic vaccine against diverse merbecoviruses, including MERS-CoV and MERS-related viruses, suggest promising protective potential against this clade. Notwithstanding these limitations, our mouse model data provide compelling proof-of-concept that AuNP-based mosaic display can induce broadly protective mucosal immunity against both sarbecoviruses and merbecoviruses. These findings support further clinical development of this platform as a next-generation mucosal vaccine strategy to enhance pandemic preparedness against emerging β-CoV threats.

In summary, we developed a size-optimized AuNP-based mucosal vaccine platform that overcomes respiratory barriers and elicits potent mucosal and systemic T- and B-cell responses against diverse sarbecoviruses and merbecoviruses, substantially outperforming simple free antigen. This work establishes AuNP-based mosaic display as a promising strategy for next-generation mucosal vaccines against emerging respiratory viruses.

Materials and methods

Cells

HEK293T (Cat#CRL-1573), Caco-2 (Cat#HTB-37), and A549 (Cat#CCL-185) cell lines used in pseudovirus neutralization assays were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Virginia, USA). Expi293F cells were purchased from Life Technologies (Massachusetts, USA; Cat#A14527). HEK293T, Caco-2, and A549 cells were cultured and propagated in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) (Meilun, Liaoning, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, California, USA), streptomycin (100 μg/mL), and penicillin (100 U/mL). Expi293F cells were maintained in Expi293 expression medium (OPM Biosciences, Shanghai, China).

Protein Expression and Purification

Recombinant RBD proteins (RBD-His-Avi and RBD-Fc) were produced using an optimized mammalian expression system in Expi293F cells as previously reported [24,[39], [40], [41], [42]]. Briefly, cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded at a density of 3 × 106 cells/mL and transfected with expression plasmids utilizing the OPM-TR01 reagent (OPM Biosciences, Shanghai, China), at a preoptimized ratio. The culture supernatants were collected seven days post transfection. For purification, RBD-His-Avi was isolated via Ni-NTA resin, whereas RBD-Fc was captured using protein A resin (GenScript, Jiangsu, China). The purity and integrity of the recombinant proteins were verified by SDS-PAGE before being aliquoted and stored at −80 °C.

Preparation and Characterization of AuNP-RBD

AuNPs were synthesized by citrate reduction of HAuCl4. Briefly, an HAuCl4 solution was brought to a boil, followed by the rapid addition of sodium citrate under vigorous stirring. The mixture was refluxed for 15 min until the solution turned a characteristic burgundy red, signifying the formation of AuNPs. Particle size was finely tuned by adjusting the molar ratio of HAuCl4 to sodium citrate. To facilitate oriented antigen display, AuNPs were first functionalized with carboxyl-terminated thiol-PEG (HS-PEG-COOH) via Au-S bonding, and streptavidin was subsequently covalently conjugated to the carboxyl groups using EDC/NHS activation chemistry. In parallel, RBD-biotin was prepared by labeling the RBD-His-Avi protein with a biotin labeling kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The biotinylated RBD was then incubated with the streptavidin-functionalized AuNPs overnight at 4 °C. For the multivalent formulation AuNP-Mix-RBD, six distinct RBD-biotin variants—selected based on STING agonist-guided serotyping of nAb responses—were premixed prior to co-incubation with the streptavidin-AuNPs under identical conditions. Following three wash cycles with PBS to remove unconjugated proteins, the coupling efficiency was indirectly determined by quantifying the residual RBD concentration in the collected supernatants.

LSPR spectra of bare, streptavidin-functionalized, and RBD-conjugated 5 nm and 15 nm AuNPs were analyzed by UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy on a Tecan Infinite M200PRO microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Canton of Zurich, Switzerland). Briefly, the nanoparticle samples were diluted to appropriate concentrations in PBS and added to a transparent, flat-bottom 96-well microplate. Absorbance spectra were recorded over a wavelength range of 400–700 nm at a resolution of 2 nm at room temperature. The shifts in the LSPR peak wavelengths were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (v10.1.2; GraphPad Software, California, USA) to corroborate the successful loading of biotinylated RBD antigens onto the AuNP surfaces.

The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of the functionalized AuNPs were measured by DLS at 25 °C using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, West Midlands, UK). Samples were diluted appropriately in Milli-Q water to avoid multiple scattering and equilibrated for 120 s prior to measurement.

The morphological characteristics and core size of the AuNPs were visualized using a transmission electron microscope (JEOL-1400 Flash, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) operating at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV. For sample preparation, 10 μL of the nanoparticle suspension was dropped onto a carbon-coated copper grid (300 mesh) and allowed to sediment for 5 min. Excess liquid was gently removed with filter paper. The grid was then air-dried at room temperature before being loaded into the microscope for imaging and observation.

Animal Vaccination

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) female BALB/c mice (6 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). SPF male hACE2-Tg mice (10 weeks old) were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China). All mice were in good health. Routine animal maintenance and noninfectious in vivo procedures were conducted in an SPF facility at Fudan University. All viral challenge experiments were strictly performed within the animal biosafety level 3 (ABSL-3) facility and were approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee of Fudan University (approval number FD-ABSL-3-302). To ensure adequate statistical power, sample sizes were calculated based on published literature and our preliminary data. Individual mice served as the experimental unit and were randomly allocated to groups via computer-generated sequences, with investigators blinded to group assignments during allocation, in vivo procedures, and data analysis. All animals that completed the experimental protocol were included in the final analysis, and no animals were excluded. To minimize environmental confounding variables, animal cages were spatially distributed across multiple racks, and treatments and measurements were performed in a rotating order. Finally, carbon dioxide inhalation was used for euthanasia to mitigate animal distress. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines, and the protocols were approved by the Institutional Laboratory Animal Care of Fudan University (2023-09-YL-LZZ-86).

To investigate the size-dependent immunological properties of AuNPs, multivalent nanovaccines were constructed using 5 nm and 15 nm AuNPs. For the in vivo study, 6-week-old BALB/c mice (n = 3 per group) were randomly allocated into experimental groups (total n = 21). Mice were intranasally immunized twice at a 21 d interval with the respective vaccine formulations containing an equivalent antigen dose of 5 μg of Delta RBD per mouse. To further explore the immune-enhancing efficacy of the nanovaccines, the STING agonist cGAMP was supplemented as an adjuvant at a dose of 20 μg per mouse in the specified groups. A separate group of mice administered with an equal volume of sterile PBS served as the negative control. Seven days after the final immunization (day 28), blood samples were collected, and the mice were euthanized.

To investigate the role of 5 nm AuNPs in promoting dendritic cell (DC) maturation in vivo, 6-week-old BALB/c mice (n = 4 per group, total n = 12) were randomly assigned to three experimental groups. The mice were intranasally administered with PBS (negative control), Delta RBD (5 μg per mouse), or AuNP-RBD (containing an equivalent dose of 5 μg Delta RBD per mouse). At 24 h post immunization, the mice were euthanized for tissue collection. mLNs were harvested, processed into single-cell suspensions, and analyzed via flow cytometry to evaluate the maturation profiles of DCs. Simultaneously, lung tissues were collected for total RNA extraction, followed by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) to determine the mRNA expression levels of relevant cytokines.

To compare the immunogenicity of 5 nm AuNP-RBD vs RBD, female BALB/c mice (6 weeks old, n = 4 per group, total n = 12) were intranasally immunized three times with a 3 w interval. Each mouse received 5 μg of RBD protein in a 30 μL volume of Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS). In the AuNP-RBD group, the antigen was conjugated to 100 μg of AuNPs, whereas the adjuvanted groups received an additional 20 μg of soluble 2′3′-cGAMP (Med Chem Express, New Jersey, USA; Cat#HY-100564A) in a total volume of 20 μL DPBS per dose via intranasal instillation. Seven days after the third immunization, blood samples were collected. Ten days after the final immunization, the mice were euthanized, and tissues were harvested for subsequent experiments.

To further compare the immunological profiles of the 5 nm AuNP-Mix and the simple RBD mixture (Mix), another group of female BALB/c mice (6 weeks old, n = 4 per group, total n = 12) was randomly assigned into three groups (the PBS control, Mix, and AuNP-Mix groups). These mice underwent the identical three-dose intranasal immunization regimen with a 3 w interval, along with the same downstream blood sampling (day 49) and tissue harvesting (day 52) schedule. Each immunized mouse received a matched total antigen dose of 5 μg formulated with 20 μg of cGAMP adjuvant.

To generate hyperimmune antisera for serotyping, female BALB/c mice (6 weeks old, n = 3 per group, total n = 42) were administered intramuscularly with 5 μg of individual purified RBD-Fc proteins, each formulated with 20 μg of STING agonist CF501 (Cas#2408723-12-4), an adjuvant developed in our previous study [24]. Mice were primed on day 0 and boosted on day 21 and day 42 to elicit high-titer nAbs for downstream antigenic characterization. Seven days after the final immunization (day 49), blood samples were collected, and the mice were subsequently euthanized.

To rule out potential background immunological interference from the adjuvant itself, an additional in vivo experiment was conducted using an independent cohort of mice (6-week-old BALB/c mice, n = 4 per group, total n = 16). The animals were randomly assigned to four groups: the PBS control group, the cGAMP alone group (20 μg of cGAMP per mouse), the RBD/cGAMP group (5 μg of Delta RBD formulated with 20 μg of cGAMP), and the 5 nm AuNP-RBD/cGAMP group (an equivalent dose of 5 μg of Delta RBD formulated with 20 μg of cGAMP). These mice received a two-dose intranasal immunization regimen administered at a 2 w interval. On day 21 (7 d after the final immunization), blood samples were collected, and the mice were subsequently euthanized.

To assess mucosal protection, male hACE2-Tg mice (10 weeks old, n = 5, total n = 15) were randomly allocated to receive three intranasal doses of PBS, Delta RBD/cGAMP (5 μg of Delta RBD formulated with 20 μg of cGAMP), or 5 nm AuNP-RBD/cGAMP (an equivalent dose of 5 μg of Delta RBD formulated with 20 μg of cGAMP) at 28 d intervals. At day 66, animals were subjected to a heterologous challenge with the SARS-CoV-2 BA.2.2 variant (1.0 × 104 plaque-forming units) via the intranasal route to simulate natural infection and assess viral clearance.

Sample Collection

Blood samples from immunized mice were collected at designated time points according to the immunization schedule. Collected sera were heat-inactivated at 56 °C for 30 min. To collect BALF, 500 μL of ice-cold PBS was instilled through a tracheal catheter. Lung tissues were dissociated using DNase I (40 μg/mL) and collagenase Ⅳ (0.5 mg/mL) at 37 °C for 30 min to obtain single-cell suspensions. Spleens and mLNs were mechanically homogenized using a sterile 1 mL syringe plunger. All resulting cell suspensions were filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer, washed with ice-cold PBS, and finally resuspended in complete RPMI 1640 (Meilun, Liaoning, China) medium for downstream analysis.

Primary BMDCs were generated from 8-week-old female BALB/c mice (n = 3). Briefly, bone marrow cells were flushed from the femurs and tibias, subjected to red blood cell lysis, and subsequently cultured in complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL GM-CSF and 10 ng/mL IL-4 for 6 d to induce differentiation into immature BMDCs.

Mucus Penetration Analysis

The mucus-penetrating behavior of AuNPs was evaluated using multiple particle tracking in artificial airway mucus as previously described [43]. To enable visualization, biotinylated RBD was labeled with Cy5 NHS ester (Meilun, Liaoning, China; Cat#146368-14-1) at a 1:1 molar ratio of dye to protein. The mixture was incubated at 4 °C overnight, followed by ultrafiltration to fully remove the free dye. Subsequently, the Cy5-labeled biotinylated RBD was conjugated to streptavidin-modified AuNPs by incubation at 4 °C overnight. Finally, the mixture was centrifuged to remove any unconjugated RBD in the supernatant, yielding the purified fluorescently labeled nano-vaccine formulation. The fluorescent complexes were introduced into artificial airway mucus and equilibrated for 30 min. Time-series imaging was subsequently performed using a LSM 980 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Baden-Württemberg, Germany), and the recorded images were analyzed using ZEISS ZEN software (v3.12) to track individual particle trajectories and calculate their MSD.

In Vitro BMDC Uptake and Activation

To assess DC responses, immature BALB/c-derived BMDCs (1 × 106 cells/well) were treated with the indicated antigens for 24 h. Maturation was evaluated by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, California, USA) after blocking Fc receptors (anti-CD16/32) and staining with fixable viability stain 510 (BD Biosciences, California, USA; Cat#564406), and an antibody panel consisting of APC-CD11c (BD Biosciences, California, USA; Cat#550261), PE-MHC-II (eBioscience/Invitrogen, California, USA; Cat#12-5321-82), BV421-CD80 (BD Biosciences, California, USA; Cat#566285), and BB700-CD86 (BD Biosciences, California, USA; Cat#742120) antibodies. Transcriptional levels of pro-inflammatory cytokine genes (Il6, Tnfa, Il12, and Il1b) were quantified via RT-qPCR using PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan; Cat#RR037A) and SYBR™ green master mix (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan; Cat#RR820A) on an Archimed Real-Time PCR System (RocGene, Beijing, China). Gene encoding glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) served as the endogenous reference gene, with relative fold changes in gene expression calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The qPCR primer sequences used in this study were identical to those previously described [24], with the exception of the following newly added primers: Gapdh (forward: 5′-AGGTCGGTGTGAACGGATTTG-3′, reverse: 5′-GGGGTCGTTGATGGCAACA-3′), Ccl5 (forward: 5′-GCTGCTTTGCCTACCTCTCC-3′, reverse: 5′-TCGAGTGACAAACACGACTGC-3′), Il-12 (forward: 5′-TGTGGAATGGCGTCTCTGTC-3’, reverse: 5′-AGTTCAATGGGCAGGGTCTC-3’), Ifng (forward: 5′-ATGAACGCTACACACTGCATC-3′, reverse: 5′-CCATCCTTTTGCCAGTTCCTC-3′), and Cd86 (forward: 5′-CTTACGGAAGCACCCACGAT-3′, reverse: 5′-CGGCAGATATGCAGTCCCAT-3′). For uptake assays, BMDCs were incubated with Cy5-labeled antigens and analyzed by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, California, USA) and confocal microscopy (LSM 980).

In Vivo DC Activation and Lung Cytokine Profiling

BALB/c mice (n = 4 per group) were immunized intranasally to evaluate in vivo activation. Twenty-four hours post immunization, mLNs were harvested for flow cytometric analysis of DC maturation (CD11c+MHC-II+CD80+CD86+). Simultaneously, total lung RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan; Cat#9108). RT-qPCR was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit and SYBR™ green master mix on the Archimed Real-Time PCR System. Data were expressed as relative fold changes normalized to Gapdh using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Ex Vivo Fluorescence Imaging

Mice (n = 3 per group at each time point) were intranasally immunized with RBD-Cy5 or AuNP-RBD-Cy5 (5 μg RBD equivalent). At 6, 24, and 48 h post administration, mice were euthanized via CO2 asphyxiation, and their lungs were harvested. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of the lungs was performed using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) spectrum imaging system (PerkinElmer, Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

ELISA was performed as previously described [24,39,44,45]. Briefly, high-binding polystyrene plates (Corning Life Sciences, Massachusetts, USA) were coated with individual RBD antigens (SARS-CoV-2, SARSr-CoV, or MERSr-CoV; 1 μg/mL) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Coated plates were then blocked using blocking buffer (PBS containing 5% bovine serum albumin [BSA]) for 2 h at 37 °C. Immunized animal serum samples were diluted 1:100, and BALF samples were diluted 1:6 in PBST (PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20) to yield 11 dilutions in total, followed by incubation with the plates for 1 h at 37 °C. After washing with PBST four times, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (goat anti-mouse IgG/IgG1/IgG2a/IgA) (Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) was applied to the plates and incubated for 45 min at 37 °C. After incubation with the secondary antibody, the ELISA plates were washed with PBST four times. Subsequently, 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate solution was added for detection. Finally, the reaction was quenched with 2 M H2SO4, and the optical density at 450 nm (OD450) was measured using the Tecan Infinite M200PRO microplate reader. ELISA endpoint titers were expressed as the highest reciprocal serum dilution exhibiting an OD450 value > 2.1-fold over the background. Serum samples with endpoint titers below 1:100 and BALF samples with endpoint titers below 1:6 were assigned default values of 1:50 and 1:3, respectively.

Pseudovirus Neutralization Assay

Production of pseudoviruses was conducted as previously described [46]. SARS-CoV-2 (D614G, Delta, BA.2, BA.5, BQ.1, XBB, EG.5, BA.2.86, and JN.1), SARSr-CoVs (SARS-CoV, WIV1, and Rs3367), and MERSr-CoVs (MERS-CoV, NeoCoV-T510F, and MjHKU4r-CoV-1) pseudoparticles were generated via co-transfection of HEK293T cells with pcDNA3.1 plasmids encoding spike proteins and the pNL4-3.Luc.R–E– reporter plasmid. Cell culture supernatants were replaced with fresh DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS at 8 h post transfection. Followed by a 48 h incubation, cell supernatant containing the pseudoviruses was harvested and stored at −80 °C. Detection of the nAb against pseudoviruses was conducted as previously described [24,39,44,46]. Briefly, Caco-2 cells (for SARS-CoV-2 variants, SARS-CoV, and MERSr-CoVs) or A549 cells (specifically for SARSr-CoV WIV1 and Rs3367) were plated at 7000 cells/well in a 96-well plate. After 12 h of adherence at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator, serially diluted serum or BALF samples were preincubated with different pseudotyped virus strains for 45 min before being transferred to the 96-well culture plate. After 48 h of incubation, relative luciferase activity (RLU) values were quantified using the luciferase assay system (Promega, Wisconsin, USA). The NT50 was characterized as the dilution factor required for serum or BALF to exhibit a 50% decrease in luminescence relative to pseudoviruses-only control groups. Samples demonstrating neutralizing titers below threshold levels (serum < 1:100, BALF < 1:6) were allocated standardized baseline values of 1:50 and 1:3, respectively.

ELISPOT Assay

To assess cellular IFN-γ+ responses, mouse IFN-γ ELISPOT assays were performed using a commercial mouse IFN-γ ELISPOT plus kit (ALP) (Mabtech, Stockholm County, Sweden) following the manufacturer's protocol and as previously described [24,39,44,46]. Briefly, wells of an ELISPOT 96-well filtration plate precoated with anti-mouse IFN-γ capture antibodies were washed and blocked. Lymphocytes were then seeded and stimulated with 10 μg/mL of Delta-RBD-His or SARS-CoV-2 RBD peptide pool (10 μg/mL; SinoBiological, Beijing, China; Cat#PP002-A) for 48 h under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). Following cell removal via extensive washing, 50 μL of biotinylated detection antibodies was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at room temperature, followed by a 1 h incubation with streptavidin-ALP. For spot visualization, BCIP/NBT substrate was applied for color development, and the reaction was stopped by washing the wells with distilled water. High-resolution micrographs of the wells were captured, and all spot enumeration was performed using an ImmunoSpot S6 analyzer (Cellular Technology, Ohio, USA).

Flow Cytometry

After red blood cell lysis, washed cells were resuspended in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (PBS with 1% BSA). Nonspecific antibody binding was blocked with anti-CD16/32 (BD Biosciences, California, USA; Cat#553141) for 15 min on ice, followed by fixable viability stain 510 staining to ensure cell viability. To characterize different cell populations, cells were incubated with the following designated antibody panels on ice for 30 min.

Spleen RBD-specific memory B cells: PE-Cy7-anti-B220 (Invitrogen, California, USA; Cat#25-0452-82), FITC-anti-CD38 (BioLegend, California, USA; Cat#165608), BV421-anti-IgD (BioLegend, California, USA; Cat#405725), APC-RBD (BD Biosciences, California, USA; Cat#554067), and PE-RBD (BD Biosciences, California, USA; Cat#554061); lymph node GC B cells: PE-Cy7-anti-B220 and APC-anti-GL7 (BioLegend, California, USA; Cat#144617); lymph node Tfh cells: FITC-anti-CD3 (BD Biosciences, California, USA; Cat#553062), AF700-anti-CD4 (BD Biosciences, California, USA; Cat#557956), APC-anti-PD-1 (Invitrogen, California, USA; Cat#17-9985-82), and PE-anti-CXCR5 (Invitrogen, California, USA; Cat#12-7185-82); splenic early TEM cells: FITC-anti-CD3 (BD Biosciences, California, USA; Cat#553062), AF700-anti-CD4, APC-anti-CD8 (BioLegend, California, USA; Cat#100712), BV421-anti-CD44 (BD Biosciences, California, USA; Cat#563970), and PE-Cy7-anti-CD62L (Invitrogen, California, USA; Cat#25-0621-82); lung early CD4+ TRM cells: FITC-anti-CD3, AF700-anti-CD4, RY610-anti-CD44 (BD Biosciences, California, USA; Cat#758265), PE-anti-CD62L (BioLegend, California, USA; Cat#161203), and PE-Cy7-anti-CD69 (BioLegend, California, USA; Cat#104512); lung early CD8+ TRM cells: FITC-anti-CD3, APC-anti-CD8, RY601-anti-CD44, PE-anti-CD62L, PE-Cy7-anti-CD69, and BV421-anti-CD103 (BD Biosciences, California, USA; Cat#748259).

Cell acquisition was performed on a Beckman Coulter flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, California, USA), and the resulting data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1).

SARS-CoV-2 Challenge Test

SARS-CoV-2 (BA.2.2 variant) challenge experiments were performed as previously reported [24,47]. Briefly, vaccinated mice were intranasally challenged with 1.0 × 104 plaque-forming units (PFU) of virus in a total volume of 40 μL PBS. Body weights were recorded daily. Terminal procedures were conducted on day 4 post exposure with subsequent aseptic collection of pulmonary and cerebral parenchyma. Trizol-based isolation methodology (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) was employed for total RNA purification from homogenized specimens. Subsequent virological assessments involved quantitative reverse transcription PCR analyses using the One Step PrimeScript™ RT-PCR system (Perfect Real Time) (Takara, Shiga, Japan) for nucleic acid quantification, following standardized laboratory protocols. The primers and probe targeting the SARS-CoV-2 N gene were as follows: SARS-CoV-2 N-F: 5′-GGGGAACTTCTCCTGCTAGAAT-3′; SARS-CoV-2 N-R: 5′-CAGACATTTTGCTCTCAAGCTG-3′; SARS-CoV-2 N-probe: 5′-FAM-TTGCTGCTGCTTGACAGATT-TAMRA-3′. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using the Bio-Rad fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (Bio-Rad, California, USA) with the following program: 45 °C for 5 min, 95 °C for 10 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, 50 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. At 4 d post challenge (dpc), lung tissues were harvested to quantify the relative mRNA levels of Ifnb1, Cxcl10, Ccl5, and Il6 via RT-qPCR, with data normalized to Gapdh using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Additionally, lungs harvested at 4 d post infection were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and processed for standard hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining following standard protocols [48,49].

Sequence Alignment and Phylogenetic Analysis

Coronavirus RBD sequences were retrieved from NCBI and aligned via MUSCLE (v12.1.2). Phylogenetic trees were generated using the Maximum Likelihood method with 1000 bootstrap replicates in MEGA v11, and visualized using the ChiPlot web server (https://www.chiplot.online/).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis data are listed in the legend of each experiment. Statistically significant differences were assessed using the unpaired t-test, one-way ANOVA with Tukey's correction, or the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's multiple comparisons, two-way ANOVA with Šidák's multiple comparisons test, or nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Quantitative results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or geometric mean ± SD throughout the study. All statistical tests were performed with GraphPad Prism (v10.1.2; GraphPad Software, California, USA).
