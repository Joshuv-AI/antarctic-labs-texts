The impact of hereditary TTP on psychological health, cognition, and well-being: a patient-reported outcomes study∗
Authors: Azra Borogovac, Joanne Albert, Julia Naifeh, Honieh Sowdagar, Marissa Schraner, Johanna A Kremer Hovinga, Sara K Vesely, James N George, Deirdra R Terrell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•hTTP patients report worse fatigue, depression, anxiety, and cognitive function compared to the general US population.•Longitudinal measurements show persistent impairments, highlighting the sustained psychological and cognitive impact of hTTP.

Introduction

Hereditary thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (hTTP) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder due to biallelic pathogenic mutations in the ADAMTS13 gene, resulting in little to no activity of the ADAMTS13 plasma protease, which is required for cleaving von Willebrand factor (VWF) multimers. Without ADAMTS13, the persisting ultralarge VWF multimers trigger uncontrolled formation of VWF-platelet microthrombi that cause microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and organ ischemia.1 The estimated prevalence of hTTP is between 0.5 and 2 cases per million,1,2 although a recent study using a worldwide genome database predicts the prevalence of hTTP biallelic pathogenic mutations to be 23 cases per million, indicating the potential existence of many undiagnosed patients.2

The clinical presentation of hTTP can range from asymptomatic disease to severe morbidity and mortality. In the newborn period, affected infants may experience severe hemolysis, hyperbilirubinemia, thrombocytopenia, and stroke.3,4 Due to a significant delay in diagnosis of hTTP, newborns often receive exchange transfusions instead of the more appropriate hTTP treatments, such as plasma infusions.3,4 Pregnancy is also a well-recognized trigger, with many women being diagnosed during this period. Without prophylaxis, these cases often present with complications mimicking preeclampsia or HELLP (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets) syndrome and most often lead to fetal loss.5 Additionally, overt complications such as stroke, kidney injury, and death can occur at any age, with the incidence of major morbidity increasing with each decade of life.6,7 Stroke is the most common morbidity and occurs in approximately 20% of patients with hTTP by 20 years of age and 50% of patients by 50 years of age, with a similar prevalence between men and women.6,7

Studies of patients with hTTP have primarily focused on long-term morbidity and mortality during prospective follow-up, with little attention given to the ongoing daily symptoms experienced from patients’ perspectives.1 In our previous study of 26 patients with hTTP, we found that neurologic symptoms were common, including recurrent headaches (88%), poor concentration (65%), and depression (62%) that often began during childhood and persisted into adulthood.8 Yet, the broader impact of hTTP on patients’ lived experiences remains poorly understood. In contrast, the long-term effects of immune-mediated TTP (iTTP) and other chronic disorders such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and hemophilia are well documented.9, 10, 11, 12 For example, iTTP survivors not only have significantly worse health-related quality of life than the US population13 but also report high rates of major depression, cognitive impairment, fatigue, and anxiety that persist for years after their acute episodes.14,15

There has been growing recognition that improving disease outcomes requires a deeper understanding of both the diagnosis and its management from the patient’s perspective.16 Patient-reported outcomes (PROs), which reflect patients’ own assessments of their health status without external interpretation, offer valuable insight into what is most important to patients about their condition or its treatment.17 The goal of this study was to evaluate PROs in patients with hTTP, comparing them with both the general US population and patients living with other chronic disorders, while also examining changes in these scores over time.

Methods

Study design and measures

We conducted a prospective study to evaluate PROs in US patients with hTTP using the National Institutes of Health’s Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) instruments. PROMIS tools are reliable, validated across multiple conditions and ages, including pediatric patients, and apply to diverse populations because they measure concepts rather than disease-specific outcomes.18,19

The domains selected for this study represented the top concerns identified by patients with hTTP in our previous qualitative interviews, in which participants described fatigue, recurrent struggles with depression, anxiety related to their disease, difficulty with attention and concentration at work or school, and physical limitations from previous strokes.8 These patient-identified concerns were mapped to the following PROMIS instruments: (1) fatigue, (2) depression, (3) anxiety, (4) cognitive dysfunction, and (5) positive affect. PROMIS measures were self-reported for adults (aged ≥18 years) and for children and adolescents (aged 8 to 17 years) and parent-proxy–reported for younger children aged 5 to 7 years. Pediatric-specific PROMIS measures were used for pediatric patients. A literature search identified other chronic disorders with published PROMIS data to serve as comparison groups.

Patient eligibility

Patients were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) confirmed diagnosis of hTTP defined by biallelic ADAMTS13 mutations and ADAMTS13 activity of <10%, (2) residence in the United States, (3) enrollment in the International HTTP Registry, and (4) ability to read and understand English. Patients were recruited through the International HTTP Registry (www.ttpregistry.net). The University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center serves as the US coordinating center for the registry and is responsible for the long-term follow-up of enrolled patients. Demographic and contact information is collected and stored in the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center database at the time of enrollment in the registry. All identifying information is pseudonymized, with only the treating physicians’ contact details shared with the registry.

Data collection

To minimize the influence of acute events, data were collected at 3 time points: baseline, 6 months, and 1 year. Baseline was defined as the participant’s first survey completion. Surveys were administered using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a secure, web-based data collection platform. PROMIS instruments, available as preprogrammed surveys in REDCap, were administered in their computer-adaptive testing versions, which reduce participant burden and improve precision by tailoring each subsequent question to previous responses.20

After completing the PROMIS instruments, participants provided information on their prophylaxis regimen, including reasons for initiating, modifying, or discontinuing treatment; frequency of prophylaxis; symptom management strategies; hospitalizations for acute episodes; stroke history; most recent platelet count; distance to their hematologist; and major life events since the previous survey. For participants who had changed prophylaxis, additional details were collected regarding reasons for the change, convenience of administration (less, the same, or more convenient), side-effect profile (fewer, the same, or more side effects), and perceived impact on symptoms (improved, unchanged, or worsened).

Statistical analysis

The t scores for the PROMIS measures were summarized overall and then stratified by pediatric and adult patients using descriptive statistics. One-sample t tests (α = .05) compared baseline mean scores in patients with hTTP with the US population’s normed mean of 50. Higher scores indicated greater impairment for fatigue, depression, and anxiety, whereas lower scores indicated greater impairment for cognitive function and positive affect. Mean baseline scores for patients with hTTP were compared with published PROMIS scores from other chronic disorders. Baseline scores were also descriptively compared between males and females, adults, and pediatric patients, those receiving and not receiving prophylaxis, and those with or without a history of stroke. For patients with longitudinal data at all 3 time points, descriptive trends were assessed, and repeated-measures analysis of variance was performed to test for significant changes over time. Analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc) with α = .05.

Ethics approval

All participants provided informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the institutional review board of the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center.

Results

The study period was from July 2022 through August 2023. Of the 33 US patients in the International HTTP Registry with a confirmed diagnosis of hTTP, 20 consented, and completed the baseline assessment. The respondents included 17 adults and 3 children or adolescents, with a median age of 37 years (range, 5-66). Most patients were women (70%), had private insurance (60%), and lived <25 miles from their hematologist (60%). Of adult patients, most were employed full-time (65%) and 71% had completed at least 4 years of college (Table 1).

Patients with hTTP vs the general US population

Compared with the US population mean score of 50 (standard deviation [SD], 10), patients with hTTP had significantly worse fatigue, with a mean score of 58.0 (95% confidence interval [CI], 52.7-63.3; P = .005); depression, with a mean score of 54.7 (95% CI, 51.1-58.2; P = .013); significantly worse anxiety, with a mean score of 56.7 (95% CI, 52.2-61.0; P = .005); and significantly worse cognitive function with a mean score of 45.4 (95% CI, 42.0-48.8; P = .011). Within the entire cohort, there was no significant difference between patients with hTTP and the general US population in the positive affect and well-being domain (mean, 49.2; 95% CI, 46.3-52.2; P = .5805; Figure 1).

PROMIS scores based on history of stroke

Eleven (55%) patients reported a history of stroke at the baseline assessment. Patients who had experienced a previous stroke scored similarly on the fatigue, cognitive function, and positive affect surveys compared with those who had not previously had a stroke (Table 2). However, those with a previous stroke history scored better on the depression (51.3 vs 58.8) and anxiety (53.1 vs 61.1) surveys. Among those with a history of stroke, evaluation of PROMIS scores comparing those on prophylaxis (n = 6) vs not on prophylaxis (n = 5) were similar in the fatigue (58.7 vs 57.8), depression (50.4 vs 51.8), and anxiety (52.8 vs 53.4) domains and worse in the cognitive function (41.9 vs 51.0) and positive affect domains (45.7 vs 55.0).

PROMIS scores based on prophylaxis use

At baseline, 11 patients with hTTP reported being on prophylactic treatment, whereas 9 were not. Anxiety scores were similar between those on prophylaxis compared with those not on prophylaxis (Table 2). However, patients on prophylaxis had descriptively worse fatigue (59.6 vs 56.0), depression (56.1 vs 52.9), cognitive function (42.9 vs 48.4), and positive affect (45.2 vs 54.1) scores than those not on prophylaxis at baseline.

Compared with the general US population, patients who were on prophylaxis at the baseline assessment had significantly worse scores on fatigue (mean, 59.6; 95% CI, 50.8-68.3; P = .0346), depression (mean, 56.1; 95% CI, 50.4-61.9; P = .0386), cognitive function (mean, 42.9; 95% CI, 38.6-47.3; P = .0047), and positive affect (mean, 45.2; 95% CI, 42.3-48.1; P = .0042). There was a trend toward worse anxiety, but it was not significantly different (mean, 56.6; 95% CI, 49.4-63.7; P = .0672).

Patients with hTTP vs other chronic disorders

The domains of interest that were significantly different from the US population at baseline (ie, fatigue, depression, anxiety, and cognitive function) were compared with published results from other conditions (iTTP,9 SLE,10 RA,11 and hemophilia12). Patients with hTTP had fatigue scores (mean, 58.0) that were almost the same as those of patients with iTTP (mean, 58.0), SLE (mean, 58.2), and RA (mean, 58.0) but were worse than those of patients with hemophilia (mean, 45.6). They also demonstrated similar PROMIS scores in the cognitive function domain (mean, 45.4) compared with patients with iTTP and SLE (mean, 45 for both). Patients with hTTP had worse depression (mean, 54.7) than patients with hemophilia (mean, 48.4), SLE (mean, 50.2), and RA (mean, 51.5). Patients with hTTP had worse anxiety scores (mean, 56.7) than patients with SLE (mean, 52.6) and hemophilia (mean, 49.9) (Table 3).

Trends over time

Fourteen patients completed surveys at all 3 time points (baseline, 6 months, and 1 year), with their longitudinal scores and prophylaxis changes shown in Figure 2. No significant differences were observed across time in mean scores for fatigue (P = .328), depression (P = .699), anxiety (P = .966), cognitive function (P = .771), or positive affect (P = .954). Of 14 patients, 9 were on prophylactic treatment during the study period, most commonly to prevent future complications such as stroke or hospitalization. The prophylaxis treatment frequency varied, ranging from weekly to every 2 to 4 weeks. Seven of 9 patients reported missing or delaying 1 or 2 doses at least once across the 3 time points, most often due to personal scheduling conflicts; 2 reported appointment cancellations by the treating center, and 1 could not recall the reason. Four patients changed their prophylaxis regimen, either initiating or switching therapies; however, PROMIS scores did not show consistent changes in relation to prophylaxis modification. Six patients reported hospitalization related to hTTP during the study period, of which 3 were for a stroke. Of the aforementioned 6 patients, 4 were on prophylaxis at the time of hospitalization (Figure 2, patients 7, 12, 13, and 14).

The PROMIS scores for 3 patients who experienced a stroke during the study period are shown in Figure 2 (patients 7, 11, and 12). Two patients developed a new stroke during the study period between baseline and the 6-month time point. The first patient (patient 7) was on fresh frozen plasma (FFP) and did not report missing any doses. Her scores all numerically worsened at the 6-month time point, but recovered to baseline at the 12-month time point. The second patient (patient 11) was not on prophylaxis at baseline and was started on FFP after a stroke. Her PROMIS scores improved for the depression and anxiety domains but did not change for the other domains. The third patient (patient 12) had a history of a previous stroke and was on FFP at the baseline time point. She had strokes between the 6-month and 12-month time points. She switched to recombinant ADAMTS13 (rADAMTS13) before the 12-month time point. Her depression score worsened over time, but her cognitive function score improved after switching to rADAMTS13.

All 4 patients who were on rADAMTS13 or plasma-derived factor VIII had previously been on FFP (Figure 2). The primary reason for changing from FFP to another form of prophylaxis was because of allergic reactions (3/4 patients), suboptimal control of TTP (2/4 patients), and convenience of administration (4/4 patients). All 4 patients reported that rADAMTS13 or plasma-derived factor VIII were more convenient and had fewer side effects than FFP.

Discussion

hTTP is well documented to cause significant morbidity throughout a patient’s life, with a particularly high risk of acute events during the neonatal period and in pregnancy.7 However, this study is the first to assess its impact on patient-reported fatigue, depression, anxiety, cognitive function, and positive affect. Compared with the general US population, patients with hTTP reported significantly worse scores in the first 4 domains among all participants and worse positive affect in those on prophylaxis. Compared with individuals with chronic autoimmune disorders reported in the literature, patients with hTTP reported similar fatigue across all autoimmune disorders, but worse depression and anxiety than those with SLE.10 They scored worse in most domains than those with hemophilia, another heritable blood disorder.12 PROMIS scores did not change over time, underscoring the persistent, and long-term burden of hTTP on patients’ daily lives.

The optimal indication and regimen for prophylactic treatment in hTTP remain uncertain, making it challenging for clinicians treating this disease.1 The decision to initiate prophylaxis is often made through shared decision-making between the patient and their physician, considering factors such as disease severity, treatment risks, previous allergic reactions to plasma, the ability to travel to the infusion center, and insurance coverage. A deeper understanding of the patient's perspectives could help guide clinicians in determining when to initiate prophylaxis. A prospective study on 87 patients from the International HTTP Registry with a median follow-up of 4.2 years found a similar annual incidence of acute episodes in patients on regular plasma prophylaxis compared with those without regular prophylactic plasma treatment.21 However, other studies have reported that the use of prophylactic plasma-derived factor VIII reduced the prevalence of thrombocytopenia and acute episodes.22,23 In November 2023, the Food and Drug Administration approved rADAMTS13 as a treatment for hTTP. Results from a phase 3 trial reported that patients receiving rADAMTS13 prophylaxis had 0 acute hTTP episodes compared with 1 event in the standard therapy arm (ie, regular plasma infusions), and improving circulating ADAMTS13 levels, platelet counts, and reducing side effects.24 With the added convenience of rADAMTS13 compared with traditional FFP, more patients may be willing to start prophylaxis earlier in their disease course. Symptoms such as fatigue, depression, cognitive impairment, or anxiety related to TTP exacerbations may be appropriate indicators for initiating prophylaxis; however, prospective studies are needed to determine the role of rADAMTS13 in improving these symptoms.

In this study, prophylaxis did not appear to improve patients' PROMIS scores. Although anxiety scores were similar for both patients on prophylaxis and those not on prophylaxis at baseline, those not on prophylaxis seemed to have numerically better scores across all the other domains than those on prophylaxis. This trend may be explained by an overall reduced severity of disease in this group; however, not having to experience the potential burdens of prophylactic treatment may also have a role. Additionally, patients who initiated or changed their prophylaxis during the study period did not show consistent improvements in their PROMIS scores over time. It is noteworthy that all 4 patients who switched from FFP to either rADAMTS13 or plasma-derived factor VIII reported greater convenience and overall symptom improvement. This benefit may be due not only to the drug’s properties compared with FFP but also to the decreased need for premedication, such as antihistamines and steroids, fewer infusion-related reactions, and less time required in the infusion clinic.

This study has several important limitations. First, the sample size was small, particularly among pediatric and male patients, which limits the generalizability of the findings. This constraint is largely due to the rarity of hTTP. The small number of patients also restricts our ability to make reliable subgroup comparisons, so many of the comparisons made in the article were done only for exploratory value and would need a larger study for validation. Additionally, because of the limited sample size, the comparisons between hTTP and the different chronic diseases were not adjusted for age or sex. Another limitation is that several important factors, such as functional status, symptom duration, and frequency of exacerbations of their disease, were not captured here. Furthermore, all participants were recruited via self-enrollment into the International HTTP Registry. This recruitment method may introduce selection bias, because individuals who are more symptomatic, more engaged with their healthcare, or more affected by their disease may be more likely to participate, potentially overrepresenting those with a higher disease or symptom burden. Finally, the PROMIS instruments assess symptoms or functionality over the past 7 days, which may not capture longer-term experiences, particularly in a disease characterized by episodic exacerbations or long asymptomatic intervals.

Strengths of the study include administering the assessment at 3 different time points to mitigate the potential influence of transient acute events (eg, recent illness, hospitalization, or other life stressors) on the PROMIS scores and asking participants about recent acute events. This strategy aimed to capture a more representative picture of each participant’s typical experience over time. Additionally, we used PROMIS measures, which were developed to be relevant across all conditions, are psychometrically sound, and have been validated with the general population, including large cohorts of diverse persons.19 Therefore, we were able to compare our results not only with those of the US general population but also with those of patients with other chronic autoimmune and hereditary disorders previously reported in the literature.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that individuals with hTTP experience persistent impairments in fatigue, mood, anxiety, and cognitive function that are comparable with or worse than those of patients with other chronic diseases. These findings suggest that patients may continue to experience significant symptoms even between acute disease exacerbations or hospitalizations, highlighting the chronic nature of their symptoms beyond episodic flare-ups. Given these results, clinicians managing patients with hTTP must recognize the broader psychological and cognitive impact of the disease. Regular assessments for fatigue, depression, anxiety, and cognitive function should be integrated into routine care, ensuring appropriate interventions, and support. Further studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up are needed to better understand the potential benefits of prophylaxis on patients' overall health and well-being.
