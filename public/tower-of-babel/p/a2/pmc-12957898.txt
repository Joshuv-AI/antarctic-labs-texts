Advancing Raman Calibration: Automated Data Generation, Monitoring, and Control in Multi‐Parallel Perfusion Mini Bioreactors
Authors: Rafael Machleid, Maximilian Goertz, Christian Grimm, Johan Trygg, Sven Mathias, Izabella Surowiec
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe biopharmaceutical industry is transitioning towards more efficient and cost‐effective production methods, driven by the need for more affordable treatments. Process intensifications techniques, such as perfusion are the means by which the biopharmaceutical industry is trying to lower costs, enhance productivity and product quality, and reduce facility footprints. The dynamic nature of perfusion processes presents considerable challenges for real‐time monitoring and control, requiring the advancement of process analytical technologies (PAT) with at‐line, in‐line, or on‐line capabilities. Raman spectroscopy has emerged as a pivotal technology, providing real‐time, noninvasive measurements of multiple analytes simultaneously, contingent upon the availability of sufficient data for calibration modeling. This study outlines the implementation of an automated data generation workflow for Raman calibration modeling within a high‐throughput perfusion miniature bioreactor system, specifically the Ambr 250 HT Perfusion. Additionally, we demonstrate the effectiveness of Raman calibration models in monitoring various cell culture parameters within perfusion cultivations, spanning multiple cell lines and monoclonal antibody products. Finally, we present the feasibility of a Raman‐based bleed‐rate control system and how it compares to the conventional cell counter‐based approach.

Introduction

The biopharmaceutical industry is undergoing a significant transformation, driven by the need for more efficient and cost‐effective production methods. Traditional fed‐batch processes, while well‐established, often fall short in meeting the demands for process intensification [1]. As a result, there is a growing interest in intensified and continuous bioprocessing techniques, such as perfusion, which offer numerous advantages including lower cost, steady‐state operation, enhanced productivity and product quality, and reduced facility footprint [2, 3].

Steady‐state perfusion processes enable a substantial increase in volumetric productivity and overall process yield compared to fed‐batch operation through maintaining a higher viable cell concentration (VCC) for extended time durations [4]. The cell specific perfusion rate (CSPR) is widely used to normalize the perfusion rate to an indicator of the cellular demand by considering the number of cells inside the bioreactor in contrast to the normalization to the bioreactor volume [5]. Other control strategies for the perfusion rate normalize the feed stream to the biomass present inside the bioreactor by considering the cell size in addition to the total number of cells [6, 7]. The monitoring and control of the VCC is required for the implementation of a cell‐based perfusion rate and maintain steady‐state process conditions. Additionally, receiving real‐time information on substrate and metabolite concentration as well as product titer and quality profile represents a key enabler for the implementation of dynamic control strategies and end‐to‐end continuous bioprocess manufacturing [8, 9, 10]. However, the dynamic nature of perfusion processes poses significant challenges for real‐time monitoring and control, necessitating the development of advanced analytical tools with at‐line, in‐line or on‐line capabilities.

Raman spectroscopy has emerged as a promising process analytical technology (PAT) tool for real‐time, non‐invasive monitoring of bioprocess applications. As such, it has been utilized for simultaneous tracking of various critical process parameters (CPPs), critical process attributes (CPAs), and critical quality attributes (CQAs), in upstream [11, 12, 13, 14] and downstream unit operations [15, 16, 17]. In perfusion processes, Raman measurement has been used for monitoring of various culture analytes, such as glucose and other sugars, lactate, amino acids, and product titer [18, 19, 20]. Additionally, automated feeding strategies for glucose [21, 22] and pyruvate [8], as well as steady‐state VCC control in pilot‐scale [23] have been presented.

To utilize Raman spectroscopy for monitoring and control in bioprocess applications, calibration models are essential. Calibration models are statistical models correlating Raman spectra with analyte concentrations. As such, the most frequently used algorithms in the (bio)pharmaceutical industry are bilinear methods, such as project to latent structures (PLS) [24] and orthogonal projection to latent structures (OPLS) [25], generally characterized by acceptable prediction accuracies and easy model interpretability.

A prevalent challenge with calibration modelling of bioprocesses is the lack of relevant high‐quality calibration data. Such data needs to be representative of the optimal process, but also needs to incorporate some degree of process variability to assure model robustness and its ability to detect process deviations. This is particularly challenging for upstream cell culture processes, as it means that processes with different culture conditions need to be performed in order to generate statistically relevant data ideally according to the DOE framework [26]. Doing so at the manufacturing scale, which can encompass volumes of more than 1000 L, is simply not feasible due to the high costs associated with the culture media, labour, time‐effort and the fact that the resulting product likely needs to be discarded as the process was not run according to its standard specifications.

The ongoing shift in the biopharmaceutical industry towards miniature/ small‐scale high‐throughput platforms to reduce process development timelines opens up the possibility of generating calibration data in small volumes and in parallel, effectively reducing the overall cost and time‐efforts. This approach has been presented in various studies for fed‐batch cultivations [26, 27, 28], demonstrating the implementation of the DOE methodology into the calibration data generation workflow. Ideally, such models are transferable from small‐ to large‐scale operations and potentially different probe configurations [27, 29]. The recent uprise of small‐scale high‐throughput systems tailored to perfusion culture enabled automated generation of Raman calibration data also for continuous processes, as demonstrated by Schwarz et al. [19].

In this study, we describe the implementation of an automated data generation workflow for Raman calibration modelling in a high‐throughput perfusion miniature bioreactor system (Ambr 250 HT Perfusion). The setup allows for the execution of experimental designs with up to 24 experiments/ vessels in parallel, without the need for multiple Raman spectrometers and/ or multiplexers with multiple probes—given the integrated Raman flow‐cell design. Furthermore, we are investigating the performance of Raman calibration models for monitoring of various cell culture parameters in perfusion cultivations across multiple cell lines and mAb products. Although only a few examples [18, 22] of cross‐clone calibration models in perfusion culture exist in literature, this study aims to expand the understanding and application of such models. In extension, we compare the performance of a Raman‐based bleed‐rate control system to the conventional cell‐counter‐based approach by performing two cultivations under the same conditions in parallel (test and control bioreactor). By demonstrating the feasibility and effectiveness of Raman‐based monitoring and control, this work lays the foundation for future advancements in autonomous bioprocess control, ultimately contributing to the optimization and efficiency of biopharmaceutical production.

Materials and Methods

Setup

We performed cell culture in the Ambr 250 HT Perfusion system (12‐fold), which was equipped with an Ambr Analysis Module including BioPAT Spectro integration (all Sartorius) for automated spectral measurements through the HyperFluxTM PRO Raman spectrometer (Tornado, a Bruker Company). Additionally, a VI‐CELLTM XR system (Beckman Coulter GmbH) was directly connected to the Analysis Module for automated cell count measurements (Figure 1).

Experimental setup with close‐up of the Ambr Perfusion vessel, excluding offline analyzers.

Furthermore, we performed nutrient and metabolite measurements off‐line using the BioProfile FLEX2 (Nova Biomedical Corporation) and mAb titer measurements on the Octet HTX system (Sartorius).

Cell Cultivations

Three proprietary production cell lines (A, B, and C), derived from the CHO DG44 host cell were selected for perfusion cultivation experiments, expressing three distinct monoclonal antibodies (refer to Table 1). Cell counting during the seed train was conducted using the CASY Cell Counter & Analyzer (OMNI Life Science). The cells underwent passaging every 2 to 3 days, with an inoculation density of 0.2–0.3 × 106 cells/mL, over a span of 7 to 8 passages from thawing to the initiation of the perfusion process. Passaging was performed in 50 mL of cell culture medium, utilizing the 4Cell SmartCHO Stock and Adaptation Medium (Sartorius), supplemented with 6 mM L‐glutamine (Sartorius). The cultures were incubated at 36.8°C, with 7.5% CO2, agitation at 103 rpm with a 5 cm orbital throw, and 80% relative humidity.

Reference Data

We used the VI‐CELLTM XR system (Beckman Coulter GmbH), connected to the Ambr Analysis Module (The Automation Partnership (Cambridge) Limited), for automated cell count measurements twice per day per bioreactor (early morning and evening at a frequency of 12 hours). For this analysis sample preparation was required (1:4, 1:10 or 1:20 dilution in PBS), which was performed automatically by the liquid handling system immediately after bioreactor sampling.

Further, one sample per day per bioreactor was taken out of the Ambr system for off‐line measurement of nutrient and metabolite concentrations on the BioProfile FLEX2 (Nova Medical Corporation), which did not require any sample preparation steps.

For titer measurements, bioreactor samples were centrifuged (6600 x g, 10 min) and stored at −20°C until the end of the process. Samples were thawed at room temperature, vortexed heavily prior to dilution (1:20 in 4Cell SmartCHO Stock and Adaptation Medium), and analyzed on the Octet HTX system (Sartorius).

Raman Spectra Acquisition

We utilized the HyperFluxTM PRO Raman spectrometer (785 nm laser, 495 mW, Tornado, a Bruker Company, Canada) to capture Raman spectra. This device was integrated with the Ambr 250 HT Perfusion system through the BioPAT Spectro interface. We set the acquisition parameters for model calibration to a total acquisition time of 300 s per sample (e.g., 0.75 s x 80 scans or 0.5 s x 120 scans; averaged over five spectra per sample). Prior to measuring each sample, a dark scan was performed, reducing the influence of ambient light variations on the Raman data. Each bioreactor had three Raman spectra recorded daily via automatic sampling using the Ambr’s liquid handler system. This system transferred samples to the Analysis Module's sampling cup (Sartorius) and a separate vial for offline analysis of nutrient, metabolite and titer levels. From the sampling cup, samples were directed to the BioPAT Spectro flow cell for Raman analysis and to the VI‐CELLTM XR system (Beckman Coulter GmbH) for cell count measurements.

The Raman spectra recorded for real‐time testing of the developed calibration models, were measured with an exposure time of 0.75 s x 400 scans, resulting in a single spectrum of 300 s total acquisition time per sample. This was done to make sure that the spectra used for real‐time prediction exhibited the same signal‐to‐noise ratio as the spectra recorded for calibration modeling.

The automated miniature perfusion bioreactor system enabled Raman and reference data acquisition without much additional manual work given the automated set‐up with at‐line integration of the Raman spectrometer. The system was not negatively impacted by high cell densities (e.g., through clogging of the integrated Raman flow cell) and performed reliably for tested VCCs of up to 110 · 106 cells/mL. Further, spectroscopic measurements were not negatively affected by high cell counts, meaning that such spectra were not systematically flagged as outliers.

Calibration Modeling

The time‐matched Raman spectra and reference values were imported into SIMCA 18.0 (Sartorius). Depending on the Y‐response, we employed different preprocessing methods for the spectral data. Asymmetric Least Squares Smoothing (AsLS) [30] baseline correction (p = 0.05, λ = 107) followed by normalization to the integral of the water band between 1550 cm−1 and 1750 cm−1 was applied for VCC model. The first derivative (quadratic with 15 points in each sub‐model and distance between each point of 1) followed by the Standard Normal Variate (SNV) filter was utilized for modeling of all other Y‐responses, namely glucose, lactate, titer, and glutamate. The effects of the utilized preprocessing on the raw spectra are displayed in Figure S1.

We examined the variability in the first derivative—SNV preprocessed Raman spectra through Principal Component Analysis (PCA). For calibration modeling, we utilized OPLS regression. OPLS effectively divides systematic variance in the X‐block into one correlating with Y‐response and one that is not correlated to Y, into orthogonal and predictive components, respectively. The predictive loading illustrates the relationship between the predictive component and the X‐block. Compared to classical PLS regression models, OPLS models offer greater model interpretability, while retaining predictive ability.

For each analyte, we created single‐Y OPLS models based on the culture and spectral data of cultivations C1‐13, serving as the training/ calibration set. The unit variance‐scaled reference values were used as Y‐response. For each model, the mean‐centered variables of the Raman spectra served as the X‐block, concentrating on the Raman shift range of 450–1800 cm− 1. Outliers were removed using the Hotelling's T2 detection tool within SIMCA.

We conducted leave‐one‐group‐out cross‐validation (LOGO‐CV) to internally validate all calibration models. Specifically, each cultivation was assigned to a separate cross‐validation group, resulting in 13 groups, each containing one cultivation. For quantification of the internal prediction errors, we used the Root‐Mean‐Square Error of Estimation (RMSEE) and Root‐Mean‐Square Error of Cross Validation (RMSECV). In general, models are deemed accurate when they have low RMSEE and RMSECV values, while the RMSEE/RMSECV ratio should ideally be close to a value of one, indicating that models are not overfit.

Further, each model's predictive performance was evaluated for real‐time monitoring using an external test set (cultivation C14), which was not used for model training. Here, we utilized the Root‐Mean‐Square Error of Prediction (RMSEP) to quantify the predictive ability on new culture data.

Online Deployment of Calibration Models through the Ambr Spectroscopy Server and SIMCA‐Q

The developed calibration models from cultivations C1‐13 were deployed online to evaluate their predictive performance for real‐time process monitoring and control. We achieved that by uploading the SIMCA USP files containing the final calibration models to the Ambr Spectroscopy Server. After doing so, the Ambr‐integrated prediction engine SIMCA‐Q (Sartorius, Sweden) was able to access the models, enabling predictions on new, incoming spectra in real‐time. The prediction values were directly accessible within the Ambr Run Time software, streamlining Raman‐based cell culture monitoring and control. We used this setup for at‐line, real‐time monitoring of cultivation C14.

To progress from monitoring to control, we extended this setup to Raman‐based bleed‐rate control in cultivation C15. This was accomplished by deploying the VCC calibration model to the Spectroscopy Server, as previously described, and assigning the output of the calibration model as input variable for the internal Ambr bleed‐rate control functionality. This approach is described in detail the following section.

The Ambr Bleed‐rate Control System

The cell concentration was controlled to individual set‐points for each bioreactor by performing automatic cell bleeding using the cell density control loop integrated in the Ambr 250 operating software. The control loop intends to maintain a constant VCC by continuously diluting the cell culture in proportion to the growth rate up to a defined bleed trigger volume of 215 mL. A bleed of 10 mL culture volume is performed once the bleed trigger volume is reached, resetting the volume to the lower pre‐defined operating set‐point. The culture dilution was suspended in case the reference VCC value was below the set‐point and the dilution rate was increased in case the VCC value was above the set‐point. Continuous VCC values were predicted by the embedded function of the cell density control loop based on measurements performed in 8 hours intervals for run1 and 2, and later in 12 h intervals for run 3 and 4. The cell density control loop requires the VCC value of the cell culture and the growth rate as input parameters. For the bioreactors in the training set (C1‐C13) the measured VCC was used as input, while Raman‐based VCC predictions were recorded but not utilized for the bleed control. The Raman‐based bleed control (C15) was realized by providing the predictions of the VCC by the Raman model to the cell density control loop, while a second bioreactor (C14) was used as a control, operating under similar conditions to those in the trainings set (C1‐C13). For the bioreactors in Run #1 and #2, a two‐day running average of the growth rate was automatically provided whereas in Runs #3 and #4, a fixed growth rate value was used as input to the control loop. Results and Discussion

Cell Cultivations in the Ambr 250 HT Perfusion

In this study, we aimed at developing generic Raman calibration models across multiple cell lines and mAb products for miniature scale perfusion cultivations. Therefore, a total of 15 CHO perfusion cultivations with three distinct mAb products have been performed. Cell culture conditions were varied to introduce sufficient process variability for subsequent calibration modeling: target VCCs ranged from 25 106 cells/mL to 100 106 cells/mL, CSPRs from 10 to 50 pL cell −1 day−1, three different batch phase media, high/ low cross‐flow rates, and temperature shifts (Table 1). The automated miniature perfusion system streamlines exploration of different perfusion settings through its semi‐automatic set‐up.

As expected, the variation in perfusion conditions resulted in distinct cell culture profiles, displayed in the time‐profile plots of nutrient, metabolite, titer, and VCC in Figure 2. Variability in cell culture profiles is desired from a calibration modeling point of view, as it helps to break the strong correlation between analytes, as commonly observed in cell cultivations, leading to more specific and robust calibration models [26].

Concentration profiles for various analytes over process time for cultivations C1‐C13. The perfusion cultivations exhibit large variations in metabolite and VCC profiles, reflecting the dynamic nature of the perfusion culture conditions, as displayed in Table 1.

Spectral Data Analysis

We developed a PCA model for cultivations C1‐13 to inspect the variability within the generated Raman data. The first, second, and third principal components accounted for 86%, 6%, and 4% of the spectral variance respectively. The scores plot of the first two components, as depicted in Figure 3 A–C and individually for each cultivation in Figure S2, demonstrated natural clustering in the beginning of the cultivations. The clustering was associated with three different batch phase media that were used (Figure 3A).

Scores plots of PCA model developed on Raman spectral data with 1st derivative and SNV preprocessing applied to the spectra. Plots are colored according to batch phase media (top), cell lines (middle), and individual cultivations (bottom) with Hotelling's T2 ellipses (99%) in grey. The first and second principal components described 86% and 6% of the variation respectively.

With the start of the perfusion and gradual batch phase media to perfusion medium exchange (starting on day 3), the individual trajectories began heading towards the same position in the scores plot. The media exchange was mostly completed around cultivation day 5, where the individual trajectories in the scores plot cross, indicating high spectral similarity. This is supported by the reference time‐profiles, which showed similar nutrient and metabolite concentrations across all cultivations during that time (Figure 2).

After medium exchange, the initial clusters associated with specific batch phase media faded. Surprisingly, cell line specific trajectories could not be observed (Figure 3). Rather, the specific cultivation conditions such as target VCC and CSPR were causing the observed variations. It is striking that the trajectories of cell line A showed much larger variance than the ones of cell lines C and B. The former was characterized by large variations in the target VCC and CSPRs, whereas the others showed less differences in these parameters (Table 1).

The target VCC had a strong influence on the individual trajectories, which became apparent when comparing cultivations C1 and C2 vs C8 and C9: C1 and C2 were cultivated with the same CSPR but different target VCCs (25·106 cells/mL vs 50·106 cells/mL), which resulted in very comparable nutrient, metabolite and titer profiles but large VCC differences from cultivation day 6 and onwards (Figure S3C). The trajectories show strong overlap from cultivation days 0 to 6 and separation starting on the same day on which the cell counts started differing (Figure S3A), while measured nutrient, metabolite and titer profiles remain highly comparable. On the other hand, cultivations C8 and C9 were performed with equal target VCCs and CSPRs but different cross‐flow rates, resulting in very comparable nutrient, metabolite, titer and cell count profiles (Figure S3C). The trajectories of these cultivations matched almost perfectly (Figure S3B), supporting the claim that the target VCC had a strong impact on the individual process trajectories as measured with Raman spectroscopy.

Further, we found that fast‐growing cultivations, for example C12 and C13, were characterized by strongly U‐ or V‐shaped trajectories in the scores plot as commonly found with Raman data from fed‐batch culture [27], while slow‐growing cultures, such as C8 and C9, were rather irregularly shaped. However, in all cases a strong change in direction of trajectories was observed as soon as the target VCC was reached and the bleeding was activated.

To summarize, we found that multiple factors influenced the trajectories of the cultivations in the scores plot of the spectral data. In the beginning, the specific batch phase medium was the biggest differentiator. Until the target VCCs were reached, the growth‐rates seemed to dictate the trajectories, with fast‐growing cultivations displaying larger spread in the second component leading to stronger U‐ or V‐shaped trajectories. In the steady state, the trajectories were mainly determined by the target VCCs and CSPRs.

The results indicate, that in order to develop robust Raman calibration models for perfusion processes, different target VCCs and CSPRs need to be explored given their strong impact on nutrient, metabolite, and titer levels and cell counts, and ultimately the spectral data. This is in agreement with a study presented by Schwarz et al. [19], demonstrating the generation of robust and accurate Raman models in steady‐state perfusion culture through controlled changes in CSPRs. The presented set‐up facilitated the data generation for robust Raman calibration modeling by enabling small‐scale, high‐throughput perfusion cell culture performed according to the DoE framework.

Predictive Modeling of Perfusion Cell Culture Analytes

We build individual OPLS models with good to excellent model‐fit and prediction capabilities for all analytes of interest, as summarized in Table 2 and depicted in Figure 4. The models generally provided high R2CV and low RMSECV values, which were obtained from leave‐one‐cultivation‐out cross‐validation. To compare different models, we calculated the RMSECV% by dividing the RMSECV by the mean measured concentration of the respective analyte, multiplied by factor 100 (same for RMSEP%). The results indicate that generic calibration models, spanning multiple cell lines and products, are feasible for perfusion cultivations. To the best of our knowledge, this has been shown previously for various analytes in fed‐batch culture [27], but in perfusion mode only for Raman data generated in the permeate stream, after the cell‐retention device [18, 22].

Comparing our prediction accuracies to the ones presented in literature, we find strong agreement for glucose, lactate, and titer (Table 3). Interestingly, the Raman measurement mode, either directly in the bioreactor or samples taken from the bioreactor (cell‐containing) vs the permeate line (cell‐free samples), does not seem to have a considerable influence on the models’ accuracy. This is in disagreement with Wan et al. [22], who claimed that their bioreactor models performed significantly worse than the permeate models due to background variations in the spectra. When it comes to glutamate and VCC, our models performed slightly worse than the ones presented in literature. Nevertheless, we find the accuracies satisfactory considering that our models were based on multiple cell lines and products, whereas most literature examples used a single cell line and product, except for Yilmaz et al. [18] and Wan et al. [22]. It is important to acknowledge that directly comparing RMSEPs can be challenging due to variations in ranges of analyte concentrations in calibration and test sets, calibration/test split ratios, experimental setups, and other factors.

In this context, it is essential to compare the accuracy of Raman‐based VCC monitoring with conventional methods, such as capacitance‐based approaches, which have long been the standard for measuring VCC in upstream cell culture. Lemke et al. [31] were able to achieve RMSEPs of 7.75 · 106 cells/mL and 4.05 · 106 cells/mL for single and multifrequency capacitance‐based models in 2 L CHO perfusion culture respectively. While our VCC model (RMSEP = 7.4· 106 cells/mL) performed worse than the novel multifrequency approach, performance was comparable to the industry‐standard single frequency approach.

Briefly, we were able to develop Raman models for monitoring various analytes in perfusion culture across different cell lines and mAb products with good accuracy. For some analytes, particularly titer, only low calibration ranges could be realized due to the continuous harvesting and medium exchange in perfusion cultivations. Noteworthy, our data was generated from an unoptimized perfusion process with high media exchange rates and therefore low titer levels. To increase the specificity and robustness of the respective models, spiking could be utilized as has previously been presented for Raman calibration modeling in fed‐batch culture [26, 27].

Comparing Raman‐ and VI‐CELLTM‐based Bleed‐rate Control Systems

To study the feasibility of Raman‐based bleed‐rate control in miniature scale perfusion bioreactors, the Ambr system's internal bleed‐rate control function was redirected to receive VCC values from the previously developed Raman calibration model—instead of the VI‐CELLTM. This set‐up was tested for cultivation C15. In parallel, a control cultivation (C14) was performed under the same initial conditions as C15 (Table 1) but relying on the VI‐CELLTM measurements as VCC input for the bleed‐rate control system. Both cultivations were operated at a VCC target of 40 · 106 cells/mL until cultivation day 19, followed by an increase of the target to 60 · 106 cells/mL for the remaining time until culture death. Up until the point of bleed activation, strong agreement in nutrient, metabolite, titer, and VCC profiles was found for both cultivation (Figure S4 ). Note, that cultivation C14 had to be terminated on day 20 due to contamination.

The results show that both bleed‐rate control approaches, Raman‐ and VI‐CELLTM‐based, were comparable and can be employed to achieve steady‐state perfusion cultivations in the miniature‐scale system. Figure 6A displays the reference VCC time‐profiles for C14 and C15 around the VCC target over time. To quantify the performance of each approach, the mean, standard deviation, and the mean absolute error (MAE) with respect to the VCC target, were calculated using the VI‐CELLTM references of both cultivations (Table 4). The results demonstrated that the VI‐CELLTM‐based approach (C14) overall yielded VCCs closer to the target than the Raman‐based approach (C15). The mean of the reference VCC was closer to the target of 40 · 106 cells/mL, while the lower standard deviation also indicated less variation around the mean for the VI‐CELLTM‐based approach (Table 4). Interestingly, the obtained MAEs were comparable (3.90 · 106 cells/mL and 5.97 · 106 cells/mL for C14 and C15, respectively), indicating that both approaches are suitable for maintaining the VCC around the specified target. Further, the Raman‐based approach demonstrated improved ability to control the cultivation at the higher VCC target of 60 · 106 cells/mL, given that the MAE was reduced from 5.97 · 106 cells/mL down to 5.13 · 106 cells/mL. This indicated that Raman‐based bleed‐rate control within ±10% of the VCC target is achievable at the higher VCC target.

When comparing the model predictions to the VI‐CELLTM references, we found good prediction accuracy with RMSEP and RMSEP% of 7.4 · 106 cells/mL and 21% for C14 and 6.6 · 106 cells/mL and 16% for C15, respectively. The Raman prediction and reference profiles for both cultivations are displayed in Figure 6B and C .

These results are in agreement with the one available example from literature presenting Raman‐based bleed‐rate control in perfusion cultivation. [23] The authors reported a mean reference VCC of 50.67 · 106 cells/mL with a standard deviation of 2.67 · 106 cells/mL, at a VCC target of 50 · 106 cells/mL. These slightly better results are best explained by the fact that the authors developed a more accurate VCC model, evidenced by a lower RMSEP of 4.35 · 106 cells/mL. Further, we attributed this to the fact that the authors developed calibration models on a single cell‐line, while we aimed for a more generic model incorporating multiple cell‐lines and mAb products. Furthermore, the literature example was based on 3 L bench‐top bioreactors with Raman immersion probes, which allowed for continuous Raman measurements and VCC predictions, enabling a PID‐based bleed‐rate control system. Such a system is certainly better suited to keep the VCC at target, compared to our at‐line sampling approach, configured to obtain new Raman VCC predictions only every eight to twelve hours.

The increased data availability of continuous measurements coupled with PID control surely improves process control around a given target. However, this approach is not feasible for Raman data generation in multi‐parallel miniature bioreactors, given the space restrictions, the need for multiple probes, and likely multiple spectrometers considering the limited number of available measurement channels per spectrometer.

Conclusion and Outlook

In this work, an automated data generation workflow for Raman calibration modeling in a high‐throughput perfusion miniature bioreactor system was presented. The system was utilized to develop generic calibration models, incorporating data of multiple cell lines and mAb products with sufficient accuracy for monitoring of multiple critical parameters in perfusion culture. Notably, our approach also enables the monitoring of titer, a capability that is not commonly demonstrated for perfusion cultures and is less frequently mentioned in literature compared to other analytes. In addition, the conventional method of controlling bleed rate, which relies on cell‐counter output, was compared to a Raman‐based approach. By employing the VCC calibration model predictions, successful and stable bleed‐rate control was achieved for VCC targets of 40 and 60 · 106 cells/mL throughout a 30‐day perfusion process.

Perfusion cultivations depend on stable process parameters to maintain steady‐state conditions, requiring the use of real‐time monitoring and control tools. Raman spectroscopy has emerged as a valuable PAT tool, offering real‐time measurements of multiple critical process parameters. Our work lays the groundwork for integrating Raman‐based calibration models into perfusion cultures, not only for process monitoring, but also for process control as part of a Raman‐based bleed‐rate system.

A significant challenge in applying Raman‐based monitoring to manufacturing‐scale perfusion culture is the generation of adequate data for calibration model development. The workflow presented in this study effectively addresses this issue by enabling automated calibration data generation at small scales in a high‐throughput manner already at a very early stage in the development of a perfusion bioprocess. This approach also facilitates the integration of calibration data generation within the DOE framework, greatly enhancing the likelihood of covering all relevant process variation and thereby developing robust calibration models. The developed workflow can further be extended by analyte spiking, as has been shown for fed‐batch culture in the past [27].

Looking ahead, we anticipate that Raman‐based methods will be pivotal in ushering bioprocessing into an era of autonomous process control. By combining Raman‐based bleed‐rate control with Raman‐based systems for medium exchange, we are well‐positioned to advance significantly toward achieving this vision in perfusion cell culture. These advancements hold the promise of delivering substantial cost savings and efficiency improvements to the bioprocessing industry.
