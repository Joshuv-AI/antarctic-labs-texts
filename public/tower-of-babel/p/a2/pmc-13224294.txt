End-of-Life Care in India: Withholding and Withdrawal after the Harish Rana Judgement
Authors: Raghu S. Thota, Raghavendra Ramanjulu
Published: 2026

The recent Supreme Court judgement in Harish Rana versus Union of India has brought renewed attention to the realities of end-of-life care in India. While it reaffirms the legal permissibility of withholding and withdrawing life-sustaining treatment, it also forces clinicians to confront a more fundamental question – are we always acting in the patient’s best interest when we continue treatment?[1]

In clinical practice, particularly in intensive care units, continuation of life-sustaining treatment is often the default. Withdrawal, on the other hand, is approached with caution – sometimes due to legal concerns, but more often due to discomfort with the decision itself. Yet, the discomfort lies not in the act of withdrawal, but in our hesitation to acknowledge futility. There is no meaningful distinction between withholding and withdrawing treatment when the intent is to avoid non-beneficial or burdensome interventions.[2]

The Harish Rana judgement does not introduce a new ethical principle – it exposes a persistent gap between what we know and what we practice. It challenges a deeply ingrained reflex in medicine: To continue treatment because we can, rather than because we should. Continuing life-sustaining treatment in the absence of meaningful recovery is not a neutral act. It is an active decision that risks prolonging suffering under the guise of care. The Court has outlined safeguards, including structured decision-making through medical boards and documentation.[1] While these are necessary, they also reveal a systemic limitation. In many real-world settings, such processes are difficult to operationalise promptly. The result is familiar – decisions are delayed, treatments go on and patients remain caught between ongoing intervention and meaningful care. Perhaps the most important contribution of the judgement is 1) explicitly declaring clinically assisted nutrition and hydration (CANH) as a form medical treatment, not just basic care, and thus can be legally withdrawn, 2) reaffirming that the “right to life” under Article 21 includes the right to a dignified dying process and 3) reframing of family decisions. By recognising the family’s choice as thoughtful and compassionate, the Court disrupts the narrative of guilt that often surrounds withdrawal of treatment.[1] In clinical settings, families are frequently made to feel that choosing to stop treatment is equivalent to giving up. It is not. It is, at times, the most responsible decision that can be made.

Despite legal clarity since the Common Cause judgement, advance directives remain underutilised.[3] This reflects not just a lack of awareness but a deeper discomfort with discussing death before it becomes imminent. As a result, decisions are made in crisis, without the patient’s voice and often under pressure.

Recent Indian consensus statements have provided a practical framework for end-of-life care in intensive care settings, emphasising shared decision-making, documentation and integration of palliative care.[2] Yet, the translation of these principles into routine practice remains inconsistent. The problem is no longer the absence of guidance – it is the absence of implementation.

The Harish Rana judgement, therefore, should not be seen as an endpoint, but as a challenge. It compels clinicians to move beyond legal compliance toward ethical clarity and clinical courage.

Because ultimately, the question is not whether we are allowed to withdraw treatment. It is whether we are willing to recognise when continuing it is no longer care.
