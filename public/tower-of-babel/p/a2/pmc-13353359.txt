Light hydrogen isotopes in terrestrial core
Authors: Yu Zhang, Wenzhong Wang, Zhengbin Deng, Zhongqing Wu, Bowen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The origin of Earth’s water remains unresolved. Hydrogen isotopes provide a key tracer of planetary water history, yet the isotope composition of Earth’s core—the planet’s largest internal reservoir—has remained unconstrained. Here, we use first-principles calculations combined with machine-learning–accelerated path-integral molecular dynamics to quantify hydrogen isotope fractionation between silicate and metallic melts under core-forming conditions. We find that core formation enriched the silicate Earth in deuterium while concentrating isotopically light hydrogen in the core, requiring the proto-Earth to have started with a lower deuterium-to-hydrogen (D/H) ratio than that preserves in the present-day mantle. Our models show that the bulk-Earth D/H ratio can be explained either by direct accretion of enstatite chondrite–dominated material or by isotopic resetting of deuterium-rich planetesimals through interactions with solar nebular gas. These results suggest that Earth’s water inventory was established during the earliest stages of accretion.

INTRODUCTION

Water is central to Earth’s evolution and long-term habitability, influencing mantle dynamics, magmatism, and plate tectonics (1–5). However, despite its importance, the origin of Earth’s water remains debated. Competing models invoke late delivery by volatile-rich bodies such as carbonaceous chondrites (CCs) or comets (6–11), inheritance from enstatite chondrites (ECs) (12–14)—the principal building blocks of the proto-Earth—which already contained water, or direct incorporation of hydrogen from the solar nebula through gas-planetesimal interactions (15–18). Each scenario could leave a distinct hydrogen isotopic fingerprint, making the bulk D/H ratio of Earth a decisive constraint on its water origin.

Earth’s mantle exhibits relatively low D/H ratios consistent with a nebular source (19), but the mantle alone does not represent the planet’s inventory: Geochemical and geophysical evidence indicates that the metallic core hosts most of Earth’s hydrogen (Fig. 1A) (20–23). Because hydrogen partitioned between silicate and metal during core formation, the bulk D/H ratio depends critically on isotope fractionation during segregation (Fig. 1B). Without this constraint, the hydrogen isotope composition of bulk Earth (BE) could plausibly span the full Solar System range—from the extremely deuterium-depleted values of calcium-aluminum–rich inclusions [δD ≈ −850 per mil (‰)] (24) to the deuterium-enriched signatures of outer Solar System bodies (δD > +1000‰) (25)—precluding robust conclusions about the origin of Earth’s water.

The key missing parameter is the equilibrium hydrogen isotope fractionation between silicate and metallic melts at core-forming pressures and temperatures. Experimental access to these conditions is limited, and strong nuclear quantum effects complicate theoretical predictions, leaving this parameter essentially unconstrained. Here, we address this gap by quantifying equilibrium hydrogen isotope fractionation between silicate and metallic melts using first-principles calculations combined with machine-learning–accelerated path-integral molecular dynamics (PIMD). We incorporate these results into models of hydrogen partitioning during Earth’s differentiation, refining estimates of the BE D/H ratio and constraining possible sources of Earth’s water. Our findings indicate that early interactions between H2-rich nebular gas and molten planetesimals could have reset their hydrogen isotope ratios to proto-Earth values, identifying nebular gas–planetesimal exchange as a fundamental process in shaping the volatile inventories of terrestrial planets.

RESULTS

Hydrogen bonding behaviors in melts

Equilibrium isotope fractionation arises from differences in vibrational energies between isotopologues and can be calculated using the Bigeleisen-Mayer equation (see Materials and Methods). Although first-principles molecular dynamics (FPMD) simulations based on density functional theory (DFT) reliably capture the structural properties of silicate and metallic melts, they cannot directly predict equilibrium hydrogen isotope fractionation owing to pronounced nuclear quantum effects. To address this, we use PIMD combined with the direct scaled-coordinates estimator (26) to calculate the equilibrium hydrogen isotope fractionation factor between silicate and metallic melts (103lnαsilicate-metal). PIMD extends classical MD by treating atomic nuclei as quantum particles, mapping each nucleus onto a ring polymer of fictitious beads connected by harmonic springs (27). This formalism enables explicit treatment of nuclear quantum effects on atomic motion and has been widely adopted in physics and chemistry. However, direct PIMD simulations of silicate and metallic melts at the DFT level remain computationally prohibitive.

To overcome this limitation, we constructed high-accuracy machine learning potentials (MLPs) based on first-principles data to accelerate PIMD simulations (see Materials and Methods). The silicate melt was modeled using Mg32Si32O106H20 and a near-bulk silicate Earth (BSE) composition (28), Mg30Ca2Fe4Si24Al3NaO92H6. To account for the influence of oxygen fugacity, we also considered two relatively reduced components, Mg32Si32O101H20 and Mg32Si32O96H20. The metallic melt was modeled using Fe100H28 and Fe93Ni5Si6O8CS3H12. The accuracy of MLPs was evaluated using test datasets constructed by randomly selecting configurations from independent FPMD and PIMD simulations. The results show that the energies and forces predicted by the MLPs closely match the DFT values (fig. S1), thereby enabling efficient and high-precision calculations of isotope fractionation factors.

The structural properties of hydrogen differ markedly between silicate and metallic melts (figs. S2 and S3 and table S1). In the Mg32Si32O106H20 and Mg30Ca2Fe4Si24Al3NaO92H6 melts, H predominantly bonds to O, forming hydroxyl with short H–O bonds of 1.10 to 1.16 Å. In contrast, H is bonded to Fe in the Fe100H28 and Fe93Ni5Si6O8CS3H12 melts, yielding longer H–Fe bond lengths of 1.78 to 1.88 Å. Upon compression, metallic melts show the expected shortening of H–Fe bonds (from 1.88 Å at ~30 GPa to 1.78 Å at ~90 GPa) and an increase in hydrogen coordination number (CN; from 6.53 to 6.63), indicating enhanced hydrogen stability at high pressures. Silicate melts, however, display an anomalous H–O bond elongation (from 1.10 Å at ~30 GPa to 1.16 Å at ~90 GPa) despite an increase in CN (from 1.33 to 1.80). This behavior is primarily attributed to pressure-induced hydrogen-bond symmetrization. Increasing CN tends to strengthen H–O bonding, whereas bond elongation weakens it. These competing effects largely offset each other, resulting in an approximately pressure-independent effective H–O bond strength in silicate melts. Similar pressure-driven H–O bond elongation has been widely reported in hydrous silicate melts and minerals (29–31). Notably, the presence of other elements (e.g., Ca, Fe, and Al) in silicates or light elements (e.g., O, C, and S) in metallic melts does not appreciably alter hydrogen bonding behavior.

Hydrogen isotope fractionation during core formation

Using PIMD and MLPs, we determined the reduced partition function ratios (103lnβ or β factors) and derived the equilibrium hydrogen isotope fractionation factor between silicate and metallic melts (103lnαsilicate-metal) under core-forming conditions (Fig. 2 and table S2). In general, shorter bond lengths coupled with higher CNs correspond to stronger bonding, larger force constants, and consequently higher β factors. In metallic melts, β factors increase approximately linearly with pressure, reflecting progressive H–Fe bond shortening and increasing CN at higher pressures (fig. S3). In contrast, β factors in silicate melts remain largely pressure independent, owing to competing effects of H–O bond elongation and increasing CN upon compression (fig. S2). The temperature dependence of isotope fractionation follows the expected ~1/T2 scaling (fig. S4 and table S3), further confirming the reliability of our calculations. Oxygen fugacity exerts a modest influence on β factors: Comparisons between H2O- and H2-dominated silicate melts reveal an ~6‰ difference in β factors (Fig. 2A). Under Earth’s core-forming conditions, H2 is expected to account for at most ~50% of dissolved hydrogen in silicate melts (32), resulting in only a limited effect on hydrogen isotope fractionation. Overall, our results demonstrate that silicate melts are enriched in deuterium relative to metallic melts. At 3000 K and 20 to 60 GPa, 103lnαsilicate-metal decreases from ~26 to ~21‰ with increasing pressure (Fig. 2B).

These values are substantially lower than previous estimates (~50‰), which were inferred indirectly by combining silicate minerals−H2O, H2O−H2, and H2−metal solids fractionation factors (33). These phase assemblages, however, do not directly represent coexisting silicate and metallic melts during core formation. The discrepancy therefore likely reflects differences in hydrogen bonding environments between melts and solid or molecular phases, highlighting the importance of directly constraining silicate-metal hydrogen isotope fractionation between silicate and metallic melts.

Using the calculated fractionation factors, we modeled the evolution of hydrogen isotopes during Earth’s core formation. The magnitude of δD variation in the BSE produced by core-mantle differentiation depends not only on the value of 103lnαsilicate-metal but also critically on the partitioning behavior of hydrogen between metallic and silicate melts. Although hydrogen partitioning remains debated at low pressure−temperature conditions (34–36), both experimental and theoretical studies (20, 21) consistently indicate that hydrogen becomes strongly siderophile at core-forming conditions, albeit with some variation in the reported partition coefficients (Dmetal-silicate). High-pressure experiments by Tagawa et al. (20) (30 to 60 GPa and 3100 to 4600 K) yielded Dmetal-silicate values of ≥29, and their core formation models suggested that 0.3 to 0.6 wt % hydrogen was incorporated into the core, leaving ~700 parts per million H2O in the BSE—implying that 95 to 97% of Earth’s water was sequestered into the core. By comparison, Li et al. (21) used FPMD simulations at 20 to 135 GPa and 2800 to 5000 K, finding a broader and somewhat lower Dmetal-silicate, sensitive to pressure, temperature, and redox state. Their models indicate that under high-pressure scenarios, the core could have captured ~75% of Earth’s water. Despite these differences, both approaches converge on the conclusion that 75 to 95% of Earth’s water was partitioned into the core, consistent with first-order geophysical estimates of water distribution between the mantle and core (22, 37).

We apply silicate-metal hydrogen isotope fractionation factors calculated at 20 to 60 GPa and 3000 to 3500 K within an effective single-stage core formation framework (see Materials and Methods). The hydrogen isotope fractionation during core-mantle differentiation was modeled using two end-member models (Rayleigh and equilibrium distillation) (Fig. 3). As the absolute hydrogen isotope composition of the BE before differentiation remains debated, we focus on the relative δD shifts imposed by core formation. Our modeling shows that core formation would shift δD in the BSE by +22 to +78‰ for a Rayleigh distillation process if 75 to 95% of Earth’s water was partitioned into the core (Fig. 3A). This value becomes smaller (+12 to +25‰) when considering an equilibrium fractionation scenario (Fig. 3B). The present-day mantle is estimated to have a δD of −60 to −150‰ (38, 39), while the BSE, accounting for mantle and surface reservoirs (δD = 0‰), would fall between −30 and −110‰, assuming one to three ocean masses of water in the mantle (37, 40, 41). These values imply that the bulk proto-Earth before core formation would have a δD of −42 to −188‰, regardless of the core formation model applied. Correspondingly, the present-day core would be expected to retain a δD of −46 to −192‰ (Fig. 3, C and D).

Equilibrium and Rayleigh fractionation models represent end-member descriptions that bracket the range of possible isotopic responses to metal-silicate separation under core-forming conditions. To explicitly address more realistic accretion scenarios, we additionally implemented a multistage core formation model in which Earth accretes through a sequence of planetary embryo impacts, with pressure- and temperature-dependent hydrogen partitioning and isotope fractionation at each stage (see Materials and Methods). As shown in fig. S6, the hydrogen isotope evolution predicted by the multistage model falls between the equilibrium and Rayleigh end-member predictions, as expected. Because any multistage model necessarily relies on assumptions regarding impactor properties, equilibration efficiency, and pressure-temperature paths, we conservatively use the equilibrium and Rayleigh models to bracket the plausible range of hydrogen isotope compositions in Earth’s mantle and core.

Notably, recent measurements of δD in olivine-hosted melt inclusions from Baffin Island lavas report an average δD of −144 ± 24‰ (39), markedly lower than typical upper mantle values (38). This depletion, alongside elevated 3He/4He ratios and 182W anomalies observed in mantle plumes, suggests a contribution from core-mantle interaction after excluding alternative explanations such as magmatic degassing, crustal contamination, or recycled subducted lithosphere. These negative δD values are consistent with our predicted δD range for the core, supporting the hypothesis that core-mantle exchange may have overprinted the hydrogen isotopic composition of deep mantle reservoirs.

DISCUSSION

Interactions between solar nebular gas and molten planetesimals

A long-standing model for the origin of Earth’s water proposes that the proto-Earth accreted predominantly from volatile-poor material, with volatiles—including water—added later by a late veneer of volatile-rich bodies such as CCs or comets (6–11). This framework was originally motivated by highly siderophile element systematics and has been widely applied to Earth’s volatile budget. However, accumulating isotopic and experimental constraints now indicate that late accretion contributed only a limited fraction of Earth’s volatiles (42–47). In particular, mass-independent isotopic signatures (45) and metal-silicate partitioning experiments (46, 47) suggest that the mass of late veneer material was very small, implying that it could have supplied at most a minor portion of Earth’s present water inventory. These observations instead point to substantial water being incorporated during Earth’s main accretion and differentiation stages, either through volatile-bearing building blocks (12–14) and/or interaction with solar nebular gas (15–18).

Nucleosynthetic isotope anomalies point to two main compositional models for Earth’s accreting material: (i) ~85% ECs + 15% CCs (48, 49) and (ii) 71% ECs + 24% ordinary chondrites (OCs) + 5% CCs (50). Both models converge on EC-like material as Earth’s dominant building block, with smaller contributions from OC- and/or CC-like sources. We note that recent nucleosynthetic isotope measurements, particularly for silicon, have been interpreted to suggest that early-formed differentiated planetesimals, rather than undifferentiated chondrites, may have constituted a substantial fraction of Earth’s accreting material (51). However, these bodies are expected to have been volatile poor due to extensive melting, metal-silicate segregation, and volatile loss (52, 53). Consequently, even if nonchondritic material dominated Earth’s early mass accretion, chondrite-like reservoirs remain appropriate reference compositions for constraining Earth’s volatile and hydrogen isotope inventory. Mass-independent anomalies in multiple elements favor the second model—71% ECs, 24% OCs, and 5% Vigarano-type (CV) or Ornans-type (CO) CCs—as the best isotopic fit for Earth (50).

Our results show that proto-Earth, before core formation, must have accreted materials with δD values ranging from −42 to −188‰. Using the average δD values and water contents (CH2O) of the least altered representatives of these groups [EC: δD = −104‰, CH2O = 0.4 wt %; OC: δD = +1013‰, CH2O = 1.0 wt %; CV: δD = −39‰, CH2O = 1.2 wt %; CO: δD = −16‰, CH2O = 4.4 wt %; Ivuna-type carbonaceous chondrites (CI): δD = 117‰, CH2O = 10.3 wt %; Mighei-type carbonaceous chondrites (CM): δD = −56‰, CH2O = 9.7 wt %; Renazzo-type carbonaceous chondrites (CR): δD = +639‰, CH2O = 4.9 wt %] (table S5) (14, 25, 54–58), the first model yields a bulk δD (−69‰) that falls squarely within this required range. This outcome implies that proto-Earth may have accreted its water directly from its building material.

In contrast, the weighted δD of the second model is ~+168‰, with an initial water content of 0.7 wt %. This budget—equivalent to ~30 ocean masses—could be sufficient to supply Earth’s present-day mantle and core (20, 21, 37, 40, 41), but its δD value is markedly higher than required by our models, largely owing to the extreme deuterium enrichment of OCs relative to ECs. A possible explanation involves evaporative hydrogen loss during early planetesimal differentiation, driven by impact-induced heating and the decay of short-lived nuclei such as 26Al (59). However, our calculated equilibrium hydrogen isotope fractionation between H2 vapor and silicate melt (−80 to −57‰ at 1300 to 1500 K; table S4) shows that evaporation preferentially removes isotopically light hydrogen, thereby increasing the δD of the residual material. As a result, the planetesimal evaporation would exacerbate, rather than resolve, the mismatch between the high δD of Earth’s building material and the lower δD values of the BE.

This discrepancy suggests the involvement of an additional, isotopically light hydrogen source to explain Earth’s present-day D/H ratio. The solar nebula, which persisted for the first ~10 Ma of Solar System history, provided an abundant reservoir of hydrogen with δD values as low as −850‰ (24). Interaction between this nebular gas and molten planetesimals could have substantially lowered their δD through isotopic exchange. Hydrogen from the nebula readily dissolves into molten planetesimals at high temperatures (15–18), while endogenous hydrogen initially present in planetesimals would degas into the surrounding nebular atmosphere (60). Although H2 solubility in silicate melts is limited at low pressures (32), hydrogen partitioning into metallic phases can substantially enhance the incorporation of nebular hydrogen in planetesimals (16), promoting hydrogen isotopic exchange between gas and melt.

To quantify this process, we developed an equilibrium model of gas-melt exchange for a range of nebular pressures, planetesimal sizes, and initial nebular D/H ratios (see Materials and Methods). Our calculations suggest that at a nebular pressure of 10−4 bar, interaction between nebular gas with δD values of −160 to −340‰ and kilometer-scale molten planetesimals could reduce the planetesimals’ δD to within the required range (−42 to −188‰) (Fig. 4). For smaller planetesimals (~0.1-km radius), a less deuterium-depleted nebular gas (−110 to −260‰) would suffice, while larger planetesimals (>3 km) would require higher nebular pressures to achieve comparable isotopic exchange. For planetesimals approaching 100 km in radius, nebular pressures would need to exceed 0.1 bar to reproduce the δD of the BE (fig. S7)—consistent with predictions that local atmospheric pressures may rise as planetesimals grow (61).

Together, our results identify two viable pathways for the origin of Earth’s water. In one scenario, the proto-Earth directly inherited water from EC-rich building material, whose hydrogen isotope composition already falls within the required range. In the other, isotopic exchange between nebular gas (δD = −100 to −850‰) and kilometer-scale molten planetesimals reset initially deuterium-rich mixtures to the observed Earth-like values (Fig. 5). The latter mechanism is quantitatively consistent with the hydrogen isotopic distribution inferred for the early Solar System during disk infall (62) and highlights the efficiency of nebular gas–magma interactions in regulating volatile inventories. Hence, our models reconcile Earth’s hydrogen budget with its isotopic composition and demonstrate that early gas-planetesimal interactions were likely a critical and potentially universal process shaping the volatile and isotopic architecture of terrestrial planets (63).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Equilibrium isotope fractionation factor

The equilibrium isotope fractionation factor (103lnα) between two phases (A and B) can be expressed in terms of the reduced partition function ratio (β) as103lnαA−B=103lnβA−103lnβB(1)where β represents the isotope fractionation between the considered system and an ideal gas. Under the harmonic approximation, β can be calculated using the Bigeleisen-Mayer equation (64), which links isotope partitioning to vibrational frequenciesβ=QhQl=∏i3Nuihuilexp(−uih2)exp(−uil2)1−exp(−uih)1−exp(−uil)(2)where Q is the partition function and the subscripts h and l refer to the heavy and light isotopes, respectively. The index i denotes the vibrational mode, and N is the total number of atoms in the cell. The parameter ui is defined as ui=ℏωi/kBT, where ℏ and kB are the reduced Planck constant and Boltzmann constant, respectively. ωi is the vibrational frequency of the ith mode, and T is the temperature in kelvin.

In general, vibrational frequencies for each phase can be calculated using DFT under the harmonic approximation, and therefore, the equilibrium isotope fractionation factors can be obtained using Eqs. 1 and 2. This approach has been widely applied to a range of isotopic systems (42–44, 65–67). However, for light isotopes such as hydrogen, nuclear quantum and anharmonic effects lead to substantial deviations from harmonic predictions, limiting the accuracy and applicability of this method under high-temperature, high-pressure conditions.

Path-integral molecular dynamics

PIMD provides an efficient framework for capturing nuclear quantum and anharmonic effects by representing each quantum particle as a classical ring polymer, in which multiple replicas (beads) are connected by harmonic springs (27). When a sufficient number of replicas is used, this approach yields accurate quantum-mechanical descriptions of atomic motion.

Despite these improvements, PIMD simulations at the DFT level remain computationally prohibitive for systems with more than 100 atoms due to the large number of required replicas. To address this challenge, in this study, we integrate machine learning–based acceleration to strike a balance between accuracy and computational efficiency, enabling applications of PIMD in complex systems.

Machine learning potentials

MLPs for silicate and metallic melts were first developed on the basis of FPMD data. Initial compositions included Mg32Si32O106H20 for silicate melt and Fe100H28 for metallic melt. To assess the effects of other elements, more compositionally complex systems—Mg30Ca2Fe4Si24Al3NaO92H6 for silicate melt and Fe93Ni5Si6O8CS3H12 for metallic melt—were also considered. Supercells were initially equilibrated at temperatures > 7000 K to ensure complete melting, followed by runs at 3000 K and 30 to 90 GPa to sample melt structures under core-forming conditions. All FPMD simulations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package based on DFT. The electronic interactions were described using the projector-augmented wave method (69), and exchange-correlation effects were treated using the generalized gradient approximation (70) with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional. Simulations were carried out in the NVT ensemble (constant number of atoms, volume, and temperature), with temperature controlled by Nosé-Hoover thermostat. The plane wave cutoff energy was set to 600 eV, and the gamma-point was used to sample the Brillouin zone. The timestep was set to 1 fs, and each trajectory was run for at least 20 ps to ensure sufficient configurational sampling for training. For iron-bearing systems, spin polarization was tested by allowing the magnetic moments of iron atoms to evolve freely.

To ensure sufficient configurational sampling, we calculated mean square displacements (MSDs) for all major elements in both silicate and metallic melts (fig. S8). The MSDs of H, Mg, Fe, Si, and O increase monotonically with time under all investigated conditions, confirming that the systems remain molten rather than glassy or solid. At 3000 K and ~90 GPa, network-forming cations in the silicate melt exhibit slow diffusion, as expected at high pressure; however, hydrogen diffuses rapidly, with MSDs exceeding the commonly adopted threshold for adequate sampling. Because hydrogen is the isotope of interest in this study, these results demonstrate that hydrogen configurational space is well sampled even under the highest-pressure conditions considered.

MLPs were trained using the DeePMD-kit (71). The embedding and fitting networks were set to (25, 50, 100) and (240, 240, 240), respectively. A cutoff radius of 6.0 Å was used to capture local atomic environments. Energies, forces, and virials were included in the loss function, with corresponding weight factors set to (0.01, 1), (1000, 1), and (0.01, 1), respectively. The total training steps were set to 2 × 106. The performance of the MLPs was evaluated against both FPMD and PIMD datasets. Test configurations were randomly selected from FPMD and PIMD trajectories at ~30 to 90 GPa and 3000 K that were fully independent of the training data. For each test set, MLP-predicted energies and forces were compared with DFT results. As shown in fig. S1, the MLPs reproduce energies and forces with root mean square errors (RMSEs) of 3.82 to 7.46 meV per atom and 0.19 to 0.23 eV/Å for silicate melts and 5.65 to 5.72 meV per atom and 0.24 to 0.27 eV/Å for metallic melts, respectively.

On the basis of the high-accuracy MLPs, machine-learning−accelerated PIMD simulations were performed using i-PI (72) and LAMMPS (73). Each atom was represented by 32 beads, which have been shown to be sufficient for hydrogen (27). The timestep was set to 0.25 fs, and each simulation was run for 20 ps. After equilibration, the final 15 ps of each trajectory was used for statistical analysis to compute β factors and associated uncertainties. The time evolution of ZSC was shown in fig. S9. Statistical errors were estimated using the block-averaging method, in which mean values and associated uncertainties were evaluated as a function of block size. The estimated uncertainty increases with block size and converges to a plateau once the block length exceeds the correlation time of ZSC (fig. S10). The uncertainty corresponding to this plateau is adopted as the final statistical error.

To investigate the effects of oxygen fugacity, additional MLPs were constructed for silicate compositions Mg32Si32O101H20 and Mg32Si32O96H20. In theoretical simulations, oxygen fugacity cannot be directly imposed as in experiments. Instead, it is usually represented by the relative abundances of redox-active species, such as H2 and H2O, which correspond to more reducing and more oxidizing conditions, respectively. Previous studies suggest that core formation occurred over a relatively narrow redox range, approximately IW–2.5 to IW–1.5 (74–76). Under these conditions, hydrogen in silicate melts is expected to occur mainly as H2O and H2, with H2 probably comprising up to a maximum of ~50% of total dissolved hydrogen (32). To bracket the uncertainty and to cover the redox range relevant to core formation, we considered three end-member cases in our calculations: hydrogen occurring entirely as H2O (more oxidizing), entirely as H2 (more reducing), and an intermediate case with H2:H2O = 1:1.

To further assess the robustness of our results, we developed additional independent MLPs for silicate (Mg30Ca2Fe4Si24Al3NaO92H6) and metallic (Fe100H28) melts using an active-learning strategy (77) that systematically expands the configurational space over a wide pressure-temperature range (20 to 140 GPa and 2000 to 6000 K). These independent MLPs yield energy RMSEs of 6.16 and 5.60 meV per atom and force RMSEs of 0.25 and 0.24 eV/Å for silicate and metallic melts, respectively. Using these updated potentials, we recalculated hydrogen isotope fractionation factors (Fig. 2 and table S2). The resulting fractionation factors are consistent with the original values within statistical uncertainty, confirming the robustness of the results.

Last, to assess the effect of evaporative hydrogen loss during early planetesimal differentiation, we calculated the equilibrium hydrogen isotope fractionation factors between nebular gas and silicate melt (103lnαgas-silicate) at 1300 to 1500 K (table S4). The silicate melt was modeled using the Mg30Ca2Fe4Si24Al3NaO92H6 composition, and the nebular gas was represented by a single H2 molecule placed in a 20 Å–by–20 Å–by–20 Å cubic box. Tests confirmed that finite-size effects were negligible. The β factors for H2 vapor were calculated using both first-principles and machine-learning PIMD, yielding consistent results and further validating the accuracy of the MLPs.

Pressure correction of the PBE functional

The pressures of silicate and metallic melts in FPMD simulations may be underestimated by the PBE functional. Huang et al. (78) suggested that PBE may underestimate pressure in liquid nonmagnetic iron by up to ~17 GPa, whereas when the magnetization of iron is taken into account, the pressure offset is reduced to ~6 GPa (79).

To assess this effect on isotope fractionation, we conservatively treat the larger offset (~17 GPa) as an upper bound for metallic melts. Under this assumption, the 103lnβ of hydrogen in metallic melts changes by only ~2 to ~3‰ (fig. S5). In contrast, 103lnβ for silicate melts is nearly pressure independent over the same range, owing to the compensating effects of CN increase and H–O bond weakening. Although the magnitude of any pressure correction for silicate melts remains debated, its influence on the silicate β factors is expected to be minor. Thus, even adopting the maximum plausible pressure correction for metallic melts only increases the silicate-metal fractionation factor by no more than ~2 to ~3‰.

This pressure correction cannot explain the discrepancy between our results and previous estimates (~50‰) (33). We note that previous estimates of silicate-metal hydrogen isotope fractionation were derived indirectly by combining fractionation factors among silicates, H2O, H2, and metals. These phases, however, cannot represent coexisting silicate and metallic melts during core formation. Therefore, this discrepancy is primarily attributed to these methodological differences, which underscores the novelty and importance of directly constraining silicate-metal hydrogen isotope fractionation.

Solar nebular gas–molten planetesimal interactions

Observations and disk evolution models indicate that an H2-dominated solar nebula persisted for at least several million to ~10 Myr (82), overlapping with the timescale of planetesimal formation and early melting of small bodies. During this interval, kilometer-scale planetesimals are expected to have been surrounded by nebular gas, making gas-planetesimal interaction physically plausible during the earliest stages of accretion. To evaluate whether interaction between solar nebular gas and molten planetesimals could plausibly modify planetary D/H ratios, we developed an equilibrium model of gas-melt exchange that explores a range of nebular pressures, planetesimal sizes, and initial nebular D/H ratios.

The specific interaction considered here involves abundant nebular H2 and relatively small, molten planetesimals. Under such conditions, chemical and isotopic exchange is expected to be efficient. A simple timescale estimate illustrates this point: The diffusion coefficient of hydrogen in silicate melts is on the order of ~10−8 to 10−7 m2 s−1, implying that hydrogen can diffuse over kilometer-length scales on timescales of ~105 years. This timescale is much shorter compared to the lifetime of the solar nebula, supporting the use of near-equilibrium exchange as a reasonable end-member description for small molten bodies. In addition, if planetesimals were only partially molten, hydrogen exchange with nebular gas would be less efficient than in the fully molten end-member case. This would reduce, but not eliminate, the degree of D/H modification. A smaller extent of melting can be compensated by plausible variations in other parameters, such as nebular gas pressure, planetesimal size, or the D/H ratio of nebular H2, all of which are explored in Fig. 4. Overall, this nebular gas–planetesimal interaction model is not intended to provide a detailed reconstruction of molten planetesimal evolution but rather serves as a first-order framework to assess whether interaction between solar nebular gas and molten planetesimals could, in principle, reset hydrogen isotope compositions before planetary accretion.

In the model, we do not explicitly model individual chemical reactions. Instead, hydrogen exchange between nebular gas and molten planetesimals is represented using isotopic equilibrium coupled with mass balance between the two reservoirs (H2gas = H2melt) (15, 60). This simplified treatment is appropriate for high-temperature conditions, under which hydrogen exchange is expected to be efficient and near equilibrium (18).
