Analytical and clinical validation of a novel proximity extension assay-based plasma biomarker panel in a cohort of prevalent neurodegenerative dementias
Authors: Giuseppe Mario Bentivenga, Angela Mammana, Simone Baiardi, Erica Vittoriosi, Andrea Mastrangelo, Edoardo Ruggeri, Claudia Marina Vargiu, Barbara Polischi, Michelangelo Stanzani-Maserati, Giovanni Rizzo, Roberta Pantieri, Alberto Raggi, Sabina Capellari, Piero Parchi

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlood-based biomarkers are increasingly recognized as promising tools for the diagnosis and monitoring of neurodegenerative diseases, offering a minimally invasive alternative to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) testing. We evaluated the analytical performance and clinical utility of the Olink Target 48 Neurodegeneration panel, a novel multiplex proteomic platform based on the proximity extension assay (PEA) technology, in a large, clinically diverse dementia cohort.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed plasma samples from 238 patients with Alzheimer’s disease (AD), dementia with Lewy bodies, frontotemporal dementia, progressive supranuclear palsy, and corticobasal degeneration, along with 65 healthy controls, quantifying 41 proteins in each sample. We assessed analytical performance using intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation, evaluated diagnostic accuracy through receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, and investigated associations between biomarker levels, clinical severity measures, and pathology-specific CSF biomarkers for AD and Lewy body pathology (LBP) using general linear models.ResultsThe platform quantified 32 proteins with variable analytical performance; nine were excluded due to poor detectability. Strong correlations were observed between PEA-based measurements and established immunoassays for plasma pTau217, NEFL, and GFAP (all p < 0.001). Plasma pTau217 demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy for AD, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) exceeding 0.91 against all comparison groups. Novel ratios combining NEFL with markers of immune function or synaptic integrity (NEFL/ITGB2, NEFL/ITGAM, NEFL/SCG2) achieved AUCs exceeding 0.93 for discriminating patients from controls, significantly outperforming NEFL alone (all p < 0.001). Thirteen proteins, spanning markers of neuroaxonal damage, myelin-associated processes, and immune function (i.e., Abeta40, Abeta42, BMP7, CLSTN3, ENO2, KLK8, MMP10, NEFL, NPTXR, OMG, RTN4R, SCG2, SDC4, all p < 0.01) showed significant independent associations with disease stage as measured by the Clinical Dementia Rating scale. Four proteins (i.e., pTau217, GFAP, SYT1, and SDC4) were significantly associated with AD pathology, while three (ENO2, ITGAM, and ITGB2) showed significant associations with LBP (all p < 0.05).ConclusionsThis multiplex platform provides multiplex biomarker measurements with potential utility for AD diagnosis and disease staging across neurodegenerative disorders. Background

Cognitive impairment due to neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) spectrum disorders, and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), affects millions of individuals worldwide [1]. Besides clinics, differential diagnosis among these disorders is supported by nuclear medicine (e.g., FDG-PET, DaTscan) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers (e.g., amyloid beta42 (Abeta42), the Abeta42/Abeta40 ratio, tau phosphorylated at threonine 181 (pTau181), and α-synuclein seeding amplification assays (αSyn SAA)) [2]. However, these approaches are too costly and invasive for large-scale implementation and are generally limited to research or highly specialized clinical settings. As a result, considerable research has shifted toward minimally invasive biomarkers, with a particular focus on blood-based measures. Among these, plasma phosphorylated tau217 (pTau217) has demonstrated diagnostic accuracy comparable to established CSF markers for the detection of AD [3, 4]. In contrast, neurofilament light chain (NEFL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) provide more limited support for differential diagnosis, yet remain of substantial interest as surrogate markers of neuronal injury and astrocytic activation, respectively [5–8].

More recently, large-scale high-throughput proteomic studies employing the proximity extension assay (PEA), a novel affinity-based method, have enabled the systematic identification of a broad range of other fluid biomarkers relevant to differentiating major dementing disorders [9–11]. Building on these advances, multiplex proteomic platforms capable of highly accurate, absolute quantification of a restricted set of such novel markers have become available. However, the analytical performance and clinical value of such platforms have never been validated in real-world patient cohorts.

In this study, we leverage one such platform, the Olink Target 48 Neurodegeneration panel, which assays 41 proteins spanning multiple biological domains, including markers linked to AD (pTau217, Abeta40, Abeta42, BACE1), DLB (DDC), nervous system cell damage (NEFL, MOG, OMG), synaptic function (NPTXR, NPTX1, NPTX2, CLSTN3, GLRX, NLGN1, STX1B, SYT1), neuroinflammation (GFAP, TREM2, AGER, HLA-DRA, MMP10), and additional processes implicated in neurodegeneration [12].

Our objectives are to (i) validate the platform analytical performance; (ii) evaluate the diagnostic performance of individual biomarkers and multimarker panels in a large, deeply phenotyped cohort representative of the most common neurodegenerative dementing disorders assessed in a third level cognitive disorders center (i.e., AD, DLB, FTLD spectrum disorders); (iii) investigate the potential association between biomarker levels and clinical features (e.g., presentation, disease severity); (iv) characterize how plasma concentrations of the most promising biomarkers vary with copathologies across diagnostic groups, as defined by CSF biomarkers for AD and Lewy body pathology (LBP).

Methods

Patient selection

We retrospectively analyzed plasma samples from 238 patients with mild cognitive impairment or dementia due to AD, DLB, frontotemporal dementia (FTD), progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), corticobasal degeneration (CBD) and plasma samples from 65 healthy controls (CTRL) submitted to the Neuropathology Laboratory (NP-Lab) at the Institute of Neurological Science of Bologna, Italy, between 2005 and 2025. CSF samples were available for all patients (but not CTRL) and used to measure established core biomarkers, as detailed below. We reviewed the full diagnostic work-up, including clinical records, neuropsychological assessments, and neuroimaging data. Neuropsychological evaluation was conducted as reported [7]. Diagnostic group categorization was determined by expert agreement, based on established diagnostic criteria [13–19]. Only patients with a “probable” (i.e., high certainty) clinical diagnosis for a given disorder were included in the cohort. No individuals with severe systemic illnesses were enrolled in the study. The final cohort included 115 patients within the FTLD spectrum, FTD (n = 59), PSP (n = 29), and CBD (n = 27). The FTD subgroup encompassed individuals with behavioral-variant FTD (bvFTD) (n = 40), nonfluent/agrammatic PPA (nfvPPA) (n = 7), semantic variant PPA (svPPA) (n = 1), logopenic variant PPA (lvPPA) (n = 3), as well as patients who met criteria for bvFTD and/or PPA but also showed evidence of both upper and lower motor neuron involvement (FTD-ALS) (n = 8). Of note, within the FTD group, 10 patients showed C9orf72 repeat expansions, while others carried pathogenic variants in GRN (n = 8), TARDBP (n = 1), and FUS (n = 2). The AD group (n = 63) included patients with a typical amnestic presentation (n = 38) as well as those with atypical variants, such as frontal (n = 2), logopenic (n = 6), posterior (n = 4), and motor (n = 13) variants [20]. All patients in the AD group exhibited a CSF biomarker profile consistent with the clinical diagnosis (i.e., low Abeta42/Abeta40 ratio and high pTau181) [7]. We also included patients with DLB (n = 60), all of whom showed a CSF biomarker profile consistent with LBP, confirmed by a positive αSyn SAA [21]. Finally, the CTRL group comprised healthy blood donors (i.e., medical history not relevant for significant diseases/medications). Before blood collection, all CTRL participants underwent medical evaluation, including a standardized interview to exclude neurological symptoms.

CSF and plasma biomarker analyses

CSF samples were obtained by lumbar puncture (LP) at the L3/L4 or L4/L5 intervertebral level, centrifuged in case of blood contamination, divided into aliquots, and stored in polypropylene tubes at -80 °C until analysis. For the AD biomarker measurements, total tau (t-tau), pTau181, Abeta42, and Abeta40 were measured by automated chemiluminescent enzyme immunoassay (CLEIA) on the Lumipulse G1200 platform (Fujirebio, Gent, Belgium). Pathological values for defining the AD status were determined using validated in-house cutoffs (Abeta42/Abeta40 ratio < 0.68) [7]. Commercially available ELISA kits were used to measure NEFL [7]. Moreover, all samples were tested for αSyn SAA to define the LBP status, as reported [21]. Beyond serving as diagnostic confirmation within their respective groups (i.e., AD and DLB), these AD and LBP status definitions were also used as markers of AD and LBP copathologies in other diagnostic groups (i.e., FTD, PSP, and CBD).

EDTA plasma samples were collected, aliquoted, and stored at − 80 ◦C according to standard procedures. NEFL and GFAP were measured on a SiMoA SR-X analyzer platform (Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA) with the SiMoA NF-light advantage and SiMoA GFAP discovery kits, respectively [7]. pTau217 was measured by automated chemiluminescent enzyme immunoassay on the Lumipulse G1200 platform (Fujirebio, Gent, Belgium). Of note, pre-analytical procedures for both CSF and plasma samples remained consistent across all collected samples, regardless of the year of collection.

Plasma proteome profiling

The PEA technology was used to quantify 41 proteins (full list provided in Supplementary Table 1) in each plasma sample using the Olink Target 48 Neurodegeneration panel (Olink Proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden). Further details regarding panel characteristics and validation data are available on the manufacturer’s webpage [12]. All proteomic analyses were conducted at the NP-Lab by personnel blinded to the clinical diagnosis. Samples were randomized across eight plates, each incorporating the appropriate intra- and inter-plate quality controls provided by the manufacturer. Each plate also included two internal control samples (one CTRL and one AD), enabling a more robust assessment of potential batch effects by allowing evaluation of assay performance across a wide range of biomarker concentrations.

Genetic analysis

Genotyping of APOE was performed in all patients with DNA viable for analyses by restriction fragment length polymorphism according to Wenham et al. [22]. All FTLD patients with a positive familial history of dementia defined by the presence of at least one neurodegenerative disorder case among the first-degree relatives and/or those with a clinical history compatible with early onset dementia were screened for variants in 27 dementia associated genes, including GRN, MAPT, TARDBP, and FUS, as previously reported [23]. They were also screened for the presence of C9orf72 repeat expansions using the two-step strategy with southern blotting confirmation [7].

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.12.2 with the following main packages: NumPy (1.26.4) [24], Pandas (2.2.3) [25], SciPy (1.13.1) [26], statsmodels (0.14.2) [27], scikit-learn (1.4.2) [28], Matplotlib (3.9.2) [29], and Seaborn (0.13.2) [30]. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The t-test, one-way ANOVA, Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis tests were variably applied depending on data distribution and group numerosity. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell frequencies were insufficient (i.e., < 5 in > 20% of cells). Post-hoc tests adjusting for multiple comparisons were performed when the analyses involved > 2 groups. Agreement between some of the plasma biomarkers measured by PEA versus SiMoA (NEFL, GFAP) or CLEIA (pTau217) was evaluated using Passing-Bablok regression. Percentage differences were calculated as [(PEA - comparator)/comparator] × 100. Spearman’s correlations were performed to test the association between biomarker concentrations. General linear models were used to assess associations between demographic (age at sampling, sex, timespan onset-sampling), clinical (Clinical Dementia Rating scale (CDR), corrected Mini Mental State Examination (cMMSE), dopaminergic treatment), genetic (APOE4 carrier status), and pathological (AD and LBP status) variables (independent predictors) and plasma biomarker concentrations (log2-transformed, dependent variables). Univariate models tested each predictor separately and were false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected (Benjamini-Hochberg, q < 0.05). Multivariate models included all significant univariate predictors for each biomarker. Categorical predictors used F-tests with partial η² as effect size; continuous and binary predictors yielded β coefficients. Two separate analyses were performed: (1) associations with demographic/clinical/genetic variables, performed both excluding and including diagnostic group as a predictor, to distinguish between associations driven by between-group differences and those reflecting associations shared across diagnostic categories; and (2) associations with AD and LBP status adjusted for age at sampling, sex, and time onset-sampling. To identify biomarkers distinguishing between neurodegenerative disorders, general linear models with log2-transformed biomarker concentrations adjusted for age and sex were used. The overall effect of the diagnostic group was evaluated using a F-test, and pairwise comparisons between all diagnostic groups were performed using contrast-based t-tests. P-values from both the global tests and pairwise comparisons were FDR-corrected (Benjamini-Hochberg, q < 0.05). For biomarkers showing significant group differences, diagnostic accuracy was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was calculated. To assess potential optimism in AUC estimates, a bootstrap optimism-correction procedure was applied (5000 iterations). DeLong’s test was used to compare AUC values between biomarkers. Optimal cutoffs were determined using Youden’s index.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The groups were matched for sex distribution, except for a higher proportion of female participants in the AD group than the DLB group (p = 0.023). Age at sampling differed significantly across diagnostic groups: DLB patients were older than those with AD (p < 0.001), CTRL (p < 0.001), and FTD (p < 0.001), whereas FTD patients were younger than individuals with AD (p < 0.001), CBD (p < 0.001), and PSP (p < 0.001). Further, CTRL subjects were younger than patients with AD (p < 0.001), CBD (p < 0.001), and PSP (p < 0.001). Assay analytical performance

First, we evaluated the panel’s analytical performance. Detectability varied considerably across proteins, with most analytes showing 0% of samples below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ). However, nine proteins, i.e., FOXO3, GDNF, HLA-DRA, LRRK2, NGF, NLGN1, SNCAIP, TP53, and WWOX, had more than 20% of samples below the LLOQ and were therefore excluded from further analyses. For proteins with > 0% but < 20% of samples below the LLOQ, concentrations were estimated automatically by the manufacturer’s software from the calibration curve and retained for downstream analyses. Importantly, protein detectability was not significantly influenced by diagnostic group membership (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). The average intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CV) were below 15% for all proteins, as assessed using Olink internal control samples. When estimated using NP-Lab controls, the mean inter-assay CV was below 25% (i.e., the expected inter-plate CV for plasma samples according to the manufacturer) for most proteins. However, six of the nine poorly detectable proteins exhibited inter-assay CVs greater than 25%, ranging from 26% to 88%. Moreover, three additional proteins (ENO2, GFAP, and BACE1) displayed CVs exceeding 25% despite being within the assay quantification range (Supplementary Fig. 3); these proteins were retained in downstream analyses given their quantifiability, but results involving these analytes should be interpreted with caution.

Correlation between PEA and established immunoassays

Next, we assessed the correlation between plasma biomarker concentrations measured by PEA and those obtained using established immunoassays: CLEIA for pTau217 and SiMoA for NEFL and GFAP (Fig. 1). Spearman correlation coefficients were high for all assays (pTau217: ρ = 0.829; NEFL: ρ = 0.894; GFAP: ρ = 0.848; all p < 0.001). However, Passing-Bablok regression analysis (PEA on the y-axis, CLEIA/SiMoA on the x-axis) revealed significant systematic differences in measured concentrations. Specifically, PEA measurements for pTau217 (0.020-12.127 pg/mL) and NEFL (293.934-18817.247 pg/mL) were substantially higher than CLEIA pTau217 (0.037–2.776 pg/mL) and SiMoA NEFL (4.980-449.120 pg/mL) values, with mean increases of 305% and 5875%, respectively. In contrast, PEA measurements for GFAP (9.228-240.186 pg/mL) were lower than SiMoA values (37.710-1298.554 pg/mL), with a mean decrease of 80%.

Biomarker association with demographic variables

Details on the associations between biomarker levels and demographic variables are shown in Supplementary Fig. 4. In the overall cohort, ITGAM, and ITGB2 were inversely associated with age at sampling, whereas NPTX2, TREM1, Abeta42, SMOC1, NEFL, Abeta40, GFAP, and TREM2 showed positive associations (all p < 0.05). Following adjustment for diagnostic group, all associations remained significant except for ITGAM (p = 0.291) and ITGB2 (p = 0.784). Sex-related differences were observed, with higher levels of NPTX1 and GFAP in females and higher levels of NPTX2, KLK8, RTN4R, and VSNL1 in males (all p < 0.05); after adjustment for diagnostic group, two additional proteins were positively associated with male sex, i.e., MMP10 and TREM1 (all p < 0.05). No associations were observed between protein levels and the interval from disease onset to sampling.

Biomarker distribution across diagnostic groups

Several proteins were differentially distributed across diagnostic groups (Supplementary Table 3, Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 5). GFAP levels were higher in AD than in FTD, CBD, PSP DLB, and CTRL (all p < 0.001), while remaining elevated in most non-AD dementia groups compared with controls (FTD p = 0.001; PSP p = 0.008; DLB p < 0.001). ITGAM was consistently lower across dementia groups compared with CTRL (all p < 0.05). Similarly, ITGB2 was lower in AD, DLB, and CBD than CTRL (all p < 0.05). NEFL showed a robust increase across all dementia groups, with the highest levels in FTD and CBD compared with CTRL, AD, and DLB (all p < 0.05). It was also increased in FTD relative to PSP (p = 0.010) and in AD, DLB, and PSP compared with CTRL (all p < 0.001). SCG2 was lower in AD, DLB, FTD and PSP relative to CTRL (all p < 0.05). SYT1 levels were lower in FTD than AD (p < 0.001). Finally, pTau217 robustly discriminated AD from all other diagnostic groups, with markedly higher levels than in CTRL, FTD, PSP, DLB and CBD (all p < 0.001). Additionally, it was higher in DLB than FTD and CTRL (both p < 0.001). Within the AD and FTD groups, there were no differences in the distribution of biomarker levels across clinically defined disease subtypes (e.g., typical vs. atypical AD presentation, bvFTD vs. PPA).

Biomarker association with clinical variables and APOE status

Details on the associations between biomarker levels, clinical variables, and APOE status are provided in Supplementary Fig. 4. CDR scores were associated with levels of fifteen proteins (Abeta40: β=-0.286, Abeta42: β=-0.196, BMP7: β=-0.135, CLSTN3: β=-0.108, ENO2: β = 0.329; ITGAM: β=-0.108; ITGB2: β=-0.172, KLK8: β=-0.153; MMP10: β=-0.240, NEFL: β = 0.400, NPTXR: β=-0.216, OMG: β=-0.274, RTN4R: β=-0.129, SCG2: β=-0.207, and SDC4: β = 0.186; all p < 0.01). After adjustment for diagnostic group, all associations remained significant except for ITAGM (p = 0.758), and ITGB2 (p = 0.544) (Fig. 3). Lower cMMSE scores were associated with higher GFAP (β=-0.026, p = 0.03) and pTau217 levels (β=-0.121, p < 0.001), with only the latter remaining significant after adjustment (β=-0.061, p < 0.001). APOE4 carrier status was associated with higher GFAP (β = 0.428), SYT1 (β = 0.251) and pTau217 (β = 1.361) levels (all p < 0.01), although only the association with pTau217 remained significant after adjustment for group membership (β = 0.509, p = 0.004). Dopaminergic treatment was associated with higher levels of DDC (β = 0.947, p < 0.001), and lower levels of ITGAM (β=-0.233, p = 0.003); however, only the former association remained significant after diagnostic group adjustment (β = 0.952, p < 0.001).

Biomarker diagnostic performance in differentiating patients from CTRL

Subsequently, we investigated the ability of biomarkers with a differential distribution between CTRL and at least three diagnostic groups (i.e., GFAP, ITGAM, ITGB2, NEFL, and SCG2) to discriminate patients from healthy participants (Supplementary Table 4). For this analysis, all non-control diagnostic groups (AD, DLB, FTD, PSP, and CBD) were pooled and compared against the control group, reflecting the potential context of use of these biomarkers, e.g., case-finding in memory clinic or primary care settings. Individually, these biomarkers demonstrated moderate to good diagnostic accuracy, with AUC values of 0.903 (95% CI: 0.855–0.943) for NEFL, 0.829 (95% CI: 0.774–0.881) for GFAP, 0.750 (95% CI: 0.693–0.805) for ITGAM, 0.737 (95% CI: 0.675–0.798) for ITGB2, and 0.728 (95% CI: 0.665–0.791) for SCG2. Given the opposite distribution observed between patients and controls, we further evaluated the diagnostic performance of protein ratios combining biomarkers predominantly increased in controls (ITGB2, ITGAM, and SCG2) with the main biomarker elevated in patients (NEFL) (Fig. 4A). All three ratios demonstrated excellent discriminatory ability, achieving AUCs of 0.939 (95% CI: 0.899–0.969), 0.946 (95% CI: 0.905–0.976), and 0.960 (95% CI: 0.922–0.985), respectively. Importantly, each ratio significantly outperformed NEFL alone in distinguishing patients from CTRL (all p < 0.001) (Fig. 4B-C). When stratifying for diagnostic group the ratios diagnostic performance remained consistently high (all AUC > 0.900) (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Biomarker performance in dementia differential diagnosis

We next evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of biomarkers with differential distribution between at least two diagnostic groups (i.e., NEFL, GFAP, pTau217, ITGB2, SCG2, and SYT1) for discriminating between dementia syndromes (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 5). DDC was excluded from this analysis given that, after adjustment for dopaminergic treatment in the general linear model, its levels were no longer significantly associated with any diagnostic group. In distinguishing AD from other neurodegenerative disorders, pTau217 demonstrated superior diagnostic performance (AD vs. DLB: AUC 0.912 [95% CI: 0.858–0.956]; AD vs. FTD: AUC 0.988 [95% CI: 0.974–0.998]; AD vs. PSP: AUC 0.985 [95% CI: 0.963–0.998]; AD vs. CBD: AUC 0.986 [95% CI: 0.964–0.998]) across all comparisons against all other markers (all p < 0.001). For differentiating DLB from FTD, NEFL emerged as the single best-performing biomarker (AUC 0.705 [95% CI: 0.609–0.797]). In the differential diagnosis between DLB and PSP, ITGB2 was the only biomarker demonstrating any, albeit modest, discriminatory ability (AUC 0.625 [95% CI: 0.506–0.745]), whereas NEFL proved effective in distinguishing DLB from CBD (AUC 0.681 [95% CI: 0.559-0.800]).Within the FTLD spectrum, NEFL was the only useful biomarker for differentiating FTD from PSP (AUC 0.665 [95% CI: 0.536–0.792]). Similarly, for the PSP vs. CBD distinction, NEFL was the only marker to exhibit any discriminatory ability (AUC 0.656 [95% CI: 0.514–0.802]).

Biomarker distribution according to AD and LBP status

We next evaluated associations between plasma biomarker levels and AD or LBP pathology status, as defined by CSF biomarkers. Four proteins were significantly associated with AD pathology: pTau217 (β = 2.540, p < 0.001), GFAP (β = 0.764, p < 0.001), SYT1 (β = 0.348, p < 0.001), and SDC4 (β=-0.313, p = 0.005). All four proteins correlated with CSF pTau181 levels, while only the first two showed significant correlations with CSF Abeta42 (Supplementary Fig. 7). Three proteins showed significant, albeit weak, negative associations with LBP: ENO2 (β=-0.672, p = 0.003), ITGAM (β=-0.161, p = 0.023), and ITGB2 (β=-0.236, p = 0.004).

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the Olink Target 48 Neurodegeneration panel in plasma samples from a large, deeply phenotyped cohort of patients with AD, DLB, and FTLD spectrum disorders. Our findings demonstrate that this novel multiplex platform quantifies 32 plasma proteins spanning diverse biological pathways implicated in neurodegeneration. Importantly, we identified specific biomarker signatures capable of differentiating among major dementing disorders and validated the association between certain markers and clinical variables (e.g., CDR or MMSE) or underlying neuropathological processes.

From an analytical standpoint, the platform demonstrated variable analytical performance across analytes. While most proteins exhibited high detectability and acceptable intra- and inter-assay variability, nine proteins were excluded due to poor detectability, and three additional quantifiable proteins (i.e., ENO2, GFAP, and BACE1) showed inter-assay CV exceeding 25%, warranting caution in interpreting results involving these analytes. Notably, the strong correlations observed between PEA-based measurements and established immunoassays for pTau217, NEFL, and GFAP support the platform’s capacity to accurately quantify these key biomarkers. However, substantial systematic differences in absolute concentrations across platforms preclude analytical interchangeability, underscore the inappropriateness of directly comparing absolute values across assays, and highlight the need for assay-specific reference ranges and cutoffs.

Consistent with extensive prior literature, plasma pTau217 emerged as the single most powerful biomarker for AD diagnosis, achieving near-perfect discrimination from all other diagnostic groups [3, 4]. These findings align with large-scale studies demonstrating that pTau217 rivals CSF biomarkers in diagnostic accuracy for AD, positioning it as a cornerstone of blood-based diagnostic algorithms [31]. The association between pTau217 levels and disease severity, as measured by cMMSE, further supports its potential utility for disease monitoring, although the cross-sectional design of our study precludes definitive conclusions regarding longitudinal trajectories [32]. Importantly, the strong correlation between plasma pTau217 and CSF pTau181, coupled with the marker’s specific association with AD pathology as defined by CSF biomarkers, reinforces the biological validity of pTau217 as a blood-based surrogate for central nervous system Abeta and tau pathology [33].

Beyond pTau217, we observed elevated GFAP levels in AD compared to all other diagnostic groups, consistent with its established role as a marker of astrocytic activation in response to amyloid pathology [34]. However, the elevation of GFAP across multiple dementia types, albeit to a lesser degree than in AD, highlights its limited specificity for differential diagnosis when used in isolation [5].

A particularly novel finding of our study is the exceptional diagnostic performance of protein ratios combining NEFL with markers of synaptic integrity or immune function. Specifically, the NEFL/ITGB2, NEFL/ITGAM, and NEFL/SCG2 ratios achieved AUC values exceeding 0.90 for discriminating patients with dementia from healthy controls, significantly outperforming NEFL alone. Blood-based markers with such discriminatory ability could prove particularly valuable in two specific contexts where NEFL can be used: in memory clinic settings, where distinguishing neurodegenerative from functional cognitive disorders can be clinically challenging; and in population-based or primary care screening initiatives, where objective, minimally invasive tools are needed to identify individuals warranting further specialist workup. Both ITGB2 and ITGAM are cell-surface proteins widely expressed on immune cells throughout the body, including peripheral leukocytes and microglia, and play critical roles in leukocyte adhesion, migration, and immune response. The observed decrease in plasma levels may therefore reflect systemic immune changes rather than CNS-specific processes, and this possibility should be considered when interpreting their potential role as biomarkers of neurodegeneration. Notably, a prior study reported elevated CSF levels of ITGAM and ITGB2 along the AD continuum [35], suggesting increased expression or shedding of these proteins in the central nervous system microenvironment. The apparent discordance between increased CSF levels reported in the literature and decreased plasma levels observed in our study is intriguing, although the mechanisms underlying this compartment-specific directionality remain unclear and warrant further investigation. The reduction in plasma SCG2 observed in our cohort also merits discussion. SCG2 is a member of the granin family of neuroendocrine secretory proteins expressed in neurons and neuroendocrine cell cytoplasm and plays a role in neuropeptide processing and secretion. A previous study reported reduced CSF SCG2 in DLB compared to controls [36]. However, the broader role of SCG2 as a biomarker across other neurodegenerative diseases and in alternative biological matrices remains unexplored. Collectively, the strong diagnostic performance of NEFL-based ratios incorporating ITGB2, ITGAM, or SCG2 suggests that these composite biomarkers reflect distinct but complementary pathophysiological processes. While NEFL captures axonal injury common to all dementia types [37], plasma ITGB2, ITGAM, and SCG2 appear to decline in neurodegeneration, possibly reflecting synaptic loss, immune dysfunction, or other disease-associated processes. We acknowledge, however, that this statistical gain comes at the cost of reduced biological and clinical interpretability, which remains limited given the incomplete characterization of these markers in the context of neurodegeneration. Accordingly, any potential future application of these composite biomarkers should not be considered in isolation but must be contextualized within the broader clinical picture. Beyond their diagnostic potential, these findings should stimulate further research into the pathophysiological role of ITGB2, ITGAM, and SCG2 in neurodegenerative disorders, including the mechanisms underlying their peripheral decline and their relationship with disease stage and progression. Whether the observed gain in statistical accuracy ultimately justifies the reduced interpretability of these composite biomarkers remains an open question, one that can only be addressed through external validation studies and a deeper biological understanding of the markers involved.

Regarding the differential diagnosis of dementia syndromes, our findings highlight the considerable gap between the diagnostic performance of blood-based biomarkers for AD versus non-AD disorders. While pTau217 provides excellent discrimination between AD and all other diagnostic groups, the ability of available markers to distinguish among non-AD dementias remains limited, with AUC values generally ranging between 0.60 and 0.75, a level that is unlikely to support clinical decision-making in isolation. In this context, NEFL showed modest utility (AUC 0.705) in differentiating DLB from FTD and CBD, consistent with prior reports of elevated NEFL levels in FTLD spectrum disorders exhibiting more aggressive neuroaxonal neurodegeneration [7, 37]. The role of DDC also deserves specific consideration. DDC has been identified in large-scale proteomic studies as a marker of nigrostriatal system impairment. Its CSF levels are elevated in subjects with DLB and parkinsonian syndromes, accurately distinguishing these patients from both healthy controls and those with AD [9, 38]. However, the diagnostic value of plasma DDC has yielded conflicting results, with more recent evidence suggesting that its levels are primarily determined by dopaminergic therapy rather than reflecting a true disease-specific signal [39]. Consistent with this, in our cohort, plasma DDC levels were no longer significantly associated with diagnostic group after adjustment for dopaminergic treatment, precluding its use as a reliable non-invasive differential diagnostic biomarker.

In the present work, we also assessed the association between plasma biomarkers and dementia stage. In our cohort, CDR scores showed significant associations with thirteen proteins independently of diagnostic group, including markers of synaptic integrity (NPTXR, CLSTN3, SCG2, KLK8), myelin-associated proteins (OMG, RTN4R), amyloid pathology (Abeta42, Abeta40), neuroaxonal damage (NEFL), extracellular matrix remodelling (MMP10), metabolism (ENO2), and cell signalling (SDC4, BMP7). The directionality of these associations differed markedly. NEFL levels increased with higher CDR scores, consistent with its role as a marker of ongoing axonal injury and neurodegeneration [40]. ENO2 and SDC4 followed a similar pattern, also showing positive associations with disease severity. Conversely, all other proteins showed inverse associations, declining as disease severity increased, likely paralleling progressive synaptic, white matter, and blood brain barrier damage. Among the identified proteins, OMG is worth mentioning, as it has recently been validated as a peripheral marker of neurodegenerative resilience, consistent with our results [41]. However, several caveats should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not allow assessment of whether these biomarkers track disease progression within individuals over time. Additionally, CDR and cMMSE may not capture disease severity equivalently across different dementia syndromes, potentially introducing heterogeneity in the observed associations. Finally, despite adjustment for diagnostic group, residual confounding by diagnosis cannot be fully excluded. Despite these limitations, our exploratory findings align with the growing need for non-invasive biomarkers that objectively reflect disease stage and clinical severity. The recent advent of disease-modifying therapies for AD has made such objective markers particularly crucial, as they can reduce the interrater variability inherent in clinical assessments alone.

Last, we investigated the association between plasma biomarker levels and underlying neuropathology, as defined by CSF biomarkers. Only a limited subset of plasma proteins showed significant associations with AD pathology or LBP. As expected, strong positive associations were observed between AD pathology and pTau217 and GFAP. Weaker associations were found between AD pathology and SYT1 (positive association), a synaptic vesicle protein involved in neurotransmitter release, and SDC4 (negative association), a proteoglycan implicated in cell-matrix interactions and neuroinflammatory responses. Regarding LBP, weak negative associations were observed with ENO2, a glycolytic enzyme predominantly expressed in neurons, and the above-mentioned integrin subunits ITGAM and ITGB2. These findings underscore the relative specificity of pTau217 and GFAP for AD pathology, while highlighting the current paucity of robust blood-based markers for LBP.

Limitations

This study comes with several limitations. First, the absence of neuropathological confirmation in most cases introduces the possibility of diagnostic misclassification, although the use of stringent clinical criteria and confirmatory CSF biomarkers mitigates this concern. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes assessment of biomarker dynamics over time and limits our ability to evaluate markers as tools for monitoring disease progression. Third, the long recruitment period and the monocentric design introduce potential sources of variability and bias that deserve consideration. While pre-analytical procedures remained consistent throughout the study period, evolving referral patterns over two decades may have affected cohort composition. Specifically, LP was historically requested more often for patients with atypical or less common presentations (e.g., PSP, CBD, FTD), whereas referrals for more prevalent conditions such as AD and DLB increased substantially in recent years. This temporal shift in referral practice, inherent to the naturalistic, single-center design of our study, may have introduced selection bias that is difficult to fully quantify, and should be considered when interpreting the relative representation of diagnostic groups in our cohort. Fourth, the relatively small sample sizes for PSP and CBD should be considered when interpreting comparisons involving these groups, and findings warrant replication in larger, dedicated cohorts. Similarly, analyses of biomarker distribution across disease subtypes within the AD and FTD groups were limited by small subgroup sizes, and the absence of significant differences should therefore be interpreted with caution. Fifth, the NEFL-based ratios identified in this study were derived and tested within the same dataset, and the control group was relatively small compared to the pooled patient cohort, which may affect the precision of ROC metrics. These ratios should therefore be considered exploratory, pending external validation in larger, independent cohorts with more balanced group sizes. Finally, our cohort consisted predominantly of European ancestry individuals and validation in diverse populations is essential to ensure generalizability of findings. This study also has some strengths, including the direct comparison of multiple neurodegenerative disorders representative of a referral center practice, and comprehensive biomarker assessment combining plasma proteomic profiling with established CSF core biomarker measurements.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings support the analytical performance and potential clinical utility of this novel multiplex plasma proteomic panel in a real-world clinical cohort and further highlight the diagnostic power of pTau217 for AD detection. The identification of novel protein ratios that enhance discrimination of dementia from healthy aging represents a potentially relevant finding, though these results remain exploratory and require external validation before any clinical application can be considered. The platform’s ability to capture associations between diverse biomarkers, spanning synaptic integrity, neuroaxonal damage, and immune function, and clinical disease severity, suggests its potential utility not only for diagnosis but also for staging, although longitudinal studies are needed to confirm whether these markers reliably track disease progression at individual level. Future research should focus on longitudinal validation of these biomarkers, exploration of their utility in prodromal disease stages, and development of more specific markers for non-AD pathologies, particularly alpha-synuclein, non-AD tau, and TDP-43 proteinopathies.
