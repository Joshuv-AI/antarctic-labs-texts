Factors influencing decision-making for facility-based childbirth in rural Nepal: a qualitative study
Authors: Shijan Acharya, Anju Gautam, Tulasa Bharati, Joanna Busza, Monika Christofori-Khadka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMaternal mortality remains a major global health challenge, with most preventable deaths occurring in low-income and lower-middle-income countries. Despite progress in reducing maternal mortality, Nepal continues to face challenges in achieving universal facility-based childbirth. Understanding how decisions about place of childbirth are made, particularly in rural settings, is critical to reducing delays in accessing obstetric care.MethodsThis qualitative study was conducted in two rural districts of Nepal. Using purposive sampling, thirty-four semistructured interviews were conducted with women who had given birth within the previous twelve months, along with their husbands and mothers-in-law. In addition, nine key informant interviews were held with healthcare providers and local authorities, and eight focus group discussions were conducted with women, men, health workers and community stakeholders. Data were audio-recorded, transcribed, translated and analysed using a thematic approach combining bottom-up coding and topic guide-based categories.ResultsDecision-making regarding place of childbirth was found to be dynamic and shaped by multiple factors across pregnancy and labour. While women were increasingly involved in decision-making during pregnancy, authority frequently shifted to husbands and mothers-in-law once labour began. Fear of complications, prior obstetric experiences, perceived competence of healthcare providers and community influence strongly affected care-seeking decisions. Despite reported birth preparedness, many women were unable to reach health facilities in a timely manner due to delayed recognition of labour, hesitancy to seek care at night, family influence and structural barriers.ConclusionsDecision-making around facility-based childbirth in rural Nepal is strongly influenced by family and community dynamics, particularly during labour. Interventions should prioritise family-centred antenatal counselling, engagement of husbands and key family members, and strengthening trust in health services. Improving service quality and addressing structural barriers are essential to promote timely utilisation of facility-based childbirth services.Keywords: Community Health, Cross-Sectional Studies, Data Collection, Female, Health Personnel

Introduction

Maternal mortality remains a major public health challenge, with about 700 preventable deaths occurring daily in 2023, 92% of which occurred in low-and lower-middle-income countries, largely concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa (70%) and Southern Asia (17%).1 In Nepal, the maternal mortality ratio (MMR) declined from 239 per 100 000 live births in 2016 to 151 in 2021, showing progress but remaining above the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) target of fewer than 70 by 2030.1 2 Despite efforts to improve maternal, neonatal and child health, delays in accessing obstetric care persist, with the First Delay; time taken to recognise complications and decide to seek care, accounting for 57% of maternal deaths, according to a 2021 verbal autopsy of 611 cases.2 3 Numerous studies have highlighted barriers to maternal health service utilisation in LMICs such as difficult geography, financial constraints, cultural norms and women’s low autonomy.4,7 The complexity of seeking maternal and neonatal care is shaped by multiple factors. Barriers like transportation difficulties, financial limitations, night-time onset of labour and the distance to health facilities intensify these challenges.8,11

Thaddeus and Maine’s three-delay framework underscores that timely recognition and decision-making before and during labour are critical determinants of accessing obstetric care and reducing maternal mortality, alongside delays in reaching and receiving appropriate care.412,19 Factors like educational level, economic status, ethnicity and place of residence play crucial roles in determining whether women choose facility-based childbirth or not.15 16 In Nepal, qualitative studies reveal that sociocultural norms, concerns about healthcare quality and logistical barriers further complicate this decision-making process.3 8 10 In rural Chitwan, evidence suggests sociocultural norms favouring home births are reinforced by concerns about healthcare quality, deterring women from choosing facility-based childbirth, even when birthing centres are nearby.12 Similarly, in Sarlahi District, concerns about the quality of public health facilities and delays in receiving appropriate care deter women from seeking facility-based childbirth services.8 While previous studies have examined the barriers to facility-based childbirth in general and extensively examined structural determinants, the account of the decision-making process during the labour as lived experiences remains underexplored in the context of rural Nepal. Despite known barriers such as geography, finance or sociocultural norms, each woman, even from the same place, experiences them differently.4 9 10

Therefore, this study seeks to understand the decision-making processes by the women and their families prior to, at the onset of labour and during labour, focusing on what or who influences women’s decision on place of childbirth. Additionally, the perceptions of healthcare providers and local authorities as the observers and the influential people of these decisions are less explored in comparison to their perceptions of the barriers in providing services.20 This study also seeks to understand how perceptions of receivers and providers compare to each other. Gaining the insights of the decision-making process can not only inform the refinement of existing maternal health programme strategies but also help in defining the contents of the recently adopted 8 antenatal care (ANC) contacts guideline that presents opportunity for supporting and easing the decision of seeking care for every woman by grounding them in lived experiences of mothers.15 16 21

Methods and analysis

Study design

This is a qualitative study using semistructured interviews (SSI), key informant interviews (KII) and focus group discussions (FGDs). Qualitative research explores the perceptions, beliefs and motivations behind observed behaviour from the perspectives of those experiencing the phenomena of interest.22 Thematic analysis was conducted using both ‘bottom-up’ and ‘top-down’ coding approaches.

Study setting

The study was conducted in Banglachuli RM (Dang) and Tribeni RM (Rukum-West), selected purposively for their rural setting, low maternal health service utilisation and mixed ethnic compositions. Dang, a remote Terai district, has diverse socioeconomic and caste groups, while Rukum-West represents a typical hilly district. Data were collected from Hansipur and Simrutu Health Posts with birthing centres. As shown in table 1, Health Management Information System indicators show facility-based childbirths, ANC visits and SBA coverage in Dang below the national average, whereas Rukum-West is slightly higher but still short of SDG targets.

Study population

SSIs were conducted with women who had given birth at home or in a facility within the last 12 months and their family members (husbands or mothers-in-law), and KIIs with healthcare providers and local authorities. FGDs were conducted with groups of community people, that is, women, men, healthcare providers and other stakeholders such as community leaders, teachers, traditional healers and Female Community Health Volunteers (FCHVs).

Sampling method

Purposive sampling was used to select participants with a range of birth experiences, including those who had experienced complications and referrals. Participants were identified by the local health worker, FCHVs, Health Mothers’ group members who were familiar with women/households who experienced pregnancy outcomes/care engagement. Diversity in ethnicity and economic status, and in the case of key informants, a range of roles and positions of authority was sought. The selection of study cluster/communities for FGDs was based on local diversity, socioeconomic differences and vulnerability, ethnicity representing diverse background and knowledge about decision making and different social dynamics.

Sampling framework

A total of 22 SSIs and KIIs were conducted in Dang, whereas 21 SSIs and KIIs were conducted in Rukum-West. Altogether, eight group discussions were conducted, 4 in each site where 8–12 participants were present in each group. As shown in table 2, a total of 19 women participants were interviewed. Besides women, a total of 13 husbands, 2 mothers-in-law, 5 health workers and 4 local authorities were also interviewed. Since the objectives of the study were made clear and sampling was done among the eligible population who were available at the time of data collection, there were no drop-outs or refusals to participate in the study. Those who were not available on the very day were followed up for 3 days.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using pretested interview guides for FGDs, SSIs and KIIs (see online supplemental file 1), tailored to participant type and focused on decision-making and maternal service use across pregnancy, childbirth and the postnatal period. All tools were translated into Nepali, refined after pretesting in Rolpa District (22 March 2021–23 March 2021), and interviews lasted 30 min for SSIs, 45–60 min for KIIs and 60–90 min for FGDs including community mapping of maternal health services, outlining pregnancy pathways and identifying barriers and facilitators using a ‘Horse & Cart’ metaphor.

Data collection techniques

Data were collected from 26 March to 9 April 2021 through SSIs, KIIs and FGDs conducted at private, convenient locations by trained local research assistants after informed consent. SSIs explored women’s experiences of decision-making and barriers to care, KIIs captured perspectives of healthcare providers and local authorities, and FGDs used participatory tools to identify community-level facilitators and barriers to maternal service use; data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached. All healthcare providers serving the study sites and local authorities concerned about the health services provided during the period of data collection were selected either for KII or for FGD. The SSI with mothers and family members was continued in each site until three consecutive interviews added no new themes and depth to the themes.

Data analysis

Interviews and FGDs were audio-recorded, transcribed and translated from Nepali to English. Thematic analysis combined bottom-up and top-down coding, with codes emerging from the data supplemented by topic guide-based codes. Using MS Excel, three team members grouped codes into categories and sub-codes, developing themes around factors influencing women’s decision-making before and during childbirth. Analysis followed two phases: first, familiarisation, framework development, coding and separate analysis sheets for SSIs, KIIs and FGDs; second, triangulation of data across sources, consistency checks, refinement of codes into themes and rechecking for contradictory evidence. Data saturation was discussed iteratively, and recruitment ended once no new themes emerged.

Patient and public involvement

Members of the public, including community representatives and local stakeholders, were engaged during the research process. Study findings were disseminated at the respective local level through meetings with municipal and ward-level leaders and health section officers. Transcripts were not returned to participants due to logistical constraints and limited literacy levels; however, participants were provided opportunities during and after the interview to clarify or elaborate on their responses.

Results

The results of the study are grouped, starting with a summary of participants’ demographic characteristics and then reflecting different themes as facilitators and barriers in decision-making for facility-based childbirths.

Demographic characteristics

Most women interviewed belonged to Janajati and Brahmin/Chettri ethnic groups, with few from Dalit groups, and slightly more lived in joint families than nuclear ones (Note: Brahmin/Chettri considered as higher class followed by Janajati (indigenous groups) and Dalits considered as lower class in traditional belief system of caste hierarchy). As shown in table 3, most women were aged 20–29, reflecting ongoing early marriage and pregnancy, and over half had two children. Facility-based childbirth experience was slightly higher than home births. Most husbands were migrant workers, often seasonal, returning during pregnancy or postpartum for support. Women and family members were primarily engaged in agriculture, household chores and child-rearing.

Thematic findings

Theme 1: Factors influencing decision-making choice of pregnant women for facility-based childbirth

Previous experiences

Previous positive experience as facilitator for facility-based childbirth: Many women, who already had childbirth at least once in a health facility, had positive birthing experiences, which further reinforced their preference. Nearly all respondents from Rukum-West and over half from Dang preferred health facilities for childbirth, often citing positive past experiences as a key factor in their decision.

We had already planned the to give birth at the health facility, knowing it would be safer since complications could occur at home. (IDI-D-F-W1)

The quality health services and facilities provided at the birthing centre also influenced women’s decision-making to give birth at a health facility, which was also quoted by the healthcare provider that the facilities such as accommodation influenced their positive decision-making.

We arrange lodging and food at the health facility for women and one companion, since some may need to stay two to three days. While some manage on their own, not everyone can afford hotel expenses. (KII-D-HCP1)

They conduct regular monthly check-ups for both mother and baby, and if any problem arises, staff also visit mothers at home. All services provided by this health facility are good. (IDI-R-F-H1)

However, previous positive experience in home birth also influenced the decision to give birth to a subsequent child at home, despite advice from the health workers. The perceived hassle of travelling to a health facility for a 'normal birth’ influenced their decision.

I preferred to to give birth at home rather than go to the doctors. Health workers at the health facility had suggested giving birth there and provided me a contact number in case of labor pain. However, since I had my first child at home, I didn’t feel it was necessary to seek services at the health facility. (IDI-D-H-W2)

Previous negative experiences as a barrier to facility-based childbirth: Similarly, negative past experiences influenced the decision-making in favour of giving birth at home. Some women opted for home births, especially in Dang, due to concerns about body exposure.

I had planned to give birth at home and did not want to go to a health facility. However, everyone insisted that I go, even though I was reluctant. I had my first child at home, and when I was pregnant with my second child, I also intended to give birth at home. It feels shy to go to the doctor and expose oneself, which is why I didn’t want to go. (IDI-D-F-W3)

Negative past experiences included dissatisfaction with the quality of infrastructure and amenities at health posts. The perceived negative experience with facility-based childbirth, such as prolonged labour and discomfort, led some women to prefer home births since they could have the facility of fire for warmth at home which lacked in health facilities.

There are heating facilities at home however, the health facility does not have a heater for pregnant women, so they shiver from the cold. During the birth of my first child, I experienced prolonged labor pain due to the cold at the health facility, and we had to take the baby to a higher-level facility in the city. This is why many women prefer to give birth at home over a health facility. (IDI-R-H-W4)

The behaviour of the healthcare staff experienced by themselves or by the other women in the community significantly influenced women’s decisions and their choice of place of childbirth.

At first, we had doubts about giving birth at a health facility. Moreover, when other women shared their experiences of being scolded during childbith if they cried, it made us feel that home childbirth would be better than facing such treatment at the health facility. (FGD-D-WG1)

Fear of complications

Women who chose to give birth at health facilities often did so due to concerns about potential complications and previous difficult childbirth experiences. Both women and some husbands expressed fears about possible complications and viewed health facilities as safer options for childbirth. Awareness of the ability of health facilities to manage complications strongly influenced their preference for these settings.

We had planned and prepared to give birth at health facility-based. The decision was made jointly with family members because of complications during the first child’s birth, and we sought their advice this time. (IDI-R-F-W5)

I was aware of the danger signs during childbirth; therefore, we had planned to give birth at Hansipur Health Post. (IDI-D-H-W6)

Family’s support for women’s choice of facility-based childbirth

Many respondents, regardless of their actual place of childbirth during the course of pregnancy, stated that they had initially preferred to give birth at a health facility. While planning for birth, decisions about the place of childbirth were often made jointly with their husbands, sometimes with additional input from other family members.

My husband is the only person I discuss my opinions with. I told him and my in-laws that there could be complications at home, so it would be better to give birth at a health facility. (IDI-D-F-W7)

Everyone in the family advised us to go to a health facility for childbirth. They said that times have changed, and it is safer to to give birthat a facility because complications can arise at home. Therefore, we went to the health facility for childbirth. (IDI-R-F-W8)

However, in some cases, decision-making power appeared to be situated more with husbands or other family members, which stood as a barrier to facility-based childbirth.

There was no one at home, only my young son. I spoke with my husband by phone. He said if the baby is born normally at home, it is fine, but if not, go to a health facility. My sister-in-law said the same. She would support me at home, but if there were difficulties, we would go to a facility. (IDI-R-H-W9)

Theme 2: Factors influencing family’s decision making after the onset of labour

Influence of people present at labour: Shifts in decision-making

Women who successfully followed their plan to give birth at a health facility often had supportive husbands and family members who helped ensure timely arrival at the facility. However, decision-making authority sometimes shifts during labour. Despite prior discussions with their husbands or family about the place of childbirth, decisions could change once labour began. Exhausted from labour pains and reliant on family members for care, the individuals present during labour played a crucial role in determining the place of childbirth.

The outcome depended on the caregivers’ knowledge and understanding of emergency obstetric services. For example, some women who initially planned home births ended up giving birth at health facilities, while others who had intended to give birth at a health facility chose home births instead, based on assurances of comfort from their family.

It was nighttime when she (daughter-in-law) started to have labor pain. We told her that we were calling an ambulance to go to the health facility, but she didn’t want to go. But we took her anyway. She didn’t have long labor as it started at 5.00 am and the baby was born at around 7.30 am. (IDI-D-F-MIL1)

My labor started at 4:00 AM, and the baby was born around 7:00 AM. I had slight bleeding and called my sister, who said it is better to have the baby at home, otherwise we should go to a health facility. I also called my sister-in-law, who came and assisted with oil massage. Then the baby was born at home. (IDI-R-H-W10)

I was having labor pain and wanted to give birth at home. My husband didn’t agree and called an ambulance. I thought I could give birth, but I couldn’t, and ended up giving birth on the way. (IDI-R-H-W11)

Labor started at 2:00 AM. I told my mother-in-law and went to the health facility by ambulance. She also contacted a community mobilizer. I didn’t want to go to the facility but went because my family wanted me to. (IDI-D-F-W12)

Influence of the time of onset of labour: Hesitancy to act on promptly

Some women faced challenges in enacting their decision to give birth at a health facility when labour pain began at night. Labour starting at night often led to delays in seeking care until morning, because some women were hesitant to inform their husband and family members until daytime, in order to not disturb their sleep or give them a hard time.

Labor began at 2:00 AM during the rainy season. Transportation was difficult, and all men had migrated. I was alone and gave birth at 5:00 AM at home, calling family only after childbirth. (IDI-R-H-W13)

Alerting family members late further postponed arranging transportation. Mobilising community support and accessing ambulance services at night proved challenging compared with daytime, complicating the process of reaching a health facility.

Her labor began at midnight, and the baby was born at noon the next day. She only informed me in the morning. I tried calling an ambulance, but she had already given birth at home. (IDI-D-H-H2)

It was late by the time we arranged materials and vehicles. My labor started at midnight, and the baby was born in an hour. (IDI-R-H-W14)

Timely recognition of signs of labour

Female respondents, who had their child in a health facility, reported that they were able to promptly recognise the onset of labour and initiate their journey to the facility. In contrast, those families where the woman did not give birth at a health facility or had experienced complications often struggled to identify true labour pains or determine the right time to seek care.

My wife had slight abdominal pain around 9-10 PM. I couldn’t tell if it was labor pain and was unsure whether to take her to the health facility. (IDI-D-H-H3)

Some women were reluctant to go to a health facility in a timely manner due to concerns about waiting for labour to progress.

I felt minimal labor pain similar to my first childbirth. I decided to wait until the pain increased before going to the health facility. (IDI-R-F-W15)

Now it is better to go to a health facility as soon as labor begins. I’ve seen women kept at home for two to three days elsewhere. (IDI-R-F-H4)

Strong cultural beliefs, including reliance on traditional healers and perceptions of supernatural causes of labour complications, delayed decisions to seek professional care and arrange timely transport for childbirth.

I consulted a traditional healer for a suspected bad spirit. The rituals helped, and she improved afterward. (IDI-D-F-H5)

Theme 3: Factors influencing the actualisation of plan for facility-based childbirth

Birth preparedness: Are women and their families truly prepared?

Despite birth preparedness plans, many women still failed to reach health facilities on time due to various reasons. Respondents described their efforts to prepare for safe childbirth beyond decision-making, including securing funds for medical expenses, arranging transportation, providing nutritious food for the mother and preparing clothing for the baby.

My husband was working in India during my childbirth. He sent money to use if needed. (IDI-D-F-W16)

I packed clothes for myself and the baby. My mother-in-law borrowed 10,000 NPR, and my husband contributed around 10–11,000 NPR. We went to the facility with this money. (IDI-D-F-W17)

We had to carry women on stretchers. We prepared clothes and money. A stretcher was provided by the health post, and my brothers sent funds from India. We kept everything ready a month and a half before childbirth. (IDI-R-H-H6)

Vehicles do come but not consistently. We had to carry her. Friends and brothers helped, and we consulted the FCHV. We had prepared money, clothes, and foods like ghee, honey, and chickens. (IDI-R-H-H8)

Family members thought they were prepared for what was needed, like (stretchers and resources like money and people). Despite birth preparations, many women still struggled to reach health facilities in a timely manner due to a lack of understanding of onset of labour pain, prompt action, complemented by structural determinants.

While looking for ANC cards and calling for an ambulance, the baby was born. Labor started at 8:00 PM, and childbirth occurred within half an hour. (IDI-D-H-W18)

My wife had slight pain around 9–10 PM. With heavy rain, difficult roads, and no one to leave the small child, I was unsure whether to go to HF or not. (IDI-D-H-H9)

Social cohesion and community support

Decision-making is supported by community members and support groups. Besides, financial assistance and help with transportation, the advice from community members plays a role in deciding the place of childbirth in both positive and negative ways.

Neighbors advised us to go to the health facility. My wife was aware of the process. We consulted the FCHV and followed her suggestion to visit the facility. (IDI-D-F-H10)

We seek support from our community, including financial and human resources to carry women on stretchers and communicate with health workers during emergencies. (FGD-D-MG1)

Despite prior birth preparedness, one woman’s plan for facility delivery was overridden during night-time labour due to lack of community support while her husband was abroad.

I was sleeping when labor started. My husband in India advised calling neighbors. Labor started at 9:00 PM, and the baby was born at 1:00 AM at home. Neighbors helped with hot oil massages and hot water with sugarcane. We waited three hours before considering the facility. (IDI-R-H-W19)

Theme 4: Perceptions of healthcare providers and local authorities

The observations from healthcare providers and local authority do not stand far apart from the voices of women and their families. Factors such as financial constraints, geographical difficulties, faith in traditional healers, not recognising signs of onset of labour timely manner and time spent in discussion among family members were also mentioned by healthcare providers and local authorities.

When we asked women about the reasons for home delivery, they commonly mentioned delays in calling neighbours for help, the unavailability of a stretcher, labour starting before the expected due date, and difficulty recognising labour. Some women believed that hard physical work makes childbirth easier, while others felt the labour pain was not severe or that they were about to go to the health facility anyway. In some cases, families felt it was better to deliver at home rather than pay for ambulance services.(KII–R–HCP)

If any complication happens, then it should be discussed with husband, mother-in-law, older sister-in-law and other family for decision-making. (KII–Dang–Healthcare Provider)

In our community, people who are financially well-off go for HF service utilization, others prefer treatment from traditional healers like “dhami-jhankri”. Nowadays, people go to HF service utilization more, but still there are people who utilize service from traditional healers. (FGD–D–HCP)

Broader social factors were also considered as barriers such as literacy level, awareness among marginalised groups, agency of women in decision making and even higher alcohol consumption among men in their community. The urgency to include husbands and other family members in the target audience of the awareness programme is also recognised by the local authorities.

A first step would be to stop drinking and substance abuse by men”, men should join women’s groups and take part in society programmes to increase awareness. They should visit health facilities to understand its importance. (KII–D–LA)

It is easy to counsel and advice the educated family and those who are aware about the importance of utilization of healthcare services, but for those who don’t understand, it still exists as a problem. (KII–D–HCP)

Among those women who hesitate to deliver at HF or prefer home delivery are mostly women from the Dalit community. They have low source of income, and the family members have poor awareness, and they care less for decision making in respect to a facility-based delivery. (FGD–D–HCP)

Till now, all the awareness activities we have conducted were targeted to women, girls in the schools but not to the men and boys. What I think is women can’t make her own decision unless she is financially independent. The source of income in family are usually men, that is why they become the decision-maker. Therefore, we should raise awareness to change our strategy to take both men and women together. (KII–R–LA)

Discussions

This study explored women’s decision-making on place of childbirth before and during labour. Decisions were dynamic and shaped by multiple factors contributing to the first delay. Prior to labour, choices were influenced by past experiences, awareness of complications and family support. After labour began, decisions were largely guided by family attitudes, timing and recognition of labour, and cultural beliefs, consistent with findings from Nepal and other countries such as Ghana, Indonesia and Ethiopia.12,17

Women with normal childbirth experience at home often preferred the same, while negative encounters with poor provider attitudes, inadequate skills, weak infrastructure and low service quality at facilities reinforced this choice, as shown in meta-syntheses from Africa, Asia and South America.23 Improving the quality of maternal health services has been brought into focus globally to achieve an increase in utilisation and receiving of appropriate care by mothers,24 which has also been the programme priority in Nepal with multiple activities such as onsite clinical coaching for healthcare providers, expansion of service sites, monitoring service standards and improving service readiness. The positive outcomes are also reflected in the current trend of facility-based births which has increased to 77.9% nationally in 2024 although regional disparity is still prevalent. Accountability and fostering trust between healthcare providers and the community could promote the general preference for facility-based birth from the early stage of decision making. However, based on the findings of this study, low-cost interventions by local governments in improving the amenities of birthing centres to ensure comfort for mothers could be a starting point.

Global focus on improving maternal health services highlights the need for quality and accountability.24,26 In Nepal, alongside national programmes like the Minimum Service Standards Package, localised efforts to strengthen provider competency and trust with communities could encourage early preference for facility-based childbirth.27 Women with past difficult births, especially those ending positively in health facilities, were more likely to choose facility-based childbirth.

Although perceived urgency and fear of complications also encouraged facility use, many who preferred home birth adopted a ‘wait and watch’ approach, often delaying timely care-seeking.18 Educating women and families about complications and the need for early facility care is crucial. This study found that while women and husbands were key decision-makers before labour, once labour began, families, especially female relatives, often dominated decisions, sometimes overriding women’s initial choices and contributing to delays14 28; echoing patterns seen in Tanzania and Ghana. Household dynamics and circumstances varied, underscoring the need for family-centred health programmes that engage husbands and relatives from the antenatal period.13 29

Maternal health policies and programmes in Nepal should expand the focus beyond cash incentives and service expansion to focus on fostering support for mothers in effective birth preparation and actualisation of care seeking decisions. The current changes in the required number of ANC contacts from 4 to 8 and reformation of national ANC visit targets present a unique opportunity to introduce more family-centric counselling which, if not well acted on, could only mean more frequent fetal growth monitoring as found in a recent study in Kavre, Nepal.21 30 31 This has also been contextualised in the data from healthcare providers and local authorities. However, further study is needed to develop context-appropriate guidance for family-centric approach in ANC counselling and how it can be integrated into current ANC service practices.3 32

Structural barriers, including male out-migration, distance, unreliable transport, poor communication and difficult geography, continue to hinder timely facility-based childbirth, despite women’s intentions, consistent with findings from Nepal and other LMICs.79 10 33,35

Addressing barriers to timely decision-making is effective only when structural challenges are also tackled. While requiring government commitment and resources, antenatal education should emphasise locally adapted birth and complication readiness plans.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This qualitative study brings forward the experiences of women and their family members who gave birth at homes as well as health facilities and how they arrived at their decisions. It also incorporates viewpoints from healthcare providers and local authorities regarding care seeking decisions, allowing data triangulation and minimising recall bias by including only women who have given birth within the past 12 months and conducting interviews in the original language. Limitations include potential sampling and geographical bias from purposive selection in a small area, limited novel insights from healthcare providers and local authorities, and findings primarily reflecting women’s and husbands’ perspectives. The study was conducted during the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic and the questionnaire was adjusted to probe its influence on their decisions. Only one woman expressed the difficulty with transport to reach the health facility because of COVID-19 restrictions. This could be owing to the efforts in continuing maternal health services, and the women interviewed had given birth in the post-lockdown period.

Conclusions

This study highlights that decisions about place of childbirth are dynamic, shaped by factors such as past experiences, fear of complications and family support, while distrust in services, delayed recognition of labour and hesitancy at night hinder timely care. Women’s involvement in decision-making during pregnancy often shifts to husbands and mothers-in-law once labour begins, emphasising the pivotal role of family members. Family-centric antenatal education, male engagement in community programmes and improved spousal communication are essential to reduce delays and promote facility-based childbirth, which needs to be solidified within ANC guidelines. Strengthening trust in healthcare providers, enhancing service quality and addressing structural barriers such as transport and communication are critical for ensuring timely facility-based births. Further research is needed to identify context-specific strategies to engage family members effectively, considering factors like migration and household type.
