Clinical Features, Mechanisms, and Management of Anxiety in Youth with Tic Disorders: A Narrative Review
Authors: Si Zhang, Fei Han, Jie Chen, Xiaohua Shen, Yan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTourette syndrome (TS) and related tic disorders (TD) are neurodevelopmental conditions primarily characterized by motor and vocal tics. However, coexistent anxiety has emerged as a pivotal yet under-recognized clinical determinant, at times overshadowing core tic symptomatology. This review delineates distinct anxiety phenotypes in youth with TD that differ from primary anxiety disorders. These specific presentations exert a profound impact on quality of life, adult outcomes, and socio-emotional functioning. By synthesizing recent literature, we highlight shared neurobiological substrates under this comorbidity, specifically focusing on aberrant connectivity within cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuits, dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and a shared genetic risk basis. Anxiety is not only a secondary symptom, but a component intertwined with the TD neurodevelopmental spectrum. The review advocate for integrated diagnosis and specific therapies to address the interplay between tics and anxiety, providing a clear framework to improve long-term psychosocial outcomes.Keywords: Tourette syndrome, anxiety disorders, comorbidities, quality of life, mechanisms, treatment

Introduction

Tic disorders (TD) are childhood-onset conditions characterized by repetitive, stereotyped, non-rhythmic motor and/or vocal tics.1 Common simple motor tics include eye blinking, facial grimacing, and shoulder shrugging, while simple vocal tics often present as throat clearing, sniffing, or grunting. Complex tics involve more coordinated movements or vocalizations, such as coprolalia (the involuntary utterance of obscene or taboo words), which is often noted for its socially stigmatizing nature. The primary features of the disorder were first detailed in 1885 by the French neurologist Georges Gilles de la Tourette. This specific constellation of symptoms was subsequently named Tourette syndrome. As research into tic phenomena advanced, these varied presentations were classified under the umbrella term of tic disorders. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5),1 classifies TD as a neurodevelopmental disorder. Based on symptom duration and presentation, TD is further divided into three primary types: Tourette syndrome (TS), chronic motor or vocal tic disorder (CTD), and provisional tic disorder (PTD). While TD was classified under behavioral and emotional disorders in the ICD-10, it has been reclassified as a neurodevelopmental disorder in ICD-11.2 This change reflects the contemporary understanding of the comorbid relationship between TD and other neurodevelopmental disorders, such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). However, some scholars have argued that this reclassification in ICD-11 may overlook the psychiatric and emotional aspects inherent to TD.3

Viewing TD as an isolated motor control issue is an oversimplification. The disorder is often deeply intertwined with a complex network of neuropsychiatric comorbidities. Historically, ADHD and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) have been recognized as core features within this comorbidity network.4–6 However, while these externalizing behaviors frequently prompt immediate clinical attention, coexistent anxiety represents a distinct, internalizing burden that often escapes early detection despite being a critical driver of functional impairment. Comorbid anxiety disorders in patients with TS not only significantly impairs quality of life (QoL) and increases subjective distress, but the associated emotional problems may also elevate suicide risk.7,8 Furthermore, anxiety disorders comorbidity in childhood is a strong predictor of a persistent TD diagnosis in adulthood.9 Severe tic symptoms may induce environmental stress, including family tension and pressure from school or the community. Crucially, the magnitude of this stress is culturally modulated; in a global context, diverse societal norms regarding behavioral conformity can heighten stigma, thereby acting as a driver of anxiety. The anxiety then feeds back to worsen tics, establishing a vicious cycle (Figure 1). Moreover, the high co-occurrence with psychiatric and behavioral conditions complicate treatment of TD, as patients must manage multiple symptoms simultaneously.10 This review summarizes the epidemiology, clinical features, underlying mechanisms, and therapeutic strategies for co-occurring anxiety in youth with TD. We aim to enhance clinical awareness of this comorbidity to promote early identification and intervention, thereby improving outcomes for patients with TD.

Search Strategy

We searched the PubMed database from its inception to August 31, 2025, using a strategy that combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords to ensure comprehensiveness and specificity. The search strategy combined concept groups for Tic Disorders (eg, “Tourette Syndrome” [MeSH], “Tics” [Keyword]) and Anxiety (eg, “Anxiety Disorders” [MeSH], “Anxiety”, “ Comorbidity” [Keyword]) with a third group of secondary keywords related to specific research domains (eg, “Epidemiology”, “Prevalence”, “Pathophysiology”, “Neurobiology”, “HPA axis”, “Treatment”, “CBT”). Synonymous terms within each concept group were combined using the “OR” operator. These main concept groups were then intersected using the “AND” operator to ensure specificity.

Epidemiology

Cross-sectional data indicate that at least 20% of children experience tics, and longitudinal data suggest tics may affect the vast majority of children at some point.11 Among children with TS, 85.7% are estimated to have at least one lifetime psychiatric comorbidity and 57.7% to have two or more.12 The prevalence of anxiety in TS is substantially higher than in the general population.13,14 The prevalence of comorbid anxiety symptoms in children with TS ranges from 31.4% to 72.7%,14–18 and is even higher in adults, at 44.6% to 80%.12,19,20 Separately, the diagnostic rate of formal anxiety disorders in children with TS is approximately 53.5%.21 Coexistent anxiety is significantly more prevalent in females with TS than in males.12,22 The high-risk period for anxiety disorders onset begins at age 4 and continues through adolescence, though onset tends to be later for females with TS than for males.12 Prevalence increases with age during this period, peaking at 14–17 years.23 The developmental trajectories of tics and anxiety often diverge. Typically, tic severity peaks between ages 10 and 12 and wanes after adolescence. While many adult patients experience a significant reduction in tics, their anxiety symptoms often persist or even worsen.12,16 Throughout the course of TD, co-occurring anxiety symptoms may thus be more prevalent than fully diagnosed anxiety disorders.

Profiling Anxiety in Tic Disorders

Anxiety symptoms in patients with TD do not always meet formal diagnostic criteria, and their clinical presentation appears to differ from that in the general population. A study by Pakpicha et al found that children with TD had significantly higher scores than controls for both state and trait anxiety.24 This suggests patients experience a dual burden, exhibiting both an underlying anxious disposition and a heightened susceptibility to immediate distress. The authors also found that the TD group scored significantly higher across all five anxiety subtypes on the Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders (SCARED) scale.24 Notably, Jennifer et al found that overall anxiety severity in the TD group was comparable to that of a clinical sample seeking anxiety treatment.25 This TD group also reported significantly higher separation anxiety and somatic symptoms than controls.25 In typical anxiety development, separation anxiety tends to decrease with age while social anxiety increases. In the TD population, however, the severity of both conditions did not differ significantly between children and adolescents.25 This suggests that the anxiety profile in TD differs from that of typical anxiety disorders, implying that interventions are not a simple stacking of standards for each condition. Children with TD may be hypersensitive to sensory stimuli and prone to associating bodily perceptions with negative emotions. This tendency may cause tics to reinforce the negative experience of anxiety and depression.30 Thus, rather than existing in parallel, anxiety and tics are deeply intertwined. The relationship between tic and anxiety severity remains contested. Although a positive correlation is generally assumed, some studies report no such association.24 This discrepancy may be reconciled, however, as the correlation appears to be most evident in patients with severe TD.31

Anxiety symptoms in TD are influenced by multiple factors. Externally, rejecting and controlling parental styles are associated with higher anxiety levels. Internally, comorbidities such as ADHD and obsessive-compulsive symptoms (OCS) may have a more significant impact on anxiety than tics per se.27 This coexistent anxiety may also increase the risk of depression in TS.17 The external environment can play a key mediating role in this anxiety-tic relationship. For instance, anxiety may exacerbate tics by eliciting negative environmental responses,28 while the social embarrassment from tics—coupled with the effort of tic suppression—can in turn lead to stress and mental fatigue.29

The Multifaceted Impact of Anxiety

Quality of Life

Comorbid anxiety impacts not only tic symptoms but is also associated with other negative outcomes, including poorer adult prognosis, sleep disturbances, and suicidal behaviors. Its effect on QoL is particularly illustrative. Adolescents with TD report significantly lower QoL scores than healthy peers, particularly in psychosocial and physical domains.26 Interestingly, the study found that this poor psychosocial QoL was not significantly associated with tic severity; rather, the domain was linked to the comorbidities themselves, such as anxiety, ADHD, depressive, and OCS.26 Previous research also indicates that anxiety and affective symptoms moderate the relationship between tic severity and functional impairment. Specifically, this association is stronger in patients with higher levels of anxiety or depression.32 Although tics are the defining feature of TD, it is often the comorbid burden that is more detrimental to QoL.33

Adult Outcomes

The factors impacting QoL in TD appear to shift with age. While children tend to attribute poor QoL more to tic and ADHD symptoms, adults associate it more strongly with anxiety or depression. For late adolescents and early adults with TD, higher state and trait anxiety levels strongly predict poorer QoL, with scores reduced across all four domains of the Gilles de la Tourette Syndrome-QoL-Children and Adolescents scale.34 As patients age, intensifying tic-related stigma and isolation accompany the emergence of emotional disorders as a key determinant of functional impairment.16 Although tic symptoms and comorbid ADHD often attenuate in adulthood, the prevalence of mood disorders and substance abuse significantly increases.35,36

Suicidality

Comorbidities in adolescents with TD not only impact QoL but may also mediate suicidal ideation and behaviors (SIB). Cross-sectional data indicate that 9.7% of adolescents with CTD report SIB.37 While tic severity and tic-related functional impairment were associated with SIB, this association was notably fully mediated by anxiety and depressive symptoms.37 Another study further confirmed this indirect pathway, identifying anxiety as the primary mediator, whereby greater tic severity predicted higher levels of anxiety, which subsequently predicted more severe suicidal ideation.38 The risk of suicidality thus appears to stem from psychiatric comorbidities—particularly anxiety and depression—rather than from tics themselves. For these youth, focusing on tic suppression alone is likely insufficient. The therapeutic focus must therefore be placed on managing and alleviating their underlying anxiety and depressive symptoms.

Sleep Disturbances

Sleep problems are commonly reported in children with TD, including insomnia, the persistence of tics during sleep, and parasomnias (eg, nightmares, sleepwalking, grinding teeth, or sleep talking).39 These problems are associated with multiple factors, such as tic severity, medication use, and comorbidities, with comorbid anxiety being a significant risk factor.40,41 Patients with CTD and comorbid anxiety report more sleep-related problems than those without this comorbidity.42 Furthermore, higher anxiety scores in TS/CTD patients are associated with abnormal movements at sleep onset or during sleep, and these disturbances correlate significantly with generalized, separation, and social anxiety symptoms.43 This link was confirmed in a large-scale population study, which identified anxiety disorders as a significant correlate of sleep disturbances in newly diagnosed TS patients.41 The “disorder of arousal” hypothesis posits that TD may involve intrinsic deficits in arousal regulation and motor circuit inhibition.44,45 These deficits may act synergistically to create a state of hyperarousal, thereby increasing nocturnal sleep disturbances. Therefore, sleep problems, anxiety, and tic symptoms may all be interrelated clinical manifestations of this single underlying dysregulation of arousal.

Underlying Mechanisms of TD and Anxiety Comorbidity

Neural Structure and Function

The neural basis of TD is commonly understood via the Cortico-Striatal-Thalamo-Cortical (CSTC) circuit model. This model comprises parallel circuits processing motor, cognitive, and emotional functions; however, these circuits are not entirely independent.46 The wide symptom spectrum of TD, from motor tics to psychiatric comorbidities, is thought to arise from dysfunction within and between these circuits.47 One such hypothesized dysfunction is a functional connectivity breakdown within the fronto-parietal network.48 This breakdown is believed to cause deficient frontal inhibitory control, which then impacts the limbic, sensory, and executive regions in CSTC circuitry, leading to tic expression and associated comorbidities. Neuroimaging studies have confirmed abnormalities in limbic system in TS patients, including the orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala.49–51 However, it is crucial to recognize that this CSTC-centric view represents a dominant but potentially simplified heuristic. Alternative interpretations suggest that some observed structural alterations—particularly in cortical regions—might not be primary etiological deficits, but rather secondary neuroplastic adaptations resulting from chronic tic suppression or the sustained physiological stress.

Recent research has further revealed pathway abnormalities specifically linked to anxiety. In TS patients, anxiety scores correlate positively with functional connectivity strength between the left insular cortex and the subthalamic nucleus (STN), suggesting this hyperconnected pathway may drive abnormal emotional processing.52 This view is supported by clinical observations, as OCD patients treated with STN deep brain stimulation (DBS) report that the anxiety triggering their obsessive thoughts is relieved first.53 The insular cortex plays a central role in interoception—the generation of bodily feelings that underpin emotional experience. This function is complemented by its role in sensory processing and motor inhibition; fMRI studies have shown that patients with poorer blink-suppression ability also exhibit weaker activity in the insula.54 The insula is therefore considered a key neural nexus linking premonitory sensory urges, emotional states, and the subsequent motor expression of tics.

Premonitory Urges and Sensory Dysregulation

Emotion can be understood as the brain’s perception and interpretation of changes in the internal bodily state.55 The strong, uncomfortable premonitory urges frequently experienced by TD patients, acting as intense interoceptive signals, may be interpreted by the brain as a negative emotional experience, such as anxiety. This interoceptively-triggered anxiety may then influence motor circuits via the limbic system, exacerbating tics and forming a sensory-emotional-motor vicious cycle.56 This process highlights the core role of emotion dysregulation in TD. Emotion dysregulation correlates with greater tic severity and comorbid anxiety/depression symptoms and serves as a key mechanism for stress-induced tic exacerbation.57 This hypersensitivity to internal signals is often part of a broader sensory dysregulation common in children with TD, such as tactile, auditory, or thermal hypersensitivity. Importantly, the severity of this dysregulation correlates positively with the number and severity of comorbidities, including anxiety, OCD, and ADHD.58,59 It should be noted, however, that these correlations are predominantly derived from clinic-referred samples, where sensory phenotypes are often more severe than in community populations. In a word, patients with TD may exist in a state of hypersensitivity to both internal stimuli and external sensory input. This state strengthens the link between perception and motor action, so patients are more likely to react with motor responses that are out of proportion to the triggering stimulus.60

The Neuroendocrine Network

Substantial evidence indicates that emotional stress is a key trigger for tics exacerbation.61–63 Stress-induced hyperactivation of the HPA axis is a critical posited mechanism. Compared to healthy controls, TD patients exhibit exaggerated Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and cortisol responses to stress, as well as elevated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) levels of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH).64 Similarly, HPA axis dysfunction, often manifesting as abnormal cortisol secretion, is prevalent in anxiety disorders.65 Thus, in the presence of persistent stress, a dysregulated HPA axis may represent a common pathophysiological basis for this comorbidity. However, findings regarding basal HPA activity in TD have been inconsistent, with some studies showing no significant deviations from controls. This variability suggests that HPA axis dysregulation may not be a universal trait but rather a feature of a specific stress-sensitive phenotype.

Downstream of the HPA axis, the neurosteroid allopregnanolone has a complex dual role. On the one hand, it possesses anti-anxiety potential via positive modulation of GABA_A receptors; on the other hand, in the TD context, its upregulation in cortical regions may paradoxically promote motor circuit hyperexcitability and exacerbate tics.66,67 Animal studies confirm that inhibiting allopregnanolone synthesis can attenuate stress-induced tic exacerbation.68 Over the long-term course of this comorbidity, anxiety itself may act as a stressor, promoting TD progression via these neuroendocrine mechanisms.

Classical neurobiological models posit that striatal dopaminergic hyperactivity is core to tics, whereas the serotonergic and noradrenergic systems primarily mediate anxiety and emotion regulation.69 These systems are not isolated; several mechanisms facilitate their interaction. The HPA axis, for instance, provides a key link by modulating the release and receptor sensitivity of these monoamines.70,71 Neurosteroids also modulate dopamine, thereby influencing both emotion and motor control.72 Monoaminergic dysregulation, driven by these upstream neuroendocrine factors, may therefore be a key downstream mechanism in the TD-anxiety interaction.73

Genetics

The development of TD and its comorbidities is considered polygenic. Current research indicates that TS shares a genetic risk basis with ADHD and OCD, involving variants in biological pathways such as the dopaminergic, serotonergic, catecholaminergic, and immune systems.74 Early candidate-gene studies suggested an association between the HTR2A rs6313 variant and TD comorbid with anxiety or OCD, though this evidence requires further strengthening.75 At the genome-wide level, Phenome-Wide Association Studies using large-scale biobanks have further confirmed a shared polygenic architecture between TD and anxiety. For example, one study calculating TD polygenic risk scores for over 330,000 individuals found that a higher genetic risk for TS was strongly associated with clinical diagnoses of anxiety disorders, neuroticism scores, and various anxiety-related traits such as worry/anxious feelings.76 Notably, this association may be mediated by comorbid ADHD and OCD.12 The complex underlying genetic interplay remains a critical direction for future research. Treatment of Anxiety

Non-Pharmacological Treatment

Non-pharmacological treatments for TD include a range of approaches, such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), Habit Reversal Training, Exposure and Response Prevention, relaxation training, social support and education, lifestyle adjustments, DBS, and Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation.10 Group Comprehensive Behavioral Intervention and Group Educational Interventions have been shown to reduce anxiety symptoms in children with CTD/TS.77 Comorbid anxiety disorders in TD predicts a poorer response to behavioral therapy.78 Therefore, clinically anxiety disorders and symptoms should be addressed before initiating TD-specific behavioral interventions. For some patients, a brief, anxiety-focused intervention prior to CBT may be sufficient to improve engagement with the primary therapy.79

Psychoeducation and social support interventions must also address accommodation and systemic avoidance. Parental hypervigilance or unwitting accommodation of avoidance behaviors may impede the child’s development of coping skills for tic management and anxiety regulation. Similarly, well-intentioned but unhelpful accommodations (such as allowing the child to avoid class to play) can also reinforce this anxiety-related avoidance.79 It is therefore critical to extend psychoeducation and consultation to key members of the ch ild’s social support network (eg, family, teachers, peers).79

DBS is a promising treatment for refractory tic disorders. Evidence indicates that DBS is effective for both core tic symptoms and comorbid anxiety, with a meta-analysis of 18 studies (n=131) confirming a significant reduction in anxiety symptoms in TS patients.80 Research targeting specific brain regions offers deeper insights into how DBS modulates anxiety. One prospective study of DBS targeting the ventral anterior and ventrolateral motor nuclei of the thalamus found the therapy significantly reduced trait anxiety without adversely affecting personality.81 The same study identified preoperative anxiety levels as a significant predictor of motor tic improvement, with lower baseline anxiety predicting better outcomes.81 For patients with severe, refractory psychiatric comorbidities, DBS monotherapy may be insufficient. A combined approach of DBS and anterior capsulotomy has shown greater efficacy, with anxiety improvement rates of 78.4%–87.9%.82 However, these results are limited by small sample sizes and potentially insufficient follow-up. Optimal target selection and ethical considerations also remain key challenges. Therefore, caution is warranted in the clinical application of DBS for this comorbidity.

Pharmacological Treatment

The management of TD with psychiatric comorbidities must be individualized. The decision of whether to prioritize treatment for tics or for the anxiety is debated. Some researchers suggest prioritizing the condition with the greatest impact on QoL, while others advocate for treating anxiety first, as its presence complicates the situation of tics. Despite this debate, some medications show potential dual efficacy for both tics and anxiety. As an atypical antipsychotic and a first-line agent for TD, Aripiprazole showed a trend toward improving anxiety symptoms in one adult study.83 Specifically, Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors are commonly used for comorbid anxiety, obsessive-compulsive, and depressive symptoms.84 An ongoing randomized controlled trial is comparing the efficacy of sertraline versus placebo for anxiety in children and adolescents with neurodevelopmental disorders, including TD, ADHD, and ASD (ID: NCT06081348). Beyond specific medications, nutritional supplements have also been investigated. A pilot study in children with TD and comorbid anxiety disorders found that L-theanine and Vitamin B6 supplementation was effective in reducing tic symptoms. While it also showed a positive trend for anxiety improvement, the between-group difference in final anxiety scores did not reach statistical significance.85 These mixed and preliminary findings underscore that there is currently no standardized pharmacological protocol for treating anxiety in TD.

Conclusions and Future Outlook

The context of TD—characterized by interoceptive hypersensitivity and persistent external stress—coupled with a shared genetic and structural basis, likely predisposes individuals to comorbid anxiety. Emotional features such as anxiety and fear, acting as stressors, then exacerbate tics, creating a vicious cycle and complicating the pathophysiology. Direct evidence for the TD-anxiety link is limited, but indirect pathways warrant investigation. For instance, Munc18-1, a key regulator of SNARE complex assembly, whose reduced expression or functional mutations have been shown to cause synaptic transmission deficits and induce anxiety-like behaviors.86 Given that abnormal neurotransmitter levels and synaptic deficits are common in neuropsychiatric disorders including TD, such pathways may represent a shared etiology. Similarly, stress and overexpression of the small GTPase Rap1 inhibit cortical excitatory signaling,87 which increases postsynaptic AMPA–type glutamate receptor endocytosis and produces anxiety-like behaviors.88 In the context of TD, reduced synaptic efficacy in the prefrontal cortex may result in insufficient inhibitory output, leading to striatal disinhibition and triggering tics,89 although these molecular regulatory mechanisms await further validation.

Comorbid anxiety in TD lacks a targeted, highly effective therapy, requiring individualized treatment approaches. Adolescents are in a phase of rapid physical and psychological development, often with intense emotional fluctuations. Clinically, practitioners must conduct comprehensive comorbidity screening for TD patients and formulate personalized, multimodal treatment plans encompassing education, behavioral interventions, and pharmacotherapy.90,91 If anxiety impedes engagement with tic-focused therapy, it may need to be addressed first. Conversely, when tic symptoms are complex and severe (particularly with coprolalia) and represent the primary impairment, stabilizing tics may be the priority. When using antipsychotics in a combined treatment, the potential adverse effects on the developing adolescent must be carefully considered. The key for future mechanistic research is to identify the critical junction points of this comorbidity, to break the vicious cycle of mutual reinforcement between anxiety and tics.
