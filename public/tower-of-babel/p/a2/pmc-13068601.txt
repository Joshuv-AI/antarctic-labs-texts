The distal vastus medialis artery – a novel recipient vessel in free flap reconstruction of the knee
Authors: Yewon D. Kim, Varun Harish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Free flap reconstruction around the knee is challenging given a paucity of suitable recipient vessels. Traditional recipient vessels frequently require specific positioning other than supine (limiting flap selection), have variable anatomy, and are located remotely and necessitate interposition grafts. We present a case series demonstrating successful use of the distal segmental artery to the vastus medialis muscle as a recipient for free flap reconstruction with a CT angiography study to further characterize this vessel. Retrospective analysis of CT angiograms of lower limbs used in reconstructive planning between 2015 and 2021 at a single major plastic surgery department were reviewed by two independent clinicians. The distal segmental vessels supplying the vastus medialis muscle were analyzed. In 64 thighs, a consistent radiologically identifiable distal segmental vessel supplying the vastus medialis was seen in 63 (98.4%) cases. In 33 (51.6%) cases, this branch arose from the descending genicular artery while in 30 (47.6%) cases, it arose from the superficial femoral artery. Therefore, the distal segmental vastus medialis vessel is a consistently identifiable recipient option in free flap reconstruction around the knee. It can be raised in the supine patient with a limited necessary dissection and no functional morbidity from sacrifice. No interposition grafts are required.

Introduction

Free flap reconstruction around the knee is challenging due to the lack of suitable regional recipient vessels. Traditional options include branches of the superficial femoral artery (SFA), end-to-side to the SFA itself, and popliteal artery (PA) branches such as the superomedial genicular artery (SMGA), descending genicular artery (DGA) or sural arteries.1, 2, 3 However, these are limited by difficult non-supine positioning, deeper and extensive dissection, and variability in vascular anatomy. Occasionally, the lack of local recipient options necessitates the need for long flap pedicles and adjunct techniques such as vein grafts and arteriovenous (AV) loops. Perforator recipients are described but they are less reliable in location and of smaller calibre.3 A consistent recipient option that can be raised in supine position has yet to be described.

The vastus medialis (VM) is a quadricep muscle found medial to the rectus femoris traditionally described by Mathes and Nahai to be supplied by a major proximal dominant pedicle with multiple distal minor pedicles from the superficial femoral artery (SFA).4 However vascular anatomy studies are limited, based purely on cadaveric dissection and report contradictory information resulting in a generally poor understanding of this region. In one study, it was reported that up to 7 major arteries supplied the muscle with multiple origins from the profunda femoris, lateral circumflex femoral artery, SFA and DGA.5 The potential of the distal segmental vessel as a potential free flap recipient remains unexplored.

Materials and methods

A retrospective review was performed of CT angiograms of lower limbs used for preoperative reconstructive planning between 2015 and 2021 at a single tertiary plastic surgery department. Retrospective review of electronic medical records was used to collect demographic information. Patients above the age of 16 with complete CT angiographic imaging of the lower limbs from hip to ankle were included in this study. Patients were excluded if there was significant vascular pathology (such as peripheral arterial disease or trauma) or artefact inhibiting the analysis of the images. All studies were analyzed by two independent clinicians. Standard axial, sagittal and coronal planes were viewed. A distal segmental vessel entering the vastus medialis was identified and observed. The distance of the origin vessel of this branch was measured relative to the tibiofemoral joint (TFJ) line as a surgically applicable surface landmark. Vessel length was measured in centimeters (cm) while caliber was reported in millimeters (mm). Information on any skin perforators and perforator characteristics were also observed. All measurements and observations were averaged between investigators and a senior surgeon mediator (VH) was used to address any conflicting observations as a third party. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27).

Case series

The findings of this study were used to plan knee reconstruction with free tissue transfer with a vastus medialis branch recipient for microsurgical anastomosis in 3 cases. In each case, the patient underwent preoperative CT angiography to confirm regional vascular anatomy and suitability of this vessel as a recipient. The patients were positioned supine and intraoperative Duplex sonography used to identify a small caliber cutaneous perforator overlying the distal vastus medialis muscle within a 10 cm radius of the superomedial corner of the patella. The cutaneous perforator was used as a landmark to guide localization of the vastus medialis artery recipient. By performing retrograde dissection of the perforator, the vastus medialis artery within the muscle was identified. Dissection proceeded until a sufficient caliber of artery was identified to facilitate microanastomosis.

Case 1

A 65-year-old male was treated for an acute periprosthetic joint infection of a right total knee replacement (Figure 2). He underwent a debridement, antibiotics and implant retention (DAIR) procedure followed by staged microsurgical free flap reconstruction. A contralateral anterolateral thigh (ALT) flap was anastomosed to the vastus medialis artery and to a saphenous vein loop for venous supply. Discussion

The distal pedicle to the vastus medialis is a consistent option that may serve as the recipient artery for free flap reconstruction around the knee. Arising from either the SFA or DGA, its main advantages lie in its consistent reliable anatomy and can be easily dissected in the supine position. While the average caliber on CT of the vessel may seem small, we note the scan represents intraluminal caliber and is likely to underestimate the true vessel caliber which in our experience is sufficiently large to facilitate end-to-end anastomosis to the lateral circumflex femoral artery pedicle of an ALT. This is a limitation of the use of pure CT angiography techniques with vascular anatomy and this study should be supplemented with cadaveric dissection studies for a more complete description of this vessel. This same limitation is the suspected underlying reason for why only 31.7% of cases in the CT study had identifiable cutaneous perforators while intraoperative Duplex ultrasound was able to readily identify skin perforators in all cases.

As an intramuscular vessel, dissection requires partial muscle division. However, sacrifice of the vastus medialis muscle has traditionally been thought to have little long term functional morbidity due to compensation from the other muscles in the quadriceps group.4 This is supported by studies in sarcoma resection that demonstrate complete sacrifice of the entire muscle has no long-term functional limitations.6 However, in all 3 cases presented, the vessel was exposed adequately with only division of superficial distal fibers while the majority of the muscle was preserved. Therefore, the functional morbidity of utilizing this recipient is thought to be negligible.

The role of pre-operative CT angiography in planning is crucial and cannot be understated. As demonstrated, modern CTs can reliably detect the VM branch to confirm its patency and position prior to surgery. This can be confirmed and marked with on table Duplex sonography for more accurate identification of this vessel. Theoretically, the same access incision could also be used to dissect the DGA as a lifeboat option if the VM branch is not a suitable recipient although this was not required in our case series.

The main limitation encountered with this option is the lack of a suitable accompanying venous option as the venae comitantes (VC) are small caliber and generally not suitable for use as a venous recipient. A separate venous recipient was required in all 3 cases and this issue was circumvented using a saphenous vein turnover or vein loop. This was readily dissected without need of a position change or additional incisions. An intact long saphenous vein with adequate length (out of the zone of injury), however, is required to be transposed into the site of arterial anastomosis. The reliability of this venous option can be improved with the use of a pre-operative Doppler ultrasound to confirm location and patency of the vessel. Other venous alternatives would include end-to-side anastomoses with the femoral vein which would not require additional incisions.

Conclusion

The VM artery is a reliable novel recipient for free flap reconstruction around the knee. Advantages include easy dissection, consistent anatomy, proximity to anterior knee defects as well as circumventing the need for interposition vein grafts and positional changes. Preoperative imaging using CT angiography and on table duplex ultrasound is crucial in planning and execution.
