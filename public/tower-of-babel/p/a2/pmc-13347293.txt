Fabrication of UiO-66(Zr)–NH2-encapsulated boehmite–chitosan granules and their application for adsorptive denitrogenation of liquid fuel
Authors: The Ky Vo, Phan Huynh Anh, Dang Thi Thanh Nhan, Van Cuong Nguyen, Van Son Nguyen, Van Thanh Khue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Herein, boehmite–chitosan granules (d ∼ 2–3 mm) encapsulating different amounts of UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 (BCS@UN-x) were efficiently prepared using a combined sol–gel and oil-drop method. The granules were characterised using FE-SEM, XRD, FT-IR, TGA, XPS, and N2 sorption. Incorporating UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 frameworks into the boehmite-crosslinked chitosan matrix yielded composite granules with enhanced porosity and stability, as well as a higher abundance of functional groups, which are beneficial for the adsorption of N-containing compounds (NCCs) from n-octane. In batch experiments, the BCS@UN granules loaded with 14 wt% UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 achieved adsorption capacities of ∼120 mg g−1 for quinoline (QUI) and ∼132 mg g−1 for indole (IND). The adsorption isotherms of the NCCs onto the granules followed the Langmuir model, achieving maximum capacities of 203 mg g−1 for QUI and 215 mg g−1 for IND. In addition, breakthrough adsorption tests indicated that 1.0 g of the prepared BCS@UN granules could effectively purify ∼9.7 L and ∼12.0 L of liquid fuel containing QUI and IND at concentrations of 1 mg L−1, respectively. Notably, these granular adsorbents could be rapidly recovered during the process and exhibited good stability. Adsorption mechanism analyses suggested that UiO-66(Zr)–NH2, integrated into an AlOOH–chitosan matrix, produced a synergistic effect that accelerated the removal efficiency of NCCs by the resulting granules. The findings indicated that producing granular adsorbents encapsulated with the nanocolloid UiO-66(Zr)–NH2via a boehmite–chitosan system is a promising strategy for broadening their practical applications.

Introduction

Energy consumption has increased notably due to the rapid expansion of the global economy and industrial activity, raising serious concerns about emissions.1,2 To meet growing energy demand while achieving net-zero carbon emissions, it is essential to replace fossil fuels with sustainable, drop-in alternatives.3,4 The conversion of biomass to biocrude is considered a promising strategy. However, biomass-derived biocrude usually contains high concentrations of NCCs and many aromatic compounds.3,5 Fuel standards generally permit greater flexibility in NCC content for ground transportation fuels; however, NCCs are strictly prohibited in jet fuels due to their low thermal stability, freezing point, and specific energy characteristics. Therefore, the removal of NCCs from liquid fuels before combustion is necessary.6,7 NCCs in liquid fuels are classified as either basic or neutral nitrogen compounds. Neutral NCCs, which contain an N atom with a lone pair delocalised within an aromatic ring (e.g., quinolines), are less basic; meanwhile, more basic NCCs contain a lone pair orthogonal to the aromatic π-system (e.g., indoles).

Typically, NCCs in fossil fuels are catalytically converted to hydrocarbons via hydrodenitrogenation under high-temperature, high-pressure conditions, which require substantial H2.8,9 Alternatively, NCCs in liquid fuels can also be eliminated by solvent extraction. However, this methodology has not been widely adopted, primarily because it entails excessive solvent use, which increases overall costs and raises environmental concerns. In this context, adsorptive denitrogenation (ADN) is regarded as the most promising strategy because it is cost-effective and straightforward to operate.7,10,11 Furthermore, the adsorption method can efficiently treat liquid fuels with high NCCs content that are unsuitable for catalytic hydrodenitrogenation.

Until now, developing adsorbent materials that meet criteria for efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and environmental impact has remained a significant challenge. Various porous structures, such as activated carbon, silica, zeolite, aluminium oxides, clay, polymers, and graphene oxide-based materials, have been exploited for ADN performance.6,7,12,13 For example, Wang et al. utilised silica gel as an adsorbent in batch adsorption experiments, achieving uptake capacities of 150 mg g−1 for pyridine and 80 mg g−1 for indole in a surrogate fuel.6 Recently, Moosavian prepared CuO/rGO and NiO/rGO adsorbents that achieved carbazole adsorption capacities of 0.335 mmol g−1 and 0.315 mmol g−1 at 278 K, respectively.14 Generally, the performance of ADN on conventional adsorbents is limited by their low surface areas and small pore volumes, prompting efforts to develop novel, more effective structures.

Boehmite (γ-AlOOH) is a vital precursor to transition aluminas. It is frequently used in various applications, including as a flame retardant, a catalyst support, and in biomedical fields, due to its low cost, non-toxic nature, and well-defined pore structure.15–18 Crab shell-derived chitosan is a natural polysaccharide, a renewable and environmentally sustainable carbon resource, recognised as a safe and biodegradable biosorbent.19 It is primarily appreciated for its efficacy as a gas barrier and moderate mechanical strength. Evidence indicates that chitosan-based films can undergo chemical modifications, be blended, or be reinforced with other functional materials to improve their mechanical properties, as well as their chemical, thermal, and biological stability.2,20,21 Their potential to remove both organic and inorganic pollutants has been extensively studied, underscoring their high versatility.22–27

Recently, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), characterised by exceptionally high porosity and tunability, have been recognised as promising alternatives for various catalytic and adsorptive applications.28–35 In the context of liquid fuel denitrogenation, the utilisation of these advanced structures remains limited, with only a few MOF and MOF-based materials explored for ADN, such as MIL-101(Cr), MIL-100(Fe), UiO-66(Zr), and UiO-66(Zr)–NH2.36 Although chitosan/MOF composites are widely studied for the removal of dyes and heavy metals from water, adsorption of CO2, and biomedical applications,37–40 there is no report on their use to treat liquid fuel. Research shows that the adsorption of NCCs onto pristine MOFs is mediated by van der Waals forces, π–π interactions, hydrogen bonding, and acid–base interactions, thereby enhancing ADN efficiency. Furthermore, combining MOFs with other structures can further improve their adsorption performance. Hu et al. synthesised MOF(Al)@GO, which exhibited high adsorption capacities for pyridine, quinoline, and indole in oil due to the synergistic effects of the MOF-graphene oxide combination.41 Recently, CuCl-doped UiO-66(Zr) reportedly achieved high AND performance toward indole and quinoline in a model fuel.42 Our recent work demonstrated that modified UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 frameworks exhibited significant improvements in ADN performance, outperforming traditional porous structures.43 These findings suggest that exploring MOF-based materials to improve liquid fuel quality remains promising, given the numerous hybrid metal–organic frameworks that have been discovered. Additionally, the direct use of nanopowder adsorbents in the ADN process complicates recovery and makes the process highly vulnerable to processing losses, thereby increasing investment costs.

This work involved preparing boehmite sol–chitosan composite granules (d: 2–3 mm) encapsulating nanocrystalline amino-functionalised UiO-66(Zr) using the oil-drop technique (Scheme 1). Granules with different MOF contents (7, 14, and 18 wt%) were produced and were characterised by FESEM, XRD, FT-IR, TGA, XPS, and N2 sorption analyses. It was observed that the granules were formed from paraffin oil and subsequently strengthened by gelation of a boehmite sol and polytriphosphate-crosslinking of chitosan, creating a stable composite matrix that effectively entrapped the UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 nanopowder. The BCS@UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 granules exhibited increased porosity and were rich in functional groups that are beneficial for adsorption. These granular adsorbents are suitable for removing quinoline and indole from n-octane in both batch and continuous-flow fixed-bed conditions. Notably, the novel millimeter-scale granules can be readily recovered during processing with minimal loss and remain stable. This study offers a practical method for producing granular absorbents encapsulated with nanocrystalline MOFs, thereby facilitating their real-world applications.

Experimental

Preparation of chitosan

Chitosan was synthesised from crab shells (Fig. S1(a)) using a conventional chemical extraction method with slight modifications. First, deproteinisation was carried out by treating the crab shells (200 g) with a 5 wt% NaOH solution (1000 mL) at 80–90 °C for 6 hours to remove proteinaceous components. The deproteinised shells were then demineralised in a 5 wt% HCl solution (∼500 mL) at room temperature until no further gas was released. To ensure complete protein removal, a second deproteinisation step was performed using 5 wt% NaOH at 90 °C for 8 hours, yielding purified chitin (Fig. S1(b)). Deacetylation of chitin (∼25 g) was performed using a 50 wt% NaOH aqueous solution (500 mL) at 90 °C for 10 h, and the process was repeated twice to further increase the degree of deacetylation. The resulting chitosan (∼20 g) was washed extensively with distilled water until the pH was neutral and then dried at 50 °C. The obtained chitosan exhibited an off-white iridescent membrane (Fig. S1(c)). XRD patterns and FT-IR analyses of the produced chitosan are shown in Fig. S2 and S3, respectively.

Preparation of UiO-66(Zr)–NH2

Nanopowder UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 were synthesised using the solvothermal method. Typically, 3.0 g of ZrCl4 (Aldrich Sigma, 98%) was dissolved in 45 mL of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) under constant stirring. To this solution, 3.0 g of 2-aminoterephthalic acid and acetic acid were added as a modulator, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 10 minutes. The solution was then transferred into a 100 mL autoclave placed in a stainless steel reactor. The reactor was heated to 120 °C for 12 hours in a convection oven. After the reaction, the yellow solid was collected by centrifugation and subsequently purified several times with DMF and ethanol to remove the reactants. The product was dried at 115 °C overnight before use.

Preparation of boehmite–chitosan/UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 granules

The AlOOH–chitosan/UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 granules were produced using the oil-drop method, as shown in Scheme 1. First, a stable boehmite sol (2 M) was prepared by hydrolysing Al(NO3)3 in NH3, following our previous procedure.18 Chitosan produced from crab shells was dissolved in acetic acid with vigorous stirring to obtain a solution of 2%. To prepare the granules, 30 mL of boehmite sol and 7 mL of chitosan were mixed, sonicated for 10 minutes, and stirred for 30 minutes to create a uniform solution. A specified amount of UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 was then added to the mixture, and the mixture was sonicated for another 10 minutes. Next, the mixture was slowly dropped into a glass column (40 cm × 5 cm) filled with NH3 at the bottom and paraffin oil at the top. The granules were collected and washed several times with ethanol. To cross-link chitosan within the granules, they were immersed in a tripolyphosphate (TPP) solution for 30 minutes and then washed multiple times with ethanol. The final samples are labelled as BCS@UN-x, where x indicates the weight percentage of UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 relative to the boehmite weight (x = 7, 14, 18%). The boehmite–chitosan (BCS) granules were prepared using the same procedure, without the addition of UiO-66(Zr)–NH2.

Characterization

The crystallinity of the samples was assessed by X-ray diffraction (XRD; Miniflex 600, Rigaku, Japan) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å). The morphology of the materials was examined using high-resolution field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; Leo Supra 55, Carl Zeiss, Germany). FT-IR spectrum was recorded using an FT-IR spectrometer (Tensor 27, Bruker, Germany). Textural characteristics of the samples were obtained by nitrogen sorption measured through N2 porosimetry (Tristar 3020, Micromeritics, USA) at 77 K. Thermal stability was evaluated by TGA (Q50, TA Instruments, USA) under an N2 atmosphere. X-ray photoelectron spectrometry (XPS; K-Alpha, Thermo Scientific, USA) was utilised to analyse the elemental composition and their chemical states.

Batch adsorptive removal of NCCs from liquid fuel

In this work, n-octane was utilized as a liquid-fuel model, and indole and quinoline were added to n-octane as nitrogen-containing model compounds. For a typical experiment, 0.3 g of granular adsorbents was introduced into 60 mL of fuel containing IND and QUI at specified concentrations, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature. During the adsorption process, 3 mL of the fuel was withdrawn at each sampling time point and filtered before analysis with a GC-FID analyser (YL6500GC, Younglin Co., Ltd., Korea).

For regenerability evaluations, the spent granules were collected, immersed in acetone for 2 hours to promote desorption, and then dried at 70 °C before reuse as a renewable adsorbent.

Fixed-bed column adsorption of NCCs

The fixed-bed NCCs removal experiments using granular samples were conducted in a glass burette (length: ∼55 cm, inner diameter: ∼1.0 cm). The burette was packed with layers consisting of sponge, quartz sand, quartz sand mixed with granular adsorbents (∼4.0 g), and sponge. The top of the column was sealed with a pressurised rubber cork. The fuel model containing NCCs (C0 = 100 ppm) was introduced into the column from the bottom to the top with a flow rate of 3 mL min−1 using a peristaltic pump. Effluents were collected and analysed using GC-FID to quantify NCCs content.

Results and discussion

Characterization

Fig. 1 shows optical images of the prepared granules. The boehmite–chitosan samples are characterised by white spheres with a diameter of approximately 2–3 mm [Fig. 1(a)]. Granules encapsulating UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 crystals display a yellow hue, which becomes more intense as the MOF content increases from 7 to 14 and 18 wt% [Fig. 1(b–d)]. It was observed that granules loaded with 18 wt% crystalline MOF exhibited a high crack density, suggesting that a high MOF content disrupted the AlOOH–chitosan matrix composite and induced strains that led to breakage during drying.

Therefore, the BCS@UN samples loaded with a moderate amount of MOF content (14 wt%) are selected for further investigation. Fig. S4 shows an SEM image of pristine UiO-66(Zr)–NH2, with crystals ranging in size from 100–200 nm. Fig. 1(e–g) compares SEM images of the prepared BCS and BCS@UN-10 sample. As illustrated, UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 crystals were effectively dispersed on the surface and within the granules. Additionally, the MOF-attached granules exhibited greater roughness and more cavities than their counterparts without MOF, indicating that the MOF crystals influence the continuous progression of boehmite gelation and chitosan polymerisation. EDX analyses indicated the fine dispersion of C, Al, O, Zr, N, and P throughout the sample, suggesting that the boehmite, chitosan, and UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 components were effectively combined into the spheres [Fig. 1(h–p)].

Fig. 2(a) shows the crystallography patterns of the prepared samples. The bare UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 pattern displays prominent peaks at 2θ ∼ 7.44, 8.45, 12.02, and 25.83°, consistent with previously reported literature.44,45 The pattern of the BCS granules shows strong peaks at 2θ ∼ 14.31, 28.12, 38.19, 48.70, and 64.89°, which are assignable to γ-AlOOH; additionally, lower-intensity peaks at 2θ ∼ 18.6° and 20.34° are attributable to the amorphous structure of chitosan.46 The absence of signals corresponding to UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 in the pattern of the BCS@UN samples suggested that these MOF nanocrystals are effectively encapsulated within the boehmite-CS matrix system. It can be observed that the γ-AlOOH peaks in the BCS@UN patterns are slightly shifted, confirming that the amino-functionalized UiO-66(Zr) frameworks were incorporated into the boehmite-CS matrix. XPS survey scan results confirmed the elemental composition of the composite granules, which are consistent with the EDS analyses above [Fig. 2(b)]. The thermal stability of the fabricated granules was examined, and the results are presented in Fig. 2(c). The TGA curve of UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 powder shows a minor weight loss at around 115 °C owing to the evaporation of solvents, while a notable weight loss at 350 °C, which is linked to framework decomposition.33,47 The TGA profile of BCS granules indicates a minor weight loss below 200 °C, corresponding to the removal of absorbed water. Additionally, the gradual decrease in mass above 200 °C can be attributed to the dehydroxylation of boehmite, along with the decomposition of chitosan.48,49 The BCS@UN granules exhibited improved thermal stability compared with the BCS owing to the encapsulated MOF.

Adsorptive removal of NCCs from liquid fuel

The fabricated granules were used to adsorb quinoline and indole from n-octane, which is considered a model liquid fuel. Fig. 3 illustrates the removal efficiency of QUI and IDN using the produced materials (discrete points). The granular BCSs exhibited modest NCCs removal efficiencies, whereas granules decorated with UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 showed significantly enhanced adsorption efficiency. Furthermore, the adsorption performance of the granules improved as the UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 content increased. As the MOF content increased from 7 to 14 and 18 wt%, the adsorption capacities for QUI increased from 84 to 120 and 139 mg g−1, and for IDN from 100 to 132 and 153 mg g−1, respectively, which surpasses other adsorbents, such as carbon spheres, AC, NaY, ytrium ion-exchanged zeolite Y, Ti-HMS, molecularly imprinted polymer, and UiO-66(Zr) (see Table S2). This supports the hypothesis that a higher content of amino-functionalised UiO-66(Zr) in the BCS granules led to more pores and active sites, thereby enhancing adsorption performance. It is worth noting that the designed millimeter-scale granules are effectively utilised without material loss during the adsorption, enhancing their practical application. It can be seen that the produced granules demonstrated greater IND uptake than QUI under similar conditions. Because the kinetic diameters of QUI and IND are pretty identical (∼0.62 nm for QUI and ∼0.58 nm for IDN), their interactions with the granules primarily affected adsorption efficiency. QUI can form hydrogen bonds only through the N atom as the acceptor; in contrast, IDN can readily form H-bonds via the H and N atoms in the N–H group, which can act as donor and acceptor, respectively. The detailed adsorption mechanism will be discussed further in the following section.

The stability and reusability are crucial factors in assessing the material's feasibility. In this case, the granular adsorbents were swiftly recovered after each use and immediately immersed in acetone for 3 hours to regenerate the materials. Afterwards, the recovered granules were dried at 70 °C overnight before proceeding to the next adsorption–desorption cycle. As shown in Fig. 4(c), the BCS@UN material retained 100% IND removal efficiency for the first two cycles, then slightly declined to 81% by the sixth cycle. The optical image of the spent granules indicates that the sample retained its spherical shape after recycling [Fig. 4(d)]. Additionally, SEM images and XRD pattern analyses of the spent material showed that its composite structure and morphology remained stable even after multiple adsorption–desorption cycles [Fig. 4(e and f)]. The results suggest that the fabricated UiO-66(Zr)–NH2-decorated BCS granules are stable and suitable for practical application.

Adsorption mechanism

Fig. 6(a) presents the FT-IR spectrum of the absorbent after IND adsorption from n-octane. The peaks around 1630 cm−1, originating from aromatic ring stretching vibrations in UiO-66(Zr)–NH2, shifted and decreased in intensity following adsorption of IND and QUI, indicating π–π interactions between the MOF ligands and NCCs. Additionally, new bands at approximately 744 and 1456 cm−1 correspond to vibrations of the out-of-plane C–H bonds in aromatic rings, and the N–H of the NCCs appeared after adsorption, confirming that these guest molecules are attached to the granules. XPS analyses of the sample used for indole adsorption are compared with those of the as-fabricated sample. The high-resolution Zr 3d spectrum shows peaks at 181.6 eV (3d5/2) and 183.4 eV (3d3/2), which belong to Zr4+ in UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 [Fig. 6(b)]. These peaks are slightly shifted to higher energy after adsorption, indicating that IND molecules interact with Zr4+ sites via: N → Zr4+ bonds. The C 1s spectrum of the BCS@UN sample shows energy peaks for carbon elements in C–C (284.5 eV, 40%), C–N (285.6 eV, 21%), C–O (286.2 eV, 27%), and CO (287.4 eV, 12%)linkages32,54,55 [Fig. 6(c)]. After IND adsorption, the C content in the C–C and C–N bonds increased to approximately 47% and 25%, respectively. Furthermore, the core-level N 1s XPS spectrum of the prepared BCS@UN exhibits peaks at 398.9 eV and 401.6 eV, corresponding to N atoms in N–H (79%) and C–N (21%) bonds in UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 and chitosan.56 The N content associated with the C–N bonds increased by 4% following adsorption, further confirming the accumulation of NCCs on the granules [Fig. 6(d)].

As noted above, combining gelated boehmite with tripolyphosphate-crosslinked chitosan produced composites with multiple mesoporous structures, thereby enabling effective diffusion of NCCs into the granules. When UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 crystallites were incorporated into the granules, they not only increased porosity but also provided additional –NH2 groups, and possibly included coordinatively unsaturated Zr4+ sites as Lewis acid sites of the MOF. The resulting granules contain numerous –OH, –NH2, and CO groups that potentially form hydrogen bonds with indole and quinoline. As noted, QUI and IDN adsorb to the granules differently due to differences in their molecular structures. When QUI forms H-bonds only via the N atom as the acceptor, IND can generate H-bonds via the H and N atoms in the N–H group, which act as donor and acceptor, respectively. In the meantime, unsaturated Zr4+ sites in the UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 attracted NCCs containing N atoms with unpaired electrons to generate: N → Zr4+ bonds. In addition, the ligands of the encapsulated UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 frameworks can also interact with the NCCs via π–π interactions. As a result, the synergistic effect significantly enhances the material's ability to capture NCCs from liquid fuel. Conclusions

Boehmite–chitosan granules (d: 2–3 mm) encapsulating nanocolloidal UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 were successfully prepared by the oil-drop approach and thoroughly characterised. The fine, crack-free granules were produced by optimising the MOF content to 14 wt%. The prepared BCS@UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 granules achieved adsorbing amounts of 120 mg g−1 for QUI and 132 mg g−1 for IND. Additionally, the prepared granules effectively removed NCCs from fuel under continuous fixed-bed conditions. Adsorption mechanism analyses indicated that decorating UiO-66(Zr)–NH2 within the boehmite–chitosan matrix improved porosity and enriched the chemical functional groups and Zr4+ sites, thereby accelerating the adsorption of NCCs onto the granules. Notably, granular adsorbents are easily recovered during adsorption and exhibit high regenerability, making them promising materials for upgrading liquid fuels.
