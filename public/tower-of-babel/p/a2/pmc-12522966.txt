KBTBD11 suppresses hepatocellular carcinoma by targeting ENO1-mediated glycolysis
Authors: Yixuan Liu, Yiran Chen, Lirui Tang, Feifei Lin, Qingyang Zhuang, Hanshen Chen, Xiandong Lin, Jinluan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Enhanced aerobic glycolysis is a hallmark of HCC progression, yet the upstream regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely understood.MethodsAn in vivo genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screen in an orthotopic HCC model was used to identify metabolic regulators. Functional assays including extracellular flux analysis, ATP and lactate quantification, ROS detection, apoptosis assays, and 3D spheroid culture were performed to evaluate the effects of KBTBD11. Protein interactions were assessed by co-immunoprecipitation, ubiquitination assays, and truncation mutant analysis. Spatial interactions and subcellular localization of KBTBD11 and ENO1 were further validated by proximity ligation assay (PLA) and immunofluorescence staining.ResultsKBTBD11 was frequently downregulated in HCC and associated with favorable prognosis. Overexpression of KBTBD11 suppressed glycolytic activity, reduced ATP and lactate levels, elevated ROS accumulation, and promoted apoptosis. Mechanistically, KBTBD11 interacted with ENO1 and facilitated its proteasomal degradation via a ubiquitin-dependent pathway. Deletion of either the BTB or Kelch domain of KBTBD11 impaired its ability to bind ENO1. Rescue experiments using ENO1 re-expression or pharmacological inhibition by ENOblock confirmed that the metabolic and phenotypic effects of KBTBD11 were ENO1-dependent.ConclusionsThese findings identify KBTBD11 as a negative regulator of ENO1-mediated glycolysis in HCC. By promoting ENO1 degradation, KBTBD11 links ubiquitin signaling to metabolic control and tumor suppression, highlighting a potential therapeutic target for metabolically active liver cancer.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, KBTBD11, ENO1, Glycolysis, Metabolic reprogramming, Ubiquitination

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for approximately 75–85% of primary liver cancers and remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related death worldwide [1]. The clinical management of HCC has improved in recent years, yet the prognosis for patients with advanced disease remains poor, owing to limited therapeutic options and high rates of recurrence and resistance [2]. These challenges underscore the critical need for a deeper understanding of the molecular pathways underlying hepatocellular carcinoma progression and the identification of potential therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Metabolic reprogramming is a key characteristic of cancer, allowing tumor cells to grow quickly and survive under stress. A notable aspect of this is the Warburg effect, where cancer cells favor aerobic glycolysis instead of oxidative phosphorylation, even when oxygen is available [3, 4]. This metabolic change boosts ATP and biosynthetic intermediates for tumor growth. Enolase 1 (ENO1), crucial in glycolysis, converts 2-phosphoglycerate to phosphoenolpyruvate, a vital glycolytic step. High ENO1 levels are found in various cancers like HCC, linked to enhanced glycolysis and tumor aggressiveness [5, 6]. However, the upstream regulatory mechanisms controlling ENO1 expression and stability in liver cancer remain poorly understood.

Given the established role of ENO1 in glycolysis and tumor aggressiveness, proteostasis emerges as a pivotal regulatory node. Cancer cells extensively reprogram metabolic flux via post-translational modifications—including ubiquitination, acetylation, and phosphorylation—which enable rapid modulation of enzyme activity, macromolecular complex formation, subcellular localization, and protein stability. The ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) is crucial for maintaining protein balance in cancer biology by selectively degrading target proteins. It regulates processes like signal transduction, cell cycle, and metabolism. Adaptor proteins, particularly from the BTB-Kelch family, often determine substrate specificity within the UPS [7–9]. KBTBD11 is a relatively uncharacterized member of this family, initially described in the context of neuronal development and cell differentiation [10]. KBTBD11 is thought to function as a substrate adaptor in the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, but its role in cancer biology is still not well understood. There is currently no research that has determined if KBTBD11 influences oncogenic pathways, protein metabolism, or tumor progression in HCC.

Exploring whether KBTBD11 influences metabolic enzymes like ENO1 in hepatocellular carcinoma is crucial, given glycolysis’s role and ubiquitin-mediated regulation’s impact on the cancer proteome. Understanding this could reveal HCC’s metabolic weaknesses and potential therapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Huh7 and Hepa1-6 cell lines were used in this study. Huh7 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, while Hepa1-6 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium with the same supplements. Both cell lines were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. KBTBD11 overexpression was generated by lentiviral transduction. Briefly, full-length KBTBD11/Kbtbd11 cDNA was subcloned into pLVX-Puro (CMV), lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells with psPAX2 and pMD2.G, and target cells were transduced and selected with puromycin (2 µg/mL) for 10 days to establish stable lines. Successful overexpression was confirmed by Western blot. Control cells received the empty pLVX-Puro vector and underwent identical transduction and selection.

Animal model

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed in a temperature-controlled facility under a 12-h light/dark cycle with free access to standard rodent chow and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Laboratory Animal Center at Fujian Medical University and conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Hepa1-6 cells (2 × 106 cells per mouse) were implanted into the liver of C57BL/6 mice using a fine needle. Tumor growth was monitored non-invasively using in vivo bioluminescence imaging (IVIS) for up to 3 weeks post-injection. At the end of the study, mice were euthanized, and liver tissues were harvested for histological analysis and further molecular studies.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion were assessed using Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size, Corning). For migration assays, Hepa1-6 or Huh7 cells (2 × 10⁴ cells per chamber) were seeded in the upper chamber with serum-free medium. For invasion assays, the upper chamber was pre-coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences). After 24 h of incubation, cells that migrated or invaded through the membrane were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and counted under a microscope in five random fields.

Colony formation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed by the colony formation assay. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well and cultured for 12 days. Colonies were fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet, and counted. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were considered positive. The colony number was quantified by image analysis using ImageJ software.

Measurement of ECAR

To assess the glycolytic activity, ECAR was measured using the Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test Kit (103020-100, Seahorse Biosciences). Briefly, 6 × 104 Huh7/Hepa1-6 cells were seeded in XFe96 Cell Culture Microplates (101085-004, Seahorse Biosciences) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 12 h. After washing with XF Base Medium, the cells were incubated in a CO2-free incubator for 1 h. Glycolysis stress test measurements were performed following the addition of glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1 µM), and 2-DG (50 mM) to assess basal glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve. ECAR data was normalized to the protein content of each sample.

Metabolic flux analysis (MFA)

Huh7 cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were treated with [U-13C6] D-glucose (4.5 g/L) for 1 h. After labeling, cells were washed with PBS, and metabolic extracts were obtained by cold methanol extraction. Samples were dried and resuspended in water for analysis. Metabolic flux was measured using LC-MS (Dionex Ultimate 3000 UPLC system). Isotopic labeling of metabolites was tracked by analyzing the M + 0, M + 1, and M + 2 fractions of glycolytic intermediates and TCA cycle metabolites. The incorporation of 13C into metabolites (e.g., glucose-6-phosphate, pyruvate, lactate, citrate) was quantified to calculate flux ratios. Data was normalized to total protein content, and statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism.

Co-immunoprecipitation–mass spectrometry (Co-IP MS)

Huh7 cells were cultured in complete DMEM to 70–80% confluence. Cells were washed with cold PBS and lysed in IP lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, pH 7.4) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C.500 µL of protein lysate was incubated overnight at 4 °C with 5 µg of anti-KBTBD11 or Flag-tag antibodies. The antibody-protein complexes were pulled down using Protein A/G agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Beads were washed three times with ice-cold IP lysis buffer. Proteins were eluted with 2× Laemmli buffer at 95 °C for 10 min and separated by SDS-PAGE. Gel bands were excised, digested with trypsin, and analyzed by LC-MS/MS on a Q Exactive Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were analyzed to obtain sequences, and data were processed using MaxQuant software for protein identification. MS data were searched against the UniProt human database. Proteins identified with ≥ 2 unique peptides and an FDR < 1% were considered significant.

Molecular docking

Protein-protein docking was performed using the GRAMM docking server, which is based on a rigid docking approach where the ligand and receptor structures are considered fixed, and only their relative orientations are optimized. The protein sequences were retrieved from UniProtKB, and 3D structures were generated using SWISS-MODEL. The generated PDB files of the receptor and ligand were submitted to the GRAMM server for docking.The docking results were ranked, and the best solution was selected based on the lowest binding energy.

Genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screening

Huh-7 cells stably expressing Cas9 (Huh-7-Cas9) were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells were transduced with the Toronto KnockOut CRISPR library version 3 (TKOv3) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of approximately 0.3. Forty-eight hours post-infection, 2 µg/mL puromycin was added for 5 days to select successfully transduced cells, and library coverage of at least 300-fold was maintained throughout the screen.

Following selection, cells were divided into two groups. One group was cultured under standard conditions for 14 days to perform in vitro screening. The other group (1 × 107 cells) was subcutaneously injected into the flanks of BALB/c nude mice. Tumors were harvested when they reached approximately 50 mm in diameter, and genomic DNA was extracted from both in vitro cultured cells and tumor tissues.

SgRNA sequences were amplified by two-round PCR incorporating Illumina adapters and indexed primers, followed by sequencing on an Illumina NextSeq platform. SgRNA abundance was quantified using the MAGeCK algorithm. Candidate genes promoting or inhibiting tumor growth were identified based on differential sgRNA enrichment, using an FDR threshold of < 0.05.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

Proximity ligation assay (PLA) was performed to assess the intracellular interaction between KBTBD11 and ENO1. Huh7 cells were co-transfected with KBTBD11 and ENO1 expression constructs and stained using primary antibodies raised in different species, followed by species-specific PLA probes conjugated with complementary oligonucleotides. Upon close spatial proximity of the target proteins, connector oligos and ligase generate a circular DNA template, which is amplified by rolling circle amplification and visualized by fluorescent probe hybridization. Red puncta indicate localized PLA signals; nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

Plasmids and constructs

Full-length human KBTBD11 (encoding amino acids 1–623) was cloned into a FLAG-tagged expression vector (pLV-Flag backbone). To investigate the domain-specific functions of KBTBD11, deletion mutants were designed as follows: ΔBTB: deletion of amino acids 136–240 (BTB domain); ΔKelch: deletion of amino acids 311–568 (Kelch repeats); Kelch: amino acids 311–566.

All truncated coding sequences were synthesized commercially, codon-optimized for human expression, and subcloned into the EcoRI/XhoI sites of the same pLV-Flag vector using standard restriction enzyme–based cloning. Internal Kozak sequences and start codons were included where appropriate for in-frame translation.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data is presented as mean ± SD. Two-group comparisons were performed using unpaired two-tailed t-tests, while multiple-group comparisons used one-way ANOVA followed by appropriate post hoc tests. Survival curves were analyzed using the log-rank test. Triple biological replications were made. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Significance was defined as p < 0.05. Asterisk annotations in figures denote: < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), p < 0.0001 (****).

Results

Genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screening identifies KBTBD11 as a potential tumor suppressor in hepatocellular carcinoma

To discover genes that inhibit tumor development in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we conducted a genome-scale CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen in vivo. We implanted Huh7-Cas9 cells with a pooled sgRNA library orthotopically in mouse livers. After 21 days, we harvested sgRNAs from tumor tissues and compared them to pre-injection controls (T1) via next-generation sequencing (Fig. 1A). Comparative sequencing of sgRNA abundance in tumor tissues versus pre-injection controls (T1) revealed a set of positively selected genes whose loss conferred growth advantage in vivo (Fig. 1B), including known oncogenic drivers such as CTNNB1 and PSMC4. Among all candidates, KBTBD11 emerged as the top-ranked gene, exhibiting the highest β-score and consistent enrichment of targeting sgRNAs (Fig. 1C and D). Overall, these findings suggest that KBTBD11 may function as a tumor suppressor candidate in HCC.

KBTBD11 is downregulated in HCC and associated with favorable prognosis

In order to assess the clinical significance of KBTBD11 in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we first identified KBTBD11 as a potential tumor suppressor gene as a result of genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screening. To validate its prognostic relevance, we then used the Kaplan–Meier Plotter database to analyze liver cancer RNA-seq data from 364 patients. The analyses showed that high KBTBD11 expression was significantly associated with longer overall survival (OS; unadjusted hazard ratio (HR) = 0.52, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.36–0.74, log-rank p value = 3e−4), recurrence-free survival (RFS; HR = 0.56, p = 0.00054), and progression-free survival (PFS; HR = 0.65, p = 0.0051) (Fig. 2A).

Evaluation of KBTBD11 expression and clinical relevance in HCC. A Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan–Meier Plotter database (n = 364) to assess overall survival (OS), recurrence-free survival (RFS), and progression-free survival (PFS) based on KBTBD11 expression. B KBTBD11 expression data were obtained from TCGA-LIHC via the GEPIA platform and from three independent GEO datasets (GSE39791, GSE45267, GSE112790). C Immunohistochemical staining of a tissue microarray containing 72 paired HCC and adjacent normal liver tissues was performed to assess KBTBD11 protein levels

Similarly, analysis using the GEPIA platform based on TCGA-LIHC data confirmed that patients with high KBTBD11 expression had improved OS (HR = 0.63, log-rank p-value = 0.0096) (Fig. 2B). We next examined KBTBD11 expression across three independent HCC cohorts from the GEO database (GSE39791, GSE45267, GSE112790), all of which consistently demonstrated significantly reduced KBTBD11 mRNA levels in tumor tissues compared to matched adjacent normal liver (all p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2B, ).To further validate this finding at the protein level, immunohistochemical staining was performed on a tissue microarray comprising 72 paired HCC and adjacent normal tissues. Consistent with transcriptomic analyses, KBTBD11 protein expression was markedly decreased in tumor tissues compared to their adjacent counterparts (Fig. 2C). Collectively, these results indicate that KBTBD11 is frequently downregulated in HCC and may serve as a favorable prognostic biomarker.

KBTBD11 suppresses HCC cell proliferation and invasion in vitro

To evaluate the functional role of KBTBD11 in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we assessed its effects on cell motility, invasiveness, clonogenicity, and 3D spheroid formation. Overexpression of KBTBD11 markedly suppressed the migratory and invasive capacity of both Huh7 and Hepa1-6 cells, while knockdown of KBTBD11 led to the opposite phenotype (Fig. 3A, B, D and E). Similarly, colony formation was significantly reduced upon KBTBD11 overexpression and enhanced by its silencing. Western blot analysis confirmed the efficiency of KBTBD11 modulation (Fig. 3C and F). In addition, 3D spheroid culture assays showed that KBTBD11 overexpression impeded tumor spheroid growth over time, further supporting its suppressive role in tumor progression (Fig. 3G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that KBTBD11 restricts the proliferative, migratory, and invasive behavior of HCC cells, consistent with its function as a tumor suppressor.

KBTBD11 overexpression suppresses tumor growth and improves survival in vivo

To validate the tumor-suppressive activity of KBTBD11 in vivo, we established an orthotopic liver cancer model using Hepa1-6 cells with stable KBTBD11 overexpression. Bioluminescence imaging showed markedly reduced intrahepatic signal in the KBTBD11-OE group relative to controls (Fig. 4A). Gross liver morphology also showed visibly smaller tumor nodules in mice receiving KBTBD11-overexpressing cells (Fig. 4B). Survival analysis demonstrated that KBTBD11-OE mice had significantly prolonged overall survival (Fig. 4C). Histological examination of liver sections revealed reduced tumor burden and invasive features in the KBTBD11-OE group (Fig. 4D). Additionally, serum ALT and AST levels were lower in these mice, indicating less liver injury (Fig. 4E). Collectively, these results support the inhibitory effect of KBTBD11 on HCC progression in vivo.

KBTBD11 interacts with ENO1 and is functionally linked to glycolytic regulation in HCC

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying KBTBD11-mediated tumor suppression in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we first performed transcriptomic profiling in KBTBD11-overexpressing Huh7 cells. Differential gene expression and KEGG pathway analyses revealed a significant upregulation of glycolysis-related signaling pathways (Fig. 5A and B). To further explore protein-level interactions, we conducted immunoprecipitation (IP) coupled with mass spectrometry, identifying 46 candidate proteins that may be associated with KBTBD11 (Fig. 5C). Gene Ontology analysis of these interactors showed significant enrichment in metabolic processes (Fig. 5D). Among these, ENO1—a key enzyme in the glycolytic cascade—emerged as a top candidate based on spectral abundance and metabolic relevance (Fig. 5E).

In silico protein–protein docking predicted a stable interaction interface between KBTBD11 and ENO1, involving multiple residues at the Kelch-repeat domain (Fig. 5F). These findings were experimentally validated by reciprocal co-IP assays (Fig. 5G). Immunofluorescence staining showed partial colocalization of KBTBD11 and ENO1 within cells, and Duolink PLA confirmed their interaction through distinct fluorescent spots in co-transfected cells (Fig. 5H and I). Together, these data identify ENO1 as a direct interactor of KBTBD11, suggesting a mechanistic link between KBTBD11 and glycolytic regulation in HCC.

KBTBD11 promotes ENO1 degradation through the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway

To investigate the mechanism by which KBTBD11 regulates ENO1 protein stability, we first confirmed that KBTBD11 overexpression suppressed ENO1 levels in Huh7 cells, whereas co-expression of HA-ENO1 partially restored its abundance (Fig. 6A). MG132 treatment led to time-dependent accumulation of ENO1 (Fig. 6B) and reversed KBTBD11-induced ENO1 downregulation (Fig. 6C), suggesting a proteasome-dependent mechanism.

Cycloheximide chase assays showed that KBTBD11 overexpression reduced, while its knockdown prolonged, ENO1 half-life (Fig. 6D and E). Moreover, ubiquitination assays demonstrated that KBTBD11 significantly enhanced ENO1 polyubiquitination, which was further stabilized by proteasome inhibition (Fig. 6F).

Inhibiting ENO1 with Enoblock, which blocks its activity without affecting protein levels, showed no change in ENO1 protein. Combining this with KBTBD11 overexpression had no additional effect (Fig. 6G), suggesting similar regulatory mechanisms.To determine which domains of KBTBD11 mediate its interaction with ENO1, we performed co-immunoprecipitation assays using domain-deletion mutants. Full-length KBTBD11 strongly interacted with ENO1, while deletion of the Kelch domain (ΔKelch) nearly abolished binding. Deletion of the BTB domain (ΔBTB) led to a moderate reduction in binding affinity, and the Kelch-only construct retained weak ENO1 association (Fig. 6I). These results suggest that while the Kelch domain is essential for direct substrate recognition, the BTB domain contributes to stabilizing the KBTBD11–ENO1 complex.

KBTBD11 suppresses glucose-derived energy metabolism in HCC cells

To further determine whether KBTBD11 directly affects glycolytic carbon metabolism, we performed stable isotope-resolved metabolic flux analysis using uniformly labeled [U-13C6]-glucose. Hepa1-6 cells stably expressing KBTBD11 or control vector were incubated with [U-13C6]-glucose, and the abundance of labeled glycolytic intermediates was quantified by LC–MS/MS (Fig. 7A).

KBTBD11 inhibits glycolytic carbon flux as revealed by 13C-glucose metabolic tracing. (A) Schematic diagram illustrating the glycolytic pathway labeled by uniformly labeled 13C6-glucose (U–13C6-glucose), highlighting the ENO1-catalyzed step from 2-phosphoglycerate to phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP).(B) LC–MS/MS–based 13C isotope tracing of glycolytic intermediates in Hepa1-6 cells stably overexpressing KBTBD11 or transduced with empty vector. Cells were cultured in medium containing 10 mM U–13C6-glucose for 4 h prior to metabolite extraction. Labeled fractions (m + n isotopologues) of each metabolite were quantified to assess glycolytic flux

Notably, KBTBD11 overexpression did not significantly alter levels of early glycolytic intermediates, including glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) and fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (F-1,6-BP), demonstrating that glucose uptake and the upper glycolytic flux remain largely unaltered. In contrast, a significant reduction in downstream intermediates, including 2-phosphoglycerate, phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), pyruvate, and lactate, was observed in KBTBD11-overexpressing cells.

The marked reductions in PEP and pyruvate, both immediate downstream products of the ENO1-catalyzed reaction, suggest that KBTBD11 impairs glycolysis by targeting this enzymatic step.

These results strongly support that KBTBD11 impairs glycolytic flux at the ENO1-catalyzed step, restricting carbon flow through the enolase reaction. Taken together, this evidence confirms that KBTBD11 suppresses glycolysis by interfering with ENO1-dependent metabolite conversion, likely via direct interaction with ENO1.

KBTBD11 suppresses glycolytic capacity in HCC cells

To elucidate the metabolic implications of KBTBD11 modulation, we initially evaluated glycolytic activity through extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) analysis. KBTBD11 overexpression significantly suppressed ECAR in Huh7 and Hepa1-6 cells, whereas its knockout enhanced glycolytic flux (Fig. 8A). Correspondingly, intracellular ATP levels were reduced following KBTBD11 overexpression and increased upon knockdown (Fig. 8B). Lactate production followed a similar trend (Fig. 8C), suggesting that KBTBD11 exerts a negative regulatory effect on glycolytic output.

To determine whether the observed effect is mediated via ENO1, we re-expressed HA-tagged ENO1 in KBTBD11-overexpressing cells. ENO1 reintroduction partially restored ECAR (Fig. 8D and E), ATP levels (Fig. 8F), and lactate production (Fig. 8C), supporting the role of ENO1 as a functional mediator downstream of KBTBD11. Subsequently, we administered ENOblock, a pharmacological inhibitor of ENO1, recapitulated the metabolic suppression observed with KBTBD11 overexpression. Notably, co-treatment with ENOblock and KBTBD11 did not produce additive effects on ECAR, ATP, or lactate output (Fig. 8G–I), suggesting that both act within the same metabolic pathway.

Finally, we evaluated the impact of KBTBD11-mediated glycolytic inhibition on cell proliferation. Both KBTBD11 overexpression and ENOblock treatment significantly impaired growth in Huh7 and Hepa1-6 cells. Importantly, their combination led to a further reduction in cell viability (Fig. 8J), reinforcing the notion that KBTBD11 suppresses tumor cell proliferation via ENO1-dependent glycolytic inhibition.

KBTBD11-induced glycolytic suppression promotes ROS accumulation and apoptosis

To investigate whether KBTBD11-mediated metabolic restraint triggers oxidative stress, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) were measured using DCFH-DA staining followed by flow cytometry. KBTBD11 overexpression increased ROS in both Huh7 and Hepa1-6 cells, whereas ENO1 re-expression attenuated this rise (Fig. 9A), indicating that ROS accumulation is downstream of ENO1 destabilization and reduced glycolytic flux. We next assessed cell death by Annexin V/propidium iodide staining; KBTBD11 overexpression significantly increased apoptosis relative to controls (Fig. 9B). These findings imply that KBTBD11 facilitates apoptosis through the induction of oxidative stress resulting from glycolytic suppression.

Discussion

Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer [11], particularly in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [12, 13], where enhanced aerobic glycolysis supports malignant proliferation under hypoxic and nutrient-stressed conditions. In this study, we identify KBTBD11 as a previously uncharacterized suppressor of glycolytic metabolism and tumor progression in HCC.

Using a genome-scale CRISPR-Cas9 screening approach [14], we identified KBTBD11 as a negative regulator of HCC cell proliferation. Subsequent analysis of multiple transcriptomic datasets revealed that KBTBD11 expression is frequently downregulated in HCC tissues, and its low expression is associated with poor clinical outcomes. Functionally, KBTBD11 overexpression significantly inhibited HCC cell growth, colony formation, and invasive ability in vitro, and reduced tumor burden in an orthotopic mouse model.

Using p53-deficient Huh7 cells [15], we minimized compensatory metabolic responses mediated by the p53 axis, thereby isolating the glycolytic effects of KBTBD11 overexpression. Our transcriptomic and proteomic analyses consistently revealed downregulation of glycolysis-related pathways upon KBTBD11 upregulation. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that KBTBD11 physically interacts with ENO1 (α-enolase), a key enzyme in the glycolytic cascade, and promotes its degradation via a ubiquitin-dependent mechanism. ENO1 catalyzes the conversion of 2-phosphoglycerate to phosphoenolpyruvate and is often overexpressed in aggressive HCC [16], where it enhances metabolic flux and tumor cell invasiveness. While ENO1 degradation via the proteasome has been described in other cancer contexts, the upstream regulators of ENO1 turnover in HCC have remained unclear.

Our findings suggest that KBTBD11 may facilitate ENO1 degradation by influencing the ubiquitin–proteasome system, although the precise molecular components involved are yet to be defined. Although the underlying mechanism remains undefined, KBTBD11 may influence ubiquitin-related pathways, potentially through its BTB domain. Future investigations are needed to clarify its exact role in this regulatory process.

Our results also point to a potential regulatory axis involving MYC. Previous reports in colorectal cancer identified a polymorphic MYC-binding element (rs11777210) through which MYC represses KBTBD11 transcription [10]. Given MYC’s well-established role in activating glycolytic enzymes, including ENO1, our study raises the possibility that KBTBD11 counterbalances MYC-driven metabolic reprogramming by destabilizing ENO1 [16, 17]. Although this MYC–KBTBD11–ENO1 axis remains to be directly tested in HCC, our findings highlight a mechanistically plausible link between oncogenic signaling, metabolic control, and protein turnover.

Clinically, both MYC amplification [18–22] and ENO1 overexpression are common in advanced HCC. Targeting glycolytic metabolism remains an appealing therapeutic strategy, but direct inhibition of metabolic enzymes often results in systemic toxicity. Our study opens the door to alternative approaches: restoring KBTBD11 function or mimicking its activity may offer a more selective means to attenuate glycolysis in MYC- and ENO1-driven tumors. Furthermore, proteolysis-targeting therapies such as PROTACs or molecular glues designed to enhance ENO1 degradation—either through KBTBD11-mediated mechanisms or synthetic substitutes—could expand the therapeutic repertoire against metabolically active liver cancers.

In conclusion, this study characterizes KBTBD11 as a suppressor of glycolysis and HCC progression through a ubiquitin-associated mechanism targeting ENO1. These findings uncover a previously unrecognized axis of metabolic regulation and indicate that targeting protein degradation machinery may offer a viable strategy for therapeutic intervention in metabolically driven liver cancers.

Conclusions

In summary, our study identifies KBTBD11 as a previously unrecognized tumor suppressor in hepatocellular carcinoma. Through integrated CRISPR screening, functional assays, and molecular interaction analyses, we demonstrate that KBTBD11 interacts with and promotes the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of ENO1, a key glycolytic enzyme. This regulatory axis suppresses glycolytic metabolism, elevates oxidative stress, and induces apoptosis in tumor cells. Our findings reveal a novel link between the ubiquitin–proteasome system and metabolic regulation in liver cancer, providing mechanistic insights into HCC progression and suggesting KBTBD11 as a potential therapeutic target for metabolically active tumors.
