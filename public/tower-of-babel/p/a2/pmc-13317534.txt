Bilateral intradiploic dermoid cysts with unilateral rupture causing hearing loss: illustrative case
Authors: Saaya Maruyama, Kiyoyuki Yanaka, Minami Saura, Toshihide Takahashi, Hitoshi Aiyama, Nobuyuki Takahashi, Noriaki Sakamoto, Aiki Marushima, Eiichi Ishikawa

Dermoid cysts are congenital lesions that arise from ectodermal inclusions during embryogenesis. They typically occur along midline structures, including the frontonasal region, posterior fossa, or spinal axis.1,2 Intradiploic dermoid cysts (which are located within the diploic space of cranial bones) are rare, and occipital bone involvement is particularly uncommon. To the best of our knowledge, only two such cases have been reported,3,4 underscoring the rarity of this location.

These lesions often remain asymptomatic unless they rupture, releasing lipid- and keratin-rich debris into the intracranial space. Rupture can also cause chemical meningitis, hydrocephalus, or cranial nerve dysfunction because of chronic inflammation.5 Presentation with isolated sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) is extremely rare. Only one report has suggested a similar mechanism—fatty debris migration into the inner ear via the vestibular aqueduct (VA).6 Furthermore, bilateral, nearly symmetric, intradiploic dermoid cysts of the occipital bone have not been reported.

Herein, we present a unique case of bilateral intradiploic dermoid cysts with near-mirror symmetry in the occipital bone, one of which ruptured into the posterior fossa and caused isolated SNHL. This case highlights an uncommon clinical manifestation and exceptionally rare anatomical configuration.

Illustrative Case

A 21-year-old woman presented to an otolaryngologist with sudden right-sided hearing loss. The results of pure tone audiometry demonstrated profound right-sided SNHL across all frequencies. The otolaryngologist assessed the prognosis for hearing recovery as poor. Despite corticosteroid therapy, the patient’s hearing continued to deteriorate. The results of auditory brainstem response testing showed no waves, and MRI was performed, leading to referral to the neurosurgery department.

No symptoms of meningeal irritation (e.g., headache or nausea) were observed. CT revealed bilateral, nearly symmetric, cystic lesions located within the diploic space of the occipital bone (Fig. 1). On the right side, the lesion had eroded the outer and inner tables of the skull and ruptured into the intracranial compartment, forming a continuous tract from the subcutaneous tissue to the posterior fossa. Moreover, the lesion extended above and below the transverse sinus, creating a tunnel-like configuration through the occipital bone. Intracranially, the disseminated contents appeared predominantly hypodense (consistent with lipid material), with some focal areas of high attenuation, possibly representing keratinous debris. Meanwhile, on the left side, the cystic lesion demonstrated similar characteristics within the diploic space, with contents resembling those of the right-sided lesion. However, compared with the right-sided lesion, the outer table of the left-sided lesion was preserved, and the inner table was eroded. Moreover, no evidence of rupture into the intracranial space was found. In addition, no VA enlargement was seen on either side. MRI revealed a T1-hyperintense signal consistent with fat scattered over the surface of the right cerebellar hemisphere and tentorium, indicating the dissemination of ruptured cyst contents into the posterior fossa (Fig. 2A–C). Importantly, T1-weighted imaging revealed a linear area of high signal intensity along the course of the right VA and within the adjacent inner ear structures. The lesions abutted the transverse sinus. This signal was suppressed on fat-suppressed sequences, confirming the presence of lipid-containing material (Fig. 2D–F).

The patient underwent resection of both lesions under general anesthesia. Initially, a right suboccipital craniotomy was performed to address the ruptured lesion. Intraoperatively, the cyst was found to extend from the subcutaneous tissue, through the diploic space of the occipital bone, and into the posterior fossa, creating a continuous tract. Moreover, the cyst wall was primarily located between the outer and inner dural layers and contained sebaceous material and numerous hair fragments, consistent with a dermoid cyst.

Notably, the cyst extended across and was in direct contact with the transverse sinus. The cyst wall was removed en bloc with the outer dural layer, where possible, to minimize the risk of venous injury. Furthermore, the portion adherent to or overlying the sinus was carefully dissected to preserve venous integrity. After the dura was opened, fatty droplets consistent with cyst contents were observed floating in the CSF. Yellowish, soft material was distributed over the surface of the right cerebellar hemisphere and tentorium. Because the material was strongly adherent to the brain surface, it was left in place to avoid parenchymal injury. Meanwhile, loosely attached debris was carefully removed (Fig. 3).

A limited craniectomy was performed over the left occipital lesion following closure of the right craniotomy. This cyst also extended into the diploic space and abutted the contralateral transverse sinus. Similar to the right-sided lesion, the cyst wall and contents of the left-sided lesion were removed as extensively and safely as possible, with conservative dissection near the sinus to avoid complications. MRI revealed total resection of the intraosseous cyst without sinus injury (Fig. 4).

Small residual fatty droplets on the cerebellar surface show no progression or mass effect.

Postoperatively, the patient recovered without complications. Although her hearing loss persisted at the 18-month follow-up visit, no new neurological deficits or signs of chemical meningitis or recurrence were observed. Histological examination confirmed that both lesions were dermoid cysts with stratified squamous epithelium, hair follicles, and sebaceous glands (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Observations

Although there are several mechanisms that can explain hearing loss from dermoid cysts, most were not relevant in our case. For example, direct compression of the brainstem or cochlear nuclei or extension into the cerebellopontine angle was not evident from our patient’s imaging. Instead, the migration of lipid-laden debris into the inner ear via the VA is the most plausible explanation for our patient’s SNHL, as hypothesized by a previous study,6 which is supported by our unique MRI findings of T1-hyperintense signal along the VA. Although the endolymphatic sac is typically a closed system,6 our patient’s cyst extended continuously across the extradural and intradural compartments surrounding the transverse sinus. This anatomical configuration suggests erosion of the extraosseous portion of the endolymphatic sac. Moreover, subsequent cyst rupture likely resulted in spillage of lipid metabolites into the endolymphatic system, leading to hearing loss. The pathophysiological mechanism of SNHL in this case likely involves multiple factors. First, lipid metabolites entering the endolymphatic system may alter the ionic composition of endolymph, disrupting the high potassium concentration essential for hair cell function. Second, fatty acids and keratin debris can trigger local inflammation within the membranous labyrinth, causing direct cytotoxic damage to cochlear hair cells. Third, the physical presence of lipid material may obstruct endolymphatic flow, leading to hydrops and mechanical distortion of cochlear structures. The profound, pan-frequency nature of hearing loss in our patient suggests diffuse involvement of the cochlea rather than focal damage, consistent with chemical injury affecting the entire organ of Corti. The lack of improvement despite corticosteroid therapy and surgical removal indicates that irreversible structural damage had already occurred before intervention, underscoring the importance of early detection.

Our case is also notable because of its extreme rarity compared with previous reports of ruptured intradiploic dermoid cysts and lipid migration. To the best of our knowledge, this case represents only the third reported instance of an intracranially ruptured intradiploic dermoid cyst and the second wherein lipid migration into the vestibular system resulted in SNHL. Although two other cases of intradiploic dermoid cysts rupturing into the posterior fossa have been reported, both resulted in chemical meningitis because of subarachnoid fat dissemination.7,8 Notably, our patient’s unique presentation of isolated SNHL without meningitis or hydrocephalus further highlights the diverse clinical outcomes of cyst rupture and suggests that the VA is a plausible route for lipid migration. Two additional cases of intradiploic dermoid cysts with an occipital location have been reported; however, neither involved inner ear dysfunction.3,4 One of these cases was a rare cause of intracranial hypertension.3

Furthermore, the bilateral, nearly symmetric, occurrence of dermoid cysts within the occipital bone is highly unusual and, to the best of our knowledge, has not been reported in this distribution. A summary of previously reported intradiploic dermoid cysts with posterior fossa involvement is provided in Supplementary Table 1. This pattern suggests a distinct embryological mechanism. It may reflect ectodermal sequestration at paired ossification centers or along paramedian fusion planes during early calvarial development, which is distinct from the typical midline origin of most dermoid cysts. The occipital bone forms from multiple ossification centers—both membranous and cartilaginous—that eventually fuse. The aberrant inclusion of ectodermal elements during this process could explain the location and symmetry seen here.9

Finally, the surgical management of the present case underscores the importance of balancing removal of the antigenic source with minimizing neurological and vascular risks. Maximal safe resection is recommended when dermoid cysts are tightly adhered to critical neurovascular structures as subtotal resection rarely results in recurrence or progression.10 The clinical significance of removing disseminated fatty droplets remains unclear, and complete removal is often technically unfeasible. In our case, the removal of lipid material from the inner ear was impossible, and the remaining droplets in the posterior fossa were asymptomatic. Therefore, resection was limited to safely accessible areas.

Considering the lesions’ bilateral nature and their proximity to the transverse sinus, we used a staged bilateral approach within a single operation. The ruptured right-sided lesion underwent subtotal resection with removal of the cyst wall and accessible contents while carefully preserving the adherent portion overlying the transverse sinus. Given the radiological evidence of inner table erosion and the risk of future rupture, a prophylactic resection of the contralateral unruptured lesion was also performed. This tailored surgical strategy, prioritizing venous sinus preservation while addressing symptomatic and potentially symptomatic lesions, proved safe and effective. The patient experienced no new neurological deficits or complications postoperatively, supporting this approach for complex bilateral intradiploic dermoid cysts.

Lessons

This case shows that a dermoid cyst, although typically midline and asymptomatic, can present with isolated SNHL when ruptured, especially via fat migration through the VA. Bilateral, nearly symmetric, intradiploic lesions in the occipital bone are exceedingly rare and may reflect embryological anomalies at paired ossification centers. Surgical removal may still be warranted to prevent delayed complications, even in the absence of meningitis or hydrocephalus. Clinicians should also consider atypical dermoid cyst rupture in young patients with unexplained SNHL and suggestive imaging findings.
