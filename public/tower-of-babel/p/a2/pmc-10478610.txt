The effect of aerobic exercise training on asthma control in postmenopausal women (ATOM): a randomized controlled pilot study
Authors: Erik Sören Halvard Hansen, Hanne Kruuse Rasmusen, Morten Hostrup, Ylva Hellsten, Vibeke Backer
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo evaluate if high-intensity interval training three times weekly for 12 weeks improves asthma control in overweight, postmenopausal women with uncontrolled, late-onset asthma.MethodsThe reported study is a randomized clinical pilot study (www.clinicaltrials.gov; NCT03747211) that compared 12 weeks of high-intensity interval training (spinning) with usual care. The five-question Asthma Control Questionnaire (ACQ-5) was used as primary outcome. Secondary measures included systemic inflammation and inflammation of the airways, body composition, and cardiac function during exercise.ResultsWe included 12 women with asthma (mean age 65 years (SD 6); mean body mass index 30 kg/m2 (SD 2)) from whom eight were randomized to exercise and four to control. Baseline ACQ-5 was 1.95 (SD 0.53) in the control group and 2.03 (0.54) in the exercise group. Patients had a mean blood eosinophil level of 0.16 × 109cells/L (SD 0.07) and a mean fraction of exhaled nitric oxide of 23 ppb (SD 25). Mixed models showed that participants in the exercise group reduced their ACQ-5 by 0.55 points (95%CI −1.10 to −0.00; P = 0.08) compared with the control group. The exercise group significantly reduced their mean body fat percentage (−2.7%; 95%CI −4.5 to −0.8; P = 0.02), fat mass (−2.8 kg; 95%CI −5.1 to −0.4; P = 0.044) and android fat mass (−0.33 kg; 95%CI −0.60- −0.06; P = 0.038). In analyses of cardiac measures, we saw no significant effects on right ventricular function (fractional area change), diastolic function or left ventricular function.ConclusionsAlthough changes in ACQ-5 were slightly insignificant, these preliminary findings indicate that aerobic exercise training can be used as a means to improve asthma control in overweight, postmenopausal women with asthma.KEYWORDS: Aerobic exercise, asthma, exercise as treatment, obesity, menopause

Introduction

Asthma is characterized by different phenotypes with different inflammatory profiles [1–3]. Many patients with asthma are women with late onset of disease and they seldom experience asthma remission. Late-onset asthma in women is often characterized by a heavy symptom burden despite extensive treatment with glucocorticoids and β2-agonists [4–6]. This leads to reduced quality of life, more severe asthma [2], and increased rates of hospital visits due to exacerbations and strongly emphasizes the need for new treatment strategies in asthma management [7,8].

Menopause is associated with increased rates of asthma exacerbations [9] and decreased forced vital capacity [10]. The mechanisms of the detrimental effect of menopause on asthma are not known, but one explanation could be weight gain, which is a common complaint in menopause and obesity has been linked with asthma severity [11–14]. However, the weight gain in menopause could be primarily associated with age, while the redistribution of fat tissue to the visceral regions is suggested as the reason for the increased weight-circumference [15,16].

Menopause and asthma are related to an increased risk of cardiac disease [15,17–19]. Women are further known to have symptoms of ischemic heart disease (e.g. chest tightness) without confirmed obstructive coronary artery disease, which in theory could be asthma [20]. Furthermore, asthma is associated with heart-related alterations like reduced right ventricular function, potentially resulting from heightened pressure in the pulmonary artery due to the obstructive pulmonary effects of poorly managed asthma [21,22].

Lifestyle modifications that include aerobic exercise training have demonstrated improved asthma control in diverse groups of asthma patients. A recent meta-analysis also revealed that consistent physical activity positively impacts asthma control [23,24]. Nonetheless, the degree to which exercise training can alleviate symptoms of asthma in postmenopausal women with late onset of disease and offset the physiological alterations occurring during menopause including cardiac adaptations, remains uncertain. Therefore, the primary aim of this pilot study was to assess if regular aerobic exercise training improves asthma control in postmenopausal women with uncontrolled asthma.

Materials and methods

We investigated whether 12 weeks of regular aerobic exercise (high-intensity interval training (HIIT)) could improve asthma control in overweight and obese, postmenopausal women with asthma as measured by the five-question Asthma Control Questionnaire (ACQ-5). Further, we assessed if a change in asthma control could be related to objective changes in systemic inflammation, airway inflammation, airway hyperresponsiveness, body composition and right ventricular (RV) function.

Trial design

The reported pilot study is a single-blinded, randomized controlled intervention study that was conducted at the Centre for Physical Activity Research, Rigshospitalet, Denmark. Patients were recruited from 1 March 2019, until 1 May 2021. Results are reported by the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines [25]. Last participant entered the study in December 2020. The aim was to include 40 postmenopausal women with late-onset asthma (>16 years at debut). Participants were block-randomized to either 12 weeks of aerobic exercise three times weekly or usual care. The methods of this trial have been described in detail previously [26]. The project was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT037420 November 7211, 2018) and was approved by the Ethics Committee in the Capital Region of Denmark nr. H-18028966 and the Danish Data Protection Agency nr. VD-2019-59.

Participants

To be included, participants had to be women in regular treatment with inhaled corticosteroids for asthma aged 45 to 75 years with an ACQ-5-score ≥1.25 and postmenopausal (six months without menstruation, Serum-Follicular Stimulating Hormone > 20 IE/L and Plasma-Estradiol <0.09 nmol/L). Further, they had to have a body mass index (BMI) 25–35 kg/m2 and an inactive lifestyle (<1 hours of vigorous exercise per week). To participate, participants had have a medical history of or a current positive bronchial challenge to methacholine or mannitol; a positive reversibility test to β2-agonist; documented peak flow variation or a positive eucapnic voluntary hyperventilation test.

Potential participants were excluded if they were current smokers or had smoked more than 20 pack years (20 cigarettes daily for 20 years); if they had pre-existing pulmonary disease other than asthma, if they had unstable cardiac disease or any other disease with a possibly significant effect on the trial.

Interventions

Following randomization, all participants received text messages three times weekly reminding them to take their daily anti-asthma medication. Participants in the intervention group underwent 12 weeks of regular HIIT training (spinning) three times weekly with each session lasting 45 minutes. All training sessions were supervised by a trained instructor. Intensity was regulated by heart rate monitors as % of maximal heart rate (HRmax) achieved at maximal oxygen consumption test at baseline. Participants in the control group received no further intervention [26]. The intervention has been described in detail previously [26].

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this trial was a change from baseline in ACQ-5 [27]. Secondary outcomes included changes from baseline in asthma quality of life measured by mini-asthma quality-of-life questionnaire (miniAQLQ); anxiety and depression measured by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS); dysfunctional breathing measured by Nijmegen questionnaire; airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) to methacholine; airway inflammation measured by fraction of exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO); changes in forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) measured by spirometry; maximal oxygen consumption (VO2max) and body composition measured by a Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scan (DEXA). Further, we measured diastolic function (E/e*) and right ventricular function (fractional area change (FAC)) during rest and increasing workloads using stress echocardiography and resting transthoracic echocardiography. Size and ratio estimates were also presented and corrected for body surface area (m2). Outcomes have been described in detail previously [26].

Sample size

The number of participants in each group (N = 20) was based on a power calculation for the primary outcome which is an ACQ-5 score reduction ≥ 0.5 (SD 0.5) with 20% dropout and 80% chance (β) of detecting significant difference compared to controls. A reduction in ACQ-5-score by 0.5 is considered clinically relevant [28]. The drop-out rate was expected to be 20% based on a previous Danish exercise study with asthma patients where 16% of patients dropped out [23].

Randomization

Randomization was done at the Department of Nutrition, Exercise and Sports by a study member who did not have any participant contact. Participants were equally randomized to exercise or no exercise in random block sizes. The randomization list was generated using the list generator (Version 1; Seed 4,308,536,499,980) from Sealed Envelope Ltd. Stratification was done for ACQ-5-score (cut-off 1.75) and BMI (cut-off 30.0 kg/m2).

Blinding

Due to the nature of the intervention, participants could not be blinded. The individual performing all investigations and measures of primary and secondary outcomes was blinded.

Statistical methods

Random effects, mixed linear regression models were used to estimate the effect of training versus control in primary and secondary outcome measures. All analyses were presented with unadjusted and adjusted estimates. In adjusted analyses, baseline value of the given outcome was included. Random effects were included for each participant, and we visually and analytically checked all variables for normal distribution, and applied transformations such as logarithms to those that were skewed to achieve normality before including them in our regression models. Demographic data were analysed by descriptive statistics. Chi-square was used for frequency distributions and Student’s t-test for continuous variables. Analyses were performed by the intention-to-treat principle with last observation (baseline examinations) carried forward in case of drop-out. R Version 4.0 was used for statistical analysis and packages lme4 (Version 1.1–27) and ggplot2(Version 3.3.3) for mixed models and visualisations, respectively [29].

Deviations from protocol

Due to the COVID-19 pandemic measures of heart rate variability (facility restrictions) and airway inflammation by sputum (laboratory restrictions) had to be excluded from the outcome measures. Further, during the COVID-19 lockdown, one participant underwent home-based training. The project had to be prematurely terminated due to unsatisfactory recruitment during the pandemic and work-out facility restrictions. Further, two participants in the control group did not do lung function testing following the intervention period and the results were thus imputed as described. Therefore, we consider this study a pilot study.

Results

We included 12 women with late-onset asthma in this study (Figure 1). By chance, participants were allocated 2:1, with 8 participants receiving the intervention, and 4 participants receiving usual care. Adherence to the intervention was > 90% (33/36 sessions) for all participants. Participants had a mean (SD) age of 64.9 years (5.8) and a BMI of 30.0 (2.3) (Table 1). Further, they had normal levels of FeNO (22.9 ppb; SD 25.0) and normal levels of blood eosinophilia (0.16 109/L; SD 0.07). Participants had a normal left ventricular function (mean ejection fraction 60.7% (7.0)). Right ventricular function, as measured by fractional area change (FAC), was 41.6% (7.6). E/e*, a marker of diastolic function, was 8.9 (4.0) (Table 2). Participants in the intervention group had higher lung function (FEV1exercise 2.34 L (SD 0.48) vs. FEV1control 1.87 L (SD 0.48)) and VO2max (Exercise: 2040 ml/min (SD 363) vs. Control: 1660 ml/min (SD 164)).

Primary outcome – ACQ-5

In a mixed model analysis adjusted for baseline level of ACQ-5, participants in the exercise group had better disease control following 12 weeks of exercise. ACQ-5 in the exercise group was reduced by 0.55 points (95%CI −1.06- −0.04; P = 0.06) compared with the control group (Figure 2). Unadjusted estimates were similar (−0.55; 95%CI −1.10- −0.00; P = 0.08).

Secondary outcomes

In analyses of secondary outcomes, we found significant effects of exercise on general fat mass and android fat mass (Table 3). Compared with the control group, participants in the exercise group reduced their general body fat by 2.8 kg (95%CI −4.8- −0.8; P = 0.022) and their android body fat by 0.33 kg (95%CI −0.60- −0.06; P = 0.038). In univariate analysis, the changes in adipose tissue and android fat mass were not associated with a significant reduction in ACQ-5 (−0.02 points/kg, 95%CI −0.13-0.18; P = 0.79 and 0.57 points/kg; 95%CI −0.55-1.70; P = 0.34, respectively). We found no significant effects of exercise on lung function, airway inflammation or airway hyperresponsiveness (Table 3; Figure 2–4).

Discussion

In this randomized controlled trial investigating aerobic exercise as a treatment for asthma, we found that 12 weeks of strenuous aerobic exercise tended to improve asthma control. Further, this improvement happened simultaneously with a significant reduction in adipose tissue.

Although the change in asthma control was insignificant, we saw a clear tendency towards beneficial effects of exercise in our study population. It is likely that the insignificant effect estimates were due to the low power of the analyses as a result of the low number of included participants – which is a limitation of our study and the reason we classify it as a pilot study. For more than 6 months during the COVID-19 pandemic, we could not include participants, and when we were allowed, many potential participants were afraid of the potentially fatal outcome of participation in training sessions if infected with SARS-COV-2 combined with difficult-to-treat asthma. In a sample size calculation taking offset in our results, we found that 20 included participants would have been enough to show a significant effect of exercise in this population (80% power and significance level 5%). However, due the small sample size, it is possible that further participants would have made the results insignificant which is a severe limitation when trying to extrapolate the results from our study to the general asthma population.

Throughout the study period, we evaluated 34 women for inclusion, using stratification by ACQ-5-score at baseline to avoid potential large between-group differences in our primary outcome. This approach proved effective (Table 1, e-Table 1). To account for potential impacts on study outcomes, we also stratified for BMI, aiming to minimize significant differences in body composition. This stratification, however, did not influence the inclusion process. Of the 34 individuals screened, 22 were excluded, primarily due to an ACQ-5-score below 1.25 at baseline and a BMI less than 25 kg/m2. Although altering our BMI inclusion criteria would have permitted more participants, such a modification was not feasible given our study hypothesis focusing on the influences of fat mass and obesity. Our results are in line with recent meta-analyses investigating the effect of exercise on asthma control, lung function, airway inflammation and exercise capacity [24,30]. Despite our results not being entirely significant, our estimates point in the same direction as both meta-analyses. Further, since our effect estimate of asthma control is clinically significant (>0.5 points), this study raises the possibility that exercise can be especially beneficial in postmenopausal women with asthma and could be proposed as add-on management in this patient group. Compared with some of the previous studies, the included group is generally older and obese [24,30]. This could explain the potentially substantial effect. However, larger studies are warranted to confirm this statement. It is possible that our choice of using mixed linear effects models could lead to overestimation of the effects. Therefore, we have included both the adjusted and unadjusted results to provide clean results free of potential overfitted models and models adjusted for relevant participants baseline values which can be used to guide further studies.

Although our study population reported high levels of medication adherence, and we tried to control for it by reminding both groups to take their medication, it is possible that the effects observed of exercise are due to increased compliance among those exercising. In exercise trials, it would be useful if an objective measure of adherence (e.g. blood sample) could be included as the validity of self-reported adherence to ICS can be very limited [31]. However, the possibility of using such measures for adherence in patients only receiving ICS is still under review [32]. A further limitation is that the participants could not be blinded. As the participants were not blinded, it is possible that knowing that you are participating in exercise with others and following a personal trainer could have beneficial effects on perceived asthma symptoms as measured by the ACQ-5. This could be partly responsible for the difference between the groups. Further studies on the mechanisms of the beneficial effects of exercise are thus warranted. Another limitation of our study is that the groups showed variability on a number of parameters at baseline (e.g. IgE, FeNO and FEV1) which could skew the results.

In our data, cardiac adaptations of the right ventricle and diastolic function did not seem to be responsible for symptoms at baseline nor for the possible changes in disease control observed in this patient group. However, because of the low number of participants, it cannot be determined. A previous study showed better right ventricular function and diastolic function in a group of sedentary women [33]. Participants in this study were generally younger (30–45 years) and it could be that age influences the different cardiac responses to exercise. Further, we observed that the patient characteristics with uncontrolled asthma and obesity in our study make echocardiographic measurements challenging – especially during increasing workloads. Other studies considering using echocardiography in similar study participants should make use of contrast agents to better visualize the different structures in order to collect optimal data [34].

Obesity is associated with low-grade inflammation and not typically Type 2-high inflammation, which is the most common inflammation in asthma [35]. In our study, the low levels of Type 2-inflammation in the airways may be influenced by the inclusion criterion requiring participants to be on regular ICS. This treatment could potentially ‘mask’ Type 2 inflammation in the airways. Furthermore, our participants had low blood-eosinophil counts (<0.30 109 cells/L), which supports our hypothesis that the women included predominantly had Type 2-low asthma. Hence, our study suggests that further research is warranted to explore the role of Type 2-low inflammation in adipose tissue on asthma outcomes. This is particularly pertinent as our patients did not show elevated levels of airway inflammation yet experienced an alleviation of asthma symptoms concurrent with body tissue redistribution.

Obesity has previously been described as an important risk factor for difficult-to-treat asthma disease [11–14] and in obese mice, IL-17A produced by group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3) has shown to induce airway hyperresponsiveness. However, our results suggest that it is not body weight itself that is the issue, but that it is the total amount of fat that is correlated to alleviation of symptoms. The interplay between adipose tissue, ILC3 and asthma must therefore be further explored.

A strength of the current study is that it was randomized with a monitored and supervised training intervention which ensured quality and adherence. Results points towards that this type of intervention would be beneficial for this patient group. It is uncertain whether the potential effects observed in this study are long-lasting, given that participants were assessed immediately following the 12-week program. A recent randomized exercise study in patients with asthma demonstrated that six months of exercise training led to a sustained reduction in ICS dose even six months after the completion of the intervention. This suggests a durable impact of exercise or a change in the training habits of the participants [36]. To better our understanding of the long-term effects of exercise in asthma, follow-up studies should be conducted following exercise intervention studies.

In general, our study weighs into the increasing amounts of evidence suggesting that exercise is a viable treatment of asthma [24,36]. We did not observe any major adverse effects of training nor did the participants who undertook training report respiratory worsenings. This is in line with previous studies showing that exercise in asthma is safe [37]. However, in our current healthcare system, consistently supervised exercise programs aren’t feasible. Therefore, future studies investigating exercise as treatment should consider how to implement exercise into regular treatment. A potential solution could be a supervised ‘learn-to-exercise’ interventions with optional follow-up consultations. Moreover, digital monitoring of prescribed exercises (e.g. through an app or pulse watches) could allow for personalized feedback and patient consultations without disrupting daily schedules.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study did not find a statistically significant effect of 12-week high-intensity interval training on asthma control in obese, postmenopausal women with asthma. Nonetheless, the results point towards a beneficial effect of exercise in line with findings from prior studies. Future research must explore feasible strategies for integrating exercise interventions into clinical practice, as continuous supervision is not sustainable for long-term treatment.
