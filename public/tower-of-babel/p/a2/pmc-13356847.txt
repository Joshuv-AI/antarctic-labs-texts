Spatiotemporal Heterogeneity and the Impact of PM1 and Nitrogen Oxides on Chronic Kidney Disease Mortality in 39 European Countries (1990–2022): A Multi-Model Analysis
Authors: Yuhe Si, Chunbao Mo, Qianqian Zhang, Yuchang Ma, Yukai Ye, Yunhao Jia, Jiahua Qian, Yihao Chen, Dian Wang, Jianxiong Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) has become a significant public health burden in Europe, with marked spatiotemporal heterogeneity. Nitrogen oxides (NOx) and fine particulate matter (PM) are considered important environmental risk factors; however, the association of submicron particulate matter (PM1) with CKD mortality and the potential non-linear exposure–response patterns of these pollutants remain insufficiently characterized.MethodsIn this ecological study, national-level aggregated data from 39 European countries from 1990 to 2022 were analyzed. Environmental exposure was assessed using population-density-weighted exposure estimates, and spatial heterogeneity was analyzed using a Geodetector approach. Linear mixed-effects models (LMM) and generalized additive mixed models (GAMM) were used to investigate the associations between environmental factors and age-standardized CKD mortality rates (ASMR). XGBoost, combined with SHAP interpretation tools, quantified the relative contributions and identified non-linear thresholds. Finally, a partial correlation network analysis revealed the interaction structures among environmental factors.ResultsAfter adjusting for confounders, NOx and particulate matter exposures showed significant positive correlations with CKD mortality risk. LMM revealed a pronounced “particle size effect”: the coefficient for PM1 (β = 0.220 deaths per 100,000 population on the raw ASMR scale) was higher than that for PM2.5 (0.199) and PM10 (0.141). GAMM results supported the robustness of these associations on the logit-transformed proportional scale, and higher HDI was inversely associated with CKD mortality (βIQR = −0.056). XGBoost and LOESS analyses further revealed non-linear characteristics: particulate matter exposure exhibited an S-shaped saturation curve, whereas NOx exposure displayed a J-shaped threshold effect (with risk steeply increasing beyond 9 ppb). Network analysis indicated a strong positive correlation between high temperatures and CKD mortality (r = 0.42), whereas humidity was inversely correlated. Spatial detection further identified PM1 as the strongest explanatory factor of spatial variation in European CKD mortality rates (q = 0.243). Conversely, a higher Human Development Index (HDI) exhibited relatively robust protective effects against CKD mortality (β = −0.056).ConclusionOur findings suggest that CKD mortality in Europe may be associated with NOx and fine PM (particularly PM1) exposure, which exhibit complex non-linear relationships. These findings support further evaluation of submicron particulate matter in environmental monitoring frameworks and suggest that transport-related pollution and heat-related climatic conditions warrant attention in strategies aimed at reducing regional CKD mortality disparities.Keywords: chronic kidney disease, PM1, nitrogen oxides, spatiotemporal heterogeneity, non-linear relationship, Europe

Background

Environmental air pollution and chronic kidney disease (CKD) have emerged as major global public health challenges, imposing an increasingly heavy burden on healthcare systems. Recent international consensus indicates that CKD affects more than 850 million individuals worldwide, posing a far greater threat than previously estimated.1 According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, CKD results in approximately 1.2 million deaths and 38.8 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) annually, with global CKD mortality having increased by 41.5% since 1990. It is currently the 12th leading cause of death globally and is projected to rise to 5th by 2040.2 The epidemiological characteristics of CKD exhibit significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity,3 suggesting that the driving factors are far more complex than previously recognized.

Traditionally, diabetes, hypertension, and smoking have been identified as the primary risk factors for CKD, contributing over 50% of the disease burden.4,5 However, these factors alone appear insufficient to explain the regional inequalities in CKD burden; for instance, increases in mortality rates in Latin America and Central Asia have far exceeded the global average.2 In recent years, “Environmental Nephrology” has emerged as an interdisciplinary field that emphasizes the critical role of environmental exposure in the onset and progression of CKD. Extensive epidemiological evidence, including large-scale cohort studies from the United States, United Kingdom, and Asia, has confirmed that long-term exposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and nitrogen oxides (NOx) significantly increases the risk of kidney function decline and incident CKD through pathways of systemic inflammation and oxidative stress.6–8 Specifically, data from the US ARIC cohort demonstrated robust associations between PM2.5 and increased albuminuria as well as incident CKD,6 while recent prospective evidence from the UK Biobank cohort demonstrated that ambient air pollution is a critical determinant driving the dynamic, multi-stage progression from healthy status to the multimorbidity and mortality of hypertension, diabetes, and CKD.9 From a pathophysiological perspective, long-term inhalation of these pollutants triggers systemic oxidative stress and acute endothelial dysfunction, which subsequently accelerate kidney microvascular damage and promote adverse cardio-renal-metabolic interactions.10 This shifting paradigm, highlighting the profound impact of non-traditional environmental hazards on progressive kidney impairment, has been extensively recognized in recent comprehensive reviews of environmental nephrology.11,12

Despite the growing body of research on the relationship between environmental pollutants and CKD, several important gaps remain. First, geographic coverage is limited: most studies have focused on single countries or regions (such as the United States, South Korea, Taiwan, and China), while comprehensive assessments across Europe—a continent that provides a unique “natural experiment” due to its diverse air quality regulations and highly varying climate zones (from the Nordic regions to the Mediterranean)—remain scarce.11,13 Crucially, within this changing geographic and climatic landscape, the intersection between air pollution and climate-related kidney vulnerability is becoming increasingly severe. As global temperatures rise, recurrent extreme heat exposure combined with physical exertion and inadequate hydration triggers intracellular pathways of dehydration and volume loss, leading to an emergent global epidemic of heat stress nephropathy that operates independently of traditional risk factors.14 How these climate-induced thermal stressors interact with ambient air pollution to exacerbate CKD mortality across European populations remains a critical, unaddressed knowledge gap. Second, insufficient attention has been given to particle size: previous studies have primarily focused on PM2.5. Whether submicron particulate matter (PM1), which has a smaller diameter and higher permeability, represents an independent risk factor for CKD mortality remains a significant research gap.8,15 From a toxicological perspective, the sub-micron scale of PM1 confers a substantially higher surface-area-to-volume ratio compared to larger particles, drastically enhancing its capacity for the adsorption and translocation of toxic heavy metals into the systemic circulation. Crucially, the breakdown of the glomerular filtration barrier (GFB)—a highly specialized structure maintained by podocytes and complex cellular networks—is a fundamental pathological hallmark of progressive kidney diseases.16 The heavy-metal-laden PM1 may directly target and disrupt this critical filtration barrier, potentially leading to accelerated kidney deterioration. Third, methodological limitations persist: traditional studies have predominantly employed linear models. Such methods may obscure potential non-linear relationships or threshold effects between air pollution and kidney outcomes, leading to misinterpretations in risk assessment.17,18

To address these gaps, this study aims to explore the association between ambient air pollution and CKD mortality using multi-country data from 39 European countries. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to employ a triangulated approach—integrating spatial detection, statistical inference, and machine learning—to systematically investigate environmental health impacts, with a specific focus on PM1 within the European context. Within this innovative analytical framework, we optimize the exposure assessment precision through population density weighting and cross-validate the results using both linear and non-linear models. This approach enables a comprehensive evaluation of the effects of various pollutants, including PM1, PM2.5, PM10, and NOx, on CKD mortality beyond traditional risk factors. By clarifying the specific contributions of different pollutants to CKD mortality burden, this study provides a scientific foundation for formulating targeted environmental health policies and CKD prevention and control strategies. Notably, while this large-scale analysis offers valuable macro-level insights across 39 countries, we explicitly acknowledge the inherent limitations of its ecological design. Because our exposure assessments rely on aggregated, national-level estimates, they may not capture individual-level exposure patterns, personal mobility, or localized microenvironments. Consequently, the observed associations represent population-level distributions, and strict causal inference remains limited within this ecological framework.

Methods

Data Sources

Data Acquisition

Age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR) of CKD across European countries were retrieved from the European Health for All Database (HFA-DB). CKD is defined by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines as abnormalities in kidney structure or function present for more than three months with health implications, typically manifested by a decreased estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and an elevated urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR). Within the established research framework, we integrated datasets of potential driving factors from multiple European countries and regions. Using a comprehensive suite of international databases, we investigated the associations between CKD ASMR and various determinants across 39 countries from 1990 to 2022. The variables included socioeconomic, environmental, and health indicators. To ensure statistical robustness, territories or nations with an extremely high proportion of missing values in meteorological or epidemiological records (such as Greenland, the Faroe Islands, and the Åland Islands within the Nordic region) were systematically excluded during the initial data-screening phase.

The Human Development Index (HDI), which represents socioeconomic status, was obtained from the United Nations Development Program (UNDP) Human Development Reports. Environmental factors—including 2-meter temperature, ambient air pollutants, surface pressure, relative humidity, 10-meter wind speed, and downward ultraviolet (UV) radiation at the surface—were obtained from the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS), ERA4, and ERA5. Data on total alcohol consumption per capita were obtained from the Global Information System on Alcohol and Health (GISAH). Age-standardized prevalence of current tobacco use was obtained from the WHO Global Health Observatory. Additionally, the ASMR for diabetes mellitus was retrieved from the HFA-DB.

Data Cleaning

Given that data sources did not identical cover historical periods or geographical jurisdictions, we restricted the temporal and spatial scope of our analysis to the periods and regions where data overlap occurred. Missing data within the merged “Health for All” database were addressed using complete case analysis (listwise deletion). To reconcile temporal inconsistencies among the multi-source datasets, higher-frequency environmental data—specifically, hourly meteorological parameters from the ERA5 reanalysis and monthly air pollutant concentrations from CAMS—were temporally aggregated into annual means to align with the annual reporting structure of the health and socioeconomic indicators. Specifically, country-year observations with missing values for CKD mortality or any key environmental, socioeconomic, and lifestyle covariates were excluded from the final analytic sample. Out of 1,386 potential country-year observations, 99 (7.14%) were excluded due to incomplete covariates or missing data. This process yielded a final analytical sample of 1,287 complete country-year observations across 39 countries spanning the period from 1990 to 2022 (Supplementary Table S1) from 1990 to 2022. Because the missingness was relatively low and primarily concentrated in specific country-year observations (eg., early historical years with evolving data collection protocols), this approach was deemed appropriate to minimize potential selection bias while preserving sufficient statistical power.

Confounding Factor Adjustment

To minimize the potential influence of confounding variables, we incorporated several covariates associated with both exposure and outcomes in our analysis of the relationship between various determinants and the ASMR of CKD. These factors included indicators of (1) socioeconomic status and lifestyle: HDI, tobacco prevalence, and per capita alcohol consumption; (2) geographical stratification: national and regional classifications were included to account for geographical differences that may reflect specific local environmental characteristics; and (3) clinical comorbidities: given the unique role of diabetes mellitus (DM) mortality as both a competing risk and a primary comorbidity factor, we applied differentiated handling of DM mortality across various models to avoid over-adjustment bias.

Analytical Methods

Five analytical approaches were applied to evaluate the relationships between environmental exposures and CKD mortality. (1) Spatial heterogeneity analysis: a geographical detector (geodetector) was employed to assess the spatial associations between environmental factors, socioeconomic indicators, and CKD mortality. The explanatory power of each factor for the spatial distribution of CKD mortality was quantified by calculating q-values with continuous variables preanalyzed using quintile discretization. (2) Linear association assessment: linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were constructed to quantify the linear relationship between environmental exposure and CKD mortality. National codes were designated as random effects to correct for baseline heterogeneity within nested hierarchical structures, and stratified adjustment strategies were employed to evaluate the independent effects of environmental factors. (3) Non-linear relationship assessment: generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs) based on β distributions were employed to capture complex non-linear associations. To satisfy the mathematical domain of the β family, the raw ASMR values were transformed into bounded proportions using the Smithson and Verkuilen (2006) method.19,20 The model utilizes a logit link function for the transformed proportional data and incorporates smooth spline functions to control for non-linear confounding factors such as year, HDI, and lifestyle. The comprehensive statistical rationale for the β-GAMM framework, data transformation formulas, and coefficient interpretations are detailed in Supplementary Materials. The results were reported as the effect values corresponding to each interquartile range (IQR) increment. (4) Importance ranking and threshold identification: To mitigate severe multicollinearity among nested particulate matter fractions (r > 0.85), three parallel XGBoost models were separately constructed for PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 (Supplementary Table S2). To reduce the influence of extreme leverage points, a global 1% right-tail truncation was applied to exposure features before model training. A 5-fold cross-validation procedure with a fixed random seed (seed = 123) was then used to evaluate predictive performance. The cross-validation results showed stable predictive performance across the three particulate-specific models, with mean R2 values exceeding 0.85 (Supplementary Table S3). Feature importance was quantified using mean absolute SHAP values, and the directional distribution of SHAP contributions was summarized in Supplementary Tables S4–S6. Non-linear thresholds were identified by applying LOESS smoothing and derivative-based inspection to SHAP dependence plots. (5) Environmental-CKD interaction network analysis: A residual-corrected partial correlation network analysis was performed to reveal the intrinsic interaction structures among the environmental factors, diabetes, and CKD. First, the residual regression eliminated linear confounding from socioeconomic and lifestyle factors. Subsequently, Pearson’s correlation matrices were calculated from the corrected residuals to build an interaction network (Detailed in Supplementary Files “Analytical Methods). Following established frameworks for long-term ecological and epidemiological studies on chronic outcomes, annual mean values were utilized for all environmental variables to capture the sustained, cumulative exposure window.

Global CKD Burden from 1990 to 2022

Significant disparities in CKD ASMR were observed across different countries from 1990 to 2022. The total mortality rate ranged from 1.5302–23.52, while the rates for males and females fluctuated from 2.3234–27.28 and 1.0193–23.04, respectively. In 1990, Greece exhibited the highest mortality rate across the total population (21.7948), males (23.8113), and females (20.4138). Conversely, Ukraine had the lowest rates for all three groups (total: 1.5302; males: 2.3234; females: 1.0193). In 2022, the highest total (23.5239) and female (23.0409) mortality rates were observed in Estonia, while Greece had the highest male mortality rate (26.1716). Ukraine had the lowest mortality rates across all three categories. Over this study period, Poland experienced the most substantial decline in mortality rates across all groups, with decreases of 46.24% in males, 42.86% in the total population, and 40.93% in females. In contrast, Denmark showed the largest increase, with a greater increase observed in females (203.01%) than in males (150.14%) and the total population (183.12%) (Figure 1A and B).

Spatial Heterogeneity Testing via Geodetector

To explore the explanatory power of environmental and socioeconomic factors on the spatial heterogeneity of CKD mortality, this study employed a factor detector analysis within the geodetector framework. The results indicate that particulate matter of various sizes and air temperatures are the primary drivers of spatial variation in CKD ASMR. Among these, PM1 exhibited the strongest spatial explanatory power (q = 0.243, p< 0.001), with the explanatory capacity showing a decreasing trend with increasing particle size (PM1: q = 0.243; PM2.5: q = 0.228; PM10: q = 0.227). Temperature also demonstrated a high level of spatial accuracy (q = 0.224, p < 0.001). Additionally, factors such as UV radiation, diabetes prevalence, and NOx (q = 0.094) showed statistically significant spatial associations (p < 0.05) despite having lower q-values. Conversely, HDI did not demonstrate significant explanatory power in this spatial detection (q = 0.005, p = 0.884), suggesting a weak consistency between its spatial distribution and that of CKD mortality (Figure 2A). To intuitively illustrate these geographical patterns, the spatial overlays of CKD ASMR alongside the primary air pollutants (PM1, PM2.5, PM10, and NOx) across European countries are presented in Figures 2B–E. Screening Key Environmental Risk Factors via LMMs

LMMs accounting for country-level heterogeneity and key confounders showed that air pollution indicators were positively associated with CKD mortality. While regional disparities exist (Figure 3A), NOx exposure exhibited a highly significant positive association with mortality (β = 0.377 per 1-unit increase, P < 0.001, Figure 3B–E); here, the unstandardized β coefficients represent the absolute change in deaths per 100,000 population (raw ASMR scale). Particulate matter also showed a significant size-dependent hazard gradient (P < 0.001): PM1 (β = 0.220) > PM2.5 (β = 0.199) > PM10 (β = 0.141). In contrast, meteorological factors (wind speed, humidity, and pressure) and ozone were generally not significant in the adjusted main model (P > 0.05). Validating Association Robustness via GAMMs

The forest plot displays the analytical results based on GAMMs, where βIQR values quantify the change on the logit-transformed scale of CKD ASMR (modeled as a proportion between 0 and 1) per 1-IQR increase in each factor. These findings indicate that air pollution and smoking are the key drivers of mortality. Smoking emerged as a significant lifestyle risk factor; for every IQR increase (8.65%) in smoking prevalence, the mortality rate increased by 0.030 units on the logit-transformed proportional scale (95% CI, 0.022–0.038). Regarding air pollution, the risk remained significant and robust, with each 1-IQR increase in NOx associated with a 0.027 unit increase on the logit scale. The pathogenic risks of PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 were highly consistent, with βIQR values ranging from 0.026–0.027 on the logit scale; however, smaller particle diameters were associated with higher βIQR. Ozone (βIQR = 0.008 on the logit scale) also showed a weak but significant positive correlation. Conversely, HDI was inversely associated with CKD ASMR on the logit-transformed proportional scale (βIQR = −0.056, 95% CI: −0.075 to −0.037). Meteorological factors such as temperature and humidity were not statistically significant (Figure 4).

Analyzing Non-Linear Dose-Response Relationships via XGBoost and SHAP

To isolate the independent health effects of each component and quantify their non-linear attributions, three independent XGBoost models were refined. SHAP swarm plots revealed that diabetes was the strongest predictor (average |SHAP| 0.938–1.021). Among environmental predictors, particulate matter metrics and NOx showed relatively large SHAP contributions, suggesting their importance in model prediction rather than establishing causal effects. SHAP values for PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 were 0.491, 0.445, and 0.387, respectively, while NOx values ranged from 0.418–0.445 across models.

Dose-response analysis revealed non-linear patterns reflecting pollutant heterogeneity. Risk curves for particulate matter (PM1, PM2.5, PM10) exhibited relatively smooth “S-shaped” profiles, with chronic kidney disease (CKD) mortality risk flattening after reaching specific saturation thresholds. Specifically, the risk growth entered a clear plateau phase at concentrations of 12.08 µg/m3 for PM1, 15.36 µg/m3 for PM2.5, and 22.51 µg/m3 for PM10. In contrast, NOx exhibited a distinct “J-shaped” threshold effect: mortality risk remained relatively stable at lower concentrations (< 9 ppb) but rose sharply once this threshold was exceeded, showing no clear saturation effect (Figure 5). This is consistent with previous epidemiological findings, where the health effects of NOx exhibited a similar non-linear threshold trend.21 Temperature and smoking also significantly contributed to model predictions, as evidenced by their high relative importance or effect estimates. Identifying Environmental Interaction Structures via Partial Correlation Networks

To investigate the interactive structure of environmental factors and their combined impact on CKD mortality, we constructed a partial correlation network adjusted for year, region, HDI, smoking rate, and alcohol consumption (false discovery rate < 0.001). The results visually revealed two major exposure clusters: NOx (r = 0.31) formed a tightly interconnected and highly intensive “pollutant cluster” with PM2.5 (r = 0.31), PM10 (r = 0.31), and PM1 (r = 0.29). This cluster exhibited high internal collinearity (r > 0.97) and demonstrated a significant, moderately positive correlation with CKD mortality, confirming its status as a core environmental risk factor. Notably, the network diagram also captured a strong positive correlation between temperature and CKD (r = 0.42), while relative humidity exhibited a significant protective effect (r = −0.32), and ozone showed a clear positive correlation (r = 0.20) (Figure 6).

Discussion

This study employed multidimensional spatiotemporal analysis and multi-model cross-validation to reveal significant geographical heterogeneity in CKD mortality across Europe. Our findings suggest that NOx and PM1 were consistently associated with regional CKD mortality patterns across multiple analytical frameworks. We validated the robustness of these associations and uncovered non-linear dose-response patterns. Furthermore, we identified potentially complex interactions between ambient temperature, smoking prevalence, and socioeconomic status.

Particle-Size-Related Association Pattern: Potential Relevance of PM1

Our findings suggest that PM1 showed the strongest statistical association with CKD mortality among the particulate fractions evaluated. However, given the nested structure and high collinearity among PM1, PM2.5, and PM10, this should be interpreted as a potential size-related signal rather than definitive evidence that PM1 is independently more harmful. As a major global air pollutant, environmental particulate matter has been positively associated with both the incidence and mortality of CKD.6,22 Previous studies have shown that each 10 μg/m3 increase in exposure to PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 is associated with significantly reduced eGFR and heightened risks of CKD and proteinuria.23 Consistent with these findings, this study confirmed a distinct particle size effect among particulate matter components: the hazard coefficient for PM1 (β = 0.220) exceeded those for PM2.5 (β = 0.199) and PM10 (β = 0.141). This finding resonates with a prior cardiovascular-renal-metabolic study, which reported that PM1 exposure is strongly associated with cardiovascular disease, with risk peaking during stage 3 disease progression (HR = 3.32, 95% CI: 2.24–4.92).24

While our findings support the “smaller is more toxic” phenomenon, caution is warranted when interpreting PM1 as definitively more harmful than PM2.5 or PM10. Due to the substantial spatial overlap and high collinearity among these particulate fractions, disentangling their independent biological effects requires careful interpretation. From a mechanistic perspective, prior toxicological studies suggest that PM1 may have a higher alveolar deposition rate and stronger capacity to penetrate biological barriers than larger particles, providing biological plausibility for the observed epidemiological association.25 Upon entering the circulatory system, their minute diameter enables them to evade mucociliary clearance and macrophage phagocytosis,26,27 potentially facilitating persistent oxidative stress damage in blood vessels and distal organs. These hypothetical pathways provide biological plausibility for how submicron particles might interact with kidney structures or operate indirectly through the cardio-renal axis.28 To contextualize these effect sizes, our results align well with previous large cohort studies. For instance, Bowe et al7 demonstrated a robust, non-linear relationship between PM2.5 and accelerated CKD progression in a massive US veteran cohort, mirroring the non-linear trajectories observed here. Similarly, Blum et al6 highlighted a substantial global kidney disease burden attributable to PM25 with comparable risk magnitudes. Regarding finer fractions, Chen et al29 provided critical cohort evidence that long-term PM1 exposure induces steep risk gradients at lower concentration ranges, further reinforcing the plausibility of our observed robust non-linear response curves for PM1.

In addition to particulate matter, NOx, a marker of traffic-related air pollution, also showed an independent positive association with CKD mortality, consistent with previous epidemiological evidence., agreeing with previous studies.30 The dense transport networks across Europe contribute to elevated NOx emission levels. Previous studies indicate that individuals residing within 0.05 kilometers of major roads exhibit significantly lower eGFR levels compared to those living 1 km away (P = 0.007),31 while large-scale meta-analyses demonstrate that each 10 μg/m3 increase in NO2 correlates with heightened CKD risk.32 This suggests that, alongside industrial emissions, traffic pollution may represent an important environmental driver of regional disparities in CKD mortality.

Despite the limitations in isolating independent particle-size effects, our analysis highlights that PM1 is associated with a prominent CKD mortality burden across Europe. Historically, Europe’s legislative framework—evolving from the 1996 Framework Directive to the Clean Air for Europe (CAFE) program—has relied heavily on mass-concentration paradigms of larger particles.33 This regulatory focus remains evident in current frameworks; even within older statutes like Directive 2008/50/EC34 and the latest 2024 revisions of the EU Ambient Air Quality Directives,35 compliance standards remain strictly tied to PM2.5 and PM10, leaving PM1 without standalone legal limit values. While the 2024 directives introduced “super stations” to monitor ultrafine particles, the current regulatory gap for PM1 warrants attention given our findings that its statistical correlation with CKD mortality (β = 0.234) is non-negligible, exhibiting an accelerated risk profile before flattening at an estimated saturation threshold of 12.08 µg/m3. This characteristic non-linear trajectory aligns with the European ELAPSE cohort project, which demonstrated steeper risk curves at lower ambient exposure ranges.36 Crucially, while large-scale screening studies, such as those by Li et al37 and Zhao et al,38 indicate that the statistical weights and physiological impacts of different particle sizes on kidney function (such as eGFR decline) may vary across populations, these variations do not negate the potentially robust role of submicron particulate matter in driving progressive kidney impairment. Consequently, these findings suggest that future European environmental policies and public health surveillance systems could benefit from integrating toxicology with regional emission inventories to address this regulatory gap, considering PM1 as a distinct parameter to advance particle-size-directed precision governance.

Non-Linear Relationships: Complex Dose-Response Patterns

Moving beyond simple linear assumptions, this study systematically elucidated the complex non-linear associations between CKD mortality, environmental factors, and comorbidities by applying GAMs and XGBoost machine learning algorithms, thereby overcoming the limitations of traditional linear models. Feature importance analysis from XGBoost indicated that diabetes was the strongest predictor of CKD mortality. The dose–response curve displayed a characteristic saturation effect: CKD mortality increased rapidly with worsening diabetes indicators at lower levels; however, beyond a certain threshold, the growth trend gradually flattens. This pattern likely correlates with the role of diabetes as a primary pathological basis for end-stage renal disease (ESRD), in which all-cause mortality rates among patients can reach 18.3 times those of the general population.39 From a mechanistic perspective, diabetes continuously activates inflammatory pathways and oxidative stress responses, thereby inducing macrovascular and microvascular complications. This significantly increases the risk of heart failure comorbidities in CKD, substantially increasing the mortality risk among patients with CKD.40,41

Regarding environmental pollutants, both particulate matter and NOx exhibited pronounced non-linear dose-response characteristics, albeit with distinct patterns. The relationship between particulate matter exposure and CKD mortality followed an S-shaped increasing curve, with a steeper slope at low-to-moderate concentrations. This suggests that predicted CKD mortality contributions increased more rapidly at low-to-moderate particulate concentrations, consistent with previous evidence linking fine particulate exposure to adverse kidney outcomes.42 Upon entering the high-concentration range, the curve’s growth rate moderates, displaying a characteristic saturation effect. This non-linear plateau aligns with previous ambient pollution studies using similar non-linear frameworks (eg., GAMs), which identified clear saturation points and subsequent “harvesting effects” in heavily polluted areas.43 Chronically, this phenomenon reflects a “vulnerable population depletion” effect driven by competing risks of mortality. Specifically, exposure to extreme air pollution is highly likely to trigger acute, fatal cardiovascular crises (such as heart failure or myocardial infarction) in the most susceptible individuals—particularly those with advanced cardio-renal syndrome or co-existing diabetes—before their progressive kidney failure fully culminates in death. Clinically, cardiovascular events remain the leading cause of premature death in patients with concurrent diabetes and CKD, representing a dominant competing risk that substantially alters mortality trajectories.44 Consequently, these individuals succumb to acute competing causes, which statistically dilutes the subsequent proportion of deaths officially recorded with CKD as the primary underlying cause. This statistical displacement is further compounded by the diminished or unexpected biological response pathways observed in highly vulnerable cohorts under controlled exposures.45 However, this interpretation remains speculative within the present ecological framework and requires validation in individual-level cohort studies with cause-specific competing-risk analyses.46,47

In contrast, NOx exposure exhibited a J-shaped threshold effect. At low exposure levels, CKD mortality risk only marginally increased with rising NOx levels. However, once exposure exceeded a critical threshold, the CKD mortality rate accelerated sharply. This biphasic pattern might be conceptually explained by the dynamic equilibrium between the body’s defense mechanisms and toxic effects. At low doses, endogenous antioxidant systems (such as glutathione and GSH) and detoxification pathways can counteract NOx-induced oxidative stress. Additionally, low levels of nitric oxide can act as signaling molecules and may temporarily contribute to maintaining kidney blood flow and redox balance.48 However, when exposure concentrations exceed the body’s compensatory capacity, it is hypothesized that damage to the glomerular filtration barrier may ensue. Based on prior experimental literature, such effects could be mediated by potential biological pathways, including the suppression of the Nrf2 antioxidant defense pathway and the activation of the TLR4/NF-κB and C5aR1 inflammatory signaling axes,49 which are known to contribute to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species and endothelial dysfunction in laboratory models.50 Notably, previous studies have indicated that NOx emissions from petrol engines can elevate levels of the vasoconstrictor endothelin-1,51 offering a plausible link to kidney hemodynamic abnormalities and exacerbated kidney injury. Nevertheless, since our study relies on ecological data, these mechanistic pathways remain biologically plausible hypotheses rather than direct evidence generated from our population-level analysis.

Spatial Interaction Between Socioeconomic Benefits and Environmental Exposure Intensity

Further exploring the spatial interaction between socioeconomic benefits and environmental exposure intensity, our results indicate that the influence of HDI on CKD mortality rates may be overshadowed by spatial environmental exposure gradients. Specifically, GAMM results show that HDI is a significant protective factor (βIQR = −0.056 on the logit-transformed proportional scale, P < 0.001), consistent with findings from the Global Burden of Disease study.52 This suggests that regions with higher socioeconomic development exhibit lower CKD mortality due to better healthcare accessibility. However, spatial detector analysis indicated that HDI explained very little spatial heterogeneity (q = 0.005). This seemingly contradictory outcome requires nuanced interpretation and likely reflects inherent methodological differences rather than an absence of socioeconomic influence. Methodologically, Geodetector evaluates spatial stratified heterogeneity via variance decomposition; because socioeconomic drivers like HDI often operate through intricate non-linear thresholds or modify baseline population vulnerability rather than acting as direct spatial determinants, their independent q-values can be mathematically diluted. This is supported by broader epidemiological evidence. The GBD Chronic Kidney Disease Collaboration (2020)2 demonstrated that the global CKD burden is deeply intertwined with socioeconomic tiers. Crucially, socioeconomic status acts as a structural backdrop dictating environmental health outcomes: Ye, J. J et al53 highlighted that lower socioeconomic populations face disproportionate environmental exposures with fewer mitigation resources, while Vicedo-Cabrera et al54 showed that community adaptive capacity heavily mediates vulnerability to environmental stressors. Thus, while environmental variables display stronger direct spatial determination here, HDI functions as a critical structural determinant governing baseline population susceptibility. This underscores the importance of employing multiple models (combining associative GAMMs and spatial Geodetectors) for cross-validation in multi-scale geographic analysis.52,55

Our findings further clarify this divergence, indicating that environmental exposure gradients, particularly NOx and PM1, may partly obscure or modify the protective association typically linked to higher socioeconomic development. NOx (β = 0.021 on the logit scale, p < 0.001) and particulate matter, especially PM1, constitute the primary spatial drivers. The more pronounced association of PM1 compared to PM2.5 and PM10 is likely driven by its greater penetrability and deposition efficiency.28,37,38 Crucially, this “masking” effect demonstrates that the health dividends of socioeconomic development are not absolute, even in affluent contexts. A recent comprehensive meta-analysis of European and North American cohorts confirmed that long-term exposure to traffic-related air pollution, particularly NOx, remains a robust driver of mortality even after strictly controlling for area-level socioeconomic status.56 For instance, empirical evidence from Greater London—one of Europe’s wealthiest metropolitan areas with advanced medical infrastructure—revealed that localized high-emission transport hubs and major roadways create concentrated zones of elevated pollutant toxicity.57 These localized pollution hotspots may counter or obscure the protective health gradients typically afforded by superior healthcare infrastructure. Consequently, hotspot areas for CKD mortality risk are not solely aligned with socioeconomic disadvantages, but exhibit a stronger spatial alignment with the geographic distribution of air pollutants,7,58 highlighting the critical need for micro-level environmental management around key emission hubs.

Meteorological conditions, particularly temperature, also appear to play an important synergistic role in this masking effect, amplifying the health risks in tandem with environmental pollutants. Network analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between temperature and CKD mortality (r = 0.42), whereas humidity had a protective effect (r = −0.33). This suggests that high-temperature heat stress and its potential to induce dehydration constitute important mechanisms that exacerbate disease burden.59,60 The positive temperature–CKD correlation and inverse humidity–CKD correlation observed in the network analysis suggest a possible interaction between heat stress and dry conditions; however, this remains an ecological hypothesis supported by prior physiological literature rather than a directly tested interaction in the present study. From a physiological standpoint, hot and dry air accelerates insensible water loss through sweat, driving rapid volume depletion and blood hyperosmolality.61 This profound hypovolemia triggers blood redistribution to cutaneous vessels, which significantly reduces kidney blood perfusion and subsequently activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), promoting kidney inflammation and tubular injury.62 Simultaneously, from an environmental and toxicological perspective, chronic dehydration under dry-hot conditions dramatically concentrates blood and urine, thereby elevating the vulnerability of kidney tubules to nephrotoxic ambient pollutants (such as PM and NOx) due to ineffective toxin excretion.63 By controlling for these environmental confounders, GAMs provide a crucial complementary perspective; after adjusting for the effects of pollutants and climatic factors, the net protective effect of HDI becomes apparent. This indicates that a higher HDI (reflecting better healthcare conditions and higher income levels) provides a foundational defense against CKD mortality. This is demonstrated by the lower age-standardized CKD mortality rates observed in regions with high sociodemographic development, primarily attributable to kidney replacement therapy accessibility,2 which is often spatially overshadowed by the potent impacts of environmental pollutants and climate warming.

Strengths and Limitations

While this study offers a robust spatiotemporal framework for understanding CKD mortality, certain inherent characteristics of cross-national research should be noted. To achieve a pan-European perspective, we utilized population-density-weighted environmental data; although this high-resolution approach significantly enhances exposure precision compared to simple geographic means, it primarily captures macro-level trends rather than individual-specific microenvironmental variations. Consequently, this ecological design cannot fully account for localized urban-rural variability, individual occupational exposure histories, or finer intra-country spatial pollution gradients, which may introduce a degree of exposure misclassification. Furthermore, as an extensive longitudinal analysis, this study focuses on identifying significant macro-epidemiological associations which, while providing a critical scientific basis for regional public health policy, invite further granular validation through prospective cohorts. Third, our reliance on annual mean values for environmental variables may not fully capture the nuanced, multi-year lagged structures or high-resolution temporal fluctuations of environmental stressors. Although CKD mortality is predominantly driven by chronic, long-term cumulative physiological damage rather than acute, short-term triggers—thereby justifying the use of annual averages in line with recent large-scale epidemiological frameworks—future investigations utilizing individual-level data are warranted to further dissect these potential temporal lag effects. Finally, although we integrated a comprehensive suite of socioeconomic and clinical covariates including HDI and diabetes prevalence, future research incorporating localized healthcare infrastructure metrics—such as the accessibility of kidney replacement therapy—could further complement the predictive depth of our environmental drivers.

Conclusion

Based on data from 39 European countries and regions, this study revealed significant spatiotemporal associations between environmental factors and CKD mortality. Results indicate that NOx and fine particulate matter, particularly PM1, exhibit strong statistical links to regional CKD mortality patterns characterized by non-linear shapes. Importantly, our findings bridge association identification and actionable policy metrics by establishing explicit empirical thresholds; The empirical thresholds identified in this study, including the 9 ppb NOx inflection point and the particulate matter saturation points, may serve as hypothesis-generating reference values for future environmental surveillance and prospective validation, rather than definitive regulatory targets. However, several limitations must be recognized. First, these findings derive strictly from ecological associations, precluding direct individual-level causal inferences. Second, exposure estimates relied on population-weighted national averages, potentially masking localized personal exposure gradients. Consequently, future prospective cohort validation remains necessary to confirm these links. From a translational perspective, these findings highlight the benefit of developing integrated environmental surveillance frameworks to monitor multi-pollutant dynamics, while establishing targeted nephrology prevention programs within socioeconomically vulnerable populations. In conclusion, this methodological triangulation fills an empirical gap in European PM1 research.
