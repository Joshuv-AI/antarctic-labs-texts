Drug-Induced Thrombotic Microangiopathy in Cancer
Authors: Michele Barbarini, Sandra M. Herrmann
Published: 2026

Drug-induced thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is an increasingly important complication of modern cancer therapy. In oncology practice, diagnosis and therapy are challenging because malignancy itself, anticancer agents, and other systemic complications may all produce overlapping manifestations of endothelial injury. Moreover, the classic clinical triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and organ injury is not invariably present, particularly when TMA is predominantly confined to the kidney. In this single-center retrospective study, Pak et al. provide a timely and clinically relevant contribution by describing one of the largest single-center cohorts of biopsy-proven drug-induced renal TMA in patients with cancer, integrating clinical presentation, anticancer drug exposure, kidney histology, and long-term outcomes.1 The study provides an important kidney-centered perspective on a complication that is frequently diagnosed presumptively and is probably both underrecognized and heterogeneous.

The authors identified 53 patients with biopsy-proven renal TMA attributed to cancer therapies over more than two decades. The cohort was clinically complex, with frequent baseline CKD, hypertension, advanced malignancy, and exposure to multiple anticancer drugs. The most commonly implicated drugs were gemcitabine, bevacizumab, doxorubicin, and tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Anemia was present in 81% of patients, whereas thrombocytopenia occurred in only 30%. By contrast, kidney manifestations were prominent: 77% developed AKI, 81% had hypertension, and 59% had clinically significant proteinuria. These findings challenge an approach that requires classic systemic hemolysis and thrombocytopenia, as they may be incomplete or absent. This distinction is important because reliance on the full hematologic triad may delay recognition until irreversible kidney injury has occurred. In patients affected by oncologic disease receiving a potentially endothelial-toxic anticancer agent, the combination of new or worsening hypertension, proteinuria, and declining kidney function should therefore raise suspicion for TMA, even when platelet count and hemolysis markers are not diagnostic.

These observations place kidney biopsy at the center of the diagnostic pathway (Figure 1). Biopsy not only confirms microangiopathic injury but also helps distinguish TMA from acute tubular injury, acute interstitial nephritis, immune-complex GN, or podocytopathies, which may require different treatment and may have different implications for continuation of anticancer therapy.

Few findings deserve emphasis. First, chronic TMA lesions were present in most of the biopsies suggesting that clinically overt disease may have been preceded by months of subclinical endothelial injury. Once chronic microvascular remodeling has developed, withdrawal of the causative drug may prevent further injury without restoring the nephron mass already lost. The association of microvascular thrombi and endothelial swelling with worse kidney outcomes further suggests that active vascular injury and chronic damage may coexist at presentation. Early biopsy, before fixed lesions predominate, could therefore have diagnostic and therapeutic relevance. Furthermore, complete renal recovery was limited. Only 39% of patients with AKI recovered within 1 year, and recovery from dialysis dependence was uncommon. These findings highlight that discontinuation of the offending agent, although essential, may not fully reverse established microvascular injury. These results appear less favorable than those reported in selected drug-specific series but are consistent with the poorer prognosis observed in this cohort.2,3 One plausible explanation is the advanced histologic stage at diagnosis: 85% of biopsies showed chronic TMA, whereas acute TMA was identified in only 36%.

An additional strength of the study is its illustration of the mechanistic heterogeneity hidden within the single-term drug-induced TMA. Gemcitabine-associated TMA is often considered a cumulative endothelial toxicity, whereas vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibition produces a characteristic renal phenotype marked by hypertension, proteinuria, podocyte-endothelial crosstalk disruption, and renal-limited TMA.3,4 Moreover, proteasome inhibitors may produce yet another pathway of injury by altering NF-κB signaling, reducing VEGF expression, and facilitating complement-mediated endothelial damage.5 However, the signal for doxorubicin and erlotinib is particularly noteworthy. Doxorubicin is better known for its cardiotoxicity, but oxidative endothelial injury provides biological plausibility for kidney microangiopathy. Erlotinib and other EGFR-pathway inhibitors have traditionally been linked to electrolyte disorders and tubulointerstitial injury.6 A recent pharmacovigilance study also identified signals supporting a possible association between TMA and doxorubicin, sorafenib, and lenvatinib.7

These differences may help explain the variable drug exposures and durations observed across implicated agents, as well as why cumulative drug dose was not clearly associated with kidney recovery in the overall cohort. As cancer regimens become increasingly layered and prolonged, attributing TMA to a single drug may be overly simplistic. Furthermore, complement findings may also differ according to the causative agent; renal complement deposition has been reported more frequently in gemcitabine-associated TMA, whereas deposits seem less common in TMA associated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors or intraocular anti-VEGF therapy.5 Thus, anticancer therapy-associated TMA may not represent a uniform biological entity, but rather a group of distinct pathologic phenotypes converging on a shared final pathway of glomerular and vascular endothelial injury. Recognizing these phenotypes may ultimately be more useful than applying a single treatment algorithm to all drug-induced cases.

Pak et al.1 confirmed that older age, lower baseline eGFR, and thrombocytopenia were associated with worse kidney failure-free survival. Similar associations between age, kidney dysfunction, and adverse outcomes have been observed in broader TMA populations.8 These factors may reflect reduced kidney reserve and more severe systemic endothelial injury. In particular, thrombocytopenia may be a marker of greater disease severity rather than an independent prognostic factor. However, interpretation of kidney failure-free survival is complicated because death was included as an event, and 81% of the cohort died during follow-up. In patients with advanced malignancy, cancer-related mortality may dominate the analysis and obscure kidney-specific prognosis. Future studies should report kidney failure and death separately and use competing-risk methods when evaluating progression to kidney failure.

This work supports a lower threshold for nephrology involvement and kidney biopsy when patients with cancer develop new or worsening hypertension, proteinuria, an unexplained decline in kidney function, or laboratory evidence of hemolysis during treatment with known or suspected endothelial-toxic agents. Biopsy is not feasible or necessary in every case, particularly when thrombocytopenia or clinical instability increases procedural risk. Nevertheless, when safely performed, biopsy can distinguish drug-induced TMA from other common competing diagnoses, including acute tubular injury, acute interstitial nephritis, GN, and malignant hypertension (Figure 1). This distinction matters because management decisions often require balancing kidney preservation against continuation of life-prolonging cancer therapy.

Treatment remains the major unresolved question. Withdrawal of the suspected drug occurred in 96% of patients and remains the cornerstone of management, but cessation alone may be inadequate when endothelial injury continues or complement activation amplifies the initial insult. Case reports and small series suggest a potential benefit of C5 inhibition in selected patients with severe or persistent injury, but prospective data are lacking, and complement abnormalities are not consistently demonstrated.9 In the current series, few patients received eculizumab, limiting inference.

In selected patients, complement blockade may enable continuation or rechallenge with essential anticancer therapy, although evidence remains limited to small observational studies.10

Despite its retrospective, single-center design and treatment heterogeneity, the study's biopsy-proven cohort provides valuable diagnostic certainty. Prospective multicenter studies should evaluate standardized outcomes and determine whether C5 inhibition improves kidney recovery and enables safe anticancer therapy rechallenge.

In summary, Pak and colleagues1 highlight that drug-induced TMA is an underrecognized complication of cancer therapy that often requires kidney biopsy for timely diagnosis. Their findings support earlier recognition of endothelial injury before irreversible vascular remodeling and kidney failure develop. As cancer survival improves and therapeutic exposures accumulate, the onconephrologic approach should shift from reactive diagnosis to proactive surveillance, recognizing the kidney as a sentinel organ for treatment-related microvascular toxicity.
