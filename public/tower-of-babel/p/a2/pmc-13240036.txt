Upper‐Body Neuromechanical Coordination Strategies During Fatiguing Sustained Dead Hangs in Climbers
Authors: Juliana Exel, Paul Kaufmann, Olivia Froschauer, Arnold Baca, Hans Kainz, Luis Mochizuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study investigated how upper‐body joint and muscle coordination strategies are related and adapted under fatigue during sustained dead hangs, a task relevant to climbing performance. Eleven climbers performed a dead hang until failure. Joint coordination variability (rCV) was assessed using a modified vector coding method in kinematic data. Muscle coordination was analyzed through EMG linear and non‐linear dependencies between signals with coherence (β/γCOH) and mutual information (β/γMI) at beta and gamma bands. Fatigue induced joint‐specific changes in coordination variability, with wrist rCV decreasing from START to END (39.6 ± 32.9% vs. 21.2 ± 12.4%; p = 0.002), showing a redistribution of coordination demands across the upper limb. At the muscle level, βCOH increased in selected postural muscle pairs, including brachioradialis–biceps (p = 0.04), trapezius–biceps (p = 0.04), and trapezius–brachioradialis (p = 0.003), while γCOH decreased for trapezius–brachioradialis (p = 0.01). In addition, βMI increased for brachioradialis (p = 0.01) and brachioradialis–biceps (p = 0.01), while γMI increased within the finger flexors (p = 0.03). Repeated‐measures correlation revealed inverse within‐subject associations between wrist rCV and trapezius–brachioradialis γCOH (rrm (10) = −0.57, p = 0.05) and finger‐flexor γMI (rrm (10) = −0.61, p = 0.03). These findings are consistent with a multi‐level compensatory strategy in which distal joint behavior becomes more constrained while proximal and local muscle coordination is reinforced to sustain suspension under fatigue, offering new insight into joint–muscle coordination during climbing‐specific isometric tasks.Keywords: climbing, coordination variability, dead hangs, EMG interdependence analysis, motor control, vector coding

Introduction

During climbing, athletes alternate between isometric and dynamic muscle contractions to regulate posture and control body displacement. This demand is reflected in training exercises designed to develop sport‐specific strength and coordination. Among these, isometric overhead hanging tasks are particularly important for this sport.

Dead hangs require supporting the entire body weight with the hands or fingertips, often with arms overhead and elbows extended. These tasks are commonly used to enhance finger flexor strength and endurance in climbers (Exel et al. 2023; López‐Rivera and González‐Badillo 2019), and are frequently involved in climbing performance. The ability to perform sustained isometric holds correlates strongly with climbing skill level and performance (Hermans et al. 2017; Medernach et al. 2015), highlighting its relevance in training and competition.

Despite their importance, the neuromuscular strategies underlying postural control during sustained overhead tasks, particularly under fatigue, remain poorly understood. Climbing requires supporting suspension with few points of contact, often relying on finger flexors and upper‐limb stabilization to support body weight. Understanding how the central nervous system (CNS) organizes joint and muscle coordination during such tasks is crucial for improving training methods, preventing injury, and mitigating performance decline during fatigue.

The neurophysiological aspects of finger flexor endurance in climbing have been studied with different protocols. Elite climbers show slower decreases in EMG spectral features compared to non‐climbers during intermittent gripping tasks, although differences disappear at failure (Vigouroux et al. 2006). Discipline‐specific differences also exist: boulder climbers show higher maximal voluntary force and rate of force development than lead climbers, despite similar endurance capacities (Fanchini et al. 2013; Fryer et al. 2017; Stien et al. 2019). Finger strength assessments using hand dynamometers have been criticized for their lack of specificity to climbing (Watts et al. 2008), whereas treadmill‐wall and isolated finger flexor tests have shown stronger associations with climbing endurance (Baláš et al. 2021). Moreover, body weight has been identified as a factor influencing maximal holding time in suspensions (Dindorf et al. 2022). However, these studies focus on isolated finger actions, neglecting the multi‐joint and whole‐body coordination demands of isometric tasks as dead hangs.

The control of complex limb movements involves coordinating multiple degrees of freedom. The CNS is thought to simplify this challenge by recruiting groups of muscles as functional units (Bernstein 1967; d’Avella et al. 2003). This allows for the generation of consistent motor output while adapting to task demands and environmental constraints. At the joint level, relative motion between segments reflects how different joints contribute to sustaining and executing a task. Techniques such as the radial vector coding (Mochizuki et al. 2026) offer a simple and interpretable method for quantifying the spatiotemporal coordination of joint kinematics, mapping the relative angular motion between joint pairs and revealing patterns of synchronization or compensation critical for understanding posture maintenance (Tepavac and Field‐Fote 2001). At the muscular level, frequency‐based analyses of EMG signals, such as coherence (COH) and mutual information (MI), provide insight into the neural organization underlying coordination. COH measures linear synchrony between muscles, revealing common neural drive, while MI captures both linear and non‐linear dependencies, offering a broader view of muscle interactions (Danna‐Dos‐Santos et al. 2014; Degani et al. 2017).

Fatigue can disrupt neuromechanical control mechanisms, altering motor strategies and forcing the CNS to adapt to reduced force‐generating capacity (Monjo et al. 2015; Santos et al. 2010). Evidence from postural control studies indicates that task stability is maintained through coordinated multi‐joint strategies rather than isolated joint actions (Creath et al. 2005). Furthermore, studies on fatigue in multi‐joint tasks show that adaptations are not restricted to the fatigued segment, but involve redistribution of movement variability toward proximal joints, suggesting a compensatory mechanism to preserve task execution under fatigue (Cowley and Gates 2017). These studies support the idea that stability can be preserved through coordinated reorganization across joints when constraints change.

This study aimed to investigate how joint and muscle coordination strategies are related and adapted under fatigue during sustained dead hangs. Specifically, we examined changes in joint coordination variability and intermuscular connectivity (COH and MI) before and after fatigue and explored their relationships as indicators of CNS compensatory mechanisms. We hypothesized that fatigue would lead to a redistribution of joint variability toward proximal joints and increased intermuscular synchronization, reflecting a multi‐level adaptation to preserve task performance.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Eleven recreational advanced‐elite male climbers (26 ± 5 years old, 178 ± 5.8 cm height; 72 ± 6.9 kg weight, 22 ± 3.3 IRCRA reporting scale), recruited from local climbing gyms, with prior experience using a hang board and no history of upper‐limb musculoskeletal injuries, participated in the study. Previous power analysis showed that, with a total sample size of 11, the present study could detect effects of dz = 0.94 or larger with 80% power. For the observed primary fatigue effect contrast, the estimated effect size was dz = 1.21, corresponding to an achieved power of 0.95.

The local Ethics Committee (reference number 00690) approved the research protocol, and all participants gave their written informed consent after receiving detailed study information.

Experimental Protocol

Each participant performed one maximal time dead hang on a 20 mm depth‐wooden edge with 4 mm radius of a custom‐built hang board until failure (Figure 1). Participants were instructed to maintain a half‐crimp finger position, fully extended elbows, and a horizontal gaze throughout the trial. The test was finished by visual inspection when any of these criteria could no longer be maintained, such as a transition from the half‐crimp to an open‐crimp or three‐finger‐drag grip, elbow flexion, or when the participant fell from the hang board. Before the test, participants were encouraged to do a 10 min warm‐up routine including jumping jacks, joint‐mobility exercises with and without elastic bands, pull and push‐ups, and assisted finger dead hangs.

Experimental set up used in the present study. Participants had to perform a maximal time dead hang on a 20 mm wooden edge (right image), in half‐crimp position of the fingers, extended arms, and maintaining horizontal sight throughout (left image).

Measurements

A 12‐camera motion capture system (Vicon Motion Systems, Oxford, UK) was employed to capture 3D trajectories of 33 retroreflective surface markers at 200 Hz sampling rate. These markers were affixed to the participants' upper body (Figure 2) in accordance with the Plug‐in Gait marker set (Kadaba et al. 1990). Following data collection, the markers were labeled, gap‐filled, low‐pass filtered (Butterworth, 6 Hz, fourth order) and the joint kinematics were calculated using the conventional gait model in Nexus 2.14.0 (Vicon Motion Systems, Oxford, UK).

Participant with the surface markers and EMG sensors used to capture upper body kinematics and muscle activity during the maximum time dead hang task performed for this study.

We recorded the surface electromyography (EMG) signal of six muscles including the flexor digitorum superficialis, finger flexor; extensor digitorum, finger extensor; brachioradialis, biceps brachii long head, biceps; trapezius, and pectoralis major. We defined the finger flexor and extensor as motor muscles and the others as postural muscles. EMG signals were recorded from both left and right limbs using wireless electrodes (Cometa, Milan, Italy) at 2 kHz sampling rate, synchronized with the motion capture system. The electrodes were placed following the guidelines from Quaine et al. (2003) for the finger flexor and finger extensor; Merlo, Bò, and Campanini (2021) for the brachioradialis; Hermens and Freriks (2022) for the trapezius and biceps, and MacLean and Dickerson for the pectoralis major (2019). The raw EMG signals were band‐pass filtered (Butterworth, 5–400 Hz, fourth order), demeaned, and rectified using the Hilbert transform as suggested by Farina et al. 2013.

Data Analysis

The influence of fatigue in upper body coordination at the joint and muscular levels was analyzed by dividing the whole task into two parts. The first part corresponded to the first 20% of the task (START), thereby presenting a rested stage. The second part corresponded to the last 20% (END) and presented a fatigued stage.

Muscles’ coordination strategies were assessed using EMG‐EMG COH and MI. COH is used to investigate the neural connectivity of moto neuron pools (Farina et al. 2013) and it was calculated for all muscle pairs using filtered and rectified EMG signals of each participant. The computation of COH was performed for START and END separately, with segment length of 2048 samples with 50% overlap, using a Hanning window, as suggested previously (Terry and Griffin 2008). MI captures both linear and non‐linear dependencies between signals, and it was also calculated for both START and END. A MI value of zero shows no shared information between signals, while higher values suggest stronger dependencies. Frequency‐specific origins of these dependencies, COH and MI were analyzed separately in the beta (13–30 Hz) and gamma (30–60 Hz) bands, which are commonly linked to motor control and sensorimotor integration in the upper limbs (Kim et al. 2017).

Joint coordination strategies were investigated throughout the amount of variability in relative motion between wrist, elbow, and shoulder. Therefore, radial vector coding, a modified version of vector coding was applied (Mochizuki et al. 2026). Vector coding is a coordinate transformation from the cartesian coordinates x and y to the polar coordinates α and r. In the classical vector coding approach, the coupling angle α represents how x and y are associated, while the radius r is a unit vector (Tepavac and Field‐Fote 2001). For this study, we did not assume that r is unit. The cartesian 3D coordinates were transformed into spherical coordinates r, θ, and φ, where r is the radius. The coupling angle θ (inclination θ) is the angle between the radius r and the z‐axis, while the coupling angle φ (azimuth) is the angle between the orthogonal radius r projection onto the reference x‐y plane and either the x or y‐axis. For spherical transformations, the r is a measure of distance. When r is constant the data describes circular motion, while r changes over time means the movement is not circular although it can still hold a periodicity. We calculated r for the joint triple coordinates of the shoulder, elbow, and wrist. Under this context, changes in r can be a measure of variability. The coefficient of variability was calculated represent such variability in terms of r and reported as the radial coordination variability (rCV). Higher rCV values indicate greater variability in joint coordination, which can be interpreted as reflecting increased contribution of the joint and associated control demands for stability.

We have averaged the left and right limbs data for analysis. After testing data normality using Lilliefors test, significant differences between START and END on COH and MI in both bands (βCOH/γCOH and βMI/γMI, respectively) were tested for each pair of muscles using Wilcoxon sign rank test.

To check for differences between rCV at START and END with shoulder, elbow, and wrist as main factors, we used repeated‐measure ANOVA. Effect sizes for the ANOVA were expressed as partial eta squared (ηp2) and interpreted according to Cohen's equivalent f‐based thresholds, where partial ηp2 values of 0.0099, 0.0588, and 0.1379 indicate small, medium, and large effects, respectively (Cohen 1969). A Tukey‐adjusted correction was applied for post‐hoc pairwise comparisons.

Repeated measures correlation (rmcorr) (Bakdash and Marusich 2017) was used to assess the relationship between muscle‐level coordination variables (COH and MI in both bands) and joint‐level rCV. This method accounts for the non‐independence of repeated observations by estimating the common intra‐individual linear association between two variables; accordingly, the repeated‐measures correlation coefficient (rrm) was interpreted analogously to Pearson's r in terms of direction and magnitude, but as a within‐subject rather than between‐subject association (near 0 = weak/no linear association; around ± 0.1 to ± 0.3 = small; around ± 0.3 to ± 0.5 = moderate; above ± 0.5 = moderate‐to‐strong/strong). In addition to rrm, we report rrm 2 as an index of shared within‐subject variance. This analysis included only COH and MI muscle pairs that showed significant differences between START and END.

The statistical significance was set as p < 0.05. Data processing and analyses were performed using custom‐built codes in Matlab 2017b (MathWorks Inc., Natick, USA), Jamovi (Version 2.3, Sidney, AUS), and rmcorrShiny (Marusich and Bakdash 2021).

Results

The repeated‐measures ANOVA indicated a significant joint effect (F = 4.1, p = 0.02, partial ηp2 = 0.22) and significant phase*joints interaction (F = 9.1, p < 0.001, partial ηp2 = 0.38) for rCV, whereas the main effect of phase was not significant (F = 2.8, p = 0.10, partial ηp2 = 0.09). Thus, as shown in Figure 3, fatigue‐related changes in rCV were not uniform across joints, but reflected a joint‐specific redistribution pattern, primarily driven by the reduction in wrist rCV. The mean total hanging time was 52.06 ± 15.95 s.

The post‐hoc analysis for joints revealed that the shoulder had a larger mean rCV (328 ± 109%) compared to the elbow (342 ± 368%; p = 0.03), while the other joint comparisons were not significant (Figure 3). The significant phase × joint interaction showed that fatigue‐related changes in coordination variability depended on the joint. Specifically, at START, the wrist showed a larger rCV (39.6 ± 32.9%) than the elbow (9.28 ± 5.17%; p = 0.02). The wrist rCV decreased significantly from START to END (39.6 ± 32.9% at START vs. 21.2 ± 12.4% at the END; p = 0.002).

Figures 4 and 5 show the mean differences for COH and MI. βCOH were significantly higher at the END for the following postural muscles pairs: brachioradialis‐biceps (p = 0.04; Z = −2.04), trapezius‐biceps (p = 0.04; Z = −2.04), and trapezius‐brachioradialis (p = 0.003; Z = −1.60). Noteworthy, trapezius‐finger extensor also showed an increased βCOH at the END (p = 0.05; Z = −1.95). The γCOH between trapezius‐brachioradialis was significantly lower at the END (p = 0.01; Z = −2.40). βMI increased at the END for the brachioradialis with itself (p = 0.01, t = −3.16) and with biceps (p = 0.01; t = −2.82). γMI was higher at the END for the finger flexors with itself (p = 0.03; t = −2.52).

Discussion

This study investigated the effects of fatigue on upper‐body joint and muscle coordination during sustained isometric dead hangs, providing new understanding into how the CNS reorganizes movement strategies under fatiguing conditions. Our main findings were: 1) joint rCV was initially highest in the wrist, whereas fatigue‐related changes were joint‐specific and reflected a redistribution of coordination variability across the upper limb; 2) muscle‐level coordination changed, with increased EMG coherence between postural muscles, and increased mutual information among postural and motor muscles, and 3) ower wrist coordination variability was associated with higher trapezius–brachioradialis γCOH and greater finger‐flexor γMI. These findings suggest that the CNS responds to fatigue by coordinately redistributing stability demands across joints and muscles to sustain task performance.

A distal‐to‐proximal redistribution of coordination variability emerged as a key feature of the fatigue response. At task onset, the wrist showed the highest rCV, reflecting its role as the most mechanically challenged joint in sustaining suspension. The wrist extension in a crimp grip is a biomechanical advantage, which optimizes force transfer from the fingers to the hold (Amca et al. 2012; Li 2002). As fatigue developed, wrist variability decreased while shoulder variability increased, suggesting a redistribution of stability demands toward proximal joints. This shift may reflect a strategy to stiffen the wrist and reduce degrees of freedom, thereby stabilizing the fatigued grip while compensating through larger adjustments at the shoulder. Similar proximal joints' compensation mechanisms have been reported in postural control during standing balance (Creath et al. 2005), supporting the notion that the CNS reorganizes joint contributions to preserve overall task stability. Although participants had to maintain the half‐crimp grip throughout the task and the trial was terminated when this posture could no longer be sustained, subtle grip‐related adjustments near task failure cannot be entirely excluded and may also have influenced wrist behavior.

Fatigue enhanced coordination among postural muscles, supporting force stabilization through increased common drive. Muscle coordination changes under fatigue were characterized by increased βCOH between postural muscles (trapezius, biceps, brachioradialis), which may reflect enhanced common neural drive to support arm suspension despite finger flexor fatigue. Beta oscillations are associated with steady‐state force control and phase‐locked synchronization (Farmer et al. 1993), suggesting that the CNS recruits a more unified motor command to sustain force output.

Gamma‐band changes were less uniform. γCOH between trapezius and brachioradialis decreased, while γMI increased for finger flexors. This discrepancy suggests that gamma‐band activity may serve dual roles: decreased intermuscular γCOH could indicate reduced precision in force modulation, consistent with previous evidence linking this band to fine motor control (Hallett 1999), whereas increased γMI within finger flexors may represent a compensatory, self‐dependent synchronization mechanism to preserve grip force under fatigue, as also reported in previous findings (Bingham et al. 2016). These observations highlight a shift from fine control to broader and redundant synchronization as a strategy when hand muscles fatigue during suspension tasks.

A key novel finding of this study was a presence of inverse within‐subject association between wrist rCV and selected muscle‐coordination variables. Specifically, lower wrist rCV was associated with higher trapezius–brachioradialis γCOH and greater finger‐flexor γMI during fatiguing dead hangs. This finding complements the redistribution of stability demands across joints and the dual role of gamma‐band activity, and is consistent with a multi‐level CNS strategy to simplify movement control under fatigue (d’Avella et al. 2003): stiffening the distal joint while reinforcing coordinated muscle activation across forearm and shoulder to counteract declining finger flexor capacity. Despite the coefficients indicated moderate‐to‐strong within‐subject associations, they do not establish causality and may also reflect concurrent fatigue‐related factors, including generalized co‐contraction or stiffening and subtle grip or postural adjustments near task failure. These results should be interpreted as hypothesis‐supporting associations rather than definitive evidence of the underlying compensatory mechanism. Even so, they emphasize the importance of considering joint and muscle coordination as a coupled system rather than independent adaptations.

Fatigue‐related coordination changes provide a basis for designing more targeted and individualized training strategies. Our findings offer practical guidance for climbing training. The redistribution of stability demands toward the shoulder under fatigue suggests that improving proximal stability and recruitment pattern (e.g., trapezius and biceps strengthening, suspension tasks emphasizing shoulder control) may enhance hanging endurance. Likewise, targeted drills to support controlled wrist mobility under fatigue could delay the stiffening strategy and reduce task failure risk. Furthermore, discipline‐specific differences and the role of body weight (Vigouroux et al. 2006; Fanchini et al. 2013; Fryer et al. 2017; Stien et al. 2019; Baláš et al. 2021; Dindorf et al. 2022) suggest that training recommendations should be tailored to individual athlete characteristics. Beyond climbing, these results inform training for sports or rehabilitation tasks requiring prolonged static holds or postural endurance under load.

This study has some important limitations. Our sample size was small, which may limit the interpretation of small‐to‐moderate effects, particularly in the repeated‐measures correlation analysis. Although the main joint‐level findings were supported by moderate‐to‐large effect sizes, non‐significant results should still be interpreted with caution. Second, only one trial per participant was recorded, and right/left sides were averaged for analysis. In running biomechanics, the literature suggests that at least 25 steps per participant should be analyzed to accurately assess traditional variables such as speed, peak vertical and braking forces, and vertical loading rate (Oliveira and Pirscoveanu 2021). However, the use of fewer trials to investigate localized fatigue effects is not uncommon, and other studies have shown the feasibility of this approach in similar contexts (Cowley et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2020). Additionally, potential bilateral differences may be important for climbers (Exel et al. 2023), and its implications should be examined in future studies. Although participants had to maintain the half‐crimp position throughout the task and the test was finished when this posture could no longer be sustained, subtle grip‐related or postural adjustments close to failure were not quantified and may have influenced wrist behavior. Finally, we did not assess the role of the lower body in maintaining the dead‐hang posture. Even though this omission does not prevent the interpretation of upper‐body neuromuscular adaptations to fatigue, it limits our ability to evaluate whole‐body coordination during the task.

Conclusions

Fatigue during sustained dead hangs triggers a coordinated redistribution of stability demands from wrist to shoulder and enhances intermuscular synchronization among postural muscles. The observed joint–muscle coordination patterns are consistent with multi‐level CNS compensatory strategies to preserve suspension under fatigue. These findings highlight the importance of training approaches that target both distal grip endurance and proximal stability for improving overall climbing performance and resilience to fatigue.
