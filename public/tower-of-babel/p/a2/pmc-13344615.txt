Impact of the implementation of an obstetric sepsis protocol on maternal and neonatal outcomes
Authors: Ashley E. Shea, Christina T. Blanchard, Elizabeth B. Ausbeck, Rubymel J. Knupp, J. Michael Straughn, Jeff M. Szychowski, Akila Subramaniam, Brian M. Casey, Alan T. Tita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMaternal sepsis is a leading cause of preventable maternal morbidity and mortality in the United States. We assessed the implementation of a standardized early‐goal‐directed sepsis care bundle on maternal and neonatal outcomes. This bundle was activated by a set of vital sign criteria in combination with providers’ concern for infection and included immediate rapid response team evaluation and standardized orders for fluid resuscitation, antibiotics, and labs.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was performed at a single tertiary care center between January 1, 2012 and December 31, 2018. Individuals were included if they were pregnant or within 6 weeks of delivery with a singleton gestation and had a recorded temperature measurement of >100.4°F (38°C) or <96.8°F (36°C) in addition to a heart rate >100 beats/min or a respiratory rate >20 breaths/min within 30 min of the documented temperature during an inpatient stay or triage visit. Groups were categorized as pre‐implementation (January 2012–September 2016) and post‐implementation (October 2016–December 2018) of the “code sepsis” protocol. The primary outcome was a composite of maternal sepsis‐related outcomes: (1) intensive care unit admission, (2) acute renal failure (defined as a creatinine > 1.1 mg/dL), and (3) maternal death. Multiple secondary outcomes were analyzed. Outcomes were compared across groups using generalized estimating equation models and adjusted for significant characteristics.ResultsOverall, 5368 pregnancies met criteria for inclusion: 56% pre‐implementation and 44% post‐implementation. Post‐implementation, 126 code sepsis protocols were activated after providers had clinical concern for infection. An identified infection diagnosis was found in 12.2% of the post‐implementation and 12.1% of the pre‐implementation group with urinary tract infection being the most common. The primary outcome occurred in 7.0% of the pre‐implementation group and 4.6% of the post‐implementation group (odds ratio [OR], 0.66; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.52–0.83; adjusted OR [aOR], 0.64; 95% CI, 0.48–0.83). Stratified analysis by temperature >100.4°F (38°C) or temperature <96.8°F (36°C) revealed an association when criteria were met with hypothermia (OR, 0.55; 95% CI, 0.39–0.78; aOR, 0.48; 95% CI, 0.33–0.70), but not hyperthermia. When activated, the protocol had >92% adherence.ConclusionImplementation of an early‐goal‐directed sepsis care pathway led to improved maternal outcomes in an obstetric population with a 36% reduction in the primary outcome.

INTRODUCTION

Maternal sepsis is a leading cause of preventable maternal morbidity and mortality in the United States [1, 2, 3]. While the overall incidence of maternal sepsis is low, occurring in 0.04% of deliveries, it is a contributing factor in up to 23% of maternal deaths [1]. Early recognition and expedient evaluation and management are key to the prevention of severe morbidity and mortality; however, there is a lack of consensus on appropriate tools to aid in the recognition of maternal sepsis due to their limited validation in a pregnant population [4, 5]. The significant overlap between the physiological adaptations of pregnancy and the signs and symptoms of infection create a diagnostic challenge that can lead to delays in both diagnosis and treatment of sepsis in pregnancy [4, 5, 6, 7].

The Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) created evidence‐based management guidelines that placed emphasis on early‐goal‐directed therapy (EGDT), including rapid evaluation, aggressive fluid resuscitation, and antibiotic treatment [8]. This treatment guideline is known to improve sepsis‐related outcomes in a nonpregnant adult population [9, 10, 11, 12]. Its application and safety in an obstetric setting are understudied, and the most appropriate screening tool to use to initiate EGDT in pregnancy remains unknown [13]. The Society of Maternal‐Fetal Medicine recommends that institutions develop their own procedures and protocols to detect maternal sepsis, avoiding the use of a single screening tool alone, and to use performance improvement programs for sepsis in pregnancy to allow for continual improvement in sepsis recognition and performance [14].

In hopes to improve recognition of maternal sepsis and therapy response times, our institution initiated a “code sepsis” care bundle in October 2016. The objective of our study was to assess the impact of implementation of a standard early‐goal‐directed sepsis care bundle on maternal and neonatal outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

To assess the impact of “code sepsis” at our tertiary care center, we performed a retrospective cohort study between January 1, 2012 and December 31, 2018. Approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB‐300001677) before study initiation. Individuals were eligible for inclusion in the cohort if they were pregnant or within 6 weeks of delivery with a singleton gestation and had vital sign criteria consistent with a systemic inflammatory response as defined by the “code sepsis” care bundle during an inpatient stay or triage visit.

“Code sepsis” was designed as an early response system to quickly identify individuals at risk of deterioration, to standardize the evaluation allowing labs to be drawn more efficiently, and to rapidly bring the expertise of critical care providers to bedside in noncritical areas following a concern for sepsis. To meet criteria for a “code sepsis,” specific vital sign criteria had to be met in addition to the care team's concern for infection. The criteria chosen were as follows: a temperature measurement >100.4°F (38°C) or <96.8°F (36°C) in addition to a heart rate >100 beats/min or respiratory rate >20 breaths/min. These criteria, derived from systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), were adjusted for the physiologic increase in heart rate in pregnancy [14, 15]. When criteria were met, the electronic medical record automatically alerted care team members. In response, any care team member could activate a “code sepsis” if there was a clinical concern for infection. Once activated via paging communication, a “code sepsis” required the immediate evaluation of the patient by the institution's medical emergency team in addition to the activation of an electronic medical record order bundle to guide providers through treatment goals. The medical emergency team included a critical care or internal medicine physician, a pharmacist, and two registered nurses specifically trained to provide urgent critical care. In addition, the obstetric provider and lead nurse of the unit were required to be present. Components of the sepsis electronic medical record order bundle included elements to be completed within both 3‐ and 6‐h time frames. Bundle elements and time frames were derived from the 2012 SSC bundles [16, 17]. The 3‐h bundle elements included obtaining lactic acid, obtaining blood cultures, initiation of broad‐spectrum antibiotics as appropriate, and initiation of an intravenous (IV) fluid bolus. The 6‐h elements included reassessment, repeat lactic acid if indicated, and initiation of vasopressors if indicated. All “code sepsis” activations were reviewed by a sepsis coordinator. The coordinator was a quality care nurse who reviewed all orders and documentation from a code sepsis activation, providing timely feedback to all members of the managing team within a week of the event via email regarding their adherence to time‐specific milestones during the patient encounter. The goal of the feedback was to help improve future performance through provider behavior change as this has been associated with improved outcomes in severe sepsis in a non‐obstetric population [18].

Vital sign criteria for inclusion in this analysis were as follows: at least one documented temperature >100.4°F (38°C) or <96.8°F (36°C) in addition to a heart rate >100 beats/min or a respiratory rate >20 breaths/min within 30 min of the documented temperature. An electronic data pull was used to create the initial temperature dataset from the medical record, identifying any individual with a temperature measurement >100.4°F (38°C) or <96.8°F (36°C) during pregnancy or 6 weeks postpartum during an inpatient stay or a triage visit. Excluded were individuals with no documented heart rate >100 beats/min or respiratory rate >20 breaths/min within 30 min of the qualifying temperature, multiple gestation, or missing vital sign information. The “code sepsis” initiative was implemented on October 1, 2016; therefore, groups were categorized as either pre‐implementation (January 2012–September 2016) or post‐implementation (October 2016–December 2018) based on the date their qualifying vital sign criteria occurred.

The primary outcome was a composite of maternal sepsis‐related outcomes: (1) intensive care unit (ICU) admission, (2) acute renal failure (defined as a creatinine >1.1 mg/dL), and (3) maternal death. There were no identified changes in criteria for ICU admission that occurred during the study period. Secondary maternal outcomes included hospital length of stay, ICU length of stay within 30 days of event, mechanical ventilation use within 30 days of event, vasopressor use within 30 days of event, hospital readmission within 30 days of the event (excluding readmission for delivery), obstetric triage unit visit within 30 days, development of a hypertensive disorder of pregnancy (gestational hypertension, preeclampsia with or without severe features, as diagnosed according to American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologist [ACOG] criteria), gestational diabetes, and any diagnosis of pulmonary edema or treatment with IV diuretic after diagnosis. Neonatal secondary outcomes included a neonatal composite outcome of neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission, neonatal sepsis (confirmed by positive blood culture, positive urine culture, or diagnosis of pneumonia), neonatal death, and arterial blood gas pH < 7.0 or base deficit < −12, Apgar < 7 at 5 min. Additionally, gestational age at delivery, preterm birth (<37 weeks), intrauterine fetal demise, NICU admission, NICU length of stay, and infant length of stay were also analyzed as secondary outcomes for those pregnant at the time of the “code sepsis.” An identified infection diagnosis was also documented if one was present using the first three clinical diagnoses listed in the electronic medical record as generated from an electronic medical record pull.

Baseline characteristics and primary and secondary outcomes were compared across groups using generalized estimating equation (GEE) models. GEE models were used to compare outcomes to account for recurrent pregnancies during the study period. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed using the pre‐implementation group as the reference. Models were adjusted baseline characteristics that did not have multicollinearity. All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 with a significance level of 0.05.

Several additional analyses were performed. We performed a stratified analysis by the presence of hyperthermia versus hypothermia. We repeated the analysis excluding identified “urinary tract infections” (separate from pyelonephritis), the most common infection diagnosis, to evaluate the impact of more severe infection diagnoses in more detail. Finally, we evaluated the adherence to time‐specific characteristics of the “code sepsis” bundle in the post‐implementation group. The proportions of individuals who had each milestone completed and whether it was completed in the recommended time frame were calculated.

RESULTS

Overall, 5368 pregnancies met criteria for inclusion in our study out of 13,993 abstracted in the electronically pulled dataset: 2979 (56%) pre‐implementation and 2389 (44%) post‐implementation (Figure 1). During the post‐implementation group, 126 “code sepsis” protocols were activated after providers had clinical concern for infection etiology during evaluation. Of these events, 120 met all “code sepsis” inclusion criteria, 6 events did not meet criteria but a “code sepsis” was activated. These individuals were included in the final dataset to analyze follow‐through of “code sepsis” steps.

Baseline characteristics comparing pre‐ and post‐implementation groups are displayed in Table 1. Compared to the pre‐implementation group, individuals in the post‐implementation group were more likely to be older and have chronic hypertension. The post‐implementation group was less likely to use tobacco, be nulliparous, and undergo cesarean section. When comparing vital sign characteristics used for study inclusion, the post‐implementation group was more likely to have a temperature <96.8°F (36°C) and less likely to have a temperature >100.4°F (38°C) compared to the pre‐implementation group (Table 1); however, prior to exclusion criteria, overall trends in hypothermia and hyperthermia were unchanged between groups. The post‐implementation group had a slightly lower average heart rate, but a higher average respiratory rate within 30 min of the documented temperature. The average systolic and diastolic blood pressures within an hour of the recorded temperature were higher in the post‐implementation group. The primary outcome occurred in 7.0% of the pre‐implementation group and 4.6% of the post‐implementation group after adjustment for imbalanced covariates, including maternal age, tobacco use, nulliparity, cesarean delivery, and chronic hypertension (OR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.52–0.83; aOR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.49–0.83) (Figure 2). When looking at individual composite components, there was a 45% decreased odds of acute renal failure in the post‐implementation group (OR, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.44–0.79; aOR, 0.55; 95% CI, 0.39–0.77). There was a decrease in ICU admission, but this finding was nonsignificant after adjustment (OR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.53–0.98; aOR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.55–1.10). Maternal death was rare, occurring in 0.24% of all pregnancies and did not differ between groups (Figure 2).

Stratified analysis was performed by temperature criteria (hyperthermia >100.4°F [38°C] vs. hypothermia <96.8°F [36°C]). When temperature criteria were met by hypothermia, there was a 49% decreased odds of the primary composite outcome (OR, 0.55; 95% CI, 0.39–0.78; aOR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.35–0.75) (Figure 3). While there was a trend toward a decreased odds in the primary composite with hyperthermia after adjustment, this was not statistically significant (OR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.74–1.42; aOR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.68–1.40). All individual components of the primary outcome remained lower in the post‐implementation group for those with hypothermia; however, there was no significant difference in composite components when evaluating only those with hyperthermia.

Additional analyses excluded individuals diagnosed with a urinary tract infection (without evidence of pyelonephritis) as urinary tract infection was the most common diagnosis and known to be less severe than other diagnosed infection etiologies. When urinary tract infection was excluded, the primary outcome remained significantly different, occurring in 7.0% of the pre‐implementation group and 4.6% of the post‐implementation group (OR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.51–0.83; aOR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.49–0.83). Individual composite components were significantly decreased for acute renal failure (pre‐ 4.6% vs. post‐ 2.7%, OR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.42–0.77; aOR, 0.54; 95% CI, 0.38–0.76). ICU admission was lower in the post‐implementation group but nonsignificant after adjustment (pre‐ 4.0% vs. post‐ 2.8%, OR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.51–0.97; aOR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.55–1.11). There was no difference in maternal death.

Analysis of adherence to time‐specific characteristics of the “code sepsis” bundle in the post‐implementation group found high compliance with recommendations. During the post‐implementation period, 126 “code sepsis” events were activated. Broad‐spectrum antibiotics, IV fluid boluses, and lactic acid blood draws were completed >92% of the time with >88% completed within the recommended 3‐h time window, with almost all being completed within the first hour (Table 5). Blood cultures were only completed 67.5% of the time. Patient reassessment by a provider following “code sepsis” event occurred 88.1% of the time, 74.6% within the recommended 6‐h time window. A lactic acid draw was repeated in 52.1% of cases on an as‐needed basis with it occurring 67.7% of the time within the recommended time. Vasopressors were rarely necessary (5.6%) and often started outside of the 3‐h time when required.

DISCUSSION

Implementation of a “code sepsis” protocol at our institution was associated with improved maternal outcomes in an obstetric population. There was 36% decreased odds of our primary composite, including ICU admission, acute renal failure, and maternal death in the post‐implementation period of the “code sepsis” protocol. This occurred despite a higher frequency of gestational hypertension and preeclampsia in the post‐implementation period.

It has been well established that performance improvement programs that include sepsis screening tools result in better adherence to sepsis bundles and lead to a reduction in mortality in the nonpregnant, general population [9, 10, 11, 12]. Studies on care bundles in pregnancy are limited. A study of 89 women in a single health center in 2021 reported poor compliance with their implemented “sepsis six care bundle” in pregnant patients, with 37% of patients receiving all six elements of the bundle and only 2% receiving the elements in the recommended time frame [19]. In contrast, our study shows high compliance with bundle elements and adds to the literature associating a sepsis care bundle with improvement in outcomes in an obstetric population. Most bundle elements had >90% compliance, apart from blood cultures that were conceivably deliberately omitted given the clinical scenario, that is, a clear diagnosis of chorioamnionitis or endometritis likely to respond to antibiotics.

The “code sepsis” bundle was activated by a clinical provider 126 times during the post‐implementation period despite 2389 individuals meeting vital sign criteria (5.3%); this highlights the importance of a multimodal approach to include a vital sign screening tool in combination with clinical suspicion of infection in determining which patients are likely to benefit from EGDT. We believe it is likely that many “code sepsis” bundle steps were still undertaken without activating the “code sepsis,” thereby impacting outcomes. While it is impossible to know the number of “missed events,” improvement in activation of appropriate alerts is essential and could be obtained through continued education and timely reminders to providers of the code sepsis bundle's existence, perhaps leveraging AI.

Vital sign criterion in isolation as a screening tool for sepsis in obstetric patients has poor specificity; however, it is a key component of early evaluation to prompt provider response. Widespread research is ongoing in identifying the best screening tool to prompt early intervention and, since the introduction of “code sepsis” criteria at our institution, several tools have been developed and modified for the obstetric population. For example, the Society of Obstetric Medicine Australia and New Zealand (SOMANZ) developed a set of guidelines in 2017, which recommended further evaluation with two or more of the following: systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg, respiratory rate >25 breaths/min, or altered mentation [20]. At the time of our intervention, no specific screening guidelines were recommended in the obstetric population; therefore, our team chose a modification of SIRS criteria based on SIRS proven efficacy in our institution's non‐obstetric population, its simplicity, and its ability to capture a wide range of patients at risk for deterioration. Regardless of the vital sign or symptoms used to prompt evaluation, as emphasized by the Society for Maternal‐Fetal Medicine, it is more important that whatever protocol chosen has appropriate follow‐through on action items associated with early‐goal‐directed care. Our care bundle, when activated, found high compliance with protocol components, including provider reassessment for clinical improvement within 6 h of an event.

On additional analysis, the post‐implementation group had substantial clinical improvement in outcomes for individuals with hypothermia. Implementation of the “code sepsis” bundle and the chosen vital sign criteria may have increased provider attention to these lower values that are not traditionally as alarming as hyperthermia, improving timely implementation of interventions in this group. Utilizing screening criteria that bring attention to these lower temperatures may be an important aspect of a screening tool in the obstetric population.

Finally, we were concerned a priori that aggressive fluid resuscitation with EGDT may lead to fluid overload in an obstetric population [14]. We found a twofold increased odds in preeclampsia following the event where “code sepsis” vital sign criteria were met in the post‐implementation group. The frequency of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy did increase slightly in our overall population during this time. The need for treatment of pulmonary edema remained unchanged between groups. Future studies should further evaluate this finding.

Our study has several strengths. This study had a large sample size in both the pre‐ and post‐implementation groups, and it is one of the few studies on sepsis care bundles to include only pregnant or recently postpartum patients within 6 weeks of delivery. We limited the inclusion to those meeting vital sign criteria for “code sepsis” for whom action is more likely taken. Additionally, our study population was racially and ethnically diverse, thus making our findings generalizable to a broader obstetric population.

Our study is not without limitations. While there were no other identifiable institutional changes or programs related to sepsis care that occurred during the period analyzed, we concede that other unknown factors may have led to the decrease in the primary composite outcome. Furthermore, the dataset from which these variables were abstracted is now several years old. Infection patterns may be different. Our infection diagnosis data were limited to the first three diagnosis codes listed in the electronic medical record for each patient and may have missed an underlying diagnosis. The significant decrease in the identification of urinary tract infections in the post‐implementation group may be due to systemic changes associated with the introduction of code sepsis or may be limited by the way the data were extracted from the records. We did not identify any institutional change around the management of urinary tract infections during the study period. We also acknowledge limited power to identify differences in rare outcomes.

Of note, recommendations from the SSC have now been updated to include 1‐h bundle elements rather than the 3‐ and 6‐h time frames used in our intervention. While currently recommended care bundle elements may be different, our study suggests that appropriate sepsis care bundles can be successfully implemented and may improve outcomes in obstetric populations.

CONCLUSION

We observed that implementation of an early‐goal‐directed sepsis care bundle was not only feasible in an obstetric population but also met with high provider compliance to guidelines and improvement in maternal outcomes. While the incidence of hypertensive disorders appeared higher in the post‐implementation group, the increase appears to be modest and outweighed by the overall benefits. Despite alterations in normal physiology during pregnancy, care bundles may help recognize and improve sepsis‐related outcomes.
