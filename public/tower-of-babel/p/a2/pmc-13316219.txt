Poor postoperative pain control is associated with prolonged length of stay after elective spine surgery: An observational cohort study
Authors: Rena Far, Jay Riva-Cambrin, Steph Casha, Michael M.H. Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProlonged length of stay (LOS) after elective spine surgery is associated with an increased risk of in-hospital complications and higher healthcare costs. This ambispective cohort study investigates the association between poor postoperative pain control and prolonged LOS, while adjusting for known risk factors and perioperative adverse events.MethodsConsecutive adult patients undergoing elective spine surgery were enrolled. The primary outcome was prolonged LOS (≥75th percentile; ≥6 days). The primary independent variable was poor pain control on postoperative day 1 (POD1; mean numeric rating scale >4 in the first 24 hours after surgery). Baseline demographics, surgical characteristics, and perioperative adverse events were collected. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses with multiple imputation were performed to investigate the relationship between poor pain control and prolonged LOS.ResultsIn 1,078 patients, the mean LOS was 4.8 days, and 57% of patients experienced poor pain control. Multivariable analyses revealed poor pain control was independently associated with prolonged LOS (OR 1.83 [95% CI = 1.16–2.89], p = .009), after adjusting for age (p = .27), female sex (p = .002), perioperative blood transfusion (p = .030), postoperative airway/breathing/circulation adverse events (p < .001), constipation (p = .013), urinary tract infection (p = .002), urinary retention (p < .001), hematoma (p < .001), surgical site infection (p < .001), neurologic deterioration (p < .001), POD1 morphine equivalent dose (p < .001), preoperative disability score (p = .04), ASA physical status score (p = .03), smoking status (p = .03), surgical levels (p < .001), estimated blood loss (p = .012), intraoperative dural tears (p = .04), and fusion surgery (p < .001).ConclusionsPoor POD1 pain control was independently associated with prolonged LOS. Improved postoperative pain experience may lead to reduced LOS after elective spine surgery.

Introduction

Prolonged length of stay (LOS) after elective spine surgery is a frequent occurrence with significant costs to patients and the healthcare system. A recent review of over 6,000 patients reported 4.2% of patients remained in hospital 1-week or longer following elective lumbar spine surgery [1]. A recent Canadian cost analysis identified the median cost per bed per additional day was $1,372, with proportionally higher costs if prolonged LOS was associated with an adverse event [2]. Previous efforts to study predictors of prolonged LOS after elective spine surgery have primarily explored non-modifiable risk factors such as patients’ demographics, comorbidities, and surgical complexity [1,[3], [4], [5]]. While these non-modifiable factors are important, they are largely beyond the clinician’s control.

Poor postoperative pain control is a modifiable risk factor that can be effectively optimized in the preoperative and perioperative phases of care. Poor pain control is a common postoperative complication affecting 30% to 64% of spine surgery patients [6]. Poor pain control is also associated with adverse outcomes both during and beyond the initial hospital stay, such as increased postoperative opioid utilization [7], decreased mobility [8], pulmonary complications [9], and development of persistent postsurgical pain [10]. Furthermore, poor pain control in the first 24 hours after spine surgery has been shown to be an independent risk factor for poor patient-reported surgical outcomes up to 2 years after surgery [11], suggesting acute surgical pain itself can affect patients’ long-term clinical course.

The relationship between poor postoperative pain control and prolonged LOS has been touted in the spine literature; however, this association has not been rigorously evaluated. Numerous interventions aimed at improving pain control after spine surgery have been associated with decreased LOS. This includes the use of preemptive analgesia [12,13], postoperative intravenous acetaminophen [14], and minimally invasive spine surgery [13]. These results suggest targeted interventions aimed at improving postoperative pain intensity may help reduce LOS, but they do not establish a direct association between patient-reported pain and LOS after adjusting for known risk factors.

Adverse events following spine surgery are common and are invariably associated with prolonged LOS [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. Further, adverse events after spine surgery are associated with higher incremental cost compared with other orthopedic procedures [2]. Detailed reporting of postoperative adverse events is uncommon in the spine surgical literature, and the specific events imparting the greatest relative impact on LOS are not well established.

The primary goal of this study was to evaluate whether poor postoperative pain control is independently associated with prolonged LOS after elective spine surgery, while adjusting for known risk factors, including perioperative adverse events. By focusing on this modifiable factor, we seek to provide insight into how improving pain management in the early postoperative period may help mitigate prolonged hospital stays after spine surgery. Secondarily, we also investigated the association between perioperative adverse events and prolonged LOS after spine surgery.

Methods

We conducted an ambispective cohort study in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement [19]. Ethics approval was obtained from the University of Calgary’s Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (REB17-2096). All patients provided written informed consent to participate in this study.

Study population

Patients were recruited from among those undergoing elective spine surgery at a single quaternary academic neurosurgical center. Those consenting to participate in research were prospectively and consecutively enrolled in the Canadian Spine Outcomes and Research Network (CSORN) registry. This study cohort included adult patients (≥18 years old) who underwent elective cervical or thoracolumbar spine surgery between August 28, 2014, and October 4, 2017.

Patients were enrolled if they required at least an overnight hospital stay following their surgery. Patients were excluded if they underwent non-elective surgery, concurrent cervical and thoracolumbar surgery, postoperative admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), received intrathecal or epidural analgesia, <2 postoperative pain measurements, or had more than one surgery performed on the same day. Further, we excluded patients who underwent anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) surgery due to their relatively shorter, predictable LOS (in our series, 79.6% of patients were discharged by postoperative day 2) and lower complication rates compared to posterior cervical and thoracolumbar procedures [20,21]. Use of preemptive analgesia, local anesthesia, and postoperative pain management protocols was not standardized.

Data collection

In this ambispective cohort study, all data elements were prospectively collected except for LOS, postoperative pain scores, postoperative morphine equivalent dose (MED), and missing adverse events data, which were retrospectively collected.

For all patients, preoperative characteristics were collected prospectively through the CSORN registry, including the patient’s age, sex, comorbidities, American Association of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status, body mass index (BMI), nicotine use, opioid use, chief complaint, principal pathology, preoperative neck or back pain, Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) depression score, EuroQol-5 dimension (EQ-5D) index score, and degree of preoperative disability as measured by either the Neck Disability Index (NDI) for cervical spine procedures, or the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) for thoracolumbar spine procedures. Surgical characteristics collected include the location (cervical or thoracolumbar), number of levels, minimally invasive surgery (MIS, defined as a muscle-splitting procedure), fusion surgery, revision surgery, operative time, estimated blood loss, and discharge disposition. A retrospective chart review was conducted to collect postoperative pain scores, MED in the first 24 hours after surgery, and missing data.

Inpatient adverse events were prospectively collected through the CSORN registry. A retrospective chart review was also performed to collect missing adverse event data and to confirm the accuracy of recorded adverse events in CSORN using the Canadian Coding Standards, Version 2022 [22]. Adverse events after patient discharge were not recorded. The adverse events were classified based on the Spinal Adverse Events Severity System, version 2 (SAVES-V2) [23]. Intraoperative adverse events included airway/ventilation complications, allergic reaction, cardiac event, cutaneous injury, dural tear, implant/instrumentation-related issue, incorrect operative site, intraoperative revision, massive blood loss, neural injury, and vascular injury. Postoperative adverse events collected included blood transfusion, ICU stay, airway or breathing problems, cardiac event, cerebrovascular event, cutaneous injury, delirium/altered mental status, dysphagia/dysphonia, gastrointestinal bleeding, hematoma, bowel obstruction, implant/instrumentation-related issue, revision surgery, infection (surgical site infection, deep/superficial wound infection, urinary tract infection, other), myocardial infarction, neurological deterioration, pneumonia, renal insufficiency, thromboembolic event, urinary retention, wound dehiscence, wound drainage, and other. Adverse event severity was coded using the SAVES-V2 and dichotomized into grade ≥3 adverse events, denoting those requiring invasive or complex treatment (ie, surgery or ICU stay) [23], and grade <3 adverse events.

After expert consensus, intraoperative complications were grouped to reduce the number of degrees of freedom needed during data analyses: airway/breathing/circulation event, dural tear, implant/instrumentation-related issue, and neurovascular injury. Similarly, postoperative complications were also grouped via expert consensus into airway/breathing/circulation event, delirium, hematoma, constipation/ileus/bowel obstruction, implant/instrumentation-related issue, urinary tract infection, urinary retention, surgical site infection, wound-related complication (dehiscence, drainage or infection), and neurologic deterioration.

Outcome

The primary outcome was prolonged LOS in days, which was defined as being at or above the 75th percentile in our study cohort. This threshold has been used in several previous studies exploring risk factors for prolonged LOS after surgery [4,16,24]. The LOS was calculated based on the number of hospital days at the surgical center and does not include days in other health care facilities (eg, different hospitals, inpatient rehabilitation centers, or long-term care facilities).

Independent variables

The primary independent variable was poor pain control on postoperative day 1 (POD1), defined as a mean numeric rating scale (NRS) score of >4 during the first 24 hours after surgery. NRS is a widely used and validated instrument to measure postoperative pain [8]. The threshold of >4 was chosen as this was the most frequently used threshold to define poor postoperative pain control in a recent systematic review [25]. This threshold was employed in 13 of 33 studies spanning various surgical disciplines [25]. Additionally, this threshold has been frequently employed in the spine surgery literature [11,26,27], and has demonstrated clinical utility as a cutoff point for tolerable postoperative pain due to its association with increased analgesic requirements, negative effects on mood, and impaired sleep [8].

The NRS scores were recorded as part of routine patient care by nurses with no knowledge of the study objectives. Patients verbally rated their pain while lying down by selecting an integer between 0 and 10, with 0 indicating no pain and 10 indicating the worst pain imaginable. Pain assessments were made at a minimum every 6 hours or before administration of analgesic medications, but more frequently if pain was more severe. Pain measurements could be deferred for medically stable patients who were asleep or off unit.

A literature search was conducted to identify known risk factors that impact LOS after spine surgery. These risk factors which were also collected by the CSORN registry included: patient age [1,[3], [4], [5],15,18,24], ASA physical status [1,3,4,15,24,28], female sex [5], BMI [3], preoperative degree of disability [1,28], comorbidities, specifically diabetes [1,4,18], and pulmonary disease [18], and surgical factors such as operative time [3,4,16,24,28], estimated blood loss [16,18,24], fusion surgery [1], and number of surgical levels [3,16,24]. Preoperative opioid use, which was associated with prolonged LOS in some studies [29] but not in others [30,31], was also collected. We aggregated patient comorbidities using the Charlson Comorbidity Index [32]. These variables were used to adjust for the outcome-exposure relationship.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed in STATA [33]. Patient demographics and adverse event totals were summarized using descriptive statistics. Collinearity between independent variables was defined by a correlation coefficient >0.8 [34]. If collinear variables were detected, one was removed after expert consensus.

Univariable logistic regression analyses were performed to investigate the relationship between prolonged LOS and each of the independent variables. All univariable analyses resulting in a p-value <.2 were included in the subsequent multivariable logistic regression analysis. A Markov chain Monte Carlo multiple imputation model with 20 imputations was used to manage missing data. Variables included in the multivariable analyses that required imputation of missing data are: baseline neck/back pain, baseline NDI/ODI score, BMI, PHQ-9 score, preoperative daily opioid use, operative time, and estimated blood loss.

The final model was derived using a backward variable selection method to eliminate nonsignificant variables until all remaining variables had p-values <.05. Age and sex were forced into the multivariable model. We also reported the results of the full multivariable logistic regression, including all variables that met the univariable cutoff. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for these models was calculated.

To assess how complication severity may impact LOS, we performed a sensitivity analysis by repeating the univariable and multivariable analyses described above but including only those adverse events classified as grade ≥3. There were no grade ≥3 events reported for several of the adverse events (urinary tract infection, delirium, constipation/ileus), thus these variables were not included in this analysis. Otherwise, all previously mentioned preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative variables were reassessed.

Sample size

Simulation studies of logistic regression analysis have shown that having at least 10 outcome events for each degree of freedom avoids bias and inaccuracy [35]. In this study, the primary outcome (LOS) was reported in all patients, n = 1,078. If 25% of these patients meet our definition for prolonged LOS, we would have sufficient sample size to evaluate 27 degrees of freedom in our multivariable analysis.

Results

A total of 93.8% of patients were discharged home without supervision following the acute hospital stay. The 75th percentile for LOS, which was our definition and threshold for prolonged LOS, was ≥6 days. Fifty-seven percent of patients experienced poorly controlled pain after surgery. A large proportion of patients (40.1%) reported taking daily opioid medications prior to surgery, demonstrating the preoperative pain burden in this population. Univariable analyses

Variables that were significantly associated with prolonged LOS in univariable analyses are listed in Table 3. Poor postoperative pain control was significantly associated with prolonged LOS (OR 2.85 [95% CI = 2.05–3.97], p < .001). The median LOS was 3 days for all patients, 4 days for those with poor pain control, and 2 days for those without poor pain control (Fig. 2). The mean LOS was 5.6 days in the poor pain control group compared to 3.8 days in those without poor pain control (p = .001). Of note, MIS was not associated with prolonged LOS (p = .58). This is likely a consequence of the study inclusion criteria, as most MIS patients were day surgery patients and thus excluded. Other variables that were not significant included intraoperative airway/breathing/circulation events and intraoperative neurovascular injury, which were both rare. Sensitivity analyses

The analyses were repeated to include only grade ≥3 adverse events. In the univariable analyses, one additional variable, intraoperative dural tear, failed to meet the p < .2 cutoff (OR = 2.47, p = .32). The subsequent grade-stratified multivariable analysis showed poor postoperative pain control remained independently associated with prolonged LOS (OR = 1.65 [95% CI = 1.10–2.49], p = .017) (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

Poor postoperative pain control is a common and clinically meaningful complication after spine surgery, affecting over half of the patients in our study. LOS is an important quality and economic metric in spine surgery, as prolonged LOS is associated with increased risk of in-hospital complications, higher healthcare costs, and greater strain on hospital resources [2]. In this study, we demonstrated that poor postoperative pain control in the first 24 hours after elective spine surgery was independently associated with prolonged LOS, after adjusting for patient demographics, surgical complexity, and perioperative adverse events. Patients with poor pain control had almost 2 times the odds of experiencing prolonged LOS.

The relationship between poor postoperative pain control and prolonged hospital LOS is likely multifactorial. Inadequate pain management impedes early mobilization [8], a cornerstone of postoperative recovery, which in turn can increase the risk of in-hospital complications such as venous thromboembolism, ileus, and delirium [36]. Poor pain control may also lead to increased postoperative opioid usage, which could increase the risk of postoperative urinary retention [37]. In our study, we found that greater POD1 morphine equivalent dose and postoperative urinary retention were both risk factors for prolonged LOS. Further, uncontrolled pain could lead to increased patient anxiety, kinesiophobia, and poor sleep that would impede rehabilitation efforts [8]. These factors, individually and collectively, could contribute to delays in achieving discharge readiness.

Poor postoperative pain control represents a modifiable risk factor for prolonged LOS, which offers a tangible opportunity to improve patient outcomes and reduce health care resource utilization. Many centers have implemented Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols for spine surgery [12,38,39]. ERAS programs provide structured, evidence-based pathways to optimize perioperative pain management. These programs incorporate multimodal analgesia, preemptive analgesics, and regional anesthesia techniques to minimize opioid use and improve pain control [12]. Preoperative risk stratification tools, such as the Calgary Postoperative Pain After Spine Surgery (CAPPS) score, can identify patients at higher risk of developing poor postoperative pain control, allowing for targeted preoperative education and individualized perioperative treatment strategies [26,27]. By prioritizing effective acute pain management through these structured interventions, it may be possible to improve recovery trajectories, enhance patient experience, and reduce hospital LOS following spine surgery.

Our investigation of specific adverse events and their impact on LOS after spine surgery is novel. Previous studies on risk factors for prolonged LOS have aggregated all postoperative adverse events into one variable [16,17]. In this study, we identified several postoperative adverse events that were significantly associated with prolonged LOS and quantified their relative impact on delaying discharge. Our analyses revealed that postoperative adverse events were most strongly associated with prolonged LOS compared to other risk factors, with postoperative surgical site infection having the largest impact (OR 65.35). Interestingly, even complications often perceived as minor, such as constipation, were associated with significant delays in discharge. The majority of our identified risk factors were modifiable, highlighting the need to treat such factors not merely as an inevitable part of the postoperative experience, but rather as opportunities to proactively prevent, detect, and manage postoperative complications to optimize recovery. For example, early postoperative urinary catheter removal, instituting a daily bowel regimen, early mobilization, and implementing a multimodal non-opioid analgesia strategy to minimize opioid use could all serve as strategies to address these modifiable postoperative risk factors.

Although postoperative adverse events were associated with the highest odds of prolonged LOS, the incidence of these adverse events was low in our cohort (range 1.4%–4%). In contrast, over half of patients experienced poor postoperative pain control, higher than all adverse events combined. Given the high prevalence of poor pain control after spine surgery, it is essential to prioritize pain management strategies to improve patient experience and LOS, alongside other strategies to prevent postoperative adverse events.

Among intraoperative factors, we found the number of surgical levels, estimated blood loss, and fusion procedures were significantly associated with prolonged LOS, consistent with previous studies [1,18]. Intraoperative dural tears similarly were associated with prolonged LOS, in line with prior literature [40,41]; however, our complication grade-stratified analysis did not show higher-grade dural tears to be associated with prolonged LOS. This suggests that other risk factors included in our model may be more important in predicting LOS than dural tears.

Our study identified female sex as being significantly associated with prolonged LOS, in line with previous studies [5]. Female sex has also been cited as an independent predictor of poor pain control after spine surgery [26]. The reasons for these findings may relate to both biological and psychosocial factors affecting both how pain is perceived, and how pain is communicated by female vs. male patients [42]. Improved education on anticipated date of discharge after surgery, targeted approaches to anticipate pain trajectories, and improve postsurgical pain management in female patients could be key to decreasing LOS for these individuals.

Interestingly, individuals who were active smokers at the time of surgery had decreased odds of prolonged LOS compared to non-smokers. A similar result has been reported in one prior study evaluating risk factors for prolonged LOS after cervical spine surgery [17]. This seemingly protective impact of smoking on LOS may be in part attributed to motivational factors arising from nicotine withdrawal symptoms experienced while in hospital [17]. Furthermore, our granular reporting and adjustment for perioperative and preoperative risk factors may have disassociated smoking status from its well-established role in precipitating postoperative complications such as infections, pulmonary adverse events, and impaired wound healing, which would typically increase LOS [43]. Because smoking status was self-reported, reporting bias or other unmeasured confounding may have influenced the findings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations owing to its observational nature. While the inclusion of a large, diverse patient cohort undergoing a range of elective spine procedures enhances the generalizability of our findings, this heterogeneity may be viewed as a limitation. We addressed this by excluding ACDF cases, which had uniformly shorter LOS. Cervical vs. thoracolumbar surgeries have different indications and pathologies, and this difference may impact LOS. However, our multivariable model showed that the location of surgery had no impact on prolonged LOS.

Although adverse event reporting was comprehensive, it was possible that some complications were not captured in the registry or in patients’ medical records. For those individuals not discharged directly home from acute care, LOS and adverse events at other inpatient facilities were not recorded in this study and would be a pertinent area for future exploration. We did not collect data on patients’ socioeconomic status, which has been identified as a potential risk factor for prolonged LOS [30], nor could we assess for sampling bias, as patients who declined CSORN participation were not included. Perioperative analgesic strategies and frequency of pain assessments were not standardized, which may have overestimated the proportion of patients with poor pain control. Finally, while the granularity of adverse event reporting allowed us to evaluate the impact of specific complications on LOS, it resulted in small event counts for some adverse events, limiting the precision of effect estimates for these less common complications. Despite these limitations, our study provides robust, clinically relevant insights into modifiable perioperative factors associated with prolonged LOS in elective spine surgery.

Conclusion

In this ambispective cohort study of 1,078 patients at a quaternary academic neurosurgical center, poor pain control in the first 24 hours after elective cervical or thoracolumbar spine surgery was independently associated with prolonged in-hospital LOS. We also identified specific postoperative adverse events, as well as intraoperative factors such as surgical complexity, which were significantly associated with prolonged hospitalization. These findings highlight the critical role of acute pain management and perioperative complication prevention as modifiable factors in reducing hospital LOS after spine surgery. Implementing targeted strategies to reduce postoperative pain may help mitigate extended hospital stays, reduce healthcare costs, and enhance patient outcomes.
