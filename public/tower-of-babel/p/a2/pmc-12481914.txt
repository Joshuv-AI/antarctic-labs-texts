The effectiveness of mobilization in treating patients with temporomandibular disorders: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Prangtip Potewiratnanond, Sudaporn Pummoung, Chidsanu Changsiripun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the effectiveness of temporomandibular joint (TMJ) mobilization in individuals with temporomandibular disorders (TMDs), using data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and before-after studies without control groups. Human studies assessing outcomes, including muscle activity, pain intensity, oral and cervical range of motion, and self-reported measures before and after mobilization, and comparing these results with those of individuals who received other conventional TMD treatments, were considered. Study selection and data extraction were performed by two independent reviewers, with a third reviewer resolving any disagreements. Of the 652 studies identified, five met the inclusion criteria. The evidence shows improvements in pain intensity, range of motion, and other patient-reported outcomes, following TMJ mobilization. These effects were observed immediately after treatment, showed a slight decrease at one week, and were further enhanced at four weeks. Compared to the control group, our meta-analyses demonstrate a significant reduction in pain intensity (mean difference = −2.90) and a significant improvement in mouth opening (mean difference = 10.76) at four weeks post-mobilization. The findings indicate that TMJ mobilization may serve as an effective short-term intervention for managing TMD, especially in the initial stages of treatment. However, further RCTs with long-term follow-up are warranted.Keywords: Mobilization, Temporomandibular disorder (TMDs), Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) are recognized as a significant cause of non-odontogenic problems in the orofacial region [1]. These illnesses encompass pain and dysfunction affecting the temporomandibular joint, masticatory muscles, and other tissues [2]. Previous studies indicate that TMD is particularly prevalent among working-age individuals, leading both economic and societal repercussions due to work absences required for treatment [1], [3], [4]. Conservative treatments, such as intraoral devices, manual therapy, physical therapy, and medication, are recommended for patients with mild to severe TMD due to their safety, ease and cost-effectiveness compared to invasive interventions [5].

Mobilization, a manual therapy approach that uses gentle motions of joints and soft tissues to improve range of motion and decrease discomfort, is frequently used as a conservative treatment for temporomandibular disorders. This non-invasive method can be classified according to its primary target tissues: joints, myofascia, or neurovascular [6]. Prior studies have suggested mechanisms that may underline the therapeutic effects of mobilization, encompassing biomechanical effects, including quantifiable movement and structural alterations within the affected tissues, and neurophysiological effects, such as diminished inflammatory markers, reduced pain sensitivity, modulation of cortical regions associated with pain processing, and activation of the sympathetic nervous system [6].

Previous comprehensive evaluations have indicated that manual therapy may enhance results for individuals with TMDs [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]. However, most of these assessments have focused on manual therapy techniques that encompass several hands-on treatments, such as massage, joint and soft tissue mobility, exercise, and muscular release. Our preliminary search for systematic reviews of manual therapy for treating TMDs yielded eight papers. Six of these articles compared the benefits of several manual treatment approaches, such as massage, exercise, muscular release, and mobilization, by combining all interventions [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]. The remaining investigation focused on the efficacy of upper cervical mobilization strategies [13], [14]. However, to our knowledge, no reviews have explicitly assessed the efficacy of temporomandibular joint (TMJ) mobilization in individuals with TMD. Consequently, a thorough evaluation is needed to synthesize current findings.

This study is to assess the effectiveness of joint mobilization in individuals with TMDs by a systematic review and meta-analysis. We aimed to compare the signs and symptoms of TMD between before and after mobilization, as well as between TMD patients treated with mobilization and those receiving other standard TMD management approaches. The results of this study may yield significant insights to enhance clinical decision-making in the conservative management of TMDs, elucidate the role of mobilization in the treatment of TMD patients, and effectively guide therapeutic options.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This review is reported in accordance with PRISMA Preferred Reporting Items for Systemic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 [15]. Its protocol has been registered in PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD42024590901).

Focused question and eligibility criteria

According to the PICOS framework, the focused question of this systematic review and meta-analysis is “Does manual TMJ mobilization effectively treat TMD?”.

Population

The population included in the study was individuals aged 18–65 years who had been diagnosed with TMD according to the Research Diagnostic Criteria for TMD (RDC/TMD) [16], Diagnostic Criteria for TMD (DC/TMD) [17] or reported signs and symptoms of TMD. Studies on 1) TMD due to infections 2) in patients under systemic medication which affect sign and symptoms of TMD 3) in patients with systematic diseases and other severe comorbidities, including rheumatic disease, neurological diseases, and cancers, were excluded.

Intervention

The intervention analyzed was manual mobilization of the TMJ performed by a physiotherapist using either an active or passive approach. Self-exercise or self-mobilization of the TMJ was excluded.

Comparator

The studies were included when there is 1) comparison between groups treated with manual mobilization versus other TMD management approaches such as oral appliances, laser therapy, exercise, pharmacotherapy, consultations, and placebo treatments 2) comparison between pre-treatment vs post-treatment in mobilization group.

Outcomes

The clinical outcomes included the following measurements: pain score, range of mouth opening, range of lateral movement, jaw function, and muscle force. Self-reported outcomes on jaw function, fear of movement, perception of change, and sleep quality were also included.

Study design

All types of intervention studies, including randomized controlled trial (RCT), non-randomized controlled trial, and before-after studies without control group, provided at least one of the measurements of the signs and symptoms of TMD before and after receiving mobilization were included. The following types of study were excluded: 1) animal studies 2) case reports 3) review articles 4) letters to the editor 5) dissertations or theses 6) conference abstracts.

Search strategy and sources

The search was carried out from inception to September 25, 2024, covering key electronic databases search including Cochrane Central, MEDLINE, CINAHL, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science. No language restrictions were applied. Exploration of grey literature was performed through Google Scholar with a pre-established limit (first 100 articles). Moreover, the reference lists of the chosen publications were manually screened to find further relevant studies.

The key words used for the electronic searches in all fields were developed as follows: (mobilization or rhythmic mobilization or passive mobilization or active mobilization or mobilization with movement or mulligan) and (temporomandibular disorder or temporomandibular joint disorder or temporomandibular pain or temporomandibular joint pain or temporomandibular joint dysfunction or temporomandibular joint syndrome or temporomandibular joint dysfunction syndrome or craniomandibular disorder or craniomandibular dysfunction or TMJ or TMD or TMJD). Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) was used to import all citations for deduplication, selecting studies, and extracting data.

Study selection

Two independent reviewers conducted the initial screening of titles and abstracts to assess the potential relevance of the articles. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. The eligible studies were then accessed for the full text and identified the studies that met the inclusion criteria by two independent reviewers. Again, in case of disagreement, the third reviewer was consulted to reach the final decision.

Data extraction and data items

Data and information of included articles were extracted and collected into the table by separately two reviewers. The table consisted of 1) study information; author’s name, publication year, study design, and country that study was conducted 2) population; sample size, TMD type, TMD diagnostic criteria, inclusion criteria, and exclusion criteria 3) intervention in experimental group 4) treatment in comparison group 5) outcomes measurement 6) follow-up assessment points 7) results. A third reviewer checked the data extraction and resolved conflicts. If any missing data were required, the corresponding authors were contacted via e-mail. Two contact attempts were made, with a two-week interval between them.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

Each included study was assessed using the appropriate tools according to the study design. The RCTs were assessed with Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for randomized trials (RoB2) [18], while the National Institutes of Health (NIH) quality assessment tool for before-after (Pre-Post) study with no control group was applied for the before-after studies [19]. Briefly, RoB2 was composed of 5 domains; randomization process, intended interventions deviations, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and reported result selection. The overall bias was categorized as 1) high risk of bias 2) low risk of bias 3) some concerns. The NIH has 12 components with “Yes”, “No”, or “Cannot determine/Not applicable/Not reported”. The quality of studies was rated as good, fair, and poor based on a “No” response reported 0–3, 4–8, and 9–11, respectively. Two reviewers independently performed the assessment. Any disagreements have been resolved through discussions with a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome parameters in this study are maximal mouth opening (MMO) and pain intensity. MMO is defined as the measurement, in millimeters, of the interincisal distance during active MMO. Pain intensity was assessed using visual analogue scales (VAS) and numeric rating scales (NRS), aligning with the Initiative on Methods, Measurement and Pain Assessment in Clinical Trials (IMMPACT) recommendations [20].

The secondary outcome parameters are jaw (lateral glide) and cervical (flexion-rotation) range of motion. The self-report questionnaires, including Jaw function limitation scale (JFLS), Temporomandibular joint dysfunction disability index (TDI), Tampa scale of kinesiophobia (TSK), Global rating of change (GROC), and Sleep quality questionnaire (SQQ), were additionally evaluated.

To synthesize these continuous variables, the weighted mean difference with 95 % confidence interval (CI) was used to estimate the effect size between the improvements in manual mobilization versus other TMD management groups, and between before versus after receiving manual mobilization at any follow-up assessment points available. The statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using the Chi-square test (p < 0.1) and I² statistic, where the heterogeneity, with low, moderate, and high heterogeneity defined by I² values of 25 %, 50 %, and 75 %, respectively [21]. The random-effect model was applied when statistical heterogeneity was assumed. We plan to perform the subgroup analysis based on the type of mobilization and TMD subgroup classification, if data are available. Moreover, publication bias was planned to be investigated for outcomes with at least 10 studies. Review Manager software version 5.4 (RevMan 5.4, Copenhagen, Denmark) was used to conduct the meta-analyses.

Results

Study selection

Of 652 studies identified, 482 studies remained after the removal of 170 duplicates. During the screening of titles and abstracts, 445 studies were excluded. Thirty-seven studies were sought for full-text review, but three studies were not obtained. Subsequently, the full texts of 34 studies were retrieved and screened against the eligibility criteria. After thorough evaluation of full texts, five studies were included in this review [22], [23], [24], [25], [26]. The most common reason for exclusion was not being involved in TMJ mobilization by physical therapists. A review of grey literature databases and manual searches did not identify any further studies. Consequently, a total of five studies were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1 through the PRISMA flowchart.

PRISMA flow diagram.

The general characteristics of the five studies are summarized in Table 1. There are three RCTs [22], [23], [24] and two before-after studies [25], [26] included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. The studies span various publication years, featuring one article from 1994 and others from 2017 to 2021, reflecting a blend of historical and modern research viewpoints on TMD treatment. The investigations were carried out in several locations: two in Australia, one in Egypt, one in Pakistan, and one in Argentina, hence boosting the generalizability of the findings across diverse populations.

Results synthesis

Muscle activity

A study by Taylor et al. assessed the percentage change in surface electromyographic (EMG) activity of the masseter muscle, demonstrating a considerable reduction relative to the sham therapy [22]. Barone's study similarly evaluated the bilateral activity of the masseter and temporalis muscles by the asymmetry index (AI) utilizing surface EMG [25]. Nonetheless, no substantial difference in the AI was detected after mobilization. Due to the high methodological heterogeneity of the measurements in these two studies, a meta-analysis to pool the EMG results could not be performed.

Pain intensity

Immediately following mobilization, studies by Barone et al. reported a reduction in pain intensity score among TMD patients [25]. Similarly, Grondin et al. and Ashraf et al. observed a decrease in pain intensity score 1-week post-mobilization [24], [26]. Over a 4-week period, mobilization was found to significantly reduce pain intensity score compared to the control group, as reported by Nambi et al., and Ashraf et al. [23], [24]. Moreover, the pressure pain threshold (PPT) of the temporalis and masseter muscles, assessed via algometry in the study by Barone et al., shown a considerable enhancement immediately following mobilization therapy [25].

A meta-analysis was performed to assess the impact of mobilization on pain intensity scores, as illustrated in Fig. 3. When compared to the control group, it demonstrates that the pain intensity score showed a significant improvement in patients receiving mobilization four-week post-treatment (MD = −2.90, 95 % CI: −3.96 to −1.85, I2 = 89 %).

Forest plot comparing pain intensity between patients receiving mobilization and control at 4 weeks.

Range of motion

Cervical range of motion

The research conducted by Grondin et al. reported that an improvement was observed for range of motion of the upper cervical spine [26]. Although, the total range of motion (ROM) of the cervical spine in sagittal plane (including full flexion and extension) did not differ, the flexion-rotation test (FRT) demonstrated significant improvement on both to the left (p = 0.024) and right (p = 0.001) sides after receiving grade III transverse medial glide to the pain side of TMJ.

Range of jaw movement

Research conducted by Barone et al., and Taylor et al. indicated that range of mouth opening increases immediately following mobilization [22], [25]. A significant improvement in range of lateral mandibular movement was also reported [22]. Additionally, Grondin et al. found that mobilization tends to enhance mouth opening in TMD patients one week after treatment [26]. A significant improvement in range of mouth opening was observed at 4 weeks post-mobilization when compared to the control group, as reported in the study by Nambi et al., and Ashraf et al. [23], [24].

A meta-analysis was performed to assess the impact of mobilization on the range of mouth opening, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Compared to the control group, it demonstrates a significant enhancement in mouth opening in patients receiving mobilization four-week post-treatment (MD = 10.76, 95 % CI: 1.70–19.82, I2 = 96 %).

Forest plot comparing range of mouth opening between patients receiving mobilization and control at 4 weeks.

Self-reported questionnaires

The 17 items of the TSK (TSK-17) utilized by Nambi et al. demonstrated a notable alteration after the four-week and three-month follow-ups [23]. The GROC scale was also employed to evaluate participants' perceptions of overall change in research [23]. Nambi et al. exhibited notable enhancements in GROC scores and sleep quality, evaluated via the SQQ, at both the four-week and three-month follow-ups in comparison to the control group [23].

The JFLS and the TDI were evaluated in research conducted by Grondin et al., and Nambi et al. [23], [26]. Grondin et al. revealed substantial changes in JFLS scores when comparing baseline measurements to those recorded one-week post-treatment [26]. Nambi et al. demonstrated significant improvements in TDI scores at both the four-week mark and the three-month follow-up [23].

Discussion

Summary of findings

Findings from this meta-analysis and comprehensive review point to TMJ mobilization as a promising treatment option for patients with myogenous, arthrogenous, or mixed types of TMD. The investigations revealed improvements in pain levels, oral and cervical range of motion, and self-reported oral function following TMJ mobilization therapies compared to control or other conservative treatments. The effectiveness of mobilization for TMD appears to be immediate after the intervention, with a slight decrease in positive effects at the 1-week follow-up, followed by greater effectiveness at the 4-week follow-up.

The findings align with previous systematic reviews on the effectiveness of manual therapy for TMD [7], [8], [10], [12]. Studies conducted by Martins et al. [7], Asquini et al. [8], Calixtre et al. [10], and Vieira et al. [12] have shown marginally positive short- and long-term outcomes in pain relief, mandibular mobility, and self-reported disability after manual therapy in comparison to control groups. Furthermore, these reviews emphasized the advantages of integrating manual therapy with additional interventions, such as photobiomodulation (PBM) therapy, Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), therapeutic exercise, or Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), resulting in improved outcomes in pain alleviation and mouth opening. Recently, photobiomodulation (PBM) therapy has been identified as a viable conservative method for managing TMD, showing advantageous benefits on pain alleviation and functional restoration [27]. The proposed multimodal biological mechanisms primarily involve the targeting of mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase, resulting in increased Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, modulation of oxidative stress, and enhanced cellular respiration, which collectively promote tissue repair and regeneration [28]. Despite interest in conservative treatment strategies, current evidence remains insufficient to determine the most effective conservative interventions, due to the multifactorial nature of TMD and the complexity of illnesses. Additionally, our meta-analysis was unable to identify the most effective conservative treatment for TMD. These findings highlight the need for further well-designed clinical trials to evaluate and compare the efficacy of various conservative therapies. Such research is essential to guide the development of targeted, evidence-based, and individualized treatment protocols for patients with different TMD subtypes.

Previous reviews frequently encompassed a wide array of manual interventions (e.g., massage, soft tissue release, spinal manipulation, joint mobilization), complicates the ability to isolate the distinct impact of joint mobilization. Our analysis offers a focused strategy, exclusively investigating TMJ mobilization, either independently or alongside conservative therapies. This limited scope facilitates a more accurate assessment of the exact effect size of mobilization. Our findings indicate results that are equivalent to, or marginally superior than, those in extensive manual treatment evaluations, likely attributable to the methodological precision of this investigation. Variations in inclusion criteria, outcome measures, and intervention heterogeneity among reviews may explain minor discrepancies in effect size and clinical significance.

Joint mobilization can alleviate pain in a number of musculoskeletal disorders when utilized as a stand-alone treatment [29], [30], [31]. It is possible to explain the pain-relieving benefits of mobilization by referring to neurophysiological systems. These mechanisms are responsible for the change in mechanics that occurs during mobilization. Mobilization is characterized by using rhythmic motions that stimulate joint proprioceptors, also known as mechanoreceptors [32], [33]. This stimulation causes a spinal cord-mediated effect through the gate control theory, which ultimately results in a reduction in the feeling of pain [34].

Muscle rigidity is increased by pain-induced stretch reflex stimulation, which may be a defense mechanism to restrict mobility [35]. Because mobilization has the ability to lessen pain, it also has the effect of reducing the stretch reflex, which results in a reduction in muscular spasms. Normal joint movements are controlled by the joint capsules, tendons, and muscles. After an injury, adhesions and collagen cross-links in the joint capsule and ligaments may become the primary extra-articular factors contributing to restricted joint movement. Joint mobilization can enhance movement by disrupting collagen cross-links, which resulting to decrease joint stiffness [36]. It has been shown to reduce both synovial and articular stiffness, leading to significant improvements in joint function and range of motion [34].

It is also possible that the effectiveness of joint mobilization will differ from patient to patient, depending on how they perceive the treatment [37]. There is a substantial possibility that patients' pain response can be significantly influenced by psychological factors, such as their expectations and beliefs regarding the treatment [38]. This could possibly either increase or decrease the perceived advantages of mobilization. Neurobiological mechanisms underlying placebo analgesia have been extensively investigated [39], [40], [41], [42]. Placebo-induced pain relief engages not only bottom-up pathways associated with nociceptive processing but also top-down modulatory mechanisms that influence pain perception [38]. These top-down processes are shaped significantly by an individual’s prior experiences with analgesic treatments. For instance, individuals with a history of opioid use may exhibit placebo analgesia mediated predominantly by activation of endogenous opioid systems, whereas those previously treated with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) may show analgesic effects via endocannabinoid pathways [41], [43]. These findings underscore the critical role of learning mechanisms in shaping placebo responses at the neurophysiological level. This subjective variability is frequently evaluated using questionnaires that require self-reporting. In this review, two out of five studies [23], [26] utilized a variety of self-reported tools, which included JFLS, TDI, TSK, GROC, and SQQ. These tools were used in conjunction with physical symptom evaluations. Following mobilization treatment, all these investigations [23], [26] found that the improvements assessed with these questionnaires matched the clinical changes documented in the patients' physical complaints.

Clinical implications

The findings suggest that TMJ mobilization could be particularly beneficial for adults experiencing myogenous or arthrogenous TMD characterized by pain and restricted mouth opening. This is supported by both objective assessments (PPT and range of motion) and subjective evaluations (pain intensity scores and self-reported questionnaires). Although subgroup analysis in specific TMD patients or mobilization techniques is impractical due to study heterogeneity, the consistent improvements noted across studies addressing these subtypes provide support for this implication. Joint mobilization has the potential to enhance clinical outcomes when combined with other conservative therapies, such as therapeutic ultrasound [23]. The identified synergistic effects require further investigation and may guide the development of multimodal treatment protocols in clinical practice.

Limitations

This study, comparable to many scientific pursuits, possesses certain limitations that warrant acknowledgment. Initially, variations in the baseline characteristics of the included studies were observed, particularly with the diagnostic criteria for TMD, the severity and duration of the illness, and the distribution of age and gender. All studies included adult participants aged 18–65 years, categorized into myogenous, arthrogenous, and/or mixed TMD subgroups. Additionally, the heterogeneity in study designs and intervention protocols, especially in relation to mobilization techniques and comparator interventions, limited the feasibility of conducting meaningful subgroup analyses. A further concern is the restricted number of included studies in this review, with three RCTs and two before-after studies, which may limit the robustness and generalizability of the findings. The limited sample sizes of the included studies reduced the statistical power and precision of the effect estimates. Moreover, the included studies examined predominantly addressed short-term outcomes, with follow-up durations extending from immediate post-treatment to a maximum of three months. The absence of extended follow-up (e.g., 6–12 months) must be taken into account when analyzing the results.

In terms of risk of bias, one RCT was rated as having a high risk of bias [24], while the other two had some concerns [22], [23], particularly regarding randomization and blinding, which could potentially affect the reliability of the results. Some of the included before-after studies also failed to blind outcome assessors to the participants’ interventions. These unblinded assessments may have led to inflated effect sizes, making TMJ mobilization appear more effective than it truly is. Although we initially planned to conduct sensitivity analyses to explore the impact of high-risk studies on the pooled estimates and to advocate for improved methodological rigor in future RCTs, these analyses could not be performed due to the limited number of studies included in each meta-analysis. Furthermore, a formal assessment of publication bias was not feasible due to the small number of studies (<10). Therefore, our findings should be interpreted with caution, as all these limitations may compromise the overall strength and reliability of the conclusions.

Future research should prioritize multicenter randomized controlled trials to improve statistical power and generalizability. It is essential to adopt standardized diagnostic criteria, such as DC/TMD, to ensure homogeneous patient cohorts and facilitate valid subgroup analyses (e.g., myogenous vs. arthrogenous TMD). Implementing core outcome sets like IMMPACT recommendations with standardized follow-up intervals (e.g., baseline, 1, 3, 6, and 12 months) will support rigorous longitudinal meta-analyses. Additionally, employing comparative-effectiveness designs that integrate joint mobilization with other conservative interventions (e.g., exercise therapy, low-level laser therapy, CBT) can help explore potential synergistic effects. Long-term follow-up and cost-effectiveness assessments are also necessary to evaluate the sustainability and economic impact of mobilization protocols.

Conclusion

The results of this systematic review and meta-analysis provide credence to the idea that temporomandibular joint mobilization is a useful tool for treating TMD problems. Mobilization methods exhibited notable enhancements in pain intensity, mouth opening range, and oral function relative to control or alternative conservative treatments. The efficacy of mobilization seems to be immediate to short-term. The findings indicate that mobilization should be seen as a potential therapy option for patients with TMD, either as an independent intervention or in conjunction with other conservative therapies.
