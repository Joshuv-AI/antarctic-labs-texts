Cell surface GRP78 and the UPR pathway as novel promising therapeutic targets in cancer: an insight into the treatment of acute leukemia by using GRP78-CART cells
Authors: Nadia Giovanna Román-Anguiano, Paola Andrea Gutiérrez-Muñoz, Tania Angeles-Floriano, Nancy Lucero Martínez-Rodríguez, Laila Gutiérrez-Kobeh, Maria C Jimenez-Martinez, Ricardo Valle-Rios
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAround 70s decade, the discovery of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) chaperones as central regulators of protein folding and quality control set a new paradigm for the understanding of cellular homeostasis. With the arrival of global proteomic approaches, several ER chaperones, including the 78-kDa glucose-regulated protein (GRP78), were unexpectedly found at the cell surface of cancer cells, linking activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR) to tumor biology. Current knowledge suggests that cell surface GRP78 (csGRP78) is a promising therapeutic biomarker, as it is virtually absent on normal cells but enriched on multiple cancers, particularly leukemias. This review summarizes the roles of the three main UPR regulators—IRE1, ATF6, and PERK—and their crosstalk with csGRP78 in leukemia, emphasizing how this network can promote survival or apoptosis. Finally, recent preclinical studies using GRP78-directed CAR-T cells are discussed, highlighting csGRP78 and the UPR pathway as attractive targets for leukemia immunotherapy.Keywords: GRP78, Acute lymphoblastic leukemia, CART cells

Introduction

The glucose-regulated protein 78 kDa (GRP78) was first described in chicken embryonic fibroblasts transformed by Rous sarcoma virus, when Stone and colleagues and Isaka et al. independently reported the up-regulation of a ~ 70-kDa protein in virus-infected cells [1, 2]. In 1977, Shiu and co-workers observed that a 78-kDa protein was regulated by glucose concentration in the culture medium and proposed the term “glucose-regulated protein 78” (GRP78) [3].

GRP78, also known as heat-shock-protein family A (Hsp70) member 5 (HSPA5) or immunoglobulin heavy-chain binding protein (BiP), belongs to the Hsp70 family and is normally localized in the lumen of the ER of eukaryotic cells. GRP78 participates in multiple essential processes, including: (i) translocation and folding of nascent polypeptides across the ER membrane; (ii) targeting of misfolded proteins for ER-associated degradation (ERAD); (iii) regulation of calcium homeostasis; and (iv) sensing of ER stress as a master regulator of the UPR [4–6].

Under homeostatic conditions, GRP78 binds to and keeps inactive the three ER membrane stress sensors that initiate the UPR in response to misfolded protein accumulation: (i) inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1); (ii) activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6); and (iii) the PKR-like ER kinase (PERK) [8, 9]. Upon ER stress, GRP78 dissociates from these sensors, which become activated and initiate signaling cascades attempting to restore ER homeostasis or, if stress is excessive or prolonged, trigger cell death [7, 9].

Intriguingly, once the UPR is initiated, a fraction of GRP78 is relocalized from the ER to the cell surface (csGRP78) and it has now been described as a hallmark in several human cancers, including pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL-B) and various solid tumors, and it is increasingly recognized as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target [5, 10–15]. In this review, we focused on the functional links between GRP78 and the UPR in leukemia, the evidence supporting csGRP78 as a leukemia-associated antigen, and the opportunities and challenges of targeting csGRP78 with CAR-T cells. In addition to its well-established roles in cancer, cell-surface GRP78 has recently been implicated in viral infections, including SARS-CoV-2, where csGRP78 may act as a co-receptor or facilitator of viral entry, underscoring its broader relevance as a stress-inducible surface protein beyond oncology.

UPR regulators

IRE1

IRE1 is a type I transmembrane protein located in the ER membrane. Its amino-terminal fragment faces the ER lumen and contains the dimerization domain, whereas the carboxy-terminal fragment faces the cytosol and harbors a serine/threonine kinase domain and an endoribonuclease effector domain [16, 17]. In humans, two isoforms exist: IRE1α, expressed in virtually all cell types, and IRE1β, whose expression is largely restricted to the gastrointestinal tract and lung mucosal epithelium [18, 19].

Accumulation of unfolded proteins in the ER lumen causes dissociation of GRP78 from IRE1 (Fig. 1), promoting IRE1 self-association and oligomerization through its luminal domain, and trans-autophosphorylation of the cytosolic kinase domain [18, 20]. This conformational change activates the endoribonuclease function of IRE1, which catalyzes the unconventional splicing of XBP1 mRNA, generating the spliced transcription factor XBP1, thus upregulating genes involved in protein folding, ERAD and lipid synthesis [21].

Canonical UPR activation under ER stress. Misfolded protein accumulation in the ER causes GRP78 to release PERK, ATF6, and IRE1, leading to signaling cascades that initially restore ER homeostasis but can trigger apoptosis if stress is severe or prolonged

ATF6

ATF6 is a type I transmembrane protein residing in the ER membrane. Its cytosolic domain contains a basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factor [17, 20]. Upon ER stress, ATF6α is transported to the Golgi apparatus where it is sequentially cleaved by site-1 protease (S1P) and site-2 protease (S2P), releasing the cytosolic fragment [17, 20]. This cleaved fragment translocates to the nucleus and activates transcription of ER chaperone genes and ERAD components to restore ER function (Fig. 1) [20, 23].

PERK

PERK is a type I transmembrane serine/threonine kinase localized in the ER membrane [20]. Under basal conditions, PERK remains inactive by binding to GRP78 through its luminal domain, while the cytosolic domain contains the kinase activity. ER stress triggers GRP78 dissociation, PERK oligomerization, and trans-autophosphorylation, resulting in PERK activation (Fig. 1) [17, 22, 24].

Active PERK phosphorylates the eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) at serine 51, transiently inhibiting global protein synthesis and decreasing the influx of newly synthesized proteins into the stressed ER [9, 24, 25]. Additionally, eIF2α phosphorylation increases the translation of mRNAs such as activating transcriptional factor 4 (ATF4) [22]. ATF4 activates important target genes: (i) the transcriptional factor C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP), involved in cell death by apoptosis, (ii) the growth arrest and DNA damage-inducible 34 (GADD34). This factor is responsible for encoding a PERK-induced regulatory subunit of protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) to modulate the activity of eIF2α and (iii) the activating transcription factor 3 (ATF3) [8, 25, 26].

Cell surface GRP78 in cancer

Cellular stress may promote GRP78 relocalization to the plasma membrane, where it interacts with various ligands and works as an atypical receptor, activating signaling pathways involved in proliferation, migration, invasion, apoptosis, inflammation, and immunity [5].

In 2003, Shin et al. showed for the first time that several ER chaperones, including GRP78, are also located to the cell surface membrane of different cancer cell types. Using global cell surface proteomics, they detected GRP78 on the surface of isolated ovarian tumor cells and on A549 (lung cancer), LoVo (colorectal cancer), and SH-SY5Y (neuroblastoma) cell lines. In tumor cells from lung cancer and malignant human breast tumors, GRP78 levels were found to be elevated [13, 14], and GRP78 overexpression has also been reported in human colon carcinoma cells [15]. In C4-2B and MCF7 tumor cells, GRP78 was also observed on the cell surface, and an anti-GRP78 monoclonal antibody (MAb159) targeting csGRP78 blocked PI3K signaling and induced cell death, illustrating that csGRP78 can be functionally exploited for therapy in solid tumors [27].

Cell-surface GRP78 in acute lymphoblastic leukemia: therapeutic potential

Cell-surface GRP78 has been reported in a broad spectrum of solid tumors and hematologic malignancies, but its prevalence and functional relevance appear to differ across cancer types [10–15, 22, 33–36]. Proteomic approaches first identified csGRP78 on ovarian cancer cells and multiple solid tumor cell lines under enhanced ER stress conditions. In hematologic malignancies, csGRP78 is consistently detected on leukemic B cells from patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), on pre-B-ALL cell lines and primary high-risk ALL samples, and on AML cell lines, such as OCI-AML3, K562, and KBM-7 [10, 33–36], and importantly, in bone marrow samples from healthy donors, low or n csGRP78 was detected [10].

In childhood high-risk ALL-B, expression of csGRP78 together with CXCR4 defines a subpopulation of leukemic B cells associated with high-risk disease at diagnosis, suggesting that csGRP78 is not only present but enriched in clinical aggressive clones. Also, leukemic B cells from bone marrow samples coexpress csGRP78 with classical leukemia-cell markers, such as CD10, CD34, and CD38, supporting its potential use in refining risk stratification and in defining targetable antigen combinations [10].

Finally, csGRP78 + leukemic cells circulate in peripheral blood, where they are more accessible to systemically delivered agents than many solid tumors, which are often protected by dense stromal barriers and abnormal vasculature. The combination of frequent csGRP78 expression on leukemic blasts, its association with a high-risk phenotype, and the relatively good accessibility of hematologic malignancies makes leukemia an attractive scenario to test csGRP78-directed immunotherapies [10, 33–36, 68, 69].

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most common type of cancer in pediatric patients. Approximately 85% of childhood ALL cases are of B-lineage, while newly diagnosed T-lineage cases are less frequent. In contrast, acute myeloid leukemia (AML) arises from malignant myeloid stem or progenitor cells that would normally differentiate into white blood cells, red blood cells, and platelets, and represents roughly 15% of all childhood leukemias [28–32].

In ALL, immunofluorescence and flow cytometry analyses demonstrated GRP78 expression on the surface of leukemic B cells, providing one of the first pieces of evidence that csGRP78 can be present on malignant hematopoietic cells [33]. In B-ALL models, an increase in HSPA5 expression has been observed, and confocal imaging in several ALLcell types (ALL-1, NALM-6, and cells from a newly diagnosed high-risk ALL patient) revealed GRP78 localization on the cell surface. Genetic deletion of HSPA5 in pre-B-ALL cells lead to severe ER stress and cell death, underscoring that GRP78 is essential for leukemic cell survival, while combined inhibition of XBP1/ERN1 and conventional chemotherapy has been proposed as a strategy to overcome therapy resistance in ALL [34, 35].

Recently, in childhood high-risk ALL, Angeles-Floriano et al. demonstrated that leukemic B cells from bone marrow samples express GRP78 on their cell surface together with CD10, CD34, CD38, and CXCR4, and showed that coexpression of csGRP78 and CXCR4 identifies a subpopulation associated with high-risk disease at diagnosis. In the same study, cell surface GRP78 was also quantified by flow cytometry on bone marrow mononuclear cells from non-leukemic pediatric controls and csGRP78 levels were very low or undetectable compared with leukemic blasts, indicating a clear separation between high-risk B-ALL cells and normal hematopoietic cells at diagnosis [10].

This differential expression pattern supports the concept that csGRP78 is highly enriched on leukemic cells while being minimally expressed on non-malignant bone marrow populations and provides an experimental basis for considering csGRP78 not just as a biomarker but as a therapeutic target in acute leukemias [10, 33–36, 68, 69].

UPR in leukemia: beyond GRP78

UPR activation in leukemia not only controls GRP78 levels but also shapes leukemic cell fate through the three canonical sensors PERK, IRE1, and ATF6. Understanding how each arm contributes to survival or apoptosis is relevant for strategies that combine UPR modulation with csGRP78-directed CAR-T cells.

PERK in leukemic cells

Several studies have described the expression and functional role of PERK in leukemic cells. In a pan-cancer analysis, PERK expression was found to be differentially regulated in leukemia samples, suggesting that PERK contributes to leukemia biology and may have prognostic value [37]. For example, in UPR-activated AML cells, PERK shows greater activation than IRE1 or ATF6, leading to increased expression of CHOP and ATF4 and to repression of mitochondrial respiration through AMPK activation by GSK621, which enhances apoptotic priming of mitochondria [38].

Pharmacologic induction of ER stress with tunicamycin in human leukemic cells activates PERK and CHOP, leading to apoptosis and providing proof-of-concept that PERK-dependent UPR signaling can be exploited therapeutically [39, 40]. Conversely, in AML KG1 cells, combined inhibition of PERK activation and the histone methyltransferase G9a increases phosphorylated p38 and enhances apoptotic cell death, whereas pretreatment with the antioxidant N-acetyl-L-cysteine abrogates this effect, highlighting the interplay between PERK, reactive oxygen species, and apoptosis in leukemia cells [41].

The NLRP3 inflammasome has been implicated in myeloid cell proliferation; NLRP3 deletion induces ER stress, activates the PERK/eIF2α/CHOP axis, and promotes apoptosis in AML models, supporting the notion that excessive PERK signaling can shift from adaptive to pro-apoptotic outcomes [42, 43]. In contrast, in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), the PERK–eIF2α phosphorylation arm acts as a pro-survival pathway downstream of BCR-ABL and confers resistance to imatinib, indicating that PERK activity may have opposite functional consequences in leukemia in a subtype and oncogenic context-dependent manner [44].

In T-ALL, leukemic-cell-derived exosomes activate PERK in endothelial cells, modulating the expression of SCF and CXCL12 and contributing to remodeling of the bone marrow vascular niche and leukemic cell dissemination [45]. Furthermore, the use of compounds in cancer models such as farnesol may increase the expression of PERK-eIF2 α-ATF3/4 genes, exerting pro-apoptotic and anti-proliferative functions as occurred in T lymphoblastic leukemia Molt4 cells [46, 47].

UPR-targeted cytotoxicity is therefore unlikely to be uniformly effective across all leukemia subtypes. In AML, predominant PERK activation enhances apoptotic priming and sensitizes cells to ER-stress-inducing agents, whereas in CML, the same PERK–eIF2α axis promotes survival and drug resistance. These context-dependent roles must be considered when designing PERK-modulating therapies and when combining UPR modulators with csGRP78-directed CAR-T cells.

Also, transcription factors are involved. For example, Myc enhancing ERAD in a UFD1-dependent pathway favoring leukemia cells has been reported. Importantly, UFD1 inactivation in T-ALL cells induced apoptosis primarily through the activation of PERK/CHOP/BCL2 [48]. Moreover, deleting PERK from Myc-expressing leukemic cells causes them to disseminate in the peripheral blood and travel to other organs or tissues [49].

Interestingly, PERK also intersects with sphingolipid metabolism: deletion of 3-ketodihydrosphingosine reductase (KDSR), essential to maintain ER homeostasis, induces ER membrane damage, apoptotic cell death, and cell-cycle arrest in leukemia cells, and suppresses PERK and ATF6 levels, further illustrating how ER membrane composition influences UPR signaling and leukemia survival [50].

These findings indicate that PERK signaling can exert either pro-apoptotic or pro-survival functions depending on the leukemic context, UPR intensity, and oncogenic background, implying that PERK-targeted strategies will need to be carefully tailored to disease subtype.

IRE1 in leukemic cells

IRE1 has also been implicated in leukemic cell survival and therapy response. In AML models, activation of IRE1 endoribonuclease function promotes XBP1 splicing, and inhibition of IRE1α induces caspase-dependent apoptosis and G1 arrest, at least in part through suppression of XBP1s, indicating that AML cells depend on IRE1–XBP1s signaling [51]. In CML, XBP1s is required for leukemia stem cell (LSC) survival and self-renewal, and its inhibition reduces survival and eliminates LSCs in murine CML models, highlighting XBP1s as a potential therapeutic check point for CML survival [56].

Valosin-containing protein (VCP/p97), an ATPase involved in protein quality control, is upregulated in several solid tumors and is associated with poor prognosis [52–54]. In pediatric AML, VCP expression negatively correlates with IRE1 and GRP78 levels, and low VCP expression with moderately high IRE1 expression is associated with improved response to bortezomib, suggesting that the relative balance between VCP and UPR proteins shapes stress sensitivity and could serve as a biomarker for proteasome inhibitor response [55]. In cultured cells from AML and in AML patients, overexpression of the mature (XBP1) and spliced (XBP1s) forms has been observed. Blocking XBP1 mRNA splicing demonstrated cytotoxicity against AML cells. Furthermore, inhibition of IRE1α induced caspase-dependent apoptosis and G1 cell cycle arrest, at least partially [57].

N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10) is highly expressed in AML, and its inhibition suppresses IRE1, CHOP, and PERK expression, thereby enhancing ER stress-induced apoptosis and further supporting the notion that AML cells rely on intact UPR signaling for survival [58]. Additional studies have shown that platinum nanoparticles or CDK inhibitors can modulate IRE1–XBP1 signaling and sensitize leukemic cells to ER-stress inducers, again pointing to the IRE1 axis as a pharmacological target in leukemia [59, 60].

Overall, AML and CML models reveal an urgent requirement of leukemia and leukemia stem cells for IRE1–XBP1s signaling, while pediatric AML data suggest that the balance between IRE1/GRP78 and VCP expression may refine patient stratification and predict responsiveness to proteasome or ER-stress-modulating therapies.

ATF6 in leukemic cells

ATF6 is the third canonical UPR sensor. Its activation requires S1P and S2P proteases. The cleaved ATF6 fragment translocates to the nucleus, where it regulates chaperon transcription and ERAD components to restore ER function [6, 9].

In AML, inhibition of ceramide transfer protein (CERT) blocks ceramide transport from the ER to the Golgi, inhibits leukemic cell growth, and triggers apoptosis through a GRP78/ATF6/CHOP-dependent pathway, linking sphingolipid metabolism, ATF6 activation, and leukemia cell death [61]. Overexpression of ATF6 can also induce apoptosis through WW domain-binding protein 1 (WBP1), further supporting a pro-apoptotic arm of ATF6 signaling under certain conditions [62]. Moreover, a model has been proposed in which UPR activation via calreticulin and ATF6 suppresses CEBPA and impairs myeloid differentiation in AML, illustrating how ATF6-driven stress responses can contribute to leukemogenesis beyond cell survival per se [63].

In CLL, ATF6 and C/EBPβ regulate expression of death-associated protein kinase 1 (DAPK1), a protein associated with autophagy and apoptosis that is often lost in tumors. ATF6–C/EBPβ-dependent induction of DAPK1 restores autophagy in CLL samples, illustrating a tumor-suppressive function of ATF6 in this context [64].

Thus, ATF6 can either promote apoptosis in AML cells via GRP78/ATF6/CHOP and WBP1 or exert tumor-suppressive functions in CLL by restoring DAPK1-dependent autophagy, again emphasizing the context-specific consequences of UPR activation in leukemia.

Key studies on GRP78 and UPR signaling in leukemia

CAR-T cells: CSGRP78 as a novel therapeutic target

Chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) applied for T-cell (CART) therapy have emerged as a transformative immunotherapy for leukemia and other cancers. CAR-T cells recognize specific antigens on the surface of malignant cells coupling antibody-like recognition with T-cell effector functions. A typical CAR contains four domains: (i) an extracellular antigen-binding domain (usually a single-chain variable fragment); (ii) a spacer or hinge domain; (iii) a transmembrane domain anchoring the receptor; and (iv) an intracellular signaling domain comprising CD3ζ with one or more co-stimulatory motifs (Fig. 2) [65–67].

Basic structure of a CAR-T cell. A standard second-generation CART cell is depicted. Classic used domains for cell signaling are shown

In leukemia models, only a few studies have explored CAR-T cells targeting GRP78, but the available data are encouraging. In AML cells expressing csGRP78, CAR-T cells directed against csGRP78 showed potent antitumor activity both in vitro and in vivo, eliminating human AML cells without affecting normal hematopoietic stem cells, and thus demonstrating antigen-specific cytotoxicity and sparing of hematopoietic progenitors [68]. A second AML study confirmed that GRP78-CAR-T cells recognized and killed AML cells with csGRP78 expression while sparing hematopoietic progenitor cells (HPCs), supporting a favorable therapeutic window within the hematopoietic compartment [69].

These preclinical findings are consistent with our observations in childhood B-ALL, where flow cytometric analyses revealed strong csGRP78 expression on leukemic blasts but very low to undetectable csGRP78 on non-leukemic pediatric bone marrow control cells at diagnosis. Together, these data suggest that, at least in the hematopoietic compartment, csGRP78 is markedly enriched on malignant cells and largely spared on normal progenitors, supporting a favorable outcome in a GRP78-directed CAR-T cells therapy [10, 33–36, 68, 69].

Beyond leukemia, several experimental models have explored GRP78-CAR-T cells in solid tumors. In lung cancer, A549 and H1299 cells and A549-derived cancer stem cells exhibit high csGRP78 expression, and csGRP78-targeting CAR-T cells effectively killed these cells in vivo without evident toxicity in other organs [70]. csGRP78 has also been reported in glioblastoma and pancreatic cancer cell lines and their stem-like counterparts; in co-culture and xenograft experiments, GRP78-CAR-T cells specifically targeted csGRP78-positive tumor cells, including cancer stem cells, and suppressed tumor growth in vivo [71–73].

Thus, it seems that csGRP78 is a versatile CAR target, since it is expressed in leukemic blasts, glioblastoma, lung and pancreatic tumors, and interestingly, targeting csGRP78 might also eradicate cancer stem cell populations in several malignancies; accordingly, recent observations support that normal cells display absent or very low GRP78 at the cell surface, in clear contrast to leukemic cells.

In benign human prostate, GRP78 is detectable but remains relatively low under non-hyperplastic conditions; GRP78 mRNA, protein and immunofluorescence signals are significantly upregulated in hyperplastic (BPH) versus normal prostate tissues, indicating that normal prostate shows only baseline GRP78 levels, and that stress/hyperplasia is required to markedly increase its expression [76].

In the hematopoietic compartment, GRP78 behaves even more clearly as a cancer-associated antigen. Cell-surface GRP78 (sGRP78) is present on cancer cells but not on normal cells [10]. Using multiparametric flow cytometry in pediatric B-ALL at diagnosis, a distinctive sGRP78 high leukemic population (co-expressing CD10, CD19, and CXCR4) is identified in high-risk patients, whereas normal counterparts and standard-risk compartments lack this sGRP78^high phenotype [10].

Taken together, these observations strongly suggest that, under physiological conditions, normal cells show absent or only minimal GRP78 expression at the cell surface, whereas leukemic cells—particularly high-risk populations—display markedly increased surface GRP78 expression. This differential expression pattern is precisely what highlights the rationale for exploiting csGRP78 as a leukemia-associated target antigen.

Clinical translation and biomarker development

Several lines of evidence support the clinical development of GRP78- and UPR-directed strategies in leukemia. At the diagnostic level, cell-surface GRP78 and CXCR4 co-expression on B-ALL blasts identify a subgroup of children with high-risk disease, and the combined analysis of csGRP78 with conventional B-cell markers (CD10, CD34, CD38, CXCR4) could refine risk stratification at diagnostics [10]. In pediatric AML, the proteomic signature defined by Valosin-containing protein (VCP/p97) and UPR components, such as IRE1 and GRP78, has prognostic value, with low VCP and relatively higher IRE1/GRP78 levels associating with improved outcome after bortezomib-containing regimens [52–54].

At the therapeutic level, leukemic cells and leukemia stem cells display a marked dependence on UPR signaling, including the PERK–AMPK and IRE1–XBP1s axes, which regulate mitochondrial priming, oxidative metabolism, and chemoresistance [38, 51, 56, 57]. Inhibiting IRE1 or blocking XBP1 splicing induces caspase-dependent apoptosis and G1 arrest in AML cells, while targeting NAT10 enhances ER stress and apoptosis by downregulating IRE1, CHOP, and PERK, further supporting UPR addiction in this disease [57, 58].

Therapeutic targeting of membrane-associated GRP78 with the BMTP-78 peptide demonstrated antileukemic activity in vitro and enabled formal toxicity assessment in rodents and non-human primates, providing an important proof-of-concept for GRP78-directed therapies and suggesting an acceptable safety window in vivo [36].

More recently, GRP78-CAR-T cells have shown potent antileukemic activity against AML blasts while sparing hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells and have also been effective in solid tumors, such as lung cancer, glioblastoma, and pancreatic cancer, where csGRP78 is expressed by both bulk tumor cells and cancer stem cells [68–73]. Despite these encouraging data, several challenges must be addressed before clinical translation. First, a systematic atlas of csGRP78 expression in normal human tissues, across different physiological and inflammatory states, is lacking. Second, UPR-targeted therapies will likely require disease- and context-specific tailoring, given the dual pro-survival and pro-apoptotic roles of PERK, IRE1, and ATF6 in different leukemic subtypes [10, 29, 30, 33–36]. Finally, careful CAR design, including safety switches and combinatorial targeting strategies, will be crucial to minimize on-target off-tumor effects and to exploit csGRP78 as a therapeutic antigen in a safe and effective manner [65–73].

Building on these principles, several groups have engineered GRP78-directed CARs that differ in their antigen-recognition motifs, spacer domains, and co-stimulatory backbones, but share a common strategy of targeting csGRP78 on malignant cells. Therapeutic antibodies, peptides, and clinical efforts targeting GRP78

Accumulating evidence shows that GRP78 is not just a preclinical target, but is being actively pursued by therapeutic antibodies, engineered cell products, and translational programs that support the feasibility of GRP78-directed strategies in humans.

First, multiple groups have generated csGRP78-directed CAR-T cells that eradicate GRP78-positive tumors in rigorous xenograft models, providing the level of efficacy and specificity typically considered before advancing into early phase clinical testing. In AML, Hebbar and colleagues developed GRP78-CAR-T cells that selectively killed primary AML blasts while sparing hematopoietic progenitor cells, and showed significant anti-leukemic activity in vivo, directly addressing on-target/off-tumor concerns that often limit clinical translation in this disease [69]. Yu et al. independently confirmed that GRP78-CAR-T cells effectively eliminated AML cells and protected mice against systemic leukemia while keeping safe normal hematopoietic stem cells, again supporting csGRP78 as a clinically promising target in hematologic malignancies [68]. Similarly, peptide-based GRP78-CAR-T constructs have been extended to solid tumors: Wang and co-workers showed that csGRP78-CAR-T cells efficiently killed lung cancer cells and cancer stem-like cells and eliminated tumor xenografts in vivo and no relapse was observed or detectable organ toxicity [70]. Yuan et al. demonstrated that csGRP78-directed CAR-T cells homed to and infiltrated pancreatic cancer xenografts and significantly inhibited tumor growth in vivo, including in chemotherapy-resistant models [73]. In glioblastoma, Wang et al. confirmed that csGRP78-targeted CAR-T cells efficiently killed glioblastoma tumor cells and glioma stem cells both in vitro and in vivo, ultimately suppressing xenograft growth without obvious tissue injuries [72]. Collectively, these convergent data across hematologic and solid malignancies support the view, emphasized by Hebbar et al., that targeting cell-surface GRP78-positive AML with CAR-T cells is feasible and “warrants further active exploration” toward clinical development.

Second, antibody and peptide-based therapeutics against GRP78 are advancing in parallel, but this also illustrate that cell surface GRP78 is a broader pharmacological target. Ibañez and co-workers recently showed that GRP78 is highly expressed on the cell surface of multiple solid and brain tumors, and that GRP78-CAR-T cells can recognize and kill GRP78-positive brain and solid tumors in vitro and in vivo, establishing csGRP78 as a promising CAR-T target in diverse solid cancers [71]. A recent study in metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), Alvarez-Elizondo et al. reported that doxorubicin increases the proportion of csGRP78 cells, and these cells are less migratory and invasive than negative csGRP78 cells, suggesting that chemotherapy-induced csGRP78 can serve as a prognostic marker for reduced metastatic potential and a rational docking point for GRP78-targeted agents in breast cancer patients [74]. Complementing these cell-therapy approaches, recent nanoparticle and peptide–drug conjugate platforms exploit the selective exposure of csGRP78 in tumors to deliver cytotoxic payloads or imaging agents, reinforcing GRP78 as a versatile therapeutic entry point rather than merely a stress biomarker [75].

Together, GRP78-CAR-T cell development, csGRP78-reactive antibodies, and csGRP78-based prognostic markers demonstrate a rapidly maturing translational pipeline of therapeutic antibodies and products directed against csGRP78, aligning with and substantiating the reviewer’s request to highlight ongoing GRP78-focused therapeutic and clinical efforts.

In the other hand, despite robust preclinical activity, GRP78-targeted CAR-T cells have not yet entered clinical trials. The existing AML models show that csGRP78-CAR-T cells can eliminate leukemic cells while sparing HSCs and HPCs, indicating a potentially favorable therapeutic index in the bone marrow compartment [68, 69]. Similarly, studies in lung, brain, and pancreatic cancer models report antitumor efficacy without obvious organ toxicity in mice [70, 71, 73].

However, GRP78 is an essential ER chaperone expressed in virtually all normal cells, and its relocalization at the cell surface is dynamically regulated by ER stress. Available data do not fully exclude the possibility that normal cells under inflammatory, metabolic, or ischemic stress may up-regulate csGRP78, raising legitimate concerns about on-target off-tumor toxicity in humans. In addition, toxicology studies of GRP78-targeted peptide such as BMTP-78 in in vivo models should be followed while demonstrating preclinical activity in leukemia and lymphoma; this highlights the need for rigorous dose-finding and safety evaluation when targeting membrane-associated GRP78 [36].

These safety uncertainties, together with general CAR-T-associated toxicities and the current lack of systematic mapping of csGRP78 expression in healthy human tissues, likely contribute to the delay in moving csGRP78-CAR-T cells into clinical testing. Rational CAR design (e.g., affinity-tuned receptors, dual-antigen targeting) and careful preclinical toxicology will be essential to safely translate csGRP78-directed CAR-T therapy.

Concluding remarks and future directions

GRP78 and the UPR pathway emerge as central, yet highly context-dependent, regulators of leukemic cell fitness. Leukemia cells frequently exploit GRP78-dependent UPR signaling to buffer oncogenic stress, while at the same time GRP78 is translocated to the plasma membrane, where it becomes a structurally accessible and functionally relevant target. In childhood high-risk B-ALL, csGRP78 together with CXCR4 identifies a leukemic subpopulation associated with high-risk disease at diagnosis, suggesting that csGRP78 is not only a biomarker of ER stress but also a marker of clinically aggressive clones that can be captured by flow cytometry in routine diagnostics.

From a therapeutic perspective, preclinical data in AML indicate that csGRP78-directed CAR-T cells can eradicate leukemic blasts while sparing hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, underscoring a potentially favorable therapeutic window within the bone marrow compartment. Based on observations in pediatric high-risk B-ALL and on these AML models, several future strategies can be projected: (i) systematically quantifying csGRP78 levels across ALL and AML cohorts to define thresholds that predict response to csGRP78-CAR-T cells; (ii) integrating UPR signatures (XBP1s, CHOP, ATF4) and regulators such as VCP or NAT10 as companion biomarkers of GRP78/UPR dependence in leukemic clones; and (iii) designing CARs with tuned affinity and/or combinatorial recognition to minimize on-target off-tumor effects while preserving potent antileukemic activity.

At the same time, the essential housekeeping role of GRP78 in normal cells and the dynamic nature of csGRP78 expression under physiological stress mandate a cautious translational pathway. A key priority will be to map csGRP78 expression in non-malignant human tissues under inflammatory, metabolic or hypoxic conditions, and to perform rigorous toxicology of csGRP78-targeting agents, as illustrated by the BMTP-78 experience in vivo models. In this context, GRP78-CAR-T cells should be developed in parallel with UPR-modulating drugs that shift leukemic cells toward a pro-apoptotic stress state, potentially lowering the antigen density threshold required for efficient CAR-T-mediated killing. Altogether, the convergence of csGRP78 as a leukemia-associated antigen, UPR as a stress-adaptation axis, and CAR-T technology, establish an interesting and rational framework for next-generation, biology-driven therapies in acute leukemias.
