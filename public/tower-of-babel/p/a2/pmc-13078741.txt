Endoscopic Salvage of Colonic Obstruction Caused by an Unidentified Fibrous Foreign Body

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThere is a large variety of intestinal foreign bodies, including sex toys, produce, and glass, and management depends on the object’s composition and location. INTRODUCTION

Foreign bodies (FB) ingestion is a common presentation in emergency departments across all age groups. In adults, most cases of FB ingestion are intentional and involve intoxication, psychiatric illness, or incarceration[1]. FB ingestion can lead to a wide variety of symptoms, ranging from asymptomatic to colonic obstruction and perforation depending on the location and duration of impaction, The nature and timing of complications depend on the object’s characteristics (size, shape, consistency), its anatomical location, and how long it remains undetected[2]. While the majority silently pass through the gut (80–90%), some become impacted at various points in the alimentary tract. Sharp or elongated items such as fish bones, chicken bones, toothpicks, or glass increase the risk of obstruction, perforation, abscess or fistula formation, especially when they reach the large bowel[3].

We present a rare case of FB impaction in the large bowel with total occlusion of the colon in an otherwise healthy lady.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 30-year-old female with no significant past medical history presented to the emergency department with a 2-day history of a severe, stabbing pain in the left upper quadrant that radiated diffusely across the entire abdomen. Additionally, she complained of anorexia and a notable decrease in stool production with mild haematochezia. Physical exam of the abdomen demonstrated tenderness to palpation and mild abdominal distension. Lab work showed microcytic anaemia without history of menorrhagia or other causative factors. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen and pelvis with contrast showed copious amounts of faeces in the proximal colon along with submucosal thickening and narrowing of a long segment in the descending colon as well as some adjacent ascites. These findings appeared to be caused by an obstruction in the proximal descending colon from a presumed fixed mass (Fig. 1). Gastroenterology was consulted and the patient was started on IV hydration and prepped for colonoscopy with two enemas. Colonoscopy revealed a 10 cm-long tightly compressed FB completely occluding the lumen of the proximal descending colon (Fig. 2). Attempts for removal with nets, and graspers were unsuccessful (Fig. 3). The bezoar was suspected to be undigested food versus unspecified fibrous material. General surgery was consulted for possible hemicolectomy but ultimately one more retrieval attempt was made via flexible sigmoidoscopy. The FB was again visualized beginning in proximal descending colon. An unusual approach was taken by advancing a balloon dilator beyond the impaction and sequentially inflating and sweeping debris towards the colonoscope. Removal of solid debris was accomplished with blindly pushing a 15 mm, 16.5 mm, 18 mm, then finally a 20 mm balloon dilator through the scope and fibrous obstruction (Fig. 4A and B). The FB was visualized in the proximal descending colon, splenic flexure, and the mid transverse colon as well as mucosal ulceration at the splenic flexure. Repeated attempts resulted in significant removal of large pieces of fibrous debris leading to improved luminal clearance (Fig. 4C). The lumen of the colon was then generously lavaged with a moderate amount of mineral oil to allow for lubrication and to aid in subsequent passage of the debris. After colonoscopy the patient was given 2 litres of polyethylene glycol leading to multiple bowel movements. Repeat CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis with contrast showed no findings suggestive of obstruction. Pathology of the retrieved material revealed acellular, fibrous material with inflammatory cells.

DISCUSSION

FB impaction in the colon is an uncommon clinical entity, given the large size of the colon, it allows for passage of materials, and FB usually impacts in the narrower small bowel[4]. Imaging, in particular CT, is usually the modality of choice for the diagnosis and localization of the FB impaction and the associated complications[5]. To date, there is no standardized or high-level evidence regarding the optimal management strategy for impacted FB in the colon[6]. While the use of balloon dilators and lavages are not independently unique techniques in colonoscopies, the use of them together with the intention of clearing an ingested obstruction is distinct. Balloon dilators are commonly used during the treatment of benign strictures like those that develop with Crohn’s disease or stenosis of anastomoses that develop postoperatively[7]. Comparatively, the use of mineral oil lavage is commonly used to help pass the colonoscope more comfortably for the patient, not necessarily to help flush out adherent debris[8]. This case shows that the combination of balloon dilators to sweep clear or dislodge an obstruction with the use of mineral oil lavages with the intent to help the patient further pass residual adherent obstruction material should be further considered. Additional success of this procedure can be attributed to the use of polyethylene glycol post-procedurally to help prevent recurrence.

While FB ingestions are commonly encountered by gastroenterologists there are limited tools available to aid in clearance of these objects, especially when they are exceptionally adherent and difficult to clear. This case highlights a unique endoscopic intervention using balloon dilation and lavage with mineral oil as an alternative to surgery in the management of colonic obstruction due to FB impaction.
