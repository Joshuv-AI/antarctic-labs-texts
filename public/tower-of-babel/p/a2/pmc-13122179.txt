K63-linked ubiquitylation of S2P–RNAPII regulates transcription in a DNAPK inter-dependent manner in response to double-strand breaks
Authors: Vasiliki Pantazi, Paul Smith, Zoltan G Pahi, Manuela Katona, Adam Pap, Zsuzsanna Darula, Roman Fischer, Iolanda Vendrell, Benedikt M Kessler, Marcel A T M van Vugt, Vincenzo D’Angiolella, Tibor Pankotai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) are highly toxic DNA lesions that can lead to genomic instability. DSBs can also interfere with other DNA-based processes, including transcription, and thereby jeopardizing cellular function. In situations of persistent DSBs, RNA polymerase II (RNAPII) needs to be removed to facilitate DNA repair. DSB-induced RNAPII removal involves multifaceted ubiquitylation, but the mechanisms involved remain elusive. Our data show that in response to DSBs, the E3 ubiquitin ligase NEDD4, and to a lesser extent CRL3 complexes, catalyse the ubiquitylation of elongating RNAPII, facilitating efficient DSB repair. Specifically, NEDD4 is identified as the specific writer of K63-linked ubiquitin chains on Serine2 phosphorylated (S2P)–RNAPII under stress, while the total pool of RNAPII is found to be modified mainly with K48-linked ubiquitin chains. We find that the ubiquitin ligases NEDD4, WWP2, and CUL3-based complexes exhibit a DNAPK inter-dependency, driving NHEJ repair and proper resolution of transcription defects caused by DSBs.

Introduction

Our genome is constantly exposed to various genotoxic stresses. DNA damage can occur anywhere in the genome, at loci where replication or transcription take place. At loci where DNA is actively transcribed, DNA damage can result in the formation of aberrant or truncated transcripts and predispose to genomic instability [1]. If DNA lesions persist or are difficult to repair, such as DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), RNA polymerase II (RNAPII) must be removed from the damaged loci to allow DNA repair. DSB-induced removal of RNAPII involves ubiquitylation-mediated processing, and regulation by DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNAPK), poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase-1 (PARP-1), and ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM), mediating either chromatin-related or direct post-translational modification of RNAPII [2–5]. However, the exact mechanism by which RNAPII is dislodged from damaged and actively transcribed chromatin remains to be elucidated, making it unclear how cells avoid clashes between the transcription and the DNA repair machinery and thereby facilitate efficient DNA repair.

Several E3 ligase complexes have been implicated in the ubiquitin-mediated turnover of RNAPII, upon UV irradiation—including the Elongin A–Elongin BC–Cullin5–RBX2 complex (referred to as CRL5Elongin) in human cells or the orthologues complex in yeast Ela1–Elc1–Cullin3–Rbx1 (denoted by Crl3Elongin) [6, 7]. Furthermore, the E3 ligase Rsp5 (the yeast orthologue of the NEDD4 and the E3 ligase complex Crl3Elongin sequentially ubiquitylate RNAPII in yeast [8]. Recently, a cullin–RING ubiquitin ligase complex, comprising CSA, DDB1, CUL4A/B, and RBX1 (CRL4CSA), was demonstrated to be essential for the ubiquitylation-mediated RNAPII degradation upon UV exposure [9–14]. Also, the WW domain-containing E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 2 (WWP2) was shown to be responsible for the K48-linked ubiquitylation and subsequent proteasomal degradation of RPB1, the largest subunit of RNAPII, at DSB lesions on actively transcribed genes [15–17]. Furthermore, WWP2-mediated RNAPII turnover occurred in a DNAPK-dependent manner, linking the ubiquitin–proteasome system and the DSB sensor protein DNAPK in regulation transcription at sites of DSBs [15–17]. Yet another study revealed that the loss of the RNAPII elongation factor SPT5 triggered the recruitment of the Cullin 3 (CUL3) scaffold protein, the VCP/p97 unfoldase, and the kinase CDK9 to facilitate the proteasomal degradation of RNAPII from promoter regions [18, 19]. This latter mechanism resulted in the activation of a non-canonical early termination pathway, where RNAPII is rapidly removed from chromatin before elongation is initiated [18, 19]. Additionally, ARMC5 was found to interact with CUL3 and RPB1, forming a distinct E3 ligase complex with CUL3–RBX1 that can ubiquitylate RPB1 [20, 21]. Although ARMC5 deletion compromised RPB1 degradation, it surprisingly did affect RNAPII stalling or transcription [20, 21]. In addition, it was recently shown that the CUL3–ARMC5 complex was responsible for the ubiquitylation of the initiating form of RNAPII [phosphorylated at the serine 5 residue of the RPB1 C-terminal domain (S5P–RNAPII)] when the integrator protein INTS6 is compromised. Upon their simultaneous loss, RNAPII escapes the promoter–proximal transcription pausing and cell growth stops [22]. Although many stress-induced regulators of RNAPII have been identified, the specific response of RNAPII to DSBs during transcription elongation remains unclear.

In this context, a key feature of the ubiquitin system is the diversity of ubiquitin chain linkages, which dictate the fate of substrate proteins and thereby regulate cellular protein turnover. Distinct ubiquitin linkages confer specific functional outcomes: for example, K48- and K11-linked chains target substrates for proteasomal degradation, whereas K63-linked chains primarily mediate non-proteolytic signalling by serving as scaffolds for protein complex assembly. Nonetheless, recent findings have connected K63 chains to proteolysis as well [23]. Accordingly, a major objective of this study was to characterize the ubiquitin linkage types associated with S2P–RNAPII; in order to elucidate how specific chains influence its regulation and fate.

In this study, we demonstrate that efficient DSB repair involves regulation of RNAPII by various E3 ubiquitin ligases and ubiquitylation mechanisms. We identify the NEDD4 ubiquitin ligase, as well as the CUL3-containing ubiquitin ligase complexes, as interactors of the elongating RNAPII (S2P–RNAPII) at DSB loci. We demonstrate the prevalence of K63-linked ubiquitylation on S2P–RNAPII, as opposed to the K48-linked ubiquitylation events that are predominantly found on the total RNAPII pool. We further illustrate that NEDD4 is the potential DSB-induced S2P–RNAPII ubiquitin writer, facilitating K63-linked ubiquitylation. Importantly, we demonstrate that WWP2, NEDD4, and CUL3 ubiquitin ligases are required for NHEJ repair by mediating the proper activation of DNAPK but their recruitment on the chromatin seems to require DNAPK. Combined, our findings provide a deeper understanding of the ubiquitin linkage types that shape damage-induced transcription resolution by linking NEDD4 to a specific ubiquitin code and connect DSB-induced S2P–RNAPII ubiquitylation, transcriptional silencing and DSB repair.

Materials and methods

Cell line generation

U2OS osteosarcoma cells containing the restriction endonuclease AsiSI were generated using the lentiviral vectors spLCv2-U6del-hPGK-M2rtTA-P2A-CrRed-wPRE and pLV-tetO-HA-ER-AsiSI (kindly provided by Dr Karolina Pircs). Specifically, lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfection with Trans Lentiviral packaging mix (pTLA1-PAK, pTLA1-ENZ, pTLA1-ENV, pTLA1-TOFF, and pTLA1-TAT/REV) to HEK293T cells, according to the manufacturer’s instructions to generate viral particles (Horizon, Cambridge, UK). Culture medium was refreshed 16 h after transfection with reduced serum (3%) medium, and cell culture supernatant was collected after 48 h. U2OS cells were transduced with lentiviral supernatant. After infection, the cells were selected using zeocin (200 μg/ml), and one clone with confirmed efficiency of AsiSI-mediated DNA cutting was selected.

Short hairpin RNAs (shRNA) against CUL3, WWP2, NEDD4, or a control shRNA (sequences shown in the Appendix) were used to generate DOX-inducible lentiVAsiSI shRNA cell lines. based on the tet-on system or U2OS-shCUL3. Six micrograms of each lentiviral plasmid was co-transfected into HEK293T cells at 80% confluency in a six-well plate with 4.3 μl of lentiviral packaging mix as described above using standard calcium phosphate transfection according to the manufacturer’s instructions to generate viral particles (Horizon, Cambridge, UK). Cell culture medium was refreshed 16 h after transfection with reduced serum (3%) medium, and the cell culture supernatant was collected after 48 h. The lentiVAsisI or U2OS cell lines were subsequently transduced with lentiviral supernatant. After infection, U2OS-shCUL3 cells were selected using puromycin (2 μg/ml), whereas U2OS cells with lentiVAsiSI-shRNAs were selected with both zeocin (200 μg/ml) and puromycin (2 μg/ml) for 10 days.

Cell culture and treatments

U2OS and PEP15–I-PpoI cells were cultured at 37°C under a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 in low glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Biosera, Nuaille, France) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Euroclone, Milan, Italy), 4 mM glutamine (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA-Aldrich), and 1× antibiotic (Biosera, Nuaille, France) while PEP15–I-PpoI cells were also grown in the presence of 800 μg/ml G418 (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA-Aldrich). U2OS LentiVAsiSI and its derivative cell lines, as well as HEK293T cells, were cultured in high glucose DMEM (Biosera, Nuaille, France) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Euroclone, Milan, Italy), 4 mM glutamine (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA-Aldrich), and 1× antibiotic (Biosera, Nuaille, France). U2OS LentiVAsiSI was maintained under selective pressure using zeocin 200 μg/ml and the derivative cell lines (shWWP2-lentiVAsiSI, shCUL3-LentiVAsiSI, shNEDD4-lentiVAsiSI, and shCTR-LentiVAsiSI) were maintained under both zeocin, 200 μg/ml, and puromycin, 2 μg/ml.

siRNA transfection

siRNA pools targeting CUL3 and NEDD4 (L-010224-00-0005, L007178-00-0005; Dharmacon, Cambridge, UK), custom siRNAs targeting WWP2 were transfected to the cells using INTERFERin (PolyPlus, Sartorius, Michigan, MI, USA) transfection reagent in antibiotic-free DMEM medium, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A non-targeting siRNA pool (D-001810-10-05; Dharmacon, Cambridge, UK) was used as a negative control. Sequences for siRNAs are listed in the Appendix.

Cell viability assays

To quantify the proliferation and viability of cells exposed to different DNA-damaging agents, cells were grown in a 96-well plate at a density of 5000 cells/well and treated accordingly with the indicated drugs. Experiments were conducted in three biological replicates. Afterwards, the medium was aspirated, and cells were washed 2× with PBS, before fixing with 4% formaldehyde/PBS (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 10 min. After 3× washing with PBS for 5 min, cells were incubated with 0.1% w/v crystal violet in 20% v/v ethanol for 30 min at 25 °C with continuous agitation. Plates were washed with distilled water and were left to dry. Images were taken using Axiocam ERc 5s under a ZEISS Primovert inverted microscope.

For analysis of synthetic lethality, cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 1000 cells/well. Twenty-four hours after seeding, 1 μM palbociclib was used for 24 h to arrest cells in G1 phase. Subsequently, 1 μg/ml DOX was used to induce shRNAs targeting WWP2, NEDD4, and CUL3. Two days after DOX addition, every 2 days for a course of 8 days, 100 ng/ml NCS was added to the cells. Cells were fixed and stained with crystal violet as described above. The experiment was conducted in three biological replicates.

To examine the epistatic effects of DNAPK inhibition with inactivation of either of the ubiquitin ligases, cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 /well and 24 h after seeding, siRNA transfection was applied as described above. Forty-eight hour post-transfection, cells were exposed to 20 µM DNAPK inhibitor or DMSO for 3 h, followed by NCS treatment (50 ng/ml) for 24 h. Crystal violet staining was applied as described above.

Immunocytochemistry

LentiVAsiSI cells were grown in 24-well plates at a density of 3 × 104 cells/well and were treated as indicated. Cells were washed with PBS and then fixed for 20 min with 4% formaldehyde/PBS (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Following 20 min permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100/PBS (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and several washing steps, cells were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in PBS-T [0.1% Tween20 (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA)]. Afterwards, cells were incubated for 24 h at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in 1% BSA/PBS-T. Primary antibodies used: γH2AX (ab2893, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) in 1:500, HA (12ca5, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in 1:200, S2056P-DNAPK (ab18192, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) in 1:200, and γH2AX (05-636-1, clone JBW301, Millipore, MA, USA). After washing steps with PBS-T (0.1%), cells were incubated for 50 min with secondary antibodies with continuous agitation. Secondary antibodies used: GAR Alexa 555 (A21429; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) in 1:1000 and GAM Alexa 488 (A11029; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) in 1:400. After several washing steps with PBS-T (0.1%), cells were mounted with DAPI-containing ProLong Gold antifade reagent (Invitrogen™, CA, USA) and visualized with a Leica Stellaris laser scanning super-resolution confocal microscope. Automated quantification of γH2AX foci/nucleus and S2056P-DNAPK was performed using FoCo, a graphical user interface that uses MATLAB (Mathworks) and ImageJ (NIH). At least 150 cells/sample were counted and considered for quantification.

Nascent RNA synthesis

Nascent RNA synthesis was visualized using the Click-iT RNA Alexa Fluor 488 Imaging Kit (C10329, Invitrogen™, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Images were taken using Olympus Fv-10i-W compact confocal microscope system (Olympus) with Fluoview Fv10i software (V2.1, Olympus). Briefly, cells were seeded in round coverslips in six-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 /well onto round coverslips, and after 24 h, cells were transfected with the indicated siRNA sequences. Forty-eight hour post transfection, cells were treated with 50 ng/ml NCS or 20 μM α-amanitin. One hour before fixation, cells were incubated with 200 μM EU. For the staining, cells attached to round coverslips were transferred into a 24-well plate, and the remaining cells in the six-well plate were used for RNA extraction and verification of siRNA silencing. Automated quantification of EU intensity per nucleus was performed using FoCo, a graphical user interface that uses MATLAB (Mathworks) and ImageJ (NIH). The experiment was conducted in two biological replicates, and 100 cells/image were counted and considered for quantification. All quantifications were performed using the GraphPad Prism 8.4.3 software, and the Tukey’s test one-way ANOVA was applied for statistical analysis for which the P-value was considered significant as follows: *≤ 0.05, ** ≤ 0.01, *** ≤ 0.001, ****≤ 0.0001.

Genomic DNA isolation

Cells were washed twice with PBS and pelleted at 6000 rpm, 4°C. Genomic DNA was isolated using the ReliaPrep™ gDNA Tissue Miniprep System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with some modifications. Cells were resuspended in 40 μl of PBS and treated with Proteinase K and lysis buffer (provided by the kit) at 56°C for 30 min. RNase was added for an additional 10-min incubation time while further steps were followed as suggested by the manufacturer’s protocol.

RNA isolation and cDNA synthesis

A total of 2.5 × 105 cells were seeded in six-well plates, treated accordingly, and RNA was isolated using the Promega ReliaPrep™ RNA Cell Miniprep System (Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol or using TRIreagent (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). For the latter approach, cell pellets were homogenized in 500 μl of TRIreagent and incubated for 5 min at RT. Hundred microlitres of chloroform (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added to the samples, which were vigorously vortexed, followed by 15 min centrifugation at 12 000 rpm at 4°C. The top aqueous phase was transferred, and 125 μl of isopropanol (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added. Samples were centrifuged at 12 000 rpm 4°C and washed with 70% ethanol, followed by elution with RNase-free elution buffer. The concentration and the purity of the RNA samples were analysed using a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). For copy DNA (cDNA) synthesis, 640 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed using the TaqMan Reverse Transcription Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Real-time quantitative PCR reactions using GoTaq® qPCR Master Mix (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, USA) were performed with the QIAGEN Rotor-GeneQ 5-plex HRM qPCR System (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) in a final volume of 10 µl (primers are listed in the Supplementary Table S1). The following thermal profile condition was used for all RT-qPCR amplifications: 95°C for 7 min, 45 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, and 60°C for 30 s.

Western blot

Cells were harvested in lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 2 mM EDTA, and 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0; Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), supplemented with 1× PIC (Roche), 20 μM DUBi (Deubiquitylase Inhibitor; PR-619, Calbiochem, CA, USA), and 1× PhosSTOP (Roche) and sonicated 10× 30 s ON/ 30 s OFF in a Bioruptor Pico sonicator (Diagenode). Protein concentration was determined with the PierceTM BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), and 30 μg of protein lysates were incubated at 95°C for 10 min with NuPAGE™ LDS Sample Buffer with reducing agent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Proteins were separated in either pre-cast NuPAGE™ 4%–12% Bis–Tris Plus, NuPAGE™ 3%–8%, Tris-Acetate gradient gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) or cast gels of specific acrylamide concentrations. Proteins were transferred onto Amersham Hybond ECL-nitrocellulose membrane (GE Healthcare) and blocked for 1 h at 5% non-fat dry milk/1% TBS-Tween20 solution. After washing with 0.1% TBS-Tween20 solution, the membranes were incubated O/N with primary antibodies. Antibodies are listed in the Appendix. Chemiluminescence detection was performed using SuperSignal™ West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Scientific™, MA, USA) and G: BOX Chemi XRQ (Syngene) system. Where applicable, ImageJ was used for quantification of protein signal.

Tandem ubiquitin binding entities pull down assay

U2OS cells were grown in 100 mm plates at a density of 106 cells/well, treated as indicated, and lysed in lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, and 2 mM EDTA) containing 1× PIC (Calbiochem, CA, USA) and 1× PhosSTOP (Roche), and 20 μM DUBi (Deubiquitylase Inhibitor; PR-619, Calbiochem, CA, USA) with gentle sonication 10 × 30 s ON/ 30 s OFF in a Bioruptor Pico sonicator (Diagenode). Protein concentration was determined as previously, and 30 μg of protein lysates (denoted to INPUT material) were heated at 95°C for 10 min with NuPAGETM LDS Sample Buffer containing reducing agent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA).

For the Tandem Ubiquitin Binding Entities (TUBE) pull down, 20 μl of 50% slurry TUBE agarose beads [TUBE2 (UM402; LifeSensors, PA, USA)] per IP was washed twice with TBS-T (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% Tween 20; Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 1xPIC (Roche), 20 μM DUBi (Deubiquitylase Inhibitor; PR-619, Calbiochem, CA, USA), and 1× PhosSTOP (Roche). 1–2 mg of protein was added to beads, and samples were incubated O/N at 4°C with continuous rotation. Beads-polyubiquitin IPs were washed thoroughly with TBS-T (0.1%) and finally eluted in 40 μl of lysis buffer and NuPAGE™ LDS Sample Buffer containing reducing agent by incubation at 95°C for 10 min. Samples were then resolved in SDS–PAGE and WB analysis as mentioned above by immunoblotting with S2P–RNAPII (Abcam, ab5095, Cambridge, UK) antibody in 1:1000, RPB1 CTD (sc-47701, Santa-Cruz, CA, USA), RPB1 N-term (sc-55492, Santa-Cruz, A, USA), S5P-RNAPII (Abcam, ab5131, Cambridge, UK), or total ubiquitin P4D1 in 1:1000 (NB300-130, Novus Biologicals, CO, USA or sc-8017, Santa-Cruz, CA, USA).

GST–UBA pull down assay

For the GST–UBA pull down, purified recombinant glutathione S-transferase (GST) tagged ubiquitin-associated domain (UBA) domain of UBQLN1 (ubiquilin; GST–UBA pull down) was first conjugated to GSH sepharose beads (Cytiva, GE17-0756-01) in lysis buffer for at least 2 h at 4°C, with continuous rotation. For each pulldown, 100 μg of recombinant GST–UBA was conjugated to 20 μl of packed slurry of 3× washed beads. One milligram of sample lysate and extracted as described previously was incubated with GST–UBA conjugated beads O/N at 4 °C with continuous rotation. Following steps were identical to the TUBE pull down protocol.

Co-immunoprecipitation

U2OS cells were grown in 100 mm plates at a density of 106/sample, treated accordingly, and lysed in lysis buffer A (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, 5 mM MgCl2, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% NP-40), supplemented with 1× PIC (Calbiochem, CA, USA) and 1× PhosSTOP (Roche), and 20 μM DUBi (Deubiquitylase Inhibitor; PR-619, Calbiochem, CA, USA) 1 h on ice. The supernatant was collected, and the pellet was further lysed with lysis buffer B (20 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, and NP-40 0.5%), supplemented with 1 μl of Benzonase enzyme (E1014, Millipore, MA, USA) and the above-mentioned inhibitors on ice for 3 h with occasional rotation. Supernatants were later pooled, and salt concentration was restored to 150 mM, and samples were sonicated 3 × 30 s ON/30 s OFF in a Bioruptor Pico sonicator (Diagenode). Centrifugation at 13 000 rpm for 10 min was used to clarify the supernatant, and protein concentration was determined with Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose beads (sc-2003, Santa-Cruz, CA, USA) were washed extensively, and 0.5 mg of protein lysate was pre-cleared with 15 μl of beads 50% slurry for 1 h at 4°C with continuous rotation. The IP was carried out by incubating the protein lysate with 2 μg of S2P–RNAPII antibody (Abcam, ab5095, Cambridge, UK), RPB1 CTD (sc-47701, Santa-Cruz, CA, USA), RPB1 N-term (sc-55492, Santa-Cruz, A, USA), S5P–RNAPII (Abcam, ab5131, Cambridge, UK), WWP2 (12197-1-AP, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), NEDD4 (21698-1-AP, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), and CUL3 (A301-109A, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA) at 4°C overnight, followed by the addition of 40 μl of beads at 4°C for 4 h. Rabbit IgG (Abcam, ab172730, Cambridge, UK) and mouse IgG antibodies (Abcam, ab18443, Cambridge, UK) were used for the detection of non-specific protein binding as a negative control. Beads were then washed extensively with NP-40 lysis buffer and eluted in 40 μl of lysis buffer containing NuPAGE™ LDS Sample Buffer and reducing agent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The beads were removed by centrifugation at 13 000 rpm, 4°C for 5 min, and the supernatants were collected for WB analysis as described previously.

MYC–CUL3 pull down

For MYC–CUL3 pull downs, U2OS-shCUL3 cells were seeded at a density of 1.8 × 106 cells/100 mm dish, and 12 h later, cells were treated with 2 μg/ml DOX. The next day, cells were transfected with 5 μg of pcDNA3.1-MYC-CUL3 or pcDNA3.1-MYC, using 10 μl of X-tremeGene HP transfection reagent (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA-Aldrich), according to the manufacturer’s instructions and incubated for 48 h. Ionizing radiation (IR; 4 Gy) was applied to the cells for 15 min or 2 h as indicated, and cells were lysed in NP-40 buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, and 2 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) containing 1× PIC (Calbiochem, CA, USA) and 1× PhosSTOP (Roche), 20 μM DUBi (Deubiquitylase Inhibitor; PR-619, Calbiochem, CA, USA) for 1 h on ice. Five hundred micrograms of protein lysate was incubated with 40 μl of MYC-magnetic beads O/N at 4°C. Samples were washed three times with NP-40 buffer and eluted in 50 μl of lysis buffer containing NuPAGE™ LDS Sample Buffer and reducing agent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Beads were removed using a magnetic holder and supernatants were retrieved and collected for WB analysis as described previously.

Ubiquitin restriction assay

Ubiquitin restriction assays were performed using the UbiCRest kit (Boston Biochem, Inc, MA, USA), either with individual GST-USP21 (H7110, UBPBio, TX, USA), OTUB1 (H3000, UBPBio, TX, USA), or 6xHIS-AMSH (H4500, UBPBio, TX, USA). Alternatively, ubiquitin restriction assays were performed using enzymes that were kindly provided by Dr Paul Elliot. All enzymes were used in 100 nM concentration.

S2P–RANPII or total RNAPII (RPB1; antibodies recognizing either CTD or the N-terminus of RNAPII) were immunoprecipitated from U2OS cells as described above and after extensive washing steps with NP-40 buffer, beads were washed with reaction buffer [20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 10 mM Dithiotreitol (DTT)] for and additional three times. The beads were then incubated either with 100 nM AMSH, OTUB1, USP2/USP21, or buffer only (reaction buffer), in a 30 μl of reaction, for 3 h at 37°C. For sequential reactions, 3 h later the indicated enzymes were added for an additional 3 h incubation. 4× NuPAGE™ LDS Sample Buffer containing reducing agent was used to terminate the reactions and samples incubated at 95°C for 10 min. Beads were centrifuged at 13 000 rpm and the supernatant was collected and analysed by WB. In addition, 40 μl of TUBE beads were used to pull down polyubiquitylated proteins in 1 mg of protein extract per enzyme reaction, and enzyme reactions were performed as described above.

Sub-cellular fractionation

The sub-cellular fractionation protocol was adopted by Gagnon et al., 2014. Briefly, 5 × 106 cells/dish were seeded and treated accordingly with 50 ng/ml NCS. Cells were pelleted at 6000 rpm, resuspended by gentle pipetting of 200 μl of ice-cold HLB buffer [10 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.3% (vol/vol) NP-40, and 10% (vol/vol) glycerol], and incubated on ice for 10 min. Cytoplasmic fraction was separated by centrifugation at 800 × g at 4°C for 8 min, and NaCl concentration was restored to 150 mM and stored at −80°C. The remaining nuclei pellet was washed 4× with HLB buffer with subsequent centrifugation steps at 200 × g, 4°C for 2 min. To prepare nucleoplasmic and chromatin extracts, nuclei were resuspended in 0.1 ml of ice-cold Wuarin-Schibler buffer [MWS; 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.0, 4 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, 0.3 M NaCl, 1 M urea, and 1% (vol/vol) NP-40], vortexed gently, and incubated on ice for 15 min followed by centrifugation at 1000 × g at 4°C for 5 min. The supernatant was collected and denoted as a nucleoplasmic fraction. The remaining chromatin pellet was washed twice with 500 μl of MWS buffer by vortexing, and incubating on ice for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 500 × at 4°C for 3 min. 0.1 ml of ice-cold NLB [20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.3% (vol/vol) NP-40, and 10% (vol/vol) glycerol] was added to the chromatin pellet, and chromatin was sonicated for 10 min 30 s ON/30 s OFF using a Bioruptor Diagenode sonicator. All fractions were clarified by max centrifugation, and proteins were analysed by WB.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

U2OS LentiVasiSI cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 106 cells/dish and treated as indicated. Cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde (Sigma–Aldrich) for 10 min and then incubated with 125 mM glycine (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 5 min to stop cross-linking. Cells were collected using centrifugation for 5 min at 2000 rpm at 4°C. Cell pellets were resuspended in cell lysis buffer [5 mM PIPES (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), pH 8.0, 85 mM KCl (Sigma–Aldrich), 0.5% NP-40 (IGEPAL, Sigma–Aldrich), 1× PIC (Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, Calbiochem), and 20 μM DUBi (Deubiquitylase Inhibitor; PR-619, Calbiochem, CA, USA)] and incubated on ice for 10 min. Cells were collected with centrifugation (2000 rpm, 5 min, 4°C). Pellets were resuspended in a nuclear lysis buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0 (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 10 mM EDTA, pH 8.0 Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA], 0.8% SDS (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 1× PIC (Calbiochem, CA, USA)] and incubated on ice for 1 h. Chromatin samples were further fragmented by sonication using a Bioruptor sonicator (Diagenode). Chromatin samples were diluted 4× with a dilution buffer [10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0 (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 0.5 mM EGTA, pH 8.0 (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 1% Triton-X-100 (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 140 mM NaCl (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and 1× PIC (Calbiochem, CA, USA)]. Thirty micrograms of chromatin was pre-cleared with 15 μl of Pierce™ Protein A Magnetic Beads (88845; Thermo Scientific™, MA, USA) for 1 h with gentle rotation, and pre-cleared chromatin was incubated O/N at 4°C with the 2 μg of S2PRNAPII antibody (ab5095, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Chromatin–antibody complexes were further incubated with 40 μl of Pierce™ Protein A Magnetic Beads (88845; Thermo Scientific™, MA, USA) at 4°C O/N. After several washing steps, chromatin–antibody complexes were eluted from beads, and DNA fragments were precipitated in liquid nitrogen. After centrifugation, pellets were resuspended in TE (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0 and 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0; Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) buffer and reverse cross-linked at 50°C for 2 h. DNAs were purified with phenol–chloroform extraction and precipitated in liquid nitrogen with absolute ethanol. Results

NEDD4 as well as CRL3 ubiquitin ligase complexes interact with and differentially ubiquitylate RNAPII in response to DSBs

Although RPB1 regulation has been extensively studied across diverse cellular contexts, the regulation of RPB1 ubiquitylation in response to DSBs remains largely unclear [6, 8, 15, 29–33]. We aimed to reveal a possible functional role of CRL3 complexes during transcription regulation and DSB repair, among its other reported roles [18–22]. To this end, we aim to identify CUL3 interaction partners in cells in which DSBs were introduced using irradiation. To capture robust interaction partners, endogenous CUL3 was depleted in U2OS cells using doxycycline (DOX)-inducible shRNAs, and subsequently CUL3 levels were reconstituted by transfection of MYC-tagged CUL3. Then, cells were left untreated or were irradiated with IR before performing MYC–CUL3 pull downs followed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) to identify CUL3 interaction partners (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Of note, the COP9 signalosome inhibitor CSN5i-3 was added to lysates to inhibit NEDD8-mediated CUL3 de-neddylation to maintain CUL3 complexes in their active state. Analysis of enriched interaction partners upon irradiation using Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment for biological pathways GO term including terms such as ‘positive regulation of transcription initiation by RNAPII’, ‘translation’, ‘gene expression’, ‘mitochondrion organization’ as well as ‘proteasomal catabolic process’. The latter GO enrichment validated our screening setup as relevant interaction partners were identified (Supplementary Fig. S1F and H) [25]. Intriguingly, we observed that certain RNAPII subunits (POLR2C, POLR2E, and POLR2M) and RNAPIII (POLR3B and POLR3D) interact stronger with CUL3 in response to IR (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1G).

We focused on the regulation of transcription of coding mRNAs and concentrated our investigation on CUL3-mediated RNAPII regulation. LC–MS/MS data also identified NEDD4—a previously characterized regulator of RPB1 turnover—as a CUL3 interaction partner with decreased interaction in response to IR [8]. However, we were unable to validate this interaction in co-immunoprecipitation experiments, suggesting that the interaction between NEDD4 and CUL3 observed by mass spectrometry may be weak or transient. Based on these notions, we hypothesized that NEDD4 and CUL3 may both be involved in RNAPII regulation, as was reported earlier in yeast [7]. Firstly, we confirmed the interaction of NEDD4 and CUL3 with the total RNAPII pool, as well as elongating RNAPII (S2P–RNAPII), both in response to DSBs induced by IR or the radiomimetic drug neocarzinostatin (NCS; Fig. 1B and C, and Supplementary Fig. S2). The aim was to validate if the effects were specific to IR, or were also observed upon other DSB inducing treatments such as NCS that when applied to the cells induces a ‘free radical attack’ generating SSBs or DSBs [34]. We included the E3 ubiquitin ligase WWP2 as a positive control, since it was previously identified as a ubiquitin ligase of RNAPII ubiquitylation upon DSB induction [9]. Notably, the association of NEDD4 or CUL3 with total RNAPII or S2P–RNAPII strengthened over time upon damage induction (Fig. 1B and C, and Supplementary Fig. S2B–F). In contrast, CUL3 exhibited strong association under physiological conditions with S5P phosphorylated-RNAPII, while WWP2 appeared to interact consistently with all analysed pools of RNAPII, with association levels being only slightly reduced after DSB induction (Fig. 1B and C, and Supplementary Fig. S2B–D), These results indicate that upon DSB induction, NEDD4 and CUL3-based complexes associate with S2P–RNAPII, while the WWP2 ubiquitin ligase constitutively interacts with RNAPII.

In addition, NCS induced robust RNAPII ubiquitylation and proteasomal-degradation upon DSB formation without leading to cellular lethality as compared to other drugs (actinomycin D, phleomycin; Fig. 1D, and Supplementary Figs S3B and C, Fig. S4A, and Fig. S5). We then analysed ubiquitylated RNAPII species using ‘pan-Tandem Ubiquitin Binding Entities’ (pan-TUBEs) pull downs in NCS-treated cells, depleted of either WWP2, NEDD4, or CUL3. Strikingly, WWP2 depletion led to a pronounced increase in S2P–RNAPII ubiquitylation under physiological conditions, a phenotype that was partly reversed upon DNA damage induction (Fig. 1E). Of note, the overall distribution of ubiquitin-conjugated S2P–RNAPII in WWP2-depleted cells at 2 h post damage induction differed from control conditions. Specifically, we observed a ‘ubiquitin smear’ with higher molecular weight, but with less density compared to control-depleted cells (Fig. 1E). In contrast, NEDD4 depletion resulted in near-complete loss of S2P–RNAPII ubiquitylation upon NCS treatment, similar to CUL3 depleted cells, albeit to a lesser extent (Fig. 1E).

As an alternative approach to test the involvement Cullin ligases in the ubiquitylation of S2P–RNAPII, cells were treated with the NEDD8 inhibitor (MLN4924)—known to inactivate Cullin ligases or the COP9 signalosome inhibitor, CSN5i-3, which stabilizes Cullin ligases in an active neddylated state by prohibiting their deneddylation [35, 36]. NEDD8 inhibition partially reduced S2P–RNAPII ubiquitylation, whereas inhibition of the COP9 signalosome led to an increase in RNAPII ubiquitylation (Supplementary Fig. S4A). These findings support the involvement of Cullin ligases in the ubiquitylation of S2P–RNAPII and further reinforce our findings implicating the CUL3 ligase in this process.

In addition, analysis of the initiating form of RNAPII ubiquitylation, S5P–RNAPII, revealed that depletion of WWP2, CUL3, or NEDD4 did not affect its response to DSBs, whereas S5P–RNAPII levels were elevated under basal conditions compared with the scrambled non-targeting control (Fig. 1E). These findings indicate that the NEDD4 and CUL3 are specifically targeting transcribing RNAPII species for ubiquitylation and not promoter bound ones (Fig. 1E). Taken together, these data suggest that the CUL3 and NEDD4 ubiquitin ligases associate with and promote the ubiquitylation of transcribing RNAPII in response to DSBs, while the WWP2 ubiquitin ligase is a constitutive RNAPII interactor that minimally contributes to the ubiquitylation of S2P–RNAPII.

The E3 ubiquitin ligases, WWP2, NEDD4, and CRL3, repress transcription in response to DNA DSBs

Having established a role for NEDD4 and the CUL3 ligase complexes in the ubiquitylation of the transcribing RNAPII upon DSB induction, we next examined their effect on gene expression. To this end, we created a DOX-inducible cell line in which we can induce the expression of the AsiSI restriction endonuclease, To temporally control AsiSI activity, a AsiSI fusion with the ligand-binding domain of the oestrogen receptor (ER) was used, so that both expression and cytoplasmic sequestration can be controlled (further referred to as LentiVAsiSI cells). AsiSI can recognize approximately 1200 target sites in the human genome, with only 200 of these sites being within transcriptionally active genes [37–39]. Addition of 4-hydroxitamoxifen (4-OHT) to promote time-dependent nuclear translocation of AsiSI resulted in elevated levels of γH2AX nuclear foci, already at 30 min post 4-OHT treatment, which persisted over time up until 8 h of treatment (Fig. 2A and B, and Supplementary Figs S6A and S7A–C). Importantly, AsiSI-mediated DNA cleavage resulted in increased S2P–RNAPII ubiquitylation, though reduced in magnitude compared to those following NCS or IR treatment likely reflecting the lower number of DSBs generated relative to IR or NCS (Supplementary Fig. S4B). We next analysed two actively transcribed genes harbouring an AsiSI site; PSMD3 and CREBZF [37, 39]. We subsequently introduced DOX-inducible shRNAs in LentiVAsiSI cells (referred to as LentiVAsiSI-shWWP2, LentiVAsiSI-shCUL3, and LentiVAsiSI-shNEDD4; Supplementary Fig. S6B and C). Following AsiSI induction and WWP2, CUL3, or NEDD4 depletion, we observed that in contrast to control cells, the transcription of PSMD3 and CREBZF genes was no longer repressed upon DSB induction when either of the ligases was depleted (Fig. 2C and D, and Supplementary Fig. S7D). Similar results were obtained upon siRNA-mediated depletion of WWP2, CUL3, or NEDD4 (Supplementary Fig7D).

To further strengthen our data, we utilized the previously characterized U2OS-based pEP15–I-PpoI cell line, which expresses the homing endonuclease I-PpoI under the same inducible system that targets 30 RNAPII-transcribed genes [17]. From these genes, we selected DAB1, RYR as coding genes with I-PpoI target sites and INTS4, which features a degenerated recognition sequence and served as a control for non-responsive break induction [10]. Importantly, we corroborated our findings that CUL3 and NEDD4 are required for transcriptional repression upon DSB induction (Supplementary Fig. S7E). Notably, WWP2 was reported earlier to being involved in transcription repression in this cell line upon DSB induction (Supplementary Fig. S7E) [9].

We next explored whether DSB induction not only affects transcriptional repression at specific damaged genes but also affects transcription response in a genome wide fashion. Simultaneously, we aimed to distinguish between enhanced mRNA stability and increased mRNA production following transcription recovery driving our observations. To this end, we measured nascent transcripts using 5-ethynyl-uridine (EU) labeling following depletion of WWP2, CUL3, or NEDD4, either under physiological conditions or upon NCS treatment (Fig. 2E–G, and Supplementary Fig. S7F). α-amanitin treatment was included as a control for blocked RNAPII and RNAPIII-driven transcription and consequent block in incorporation of new ribonucleotides into nascent transcripts. Quantification of pan-nuclear EU signal intensity revealed that transcription was not suppressed upon depletion of any of the ligases, either in unperturbed or NCS-treated conditions (Fig. 2E–G). Instead, overall EU incorporation levels were elevated, indicating that ongoing nascent RNA synthesis was not downregulated in these conditions. Of note, EU labelling reflects genome-wide transcriptional activity, driven by multiple RNA polymerases, with prominent nucleolar enrichment attributable to RNAPI-driven transcription, as evidenced by the residual nucleolar EU signal following α-amanitin treatment (Fig. 2E). The observed elevated transcription was not explained by increased RPB1 expression, which remained unchanged throughout our treatments (Fig. 2G). Conversely, S2P–RNAPII or total RNAPII protein levels were elevated upon depletion of either ubiquitin ligase upon DSB induction, confirming that post-transcriptional phenomena influence the evasion of transcription silencing (Fig. 2H–I). Based on these data, we propose that DSB-induced transcriptional attenuation is neither a gene-specific phenomenon, nor the consequence of increased mRNA stability or altered RPB1 expression (Fig. 2G). Rather, post-transcriptional control by E3 ubiquitin ligases WWP2 and NEDD4 as well as CRL3 complexes upon DSB induction.

K48-linked ubiquitylation targets total RNAPII enzyme, while K63 linkages are assembled on S2P-RNAPII

In our experimental systems, a range of DSB-inducing agents (AsiSI, IR, NCS, and phleomycin) consistently promoted ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation of elongating RNAPII, as indicated by the accumulation of ubiquitylated S2P–RNAPII following proteasome inhibition with MG132 (Supplementary Figs S4 and S5B–D). Both pan-TUBE pull down assays as well as pull down of GST-tagged ubiquitin-associated domain (UBA) of UBQLN1 (ubiquilin) immobilized on beads (GST–UBA pull down) demonstrated increased ubiquitylated S2P–RNAPII (Supplementary Figs S3–S5). We subsequently examined which ubiquitin linkage types were involved in RNAPII degradation. Thus, we performed ubiquitin restriction digestion reactions (UbiCRest), using recombinant linkage-specific deubiquitylase (DUB) enzymes OTUB1 and AMSH, which specifically cleave K48- and K63-linked polyubiquitin chains respectively [40]. As positive controls, we used Ubiquitin Specific Peptidases USP21 or USP2, which are pan-ubiquitin degrader enzymes. U2OS cells were treated with NCS for 1 or 2 h, with or without proteasome inhibitor MG132, and subsequently immunoprecipitation of endogenous S2P–RNAPII was performed. Reciprocally, we performed pan-TUBE pull down assays followed by UbiCRest reactions to digest ubiquitin molecules that had post-translationally Ub-modified S2P–RNAPII (Fig. 3A). Surprisingly, we found that ubiquitin could only be cleaved with AMSH post-DSB induction, indicating that modification of elongating RNAPII with K63-linked ubiquitin chains is essential to initiate its degradation. (Fig. 3B, C, and E, and Supplementary Fig. S8A–C and H). In contrast, we did not observe ubiquitin cleavage upon OTUB1 treatment, indicating a ubiquitylation mechanism independent of K48 linkage types (Fig. 3B, C, and E, and Supplementary Fig. S8A–C and H). These results align well with recent reports describing a novel function for K63 chains as a ubiquitin modification directing specific substrates to proteasomal degradation, which we show is also applicable to S2P–RNAPII [23]. However, since single enzyme treatment did not result in complete cleavage, we hypothesized that heterogenous ubiquitin chains might be present and so we used OTUB1 and AMSH in a collaborative fashion. Indeed, when we combined treatments with sequential AMSH and OTUB1 treatment of IR-treated cells resulted in a further reduction in the ubiquitin-conjugated S2P–RNAPII (Supplementary Fig. S8E–G and I), These data point at complex ubiquitin chain architecture, possibly involving K48 linkage types extended by K63 linkages, with a potential scaffolding role to K48 linkages. As a consequence, these ubiquitin chains may be poorly accessed by OTUB1 (Supplementary Fig. S8E–G and I). We propose that the elongating RNAPII is mainly targeted by K63-linked ubiquitin chains as a first response to DSBs, subsequently creating complex branched ubiquitin chains that regulate proteasomal degradation of RNAPII. Since the combined use of AMSH and OTUB1 did not lead to complete ubiquitin chain cleavage, other ubiquitin linkage types could also be present, including M1, K6, K11, K27, K29, and K33 linkages. While we hypothesize that S2P–RNAPII may also be modified by these linkages, our findings indicate that K63-linked ubiquitin chains are dominant.

We next investigated the ubiquitin linkage types targeting the total RNAPII pool. After treatment with IR and proteasome inhibitors, total RNAPII immunoprecipitation was performed (anti-RPB1 CTD sc-47701 or anti-RPB1 N-terminus sc-55492), followed by UbiCRest digestion. Contrary to our observations in S2P–RNAPII immunoprecipitations, we observed that K63 chains are secondary to K48 chains on the total RNAPII pool (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. S9A). At Immediate and delayed timepoints following DNA damage induction, RNAPII molecules predominantly exhibited K48-linked ubiquitin chains, with fewer K63 linkages (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. S9A). Treatment with OTUB1 followed by AMSH treatments, but not in reversed order, resulted in near-complete loss of ubiquitin chains on RNAPII, similar to treatment with pan ubiquitin chain degrader USP21) (Fig. 3D). These data underscore the presence of both linkage types on RNAPII, while also demonstrating hierarchy in chain formation and complexity (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. S9A).Collectively, the data suggest that total pool of RNAPII is sequentially modified by K63 and K48 linked ubiquitin chains, which mark RNAPII for degradation. In contrast, our data indicate that K63-linked ubiquitin chains selectively modify elongating RNAPII that encounter DNA breaks, which we hypothesize facilitates the signalling and recruitment of repair factors to efficiently repair DSBs.

K63 ubiquitin-linkage types on S2P–RNAPII are conjugated by NEDD4 in response to DSBs

We next aimed to identify the E3 ligases responsible for generating ubiquitin-conjugated S2P–RNAPII upon DSB induction. We hypothesized that inactivation of a specific ligase would block the utilization and extension of a specific ubiquitin linkage type. Therefore, we depleted the WWP2, CUL3, or NEDD4 E3 ligases using siRNA, while overexpressing HA-tagged ubiquitin constructs, allowing linkage only via K48 (HA-ub-K48only) or only via K63 chains (HA-ub-K63only). In addition a construct that expresses all ubiquitin linkage types (HA-Ub-WT) or a construct that contains mutations in all (HA-ub-K0) were used as controls. Subsequently, S2P–RNAPII immunoprecipitations were performed upon NCS treatment (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. S9B). Depletion of NEDD4 prevented K63 ubiquitin linkages in response to DSB induction, which was observed in NCS-treated control-depleted cells (Fig. 3F). Moreover, even upon proteasome inhibition, we observed that depletion of NEDD4 led to a substantial decrease in the poly-ubiquitylation of S2P–RNAPII in pan-TUBE pull down assays, confirming the essential role of NEDD4 in the proteasome-dependent ubiquitylation of S2P–RNAPII (Supplementary Fig. S9C). In contrast, depletion of CUL3 or WWP2 had no further effect on S2P–RNAPII ubiquitin conjugation upon NCS treatment at analysed time-points (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. S9B). Remarkably, CUL3 silencing in NCS-treated cells resulted in increased level of K48-linkage type related signal, which was not observed in the control. Furthermore, under proteasome inhibition, CUL3 depletion only marginally inhibited S2P–RNAPII ubiquitylation, mainly during steady-state or prolonged DNA damage conditions (Supplementary Fig. S9C), unlike treatment settings without proteasome inhibition (Fig. 1F). In conclusion, we propose that CUL3-based E3 ubiquitin ligase complexes may only play an auxiliary role in shaping the ubiquitylation modification of S2P–RNAPII in response to DSB induction. In contrast, we propose that NEDD4 is the predominant ubiquitin ligase targeting S2P–RNAPII for K63-linked ubiquitin chains upon DSB induction for proteasome-dependent proteolysis.

RNAPII E3 ligases exhibit interdependency with DNAPK in promoting NHEJ

Next, we sought to examine the temporal dynamics of elongating RNAPII interaction partners following DSB induction. To this end, we treated U2OS cells with NCS for 30 min or 2 h, immunoprecipitated S2P–RNAPII and performed LC–MS/MS. We utilized the significantly interacting proteins of each condition and performed Gene Ontology (GO) to analyse enriched biological pathways (Fig. 4A, and Supplementary Figs S10 and S11). Immediately after break induction, we observed an enrichment of ‘proteasome-mediated, ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic process’ while only at later time points after upon DSB induction, we found enrichment of ‘DNA Damege Response (DDR) proteins’ as enhanced interactors of S2P–RNAPII, suggesting that RNAPII ubiquitylation events potentially prime proper DSB repair (Fig. 4A, and Supplementary Figs S10 and S11). In addition, various relevant proteins in the context of transcription regulation, DNA repair, mRNA splicing, apoptosis, and chromatin modifying factors were part of the RNAPII interaction complex throughout damage induction, validating the experimental approach (Supplementary Figs S10 and S11). In summary, our work suggests that immediately following DSB induction, elongating RNAPII is targeted for ubiquitylation, possibly priming RNAPII for interaction with DDR factors.

To investigate the role of these ubiquitin ligases in maintaining cellular fitness, we performed cell viability assays. To this end, cells were synchronized in G1 phase using palbociclib to avoid effects of replication stress, and 24 h later induced shRNA-mediated depletion of ubiquitin ligases cells for 48 h, prior to addition of NCS. We observed that NEDD4 and CUL3 depletion in NCS-treated cells decreased cell survival, with CUL3 depletion showing the strongest effect potentially due its pleiotropic effects (Fig. 4C, and Supplementary Fig. S12C and D). Based on these observations, we next tested whether these proteins regulate DSB repair pathways. To this end, we conducted subcellular fractionation and analysed the chromatin association of key non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) proteins, including the activated form of DNAPK (S2056P–DNAPK), and Ku80 in WWP2, NEDD4, or CUL3-depleted cells, upon DSB induction (Fig. 4B, and Supplementary Fig. S12A and B). Of note, DNAPK inhibition was previously shown to alleviate transcriptional repression and prevent the ubiquitylation of RNAPII in response to DSBs [9, 10]. Indeed, upon DNAPK inhibition, elongating RNAPII remained bound to chromatin, with a noticeable reduction in its ubiquitylation level and a robust rescue of transcription activity of target genes CRNKL1 and CREBZF harbouring AsiSI restriction sites (Supplementary Fig. S13A–C). Accordingly, subcellular fractionation analysis showed efficient chromatin recruitment of S2056P–DNAPK and Ku80 upon DSB induction while, depletion of WWP2, NEDD4, or CUL3 significantly altered the chromatin bound fraction S2056P–DNAPK and Ku80 (Fig. 4B, and Supplementary Fig. S12A and B). Specifically, WWP2 depletion reduced both DNAPK and Ku80 chromatin recruitment, only at later stages of DNA damage responses, whereas NEDD4 depletion caused a delay in the recruitment of these repair proteins. In contrast, depletion of CUL3 led to a marked decrease in S2056P–DNAPK activation and chromatin recruitment following DSB induction, such that chromatin-associated S2056P–DNAPK and Ku80 levels did not exceed those observed under physiological conditions. We next aimed to validate our results using microscopy analysis. Depletion of CUL3 or NEDD4 prevented S2056P–DNAPK foci formation at 30 min or 2 h after DNA damage induction, while WWP2 depletion did not influence S2056P–DNAPK foci formation in line with the fractionation results shown at the same time points of break induction (Fig. 4D and E, and Supplementary Fig. S12E). In contrast, γH2AX foci formation was unaffected by depletion of these E3 ligases. (Fig. 4D and E, and Supplementary Fig. S12E). Of note, in our cell viability assays we did not observe any additional effects of combined inhibition of DNAPK with siRNA-mediated depletion of any of the ligases upon DSB induction, suggesting that these proteins function in a shared pathway upon damage induction and depletion/inhibition of either of them is already detrimental to the cells (Supplementary Fig. S13D). Combined, our data point to a ubiquitin ligase-dependent regulation of NHEJ involving CUL3 complexes and NEDD4 at sites of ongoing transcription, that determines cell viability upon DNA breaks.

WWP2 was previously shown to depend on DNAPK for ubiquitylation of RNAPII [10]. We next investigated if a similar relation applied to either CUL3 or NEDD4 with DNAPK. To do this, we employed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) combined with qPCR (ChIP–qPCR) in pEP15–I-PpoI cells. This approach allowed us to precisely measure dynamics of recruitment of proteins to DSB sites, in the presence or absence of DNAPK inhibition using NU7026 (Fig. 4F and Supplementary Fig. S13E). Our data show that NEDD4, or CUL3 chromatin recruitment following DNAPK inhibition, was reduced at the break site, similar to what has been reported for WWP2, suggesting a strong interdependence between the ligases and DNAPK [17]. It appears that the ligases NEDD4 and CUL3 are recruited to the break site in a manner that depends on an active DNAPK, where they facilitate the proper chromatin recruitment of DNAPK potentially via S2P–RNAPII ubiquitylation (Fig. 4B–F, and Supplementary Figs S12 and S13E). This reciprocal relationship underscores the intricate coordination during resolution of transcription defects caused by DSBs, highlighting the dual essential role of WWP2, NEDD4, and CUL3 in mediating RNAPII ubiquitylation, and chromatin association of early NHEJ proteins in response to DNA damage.

WWP2, NEDD4, or CUL3 depletion releases S2P–RNAPII to facilitate transcription elongation

Based on our subcellular fractionation analysis, depletion of WWP2, CUL3, or NEDD4 significantly affected the chromatin occupancy of elongating RNAPII (Fig. 4B). Specifically, the depletion of WWP2 or NEDD4 resulted in robust association of RNAPII at chromatin, indicating that WWP2 and NEDD4 play crucial roles in regulating the chromatin dissociation dynamics of S2P–RNAPII and consequent transcriptional activity (Fig. 4B). Of note, depletion of WWP2 resulted in an overall increase in chromatin occupancy of S2P–RNAPII, showing that WWP2 increases the overall basal transcription levels, without altering DSB-induced S2P–RNAPII dynamics (Fig. 4B). Specifically, NEDD4 appeared to be the main ligase that regulates chromatin-bound transcribing RNAPII upon DSB-induction (Fig. 4B). Remarkably, basal protein levels of S2P–RNAPII were reduced upon CUL3 depletion but were restored after DSB conditions (Fig. 4B). These findings underscore the distinct yet interconnected roles of WWP2, CUL3, and NEDD4 in regulating RNAPII chromatin binding dynamics and the transcription cycle upon DNA break induction.

To further investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms of transcription regulation in response to DNA breaks, we conducted ChIP–qPCR experiments in the LentiVAsiSI cell line (Fig. 5). We depleted WWP2, NEDD4, or CUL3 and subsequently monitored the chromatin association of elongating RNAPII and included γH2AX as a positive control for DNA break induction at the CREBZF and PSMD3 genes. As a negative control, we examined the chromatin binding of S2P–RNAPII and γH2AX at an intergenic region (Supplementary Fig. S14C).

Immediately after break induction, γH2AX was detected along the entire gene, indicating effective break induction and DDR activation (Fig. 5A and B, and Supplementary Fig. S14A and B). Notably, we observed a reduction in γH2AX signal at locations approximately 15–20 kbp away from the break site, specifically within the CREBZF promoter and the PSMD3 3′UTR (Supplementary Fig. S14A and B). Importantly, in our experimental setup, we could recapitulate the immediate and robust transcription repression following DSB induction, as evidenced by reduced chromatin-bound S2P–RNAPII at damaged genomic loci (Fig. 5A and B, and Supplementary Fig. S14A and B). Remarkably, relative quantification of our data showed that transcription was not repressed when either WWP2, NEDD4, or CUL3 was depleted (Fig. 5A and B). WWP2 and CUL3 depletion resulted in increased S2P–RNAPII chromatin occupancy at all observed time points following DSB induction, whereas NEDD4 triggered a strong S2P–RNAPII association with chromatin, with a progressive increase over time, consistent with our chromatin fractionation data (Fig. 5A and B). We also provide the non-normalized analysis of our data, to show that depletion of each ligase resulted in an overall reduction in S2P–RNAPII occupancy at basal levels, which was rescued in response to damage (Supplementary Fig. S14A and B). Thus, we observed that chromatin retention of elongating RNAPII molecules is regulated by WWP2, CUL3, and NEDD4 and their depletion leads to a sustained transcription despite the presence of damage.

Overall, our findings indicate that NEDD4 primes K63-linked ubiquitin chain formation on elongating RNAPII molecules, and the absence of NEDD4 leads to continued transcription during a persistent DNA break response, while simultaneously affecting the activation of DNAPK. We also find that CUL3 is involved in transcription silencing and NHEJ repair in response to DSBs yet only playing an auxiliary role in the ubiquitylation of S2P–RNAPII when compared to NEDD4. Concerning WWP2, we find that its depletion results in robust chromatin association of S2P–RNAPII molecules, both during basal and DSB-induced conditions, accompanied by increased transcription rates, recapitulating previous reports [15]. Yet, we noticed that WWP2 is dispensable for S2P–RNAPII ubiquitylation. Combined, our data reveal the crucial and interconnected roles of WWP2, CUL3, and NEDD4 in modulating RNAPII dynamics and transcriptional regulation upon DNA break induction, essential for maintaining cellular viability (Abstract Figure).

Discussion

RPB1, the largest subunit of RNAPII, is degraded by the ubiquitin-proteasome system upon UV-induced DNA damage. The ‘last resort’ model implies that persistently stalled RNAPII is degraded at DNA lesions to allow NER factors to repair at actively transcribing DNA strands [41]. Mutation of lysine K1268 in RPB1 in mice highlighted the critical role of RNAPII ubiquitylation and gene expression, indicated by shorter lifespan, premature ageing, and neurodegeneration [9]. This notion also underscores the need for robust RNAPII clearance in response to DNA lesions, including UV radiation to allow DNA repair through NER pathway [10–14]. Specifically, CRL4CSA complexes were shown as likely candidates for human RPB1 ubiquitylation, facilitated by ELOF1-mediated positioning of UVSSA and CRL4CSA on stalled RNAPII [10]. In contrast to the extensive knowledge on UV-induced RNAPII ubiquitylation, the response to DNA DSBs is less clear. Our research was focused on the ubiquitylation of S2P–RNAPII in response to DNA DSBs, identifying specific E3 ubiquitin writers, ubiquitin chain features and the impact on transcription and DNA repair.

We describe that the ubiquitylation of transcribing RNAPII upon encountering a DSB is proteasome-dependent, and mediated predominantly by the NEDD4 E3 ubiquitin ligase, and to lesser extent by CUL3 ubiquitin ligase complexes, with distinct temporal kinetics. Although CUL3 depletion had the most pronounced impact on cellular fitness, this likely reflects the disruption of multiple CUL3-based complexes. Notably, however, its effect on S2P–RNAPII ubiquitylation was less prominent than that observed upon NEDD4 depletion. In contrast, the WWP2 ubiquitin ligase appears responsible for dissociation of RNAPII from chromatin and consequent transcriptional repression. We furthermore show that NEDD4 is an indispensable component of S2P–RNAPII ubiquitylation upon DNA break induction, ensuring accurate DNA repair and cellular viability. In our experimental setup, CUL3 ubiquitin complexes exhibited an auxiliary yet important role in the clearance of S2P–RNAPII molecules by mediating proper transcription repression under stress. Although CUL3 strongly and persistently interacted with S2P–RNAPII upon break induction, CUL3 depletion had a less pronounced impact on the ubiquitylation of S2P–RNAPII when compared to NEDD4 depletion. Our data do not exclude the possibility that CUL3 depletion leads to compensatory upregulation of another ubiquitin ligase which could drive S2P–RNAPII ubiquitylation. In such case, CUL3 then serves a regulatory role in fine-tuning RNAPII ubiquitylation, which is in line with the observation that inhibition of the proteasome and CUL3 depletion rescued S2P–RNAPII ubiquitylated species suggesting that RNAPII could be properly ubiquitylated (Supplementary Fig. S9B).

Recent reports described significant roles of CUL3 complexes in different biological contexts [18–21]. Specifically, ARMC5 was identified as the substrate recognition subunit of the CUL3 complex that ubiquitylates RPB1 in unperturbed conditions and its inactivation caused neural tube defects both in mice and humans [20, 21]. In addition, the loss of the integrator protein INTS6 was shown to result in S5P–RNAPII ubiquitylation by the ARMC5–CUL3 [22]. Although the primary aim of the current study was not to characterize substrate adaptors of CUL3, ARMC5 was not identified among the enriched proteins after IR in our MYC–CUL3 pull down LC–MS/MS data. We hypothesize that in response to DSBs, different CUL3-based complexes assemble to target the elongating RNAPII, and examining the effects of the specific CUL3-adaptor will possibly enhance the phenotype that CRL3 complexes exhibit in our findings. Accordingly, our findings suggest that CRL3 complexes control physiological recycling of elongating RNAPII, but affect this process indirectly, and may involve currently elusive substrate adaptors. Importantly, CUL3 functions as a scaffold within multiple Cullin–RING ubiquitin ligase (CRL3) complexes, each regulating distinct substrates. Consequently, depletion of CUL3 may lead to pleiotropic effects that may confound the interpretation of our results. Notably, although our S2P–RNAPII proteomic analysis revealed early enrichment of proteasome-associated factors following DSB induction, the specific E3 ligases functionally characterized in this study (WWP2, NEDD4, and CUL3) were not detected in this dataset. Instead, other cullin scaffold proteins, including CUL1 and CUL4, were identified, with the latter one previously characterised to ubiquitylate RNAPII during TC-NER. [9–14]. Why hypothesize that the discrepancy could have risen from the differences in the experimental procedures and this likely reflects the dynamic and context-dependent nature of RNAPII-associated ubiquitin ligase complexes. Nevertheless, our biochemical analyses define the specific contribution of WWP2, NEDD4, and CUL3 to this response.

In addition to identifying ubiquitin writers on RNAPII, our study uncovered features of ubiquitin chain confirmation. K63 ubiquitin linkage types have been demonstrated to have regulatory roles in DNA repair and autophagy among other pathways [42–46]. Our data reveal a novel function for K63 ubiquitin chains in regulation of elongating RNAPII in response to DSBs, with NEDD4 as a responsible E3 ligase. We further show that this process is proteasome-dependent and is required to repress transcription during a prolonged DDR response, associated with deregulated NHEJ repair. In the context of the total RNAPII pool, we found that K48 linkages are dominant over K63 linkages. Furthermore, OTUB1-mediated cleavage of K48-linked ubiquitin chains allowed AMSH-mediated K63 cleavage, which was distinct from the regulation of S2P–RNAPII. These findings align well with a previous study, demonstrating that K48 is linked to the ubiquitylation of the total RNAPII pool [15]. Despite the observed differences in the ubiquitin writer complexes acting on RNAPII, our data are in line with previous reports wherein the yeast Rsp5 primes for K63 linkage types, which are further extended by K48 linked ubiquitin by the Elc1/Cul3 complex [8]. Taken together these data point toward a model involving K48 linkage types priming for K63-linked ubiquitin chains, extended by NEDD4, regulated by the DDR and leading to subsequent proteasomal degradation. The complexity of ubiquitylation modification with mono- ubiquitylation or hetero-or homotypic poly-ubiquitylation of branched or linear chains, involving various target lysine residues, precluded us from delineating the exact architecture of ubiquitin chains on RNAPII. Further exploration is therefore needed to unravel whether the K63-linkage types are extended onto K48 linkages, creating a heterotypic ubiquitin chain or whether RNAPII ubiquitin chains are homotypic.

Our experiments further underscore the intricate interplay between DNAPK and the ubiquitin ligases WWP2, NEDD4, and CRL3 complexes. We showed that DNAPK activation and RNAPII chromatin association in response to DSBs depends on the multiple ubiquitin ligases examined in this study, although to various extent—CUL3 and NEDD4 have stronger effects than WWP2—and with differential temporal dynamics. Moreover, the recruitment of ubiquitin ligases to DSB sites is DNAPK-dependent. These data, together with previous reports, demonstrate that these ubiquitin ligases together orchestrate RNAPII chromatin association and are necessary for efficient NHEJ DNA repair [17]. Furthermore, both DNAPK and the studied ubiquitin ligases are required to repress transcription and prevent that elongating RNAPII remains associated and active on damaged chromatin. We propose that S2P–RNAPII ubiquitylation upon DSB induction, through the coordinated action of CRL3 and NEDD4, regulates its proteasomal degradation safeguards genomic integrity by allowing repair via NHEJ. We realize that other factors may be involved, as these ubiquitin ligases may also regulate other transcription or repair factors and could regulate each other. For instance, we observed that NEDD4 exhibited significantly reduced interaction with CUL3 complexes following IR exposure in LC–MS/MS experiments, although these observations could not be validated in immunoprecipitation assays. We interpret these data to indicate that upon DSB induction, NEDD4 is redistributed away from CUL3-enriched assemblies toward the chromatin-associated RNAPII complex. This suggests a potential mechanism in which E3 ligases act redundantly to ensure S2P–RNAPII ubiquitylation to maintain genomic stability.
