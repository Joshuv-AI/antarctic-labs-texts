Posterior acetabular wall fracture while playing basketball in a young Hispanic male: A case series☆
Authors: Rafael Baella Anzalota, José J Aponte Reyes, Alexandra Claudio-Marcano, Marcantonio V Pinci, José J Martinez Ramos, Patricia S Serrano Boett, José I Acosta Julbe, Norman Ramirez Lluch, Luis Lojo-Sojo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCaseWe report two cases of young Hispanic males with posterior acetabular fractures sustained during low-energy basketball injuries, a mechanism rarely described in the literature. Both patients underwent open reduction and internal fixation within 24 h of admission. Detailed evaluations, surgical procedures, and follow-up outcomes are provided.ConclusionThese cases highlight an unusual presentation of acetabular fractures in young males from low-energy sports injuries. The findings emphasize the need for further research into the mechanisms, management, and natural history of low-energy acetabular fractures in young patients.Keywords: Posterior acetabular fracture, Basketball injury, Hispanic acetabular pattern, Low-energy trauma, Open reduction internal fixation of acetabular fracture

Introduction

Fractures of the acetabular wall often present in a bimodal distribution, predominantly affecting older adults during low-energy incidents such as a fall from standing height, and in younger individuals, associated more frequently with high-energy trauma, commonly from road traffic accidents [1], [2]. These fractures can also result from other high-energy incidents like sports-related injuries or falls from significant heights [2]. The surgical management of such injuries remains challenging due to the requisite high level of surgical expertise [2]. Even with prompt operative intervention, complications such as femur head avascular necrosis and hip joint degenerative arthritis are still prevalent [1]. Recognizing the specific fracture pattern is crucial for treatment approaches and prognostic considerations. While high-energy trauma remains the predominant mechanism of acetabular fractures in young individuals, our cases underscore the possibility of these injuries occurring during low-energy mechanisms in sports. The limited documentation of such cases highlights a significant gap in the literature. We describe two unusual cases of healthy young Hispanic males who sustained a posterior acetabular fracture following an injury while playing basketball. We obtained written consent from the patient to use the de-identified medical information of both patients.

Case 1

A 25-year-old Hispanic male patient with an unremarkable past medical history presented to the emergency room after sustaining a left posterior acetabular wall fracture with associated dislocation while playing basketball. He states that the incident occurred while he attempted to retrieve the ball from the offside of the court, stepped on grass, slipped, and fell into a split position. He experienced severe left hip pain and was unable to stand up or bear weight immediately after the accident.

Upon initial physical examination, the left leg exhibited external rotation, abduction, and noticeable shortening. The patient was found neurovascularly intact without any signs of sciatic nerve compromise. Radiographic imaging revealed a left posterior wall acetabular fracture accompanied by hip dislocation, with an Orthopaedic Trauma Association (OTA) classification of type 62-A1.1 (Fig. 1A–B). Closed reduction under conscious sedation was performed using the Allis Whistler maneuver, resulting in anatomical femoroacetabular congruence in post-reduction radiographs. The Allis Whistler maneuver is a technique used to reduce posterior hip dislocations. The patient is supine, and with the pelvis stabilized by an assistant, gentle in-line traction is applied to the flexed hip at approximately 90 degrees. Simultaneously, gradual upward traction, combined with gentle internal rotation and adduction, is performed to guide the femoral head back into the acetabulum while minimizing the risk of iatrogenic injury.

A. Left hip antero-posterior view showing posterior acetabular wall fracture with concomitant hip dislocation before closed reduction. B. Left hip antero-posterior view showing posterior acetabular wall fracture with concomitant hip dislocation after closed reduction.

A computed tomography (CT) scan was ordered, which confirmed the fracture and yielded further information about its extent (Fig. 2). A comprehensive metabolic assessment was performed to rule out underlying bone disease, but the results were unremarkable. Within 24 h of admission to the hospital, the patient underwent open reduction with internal fixation (ORIF) and labrum repair.

CT scan of pelvis without contrast showing posterior wall acetabular fracture.

The patient was taken to the operating room and placed in the lateral decubitus position to ensure optimal exposure to relevant anatomical structures. The Kocher-Langenbeck approach was employed, with careful dissection to expose the fracture and joint. Subsequently, femur head anatomical reduction and debridement of the wall fragment were performed. Fracture stability was achieved using two Eliel Ortho Medical © Acumed Pelvis 2-hole hook plates with the insertion of four screws (Acumed, Oregon, United States). Labrum repair was performed using Orthopaedic and Surgical Solutions Inc. (OSSI) Parkus 3.5 mm suture anchors in a two-thread repair (OSSI, Puerto Rico, United States). Stress testing confirmed the stability of the fixation of the wall fragment and joint. Postoperative imaging of the left hip revealed adequate hardware placement and satisfactory anatomical congruence and alignment (Fig. 3). No complications were encountered during the procedure.

Postoperative left hip antero-posterior radiograph.

The patient had an unremarkable postoperative course. He was discharged home with pain management, deep venous thrombosis (DVT) prophylaxis, and instructions to return to clinics in 1 week. Additionally, he was instructed to maintain non-weight bearing on the left lower extremity (LLE) for two months and encouraged to practice active range of motion (AROM) and passive range of motion (PROM) of the left hip. At the four-month follow-up visit, he walked independently and resumed daily activities. At the 1-year postoperative mark, the patient exhibits favorable clinical outcomes, reporting a full range of motion without pain or discomfort.

Case 2

Case of a 22-year-old Hispanic male with an unremarkable medical history who sustained a right posterior acetabular wall comminuted fracture with an intra-articular fragment. While playing basketball, he ran down the court, locked his right knee, and felt his hip give out with a sudden feeling of sharp pain over the right hip area and inguinal area. After the incident, he could not ambulate nor bear weight on the right lower extremity (RLE).

Upon initial assessment, physical examination revealed soft compartments, no gross deformity, and a neurovascularly intact RLE. Radiographic imaging revealed that the patient sustained a posterior wall acetabular fracture (Fig. 4A–B). To further characterize the fracture and to determine surgical planning, a Pelvic CT without contrast was performed, demonstrating a posterior wall acetabular fracture with intra-articular fragment with an OTA classification of type 62-A1.1 (Fig. 5). A comprehensive metabolic assessment was performed to rule out underlying bone disease, but the results were unremarkable.

A. Right hip antero-posterior radiograph. B. Right hip judet view.

CT pelvic scan of posterior wall acetabular fracture.

Within 24 h of admission to the hospital, the patient was taken to the operating room for ORIF of the right acetabular fracture. A Kocher-Langenbeck approach was used, and the fracture and joint were accessed with careful dissection. With direct visualization of the fracture, the joint was distracted and irrigated, and intra-articular loose cartilage was removed from the joint. The posterior acetabulum was stabilized with an Eliel Ortho Medical © Acumed Pelvis system, a two-hole hook plate, a three-hole hook plate, and an eight-hole buttress recon plate (Acumed, Oregon, United States). Postoperative imaging of the pelvis and hip revealed adequate hardware placement and anatomical reduction and fixation (Fig. 6). No complications were encountered during the procedure.

Postoperative right hip antero-posterior radiograph.

The patient was discharged home with pain management, DVT prophylaxis, and instructions not to bear weight on the RLE for two months. He was instructed to return to clinics in one week and encouraged to maintain AROM and PROM of the right hip. During the first postoperative visit, there was minimal pain with active right hip flexion. At the one-year follow-up visit, the patient could ambulate without assistance and jump. Hip flexion, extension, and abduction were comparable to the contralateral side, and he reported no pain or discomfort.

Discussion

Fractures in the acetabulum among younger individuals are rare and typically arise from high-energy trauma. A decreasing trend in the incidence of such injuries has been observed since the reinforcement of seat belts, with the latest report indicating a rate of 3 in 100,000 [2]. Acetabular fractures resulting from low-energy mechanisms, such as falls from standing height, are highly unusual in healthy young adults and are typically seen in elderly osteoporotic individuals [3]. We describe two cases that occurred while playing basketball when they sustained a posterior acetabular wall fracture, the most common type reported [4].

Although increased sporting activities are a risk factor for fractures, there is limited data concerning acetabular fractures in young patients caused by low-energy incidents in the absence of metabolic bone disease. Sperring et al. described a comparable case of a 26-year-old male who suffered a transverse-posterior acetabular wall injury while playing basketball [3]. While the literature reports only one similar instance of an acetabular fracture resulting from a low-energy incident in a young person, there are a few reports of high-energy incidents related to high-contact sports [5], [6]. Good et al. described the case of a 26-year-old Caucasian male who sustained a posterior wall-posterior column right acetabular fracture while playing rugby [6]. The patient had his right leg planted when he was tackled, causing him to land on his flexed right knee. Giza et al. documented the case of a 28-year-old male who suffered a left hip acetabular fracture-dislocation in the context of a football injury [5]. Additionally, Nowell et al. recently reported a case of a young adult who sustained a posterior wall acetabular fracture following a low-energy ground-level fall without evidence of underlying metabolic bone disease [7].

Although there is scant literature on acetabular fractures among Hispanics, the acetabular morphology study by Torres-Lugo et al. provides insights into anatomical factors that may explain the fractures in our cases [8]. Hispanic males have a significantly reduced posterior acetabular sector angle (PASA), indicating less posterior wall coverage of the femoral head. They are more prone to acetabular retroversion, which shifts the weight-bearing zone anteriorly and increases posterior wall stress. These features predispose the posterior acetabulum to failure under shear and torsional forces. In basketball, dynamic movements such as pivoting, jumping, and abrupt directional changes generate posteriorly directed forces, as shown in the mechanisms of these cases. For individuals with reduced PASA and retroversion, these forces can overwhelm the posterior wall, even during low-energy mechanisms. These mechanisms align with the reduced posterior support and altered biomechanics described by Torres-Lugo et al., which could have predisposed these patients to their fracture patterns [7].

Awareness of these anatomical variations is critical for clinical management. These documented instances underscore the significance of identifying and thoroughly examining acetabular fractures in young Hispanic individuals, irrespective of the energy level involved in the inciting incident. Increasing knowledge and documentation ensures appropriate management and averting potential long-term complications. While fractures in older adults are often linked to osteoporosis, these cases involved healthy, young Hispanic males with no underlying bone disease. This highlights the need for multi-centered studies to explore potential biomechanical or cultural factors that may predispose specific populations to such injuries. Continued research focusing on young individuals and developing targeted guidelines for their management is essential to assess the long-term postoperative prognosis, improve outcomes, and enhance the understanding of acetabular fractures in this demographic. Also, future research is needed to determine the prevalence or implications of acetabular differences in the Hispanic population.
