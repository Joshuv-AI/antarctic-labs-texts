A high-gain symmetric-bipolar buck-boost AC-AC converter with step-changed output frequency and rectifier operation
Authors: Mostafa Karimi Hajiabadi, Ali Mosallanejad, Ahmad Salemnia, Alireza Lahooti Eshkevari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper introduces a new single-phase non-isolated symmetric-bipolar buck-boost alternating current-alternating current (AC-AC) converter, along with its associated commutation method. The topology comprises five switches, which can be implemented using industrialized single- or dual-diode and Insulated Gate Bipolar Transistor (IGBT)/Metal Oxide Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor (MOSFET) modules. The proposed buck-boost AC-AC converter inherits all the beneficial features of existing designs, including the ability to generate both inverted and non-inverted output voltages, provide continuous input current, and offer the capability to change the output voltage frequency in a stepwise manner, as well as rectify the output voltage. The proposed AC-AC converter can achieve a high output gain because a two-winding coupled inductor has been integrated into its converter structure. This way, the buck-boost coverage range of the topology can be adjusted by the appropriate selection of the winding ratio of the coupled-inductor. Compared to its counterparts, the proposed converter performs better in terms of voltage gain, switching device power (SDP), volumetric indices of inductors and capacitors, and efficiency. These features make the proposed converter suitable for traction systems and dynamic voltage restorer (DVR) applications. This article details the operating principle of the proposed topology and presents experimental results validating its performance as an AC voltage regulator and a DVR circuit.Keywords: AC-AC converter, Buck-boost, Bipolar converter, Non-isolatedSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

AC-AC converters are classified into direct and matrix types, with options for isolated and non-isolated configurations1. Direct AC-AC converters are characterized by their low cost, compactness, and ease of control. However, they face major drawbacks, like commutation challenges2, limited voltage conversion ranges3, asymmetric bipolar operation4, and a limited number of output functions5. In contrast, matrix converters (MCs) offer unique features, including symmetric bipolar voltage conversion, voltage rectification, and step-changed output frequency. These features make MCs suitable for various applications, such as DVRs, motor drive systems, and solid-state transformers6. The isolated AC-AC converters use high-frequency transformers (HFTs) to meet grid connection and isolation requirements. The generation of asymmetric output voltage7,8, large component counts9, and high magnetic requirements10remain significant challenges for isolated AC-AC converters.

Conversely, HFTs are not included in non-isolated AC-AC topologies11–27. HFT reduces magnetic requirements and the size and cost of the topology. Zuckerberger, et al. 11 presented the first non-isolated buck-boost AC-AC converter, which suffers from high voltage and current stresses on its switching devices as well as poor input and output power quality. It can produce unipolar output voltage and necessitates a costly tapped-winding transformer. The reference12proposes a non-isolated Z-source AC-AC converter that provides a bipolar buck-boost output voltage. However, it has poor input current quality and requires an inductor-capacitor (LC) filter, which increases the converter’s component count, volume, and cost, and necessitates many switches, reducing efficiency and complicating control and commutation. The non-isolated bipolar buck-boost converter in 13 has the same weak points as 12.

Proposal14suggests a single-phase buck-boost MC with modified switching cells. It addresses the commutation problems by replacing bidirectional switches with unidirectional switches and diodes. Nevertheless, this topology still requires a large number of power switches. The15modifies the conventional Z-impedance network and integrates it into a standard four-switch H-bridge direct current-alternating current (DC-AC) converter, thereby eliminating the need for bidirectional switches and enhancing the converter’s output capabilities. However, it requires numerous power switches and diodes, has a quasi-continuous input current, and necessitates an accurate zero-cross detector circuit for the input voltage. Another non-isolated buck-boost AC-AC converter that features safe commutation and continuous input current has been introduced16; however, it still comprises many active and passive elements, and the control complexities of the converters presented in 14 and 15 remain.

In 17, a new non-isolated converter with symmetric-bipolar step-down and step-up operations is introduced, featuring continuous input current and reducing the number of power switches to 8. However, in each half-cycle of the input voltage, four power switches operate at high frequency, reducing the converter’s efficiency. The converters in 18 and 19 address the commutation problems of prior non-isolated designs by using the nondifferential AC chopper concept and the dual-buck structure, respectively. Meanwhile, they provide different output operating modes. However, both converters face the same challenges as the converter in 17, including the need for numerous components, a large volumetric index, and high converter cost.

A switched-capacitor-based AC-AC converter has been proposed20, which supports in-phase and anti-phase output modes. It comprises eight power switches, four of which are high-frequency (HF) operated switches, which account for 55% of the total power losses. Additionally, this converter only offers symmetric-bipolar step-down conversion, which limits its ability to compensate for voltage sags. The21introduces a family of non-isolated buck-boost AC-AC converters with ten semiconductor devices. Besides the high number of switches and diodes, the topology has an input current discontinuity. A novel design in 22 integrates a soft-switching network into the topology of 20, thereby reducing switching power losses in HF switches. However, the topology’s power-sag mitigation capability remains limited. Furthermore, incorporating soft switching enhances the converter’s operational sensitivity to the accuracy of the soft-switching impedance network.

The23suggests a novel five-switch bipolar buck-boost converter. The proposed topology benefits from inherent commutation by eliminating the need for bidirectional power switches. Moreover, it achieves continuous input current, symmetric bipolar operation, simultaneous inverting and non-inverting output voltage, and step-changed output frequency operation. However, the voltage gain of the proposed converter remains low. The24presents a novel bipolar-output AC-AC converter with continuous input and output currents based on a new switching cell, but it still requires a large number of active switching devices. The output voltage gain of the converter in 24 has been improved by using two coupled inductors25, but the design in 25 still has the same problems as 24. The converter in 26 introduces a high-gain buck-boost AC-AC converter that offers both inverted and non-inverted output voltages. However, this topology achieves high gain using two coupled inductors, lacks continuous current at both input and output ports, and suffers from low component utilization, as it is a dual-stage design with only one active stage in inverting or non-inverting modes. The direct AC-AC converter in 27 improves on the converter introduced in 23 by eliminating one diode from its circuit structure, while maintaining its superior features. Nevertheless, the resulting design offers low output gain. The converters in 28,29 offer buck-boost operation with fewer active and passive components. But they produce unipolar output voltage with low gain and cannot provide features such as step-change output frequency and voltage rectification.

The direct buck-boost converter in 30 provides bipolar operation with continuous input and output current. However, regardless of low buck-boost gain, this AC-AC converter needs distinct switching method to generate buck/boost and buck-boost operation. Reference31presents a new bipolar buck-boost AC-AC converter with continuous input and output currents, but still, it offers low buck-boost factor. The proposal32introduces a bipolar AC-AC converter with symmetric-bipolar and step-changed output frequency operations. Nevertheless, it generates low buck-boost factor and needs large number of diodes. The 33 and 34 suggest two single-phase non-isolated AC-AC converters with bipolar operation. This structure in 33 can be equipped with different impedance networks to achieve high output voltage gains. The second topology34uses switched-capacitor technique to boost the output voltage gain. However, both circuit topologies only offer boost operation, making the proposed topologies unsuitable for DVR applications.

The back-to-back AC-DC-AC converter comprises two fully IGBT/MOSFET-based folding and unfolding H-bridge converters, along with a bulky DC-link with high capacitance. A back-to-back converter inserts a DC intermediate link between the rectifier and the inverter, so the output voltage and frequency are generated independently of the source. This gives full control over active/reactive power and easier control of dynamics. Although this converter offers many useful features, such as high-quality amplitude and frequency control, which increase its applications, it also has the following disadvantages.

Bulky DC-link capacitors having a short life, high volume, and cost.

This way, the AC-AC converter requires lower volume, footprint, and cost than a back-to-back AC-DC-AC converter.

Proposed topology

The proposed converter schematic is shown in Fig. 1. It includes five power switches (S1-S5), five diodes (D1-D5), two capacitors (C1, CO), one input inductor (Lin), and one coupled inductor (CI). The CI is modeled using a magnetizing inductance (Lm) and a leakage inductance (Llk). The turns ratio of the CI and its coupling coefficient k are defined as n=N2/N1 and Lm/(Lm+Llk), respectively, for future calculations. The AC input voltage, vin, is rectified using D1-D4. A high-frequency link, vd, is made by switch S1. This high-frequency link is fed to the primary side of a CI through a series capacitor (C1). The high-frequency component of the primary voltage of the CI is gathered with its secondary voltage and fed to an H-bridge via a series diode (D5). The H-bridge has four power switches, labeled S2 through S5. It converts the resulting high-frequency link (vx) into a pulsating AC voltage, and the output capacitor CO extracts its fundamental component.

Schematic diagram of the proposed topology.

Figure 2 depicts the critical waveforms of the converter. A high-frequency pulse signal is generated by comparing a carrier signal vtri with a constant switching frequency fS and a reference wave Vref. The resulting pulse signal, with duty ratio D, is fed to switch S1. As a result, two switching states are made. On the CI’s secondary, one switch pair (S2, S5) or (S3, S4) is kept on for each half-cycle of input voltage. Figure 2 also illustrates the output frequency step-change operation at two different frequencies: fin/2 and 2fin. The inverting and non-inverting output operation, the step-change output voltage frequency function, and the output voltage rectification mode are realized by changing the switching pattern of S2-S5.

Step control of the output voltage frequency

To change the output voltage frequency, the switching frequency of the line-frequency-operated switches (S2-S5) must be changed [See Fig. 2]. The output voltage frequency is a multiple integer of the input frequency in both step-up fO=n×fin and step-down output frequencies fO=(1/n)×fin, where n can be a positive integer number (n = 1, 2, 3, 4, …).

Output voltage rectification

According to Fig. 2, switches S2 and S5 must remain on during both the positive and negative half-cycles of the input voltage to produce a positive rectified voltage. Conversely, the switches S3 and S4 must be enabled for both positive and negative half- cycles of the input voltage to generate a negative rectified voltage.

Conclusion

A single-phase, non-isolated, symmetric bipolar AC-AC converter was introduced. It was shown that the topology can provide both buck and boost operations in both in-phase and out-of-phase output voltage modes. In addition, it can perform step-change output frequency control, which provides both step-up and step-down frequency control. Using a specific switching pattern, the converter can operate as a single-phase AC-DC rectifier. The proposed converter achieves a high buck-boost gain by integrating a coupled inductor into the topology. Additionally, the proposed converter features inherent commutation, eliminating issues with bidirectional switches and dead time, and operates with a continuous input current, thereby ensuring a low total harmonic distortion of the input current. It was demonstrated that the topology performs better than its counterparts in terms of switching device power, the volume metric index for magnetic components and capacitors, and efficiency. This article used experiments to demonstrate the converter’s superior performance as an AC voltage regulator and dynamic voltage restorer.
