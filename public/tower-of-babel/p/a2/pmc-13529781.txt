Corrosion-resistant Ti–Zr–Sn–Ta–Mo alloys with enhanced passivation for orthopedic implant applications
Authors: El-Sayed M Sherif, Ibrahim A Alnaser, Hassan Alshehri, Ahmed Fouly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHip joint degeneration is a major cause of disability worldwide, driving the need for durable implant materials with superior corrosion resistance. Titanium (Ti) alloys are widely used in orthopedic applications, yet long-term performance can be limited by electrochemical degradation in physiological environments. In this work, the influence of Mo and Ta additions on the corrosion behavior of Ti–10Zr–2Sn alloys was investigated. Three compositions—Ti–10Zr–2Sn–2Ta–6Mo, Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo, and Ti–10Zr–2Sn–6Ta–2Mo—were fabricated and evaluated in 3.5% NaCl solution using current–time response, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, and cyclic polarization. All alloys exhibited improved passivation with extended exposure time (1–48 h), attributed to the growth of protective oxide layers. Among them, Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo demonstrated the highest polarization resistance and stability, with no evidence of pitting observed by SEM/EDS. These findings highlight the promise of tailored Ti–Zr–Sn–Ta–Mo alloys for orthopedic implants, offering improved corrosion resistance and potentially longer clinical lifetimes.Keywords: Ti alloys, Powder metallurgy, Ta addition, Corrosion, Electrochemical techniquesSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

Hip joint disease is a prevalent musculoskeletal disorder frequently linked to functional impairment and anatomical alterations in the joint, which may result in disability. The hip, being a synovial joint, is susceptible to inflammation. The increasing need for orthopedic implants has catalyzed the advancement of materials that provide strength, biocompatibility, and durability. Titanium (Ti) and its alloys are favored for biomedical implants over stainless steel and Co-Cr-based alloys because of their enhanced strength, corrosion resistance, and biocompatibility.

The outstanding properties of Ti-based alloys have made them suitable for various applications such as aerospace, military, marine, automotive, parts for disables in the medicine, and offshore, etc.1–3. These alloys have high strength with excellent good mechanical characteristics low density, extraordinary corrosion resistance, and high biocompatibility4,5–7. They been have gaining a lot of interest in medicine because of their great biodegradability and biocompatibility, great mechanical performance, and corrosion resistance8,9. Employing it in the biomedical field is advantageous because they demonstrate a robust affinity for osseointegration and exhibit a reduced elastic modulus10. Ti-based alloys started to be employed in several biomedicine employments including prosthetic dentistry in early 1970s11. The Ti–6Al–4 V alloy that combines excellent physical, mechanical, corrosion resistance was firstly used in biomedicine as a substance for the restoration of damaged tissues and in transplantation procedures within the human body12–17. Because of the extensive uses of Ti–6Al–4 V alloy over the past decades, scientists have found some side effects associated with its use, which arise from the existence of V and Al. These species will release from the alloy leading to the existence of V+ 5 and Al+ 3 in the surrounding medium inside the human body. These species are toxic radicals that may in turn cause the manifestation of Alzheimer’s disease18,19.

Alloying Ti with certain metals like molybdenum (Mo), zirconium (Zr), tantalum (Ta), tin (Sn), has been reported to improve the mechanical properties and corrosion resistance of Ti20–25. The primary objective of boosting mechanical characteristics and corrosion passivation is the development of chemically inert, stable, and self-protecting layers of TiO2 and other oxides, including ZrO2 and Ta2O5, on their surfaces. Alloying Sn within Ti has been reported to strengthen it. The addition of Sn to Ti does not cause any allergies because it is not toxic, rendering it secure for alloying with Ti26. The advantage of adding Sn to Ti is that the presence of Sn lowers the fusion temperature of Ti and further decreases in the fusion temperature are obtained when the content of Sn increases27. The inclusion of Sn into Ti significantly enhances the strength of Ti and concurrently improves its corrosion resistance28,29. Adding Sn and increasing its content within the Ti alloy provided an increased corrosion resistance when the alloy was tested in a simulated body fluid30.

The presence of Zr enhances corrosion passivation and biocompatibility, while also augmenting hardness when alloyed with Ti25. Alloying Zr with Ti has another advantage in decreasing the Ti temperature of fusion and this effect increases when the content of Zr increases within Ti and that reflects on reducing the cost of the production of Ti–Zr alloys31. The effect of adding Zr on mechanical properties such as tensile properties, hardness, etc. of some Ti–Zr alloys, in order to assess their suitability for biomedical applications, has been reported to remarkably increase these mechanical properties32,33. The corrosion resistance in simulated body fluid was highly improved when adding 15%Zr to pure Ti as understood from the earlier work25. The same effect was also found when 12%Zr was added to pure Ti in 3.5% NaCl solution8.

Alves et al.34 investigated the influence of 10% Mo on the corrosion passivation of Ti and found that alloying 10% Mo with Ti highly decreases its corrosion by increasing its corrosion resistance. This effect of adding Mo was found to be less hazardous than adding V and/or Al because Mo does not dissolve or causes the release for toxic cations. Besides, it improves the mechanical parameters of the composed alloy34,35. The influence of adding Mo on the passivation of corrosion of Ti alloys has been reported in many studies and confirmed that Mo additions have a positive effect on enhancing the corrosion resistance in different corrosive media36–38. The presence of Ta with Ti was reported to superiorly improves the mechanical properties and decreases the elastic modulus39. Adding Ta into Ti–Zr alloy was found to improve the mechanical properties29 and also enhance the passivation of corrosion40. Ta enhances the passivation of Ti alloys by reducing the corrosion current via decreasing the anodic and cathodic currents, which decreases the rate of corrosion40.

The objective of this study was to manufacture Ti–10%Zr–2%Sn–2%Ta–6%Mo alloy, Ti–10%Zr–2%Sn–4%Ta–4%Mo alloy, and Ti–10%Zr–2%Sn–6%Ta–2%Mo alloy using the technique of powder metallurgy. The aim was extended to investigate the behavior of corrosion after different exposure intervals for the alloys in the sodium chloride (3.5% NaCl) solution. The behavior of corrosion was investigated by employing cyclic potentiodynamic polarization (CPP), chronoamperometric current over time at 500 mV, and impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements. Additionally, SEM images and EDX analyses were used to investigate the morphology and the elements found on the surface of the manufactured alloys.

Experimental details

Ti, Zr, Sn, Mo, and Ta powders were supplied by the NanoChemaZone Inc. (38 Truesdale Cres, Guelph, ON, Canada) and were used as received. The purity and average particle size of each of these powders were approximately 99% and 10 μm, respectively. Powder metallurgy was used to produce the following Ti-based alloys: Ti–10Zr–2Sn–2Ta–6Mo alloy, Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy, and Ti–10Zr–2Sn–6Ta–2Mo alloy. The scientific rationale for selecting the compositions of these alloys is usually based on a combination of biocompatibility requirements, β-phase stability, low elastic modulus, mechanical strength, and corrosion resistance. The composition can be understood as a carefully balanced alloy-design strategy rather than simply a mixture of biocompatible elements. Moreover, to compare the effect of increasing Ta addition from 2 to 6% with decreasing Mo content from 6 to 2% on the corrosion of the alloy that contain fixed amounts of 80%Ti, 10%Zr, and 2%Sn.

The manufacturing of these alloys was processed as follows: (i) the necessary proportions of titanium, zircon, tin, molybdenum, and tantalum powders are mixed. (ii) A 9:1 ratio of steel balls to powder was used in the experiment, and each mixture was placed in a steel jar with the balls. (iii) A desktop 220 high energy vibratory ball mill was used to position the jar containing the grains and balls. (iv) To maintain uniform distribution, the powders and ball were ground at a speed of 2000 rpm for a duration of 30 min. (v) An induction heat furnace was used to sinter the milling mixture inside a cylindrical graphite die that was 2 cm long and 1 cm diameter. The temperature was maintained at 1200 °C for 5 min while the pressure was maintained at 40 MPa25,30,40. The chemical compositions of the manufactured alloys are listed in Table 1 for easier comparison. High-purity NaCl salt was purchased from Merck; the choose of this medium was because it simulates the presence of chloride ions in seawater. The working electrodes (alloys) had a cylindrical shape with a diameter of 10 mm. Each electrode was welded to a copper wire from one face and mounted in epoxy resin to allow only one face to be exposed to the chloride solution. The surface of the alloys, which were not mounted in epoxy, were set by polishing them with sand papers of diverse grits starting from 150 and up to 800 grit25. The silver/silver chloride reference electrode (Ag/AgCl) and the platinum sheet counter electrode were also employed.

The corrosion experiments were conducted using an Autolab machine model PGSTAT302N. The CPP, experiments were obtained by scanning the potential from − 800 mV in the forward direction up to 800 mV and then in the backward direction again. The potential was scanned at a rate of 0.001667 V/s for both the forward and backward directions. An active anodic potential of 500 mV was used to conduct the chronoamperometric current over the swept duration. After the alloys were subjected to a corrosion test, their surfaces were examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, model JSM-7400 F) and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The Nyquist and Bode spectra of the EIS were measured from the open-circuit potential value (EOC) over a frequency range between 100000 Hz and 0.1 Hz. These EIS data were collected at a scan rate of 10 points per decade using the Powersine software41. All experiments were performed in triplicate after 1 h, 24 h, and 48 h, and each run was obtained using a new polished surface a new and was immersed in a new test solution for 1 h before measurements at room temperature.

Results and discussion

Cyclic potentiodynamic polarization CPP measurements

CPP measurements have been carried out to understand the corrosion of various materials mostly metallic ones in aggressive environments under various conditions8,25,30,40. The CPP curves shown in Fig. 1 were obtained for (1) Ti–10Zr–2Sn–2Ta–6Mo alloy, (2) Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy, and (3) Ti–10Zr–2Sn–6Ta–2Mo alloy after 1 h exposure to the chloride solution. CPP experiments were carried out after 24 h and 48 h and the curves are displayed in Figs. 2 and 3, respectively. The parameters extracted from the CPP data were obtained and tabulated in Table 2. The parameters of this table can be defined as follows; βc and βa are the cathodic Tafel and anodic Tafel slopes, which were calculated from the slopes of the lines that extrapolated next to the linear Tafel areas. ECorr and jCorr represent the corrosion potential and corrosion current, while RP and RCorr denote the polarization resistance and corrosion rate, respectively. The ECorr and jCorr values were also obtained from the Tafel lines of the cathodic and anodic branches. At last, the values of RP and RCorr were collected from the equations presented in the previously published work8,25,30.

The curves of Fig. 1 depict that scanning the potential decreases current for all alloys until it reaches the minimum value. This current behavior is because of the reduction of oxygen in the cathodic side8,25,30. The current started to increase after crossing the value of jCorr (corrosion current refers to the minimum value of current), which is due to the increase of potential towards the active values and the dissolution of a thin oxide(s) layer. The current continues rising in the anodic direction until scanning to the maximum applied potential (0.80 V). Scanning the potential in the opposite way led to a decrease in the obtained currents for all alloys confirming that there is no pitting corrosion taking place at this condition. The curves in Fig. 1 also indicate that the alloy containing 4%Ta and 4%Mo (Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy, curve 2) presents the lowest cathodic and anodic currents as was also confirmed by Table 2. Where, Ti-10Zr-2Sn-4Ta-4Mo alloy recorded the lowest RP value (166.23 Ωcm2) and the highest values of jCorr (0.23 µAcm− 2) and RCorr (0.00217 mmpy). Ti–10Zr–2Sn–2Ta–6Mo alloy showed the second highest passivation performance with the values of RP, jCorr, and RCorr recorded as 129.15 Ωcm2, 0.32 µAcm− 2, and 0.00302 mmpy, respectively. Figure 1; Table 2 both indicate that the corrosion passivation of the alloys decreased in the following order; Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy > Ti–10Zr–2Sn–2Ta–6Mo alloy > and Ti–10Zr–2Sn–6Ta–2Mo alloy. Here, increasing Ta content can significantly affect the corrosion behavior of titanium alloys because Ta contributes to the formation and stability of the passive oxide film that protects the metal from attack in physiological and other corrosive environments.

Figure 2 illustrates that the alloys show a similar behavior to that shown in Fig. 1 but with higher RP values and lower values for jCorr, and RCorr. Table 2 confirmed that RP recorded higher values, while jCorr, and RCorr recorder lower values when in comparison with to the recorded values after only 1 h. The influence of extending time of exposure is better seen in Fig. 3 for the alloys after 48. Here, the immersion in the solution for 48 h decreased all currents for the alloys to the lowest values obtained. The parameters listed in Table 2 elucidated that increasing the time of immersion lowers the values of jCorr and RCorr, as well as, rises the values of RP. Moreover, the pitting corrosion does not take place at all conditions as was indicated by the lower currents obtained from the backward direction than the currents of the forward direction.

Curves obtained at 500 mV for three different alloys after 48 h of exposure to a salt solution: (1) Ti-10Zr-2Sn-2Ta-6Mo, (2) Ti-10Zr-2Sn-4Ta-4Mo, and (3) Ti-10Zr-2Sn-6Ta-2Mo.

The current obtained for Ti–10Zr–2Sn–2Ta–6Mo alloy at the initial moments abruptly increased in the first 2 min (Fig. 4, curve 1), possibly due to the stripping of a thin oxide layer formed during the 1 h immersion before applying the potential of 500 mV. The current then rapidly decreased in the following 10 min as a result of a formation of an oxide film on the alloy’s surface. The current then slightly decreased with time to record almost 3 µA/cm2. The current recorded for Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy (Fig. 4, curve 2), showed the same behavior of Ti–10Zr–2Sn–2Ta–6Mo alloy but at lower values over time. A similar current over time behavior was also obtained for the third alloy, Ti–10Zr–2Sn–2Ta–6Mo alloy (Fig. 4, curve 3); the values of current for this alloy were lower than that obtained for Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy and higher than the currents obtained for Ti–10Zr–2Sn–2Ta–6Mo alloy. This behavior for the three alloys does not indicate on the occurrence of the pitting corrosion for the three alloys. Also, the lower absolute values of current obtained for Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy reveals that this alloy has the highest resistance to the uniform corrosion. CCT results thus confirm that the corrosion of the alloys decreases in the chloride solution in the following order Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy < Ti–10Zr–2Sn–6Ta–2Mo alloy < Ti–10Zr–2Sn–2Ta–6Mo alloy.

The change in currents over time after 24 h shows similar behavior as shown in Fig. 5. This behavior was also observed for the alloys after 48 h as shown in Fig. 6. The clear difference between the values of currents after the various exposure periods is that the highest currents are obtained after the shortest immersion time, 1 h. As the period of time increases, the output currents decrease with the highest current values obtained for the alloys shown. The lower currents obtained at this condition have resulted from a thicker oxide film formation during the long immersion time for the alloys. The results obtained from the chronoamperometric measurements confirm the CPP data that the passivation to corrosion is the highest for Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy and that decreases for Ti–10Zr–2Sn–6Ta–2Mo alloy, while it is the lowest for Ti–10Zr–2Sn–2Ta–6Mo alloy. This effect increases as the exposure time increases. Moreover, all alloys do not suffer pitting corrosion under these conditions.

Surface investigations

The surface morphology of the alloys that were subjected to the chloride solution for 48 h was examined by collecting SEM images, which were then subjected to 500 mV for 40 min. The EDX spectra were also taken for the investigated alloys. When subjected to the aforementioned circumstances, the surface of the Ti–10%Zr–2%Sn–2Ta–6Mo alloy was examined using SEM and EDX, as shown in Fig. 7a. Localized corrosion has caused some surface damage, as shown in the SEM image. The dark regions appear on the image belong to some defects in the corrosion products formed on the surface during the immersion in the chloride solution. The EDS profile taken for the surface as shown inside the Table that is present on the SEM image confirmed the appearance of spectra for all alloying elements as well as the presence of Na and Cl. The surface has a top layer containing 77.40% Ti, 11.39% Zr, 6.45% Mo, 2.41% Sn, and 1.51% Ta, in addition to the presence of 0.40% Na and 0.44 Cl (all in weight%). The EDS spectra indicate that all alloying elements are present on the surface of the alloy almost with their original percentages, which confirms that the alloy does not dissolve under these conditions. The detection of Na and Cl in small percentages strongly indicates the deposition of NaCl salt on the surface of the alloy.

As was also shown in the SEM image shown in Fig. 7a, the black regions become clearer but its number decreased indicating that the surface is more covered with thicker corrosion products that overcome the many defects appeared in the SEM image seen in Fig. 7a. The surface contains the following principal alloying elements, according to the elemental analysis performed by the EDS spectra and displayed in Fig. 8: 79.63% Ti, 10.67% Zr, 4.01% Mo, 2.01% Sn, and 3.68% Ta. This also indicates a high corrosion resistance of the alloy after a long immersion time, 48 h, and the high anodic potential, 500 mV.

In addition to 0.03% Na and 0.09 Cl, the surface’s top layer comprises 80.17% Ti, 10.93% Zr, 2.15% Mo, 1.54% Sn, and 5.10% Ta, according to the EDS profile that was acquired and shown in Fig. 7c’s Table. The SEM image and EDS profile thus indicate that the alloy does not dissolve at these conditions. The surface investigation agrees with the polarization and chronoamperometric measurements that the corrosion passivation of the tested Ti alloys decreases in the order Ti–10%Zr–2%Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy > Ti–10%Zr–2%Sn–6Ta–2Mo alloy > Ti–10%Zr–2%Sn–2Ta–4Mo alloy.

All obtained Nyquist plots after all times of immersion for the alloys in the chloride solution show one semicircle. The plots obtained for the Ti alloys after 1 h (Fig. 8) show that Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy has the widest diameter, which indicates that this alloy has the highest resistance to corrosion. Ti–10Zr–2Sn–6Ta–2Mo alloy has a smaller diameter compared to Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy meaning that its corrosion resistance is lower. The smallest diameter is seen to be for Ti–10Zr–2Sn–2Ta–6Mo alloy, which reveals that it has the lowest corrosion resistance. Table 3 also confirmed that RS, RP1 and RP2 reported higher values for the alloy that has 4%Ta + 4%Mo. The values of these resistances decrease for the alloy that contain 6%Ta + 2% Mo but the lowest values were recorded for the alloy with 2%Ta + 6% Mo. Therefore, the overall polarization resistance (RP), which is considered as the sum of RP1 and RP2 (RP1 + RP2), is the highest for the alloy that has 6% Ta. The values of YQ1 and YQ2 with the “n” values are almost “1.0”, which represent double layer capacitors30 Their values are the lowest for Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy and the highest for Ti–10Zr–2Sn–2Ta–6Mo alloy.

The extension of the immersion to 24 h (Fig. 9) increased the width for all alloys because of increasing the values of the real resistance (Z′) and the imaginary resistance (Z′′). The alloy that contains 4%Ta and 4%Mo still show Z′ and Z′′ the highest, which is reflected in its semicircle having the widest diameter and thus the highest resistance to corrosion. This was further confirmed by the high values of RS, RP1 and RP2 and the low values of YQ1 and YQ2. This effect is further evidenced with increasing the exposure time to 48 h due to increasing the corrosion passivation for all tested alloys.

The values of |Z| and Ŧ for all alloys were found to be greater when the time in NaCl solution was increased to 24 h before measurement. The fact that the Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy maintains its top performance under these conditions suggests that increasing the passivation period to 24 h is beneficial. The obtained values of |Z| and Φ for all alloys show further increases when the immersion time is increased to 48 h before impedance data is collected, confirming that longer immersion times significantly improve the passivation of the alloys. The results from chronoamperometric and CPP experiments corroborate the data from EIS measurements, showing that the tested alloys’ corrosion resistance increases for longer exposure times (24 and 48 h, respectively), with Ti–10Zr–2Sn–6Ta–2Mo alloy having the lowest corrosion resistance and Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy having the highest. Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy provides superior corrosion resistance because when the balanced Ta/Mo ratio is optimized, the alloy benefits from both enhanced passive-film chemistry and favorable microstructural stability. Here, Ta contributes by forming highly stable oxide form within the passive layer. This leads to increases film compactness, improves resistance to chloride penetration, reduces passive current density, and enhances long-term film stability. Additionally, Mo contributes through reduction of defect density in TiO₂, formation of Mo oxide, improved repassivation after local film damage, and suppression of localized corrosion propagation. Thus, the balanced Ta/Mo ratio leads to strengthen the passive barrier via Ta and repair and stabilize the passive film by Mo.

Conclusions

In this study, the manufacturing of Ti–10Zr–2Sn–2Ta–6Mo, Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo, and Ti–10Zr–2Sn–6Ta–2Mo alloys was carried out. The electrochemical corrosion behavior in the sodium chloride solution after various immersion times was reported using CPP, chronoamperometric current over time, and EIS measurements. It was found that all tested alloys provided excellent resistance to corrosion results by the formation of mixed oxides of TiO2, ZrO2, SnO2, Ta2O5, and MoO2. The titanium alloy with equal concentrations of Mo and Ta (4% Mo and 4% Ta) showed the highest corrosion resistance. This is because the Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy has the lowest currents and corrosion rates after all tested exposure periods as confirmed by the CPP data. The CCT curves also revealed that all alloys do not suffer pitting corrosion and the Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy recorded the lowest currents over 36 min of applying 500 mV as anodic potential. The results of the Nyquist and Bode spectra agreed with the CPP and CCT measurements indicating that the corrosion resistance decreases in the following order Ti–10Zr–2Sn–4Ta–4Mo alloy > Ti–10Zr–2Sn–6Ta–2Mo alloy > Ti–10Zr–2Sn–2Ta–6Mo alloy. All results proved that both Mo and Ta improves the corrosion resistance of the tested alloys and Ta is more beneficial in reducing the intensity of corrosion takes place. Increasing the immersion time of the alloys to 24 h enhanced the corrosion resistance for all alloys and this effect was found to be remarkably increased with extending exposure time in the chloride test solution to 48 h.
