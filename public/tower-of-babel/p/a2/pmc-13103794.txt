Effect of Equivalence Ratio on Combustion Characteristics under Propane–Air Flame Acceleration Conditions
Authors: Yangyang Yu, Li Gu, Ziyang Liu, Dan Wang, Guopeng Wu, Wenwei Yang, Yang Zheng, Jianghao Yu, Yanyan Han, Junhong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An improved constant-volume combustion bomb (CVCB) with a double-layer perforated plate was employed to investigate the combustion characteristics of propane–air premixed flames at different equivalence ratios (Φ = 0.8–1.25). Combined three-dimensional simulations and high-speed Schlieren imaging were used to analyze the coupled evolution of flame, flow, and pressure fields in confined space. The simulations revealed that before reaching the plate, expansion-induced flow and geometric contraction accelerated the unburned gas through the orifices, while pressure-driven jets near the orifices stretched and wrinkled the flame front, forming a complex flow–combustion structure. Experimentally, three flame development stages were identified: primary jet propagation, secondary jet acceleration, and flame–shock wave coupling. As Φ increased, both flame velocity and combustion intensity rose, and the flame mode transitioned from turbulent to quasi-detonative. Overpressure and high-frequency (4 kHz) filtered pressure analyses showed that both parameters increased with Φ, reaching 3.16 and 0.501 MPa at Φ = 1.25. The enhanced oscillations were attributed to local autoignition near the perforations and spontaneous ignition in the end region. This study clarifies the mechanisms of secondary jet flame acceleration and local autoignition in confined double-plate systems, providing new insight into explosion dynamics and safety control in fuel storage and transportation environments.

Introduction

The exploitation of hydrocarbon fuels such as oil and natural gas, transportation pipelines, and storage and transportation vehicles carrying oil and gas are major hazard sources. Accidents caused by improper operation and sudden collisions are sudden, complex, and more harmful, which often lead to serious consequences such as combustion and explosion. The fuel and gas system is the weakest link in the whole vehicle safety of tactical vehicles. In the military implementation of combat missions, once it is hit by enemy artillery fire, it can easily lead to catastrophic consequences. The study of the initial conditions of combustion, deflagration, and detonation in similar enclosed space is the premise of preventing such accidents. Therefore, the study of explosion mechanisms in enclosed space has important theoretical value and engineering significance for gas safety.

In the field of combustion physics and practical engineering application scenarios, the development law of flame evolution and the internal mechanism of flame combustion acceleration in closed space have always occupied an important position in the core research topics. Shelkin proposed a flame acceleration theory (Shelkin mechanism) based on the combination of combustion product expansion, wall friction effect, and turbulence through theoretical analysis. Bychkov , developed another obstacle-induced acceleration mechanism. When the flame burns through the obstacle, it accelerates due to the combustion delay effect, and the flow constraint caused by the channel blockage forms a jet flame. Flame propagation accelerates through obstacles, and obstacles have a certain incentive effect on the flame. − Dorofeev reviewed the mechanism of flame acceleration and its application in explosion safety. The physical phenomena of flame acceleration are briefly summarized. The flame acceleration propagation and flame propagation modes in different pipeline environments, such as smooth wall tube, unconstrained mixture with or without blockage, semiconstrained environment, and blocked channel environment, are studied. In the closed pipe, the flame interacts with the obstacle to produce turbulence, and the flame velocity will increase, which is caused by the positive feedback of turbulence and flow field distortion coupling. − Obstacles will change the local turbulence level and turbulence length scale, thus increasing the degree of explosion. − Perforated plate is a special blocking structure. When Wei carried out the study of flame evolution based on Bychkov theory, the plate model was introduced, and it was found that when the flame front contacted with the plate, a high-velocity jet flame would be formed at the orifice. The jet flame transits from laminar to turbulent in the integration stage, and the turbulent flame has a self-accelerating phenomenon of propagation velocity in the early stage of development. Perforated plates are usually installed in large oil tanks, tanks, and tubing to effectively suppress the violent sloshing of fuel due to inertia by increasing the damping effect, thus affecting the fuel container or pipeline. However, in an accident, the flame propagates faster when it passes through the plate. , The interaction between the plate and the flame will lead to a higher velocity. Due to the channel contraction effect and the enhancement of turbulence intensity, the overpressure and the spontaneous combustion of the unburned gas in the end area are generated, which aggravates the explosion process. The overpressure is coupled with the secondary deflagration effect formed by the spontaneous combustion of the end gas, which significantly aggravates the explosion dynamics process, and its comprehensive damage effect often far exceeds the subsequent fire accident itself. The flame accelerates through the orifice plate and induces a more intense combustion explosion phenomenon. Professor Wei Haiqiao’s research group at Tianjin University has done in-depth research in this area and found different phenomena and pressure oscillations caused by flame acceleration. The accelerated evolution process (i.e., laminar flame, jet flame, and turbulent flame) of flame passing through the plate under different initial pressures , was systematically studied by using a constant-volume combustion bomb (CVCB) experimental device. Based on the dynamic capture results of the high-speed Schlieren imaging system, it was found that when the initial pressure parameters changed, a complex nonlinear interaction phenomenon occurred between the flame leading edge and the reflected shock wave. By controlling the opening size of the control plate, three combustion modes under the coupling of reflected shock wave and turbulent flame with different characteristics were successfully induced, namely, normal combustion mode, oscillating combustion mode, and terminal gas spontaneous combustion mode. Among them, after the shock wave is reflected on the wall of the channel, the evolution of the oscillating flame is caused by the pressure superposition. The spontaneous combustion of the end gas is the result of the local high-temperature and high-pressure environment generated by the supersonic flame and the shock wave compression. Zhou observed three different combustion modes of gasoline at different oxygen concentrations in a CVCB with a perforated plate, including normal combustion without terminal gas spontaneous combustion, nondetonation terminal gas spontaneous combustion, and developed detonation terminal gas spontaneous combustion. The spontaneous combustion of the terminal gas that develops into detonation is positively correlated with the velocity of the primary flame, but it requires a lower velocity and no shock wave to develop detonation. In this study, the propagation law and characteristics of the turbulent flame structure induced by the combustion flame through the plate in the end region were systematically analyzed by means of experimental methods under the constraints of the plate. When the turbulent flame reaches the end region, six typical turbulent propagation modes can be observed. Based on the self-designed CVCB and converge three-dimensional numerical simulation method, Zhao et al. , studied the mechanism and influencing factors of flame acceleration through the plate in a closed space, and systematically discussed the formation mechanism of the end gas spontaneous combustion phenomenon. At the same time, the influence of oxygen concentration on the flame-shock evolution process and pressure oscillation behavior was elaborated in detail, and the key role of oxygen concentration in this series of physical processes was fully demonstrated. Under oxygen-rich conditions, the probability of spontaneous combustion at the end of the gas increases significantly. The problem of end-gas autoignition in detonation development is further studied, focusing on the randomness of detonation development. Gao et al. studied the evolution law of accelerated flame and the formation mechanism of shock waves with different intensities, analyzed the influence of pore size and porosity on the acceleration effect of flame evolution, and discussed the mechanism of the above parameters on the intensity of flame shock waves and pressure waves. It is found that the laminar flame will accelerate after passing through the plate, and the initial conditions for generating a clear and identifiable shock wave are clarified. Experimental studies have shown that the dynamic coupling of reflected shock waves and combustion flames can trigger a reciprocating flame structure. Johansen and Ciccarelli pointed out the turbulent propagation enhancement mechanism in the early stage of flame combustion development in the blocked tube during the experiment. The results show that with the increase of the blockage ratio, the turbulent propagation characteristics of the flame will be significantly enhanced. This enhanced turbulence characteristic effectively expands the surface area of the flame, thereby increasing the combustion rate and ultimately promoting the phenomenon of accelerated flame propagation. Wang et al. found that there was a significant interaction between obstacles and maximum pressure rate (dp/dt) max. When the distance between the plates is small, the heat dissipation effect of the orifice plate structure is significantly enhanced, resulting in a significant decrease in (dp/dt) max. When the distance between the plates increases, the disturbance of the flame intensifies, so that (dp/dt) max increases significantly. Wang et al. found that the influence of obstacles on (dp/dt) max presents a significant bidirectional characteristic, and its effect is determined by the blockage rate and the spatial position of the perforated plate. However, the perforated plates usually installed in large oil tanks, tanks, and tubings are double-porous plate structures. The flame passing through the double-porous plate produces more complex combustion phenomena, and the relationship with the initial conditions is unknown. Therefore, further more detailed research is needed.

In this study, a systematic investigation was conducted to clarify the combustion behavior of propane–air premixed flames propagating through a double-layer perforated plate. The main objective was to elucidate the coupling mechanisms among flame evolution, flow dynamics, and pressure oscillations in a confined environment, and to determine how variations in the equivalence ratio (Φ) regulate these processes. Specifically, the study aimed to identify the characteristic stages of flame developmentnamely, the primary jet propagation stage, the secondary jet formation and acceleration stage, and the flame–shock wave coupling stageand to reveal the mechanisms of secondary jet acceleration and local autoignition associated with these stages.

To achieve these objectives, an improved constant-volume combustion bomb (CVCB) equipped with double-perforated plates was employed. High-speed Schlieren imaging was used to capture the transient evolution of the flame front, enabling quantitative analysis of flame velocity and structural morphology. Simultaneously, a high-frequency (4 kHz) pressure acquisition system was utilized to measure overpressure evolution and oscillation behavior. Each test condition was repeated three times to ensure experimental repeatability.

Furthermore, three-dimensional numerical simulations based on the SAGE combustion model and the RNG k−ε turbulence model were performed to analyze the internal flow field, temperature distribution, and intermediate species formation. The spatial distributions of CH2O and OH were selected as indicators of low- and high-temperature reaction zones, respectively. By integrating experimental and numerical approaches, this study systematically explored the regulation effect of Φ on flame acceleration, pressure oscillation, and combustion intensity, providing theoretical guidance for understanding confined combustion and preventing explosion hazards in engineering applications.

Numerical Setup

To further investigate the internal flow field and distribution of combustion intermediates within the constant-volume combustion bomb, three-dimensional CFD simulations were performed as shown in Figure . The base grid size was set to 2 mm, and fixed local refinements were prescribed in critical regions to ensure sufficient resolution of flame development and flow–structure interaction. Specifically, four refinement levels were applied in the spark ignition region, while two refinement levels were imposed in both the initial flame development region (including the first perforated plate) and the second perforated plate region.

3D model of computational domain.

To accurately capture flame propagation and strong gradient evolution during combustion, adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) with three additional refinement levels was activated based on temperature and reaction rate gradients. As combustion progressed, the mesh was dynamically refined, and the total cell number increased to a maximum of approximately 5.0 million cells.

Ignition was modeled using an energy deposition approach to represent spark discharge. A localized high-energy source was introduced at the geometric center of the chamber, positioned 5 mm away from the wall. The energy deposition duration was 0.7 ms with a total energy input of 0.05 J, which was sufficient to initiate combustion while maintaining numerical stability.

The submodels employed are listed in Table . The combustion model was the SAGE detailed chemistry solver coupled with the USC-MECH II chemical kinetics mechanism. The turbulence model adopted was the RNG k−ε model. For emission prediction, the Hiroyasu–NSC model was used for soot formation, and the Extended Zeldovich model was employed for nitrogen oxides. Results and Discussion

Fundamental Combustion Behavior

Based on three-dimensional numerical simulations, a comparative analysis was conducted on the propagation and combustion behavior of a propane premixed flame under the constraints of a double-layer perforated plate. Under representative conditions of a premixed pressure of 0.3 MPa and an equivalence ratio of 1, the temporal evolution of the density gradient field from 0 to 1.5 ms was analyzed to characterize the detailed flame propagation process, which is illustrated in Figure .

Evolution of the flame development in the whole combustion bomb during 0–1.5 ms.

At the initial stage (t < 0.3 ms), a nearly spherical flame kernel was formed near the ignition location. The density gradient distribution was primarily concentrated at the flame front due to rapid heat release and gas expansion. The flame propagated symmetrically until it approached the first perforated plate.

As the flame interacted with the first perforated plate (approximately 0.4–0.8 ms), the initially smooth flame front was segmented into multiple jet-like structures corresponding to the hole distribution. The confinement imposed by the perforations led to localized acceleration of the burned gases, resulting in intensified density gradients near the hole exits. The flame front became highly corrugated, and distinct trailing structures were observed downstream of the plate.

After penetrating the first perforated plate, the flame exhibited enhanced stretching and deformation. The jet-induced flow accelerated the flame propagation in the axial direction, while lateral expansion was temporarily restricted by geometric confinement.

When the flame reached the second perforated plate (approximately 1.0–1.5 ms), similar but more pronounced jetting behavior occurred. The interaction between the accelerated flame front and the second plate generated stronger localized gradients and elongated jet-like flame structures. Downstream of the second plate, the flame front evolved into a more irregular morphology, accompanied by intensified expansion and flow–flame coupling.

Besides, the evolution of the temperature field, density gradient, and velocity field throughout the flame development process was investigated (see Figure ). It can be observed that sparse yet discernible streamlines have appeared to the right of the flame front before it reached the surface of the rightmost porous plate. It indicated that the volumetric expansion of the upstream flame and the geometric contraction-induced effect jointly acted on the unburned mixture, prompting it to flow around and accelerate downstream through the pores ahead of time. Simultaneously, the spatial confinement imposed by the perforated plate introduces asymmetric velocity gradients, forming localized acceleration zones and shear layers near the orifices.

Evolution of the temperature field, density gradient, and velocity field throughout the flame development process.

As the flame approached the perforated plate, the high-temperature and high-density-gradient regions migrated toward the orifice area. The pressure difference between the upper and lower sides of the plate drove jet flows through the perforations, which stretched and deformed the flame front. It was observed that the flame-front temperature did not exhibit a noticeable decrease, while the streamlines within the first contacted hole became significantly elongated. When the flame neared the plate, a large pressure gradient was established on both sides of the orifice, accelerating the gas flow through the pores and leading to elongated and intensified streamlines.

At this stage, supported by high temperature and strong reaction rates, the flame front maintained a considerable heat release intensity without evident temperature decay. This finding indicated that the dual-layer perforated plates did not significantly suppress flame propagation but rather induced morphological reconstruction of the flame near the orifices. The interaction between jet flows and recirculation zones at these locations caused local stretching and wrinkling of the flame front, forming a complex three-dimensional flow–combustion coupled structure.

When the flame propagated through an increasing number of perforations, the streamlines on the right side of several holes became progressively elongated, revealing that the gas jets emerging from the perforations began to interact in the downstream region. Driven by thermal expansion, each orifice generated an individual jet, and the interference among these jets resulted in local flow acceleration and convergence, forming an extended streamline zone behind the plate.

With the flame developing further downstream, the fronts issuing from neighboring perforations gradually approached one another and eventually coalesced, producing a more continuous and coherent reaction zone. This coalescence strengthened the spatial continuity of heat release and expanded the high-temperature region on the right side of the plate. In addition, a mild temperature increase was detected on the left side, which was mainly attributed to heat transfer through convection and radiation from the adjacent burning zones.

To gain deeper insight into the combustion process, the distributions of representative low- and high-temperature intermediate species were subsequently analyzed. CH2O was selected as a representative low-temperature intermediate species, as it was widely recognized as a key product formed during the low-temperature oxidation stage and the preignition process. Previous experimental studies employing formaldehyde PLIF diagnostics demonstrated that CH2O distributions effectively delineated cool-flame structures and first-stage ignition behavior in both spray and premixed combustion systems. −

In contrast, OH radicals were considered characteristic high-temperature products, as they were strongly correlated with the main heat release zone and high-temperature oxidation reactions. Numerous optical diagnostic studies using OH chemiluminescence and laser-induced fluorescence confirmed that OH distributions closely corresponded to the main reaction zone and regions of intense heat release. −

Accordingly, CH2O and OH were adopted in this study to characterize the evolution of low-temperature chemistry and high-temperature combustion processes, respectively. As illustrated in Figure , the spatial distributions of CH2O and OH radicals exhibited distinct separation patterns during the propagation of the propane premixed flame through the dual-layer perforated structure. CH2O, representing the low-temperature intermediate species, was primarily distributed in the unburned region ahead of the flame front, whereas OH, a typical high-temperature radical, was concentrated within and behind the flame front. The flame front thus served as a clear demarcation between the preoxidation and main oxidation zones. Throughout the entire flame propagation process across the rightmost perforated plate, the CH2O concentration field remained relatively uniform, and no significant variation in peak magnitude was observed, suggesting that the low-temperature oxidation kinetics were only weakly influenced by the perforated configuration.

Evolution of the low- and high-temperature intermediate species throughout the flame development process.

In contrast, the OH radical field showed a pronounced increase in both intensity and spatial extent as the flame advanced through the perforations. The OH concentration increased sharply from the flame front toward the inner flame zone and then gradually decreased downstream, corresponding to the evolution of high-temperature oxidation and heat-release regions. When the flame penetrated multiple orifices, the OH-rich zones originating from different perforations began to merge, forming a continuous high-temperature layer downstream of the plate. This merging behavior indicated enhanced coupling between jet-induced turbulence and the main reaction zone, which promoted more uniform combustion and stabilized the overall flame structure.

Moreover, the asymmetry of OH distribution near the perforated region reflected the local jet–flame interactions: the high-velocity jets accelerated the gas flow and stretched the flame front, while the adjacent recirculation zones sustained the reaction by providing residual radicals and heat feedback. Consequently, the dual-layer perforated plate not only maintained the integrity of the low-temperature preoxidation region but also intensified high-temperature reactions downstream, demonstrating its dual role in flame stabilization and combustion enhancement.

The Influence of Equivalence Ratio on Flame Evolution Morphology and Velocity

Considering the interaction between equivalence ratio and combustion characteristics, the combustion behavior of the double-perforated plate under different equivalence ratios was analyzed, including flame evolution, propagation velocity, overpressure development, and pressure oscillations. Based on high-speed Schlieren imaging technology, the influence of the equivalence ratio (Φ) on the flame evolution mode and propagation velocity was systematically investigated and clarified in a closed experimental chamber with an initial pressure of 0.3 MPa. Figures and present the Schlieren images of flame propagation at different equivalence ratios (Φ = 0.8, 1.0, 1.1, and 1.25).

Flame propagation process under Φ = 0.8 (a) and Φ = 1.0 (b).

Flame propagation process under Φ = 1.1 (a) and Φ = 1.25 (b).

Similarly, within the observation region, the variation of Φ exhibited a weak regulatory effect on the flame velocity in front of the B plate, but exerted a pronounced influence on the flame acceleration process behind the plate. Therefore, the analysis in this study primarily focused on the variation in flame velocity after the flame penetrated the B plate. As the stoichiometric ratio of the mixture increased, four distinct combustion phenomena were observed during the flame propagation process, namely, turbulent flame, local autoignition, jet-induced spontaneous combustion, and end-wall spontaneous combustion.

The turbulent flame referred to the increase in wrinkling and distortion of the flame front after jet-induced acceleration. Compared with the relatively smooth flame front of a laminar flame, the accelerated jet flow enhanced flame surface corrugation, resulting in a clearly irregular and turbulent flame structure.

As shown in Figure b, a bright yellow flame appeared in the later stage of combustion, located away from the second perforated plate. This phenomenon indicated the occurrence of locally intensified incomplete combustion and secondary combustion behavior, and was therefore defined as local autoignition.

As illustrated in Figure a, the bright yellow flame region migrated closer to the perforations of the second plate. Moreover, the spatial location of the bright yellow flame became consistent with that of the initial jet flame. This behavior was attributed to enhanced jet–flow interaction and was defined as jet-induced spontaneous combustion.

When Φ increased to 1.25, bright yellow flame regions were observed on the second plate at the early stage of jet formation. Since the spontaneous combustion occurred directly at the end-wall region near the perforated plate, this phenomenon was defined as end-wall spontaneous combustion.

When Φ was 0.8, 1.0, 1.1, and 1.25, the secondary flame (laminar flow) in region (II) passed through the B plate and exhibited a morphological transition from a jet flame to a turbulent flame. In region (III), this process corresponded to the primary jet flame propagation stage. At Φ = 0.8 (t = 1.625 ms), Φ = 1.0 (t = 1.350 ms), Φ = 1.1 (t = 1.200 ms), and Φ = 1.25 (t = 0.900 ms), a distinct secondary flame was clearly observed.

Figure further illustrated that the flame velocity began to accelerate at approximately 70 mm for all four equivalence ratios, indicating that the primary flame in region (II) started to pass through the B plate. Consequently, a secondary jet flame phenomenon was initiated in region (III) due to the interaction between the flame and the jet flows through the perforations. When Φ = 0.8, this phenomenon was not pronounced and the overall velocity exhibited fluctuations. However, at Φ = 1.0, 1.1, and 1.25, the velocity clearly showed a secondary acceleration phase. With increasing equivalence ratio, both the maximum propagation rate of the primary flame in region (III) and the velocity of the secondary flame displayed a synchronous growth trend.

Specifically, the results indicated that an increase in the equivalence ratio led to a gradual rise in both peak overpressure and oscillation amplitude. The enhanced reactivity of the mixture promoted more rapid flame acceleration and intensified pressure fluctuations within the chamber. The high-frequency pressure signals captured at 4 kHz effectively reflected the transient pressure wave propagation and acoustic coupling induced by the flame–flow interaction. These oscillations corresponded to the rapid combustion events associated with the secondary jet flame and local autoignition phenomena, suggesting that the pressure field evolution was strongly correlated with the spatial development of the turbulent flame.

As shown in Figure , when Φ = 0.8, the maximum peak overpressure reached 1.72 MPa, and the maximum filtered pressure amplitude was 0.056 MPa. It was noteworthy that the peaks of these two parameters did not occur simultaneously. This discrepancy was attributed to the secondary jet combustion that developed in region (III) after the primary flame in region (II) penetrated the B plate. As the flame continued to evolve, the overpressure gradually increased, eventually reaching the maximum value of 1.72 MPa; however, the combustion intensity at this stage was not the highest.

When Φ = 1.0, the maximum peak overpressure increased to 2.01 MPa, while the maximum filtered pressure amplitude reached 0.179 MPa. The time points corresponding to these two peaks were also inconsistent. The earlier occurrence of the maximum filtered pressure amplitude was caused by the enhanced local autoignition induced by compression effects, which produced the highest combustion intensity before the overall chamber pressure reached its maximum. As the flame further developed and propagated, the maximum peak overpressure was subsequently achieved.

At Φ = 1.1, the maximum peak overpressure rose to 2.27 MPa, and the maximum filtered pressure amplitude reached 0.306 MPa. The continuous compression waves ahead of the flame front induced local autoignition at location f, generating pronounced pressure oscillations. When the filtered pressure reached 0.214 MPa, spontaneous combustion occurred near the jet orifices on the B plate (location g), further amplifying the oscillations. Subsequently, both the direct impingement of shock waves on the end wall and the secondary impacts from reflected shock waves triggered spontaneous combustion of the end gas. The resulting maximum peak overpressure reached 2.27 MPa, with a corresponding filtered pressure amplitude of 0.275 MPa.

At Φ = 1.25, the maximum peak overpressure further increased to 3.16 MPa, and the maximum filtered pressure amplitude reached 0.501 MPa. The extreme overpressure was primarily caused by spontaneous combustion occurring when the flame propagated into the end region, generating an intense pressure rise. The subsequent propagation process was dominated by the strong interaction among jet-induced spontaneous combustion, end-wall spontaneous combustion, and the residual unburned mixture, which produced the highest filtered pressure amplitude observed in the tests.

A systematic analysis of the influence of equivalence ratio (Φ = 0.8, 1.0, 1.1, and 1.25) on the maximum peak overpressure and maximum filtered pressure in confined-space combustion was conducted based on Figure . The results demonstrated a clear positive correlation between the equivalence ratio and both peak overpressure and filtered pressure amplitude. As the equivalence ratio increased, the reactivity and heat release of the mixture intensified, leading to higher-pressure peaks and stronger oscillations. Analysis of the experimental results further revealed that, for Φ = 0.8, 1.0, 1.1, and 1.25, the peak overpressure and filtered pressure amplitudes were significantly and positively correlated with the flame propagation velocity.

As Φ increased from 0.8 to 1.25, the flame propagation velocity and secondary jet acceleration both increased, accompanied by enhanced local autoignition near the perforation orifices and spontaneous ignition in the end region.
