Alström syndrome in China: epidemiologic trends, geographic distribution, and clinical-socioeconomic profiles under innovative care models

Introduction

Alström syndrome (ALMS; MIM #203800) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the ALMS1 gene. It is characterized by early-onset obesity, severe insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, and progressive multisystem involvement, including cardiomyopathy and metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). The clinical manifestations of ALMS typically begin in infancy, with the severity and number of affected organ systems increasing with age [1, 2]. Notably, patients with ALMS who develop MASLD are more likely to have metabolic associated steatohepatitis and progress to more severe fibrosis than MASLD patients without ALMS [2]. The ALMS1 gene encodes a protein that is broadly expressed across various tissues and localized to the proximal ends of centrioles and the basal bodies of cilia [3]. The ALMS1 protein plays critical roles in ciliary maintenance and function, endosomal transport, cell cycle regulation, metabolic homeostasis, and cell differentiation [4, 5]. Since cilia are present in nearly all human cell types—including those in the respiratory tract, kidneys, and reproductive organs [5] —this widespread expression likely accounts for the diverse and systemic dysfunction observed in individuals with ALMS.

ALMS is extremely rare, with an estimated prevalence of 1–9 per 1,000,000 individuals, and approximately 1,000 cases reported globally to date [6]. The clinical presentation is highly heterogeneous and age-dependent, often resulting in misdiagnosis or significant diagnostic delay. Since ALMS was first described in 1959 [7], symptom variability—even among family members—has posed challenges for timely clinical recognition. The 2020 European clinical management guidelines for ALMS emphasize key diagnostic indicators, including vision and hearing impairment, cardiomyopathy, childhood-onset obesity, severe insulin resistance, progressive MASLD, renal dysfunction, respiratory disease, and genitourinary involvement [2].

As of December 2024, 161 individuals have been diagnosed with ALMS in China. Delayed diagnosis can lead to life-threatening complications and profoundly affect patients, caregivers, and families. The rarity, clinical complexity, and age-related progression of ALMS often hinder timely recognition and comprehensive management, contributing to disease progression, psychological distress, and substantial financial burden. While numerous international rare disease organizations—such as the International Rare Diseases Research Consortium [8], EURORDIS–Rare Diseases Europe [9], and the Asia Pacific Alliance of Rare Disease Organizations [10] —have made significant contributions to advancing global care, there remains a lack of specific guidance tailored to the ALMS population in China.

In response, we initiated a China-adapted care model primarily involving consultant physicians and patient advocacy organizations. This study documents the growth in diagnosed cases over the past five years under this model, and describes ongoing challenges in patient management, support systems, and diagnostic pathways. Notably, among the diagnosed individuals in this study, 150 of 161 (93.8%) were children. Early intervention during critical developmental periods may help delay disease progression and improve long-term outcomes [2]. Furthermore, this study examines diagnostic timelines and contributing factors, highlights the care model’s role in improving health equity and care coordination, and identifies key symptom clusters—such as the co-occurrence of visual impairment and cardiomyopathy—that may serve as clinical “red flags” for earlier genetic testing and diagnosis.

Methods

The model of patient advocacy-consultant doctors in ALMS management

Established in 2017, the Alström Syndrome Greater China Association (ASGCA) was initiated by a patient advocacy–led alliance with support from medical professionals, aiming to represent stakeholders affected by ALMS across China. According to its strategic plan, ASGCA’s mission is to collaborate with national and regional policymakers, industry stakeholders, and healthcare professionals to raise awareness, foster partnerships that promote equitable and affordable diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, and ultimately improve health outcomes and quality of life for patients and their caregivers.

ASGCA plays a central role in patient engagement and community coordination (Fig. 1). To enhance communication and knowledge dissemination, the association utilizes multiple platforms, including its official website, verified WeChat accounts, WeChat-based communication groups, a WeChat Video Channel, and in-person exchange meetings. In collaboration with non-profit organizations, ASGCA also conducts public awareness campaigns. These communication channels collectively facilitate peer support, clinical information exchange, and public advocacy, thereby strengthening the visibility, cohesion, and outreach of the ALMS community nationwide.

In parallel, designated healthcare providers and follow-up hospitals—particularly Shanghai Children’s Medical Center—have implemented coordinated strategies to improve ALMS patient care and awareness. These efforts include: (1) organizing annual multidisciplinary team (MDT) consultation sessions; (2) establishing collaborations with charitable organizations and commercial partners (e.g., insurance providers) to secure support and expand outreach; (3) utilizing social media platforms such as official WeChat accounts to disseminate disease education content; (4) participating in domestic and international academic conferences to raise disease awareness; and (5) actively tracking newly reported cases in both domestic and international literature to facilitate timely patient engagement.

Subjects

Between January 2020 and December 2024, a study was conducted using patient data from the ASGCA (https://www.alstrom.cn/) database. ASGCA is a patient-centered organization committed to supporting individuals affected by ALMS. Patients included in this study were diagnosed between September 1, 2014, and December 1, 2024, and were from diverse geographic regions across China.

Data were collected via an online questionnaire administered through the largest and most secure online survey platform in China, which provides built-in features to enhance response quality. Previous studies have shown that internet-based surveys yield higher response rates than traditional mail surveys [11] and produce more accurate data than telephone interviews, particularly when addressing relatively sensitive topics [12].

Project coordinators conducted structured interviews focusing on patients’ diagnostic journeys and multiple dimensions of ALMS care. Topics included the diagnostic process, access to treatment, availability of services, information dissemination, and support systems. Sample questions included: “Please describe your journey to being diagnosed with ALMS” and “Have you received any social welfare benefits?” Responses were recorded in Microsoft Excel using a standardized data extraction form. A total of 161 patients were diagnosed during the study period, with ages ranging from 1 to 36 years (Fig. 2). All responses were manually reviewed by the research team, and any extreme or inconsistent entries were individually verified. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to data collection.

The questionnaire gathered the following self-reported information from patients or their caregivers: (1) Sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, educational level, household income); (2) Diagnostic and treatment history (e.g., age at symptom onset and diagnosis, current treatment status); (3) Economic burden (e.g., out-of-pocket medical expenses).

Statistical analysis

Sociodemographic and disease-related characteristics were summarized by patient clusters. Statistical analyses and plotting were conducted using R version 4.4.3. Categorical variables were expressed as number and frequencies (%) and continuous variables for ALMS patients are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Participants with missing data for specific variables were excluded from the corresponding analyses.

Results

Growth trajectory of ASGCA membership from 2020 to 2024

The combined efforts of the ASGCA and clinical institutions have led to measurable growth in the organization’s influence and coverage. Membership trends from 2020 to 2024 are presented in Fig. 3. During this period, the ASGCA experienced a consistent and substantial increase in registered members, reflecting enhanced outreach and engagement within the rare disease community. Specifically, membership grew from 50 individuals in 2020 to 90 in 2021, representing an 80% increase. The upward trend continued with 104 members in 2022, 127 in 2023, and 161 in 2024—representing more than a threefold increase since 2020. This steady growth highlights the expanding recognition and impact of ASGCA’s initiatives in advocacy, patient support, and professional collaboration.

Geographic distribution and temporal trends of patients with ALMS in China

Among the 161 patients included in this study, all were of Chinese ethnicity. As shown in Fig. 4A, patients were widely distributed across mainland China, spanning 29 of the 34 provincial-level administrative divisions. This reflects the extensive geographic reach of the ASGCA network. Figure 4B summarizes the distribution by macro-regions, showing that East China accounted for the highest proportion of patients (approximately 48.9%), followed by South Central and North China. These patterns may reflect regional differences in healthcare infrastructure, diagnostic capacity, and access to referral pathways.

Figure 4C illustrates the temporal trend in the absolute number of diagnosed patients from 2020 to 2024. The observed increase is likely attributable to greater diagnostic awareness, broader outreach by the ASGCA, and the implementation of enhanced patient registration systems via digital platforms such as WeChat. These platforms have proven particularly effective in facilitating outreach beyond major urban centers like Shanghai. Notably, Fujian, Guangdong, Shandong, and Henan provinces exhibited the most substantial growth in ALMS case reporting during this period.

Demographic and clinical features of the patients

Key demographic and clinical characteristics of the ALMS cohort are presented in Table 1. Of the genetically confirmed individuals, 150 of 161 (93.8%) were younger than 18 years at the time of diagnosis. We analyzed the age at onset of initial symptoms, revealing that retinal degeneration most commonly began during infancy (Fig. 5A). To assess diagnostic delays, we examined the interval between symptom onset and confirmed diagnosis. Among the 94 patients with available data, only 30 (31.9%) were diagnosed within one year of symptom onset, while 64 (68.1%) experienced delayed diagnosis. Of these, 27 (28.7%) received a diagnosis 5–20 years after symptom onset, and 37 (39.4%) were diagnosed within 1–5 years (Fig. 5B).

As part of the diagnostic pathway analysis, we identified key phenotypes that contributed to successful diagnosis (Fig. 5C). Ophthalmologic referral was the most common route (53.1%). Other phenotypes prompting evaluation included cardiovascular disease (15.6%) and early-onset obesity (12.3%). Compared with patients lacking these symptoms or presenting later, individuals exhibiting both early-onset cardiomyopathy and visual impairment tended to receive a more timely diagnosis. This pattern is consistent with findings from United Kingdom (UK)-based ALMS cohorts [13]. Additionally, 5.6% of patients underwent genetic testing due to the presence of similar phenotypes in family members, underscoring the importance of offering genetic screening to first-degree relatives of affected individuals.

Socioeconomic characteristics

A total of 93 economic questionnaires were completed, of which 13 were excluded due to duplication or insufficient information. Additionally, 20 respondents were under school age, and one individual was no longer school-aged, as shown in Table 2. Among the valid responses, 22.8% of patients had attended special education schools, 41.3% resided in rural areas, and only 16.3% reported receiving any form of social welfare benefits. Regarding parental employment status, 56.3% of families indicated that both parents were employed, 38.7% reported employment of only one parent (either mother or father), and 5% of families reported that neither parent was employed.

Current status of medical visits

Among parents of children with Alström syndrome who participated in the survey (Fig. 6), 51.3% had attended MDT clinic since 2020, while 48.7% had not. Among those who did not attend, 58.7% cited long travel distances as the primary barrier, 20% reported a lack of available time, and the remaining respondents mentioned factors such as high transportation costs or difficulties in bringing young children to appointments. Regarding preferred follow-up models, 52.5% expressed a preference for annual health check-ups and consultations organized by patient advocacy associations. Another 37.5% favored receiving medical examinations locally, followed by participation in consultations organized by patient groups or through remote telemedicine services. Only 10% preferred a fully localized model in which both examinations and consultations are conducted within their home region.

Discussion

Overview of key findings

This study provides the first comprehensive evaluation of the diagnostic challenges, healthcare access disparities, and the role of patient advocacy organizations in the management of ALMS in China. Through collaboration with such organizations, we demonstrated a care model that was associated with a significant increase in diagnosed cases in recent years. Because the data are cross-sectional and descriptive, we avoid making comparative claims about screening timeliness, diagnostic intervals, or follow-up engagement. Instead, we interpret the observed patterns as hypothesis-generating and suggest that coordinated, patient-centered models may warrant prospective evaluation to determine their impact on screening access, diagnostic delay, and longitudinal care.

However, Based on an estimated ALMS incidence of 1–9 per million [6] and a projected patient population of approximately 1,400 in China, our findings indicate that a substantial number of individuals likely remain undiagnosed or have not yet accessed patient organizations or specialized care.

We also examined regional distribution relative to population size. Some provinces with a high number of ALMS patients (e.g., Guangdong, Shandong) also have large populations, as expected. However, other regions demonstrated unexpected patterns, which may reflect differences in diagnostic capacity, clinician awareness, or connectivity to referral networks. Notably, Fujian province, despite having a smaller population, reported the highest number of confirmed cases, suggesting that diagnostic awareness and local patient advocacy strength are critical contributors to case identification. These findings imply that disparities in ALMS detection are not merely due to population size but involve systemic and geographic inequities.

Visual impairment, cardiomyopathy, and obesity were the primary clinical features prompting diagnosis, with ophthalmologic referral being the most common pathway. Despite this, diagnostic delays remain substantial: only 31.9% of patients received a diagnosis within one year of symptom onset, and 28.7% experienced delays of 5–20 years. The majority were diagnosed before the age of five. ASGCA has played a pivotal role in facilitating diagnosis across regions and in mitigating some of the delays associated with local disparities in healthcare infrastructure. Significant inequities in access to specialized services and follow-up care were identified. Although multidisciplinary consultations and ASGCA-led programs were available, nearly half of families did not participate, citing geographic and logistical barriers. This emphasizes the need for expanded telemedicine capacity and decentralized care pathways to enhance access.

In China’s hierarchical hospital system, rare disease services are primarily concentrated in tertiary hospitals, presenting challenges for families in remote areas. This contributes to regional imbalances in diagnostic capacity and specialist access, especially in northwestern provinces. These data underscore the need for regional capacity-building and enhanced clinician training in rare disease recognition and referral.

Digital platforms such as WeChat, used by over 1.2 billion people in China, have become essential for rare disease community engagement and peer support [14]. While national public health outreach is typically institutional, ASGCA’s use of WeChat for education, triage, and ongoing communication represents a patient-led strategy that helps overcome structural limitations in the absence of a national rare disease referral system.

Families of children with ALMS often face compounded challenges involving medical, educational, and social domains. Socioeconomic data from our cohort highlight that many families reside in rural areas with limited social protection. While most families had dual-income earners, a considerable proportion relied on single-income or unemployed caregivers. Among 54 school-aged patients, only 18 (33.3%) had attended special education institutions, despite universal vision impairment [2], indicating insufficient educational support and awareness in rural areas.

International experience highlights collaboration among patient organizations, coordinated multidisciplinary services, and supportive policy instruments. Relative to this pattern, China’s current strengths include a WeChat-supported nationwide clinical network that facilitates information-sharing and referrals, concentrated tertiary-hospital expertise, and growing patient-partnered collaborative care participation. Remaining priorities are to build independent advocacy capacity, standardize multicenter pathways with sustainable support, and develop patient-partnered registries to strengthen longitudinal follow-up.

In contrast, ALMS management in China is largely physician-led, with diagnosis and treatment concentrated in major urban hospitals. The national infrastructure for rare disease coordination remains limited. Regional variation in specialist access persists. In this context, patient organizations such as ASGCA have emerged to facilitate patient identification, referrals, and follow-up, often relying on social media and informal networks. While not a replacement for formal systems, this approach offers a pragmatic solution in settings with limited healthcare resources and geographic disparities.

Future directions and lessons learned

In high-income countries, patient advocacy organizations are instrumental in shaping policy, supporting research, and improving care coordination. Social media enables real-time communication and peer support beyond traditional research-based data collection [15]. Although formal evaluations of ASGCA’s impact are limited, its activities—such as supporting diagnosis, fostering peer networks, and addressing healthcare inequities—align with the roles documented for advocacy groups in other rare diseases [16]. These efforts may help create a more structured ALMS care ecosystem.

Currently, pediatric rare disease care in China follows a tiered referral system [17]. Community health centers initiate early screening and risk identification, while primary and secondary hospitals manage routine care and escalate complex cases to tertiary centers. The National Pediatric Rare Disease Center oversees network coordination, specialist consultations, and capacity building. Together, these elements form a rapid-response system for rare disease management.

To address the challenges of uneven rare disease awareness and regional resource disparities, we propose establishing a distributed ALMS clinical network. Shanghai Children’s Medical Center would serve as the central coordination hub, linked to regional sub-centers responsible for multidisciplinary evaluations and standardized care (Fig. 7). Remote consultation services would extend expert input to underserved regions. This approach aims to enhance equity, access, and continuity of care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The economic dataset was relatively small, potentially affecting the generalizability of cost-related findings. Incomplete reporting limited the accurate classification of direct medical, non-medical, and indirect costs. Data on symptom onset and diagnosis timing were missing for 67 patients, introducing potential bias in diagnostic delay analyses. These limitations may have influenced the interpretation of subgroup trends, particularly related to economic burden.

Future studies should incorporate broader assessments of psychosocial and community outcomes to more comprehensively address patient and caregiver needs. A longitudinal cohort study is planned using standardized measures to evaluate the effectiveness of this care model in improving pediatric ALMS outcomes over time.

Conclusion

The patient advocacy–consultant physician model has proven effective for ALMS management in China. However, many potential patients across the country remain undiagnosed or unidentified. Approximately half of those diagnosed have not received systematic evaluation or regular follow-up by specialized clinicians. As most identified cases in our dataset are pediatric, a nationwide clinical network should emphasize pediatric services while simultaneously developing adult pathways to address potential under recognition beyond pediatric centers. Such a network could help bridge critical gaps in diagnosis, long-term monitoring, and multidisciplinary management, ultimately improving outcomes and quality of life for affected children and their families.
