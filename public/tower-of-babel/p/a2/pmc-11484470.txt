Genome-wide identification of AP2/ERF gene family in Coptis Chinensis Franch reveals its role in tissue-specific accumulation of benzylisoquinoline alkaloids
Authors: Mengyu Zhang, Pingping Lu, Yating Zheng, Xue Huang, Junnan Liu, Han Yan, Huige Quan, Rui Tan, Fengming Ren, Hezhong Jiang, Jiayu Zhou, Hai Liao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Plant-specific AP2/ERF gene family encodes proteins involved in various biological and physiological processes. Although the genome of Coptis chinensis Franch, a plant producing benzylisoquinoline alkaloids (BIAs), has been sequenced at the chromosome level, studies on the AP2/ERF gene family in C. chinensis are lacking. Thus, a genome-wide identification of AP2/ERF gene family in C. chinensis was conducted to explore its role in BIAs biosynthesis.ResultsA total of 96 CcAP2/ERF genes were identified and categorized into five subfamilies, including 43 ERFs, 32 DREBs, 17 AP2s, 3 RAVs, and 1 Soloist, based on their structural domains. These CcAP2/ERF genes were unevenly distributed across nine chromosomes. Analysis of gene duplication events identified 17 CcAP2/ERF gene pairs in the genome, with 7 involved in tandem duplication events and 10 involved in segmental duplicate events, indicating that both types of duplications contributed to the expansion of the AP2/ERF gene family. The Ka/Ks ratio analysis suggested that the CcAP2/ERF gene family underwent strong purifying selection. Two phytohormones, methyl jasmonate and abscisic acid, were identified as potential key inducers of BIAs biosynthesis due to the cis-acting element prediction. Analysis of the spatial transcriptomic data revealed that 28 differentially expressed AP2/ERF genes had the highest or relatively higher expression levels in the rhizome, 17 of which positively correlated with the tissue-specific accumulation of BIAs. Further real-time PCR verification and protein-protein interaction analysis indicated that DREB1B might be one of the central regulators in a highly complex BIAs biosynthesis network.ConclusionThese findings provide significant insight into the function of AP2/ERF genes in C. chinensis, particularly in the regulatory network of BIAs biosynthesis in C. chinensis. This study also identifies candidate genes for metabolic engineering to increase BIAs content in C. chinensis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10883-1.

Introduction

The rhizomes of medicinal plants provide various bioactive metabolites, such as phenolic acids, ginsenoside, tanshinone and saponin, and hence have been valued as traditional medicine resources for centuries [1–4]. Since the rhizome of Coptis chinensis Franch (Huang Lian) was first introduced in “Divine Farmer’s Materia Medica”, the earliest medical book in China 2000 years ago, it has been widely used in the clinic to treat gastritis, duodenal ulcer, and digestive tract ulcer [5–8]. Modern pharmacology indicates that the pharmacological effects of C. chinensis are mainly due to the benzylisoquinoline alkaloids (BIAs) in its rhizome. BIAs in C. chinensis, such as berberine, coptisine, palmatine, epiberberine, columbamine and jatrorrhizine, have antibacterial, antioxidant, antihyperglycemic, anti-inflammatory, and immunoregulatory effects [9–11]. Due to the low BIAs content together with its slow accumulation in C. chinensis rhizome, the traditional preparation of BIAs by chemical extraction is insufficient to meet the huge market demand. Our current HPLC, Principal component analysis (PCA) and clustering results showed that BIAs were highly enriched in the rhizome tissue of C. chinensis, prompting significant interest in identifying genes related to BIAs biosynthesis through spatial transcriptomic data between rhizome and other tissues.

The biosynthesis pathways of BIAs have been reported by He et al. [12] who summarized the biosynthetic pathway into upstream and downstream stages. In the upstream stage, BIAs biosynthesis begins with the conversion of L-tyrosine to produce the central pathway intermediate (S)-reticuline via a series of hydroxylation, O- and N-methylation reactions. Next, various end-products are then yielded by multistep transformations catalyzed by O-methyltransferases [13] and CYP450s [14] (Fig. S1). In addition to these biosynthetic enzymes, transcription factors and transporters also contribute to the BIAs biosynthesis [15].

APETALA2/ethylene responsive factor (AP2/ERF) family is a large family of plant-specific transcription factors [16, 17]. These AP2/ERF transcription factors are characterized by a conserved AP2/ERF domain, which is composed of 60–70 amino acid residues associated with DNA binding. They are primarily divided into five subfamilies: AP2 (APETALA2), DREB (dehydration-responsive element-binding protein), ERF (ethylene-responsive element-binding protein), RAV (related to ABI3/VP), and Soloists (a few unclassified factors) [18]. AP2 subfamily members typically contain 2 AP2/ERF domains, while members in other subfamilies contain only one AP2/ERF domain. Additionally, RAV subfamily members process a B3 domain. AP2/ERF genes encode proteins involved in various biological and physiological processes [19–21]. Specifically, AP2/ERFs from Oryza sativa (O. sativa) played crucial roles in growth and development, and also in enhancing rice resistance to various biotic stress [22]. AP2/ERFs from Zea mays (Z. mays) and Arabidopsis thaliana (A. thaliana) were involved in plant growth, development, and response to adverse stress [23, 24]. Changes in SNPs in lotus AP2/ERFs can affect the size and sugar content of Nelumbo nucifera (N. nucifera) rhizome [25]. Notably, the transactivation activities of poppy AP2/ERFs on BIAs biosynthetic genes, 6OMT and CYP719A5, have been confirmed [26]. Thus, it was reasonable to hypothesize that AP2/ERFs from C. chinensis might have the ability to bind the cis-elements in the promoters of biosynthetic genes, regulating the biosynthesis of BIAs in the rhizome of C. chinensis. However, the genetic background of AP2/ERFs in C. chinensis and their roles in the biosynthesis of BIAs remain scarce.

Currently, the genomic identification of gene family became feasible due to the high-quality genome of C. chinensis [27]. In this study, a comprehensive investigation of the AP2/ERF gene family, including physicochemical properties, phylogenetic relationships, conserved domains, gene structures, conserved motifs, and cis-acting elements as well, was performed using the C. chinensis genome (accession number of PRJNA662860). Subsequently, the candidate AP2/ERFs involved in the BIAs biosynthesis were selected via the correlation analysis between the transcriptional levels of AP2/ERF genes and the content of BIAs in various tissues. Finally, the key candidate AP2/ERFs and the corresponding regulatory network were identified through a protein-protein interaction network. Therefore, the goal of this study was to identify the potential key AP2/ERFs involved in the tissue-specific accumulation of BIAs in C. chinensis. Overall, this work provides not only molecular insights into the potential regulatory roles of AP2/ERFs in BIAs biosynthesis but the candidate AP2/ERFs for the genetic transformation of C. chinensis.

Results

Identification and characterization of CcAP2/ERFs in Coptis Chinensis Franch

A total of nonredundant 96 AP2/ERFs (CcAP2/ERF1 to CcAP2/ERF96) were identified from C. chinensis using Blast, CDD and SMART methods. Physicochemical analysis showed significant differences among the 96 CcAP2/ERFs, with protein sizes ranging from 73 (CcAP2/ERF2) to 698 (CcAP2/ERF40) residues (average length 290.8), molecular weights ranging from 8.43 (CcAP2/ERF2) to 77.04 (CcAP2/ERF40) kDa (average weight 32.53 kDa), and theoretical isoelectric points ranging from 4.59 (CcAP2/ERF26) to 10.95 (CcAP2/ERF68 and CcAP2/ERF69) (Table S1). Subcellular localization prediction revealed that 66.7% of CcAP2/ERFs were expressed in the nucleus, while 29.2% and 4.2% of which were localized in cytoplasm or nucleus, and cytoplasm, respectively (Table S1). Although the prediction may not be entirely consistent with the actual subcellular localization of AP2/ERFs, it still suggests their function as regulatory agents in transcription initiation.

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of CcAP2/ERFs

The phylogenetic analysis of 96 CcAP2/ERFs and 147 AtAP2/ERFs was performed using the NJ method (Fig. 1). Similar to the evolutionary classification of AP2/ERFs in Arabidopsis, the CcAP2/ERF family was also clustered into five subfamilies (Table S2). As CcAP2/ERF47 (Cch00015115) did not cluster with other AP2/ERFs, it was classified into the subfamily Soloist. In C. chinensis, the ERF subfamily was the largest subfamily (43 members), followed by DREB (32 members), AP2 (17 members), and RAV (3 members) subfamilies. To date, AP2/ERF gene families have been identified on a genome-wide scale from several plants, such as Arabidopsis (147 members), California poppy (134 members) [26], Liriodendron chinense (104 members) [28], Melilotus albus (100 members) [29], and Zea mays as well (76 members) [30]. Similar to C. chinensis, the ERF subfamily was the largest subfamily among these aforementioned species (Table S2).

Motif composition, conserved domains and gene structure analysis of CcAP2/ERFs

Using the online MEME suite, ten conserved motifs in CcAP2/ERFs were predicted (Table S3), with four motifs (motifs 1, 2, 3, and 5) identified as the core conserved motifs in CcAP2/ERFs (Fig. 2B). These aforementioned four motifs were essential for the conserved AP2/ERF domain. Furthermore, the number and distribution of motifs varied among different subfamilies, potentially leading to divergent biological functions. For instance, motifs 4 and 6 were specific to the AP2 subfamily, while the A-1 group in the DREB subfamily contained motifs 7, 8 and 10, and the A-4 group in the DREB subfamily contained motifs 7 and 8. Additionally, three members (CcAP2/ERF36 (Cch00011958), CcAP2/ERF37 (Cch00011959), and CcAP2/ERF38 (Cch00011961)) in the ERF subfamily (B-3 group) contained motif 9, suggesting that these CcAP2/ERF genes might have distinct biological functions from members in other subfamilies or groups. Overall, the motif composition of AP2/ERFs within the same subfamily was similar, indicating that these proteins might have similar functions and supporting the results of phylogenetic analysis.

Although CcAP2/ERFs contained conserved AP2 domain, they also exhibited various conserved domain architectures among different subfamilies (Table S4, Fig S2). For instance, besides the AP2 domain, the RAV subfamily also possessed a B3 domain, while the AP2 subfamily contained an additional conserved AP2 domain. Intriguingly, although CcAP2/ERF53 (Cch00017997) contained one AP2 domain and one B3 domain, it was still clustered into the AP2 subfamily possibly due to its AP2 subfamily-specific motif 4. In addition, CcAP2/ERF53 (Cch00016164) carried 2 F-box domains that are crucial for plant growth, development, and stress responses [31].

To gain insights into the gene structures of CcAP2/ERFs, we identified the intron-exon arrangements of 96 CcAP2/ERF genes (Fig. 2C), of which 14 genes contained untranslated regions (UTRs). Furthermore, 39 of the 96 CcAP2/ERF genes (40.6%) contained introns ranging from 1 to 10, with CcAP2/ERF73 (Cch00026821) and CcAP2/ERF67 (Cch00024899) carrying the maximum of 10 introns. Intriguingly, we found that genes in the AP2 subfamily contained 6 or more exons compared to genes in other subfamilies, which might influence the transcription efficiency and protein concentration in cells. However, the gene structure within the same subfamily was generally conserved, which was consistent with the results derived from the motif and domain analyses in Fig. 2.

Chromosomal localization and gene duplication analysis

Replication events in plant genomes contribute to the gene expansion and primarily consist of tandem and segmental duplication [32]. Tandem duplicated genes are closely arranged on the same chromosome, forming hot zones of gene clusters with similar sequences and functions, while segmental duplication results from chromosomal rearrangements, causing replicated genes to be localized on different chromosomes [33]. To investigate the tandem duplication, the chromosomal localization of CcAP2/ERF genes was examined, revealing that 96 CcAP2/ERF genes were unevenly mapped to nine chromosomes of C. chinensis (Fig. 3). Chromosomes 2 and 8 harbored the largest number of genes (14 each), followed by chromosomes 3 and 7 (13 each), while chromosome 9 had the smallest number of genes (5 genes). Furthermore, tandem duplication was observed among 7 pairs of CcAP2/ERF genes, primarily in the DREB, ERF and AP2 subfamilies. Notably, 7 genes (CcAP2/ERF10 (Cch00003553) to CcAP2/ERF14(Cch00003559)) in the DREB subfamily formed a tight cluster on chromosome 8. MCScanX collinearity analysis provided insight into segmental duplication events, revealing 10 pairs of duplicated genes located on different chromosomes (Fig. 3). Based on chromosomal localization and intraspecies collinearity analyses, CcAP2/ERF genes showing tandem duplication or segmental duplication were clustered in the same subfamily, indicating that both types of duplication were the evolutionary driving forces for the expansion of CcAP2/ERF gene family.

Synteny and evolutionary selection pressure analysis of CcAP2/ERF genes in C. chinensis and genes from other species

The interspecies synteny analysis between CcAP2/ERF genes and those from other species, including two monocots (Z. mays and O. sativa) and three dicots (A. thaliana, Papaver somniferum (P. somniferum), and N. nucifera), was conducted (Fig. 4, Table S6). This analysis revealed a whole-genome duplication (WGD) event in C. chinensis, with the syntenic genes in C. chinensis corresponding to 4, 24, 30, 74 and 90 copies in Z. mays, O. sativa, A. thaliana, N. nucifera, and P. somniferum, respectively. Compared with other four species, the closest relationship between C. chinensis and P. somniferum was confirmed based on the maximum number of syntenic genes, consistent with the hypothesis that the species with close relationship always contain similar chemical compounds and genetic backgrounds [34]. It was highlighted that N. nucifera and P. somniferum are also enriched in BIAs, and hence the syntenic genes among these three species might provide candidate genes involved in the BIAs biosynthesis. More importantly, since AP2/ERFs from P. somniferum have been previously reported to regulate the biosynthesis of BIAs, their homologous copies in C. chinensis potentially resembled similar functions. Additionally, CcAP2/ERF36 (Cch00011958) and CcAP2/ERF23 (Cch00006533) underwent genomic synteny among six species, implying the conserved function of both genes (Fig. S3). In addition to tandem and segmental duplication, WGD was identified as a third event contributing to the expansion of the AP2/ERF gene family and improving its biological functions.

The ratio of nonsynonymous (Ka) to synonymous substitutions (Ks) was investigated to understand how selection pressure influences protein-coding genes (Table S7). It is worth noting that synonymous mutations are generally considered to be neutral, while nonsynonymous mutations are subject to natural selection. Those genes undergoing positive selection, neutral evolution or purifying selection exhibit Ka/Ks values > > 1, = 1 or < < 1, respectively. In this study, the Ka/Ks ratios of C. chinensis, A. thaliana, N. nucifera and P. somniferum were far less than 1 (Fig. 4B), suggesting that AP2/ERF genes in these plants were under strong purifying selection.

The cis-regulatory element analysis in the promoters of CcAP2/ERFs

The cis-elements in promoters (CREs) are crucial for regulating gene expression patterns and functions [35]. Then the upstream 2000 bp sequences of all 96 CcAP2/ERF genes were analyzed to identify the CREs. A total of 2498 CREs were detected in the promoters of CcAP2/ERF genes and classified into three groups: growth/development-related, hormone-responsive, and stress-responsive elements (Table S8) [36]. Among these groups, growth/development-related elements formed the largest group (1243 elements), with three light-responsive elements, G-box, Box-4, and GT1-motif, contributing 315 (31.9%), 168 (17.0%), and 96 (9.7%) elements, respectively. In addition, 59 meristem-specific elements (CAT-box) were observed, which are involved in the development of root apical meristem [37]. Considering that the rhizome and the root of C. chinensis might originate from multiple non-root apical meristems, the CAT-box in C. chinensis might play an important role in its development and growth. Secondly, hormone-responsive elements formed the second largest group (729 elements), with abscisic acid-responsive element (ABRE) and MeJA-responsive elements (CGTCA-motif and TGACG-motif) contributing 267 (36.6%) and 254 (34.8%) elements, respectively (Fig. 5). ABA is known to be responsible for plant growth and development, stress response and hormone regulation as well [35]. Previously, MeJA has been reported to be a key inducer of BIA biosynthesis [26]. We found that 73 CcAP2/ERF genes harbored CREs responsive to MeJA, with CcAP2/ERF54 (Cch00018344) and CcAP2/ERF66 (Cch00023885) having the highest number of MeJA-responsive elements (5 each). The smallest group included stress-responsive elements (526 elements), with anaerobic-responsive elements (256, 48.6%) forming the largest subgroup. Moreover, a total of 84 MBS, 67 L and 46 TC-rich repeats were identified as drought-, cold-, and defense-responsive elements, respectively.

Differential accumulation of BIAs in various tissue of C. Chinensis

In accordance with the Chinese pharmacopoeia 2020 edition, BIAs such as berberine, coptisine, epiberberine, and palmatine are bioactive compounds in C. chinensis. In addition to these BIAs, columbamine and jatrorrhizine were also detected in this study. HPLC results indicated that these six types of BIAs were primarily distributed in the rhizomes, while other tissues (leaf, petiole and root) contained relatively low contents. Specifically, berberine in the rhizome was specifically 8.87, 5.61, and 4.10-fold higher than that in root, petiole and leaf tissues, respectively, whereas the content of coptisine in the rhizome was 39.32, 4.17 and 2.87 times more abundant than it was in the leaf, petiole and root, respectively. Furthermore, the remaining four types of BIAs were also more abundant in the rhizome compared to the other three tissues (Fig. 6B). According to the spatial distribution of BIAs, a PCA plot was generated in terms of tissues. As depicted in Fig. 6C, the PC1 axis accounted for 95% of the components and a total of 12 dots representing different tissues were clustered based on tissue type. The results strongly supported that the tissue-distribution of the six types of BIAs, providing a basis for the spatial enrichment analysis in C. chinensis. According to the criteria by Liao et al. [38], BIAs whose concentration in the rhizome was 1.5-fold higher (P-value < 0.05) than those in other tissues were designated as rhizome-enriched BIAs, all BIAs in this study were enriched in the rhizome (Fig. 6D). Overall, these results indicated a potential basis for correlation analysis based on the tissue-specific biosynthesis of BIAs.

Transcriptome-based and real-time PCR investigation on the expression patterns of CcAP2/ERFs in various tissues

A total of 16 RNA-sequencing datasets, comprising 4 biological replicates for each of four tissues, yielded an impressive 12.58 Gb of clean data with a Q30 ratio of at least 98.34%. These clean data were assembled into 41,925 long transcripts (> 1000 bp) with 76.31% sequence coverage of the C. chinensis genome. By comparing the transcriptomic data of the rhizome with other tissues containing low BIAs levels, we identified the expressional profiles of 96 CcAP2/ERF genes, highlighting their distinct roles in tissue formation and development, stress response, and BIAs biosynthesis as well (Table S9). Among the 96 CcAP2/ERF genes, CcAP2/ERF2 (Cch00001417) was defined as a pseudogene since it exhibited FPKM value of 0 in all four tissues. CcAP2/ERF82 (Cch00032626) was expressed only in the rhizomes, while CcAP2/ERF25 (Cch00007017) was detected only in the roots, implying their divergent roles in the tissue-specific growth and metabolism. CcAP2/ERF5 (Cch00002986), CcAP2/ERF18 (Cch00005719), and CcAP2/ERF43 (Cch00014684) showed constantly high expression (FPKM > 500) across all four tissues, indicating their conserved functions in C. chinensis (Fig. 7).

In accordance with the differential expression analysis, 4, 6 and 2 CcAP2/ERF genes in leaf, petiole and root were upregulated compared to the rhizome, while 20, 19 and 6 genes in these tissues were downregulated, respectively (Table S10). Intriguingly, the differences in the number of CcAP2/ERF genes in the underground tissues (rhizome and root) (24 and 28) were much larger than that in the aerial tissues (7 in petiole) in respect to leaves. This differentiation might be due to the divergent responses of subterranean and aerial tissues to environmental stimuli. The rhizome and root grow in constantly dark conditions, while the leaf and petiole grow in photoperiod cycles, leading to the induction of light-responsive AP2/ERF genes and resulting the tissue-differentiated growth and metabolism [28].

CcAP2/ERFs related to BIAs biosynthesis

Transcription factors (TFs) play crucial roles in the temporal and spatial regulation of target genes via specific binding to the cis-elements in their promoters. Consequently, they are considered as master regulators of the biosynthesis of downstream products. We then calculated the R-values between the expression patterns of CcAP2/ERF genes and the contents of BIAs in various tissues. Totally, 20 CcAP2/ERF genes showed correlation with BIAs, 17 of which showed a positive correlation with at least one type of BIAs, while 3 exhibited a negative correlation with at least one type of BIAs (Table S11). The genes encoding 17 CcAP2/ERFs showed high correlations (R-value > 0.9, P < 0.05) with BIAs. Specifically, 6, 14, 7, 11, 11 and 7 genes exhibited positive correlations with berberine, columbamine, coptisine, epiberberine, jatrorrhizine, and palmatine, respectively. Notably, CcAP2/ERF26 (Cch00007092), CcAP2/ERF30 (Cch00009226), and CcAP2/ERF88 (Cch00033669) positively correlated with up to five types of BIAs, whereas CcAP2/ERF92 (Cch00036859) correlated only with coptisine, reflecting its specific role in the regulation of coptisine biosynthesis. Given that bioactive BIAs showed a positive correlation with 17 CcAP2/ERF genes but a negative correlation with only 3 CcAP2/ERF genes, it could be inferred that the CcAP2/ERFs generally played a positive role in BIAs biosynthesis in C. chinensis (Table S11). Real-time PCR results verified that, except for CcAP2/ERF10 (Cch00003553), CcAP2/ERF17 (Cch00004368), CcAP2/ERF26 (Cch00007092) and CcAP2/ERF67 (Cch00024899), the other 9 CcAP2/ERF genes related to the BIAs biosynthesis had the highest mRNA levels in the rhizome, which coincided with the differential expression analysis based on transcriptomic sequencing (Fig. 8).

Protein-protein interactome network analysis

In the genome of C. chinensis, 68 enzymatic genes involved in the BIAs biosynthesis were identified. Among these, 7 genes possessed core a GCC-box responsible for the AP2/ERF binding, while 22 genes harbored GCC-box-like elements (GCAGCC/GCCACC). In addition, MEME FIMO analysis indicated that another 44 genes might be tightly regulated by AP2/ERFs, presumably highlighting their potential regulatory role in BIAs biosynthesis (Table S12). Additionally, members of ABCB family have been confirmed to function in the subcellular trafficking of BIAs intermediates in plants [39]. The promoters of ABCB subfamily transporters were evaluated and hence 69 CcABCBs were proposed to be regulated by CcAP2/ERFs (Table S12). Similarly, the transcriptional regulation of WRKY and bHLH families on BIAs biosynthesis has been highlighted by Huang et al. [40] and Liu et al. [41], identifying 45 CcWRKYs and 66 CcbHLHs as potential regulatory targets of CcAP2/ERFs (Table S12).

Subsequently, the protein-protein interaction network using the aforementioned 9 CcAP2/ERFs, 68 enzymes related to BIAs biosynthesis, 69 CcABCBs, 45 CcWRKYs and 66 CcbHLHs was predicted via the STRING database (Fig. 9A). The interactome network analysis revealed that 120 nodes (genes) interacting with 2046 neighbors with average number of 17.05. The interactome was reliable, as indicated by an average local clustering coefficient of 0.578 and a PPI enrichment P-value < 1.0E-16. Furthermore, BIAs metabolism might involve the formation of a multienzyme complex due to the close interaction network among the enzymes related to BIAs biosynthesis (Fig. 9B). Among the enzymes related to BIAs biosynthesis, CcCAS (Cch00017821), CcCTS (Cch00030902), CcCoOMT (Cch00004695), CcNMCH (Cch00030905), and Cc4’OMT (Cch00016647) were the top five enzymes potentially acting as central catalysts in the BIAs biosynthesis, interacting with 20, 8, 7, 7 and 6 candidate proteins, respectively (Table S13). Within the ABCB family, CcABCB26 and CcABCB27 were the top two members interacting with 18 candidate proteins, including ABCBs, TFs, and enzymes related to BIAs biosynthesis, supporting their roles in the trafficking of BIAs-group metabolites (Fig. 9C). Among the WRKY and bHLH families, WRKY40 and bHLH93 interacted with the top candidate proteins of 45 and 31, respectively (Table S13, Fig. 9D and E).

More importantly, DREB1B (Cch00003559), ERF012 (Cch00006818), and AP2 type ERF (Cch00036859) interacted with Cc4’OMT, CcCTS and CcCAS, respectively. Furthermore, DREB1B, ERF012 and ERF003 (Cch00033669) were the top three AP2/ERFs, interacting with 10, 8 and 6 candidate proteins, respectively. Intriguingly, these enzymes were among the above-mentioned top five candidate enzymes, and hence were hopeful regulators on the BIAs biosynthesis in C. chinensis. A previous study supported that overexpression of Cc4’OMT led to enhanced accumulation of BIAs in C. japonica [42], highlighting its central role in the metabolic engineering of BIAs biosynthesis in C. chinensis. Both DREB1B and Cc4’OMT genes revealed the highest expression levels in the rhizome with a positive correlation (P < 0.01) based on spatial transcriptomic data, by which co-expression tendency between the two genes was observed. Consequently, DREB1B was regarded as a central regulator involved in the rhizome-specific enrichment of BIAs in C. chinensis due to the following reasons: (1) DREB1B gene showed a high positive correlation with BIAs contents; (2) DREB1B was the top one CcAP2/ERF interacting with 10 candidate proteins; (3) Cc4’OMT was positively associated with the accumulation of BIAs biosynthesis. Future researches should focus on verifying the physiological role of DREB1B and its interactive proteins.

Discussion

The tissue-specific accumulation of bioactive ingredients has been widely observed in medicinal plants, such as Fritillaria unibracteata and Ligusticum Chuanxiong [38, 43]. In this study, the rhizome-specific accumulation of BIAs was also observed in C. chinensis through HPLC, PCA together with clustering analyses (Fig. 6), establishing it as a model plant for the study of BIAs biosynthesis. The tissue-specific biosynthesis is associated with complex genetic procedures, including environmental response, transcriptional regulation, as well as enzymatic conversion. During the biosynthetic procedures, TFs play central role in linking upstream environmental factors to downstream functional enzymes. AP2/ERFs, acting as regulators in plant growth and development, stress response, as well as bioactive ingredients biosynthesis, have been identified at the genomic level in multiple plants including higher model plants, moss, and even lower algae [44, 45]. In this study, 96 CcAP2/ERFs clustering into five subfamilies were identified, exhibiting similar numbers and subfamily classification pattern to other verified plants (Fig. 1). The total number of AP2/ERFs in higher plants exceeded that in primitive algae, primarily due to the expansion of AP2/ERFs in higher plants [44]. However, the subfamilies AP2 and ERF were also found in primitive algae, suggesting their functional conservation, even though the specific mechanism has not been reported. Tandem duplication, segmental duplication as well as WGD event were crucial factors influencing the gene expansion and enrichment of the functional diversity during evolution, aligning with the occurrence of ancient WGD within the C. chinensis genome.

Although the direct mechanism of CcAP2/ERFs in regulating the BIAs biosynthesis have not been identified, clues from other medicinal plants suggested that AP2/ERFs played a regulatory role. AP2/ERFs have been shown to positively regulate biosynthesis of various types of alkaloids, such as indole-, iridoid-, and steroidal alkaloids [46–50]. In this study, 28 differentially expressed CcAP2/ERF genes exhibited a spatial distribution similar to that observed in Caucasian clover [51], with the highest or relatively higher expression levels in the rhizome. Meanwhile, AP2/ERF genes have been identified to influence the development, stress response and internal ingredients of the rhizomes [25, 45]. Therefore, the spatial pattern of CcAP2/ERF genes provided valuable genetic profiles and resources for studying tissue-specific development, stress response and metabolite biosynthesis. More importantly, 17 out of the 28 CcAP2/ERF genes exhibited a high correlation (R-value > 0.9, P < 0.05) with the accumulation of BIAs in various tissues, suggesting their positive roles in BIAs biosynthesis. Further real-time PCR verified that 9 of these 17 CcAP2/ERF genes exhibited uniformly high expression in the rhizome, consistent with the differential expression analysis from transcriptome data.

Combining our genomic-wide with BIAs-enriched data, we proposed an AP2/ERF-related regulatory network in C. chinensis that included three main stages. First, numerous cis-elements in the promoters of CcAP2/ERF genes were identified as potentially involved in response to environmental stress (526 elements) and plant hormones (729 elements). This suggested that the differential expression pattern of CcAP2/ERF genes might result from suitable growth environment, climate, plant hormones and environmental factors [45, 48]. Meanwhile, the 1243 growth/development-related elements in the promoters provided candidate genes for revealing the molecular mechanism of rhizome formation. In the second stage, 28 CcAP2/ERF genes showing transcriptional upregulation in the rhizome likely played crucial roles in rhizome formation, stress response and hormone response. In addition, environmental stress has significant impacts on rhizome, whose formation and development are of significance [52]. Previous studies on Phyllostachys edulis [53] and ginger [45] have indicated that several TFs from rhizome mediated response to biotic and abiotic stress. Intriguingly, tissue-based correlation of CcAP2/ERF genes expression and BIAs content supported physiological functions of 17 CcAP2/ERFs in BIAs biosynthesis. In accordance with the further real-time PCR verification, 9 CcAP2/ERFs were put forward as candidate central regulators. In the final stage, we carried out PPI analysis, by which the interaction among CcAP2/ERFs, biosynthetic enzymes, WRKYs, bHLHs and ABCB transporters was identified. As a result, DREB1B emerged as a central regulator of BIAs biosynthesis. Other proteins that interacted with DREB1B and Cc4’OMT might play roles in the subtle regulation of BIAs biosynthesis by forming protein complex. Accordingly, the gene regulatory network involved in the CcDREB1B and Cc4’OMT was put forward, which served as a guide for future research. Considering that both DREB1B and Cc4’OMT genes were greatly enriched in rhizome, where the BIAs accumulated, both favorable associations might provide targets for the metabolic engineering of BIAs (Fig. 10).

Conclusion

In this study, a total of 96 AP2/ERF genes were identified in the C. chinensis genome, including 43 in the ERF subfamily, 32 in the DREB subfamily, 17 in the AP2 subfamily, 3 in RAV family, and 1 in the Soloist subfamily. Their phytochemical characteristics, structural features, evolutionary relationships, chromosomal localization, and duplication events were analyzed. In addition, the expression profile and correlation analysis of AP2/ERF genes showed that some of the genes positively correlated with the tissue-accumulation of BIAs. DREB1B might be a central regulator involved in a highly complex BIAs biosynthesis network. These findings provide significant insight into the function of AP2/ERF genes in C. chinensis, and candidate genes for metabolic engineering to increase BIAs content in C. chinensis.

Materials and methods

Materials

The 5-year-old C. chinensis samples were collected from Huangshui Town, Shizhu County, Chongqing City, China (30°14’24.295 N, 108°23’42.644E). The C. chinensis plants were cultivated by Chongqing Wanglong Huanglian Technology Co. Ltd and our collection was approved by the company. The collected C. chinensis plants were identified by professor Fengming Ren, and stored in the laboratory of the School of Life Science and Engineering in Southwest Jiaotong University. The samples used for transcriptome sequencing were immediately frozen in liquid N2 and stored subsequently at -80 °C. The samples used for phytochemical analysis were cultivated in a greenhouse at 22 °C with a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod.

Identification and characterization of the CcAP2/ERF gene family

A hidden Markov model (HMM) file of the conserved AP2/ERF domain (PF00847) was downloaded from HMM3.0 (http://hmmer.org/) and used as a query to blast against the genome of C. chinensis, which was available in the NCBI BioProject database under accession number of PRJNA662860. The identified AP2/ERF candidate sequences were further confirmed by use of the CDD tool in the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi/) and the SMART database (https://smart.embl.de/).The physicochemical properties of confirmed CcAP2/ERFs, including aliphatic index, grand average of hydrophobicity, molecular weight, protein size, and theoretical isoelectric point were predicted on the Expasy website (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). Additionally, subcellular localization was predicted using Plant-mPLoc (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/plant-multi/) [54].

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of CcAP2/ERF genes

Multiple sequence alignment of AP2/ERF proteins from Arabidopsis thaliana and C. chinensis was performed using Clustal W. The alignment data was subsequently imported into MEGA11.0 software (https://www.megasoftware.net/). The AP2/ERF tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method with Jones-Thornton-Taylor (JTT) model and 1000 bootstrap replicates [55]. The resultant tree was further visualized using Evolview (https://www.evolgenius.info/evolview/) [56]. Based on the conserved domain characteristics, all the identified CcAP2/ERFs were categorized into different subfamilies.

Motif composition, conserved domains, gene structure, and cis-acting elements analysis

In accordance with the online MEME Suite Version 5.5.5 (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme), conserved motifs in CcAP2/ERFs were identified with default setting, allowing for a maximum of 10 motifs. Conserved domains in CcAP2/ERFs were obtained using the NCBI Batch CDD Search database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi/) and visualized using TBtools. The GFF file of the C. chinensis genome was downloaded from the NCBI database, and gene structure information of CcAP2/ERFs was extracted and visualized using TBtools. Finally, the cis-acting elements were extracted from the upstream 2000 bp region prior to the start ATG codon using the PlantCARE database (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [57].

Chromosome localization, collinearity and Ka/Ks analyses

All genome-related files were obtained, including those of two monocots and four dicots: O. sativa (http://www.ricesuperpir.com/web/nip); Z. mays (https://data.cyverse.org/dav-anon/iplant/home/laijs/Zm-Mo17-REFERENCE-CAU-2.0/); A. thaliana (http://col-pek.arashare.cn/); C. chinensis (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCA_015680905.1/); N. nucifera (http://nelumbo.cngb.org/nelumbo/); and P. somniferum (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_003573695.1/). Then, TBtools was used to analyze the chromosomal location information of CcAP2/ERF genes from the GFF file of the C. chinensis genome. MCScanX and TBtools were employed to identify tandem duplicated genes of CcAP2/ERFs, and the inter-species collinearity of CcAP2/ERFs between C. chinensis and other plants was analyzed with an E-value of 10− 3 [58]. The non-synonymous substitution (Ka) to synonymous substitution (Ks) [Ka/Ks] ratios of duplicated CcAP2/ERF gene pairs were calculated with TBtools [45]. The Ka/Ks plot was drawn using the R package ggplot2 and reshape2.

HPLC analysis on BIAs in various tissues

0.1 g of dried samples from four tissues of C. chinensis (leaf, petiole, rhizome and root) were ground into powder and extracted with 25 mL of methanol via an ultrasonic-assisted method. The ultrasonic-assisted extraction was performed with a frequency of 40 Hz and 250 W power for 30 min. The resulting suspension was filtrated through a 0.22 μm membrane and analyzed using an Agilent 1260 HPLC system. The elution was conducted on an Agilent Extend C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) with a mobile phase of acetonitrile-water containing 0.3% phosphoric acid and 0.2% triethylamine (70:30, v/v). The flow rate of the mobile phase was set at 1 mL min-1, and 5 µL of the sample was used for analysis. The column temperature was maintained at 20 °C, and the detection wavelength was set at 345 nm. The standard curve for six types of reference standards (berberine, columbamine, coptisine, epiberberine, jatrorrhizine, and palmatine) was generated using the same elution conditions.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) based on dimension reduction was utilized to assess the trends shaping BIAs distribution [59]. Specifically, PCA was performed with R software (Version 4.3.3) to explore the spatial accumulation pattern of six types of BIAs across different tissues. The contents of the six types of BIAs in various tissues were organized into a six-dimensional vector corresponding to the six types of BIAs, and then they were transformed into principal components. Next, the first two axes, accounting for most of the component influencing the BIAs distribution, were calculated. The R package built-in scale function and Factoextra were utilized for data normalization and visualization of the PCA plot, respectively.

Transcriptomics analysis and real-time PCR validation

Following the methods reported by Qin et al. [60], four tissues (leaf, petiole, rhizome and root) collected from 5-year-old C. chinensis (4 biological replicates) were utilized for RNA extraction with TRIzol® Reagent (Invitrogen). After removing the genomic DNA by DNase I (TaKara), the qualified RNA was used for library construction. Sequencing of the libraries was carried out on Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform (Shanghai BIOZERON Co., Ltd).

Correlation analysis and co-expression network construction

A co-expression network was constructed using SPSS version 27.0 based on the correlation coefficient analysis between the spatial distribution of CcAP2/ERF genes and BIAs contents in four tissues. Finally, it was visualized using Cytoscape (v3.9.1) [61].

Target gene-transcription factor regulatory network construction

The motif file of CcAP2/ERF was downloaded from the Jaspar database (https://jaspar.genereg.net) and analyzed alongside the promoter sequences of target genes using MEME FIMO (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/fimo). The resulting files were visualized using Cytoscape (v3.9.1).
