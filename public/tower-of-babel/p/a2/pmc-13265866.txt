Immediate and long-term effects of post-hatch feed deprivation on performance, intestinal morphology and nutrient metabolism in on-farm hatched broilers
Authors: Niamh Cleiren, Rutger Smets, Geert PJ Janssens, Luc Duchateau, Marta Lourenço, Evelyne Delezie, Gunther Antonissen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a consequence of the inevitable hatch window and the processing and transporting steps in conventional hatchery practices, broiler chicks may spend up to 20 - 60 h without access to feed and water post-hatch. To assess the immediate and long-term effects of post-hatch feed deprivation, 960 Ross308 broilers were allocated to a randomized complete block design with time of hatch during the hatch window as the blocking factor. Within each block, chicks were randomly assigned to one of three treatments: immediate feed access post-hatch (T0), feed access at 24 h (T1), or 48 h biological age (BA) (T2). Performance parameters were measured at the end of each feeding phase, while relative organ weights, duodenal morphological parameters and fasted nutrient metabolite concentrations were assessed as repeated measures at 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, 96 h, days 7 and 10 BA, and at days 14, 28 and 42. Between hatch and day 11, T2 chicks had a higher average daily feed intake compared with T0 and had the highest feed conversion ratio (FCR). Overall, between hatch and day 42, T2 chicks had the least efficient FCR and tended to have the lowest body weight at the end of each feeding phase. Significant effects of post-hatch feed deprivation on the physiological measurements were limited to the relative bursa (RBW) and liver (RLW) weights and were limited to the first two weeks post-hatch, with T2 chicks having a lower RBW compared with T1 between hatch and day 11. Concurrently T1 chicks had a lower RLW compared with T0, which reversed by day 14 as T1 had higher RLW than T0. No effects of post-hatch feed deprivation were present on duodenal morphology and fasted nutrient metabolites, however the effect of sex was present in several of these and relative organ weight parameters. Due to the frequent sampling schedule, insights were gained into the development of post-hatch nutrient metabolism, which may help to further optimize nutritional composition of starter feeds for on-farm hatched broilers.Keywords: Broiler, Early feeding, Duodenal morphology, Lipid metabolism, Performance, Yolk sac utilization

Introduction

In current western European hatchery practices, it may take up to 20 – 60 h post-hatch before newly hatched chicks gain their first access to feed and water. This substantial feed and water deprivation period post-hatch is partly due to the hatch window, the time span between the first and the last chick hatching within the same batch. This inevitable hatch window varies between 24 and 48 h (Willemsen et al., 2010) and is influenced by incubation factors such as implemented temperature schemes (Wijnen et al., 2020) and CO2 levels during late incubation (Tong et al., 2015), as well as by egg weights (Vieira et al., 2005; Yameen et al., 2021).

During the time between hatch and the first access to feed and water, chicks rely solely on their yolk sac and body reserves for survival. Efficient utilization of the lipid contents of the residual yolk, by manner of complete oxidation via the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, ideally requires the presence of a carbohydrate substrate as oxaloacetate derived from glucose is essential for incorporating fatty acids into the TCA cycle. Making up less than 1% of the residual yolk (Ravindran and Abdollahi, 2021), carbohydrates are endogenously derived from liver and muscle glycogen in the absence of a dietary source. Prolonged feed deprivation post-hatch has been shown to rapidly deplete these glycogen reserves within 24 to 48 h (Kornasio et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2020), subsequently necessitating the use of muscle proteins as an energy-inefficient glucogenic source (Noy and Uni, 2010; Kornasio et al., 2011), thereby compromising early muscle development. The disadvantageous consequences of this on later broiler performance have been broadly described (Uni and Ferket, 2004; Bhanja et al., 2009; Cardeal et al., 2020). In order to reduce the post-hatch feed and water deprivation period, alternative hatching systems such as on-farm hatching with provision of feed and water have been developed (van den Brand et al., 2023).

The importance of the optimal support of newly-hatched chicks with residual impact on performance and health parameters at a later age has also been highlighted in the review by Jha et al. (2019) on early nutritional strategies on poultry gut health. It is crucial to underline that during the first days post-hatch, the gastrointestinal tract of broiler chicks will experience a higher growth rate relative to body weight compared with any other organs (Uni and Ferket, 2004; Ravindran and Abdollahi, 2021), largely attributed to the expansion of the small intestine which will undergo rapid morphological developments (Sklan, 2001). By providing the post-hatch chick with the optimal support to sustain these rapid developments, the functional intestinal tract in turn can provide the chick with the necessary nutrients for further growth. Various studies have investigated the impact of post-hatch feed and water deprivation on broiler chicks, and an extensive review and meta-analysis have been carried out by de Jong et al. (2017). Several studies describe the effects of early feed and water deprivation on small intestinal morphological parameters (i.e. villus height, crypt depth, villus to crypt ratio), mainly focusing on the first week post-hatch. However, based on the current literature, few studies have been conducted into the long-term effects of post-hatch feed and water deprivation on small intestinal morphology.

In order to provide newly-hatched chicks with the best possible nutrients during the critical transition from the lipid-rich yolk to the carbohydrate- and protein-based starter feeds, a deeper understanding of the post-hatch broiler nutrient metabolism is required. Impact on this metabolism can be assessed through plasma levels of triglyceride (TRIG), uric acid (UA) and non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA). In post-hatch chicks, TRIG can be a reflection of hepatic (re)synthesis or a reflection of fat absorption (March, 1984; Hernawan et al., 2012). Plasma UA concentrations may serve as a measure of protein catabolism of both a dietary source and the body’s own tissue (Okumura and Tasaki, 1969; Donsbough et al., 2010; Polese et al., 2025), while NEFA are a reflection of body fat mobilization (Serr et al., 2009; Sutherland et al., 2019). Information on these three parameters combined can provide insights on the inputs to the TCA cycle and energy metabolism of the animal.

This study set out to determine the impact of a 24 h or 48 h post-hatch feed deprivation on duodenal morphological parameters, plasma nutrient metabolite concentrations, performance parameters and relative organ weights during the first week post-hatch and in older broilers.

Materials and methods

The animal trial in this study was performed in compliance with the European guidelines for the care and use of animals in research (Directive 2010/63/EU) and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Flanders Research Institute for Agriculture, Fisheries and Food (ILVO) in Merelbeke-Melle, Belgium under authorization number 2022/429.

Incubation

A total of 1600 candled Ross308 eggs, originating from a 33-week old parent flock, were obtained from a commercial hatchery (Vervaeke-Belavi, Tielt, Belgium) at day 18 of incubation (432 h incubation time (IT)).

In preparation for the arrival of the eggs at ILVO, a hatching zone measuring 3.2 m by 3.68 m was created and provided with a 5 cm deep layer of wood shavings. Upon arrival of the eggs at ILVO, the stable temperature was 34 °C with a floor temperature ranging between 26 and 28 °C. Eggs were manually placed inside the hatching zone. By use of Ovoscans (NestBorn, HFHC nv, Hoegaarden, Belgium), the hatching process and stable climate were closely monitored based on egg-shell temperature. Hatchability was 95.9%.

Hatch window blocks

Out of the 1600 eggs, a total of 960 hatched chicks were included in the trial. Chicks that hatched outside the 482 h and 503 h incubation time time-frame were excluded from the study to limit the spread of hatch and thus biological age. Hatched chicks were divided into eight hatch window blocks (HWB), corresponding to time of hatch during the hatch window. Once 120 chicks had hatched, they were collected from the hatching zone and sexed prior to randomly assigning 40 chicks to each of the three treatment groups, ensuring similar sex distribution across all treatments within the HWB. The experiment therefore followed a randomized complete block design, with HWB as the blocking factor and treatments randomly assigned within each block.

Treatments

The three treatments applied in this trial consisted of immediate access to feed post-hatch (T0), access to feed at a biological age (BA) of 24 h (T1) and access to feed at 48 h BA (T2). An average BA of 0 h was determined for all chicks within a HWB, which was implemented for the feeding times of the different treatments and for sampling times during the first two weeks post-hatch. Water was available ad libitum for all treatments.

Housing and husbandry

HWB and treatment were randomly assigned to each of the 24 identical pens. Broilers were housed on a solid floor with an area of 2.1 m² covered in 3 kg/m² wood shavings. Light was constantly provided during hatch. The 23L1D cycle was implemented on day 1 when most of the HWBs had a biological age of 24 h and lasted until day 7. Between day 7 and the day before slaughter, day 41, a 18L6D cycle was used. The stable temperature was gradually decreased from 34 °C on day of arrival of the eggs to 32 °C on day 2 and then held until day 7. From day 7 onwards, temperature gradually decreased by 4 °C each week until 22 °C, which was then maintained until slaughter age. On day 2, all chicks were vaccinated against Newcastle disease (Avinew Neo, Boehringer Ingelheim, Ixelles, Belgium) by eyedrop method and subcutaneously vaccinated against Infectious Bursal Disease and Marek’s disease (0.2 ml Vaxxitek HVT + IBD, Boehringer Ingelheim, Ixelles, Belgium). Vaccination against Newcastle disease was repeated on day 16 through the drinking water (Avinew Neo, Boehringer Ingelheim, Ixelles, Belgium).

Feed

T0 chicks gained access to feed immediately after being placed in the pen. T1 chicks received feed at the average age of 24 h per HWB and T2 received feed at 48 h BA per HWB. All treatments received the same feed throughout the hatching period (d0 - d2; according to the dietary treatments applied) and the rearing period. The rearing period consisted of a starter (d0 - d11), a grower (d11 - d24) and a finisher (d24 - d42) phase. The feed given during the hatching period (d0 - d2) was identical to the feed given during the remainder of the starter phase (d2 - d11). During hatching and starter phase the feed consisted of a mash, while pellets were given in the grower and finisher phase. Feed was formulated to meet the nutritional requirements of the breed. The composition and nutritional values of the feed for the different rearing phases are given in Table 1. All diets included a non-starch-polysaccharide enzyme (100 mg/kg, Ronozyme Multigrain, DSM, Heerlen, the Netherlands), a phytase enzyme (200 ppm, Ronozyme HiPhos, DSM, Heerlen, Netherlands) and coccidiostat diclazuril (500 ppm, Coxiril, Huvepharma, Sofia, Bulgaria).

Performance parameters

All chicks per pen were weighed simultaneously upon placement post-hatch, at every feed change (i.e. day 11 and day 24) and at slaughter age (day 42). Average body weight (ABW) on these timepoints and average daily gain (ADG) by feeding phase were calculated per pen. For every feeding phase, feed in and feed out was weighed per pen. Average daily feed intake (ADFI) and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were subsequently calculated. Mortality was recorded daily and used to correct performance parameters.

Sampling

Necropsy sampling took place at 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, 96 h, day 7 and day 10 BA, on day 14, day 28 and at slaughter age. For timepoints 48 h, 72 h, 96 h, day 7 and day 10 BA, 4 chicks per pen were euthanized (2 male, 2 female). Two birds were euthanized per pen for timepoints 24 h, day 14, day 28 and at slaughter age (1 male, 1 female). Table 2 displays the exact number of animals that were sampled for each parameter, at each sampling timepoint. Up to the BA of 7d, chicks were euthanized through decapitation. Chicks 10 days and older were euthanized by intravenous pentobarbital sodium injection (200 mg/ml).

Relative organ weights

Relative weight was determined for liver (RLW), spleen (RSW) and bursa (RBW) by dividing the weight of the corresponding organ by live chick weight (CW). During the first week post-hatch, yolk sac weight (YSW) was also measured to determine relative yolk weight (RYW = YSW / CW).

Duodenal morphology

Transversal duodenal samples measuring 1.5 – 2.0 cm taken approximately 1 cm above the duodenal loop were taken from a female and a male bird per pen. Segments were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 7 days and subsequently stored in distilled water until further preparation. Subsections were cut from the samples, then dehydrated in xylene and embedded in paraffin, after which they were cut into sections of 5 µm using a microtome (HM360, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and further processed following the protocol described by De Maesschalck et al. (2015). Slides were stained using the Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) stain. Microscopic images of clear transections of the duodenal wall were captured using a Leica DM LB2 microscope equipped with a camera and the Leica Application Suite X computer-based image analysis software version 5.2.0 (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Morphological parameters were measured using ImageJ Image Processing and Analysis software (Schneider et al., 2012). Average villus length (AVL) was determined by randomly measuring 5 villi from villus tip to villus-crypt junction. Similarly, average crypt depth (ACD) was calculated by measuring 5 random crypts from villus-crypt junction to base and average muscular wall thickness (AMT) was determined by measuring the thickness of the circular and longitudinal muscular layers combined at 5 different locations across the section. Finally, villus to crypt ratio (VCR) was calculated by dividing AVL by ACD.

Nutrient metabolism

Non-esterified fatty acid and TRIG concentrations were analyzed in plasma derived from four 4-hour fasted chickens at 48 h, 72 h, 96 h, 7d and 10d BA and two 4-hour fasted chicks at 14 days, 28 days and 42 days of age. Uric acid concentrations were measured in the same samples at 48 h, 72 h and 96 h BA for T0 and T2. In preparation for taking the blood samples, an equal amount of male and female birds were randomly selected per pen and separated into empty crates for 4 h, taking away their access to feed and water. After 4 h, 0.3 ml blood was drawn from the jugular vein and collected in 1 ml plasma tubes (Vacutest K3 EDTA 1 ml, Vacutest Kima S.R.L., Arzergrande, Italy) up to 10d BA. From day 14 onwards, 1.2 ml was drawn and collected in 4 ml plasma tubes (Vacutest K2 EDTA 4 ml, Vacutest Kima S.R.L., Arzergrande, Italy). Blood was centrifuged for 10 min, 3,000 rpm at 4 °C after which plasma was collected, pooled by sex in Eppendorf tubes and stored at -20 °C until further use. Analyses for NEFA, TRIG and UA concentrations were done using the RX Monza analyzer (Randox Laboratories Ltd., Crumlin, UK) with the RX Monza FA 115, RX Monza TR 210 and RX Monza UA 230 kits respectively.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SAS software version 6.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Performance parameters were analyzed separately at each feeding phase by use of a linear fixed effects model, with HWB as a fixed blocking factor and treatment as fixed effect. The relative organ weight, duodenal morphology and nutrient metabolism parameters were analyzed by a mixed model to cope with the repeated measures in the pen over time. A mixed model (mixed procedure in SAS) was fitted to each response separately for each feeding phase, with pen as random effect, and treatment, sex, sampling timepoint, and all two-way interactions as fixed effects factors. Additionally, as with the performance analysis, HWB was taken into account as a fixed blocking factor. Tukey-adjusted multiple pairwise comparisons were performed between treatment groups using a global significance level of 5%.

A significant interaction between sex and treatment was found for RYW (P = 0.010). The significant interaction most likely originated from the larger spread of RYW at 24 h BA in female chicks compared to males, skewing the treatment least-squares means and subsequent interaction statistic. Otherwise, progression of RYW between 24 h to 96 h BA appeared similar across all treatments in both sexes. In general, female chicks tended to have higher RYW than male chicks (P = 0.060), but by 72 h BA, numerical differences between both sexes became negligible (data not shown). Across all treatments, the residual yolk made up around 9.1 – 9.8% of the live chick weight at 24 h BA, halving to around 3.9 – 4.6% by 48 h BA. By 72 h BA, the RYW once more halved to around 1.4 – 2.0%, and by 96 h BA, the yolk made up less than 1% of the total body weight across all treatments and sexes (data not shown).

The interaction between treatment and timepoint was significant for RLW during the starter phase (P < 0.001), indicating that the post-hatch fasting time impacted the proportional liver development (Fig. 1). Between 24 h and 48 h post-hatch, T0 and T1 chicks gained, on average, around 15.1% of their total body weight, whereas chicks fasted for 48 h lost around 3.2% of their body weight during this period (individual live chick weight at sampling, supplementary data). The liver of T2 chicks proportionally gained just around 0.6% of the total body weight between day 1 and 2, compared with T0 and T1 chicks where the liver proportionally gained around 1.1% on average. After gaining access to feed at 48 h, T2 chicks gained 25.0% of their total body weight between 48 h and 72 h BA, with a 1.4% increase of liver weight in proportion to body weight. This proportional increase was almost double the proportional increase in T0 (0.8%) and T1 chicks (0.6%). By 96 h BA, the liver took up around 5.1% of the total body weight across all treatments, and subsequently gradually declined to around 4.2% at day 14. By day 28, the RLW was around 2.8% across all treatments, which declined further to around 2.3% at slaughter age.

Both in the starter and grower phase, the RLW of T1 chicks were significantly lower compared with T0 chicks (P = 0.049), with an intermediate RLW present in T2 chicks. By day 14, T1 chicks showed a significantly higher RLW compared with T0 chicks (P = 0.044), with T2 chicks again maintaining intermediate values.

The T2 chicks had significantly lower RBW during the starter phase compared with T1 chicks (P = 0.046). Intermediate bursa to body weight proportions were present in immediately fed chicks.

No further main effects of treatment were present during any feeding phase, for any of the determined relative organ weights (liver, spleen, bursa).

Significant main effects of sex were, however, largely present on the relative organ weights, especially during, but not limited to, the starter phase. Between day 1 and 11 BA, female chicks had higher RLW (P < 0.001), lower RSW (P = 0.019) and lower RBW (P < 0.001) compared with their male counterparts. There was a tendency for female chicks to continue having lower RBW at the beginning of the grower phase (P = 0.054), however this effect was no longer present during the finisher phase. No further significant main effects of sex were found on either RBW or RSW at 14 days, yet between day 24 and slaughter age, female chicks were again found to have higher RLW (P = 0.013).

Discussion

Despite the straight-forward nature of the potential treatment groups, i.e. various delays to feed access post-hatch, early feeding studies can vary substantially in terms of managing the inevitable hatch window and the subsequent variable biological ages of the chicks incorporated in the study (Willemsen et al., 2010; de Jong et al., 2016). Within the same batch, time of hatch during the hatch window alone causes physiological differences between chicks at hatch, with studies reporting decreased residual yolk weights and increased relative liver weights in late hatchers compared with chicks hatched early during the hatch window (van de Ven et al., 2011, 2013). By closely monitoring the eggs throughout the hatching process, chicks can be grouped in similar ages providing more homogenous and standardized treatment groups, as was applied in the current study. Furthermore, studies into the effect of post-hatch feed deprivation on chicks hatched at differing times during the hatch window may further differ in applying feeding delays that were either equal across HWB or that varied in length according to time of hatch. Studies applying the former strategy reported higher body weight gains in chicks hatched late during the hatch window (Careghi et al., 2005; Wang et al., 2014), whereas in studies in which a set common timepoint was used at which the chicks were granted feed access regardless of their biological age, greater benefits on body weight were reported in early hatchers given immediate access to feed post-hatch (Lamot et al., 2014; Deines et al., 2021). By incorporating time of hatch into the study design of the current study, as well as by implementing an intensive, frequent sampling schedule based on the average biological age per HWB during the first 10 days post-hatch, thorough insights were achieved into the metabolic and growth mechanisms of early post-hatch chicks. Following up the treatment groups throughout the full rearing cycle provided a comprehensive view on the lasting impact of post-hatch feed deprivation on fast-growing broilers. Moreover, to the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study comparing different post-hatch feeding times in chicks that had all hatched on-farm.

Performance

Broiler performance was impaired by a 48 h post-hatch feed delay, yet a 24 h post-hatch delay appeared compensable. Chicks deprived of feed for 48 h post-hatch tended to have lower body weights across the entire rearing cycle compared with those that had immediate access to feed, whereas intermediate weights were attained by chicks deprived for only 24 h. Multiple studies described similar findings, with weight differences between fed and 24 h feed-deprived chicks being reported at the latest at 16 days post-hatch, besides being absent at slaughter age (Juul-Madsen et al., 2004; Abed et al., 2011; Özlü et al., 2020). Additionally, studies also including a 48 h feed deprivation treatment group reported having significant reductions in body weight at day 42 compared with immediately fed chicks (Juul-Madsen et al., 2004; Abed et al., 2011), whilst a similar trend was present in the current study. The complementary results across these studies indicate a certain threshold between 24 h to 48 h biological age prior to which the broiler chick can efficiently compensate adverse effects of post-hatch feed deprivation in terms of performance. Setting out from this hypothesis, Özlü et al. (2020) performed two trials with multiple post-hatch feed deprivation times, with 2 h to 4 h intervals between the treatment groups. Both trials suggest that a feeding delay 28 h post-hatch had significantly lower weights at day 35 compared with those that had immediate feed access.

The meta-analysis by de Jong et al. (2016) reported either no or a negative effect of 48 h feed delay on feed intake. Remarkably, our study shows increased feed intake in the chicks with 48 h delayed feed access. Within the current study, between 4 h and 60 h BA, crop fill assessments were carried out every 4 h per pen once given access to feed (data not shown). Of the chicks with immediate access to feed post-hatch, 50% had a filled crop by 28 h BA, gradually increasing to all crops filled by 44 h BA. In contrast, chicks that only got access to feed at 48 h BA immediately reached 100% crop fill rates by the following assessment at 52 h BA. Nevertheless, this apparent overconsumption of feed was not able to translate into compensated growth, as seen in their lower feed utilization efficiency and lower final body weight.

Relative organ weights and duodenal morphology

Many studies have analyzed the impact of early feed access on post-hatch yolk absorption, however results between these studies tend to vary and a clear conclusion remains absent (de Jong et al., 2016; van der Wagt et al., 2020). A commonly proposed hypothesis entails that early feed access stimulates the peristaltic motility of the intestines, thus facilitating yolk absorption (Noy and Sklan, 2001; Bhanja et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2020). However, as in the current study, multiple studies have also reported the absence of significant differences in residual yolk weights between immediately fed and up to 48 h post-hatch feed-deprived chicks (Maiorka et al., 2003; Pires et al., 2007). As concluded in the review by van der Wagt et al. (2020) on yolk sac utilization, many incidental factors may impact post-hatch yolk absorption, such as differences in incubation and brooding temperatures, providing a possible explanation for these discrepancies between early feeding studies.

Before 48 h BA, chicks deprived of feed appeared to have a lower liver to body weight proportion compared with those that had been fed, a result which has also been reported in previous studies (Maiorka et al., 2003; Pires et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2020). The importance of exogenous feed on the post-hatch development of the liver is further emphasized as there is a greater increase of proportion of liver weight in T2 chicks between 48 h and 72 h BA, once they had gained feed access, compared with those that had been fed earlier. This seems to be in accordance with the findings of Wang et al. (2020), who reported a higher RLW at 72 h BA in chicks which had been feed deprived up to 48 h BA compared with earlier fed birds. Several studies have set out to uncover the regulatory mechanisms and main metabolic pathways in hepatic tissue which are affected by delayed access to feed post-hatch: a recurring finding across these studies entails the downregulation of genes and transcription factors involved in hepatic lipogenic pathways in feed deprived chicks, which are then described to be quickly restored upon feed initiation (Richards et al., 2010; Hicks et al., 2019; Payne et al., 2019; Cogburn et al., 2020). Along with the apparent compensatory relative growth of the liver observed in the current study, these studies further highlight the importance of post-hatch feed consumption on early liver development, which in turn is crucial for early metabolic programming (Richards et al., 2010; Hicks et al., 2022).

During both the starter and the finisher phase, female chicks had a higher liver to body weight proportion than male chicks, regardless of treatment. In the few studies that compared relative organ weights between sexes during the starter phase, no differences in sex were found in RLW during the first three days post-hatch (Pires et al., 2007; van de Ven et al., 2011), yet similar to the results of the present study, Ahmadzadeh et al. (2025) reported a higher RLW at day 10 for female chicks. Significantly higher RLW have also been described in female broilers between 24 and 42 days of age (van der Klein et al., 2017; Stęczny and Kokoszyński, 2020; Ahmadzadeh et al., 2025), corresponding with the finisher phase in the present study. Concurrently during both starter and finisher phase, fasted plasma TRIG levels in the present study were also elevated in female chicks compared with males. Female broilers are more likely to have a higher fat deposition at slaughter age than males (Benyi et al., 2015; Stęczny and Kokoszyński, 2020; England et al., 2023). Additionally, gene expression of hepatic fatty acid synthase (FAS), a critical enzyme complex for the biosynthesis of fatty acids, has been described to be upregulated in 42 day old females broilers when compared with males (de Souza Khatlab et al., 2018). Considering the vital role of the liver in the fat metabolism, including the storage of and the de novo synthesis of TRIG, this may possibly be linked with the higher RLW and TRIG results observed in females in the current study.

Limited to the starter phase, a significant decrease in relative bursa weight was observed in chicks deprived of feed for 48 h post-hatch. Lower bursal weights have been previously reported in 72 h feed deprived chicks (Dibner et al., 1998; Bar-Shira et al., 2005), as well as in conventionally hatched broiler chicks compared with those hatched in a hatching system with immediate access to feed and water (Madej et al., 2024). In addition, early access to feed has been described to have a significant impact on several other immunological parameters, such as the enhanced proliferation of B and T lymphocytes in the hindgut (Bar-Shira et al., 2005) and the altered expression of several immune-related genes in ileal tissue (Miska et al., 2022). As in the current study, the initial disadvantage of the feed deprived chicks appeared transient. However, given the broad scope of parameters related to the development of the immune system, current literature on the impact of early feeding on the immune system development and it’s underlying mechanisms is sparse, making it an interesting area for future research.

In the present study, the main effect of sex also appeared to be significant for RSW and RBW between hatch and day 11, an effect which, to the authors’ knowledge, appears scarce in current literature. As aforementioned, few studies have compared relative organ weights between female and male chicks during the first days post-hatch and no differences were found for RSW and RBW between days 2 to 10 (Pires et al., 2007; Shinde Tamboli et al., 2018; Ahmadzadeh et al., 2025). Likewise, few studies have compared intestinal morphological parameters between sexes before 2 weeks of age. In the present study, female chicks had significantly deeper duodenal crypt depths and a corresponding lower VCR during the starter phase, similar to the significantly deeper jejunal crypts reported in female Cobb500 broiler compared with males in a study comparing incubation systems (Villanueva et al., 2016). In contrast, in a study comparing different dietary net energy/lysine ratio’s, female chicks were found to have significantly higher jejunal VCR in the first and second weeks post-hatch (Ban et al., 2025). As sex is often a random variable or blocking factor present in the larger study design, possible causes of these early significant differences between the sexes attained during the current study remain unclear. However, since some studies indicate sex differences on the above-mentioned parameters, it may be worthwhile to include replicates of each sex in future studies, especially when extrapolating research findings to as-hatched broiler flocks in the industry (England et al., 2023).

Significant differences between the different post-hatch feed deprivation times on the duodenal morphological parameters remained absent in the current study, which is in stark contrast to multiple previous early feeding studies in which detrimental effects of post-hatch feed deprivation on duodenal morphology were reported (Gonzales et al., 2003; Liu et al., 2020; Gaweł et al., 2025). In order to gain more insights into the impact of post-hatch feed and water deprivation on the small intestinal morphological development, alternative microscopic analyses have been implemented in previous studies: a 48 h feed deprivation post-hatch was described to significantly affect the differentiation and distribution of epithelial cell sub-types up to 10 days of age (Reicher et al., 2020) and caused reductions in villi sizes leading to more, yet smaller, villi per area in feed-deprived chicks (Maiorka et al., 2003). Furthermore, increased post-hatch feed deprivation times have also been reported to interfere with the mucosal development of the jejunum and ileum during the first week post-hatch (Ganjali et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2020; Reicher et al., 2020). The discrepancy of the findings of the current study with the available literature may be partially caused by the ad libitum availability of water in the post-hatch feed deprivation groups (Maiorka et al., 2003), differences in breeder flock age (Mahmoud and Edens, 2012), as well as by the age of the birds at sampling (Akram et al., 2025).

Nutrient metabolism

Multiple studies have set out to decode the changes in metabolism as chicks gradually shift from lipid-rich nutrients derived from the residual yolk to the carbohydrate-based exogenous feed, with some studies even comparing the impact of various post-hatch feed deprivation times on this metabolic switch. However, results between the different studies vary and multiple aspects remain insufficiently understood. Discrepancies between studies may be partially explained by the unknown time since last feed intake of the sampled animals. A study by Buyse et al. (2002) describes the pre- and post-prandial changes in plasma metabolite levels of broilers at several time-intervals following a set feeding time. UA concentrations were found to increase immediately following feed intake, reaching peak concentrations after 50 min, after which values remained relatively stable up to 2 h. TRIG levels likewise increased post-prandially, however more gradually, with increased levels still being measured 200 minutes following feed intake. In contrast, NEFA levels sharply declined within 20 minutes following initiation of feed intake, after which it then gradually increased, not yet reaching similar pre-prandial levels at 200 minutes post-feed (Buyse et al., 2002). When sampling blood from random chickens in trials where, excluding the feed deprivation treatment immediately post-hatch, feed is otherwise available ad libitum, plasma metabolite levels may thus vary as a result of the unknown time since last feed intake. In order to remove this influential factor from the plasma metabolite analysis, a pre-determined fasting period was implemented in the current study prior to taking the blood samples. Furthermore, in the current study, random chicks from each pen were sampled frequently during the first week post-hatch at the biological ages of 48 h, 72 h, 96 h and 168 h, providing enough continuity to roughly assemble a potential progression of the parameters over time.

Across all treatments, TRIG concentrations increased during the first days post-hatch, peaking at 96 h BA. A similar increase in TRIG concentration was described in the few studies that measured plasma or serum metabolic concentrations frequently during the first week post-hatch, reaching peak concentrations at either day 3 (Hicks et al., 2019), day 4 (Latour et al., 1995) or day 5 (Noy and Sklan, 2001). Considering the disparities between the studies in terms of use of either chronological or biological age, as well as the different timings of the sampling timepoints, the highest concentrations of plasma TRIG concentrations may be present between 96 h to 120 h post-hatch, after which the concentrations gradually decrease and reach a plateau at 2 weeks. Significantly higher levels of serum TRIG were also observed in turkey poults at 3 days of age compared with older birds, in a study that had fasted the animals prior to blood sampling (Szabó et al., 2005). The timeframe of this peak coincides with the timeframe during which the residual yolk sac is almost fully absorbed and the chick no longer relies on yolk sac nutrients for energy metabolism. Beyond the major metabolic shift that occurs when the chick consumes its first feed (Hicks et al., 2019), these results suggest that the metabolism is further challenged around four days post-hatch as yolk reserves are depleted and the chick becomes almost entirely reliant on feed-derived nutrients. Future research may further explore the dynamics of fasted nutrient metabolites during the first week post-hatch and assess the impact of this apparent second metabolic shift around 4 day BA, which in turn may provide insights into the optimal nutritional compositions of starter feed.

Despite marked changes of these metabolites over time, no marked differences were noted between the treatments in terms of fasted plasma nutrient metabolites. The lack of differences in the concentration of these metabolites between the various post-hatch feed deprivation times suggests that these are regulated by a rigid innate metabolic program capable of maintaining homeostasis independent of first feed intake. Considering the established impact of post-hatch feed deprivation on the expression of genes involved in hepatic lipogenic pathways (Richards et al., 2010; Hicks et al., 2019; Payne et al., 2019; Cogburn et al., 2020), it is still possible that the identification of the effects of post-hatch feed deprivation on nutrient metabolism may require a more elaborate panel of metabolites.

Conclusion

To conclude, significant differences between the different post-hatch feed deprivation times remained absent for the evaluated duodenal morphological parameters and fasted nutrient metabolites but were present for performance and relative organ weights. This indicates that, under the experimental conditions applied, delaying feed access for up to 48 hours post-hatch did not measurably affect early duodenal morphology nor post-hatch nutrient metabolism. However, considering the attained differences for the performance and relative organ weight parameters, as well as the currently available literature, research on the impact of post-hatch feed deprivation in broilers remains valuable and methods diminishing this post-hatch delay in feed should be further explored.

Further research could focus on the dynamics of fasted nutrient metabolites during the first week post-hatch and investigate the potential impact of the apparent second metabolic shift observed around 4 days of age. Such insights may contribute to a better understanding of early metabolic adaptation and help optimize the nutritional composition of starter feeds for on-farm hatched broiler chicks.

In addition, given the sex-related differences in relative organ weights, morphology and metabolic parameters, future research may benefit from including replicates of each sex. This would improve the interpretation and extrapolation of findings to commercially relevant as-hatched broiler flocks in the poultry industry.
