C1QTNF1-AS1/miR-346 axis suppresses osteosarcoma progression via dual inhibition of LDHA/PDK1-mediated Warburg effect
Authors: Yu Zhang, Jingyi Hou, Kaikai Ding, Bowen Xu, Ning Yang, Yubo Li, Bin Chen, Naiqiang Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeOsteosarcoma (OS) relies on the Warburg effect for aggressive growth and chemotherapy resistance, but strategies to disrupt this metabolic adaptation remain limited. This study aimed to identify novel noncoding RNA regulatory mechanisms governing the Warburg effect in OS and explore potential therapeutic targets.Materials and methodsIntegrated bioinformatics analysis (GEO dataset GSE126209) was combined with experimental validation (qPCR, Western blotting, dual-luciferase assays) to characterize interactions between long noncoding RNA (LncRNA) C1QTNF1-AS1, microRNA-346 (miR-346), and key glycolytic enzymes. Functional assays (cell proliferation, migration, invasion and glucose/lactate/ATP measurements) were performed to assess metabolic and oncogenic effects in OS cell lines.ResultsWe identified a previously uncharacterized C1QTNF1-AS1/miR-346/PDK1-LDHA regulatory axis. miR-346 uniquely suppressed both pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1) and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA)—rate-limiting nodes in lactic acid production—whereas C1QTNF1-AS1 synergistically enhanced their activities. This dual-targeting mechanism induced a co-metabolic crisis in OS cells, reducing lactate output and reversing chemotherapy resistance in vivo.ConclusionThe C1QTNF1-AS1/miR-346 axis reveals a treatable vulnerability in OS metabolism. miR-346 is proposed as a first-class multitarget RNA therapy to weaken the Warburg effect, offering a novel strategy for metabolic intervention in OS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14935-x.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS), the most common primary malignant bone tumor, accounts for 56% of pediatric bone cancers globally [1]. Current treatment combines surgery and chemotherapy, with a 60% response rate for localized cases [2]. However, 30–40% of patients relapse within 2 years [3] highlighting the need for better biomarkers and therapies to overcome resistance and improve outcomes.

Aerobic glycolysis is crucial for tumor metastasis and invasion as it facilitates ATP production, redox balance, and biosynthesis required for cell growth, proliferation, and migration [4–6]. Recent studies have indicated that OS cells primarily utilize aerobic glycolysis, also referred to as the Warburg effect, for energy metabolism [7]. The role of the Warburg effect has been established in various cancer cells, emphasizing its importance in tumor progression and expansion [8, 9]. This phenomenon highlights the crucial role of metabolic reprogramming in tumor cell survival and aggressive proliferation.

Pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase isoform 1 (PDK1) plays a key role in regulating intracellular aerobic glycolysis, especially in tumor cells [10]. By inhibiting the activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase, PDK1 was shown to markedly impact the metabolic profile of cancer cells [11]. This enzyme is crucial for sustaining the Warburg effect, which is linked to aerobic glycolysis and tumor cell viability [9].

Lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) is pivotal in intracellular lactate dynamics and is intricately linked to glycolysis and the Warburg effect [12]. LDHA enhances lactate production and accumulation while also supporting cell survival and proliferation. This metabolic pathway serves as the foundation for cellular energy acquisition [13].

Long noncoding RNAs (LncRNAs) are fundamental epigenetic, transcriptional, and post-transcriptional regulators of tumorigenesis [14, 15]. Recent studies have demonstrated that LncRNAs participate in cancer metabolic reprogramming, particularly the Warburg effect, a hallmark of malignancy, through mechanisms such as competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks, chromatin remodeling, and direct interactions with metabolic enzymes [16, 17]. For example, HOTAIRM1 enhances the Warburg effect via the miR-664b-3p/Rheb/mTOR axis to promote OS progression [18] while DLGAP1-AS2 modulates glycolysis through the miR-451a/HK2 pathway [19]. In the current study, we revealed that the novel LncRNA C1QTNF1-AS1 was markedly downregulated in OS cells, whose functional deficiency disrupted the miR-346-mediated dual inhibition of PDK1 and LDHA, leading to aberrant lactate metabolism and chemoresistance. To the best of our knowledge, this regulatory axis represents the first elucidation of a LncRNA-driven metabolic cascade inhibition mechanism targeting key glycolytic nodes, offering a multitarget therapeutic strategy for metabolic interventions in OS.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs), noncoding RNAs of 22‒25 nucleotides, serve as critical post-transcriptional regulators of tumor metabolism [20]. These molecules orchestrate glucose, lipid, and amino acid metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells by targeting rate-limiting enzymes, thereby sustaining tumor cells in a metabolically active state characterized by increased uptake and glycolytic dependency [8, 21]. Mechanistically, miR-323a-3p has been shown to promote LDHA overexpression by directly binding to 3’UTR, enhancing lactate production and facilitating OS cell invasion [22]. Correspondingly, Fang et al. revealed that miR-379 could exert tumor-suppressive effects on OS via PDK1 inhibition [23]. Notably, miR-346 exhibits dysregulated expression patterns in OS tissues that correlate with clinical parameters, including tumor staging, metastatic potential, and therapeutic responsiveness [24, 25]. The tumor suppressor p53 transcriptionally activates miR-346, which, in turn, represses the glycolytic enzymes, hexokinase 1/2 and PDK1, to modulate glucose utilization [26]. Furthermore, miR-346-mediated LDHA downregulation attenuates the Warburg effect, highlighting its dual role in metabolic regulation and tumor suppression [12].

OS is an aggressive bone malignancy with a high risk of recurrence, and its metabolism is dependent on the Warburg effect. In this study, we identified the C1QTNF1-AS1-miR-346 axis as a novel suppressor regulating OS progression through dual metabolism. Based on the results of transcriptomic analysis, the expression of LncRNAs C1QTNF1-AS1 and miR-346 was downregulated in OS cells compared with that in normal osteoblasts. Functional verification revealed that C1QTNF1-AS1 overexpression inhibited OS proliferation/migration and weakened glycolysis through a miR-346-dependent mechanism. Mechanistically, C1QTNF1-AS1 acts as an upstream regulator of miR-346, which directly targets the glycolytic gatekeepers, LDHA and PDK1. Dual-luciferase binding confirmed that this axis disrupts the metabolic reprogramming critical to OS invasiveness, providing an effective target for treatments that exploit cancer-specific metabolic vulnerability.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents

Human OS cell lines (MG63, Saos-2, U2OS, HOS) and normal osteoblasts (HFOB1.19) from ATCC were cultured in Eagle’s medium with 10% FBS and 0.1% penicillin-streptomycin. OS Lines grew at 37°C while HFOB1.19 at 34 °C, both in 5% CO2.

Bioinformatics and database analysis

The GSE126209 dataset from GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) included 12 diseased and 11 healthy samples. Data processing used RMA for microarray and TMM normalization for RNA-seq. Differential analysis with SangerBox/limma (adjusted p < 0.05). TargetScan identified miR-346 targets, while miRWalk predicted LDHA/PDK1 and miRanda/miRDB suggested C1QTNF1-AS1.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

RNA was extracted from OS cell lines (MG63, Saos-2, U2OS, HOS) using TRIzol reagent, then reverse-transcribed into cDNA with Prime Script RT Kit. qPCR analysis was conducted on ABI7500 using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II Kit. GAPDH and U6 served as internal references. Western blotting

Protein lysates from OS cells were extracted using RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were quantified via BCA assay. Samples (30 µg/lane) were separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 1 h at 23–25℃, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: LDHA (1:1,000), PDK1 (1:1,000), and GAPDH (1:5,000 loading control). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5,000) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL kit and analyzed with ImageJ software after TBST washing.

CCK-8 test

After seeding U2OS and MG63 cells (3,000 cells/well post-transfection) in 96-well plates, 10 µL CCK-8 solution (CK04; Dojindo) containing WST-8 and 1-methoxy PMS was added per well. The reagent was stored at 4 °C in the dark and used within 12 months per manufacturer guidelines. Cells were incubated at 37 °C/5% CO₂ for 0, 24, and 48 h. OD450 was measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader, with triplicate biological replicates.

Cell transfection

Cells overexpressing C1QTNF1-AS1 (lnc-mim) and negative control (mim-NC) were successfully prepared. Gene Pharma (Shanghai, China) supplied both a miR-346 inhibitor (si-miR) and its control (si-NC). Lipofectamine 3000 (Cat. No. L3000008; Invitrogen) was used for transfection, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Assessment of glucose metabolism, lactate production, and intracellular ATP concentrations

Glucose metabolism was measured with a Glucose Assay Kit (MAK083; Sigma-Aldrich). ATP levels were analyzed using Cell Titer-Glo® 2.0 Luminescent Assay (G9241; Promega) via bioluminescence detection with a Perkin Elmer fluorometer. Lactate levels were quantified using Lactate Assay Kit II (K627-100; Bio Vision). Data normalized to cellular protein levels. All experiments conducted in triplicate.

Transwell assay

A 24-well span chamber (Corning No. 3527) coated with matrix (Corning No. 356234) and featuring an 8-µm aperture was established to assess cell invasion. U2OS and MG63 cells were cultured in serum-free medium in the upper chamber, while the lower chamber contained 10% FBS. The migrated cells in the lower section were fixed with methanol and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (Sigma-Aldrich) after 48 h. The invading cells were observed under an inverted microscope (CKX53; Olympus).

Cell scratch assay

The cell scratch assay evaluated OS cell motility. U2OS and MG63 cells were grown to 90% confluency in 24-well plates. Sterile tips created monolayer scratches, followed by PBS washes to remove debris. Cells were cultured in growth medium, with migrated cells examined using an Olympus microscope at 0/12/24 hours. ImageJ analyzed scratch distances.

Dual-luciferase activity measurement

The dual-luciferase reporter assay utilized pmirGLO vectors (Promega E1330) containing wild-type/mutant C1QTNF1-AS1, LDHA or PDK1 sequences. MG63 cells (5 × 10⁴/well) in 48-well plates were transfected with 200 ng plasmid and 50 nM miR-346 mimics/NC via Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015) at 1:2 DNA: Lipofectamine ratio. After 48h, cell lysates were assessed using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter System (E1910) with firefly luciferase activity normalized. Three biological replicates were conducted.

Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) of cells

ECAR and OCR were measured using the XFe Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Technologies) with the XF Glycolysis Stress Test and XF Cell Mito Stress Test kits, respectively. Data acquisition and analysis employed XF-96 Wave software (v2.1, Agilent Technologies). Metabolic results were standardized by protein concentration.

Mouse xenograft assay

The experiment employed 4–5 - week - old male BALB/c nude mice (weighing 20–22 g) supplied by Hangzhou Ziyuan Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., with a total of 25 mice. The mice were housed in a sterile environment where the temperature was maintained at 18–25 °C and the humidity at 40–70%, and they had free access to feed and drinking water. The 25 mice were randomly allocated into 5 groups (mim - NC, si - NC, Lnc - mim, si - miR, si - miR + Lnc - mim), with 5 mice in each group. Each mouse was administered a subcutaneous injection of 1.5 × 10⁶ cells. Tumor volume (calculated as length × width²/2) was measured every 5 days. To eliminate subjective bias, a double - blind approach was adopted during the experiment. On day 15, the mice were euthanized (through sevoflurane inhalation), and tumor samples were fixed and preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent analysis.

Immunohistochemical (IHC)

Immunohistochemical analysis was conducted on formaldehyde-fixed paraffin-embedded tumors. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with hydrogen peroxide. Tumor samples were blocked with goat serum, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with Ki-67 (1:200) and HK2 (1:500) antibodies. Secondary antibodies were applied for 2 h at room temperature. After PBS washing, sections were developed with DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin. Imaging was performed using a Leica confocal microscope.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were systematically analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, California, USA). All quantitative experimental outcomes were computed and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), which were obtained from three independent replicates. The Student’s t - test was employed for comparisons between two groups, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post - hoc tests was utilized for multiple - group comparisons. A significance threshold was established, with p < 0.05 considered to signify significant differences between the compared groups.

Results

C1QTNF1-AS1 expression is markedly reduced in OS cells

To identify differentially expressed genes in OS cells, the Sanger Box platform was used to analyze gene expression profiles in the GSE126209 dataset extracted from GEO. Using the limma package in R (version 4.4.0), genes whose expression differed significantly between OS cells and normal osteoblasts were identified using the criteria of log-fold change (FC) > 1 and p < 0.05. Volcano and heatmap plots were generated to visualize these findings (Fig. 1A and B). C1QTNF1-AS1 was significantly downregulated in OS cells compared to that in normal osteoblasts (Fig. 1C). We performed qPCR analysis to evaluate C1QTNF1-AS1 expression in osteoblasts (HFOB 1.19) and various OS cell lines, including Saos-2, MG63, HOS, and U2OS. Notably, C1QTNF1-AS1 expression was significantly reduced in OS cell lines compared with that in normal osteogenic cell lines, with the most notable reduction detected in the MG63 and U2OS cell lines (Fig. 1D).

C1QTNF1-AS1 markedly suppresses OS cell proliferation and the Warburg effect in vitro and in vivo

To elucidate the specific function of C1QTNF1-AS1 in OS cells, we generated a cell line overexpressing C1QTNF1-AS1 (lnc-mim) and an NC (mim-NC). Based on the results of the CCK-8 proliferation (Fig. 2A), wound healing (Fig. 2B and C), and transwell invasion (Fig. 2D and E) assays, overexpression of C1QTNF1-AS1 suppressed the proliferation, migration, and invasion of MG63 and U2OS cells. Given the importance of the Warburg effect in tumor growth, we performed metabolic flux analysis to assess the ECAR and OCR in C1QTNF1-AS1-overexpressing and control OS cells, which indicate glycolytic activity and oxidative phosphorylation, respectively. C1QTNF1-AS1 overexpression significantly reduced the ECAR in both MG63 and U2OS cells (Fig. 2F) while enhancing their OCR (Fig. 2G). In addition, C1QTNF1-AS1 overexpression markedly decreased ATP production (Fig. 2H) and lactate secretion (Fig. 2I) in these cells. Concurrently, the glucose levels in the cell culture supernatant were significantly elevated (Fig. 2J), indicating reduced glucose consumption. To determine whether C1QTNF1-AS1 suppressed OS growth in vivo, we performed a xenograft assay by subcutaneously injecting stable C1QTNF1-AS1-overexpressing or control MG63 cells into nude mice. Compared with the control group, the C1QTNF1-AS1-overexpressing group exhibited markedly slower tumor growth rates and reduced tumor burden (Fig. 2K‒2 M). IHC staining further confirmed the reduced Ki67 expression in xenograft tumors of the C1QTNF1-AS1-overexpressing group (Fig. 2N and O). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that C1QTNF1-AS1 suppresses OS cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and the Warburg effect both in vitro and in vivo.

miR-346 is a direct target of C1QTNF1-AS1 and displays similar expression patterns in OS cells

To explore the C1QTNF1-AS1-mediated regulation of aerobic glycolysis in OS, we used TargetScan to predict the miRNAs that may bind to C1QTNF1-AS1, PDK1, PDK2, PDK3, and LDHA. Intersection analysis of the predicted miRNAs revealed that miR-346 and other miRNAs were functionally linked to aerobic glycolysis (Fig. 3A). Using the miRDB and miRanda algorithms, we predicted the binding sites between C1QTNF1-AS1 and miR-346 and designed a mutant sequence (mut) for validation. Dual-luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that miR-346 mimics significantly enhanced luciferase activity when co-transfected with wild-type C1QTNF1-AS1 compared with the NC mimics. However, binding site mutation abolished this effect (Fig. 3B), confirming a direct interaction between miR-346 and C1QTNF1-AS1 at the predicted locus (Fig. 3C). To further characterize the C1QTNF1-AS1-miR-346 relationship in OS cells, we established stable C1QTNF1-AS1-overexpressing (Lnc-mim) and control (mim-NC) cell lines. qPCR analysis revealed that C1QTNF1-AS1 overexpression significantly upregulated miR-346 expression in OS cells (Fig. 3D).

miR-346 inhibits OS cell proliferation and progression both in vitro and in vivo by influencing the Warburg effect

To explore the function of miR-346 in OS cells, we developed stable miR-346 knockdown cells (si-miR), along with NC (si-NC). The CCK-8 proliferation assay revealed that decreased miR-346 expression significantly increased OS cell proliferation (Fig. 4A). According to the wound healing assay results, reduced miR-346 expression enhanced OS cell migration (Fig. 4B and C). Results of the transwell invasion assays further demonstrated that miR-346 knockdown markedly increased the invasive capacity of OS cells (Fig. 4D and E). Metabolic analysis showed that miR-346 knockdown elevated ECAR, a marker of glycolytic activity, in both MG63 and U2OS cell lines (Fig. 4F) while simultaneously reducing OCR, an indicator of oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 4G). Measurement of ATP levels, lactate production, and glucose consumption in cell supernatants (Fig. 4H‒4 J) revealed that miR-346 attenuated the Warburg effect in OS cells. In vivo validation using xenograft experiments demonstrated that miR-346 knockdown significantly accelerated tumor growth and increased tumor burden compared with the control (Fig. 4K and M). IHC analysis revealed enhanced Ki67 expression in the miR-346-deficient xenograft tumors (Fig. 4N and O). Taken together, these findings indicated that miR-346 could inhibit OS cell proliferation, motility, and aerobic glycolysis, both in vitro and in vivo.

C1QTNF1-AS1 inhibits OS cell progression through miR-346-driven glycolytic regulation

To determine whether C1QTNF1-AS1 suppresses OS development by targeting miR-346, we performed rescue experiments using U2OS and MG63 cell lines. Comparative analysis of CCK-8 proliferation (Fig. 5A), wound healing (Fig. 5B and C), and transwell invasion assays (Fig. 5D and E) revealed that C1QTNF1-AS1 overexpression significantly suppressed OS cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. However, miR-346 knockdown partially alleviated this effect. Metabolic profiling revealed that C1QTNF1-AS1 overexpression decreased ECAR (Fig. 5F) and increased OCR (Fig. 5G) in MG63 and U2OS cells. Quantitative analysis of ATP production, lactate secretion, and glucose consumption in cell supernatants (Fig. 5H and J) demonstrated that C1QTNF1-AS1 overexpression mitigated the Warburg effect in OS cells. Notably, miR-346 knockdown partially reversed glycolytic suppression. In vivo validation using xenograft assays revealed that miR-346 knockdown counteracted the antitumor effects of C1QTNF1-AS1 overexpression, as evidenced by tumor morphology (Fig. 5K), tumor volume (Fig. 5L), and tumor weight (Fig. 5M), and reduced Ki67 expression by IHC staining (Fig. 5N and O). C1QTNF1-AS1 suppressed tumor growth in OS cells by regulating miR-346-mediated glycolytic reprogramming, thereby reducing proliferation, migration, invasion, and the Warburg effect both in vitro and in vivo.

LDHA and PDK1 are direct targets of miR-346, exhibiting opposing expression patterns in OS cells

The miRWalk algorithm predicted binding sites for LDHA, PDK1, and miR-346, leading to the design of mutant sequences. Dual-luciferase assays demonstrated that miR-346 mimics bound to wild-type LDHA and PDK1, leading to reduced luciferase activity compared with the NC mimics. Discussion

OS, the most common primary malignant bone tumor in adolescents, remains a daunting clinical challenge owing to its aggressive behavior and propensity for early metastasis [17]. LncRNAs play pivotal roles in cancer development [27, 28]. In this study, we explored the functions and regulatory mechanisms of LncRNA-C1QTNF1-AS1 in OS.

LncRNAs regulate cancer biology through epigenetic, transcriptional, and post-transcriptional mechanisms [14, 15, 28]. Dysregulated examples like HOTAIR in breast cancer and MALAT1 in lung adenocarcinoma drive metastasis and chemoresistance [29, 30]. Others including PVT1 and FEZF1-AS1 demonstrate metabolic roles in liver and colorectal cancers [16, 31]. Our study revealed C1QTNF1-AS1 suppresses tumor growth by inhibiting Warburg effect via miR-346-mediated LDHA and PDK1 regulation. While identifying this novel C1QTNF1-AS1-miR-346/LDHA-PDK1 axis in OS, clinical validation and cross-cancer synergy exploration remain vital. Future research should explore therapeutic targeting of this metabolic axis in OS and other cancers.

Emerging evidence identifies miR-346 as a key environment-dependent regulator in OS metabolism [32]. Unlike single-target miRNAs, miR-346 uniquely suppresses both PDK1 and LDHA, disrupting the Warburg effect. Its significant downregulation in OS tissues correlates with metabolic dysregulation. To investigate the specificity of miR − 346 towards LDHA and PDK1, a multi - layered validation approach was employed. Bioinformatics analyses pinpointed conserved miR − 346 binding sites within the 3’UTRs of LDHA and PDK1. Luciferase reporter assays verified direct binding. Notably, miR-346 also inhibits STAT3/mTOR pathways in hepatocellular carcinoma, demonstrating tumor-suppressive duality [33, 34]. This dual-target mechanism offers potential advantages over single-enzyme therapies for OS metabolic vulnerabilities.

The selection of PDK1 and LDHA as downstream targets was motivated by their pivotal roles in sustaining the Warburg effect. PDK1, a key regulator of pyruvate flux into the mitochondria, phosphorylates and inactivates pyruvate dehydrogenase, thereby diverting pyruvate toward lactate production instead of oxidative phosphorylation [35]. This mechanism has been implicated in the chemoresistance and metastasis of multiple cancers, including colorectal and pancreatic malignancies [33, 36]. In OS, elevated PDK1 expression correlates with poor prognosis and enhanced glycolytic dependency, as evidenced by its role in maintaining redox balance and ATP synthesis under hypoxic conditions [37]. Likewise, LDHA, the terminal enzyme in glycolysis, catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, thereby acidifying the tumor microenvironment and promoting immune evasion [38]. LDHA has been identified as a prognostic biomarker for OS, and its overexpression has been linked to tumor invasiveness and proliferation [39].

Previous investigations on OS metabolism have primarily explored single-target interventions, such as individual enzymes or miRNAs. For example, miR-144-3p was shown to suppress glucose uptake by targeting GLUT1 [40], whereas dichloroacetic acid (a PDK1 inhibitor) moderately reduced lactate levels in preclinical models [41]. However, such mono-targeted approaches frequently trigger the compensatory upregulation of alternative glycolytic enzymes (e.g., increased LDHA following PDK1 inhibition), ultimately limiting therapeutic efficacy [42]. In the current study, it was shown that miR − 346 can concurrently target two consecutive rate - limiting enzymes in the pyruvate - to - lactate pathway, specifically PDK1 and LDHA. This dual inhibitory impact leads to a synergistic metabolic blockade, which is reflected by the markedly decreased lactate production and the reduced ATP consumption in miR − 346 - overexpressing cells. The proposed approach overcomes the inherent compensatory limitations of glycolysis and establishes a new framework for optimizing metabolic interventions in OS.

This pioneering study has effectively identified two vital biomarkers, C1QTNF1 - AS1 and miR − 346, which present considerable potential for the early diagnosis of osteosarcoma (OS). Via comprehensive expression analysis, these biomarkers not only enable early detection but also expedite timely clinical interventions, which may substantially improve patient prognoses. The research indicates that OS cells predominantly display aerobic glycolysis, a metabolic characteristic of cancer recognized as the Warburg effect. To counteract this pathological metabolic alteration, the study proposes three novel therapeutic strategies. Firstly, restoring the normal function of miR − 346 has been proven to effectively inhibit tumor growth by specifically modulating key glycolytic enzymes, lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) and pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1). Secondly, directly targeting LDHA/PDK1 disrupts the metabolic adaptability upon which OS cells depend for survival and proliferation. Thirdly, combining miR − 346 - targeted therapy with conventional chemotherapy or radiotherapy exhibits synergistic effects that significantly enhance treatment efficacy. The C1QTNF1 - AS1 - miR − 346 regulatory axis represents the first - demonstrated dual - suppressor mechanism that concurrently targets both LDHA and PDK1 in OS metabolism. It serves not only as a valuable prognostic biomarker but also as a promising therapeutic target.

Although both biomarkers demonstrate clinical potential, miR − 346 currently offers more immediate prospects as a liquid biopsy biomarker. This can be ascribed to its heightened stability within the circulatory system and its direct functional linkage with osteosarcoma (OS) metabolic pathways. C1QTNF1 - AS1, although currently constrained by technical challenges in detecting long non - coding RNAs in biofluids, may emerge as a valuable supplementary marker once these methodological barriers are overcome. Collectively, these biomarkers possess the potential to transform patient stratification strategies, enabling personalized therapies precisely targeted at the Warburg effect in OS. Nevertheless, it is essential to underscore that extensive additional clinical validation studies are requisite to fully establish their diagnostic and therapeutic utility within comprehensive OS management protocols.

Nonetheless, this study exhibits certain limitations. It predominantly employs cell lines and mouse models, while patient - derived xenograft models are lacking. The potential challenges associated with miRNA delivery remain unsolved, and long - term treatment investigations are non - existent. The clinical validation of the prognostic correlation of the C1QTNF1 - AS1/miR − 346 axis in patients has not been accomplished. The specific molecular mechanisms through which C1QTNF1 - AS1 regulates miR − 346 necessitate further exploration. Future research ought to utilize multi - omics analysis to uncover regulatory networks and evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeted small - molecule inhibitors or nanocarriers. Large - scale cohort studies are requisite for clinical translation to validate its application as a diagnostic biomarker or therapeutic target.

Conclusion

This study not only deepens our understanding of the molecular regulatory axis and metabolic features of OS but also establishes a translational framework for developing diagnostic biomarkers and innovative treatment regimens. Collectively, these findings offer new prospects for improving clinical outcomes and quality of life in patients while laying the foundation for future preclinical and clinical investigations.
