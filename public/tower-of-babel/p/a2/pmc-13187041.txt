Low-dose chronic exposure to hemin and kynurenine enhances metabolic adaptation in a colorectal cancer cell model
Authors: Elien Alderweireldt, Charlotte Grootaert, Stijn Luca, Filip Van Nieuwerburgh, Andreja Rajkovic, John Van Camp, Olivier De Wever
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic adaptation enables cancer cells to persist under fluctuating nutrient conditions and is a major driver of tumor progression, including colorectal cancer (CRC). The contribution of diet, such as red and processed meat, in tumor progression remains poorly understood. Here, we investigated how repeated exposure to hemin (HEM) and kynurenine (KYN), alone and in combination, shapes CRC cell metabolism under nutrient deprivation. Using respirometry and transcriptomics in 3D spheroids, we found that HEM and KYN induced distinct, context-dependent metabolic adaptations, differentially affecting glycolytic and oxidative energy pathways depending on nutrient availability. Co-exposure to HEM + KYN maintained energy production during glucose deprivation through enhanced lipid storage, revealing a mechanism by which red meat–derived metabolites reinforce metabolic plasticity. These findings demonstrate that red meat–derived metabolites modulate key bioenergetic pathways in a complementary manner and that their combined presence amplifies cancer cell adaptability, highlighting a potential mechanism linking diet to CRC progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43267-4.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Bioenergetics, Metabolism, Red meat, Hemin, KynurenineSubject terms: Biochemistry, Cancer, Cell biology

Introduction

Dysregulated metabolism is a hallmark of cancer, enabling sustained growth and survival under stress1. Because cancer and healthy cells differ in their metabolic routes and nutrient use, understanding substrate dependencies can inform therapeutic strategies.

In colorectal cancer (CRC), tumor cells face two distinct nutrient environments: the colon lumen and the blood supply, shaped by diet and microbial fermentation2. High red and processed meat intake correlates with increased CRC risk3,4, whereas fiber-rich diets appear protective5. Red and processed meat are classified as type 2B and 2A carcinogens respectively6,7. Red meat is a source of protein, fat, vitamin B12, and iron, but its digestion and microbial metabolism release compounds that may influence bioenergetics and signaling8,9.

Heme and tryptophan metabolites are more abundant after red meat digestion compared to white meat digestion10. Heme has known genotoxic effects11,12 and is essential for energy production with potential modulating effects in cancer bioenergetics13. Recent studies have linked tryptophan catabolism to the suppression of antitumor immune cell activity, with its derivative kynurenine (KYN) as the main modulatory metabolite14. As aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) activator, KYN may also affect carcinogenic processes including growth15–17 and possibly energy metabolism18. Because heme availability drives tryptophan conversion toward KYN19,20, both metabolites are expected in the colon lumen after meat consumption. Their combined impact on CRC cell metabolism remains unclear.

Here, we applied a low-dose, repeated-exposure design to mimic chronic exposure to hemin (HEM; a stable form of heme) and KYN in 3D CRC spheroids. Butyrate (BUT), a microbial short-chain fatty acid produced from dietary fiber fermentation, was included as a reference compound due to its well-documented anticancer effects21–24. We investigated whether repeated-dose exposure to these metabolites alters CRC bioenergetics, and how this influences metabolic flexibility, defined as the ability of cells to utilize and switch between different substrates25. Nutrient-deprivation challenges with glucose (Gc), pyruvate (P), fatty acids (FA), or glutamine (Gt) were used to probe how dietary metabolites shape cancer cell adaptation.

Results

Optimization of experimental settings

Metabolite concentrations were selected based on literature-reported physiological relevance16,26,27 and subtoxic thresholds28,29. HCT116 cells were chosen as representative model from a panel of cell lines (HT-29, Caco-2, HCT8/E11, and CT26) because they displayed the best dose–response relationships in line with literature reports with BUT as major reference point (Figure S1A). The HCT116 spheroids formed reproducibly in agarose molds throughout long-term culture (Figure S2A-B). Vehicle controls (PBS, DMSO) did not affect growth (Figure S2C-D). Immobilization in Matrigel (Figure S3A) enabled Seahorse measurements with stable well-to-well distribution which is consequent to the replicates originating from the same spheroid suspension (Figure S3B).

Repeated exposure to meat-derived metabolites altered basal bioenergetics

In a first set of experiments HCT116 spheroids underwent a repeated, low-dose regime in nutrient-rich culture medium (Fig. 1A). As HCT116 spheroids lack a defined apical–basolateral polarity and lumen-facing architecture, metabolite exposure in this model reflects external-medium exposure of a three-dimensional cellular aggregate rather than true luminal or apical exposure. While this limits direct modeling of epithelial luminal access, it may still capture aspects of metabolite exposure relevant to poorly organized tumor tissue in vivo, where epithelial polarity is often compromised. Moreover, while spheroids were exposed repeatedly to metabolites via the culture medium over 7–9 days, penetration into the spheroid interior and potential periphery-to-core concentration gradients were not directly quantified. Therefore, any reference to diffusion or time-dependent uptake should be interpreted cautiously.

Spheroids treated with HEM or KYN showed reduced initial OCR (Fig. 1B) without changes in ECAR (Fig. 1C) compared to the UT condition, whereas combined HEM + KYN exposure increased ECAR and induced a more glycolytic profile. In contrast, BUT reduced both OCR and ECAR, consistent with a quiescent metabolic state (Figure S4A-C).

Nutritional challenges reveal metabolic adaptation

When spheroids were challenged with nutrient-depleted media (Fig. 2A), each pretreatment revealed a distinct adaptive response. To compare the kinetic OCR and ECAR profiles between conditions in a quantitative way, linear regression analysis was applied on the kinetic graphs, and the relative effect of the pretreatment vs. untreated spheroids was quantified by the models’ effects (intercepts) (Fig. 2B).

In line with initial OCR and ECAR shifts, HEM-pretreated spheroids showed an overall lower OCR compared to UT spheroids in MGcGtP medium (Figure S5A), and this decrease was lowest after 2DG injection, suggesting that glucose is an important fuel for the TCA cycle (Fig. 2C). Moreover, the OCR-decreasing effect of BPTES injection implies an increased dependency on glutamine compared to UT spheroids (Figure S5A). HEM pretreatment also decreased the maximal respiration, i.e. the difference in OCR before and after FCCP injection, upon etomoxir injection (Fig. 2D) suggesting FA oxidation as an additional source of acetyl-CoA. In contrast, HEM-pretreated spheroids displayed an increased ability to adapt to disrupted nutrient supplies, especially of glucose. Indeed, in media M, MGt, and MGc, the HEM-pretreated spheroids sustained higher levels of OCR and ECAR compared to UT spheroids after 2DG injection (Fig. 2C). The direct increase in OCR and ECAR as a response to 2DG indicates that alternative substrates may support the cells’ bioenergetic processes, such as glutamine (or other AAs) (Figure S7A). Moreover, in the nutrient-poor medium M, we found a significant increase in maximal respiration of HEM-exposed versus UT spheroids after blocking different steps of the glycolysis (Fig. 2E-F), thus, a larger portion of the mitochondrial respiration of HEM-pretreated spheroids was fueled through other nutrients than glucose/pyruvate. This is supported by the observation that HEM-exposed spheroids placed in MGt medium displayed a higher maximal respiration compared to UT spheroids (Fig. 2G), and that the ratio OCR: ECAR is higher than for UT spheroids (Figures S6C, S7C, S8C).

KYN-exposed spheroids transferred to MGcGtP medium display decreased OCR levels combined with no significant effect on ECAR compared to UT spheroids upon 2DG and BPTES injection, which suggests that OXPHOS relied more on glucose and glutamine (Fig. 2C). Interestingly, in MGt medium, KYN-pretreated spheroids had an overall higher OCR level and a larger response to FCCP injection (Figure S7A), implying a more active mitochondrial metabolism under these circumstances compared to UT spheroids, as also observed by the higher OCR: ECAR ratio after 2DG and BPTES injection (Figure S7C). In MGc medium, KYN-pretreated spheroids maintained a higher OCR upon UK5099 but not 2DG injection, suggesting less dependency on mitochondrial pyruvate transport compared to UT spheroids (Fig. 2C). In parallel, the overall OCR effect was higher in media where both glucose and pyruvate were present (MGcP), but not when glucose uptake, glutaminolysis or FA oxidation were blocked (Fig. 2C, Figure S7A), implying metabolic dependency on all these processes.

HEM + KYN-pretreated spheroids displayed a highly energetic phenotype even when varying the medium composition, as generally they maintained higher levels of overall OCR and ECAR levels compared to the UT, KYN, and HEM-pretreated spheroids (Fig. 2C). For example, in unsupplemented medium M, the HEM + KYN condition displayed an increased maximal respiration compared to the UT control (Fig. 2H). Also, in medium M combined with 2DG injection, all three pretreatments showed an increased capacity to maintain higher OCR levels compared to UT, and in case of KYN and HEM + KYN also ECAR (Fig. 2C, Figure S6). In the same medium, BPTES differentiated the HEM + KYN-pretreated spheroids from the rest with higher levels of OCR and ECAR (Fig. 2C, Figure S6A-B), showing an easier switch in metabolism upon inhibition of glutamine metabolism, potentially by switching to lipid metabolism. HEM + KYN exposure also had a differential effect on the remaining ECAR levels after 2DG injection. In MGc medium, glucose uptake inhibition had the largest impact on ECAR reduction (Fig. 2I), pointing to a larger dependency on glucose supply of these cells. In contrast, when pyruvate was also present in the medium, HEM + KYN-pretreated spheroids displayed higher levels of OCR and ECAR compared to UT but also compared to the single treatments in most cases (Fig. 2J, Figure S9). As this was not observed in glucose only medium, a significant role for pyruvate and lipid metabolism is implied.

In contrast, BUT stimulated a more aerobic metabolism in HCT116 spheroids, as shown in Figure S4D. Here, the cells were less energetic, as both OCR and ECAR levels were lower compared to UT spheroids upon 2DG injection. Moreover, the negative difference in OCR after 2DG addition in a nutrient-rich medium suggests that BUT-pretreatment may stimulate the mitochondrial respiration of added glucose rather than glycolysis. The opposite effect in M and MGt medium implies an adaptive mechanism upon glucose shortage, as also observed in case of MPC inhibition by UK5099. This is potentially realized by glutamine in case of its supply in the medium, which is supported by the increased OCR in BUT-pretreated spheroids in glutamine-supplemented medium in the control (C) condition. Only limited differences in ECAR responses between the BUT and UT condition were observed, supporting the hypothesis that BUT mainly affects mitochondrial respiration.

The impact of pretreatments on the transcriptome reveals different mode-of-actions of individual and combined metabolites

Bulk mRNA sequencing performed on meat-derived metabolite pretreated spheroids with or without an additional nutritional challenge (3 h MGt medium versus nutrient-rich culture medium) gave further insight on the mode-of-actions of their altered metabolic flexibility. Indeed, the number of differential genes expressed as a response to nutrient deprivation was significantly different depending on the pretreatment (Fig. 3A). Indicative of (metabolic) adaptability in response to nutritional challenges, the number of altered genes was highest after HEM exposure and lowest after KYN exposure. When comparing these sets of differentially expressed genes, it was observed that at least part of the effects was pretreatment-specific. UT and KYN exposure had the largest overlap with other conditions (64% and 73% of differentially expressed genes are shared), whereas HEM and HEM + KYN pretreatment induced a higher number of uniquely altered genes (70% and 57% of differentially expressed genes are not shared) (Fig. 3B).

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on the differentially expressed genes in MGt medium compared to nutrient-rich medium showed pretreatment driven changes in major pathways involved in the response to nutritional challenges. Figure 3C demonstrates for example that UT spheroids reacted to nutrient deprivation by increased glycolysis gene expression, in contrast to meat-derived metabolite pretreatments. Furthermore, we observed a higher role for OXPHOS and FA metabolism in HEM + KYN pretreated spheroids compared to the other conditions, as well as in shifts in other gene sets including hypoxia and MYC targets.

To investigate whether these metabolic adaptations were associated with changes in lipid accumulation within the spheroids, intracellular lipids were stained by AdipoRed (Fig. 3D-E). As a control, we included BUT treatment, which produced a slight, non-significant increase in triglyceride levels (Figure S4E-F). In contrast, meat-derived metabolites induced significant changes in spheroid lipid content. Decreased lipid storage was observed upon HEM-exposure, whereas it was increased by KYN and the combination of HEM + KYN. Additionally, cancer cells may also rely on glycogen when the external supply of nutrients is limited. We observed that individually, HEM and KYN cause upregulation of the glycogen synthase 1 (GYS1) gene, responsible for glycogenesis, when in MGcGtP medium (Fig. 3F). In contrast, the combination treatment of HEM + KYN had no significant effect. No impact of pretreatment was found for expression levels of PYGL, encoding for the glycogen degradation enzyme glycogen phosphorylase B, in both media.

Changes in the differentially expressed genes suggest an interactive metabolic effect of HEM + KYN treatment

Sub-selections of genes were made based on the definition of hallmark gene sets by the MSigDB database30. As such, the impact of pretreatment was assessed on specific hallmark pathways related to metabolism at the gene expression level. Figure 4A shows the differentially expressed genes related to OXPHOS, glycolysis or FA metabolism in pretreated spheroids in MGt medium compared to nutrient-rich medium. Corresponding fold changes and adjusted P values for all displayed genes are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Nutrient deprivation caused differential expression of OXPHOS genes only in case of HEM pretreatment. ACAT1 and SDHC downregulation and TCIRG1 upregulation were observed. In contrast, for all pretreatments and the UT control, changes in glycolysis gene expression were found in response to nutrient deprivation. Nevertheless, specificities for each condition were found, for example, CACNA1H downregulation upon nutrient deprivation was uniquely found in UT spheroids, and VEGFA was upregulated in all conditions except for KYN (Fig. 4A, Figure S10A). Although there were no differentially expressed genes that are shared by HEM, KYN, and HEM + KYN pretreated spheroids and not by UT spheroids, implying a distinctive effect of each metabolite, it was remarkable that particularly HEM pretreatment altered the gene expression response to nutrient deprivation resulting in 8 unique differentially expressed genes (Figure S10A). Moreover, FUT8 downregulation was shared by the KYN and HEM + KYN condition, whereas EGFR was downregulated only after HEM + KYN pretreatment. A collective response within the glycolysis gene set to nutrient deprivation independent of pretreatment was ANKZF1 downregulation and DDIT4 upregulation (also by BUT pretreatment, Figure S11).

FA metabolism genes were not altered upon nutrient deprivation in case of HEM + KYN pretreatment, which is unexpected as the individual treatments both had significant effects (Fig. 4A, Figure S11B). Uniquely altered genes included MLYCD, ACSL1, SDHC, and AOC3 for HEM-pretreated spheroids and D2HGDH for KYN-pretreated spheroids.

Moreover, HEM + KYN pretreatment increased the expression of glucose and KYN transporters (Fig. 4B), with the affected transporter type depending on the richness of the medium. Although upregulation of these transporters suggests an increased capacity for extracellular substrate availability, direct uptake of KYN or HEM was not quantitatively assessed and uptake-related interpretations should therefore be made with caution.

Discussion

This study explored how red meat-derived metabolites influence CRC cell metabolism under chronic, low-dose exposure. The applied doses were selected to reflect physiologically relevant, sub-toxic concentrations, allowing assessment of chronic exposure effects on CRC metabolism and proliferation. BUT was applied below 100 µM based on oxidative stress and viability assays, consistent with reports of short-term anti-tumor activity at concentrations < 1 mM28. HEM was tested at 0.1–1 µM, in line with estimates from average red meat intake and previous experimental studies12,27,31–33. KYN dosing was guided by cytotoxicity data and reported levels around 10 µM in CRC models and tissues16,34,35. These concentrations remain below acute toxicity thresholds but are suitable to mimic chronic exposure that promotes metabolic adaptation rather than direct mutagenesis, thereby increasing translational value. HCT116 cells were selected for their well-characterized genetics, robust spheroid formation, and relevance to CRC progression. Their intact APC gene confers sensitivity to BUT-induced apoptosis, and their high invasive potential makes them particularly suited for studying dietary metabolite effects on tumor metabolism in a physiologically relevant 3D context36,37.

We found that HEM, KYN, and their combination drive distinct bioenergetic adaptations (Figure S12). Our findings suggest that the metabolic adaptations induced by HEM and KYN are largely dependent on repeated low-dose exposure, as acute treatments primarily elicit transient or modest effects and may not capture longer-term adaptive reprogramming relevant to chronic dietary intake. HEM supported glycolysis and mitochondrial respiration under nutrient stress, potentially by promoting glycogenesis during nutrient-rich conditions. While HEM has been linked to CRC risk through its role in lipid oxidation products and N-nitroso compound (NOC) formation or by inducing cytotoxicity and genotoxicity12,38,39, its impact on tumor bioenergetics has remained less clear. Here, we show that repeated low-dose HEM exposure in nutrient-rich conditions stimulated the glycolysis-to-OXPHOS ratio, a feature of malignancy40. HEM improved metabolic flexibility by distributing glucose between glycolysis and the TCA cycle, supplemented by glutamine and fatty acid utilization, suggesting its main effect occurs at the glycolysis pathway (Figure S12A). Chronic exposure may enhance glucose flux toward glycolysis, with more pyruvate converted to lactate rather than acetyl-CoA, consistent with reported effects of HEM on enzymes such as PDH and on glucose uptake33. The flexibility observed after glucose uptake inhibition and the changes in glycolysis-related gene expression suggest that alternative pyruvate sources, particularly amino acids such as glutamine41, are redirected toward lactate production following HEM exposure.

KYN conditioned CRC cells to better withstand nutrient limitations by using oxidative fuels such as glutamine and FAs. Under nutrient-rich conditions, KYN-pretreated and untreated spheroids displayed only minor differences, with a slightly higher dependency on glucose and glutamine for mitochondrial metabolism in the former. As in most cancer cell lines, both glycolysis and TCA cycle contribute to bioenergetics42,43 and KYN did not alter this balance when nutrients were abundant. Nutrient deprivation, however, revealed stronger shifts, including sustained glycolysis, higher mitochondrial respiration with glucose present, and increased oxidative metabolism supported by glutamine (Figure S12B). Unlike HEM, KYN mainly affected oxidative metabolism, indicating a distinct mode of action. The increase in lipid storage in KYN-exposed cells further provided an oxidative substrate pool to buffer against nutrient scarcity. Beyond metabolism, KYN acts as an AHR agonist and activator of Akt and Wnt signaling17, and AHR inhibition has been shown to deregulate CRC metabolism and fatty acid synthesis18. External KYN supply also expands kynurenine pathway intermediates, leading to NAD⁺ production, which supports glycolysis, mitochondrial function, PARP activity, and may contribute to chemotherapy resistance44,45. Thus, dietary KYN or its precursors could reinforce multiple processes that favor CRC progression.

BUT exposure favored oxidative metabolism fueled by butyrate itself, glucose, and glutamine. Under nutrient deprivation, BUT-pretreated cells also mobilized intracellular reserves toward OXPHOS, indicating a more balanced metabolic profile. As a known HDAC inhibitor, BUT regulates metabolism through several mechanisms, including through the cells’ phosphotransfer networks23, AMPK activation46, inhibition of PKM2, modulation of PDH expression47, and GPR109a signaling21,48,49. Its well-characterized bioenergetic effects validate its use as a control treatment against which the less understood effects of HEM and KYN could be benchmarked.

The combined exposure to HEM and KYN produced highly energetic CRC cells that maintained OCR and ECAR under nutrient deprivation, with pyruvate and lipid metabolism playing central roles (Fig. 4C). These cells displayed broad metabolic flexibility, sustaining glycolysis without compromising the TCA cycle and supported by increased lipid storage and glycogenesis. Both processes are recognized as promoting CRC cell survival under stress conditions and facilitating metastasis50,51. We suggest that pyruvate and fatty acid metabolism are key to this adaptability, as indicated by extracellular flux data and gene expression profiles. Because pyruvate metabolism is often deregulated in cancer52, everting this state typically requires high energy expenditure, but HEM + KYN exposure may allow cancer cells to bypass this cost by channeling amino acids into pyruvate production, thereby enhancing flexibility (Figure S12C).

The combination’s effect on lipid metabolism appears to be driven largely by KYN. Lipid droplets supply fatty acids for phospholipid synthesis, protection against ROS53,54, and EMT-driven invasion under acidic stress51. Increased lipid storage may therefore grant additional flexibility under substrate scarcity and is a hallmark of CRC progression and stemness55,56. Compared to KYN alone, HEM + KYN-pretreated spheroids shifted toward higher glycolytic activity while maintaining OXPHOS, even when glucose uptake was blocked, likely by deriving pyruvate from amino acids or TCA intermediates57. This dynamic adaptability may also relate to heme’s role as a cofactor in electron transport and oxygen handling58.

Finally, the interaction between HEM and KYN may be self-sustaining. We observed higher expression of KYN-associated transporters following combined treatment, and KYN itself has been shown to stimulate SLC7A11 expression to further enhance import59. HEM + KYN pretreatment also upregulated MYC target genes, consistent with reports that MYC promotes tryptophan transporter expression and KYN pathway activity, thereby increasing intracellular KYN levels16. Although Seahorse-based perturbations primarily interrogate downstream substrate utilization and metabolic dependency rather than transporter-mediated uptake per se, the pronounced sensitivity to glucose pathway inhibition under HEM + KYN conditions supports a functional reliance on extracellular nutrient availability. Elevated KYN has been detected in CRC patient tissues and plasma, particularly at advanced disease stages60. Together, these findings suggest that KYN acts as a driver of metabolic adaptation to changing nutritional environments, with HEM reinforcing these processes and thereby promoting cancer cell survival and progression.

Conclusion, limitations and future perspectives

This study reports a role for specific dietary metabolites in shaping the metabolic status and adaptability of CRC cells under nutrient stress. For the first time, we demonstrated an interactive effect of two major red meat metabolites on cancer cell flexibility as a mode-of-action by which the nutritional environment may promote CRC progression. These findings underscore the relevance of mode-of-action research on metabolite combinations, repeated-dose exposure and nutrient stress and point to the need for more complex and dynamic models to fully understand metabolism-induced cancer progression.

This work has certain inherent limitations. First, extracellular flux data were not normalized to protein or spheroid size. Although spheroid numbers per well were highly consistent due to the use of standardized agarose molds, minor variability cannot be excluded. Such variability is expected to be random rather than systematic, as spheroids were equally distributed across wells and signals were averaged from multiple replicates, which makes statistical comparisons conservative. Protein normalization after Seahorse assays was technically unfeasible because of Matrigel presence, and microscopic imaging could not reliably capture entire wells. Single-spheroid assays may provide a useful alternative to address this in future experiments. Second, although delayed metabolic responses suggest progressive intracellular engagement, penetration into the spheroid interior and periphery-to-core concentration gradients were not directly measured and thus cannot be quantitatively inferred. Future studies incorporating labeled metabolites, media depletion analyses, or spatially resolved readouts will be important to directly assess uptake kinetics and intra-spheroid gradients. Moreover, although transporter expression changes were observed, future studies incorporating transporter inhibition, genetic modulation, or direct uptake measurements will be required to conclusively link these changes to metabolite internalization. Finally, experiments were performed in a single CRC cell line (HCT116). This model was chosen for its well-characterized genetic background, intact APC gene, defined EMT traits, and ability to form compact spheroids, making it highly suitable for 3D culture. Nonetheless, reliance on one line restricts generalizability. Comparative studies using additional CRC cell lines with different mutations (e.g., p53) or primary cells would be valuable to confirm that the observed effects are not specific to this genetic context. In addition, while the present study focuses on metabolic plasticity in colorectal cancer spheroids, extending these analyses to normal and precancerous intestinal epithelial models will be important to delineate phenotype- and genotype-dependent metabolic responses to dietary metabolites.

Methods

Experimental model and subject details

Cells

Human colorectal (adeno)carcinoma cell lines HCT116 (ATCC® CCL-247) were cultivated in 25 cm² Corning CellBIND cell culture flasks (Greiner Bio-one, Vilvoorde, Belgium) and placed in a water-saturated atmosphere of 10% CO2 at 37 °C. For the cell growth medium, high glucose DMEM medium with GlutaMAX™ (10566016, Gibco™, Thermofisher, Merelbeke, Belgium) was supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (VWR, Leuven, Belgium), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (pen/strep) (15140122, Gibco™, Life Technologies), and 1% MEM non-essential amino acids (NEAA) solution (11140050, Gibco™, Life Technologies). The medium was refreshed every 2–3 days and at 80–90% confluency, cells were detached with 0.25% (v/v) trypsin-EDTA (25200072, Gibco™, Life Technologies) and sub-cultured in the by ATCC recommended ratio. Determination of the number of viable cells was performed in a Bürker chamber using Trypan blue Dye.

Spheroid formation

Sterile Microtissues® Petri Dish flexible molds (Sigma) were filled with a heated 2% agarose I (0710, VWR) in 0.9% (w/v) NaCl solution. Once cooled down, the agarose wells were removed from the molds and placed in 12-well plates. Cell growth medium was added to wash (3x) and equilibrate the agarose wells. Next, a single-cell suspension was added to 12-well Microtissues® agarose wells at 25,000 cells/well (131,000 cells/mL). After 30 min and a visual check that the cells settled into the agarose well cavities, additional cell growth medium was added and the cells were incubated at 37 °C and 10% CO2.

Spheroid exposure

Working solutions sodium butyrate (303410, Sigma-Aldrich), hemin (51280, Sigma-Aldrich) or L-kynurenine (K8625, Sigma-Aldrich) were prepared in demineralized water or dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO), sterilized through a 0.22 μm filter (Merck Millipore, Massachusetts, USA), and further diluted in the appropriate medium as to not exceed a final DMSO concentration of 0.1%. 24 h after seeding, the formed spheroids were exposed to the selected compounds dissolved in exposure medium (high glucose DMEM medium with GlutaMAX™ supplemented with 5% FBS and 1% pen/strep) for 7–9 days. The medium was replaced three times during that period, the last time 24 h before the assay. Visual monitoring of the spheroid growth was done with a ZEISS phase contrast microscope and camera, and ZEISS ZEN Software (Version 3.8) was used for size quantification.

Method details

Acute toxicity experiments

HT-29 (ATCC® HTB-38), Caco-2 (ATCC® HTB-37), CT26-Luc (in-house modified from ATCC® CRL-2638), and HCT8/E11 Sc1 Luc eGFP3.2 (in-house modified from ATCC® CCL-244, referred to as HCT8/E11) were cultivated and maintained under the same conditions as HCT116 cells. For HCT8/E11 cells, the medium was additionally supplemented with blasticidin (10 µg/mL) to maintain transfection. Cells were seeded at a density of 20,000 cells per well in flat-bottom, tissue culture–treated 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One) in growth medium (10% FBS, NEAA). Twenty-four hours after seeding, the medium was replaced with treatment medium containing the metabolites, prepared without FBS or NEAA. After 48 h of treatment, a Sulforhodamine B (SRB) protein assay was performed. This assay relies on SRB’s ability to bind to protein amino acid residues in adherent cells fixed with 50% trichloroacetic acid (TCA), producing absorbance signals proportional to cellular protein content.

Spheroid necrosis visualization

HCT116 spheroids were seeded at 5,000 cells per well in a 384-well ultra-low attachment (ULA) round-bottom plate (Sbio). Gibco DMEM (1 g/L glucose with pyruvate, Cat. 11885084) served as the base medium and was supplemented with 10% FBS, GlutaMax, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA) to generate low-glucose (LG) medium. High-glucose (HG) medium was prepared by adding glucose from a 1 M stock to a final concentration of 4.5 g/L. After one week, 60 µL of medium per well was replaced with 10 µM Ethidium Homodimer I (Thermo Fisher) in PBS containing Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺. Following a 30-minute incubation, spheroids were imaged using the IncuCyte SX5 live-cell analyzer.

Bradford protein assay

Spheroids encapsulated in alginate beads were harvested using citrate-EDTA buffer, which dissolved the bead matrix and released the spheroids without compromising cell viability. After gentle centrifugation, 250 µL 0.1% Triton X-100 was added, and the suspension was agitated for 20 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 5 µL of the lysate was transferred to a 96-well plate. Then, 250 µL of Bradford reagent was added, and the plate was incubated for approximately 30 min before measuring absorbance at 595 nm.

Nutritional challenge of pretreated spheroids

The HEM, KYN, HEM + KYN or BUT-pretreated spheroids were transferred to different media for 3 h, composed of Seahorse XF DMEM medium (pH 7.4) (M) supplemented with glucose (10 mM) (Gc) (Agilent), L-glutamine (2 mM) (Gt) (Sigma), and/or pyruvate (1 mM) (P) (Agilent). Spheroids were washed twice with PBS before adding the appropriate medium. The spheroids were incubated in these media for 3 h at 37 °C and 10% CO2 before continuing with the analyses.

The differences in composition of each medium, as well as a comparison to physiological plasma levels and the DMEM medium used for cell cultivation can be found in Figure S13. Extracellular flux analysis was performed in all the media, and RNAseq on MGcGtP and MGt medium. Specific metabolic stressors were applied to block key transporters or enzymes in one of the substrate oxidation pathways (glycolysis, glutaminolysis, and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) (Figure S14A-B). The assay also includes sequential injection of ETC modulators (Figure S14C).

Extracellular flux analysis

Optimization of spheroid seeding and adhesion to Seahorse plates

Extracellular flux analysis was performed using Agilent Seahorse real-time cell metabolic analyzers and consumables. We used flat-bottom Seahorse plates which required additional optimization, as the O2- and pH-sensors move up and down in the well causing turbulence in the medium, which would suspend the spheroids and result in highly variable results. Therefore, a layout was tested where the spheroids were immobilized in a thin layer of Matrigel (growth factor reduced, 1553620, Corning) at the bottom of the well. A 96-well Seahorse XF96 cell culture microplate was coated with 5 µL Matrigel on ice, after which 10 µL spheroid suspension was added (resulting in a 30% Matrigel solution). The plate was placed at 37 °C for 15 min to allow the gel to set and fixate the spheroids before the additional medium was added to a total volume of 185 µL. The assay medium was Seahorse XF DMEM medium (pH 7.4) (Agilent) supplemented with glucose (10 mM), L-glutamine (2 mM), and pyruvate (1 mM). Microscopic pictures were taken to verify homogenous spheroid counts and fixation.

Substrate oxidation stress test assay

The Seahorse XF96 (Agilent) apparatus and the accompanying consumables (plates, cartridges, inhibitor kits) were used for measurements. On the day of the analysis, the spheroids were washed 3 times with assay medium, suspended, and seeded onto a Matrigel-coated Seahorse assay 96-well plate. Microscopic pictures were taken of each well to assure an even distribution of spheroids between different wells. The Seahorse cartridge was prepared according to the guidelines described in the Seahorse substrate oxidation assay kit manual (Agilent)61. In short, the cartridge fits on top of the Seahorse 96-well plate and provides 4 injection ports for each well, which allows the user to inject 4 stressors, inhibitors, or stimulators at different time points, whilst a pH-sensor and O2-sensor measure the effect on extracellular acidification and O2 consumption in real-time in each well. The cartridge was hydrated overnight in sterile H2O in a CO2-free environment at 37 °C and placed in XF calibrating solution for at least 1 h before the assay. The injection strategy included 4 sequential injections, which is established by 4 injection ports in each well (A to D), of which the content is added to the well at a certain time point during the assay. After each injection, there were 4 cycles of equilibration and measurement, resulting in the different data points on the kinetic profile. For port A, we varied the stressor that was injected to investigate the effect on different metabolic routes in the cells (referred to as “metabolic stressors”), as shown in Table 1. An excess of 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG) competes with glucose for the GLUT-transporters at the cell membrane and is not further metabolized into glucose-6-phosphate (G-6-P), thereby limiting glucose transport and metabolism. UK5099 inhibits pyruvate transport into the mitochondria and therefore avoids that glucose fuels the TCA cycle. BPTES (bis-2-(5-phenylacetamido-1,3,4-thiadiazol-2-yl)ethyl sulfide) is a glutaminase (GLS1) inhibitor. Etomoxir inhibits fatty acid oxidation (FAO) by binding to carnitine palmitoyl-transferase 1a (CPT1a), an enzyme that binds carnitine to long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs) to allow transport to the mitochondria. As a reference, the assay medium was added to port A of some wells, and all dilutions were made in this assay medium.

The injection ports B, C, and D contained oligomycin, FCCP, and rotenone/antimycin A respectively, which specifically target a step in the ETC (referred to as “mitochondrial stressors”). Oligomycin inhibits ATPase activity and therefore blocks the ADP to ATP conversion. Next, FCCP exposure eliminates the mitochondrial membrane potential and the proton gradient, resulting in the uncoupling of ATP production and oxygen consumption. Finally, a mixture of rotenone and antimycin A (Rot/AA) was used to shut down the oxidative respiration in the mitochondria completely by inhibiting respiratory complexes I and III, respectively.

After the extracellular flux assay, representative microscopic pictures were taken to check the intactness and homogenous distribution of the spheroids in the different conditions. In terms of replicates, each condition was tested in at least 4 replicates, and in case of M, MGt, and MGcGtP media repeated at least 2 times.

Lipid staining

The AdipoRed™ Assay Reagent (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) accumulates in intracellular lipid droplets and becomes fluorescent in a hydrophobic environment, allowing measurement of intracellular triglycerides. The spheroids were washed 3x with PBS and fixated with 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C. Then, 2.5% AdipoRed reagent was added for 15 min in the dark. After a washing step with PBS and DAPI staining, the spheroids were washed 3x with PBS, suspended in mounting medium, and transferred to glass slides for imaging with fluorescence microscopy.

Quantification of mean fluorescent intensity (MFI) of lipid droplet staining was performed in ImageJ. The MFI of AdipoRed™ and DAPI staining was measured for a region of interest. Background fluorescence was subtracted from each value before calculating the AdipoRed/DAPI ratio for a normalized output.

RNA sequencing

RNA isolation

8-day old pretreated spheroids were subdivided into two groups. Spheroids in group 1 (“nutrient-rich medium”) were washed 3 times with PBS, suspended, and transferred to a low-binding Eppendorf tube. The spheroids settled by gravity and the supernatant was discarded. The pellets were stored at −80 °C for further processing. For the spheroids in group 2, the medium was replaced with Seahorse XF DMEM medium (pH 7.4) supplemented with L-glutamine (2 mM), after 2 washing steps with PBS. The plates were incubated for 3 h at 37 °C in a CO2-free environment. This timing was applied to match the timeline of the extracellular flux analysis experiments, and thus to enable consolidation of outcomes. Next, the same procedure as for group 1 was applied: the spheroids were washed and collected. RNA extraction was performed with the RNeasy® Mini Kit (Qiagen), following the instruction handbook. In short, 350 µL lysis Buffer RLT was used to lyse the spheroids, 350 µL of 70% ethanol was added, and the total volume was transferred to an RNeasy Mini spin column placed in a collection tube to be centrifuged at 8,000 g for 15 s. The flow through was discarded, and the column was washed with Buffer RW1 and Buffer RPE, before eluting the RNA in 30 µL RNase-free water.

RNA quantification

A Qubit® RNA BR (broad range) assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to determine the RNA content in each sample. In short, all samples were diluted 1:100 in 0.5 mL PCR tubes using a Qubit Working Solution, vortexed, and incubated at room temperature for 2 min. A Qubit 2.0 fluorimeter was first calibrated using the standards provided in the kit before reading the samples.

DNase treatment

A DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific) treatment was applied to remove single- and double-stranded DNA in the samples. For each µg of RNA in the sample, we added 1 µL 10X reaction buffer with MgCl2, 1 U DNase I RNase-free, and 8 µL DEPC-treated Water. After incubation at 37 °C for 30 min, 1 µL EDTA (50 mM) was added to avoid RNA hydrolysis and the samples were incubated at 65 °C for 10 min and stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

RNA processing, sequencing, and analysis

The total extracted RNA’s concentration and quality were checked using the ‘Quant-it ribogreen RNA assay’ (Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY, USA) and the RNA 6000 nano chip and pico chip (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), respectively. The RIN values were between 8.1 and 10 for all samples. Subsequently, 48 ng of RNA was used to perform an Illumina sequencing library preparation using the QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq Library Prep FWD Kit (Lexogen, Vienna, Austria) including Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMI) according to the manufacturer’s protocol using 18 enrichment PCR cycles. Libraries were quantified by qPCR, according to Illumina’s protocol ‘Sequencing Library qPCR Quantification protocol guide’, version February 2011. A High sensitivity DNA chip (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, US) was used to control the library’s size distribution and quality. Libraries were equimolarly pooled and sequenced on an Illumina Nextseq 500 flow cell together with 20% PhiX Sequencing Control library, generating 75 bp single end reads. On average, 6.77 × 106 ± 1.3 × 106 reads were generated per sample. The FASTQ files of the samples were processed using the RNA-seq pipeline version 3.5 on the nf-core framework for community-curated bioinformatics pipelines62. This analysis workflow is designed to be highly reproducible and is implemented using the Nextflow63 workflow engine. The pipeline takes raw RNA-seq data from FASTQ files as input and performs several steps to process and analyze the data. These steps include quality control of the FASTQ files using FastQC version 0.11.9. and UMI extraction using UMI-Tools64 version 1.1.2. Adapter sequences are trimmed from the reads using Cutadapt65 version 3.4. The reads are aligned to a human reference genome GRCh38 using the splice-aware STAR mapper66 version 2.6.1d. Features were counted at the gene level as defined by GRCh38 using Salmon67 version 1.5.2.

For downstream statistical analyses, only genes expressed at a counts-per-million (cpm) above 1 in at least 5 samples were retained. Principal Component Analysis (PCA)68 and heat map plots69 were generated using R on rlog transformed70 counts to explore if the samples from different treatment groups clustered together and to detect outlier samples. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using edgeR’s71 quasi-likelihood method. Genes with and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and a fold change of 2 were considered significantly differential. PCA and cluster plots, using only the differentially expressed genes, were also created.

The GAGE R package72 was used for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) based on the human gene ontology (GO) database h.all.v7.0.entrez.gmt, and the KEGG database73–75 in the R GAGE package itself. GO-terms and KEGG pathways were considered differentially expressed between conditions when the log fold change of the genes in the GO-term or pathway was significantly different with a GSEA FDR smaller than 0.05. Differentially expressed KEGG pathways were visualized using Pathview76, showing the log fold change of each enzyme in the pathway. Additionally, GSEA was performed using the GSEA software (v4.2.3) and Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) Hallmark Gene Signatures30. For each pretreatment, the enrichment of hallmark gene sets in the nutrient-deprived medium compared to the nutrient-rich medium was tested. Gene sets were considered significantly enriched when q-value FDR < 0.05, and normalized enrichment scores (NES) were used for further calculations of e.g., z-scores across the pretreatments. Gene subsets were also based on the MSigDB Hallmark gene signatures. The differentially expressed genes within these subsets were identified through the same workflow as described above for the whole dataset.

Quantification and statistical analysis

General statistical analysis

SPSS software (version 25.0, SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) or R (i386 4.1.2) was used for statistical analysis. To compare the effect of the pretreatments on the outcome parameter in comparison to the untreated condition, one-way ANOVA was performed. The appropriate post-hoc test was selected depending on the homoscedasticity (checked with Levene’s test): Tukey SD or Dunnett-t2 test. Additionally, independent Student’s t-tests were used to compare two conditions. In all tests, the significance level was set at 95% (α = 5%), and changes were considered significant if p < 0.05. In the figures, significant differences are shown by *, **, or *** if p < 0.05, p < 0.01, or p < 0.001 respectively.

Modeling of Seahorse data

The data from different runs (biological replicates, defined as plates ran at a different moment in time and using cells from a different passage number) were exported and pooled for each combination of pretreatment, medium composition, and stressor. For each run, at least 3 technical replicates were included (different wells). Data analysis, significance testing, and visualization were performed in the software R (i386 4.1.2).

After each injection, the OCR and ECAR were measured at 4 time points with a 4-minute equilibration period in between. Given that the general profile is decreasing over time, and that potential jumps will influence the slope, we continued by modeling these curves with linear and quadratic regression analysis. Based on interaction testing for both models, the linear regression analysis proved to be sufficient to attribute the variability of the systems when including three variables (medium type, inhibitor in port A, and pretreatment). Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s multiple comparison tests were applied to the fitted models comparing pretreatments within the same medium and stressor, with the level of significance set at 0.05. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant and shown as * for p < 0.05, ** for p < 0.01, and *** for p < 0.001. The relative effect of the treatment vs. the reference/control was quantified by the models’ effects (intercepts). The control groups were defined for each parameter: untreated for the parameter ‘pretreatment’, medium injection for the parameter ‘injected stressor in port A’, and nutrient-rich medium (containing glucose, pyruvate, and glutamine) for the parameter ‘medium composition’. For visualization, only the significantly different quantified effects were displayed, and a dual color scale was applied to create a heat map expressing the magnitude of the difference compared to the control.
