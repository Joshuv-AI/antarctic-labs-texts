Using Real-World Data to Support Claim Expansion of the Cobas SARS-CoV-2 Test in Asymptomatic Testing: Data from the National Football League Occupational Surveillance Program
Authors: Baiyu Yang, Erin B Wasserman, Akos Somoskovi, Daniel Jarem, Patrick Volkir, Christina Mack, Elodie Baumfeld Andre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionClinical evidence is needed for performance of novel coronavirus disease (COVID-19) molecular tests in asymptomatic populations to support regulatory decisions. To support claim expansion of a direct molecular test, clinical use and performance of the Cobas® SARS-CoV-2 qualitative test for use on Cobas 5800/6800/8800 Systems (“candidate test”) for detecting SARS-CoV-2 in individuals who are asymptomatic were investigated.MethodsThis study leveraged real-world data from the 2020 National Football League (NFL) COVID-19 Surveillance Program during August 1, 2020–January 3, 2021. NFL players and staff from 32 clubs had nasal samples prospectively collected on a near-daily basis. Samples underwent reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) testing by a single provider across five laboratories. The study population included individuals whose samples were tested on the candidate test. A comparator algorithm was constructed based on comparator test results and clinical adjudication within the NFL program. Results from the candidate test were compared against the comparator algorithm to calculate positive and negative percent agreements (PPA and NPA). Two-sided 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for PPA and NPA were determined (Wilson score method).ResultsA total of 1776 samples were included in the final analysis. All 11 samples with positive comparator status were positive on the candidate test (PPA 100% [95% CI 74.1, 100]). Of 1765 samples with negative comparator status, 1762 were negative on the candidate test (NPA 99.8% [95% CI 99.5, 99.9]). Results were consistent when stratified by age and sex.ConclusionsBased on data collected during this occupational testing protocol, our study provides strong evidence supporting real-world performance of this test in an asymptomatic population. These findings, complemented by data from an existing clinical study, provided the totality-of-evidence that supported the US Food and Drug Administration clearance for the expanded claim of the candidate test for the qualitative detection of SARS-CoV-2 in the asymptomatic population.Keywords: Asymptomatic population, Claim expansion, Clinical performance, Diagnostic assay, Negative percent agreement, Occupational testing, Positive percent agreement, Real-world data, SARS-CoV-2

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? During the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID 19) pandemic, the Cobas® SARS CoV 2 qualitative test was brought to the U.S. market as an in vitro diagnostic (IVD) assay for the detection of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS CoV 2) under the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) process.With the end of the public health emergency status, in 2022 the FDA granted 510(k) clearance for this test to be used in symptomatic patients suspected of having COVID-19. Additional clinical evidence was required to expand this claim to include asymptomatic testing.This analysis investigated whether the Cobas SARS-CoV-2 qualitative test could accurately detect SARS-CoV-2 in individuals who are asymptomatic, in support of regulatory decisions for claim expansion. What was learned from the study? In this analysis of real-world data from 1776 samples, the candidate test had a positive percent agreement of 100% and a negative percent agreement of 99.8% compared to a gold standard.Robust real-world evidence reinforced the performance of the Cobas SARS-CoV-2 test, supporting FDA clearance for an expanded claim allowing its use in detection of SARS-CoV-2 in individuals who are asymptomatic.This study represents a significant breakthrough using real-world data for regulatory clearance of an IVD assay.

Introduction

Since the initial outbreak in December 2019, the novel coronavirus disease (COVID-19) has rapidly evolved into a global pandemic. An estimated 1.2 million people had died of COVID-19 in the USA as of May 2025 [1]. The testing positivity rate, as of April 2025, was 3.6% in the USA [2], with suggestions from experts that COVID-19 will continue to play a major role in our lives for the foreseeable future [3].

During the pandemic, many molecular diagnostic tests for SARS-CoV-2 were brought to the market in the USA under the emergency use authorization (EUA) process, which allows the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to facilitate the availability and use of medical products needed during public health emergencies. The Cobas® SARS-CoV-2 qualitative test for use on the Cobas 5800/6800/8800 Systems (“candidate test”; Roche Molecular Systems, Branchburg, New Jersey, USA) was first granted EUA in March 2020 [4] and was amended in April 2021. This authorized the test to be used for qualitative detection of nucleic acids from SARS-CoV-2 in any individuals, including those suspected of COVID-19 by their healthcare provider and those without symptoms or other reasons to suspect COVID-19.

With the end of the public health emergency status in the USA in 2023, in vitro diagnostics (IVD) developers were obligated to pursue an FDA premarket submission pathway to ensure the continuous availability of the test on the market. In October 2022, the FDA granted 510(k) clearance for this test to be used in nasal and nasopharyngeal samples from patients who are symptomatic who are suspected of having COVID-19 as determined by their healthcare provider. Additional clinical evidence was needed to expand this claim to include asymptomatic testing, which is recommended by several healthcare authorities in certain situations. For example, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has recommended that patients who are asymptomatic with close contact with someone with SARS-CoV-2 infection should have a series of three viral tests for SARS-CoV-2 infection over five days [5]. In addition, the European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID) has issued a guideline that recommends testing in individuals who are asymptomatic in healthcare facilities in some scenarios, with the aim to prevent SARS-CoV-2 transmissions in these settings [6].

A few studies have examined the performance of the candidate test in asymptomatic testing; generally, these studies suggested good agreement between the candidate test and other available tests in asymptomatic populations [7–9]. For example, a study in Columbus, Ohio, reported high concordance between the results of the Cobas 6800 System and the DiaSorin Molecular Simplexa COVID-19 Direct RT-PCR (reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction) assay in an asymptomatic population with known exposure to COVID-19 or testing for preoperative screening [7]. However, none of these studies were designed in the manner typical of data that is intended to be used for regulatory purposes. Equally important as direct comparison between tests was the observed performance of the candidate test as a diagnostic in a public health setting, which was observed broadly, and published by several closely monitored populations, including the National Basketball Association (NBA) Bubble [10, 11] and the 2020–2022 National Football League (NFL) seasons [12–14].

To generate evidence suitable for regulatory purposes for the label expansion, we leveraged real-world data (RWD) from the 2020 NFL COVID-19 Surveillance Program [14]. The program was designed to enable the start of the 2020 NFL season by implementing comprehensive, evidence-based monitoring protocols. RT-PCR testing was performed across 32 NFL clubs (players, club staff, and any individual present at a club facility) across the USA, mirroring 32 distinct communities. The majority of individuals were tested on a near-daily basis by a single provider across five US laboratories; results were ready within 24 h. Mack et al. described the NFL COVID-19 Surveillance Program in full in their publication in Annals of Internal Medicine [14]. Because 15 of the 32 clubs had samples sent to a laboratory that used the Cobas SARS-CoV-2 test, we conducted additional analyses of this dataset with a focus on the candidate test. The goal was to evaluate the clinical performance of the candidate test against the comparator status (based on results from comparator tests and clinical adjudication) in a real-world asymptomatic population, and generate evidence to support its claim expansion in asymptomatic testing.

Methods

Data Source

This study leveraged data from the NFL COVID-19 surveillance program for the 2020 NFL season. In this program, a positive test result triggered adjudication of final case status based on an algorithm developed by the medical and operational consensus team [14].

For individuals with consistent test results, a recommendation was made to the consensus group based on subsequent testing and follow-up actions, and the case was then reviewed by the medical team before assignment of final case status. For individuals with inconsistent follow-up PCR results, the consensus team considered many other factors, such as follow-up test results, previous SARS-CoV-2 infection, point-of-care test results, serology test results, and potential exposure both within and outside of the NFL environment, as well as cycle threshold values from the RT-PCR tests.

The consensus team was composed of league-level physicians, epidemiologists, and laboratory testing experts serving as consultants to the NFL and who were not affiliated with any specific club. As such, they had no incentive to adjudicate cases in a direction that could cause football-related advantages. Their goal was to make an objective, data-driven decision to ensure appropriate clinical care and isolation of infected individuals to limit the spread of SARS-CoV-2, while allowing those who were not sick to continue normal activities. Individuals with a negative test result were assumed to be SARS-CoV-2 negative, as they were tested 6–7 times per week and monitored for symptoms daily.

The NFL COVID-19 monitoring program captured each individual’s symptom status at the time of positive test result receipt. Each club was responsible for reporting the symptom status and clinical information of individuals with a positive test to trained contact tracers, who presented symptom status and test results with the medical consensus team. For individuals testing negative, symptoms were not reported to the centralized database. Given the strict behavioral protocols in place [13], close monitoring and central governance, and documented compliance of the medical staff under employment rules [13], there is confidence that the individuals with negative test results were predominantly asymptomatic and/or infection was ruled out through diagnostics and clinical evaluation.

Per FDA guidance [15], RWD used for regulatory purposes must demonstrate sufficient relevance and reliability to generate real-world evidence for regulatory decision-making. Given its high data quality, the NFL dataset was considered “fit-for-purpose” for the evaluation of COVID-19 test performance in a real-world setting. To create the “totality of evidence,” the NFL data was complemented by data from an existing clinical study, the Test Us At Home (TUAH) study [16], which became available through a partnership with the FDA. The TUAH study was originally designed to evaluate the antigen test performance against a composite PCR comparator consisting of three PCR tests including the candidate test. We performed a re-analysis of the data by comparing the candidate test result against the other two PCR tests as the comparator. For the purpose of this manuscript, the TUAH analysis will not be presented in detail.

This analysis was conducted in accordance with applicable regulations. As the data used were from an occupational monitoring program (as opposed to a research study), no separate institutional review board (IRB) submission was required, and individuals were not required to provide written, informed consent. Both the NFL and the NFL Players Association (NFLPA) approved the submission of the master data file to the FDA.

Study Population

The study population was sourced from individuals from the 15 clubs whose samples were tested on the candidate test. Because there was no adjudication for those tested negative within the NFL program, we created a study cohort that was restricted to scenarios in which there was additional information to validate the comparator status (defined under the “Determination of Comparator Status” section) for each individual.

Each individual was tested repeatedly; therefore, the cohort was built on the “adjudication episode” level (i.e., each test and the accompanying adjudication/comparator status) rather than the individual person level. Adjudication episodes were eligible for inclusion in the cohort if more than one sample was collected from the same person on the same day between August 1, 2020 and January 3, 2021, with one sample tested on the candidate test and at least one other sample tested on a comparator test, both on the first day of this episode. This allowed for additional information to determine the comparator status (please see more details in the section below). On the basis of FDA feedback, additional samples were included if the first positive result (day N) was on a comparator test, but the individual had another sample collected and tested on the candidate test on the day after the first positive test, day (N + 1). Comparator tests in the NFL dataset included Aptima® SARS-CoV-2 Assay from Hologic (EUA200734), TaqPath™ COVID-19 Combo Kit from Thermo Fisher Scientific (EUA200010), Accula™ SARS-CoV-2 Test from Mesa Biotech, now part of Thermo Fisher Scientific (EUA200028), and the Sofia SARS Antigen FIA (EUA200742). All samples were collected using anterior nasal swabs, and there were no known deviations from authorized protocols.

There were 11,280 samples from the candidate test that were accompanied by at least one other same-day sample tested with a comparator test. Three additional samples were collected on day (N + 1) following a day N positive result on a comparator test and had sufficient data to be included in the analysis, per FDA’s recommendation during the review. Out of these 11,283 samples, 33 were duplicate samples from the same individual on the same day, both of which were tested with the candidate platform; the duplicates were excluded. Another 170 samples were collected after a prior-day positive test for the purpose of adjudicating the case status and were therefore also excluded.

The remaining 11,080 samples represented the initial testing in potentially eligible adjudication episodes. Episodes were excluded if the positive test results were determined to be due to lab contamination or viral shedding (persistent positive; N = 2); the individual was known to be symptomatic on the day of the candidate test result (~ 12–24 h following sample collection; N = 14); and the samples were collected from non-players and non-staff individuals (e.g., contractors), as they did not go through the medical adjudication process to determine case status following a positive test result (N = 702). Additionally, per FDA feedback, all samples with only an antigen test comparator were excluded from final analysis because of known limitations in the sensitivity of antigen tests in asymptomatic populations (N = 6959) [17, 18]. After the exclusion criteria were applied (Fig. 1; cases where only antigen-based tests were available have been included in this figure in order to reflect the total scope of the surveillance program, though these were excluded from the final analysis to maintain a PCR-to-PCR comparison), a total of 3403 episodes remained in the study population.

Determination of Comparator Status

A comparator algorithm was created following the guidance from the FDA, as shown in Table 1. Regardless of the candidate test result at day N, the “comparator status at day N” was determined by the algorithm based on a combination of the comparator test results and the clinical adjudication status from the NFL COVID-19 Monitoring Program. Per FDA’s feedback, if the comparator test on day N was Accula or TaqPath and the result was negative, a second negative result on the following day was required in order to classify the comparator status as negative; if the second-day result was not available, the comparator status would be considered “undetermined.” If the comparator test on day N was Aptima and the result was negative, the final comparator status would be negative regardless of the clinical adjudication result. If the comparator test on day N was positive, the final comparator status would be determined according to the clinical adjudication results.

Statistical Analysis

The algorithm was applied to each of the 3403 episodes to assign the comparator status. Subsequently, qualitative test results from the candidate test were compared to the assigned comparator status. Positive percent agreement (PPA) was calculated as (number of episodes with positive candidate test results and positive comparator status ÷ total number of episodes with positive comparator status) × 100%. Similarly, negative percent agreement (NPA) was calculated as (number of episodes with negative candidate test results and negative comparator status ÷ total number of episodes with negative comparator status) × 100%. PPA and NPA were calculated for the overall cohort, as well as stratified by age and sex. Calculations were performed using the statistical programming language R version 4.1.0 [19] and SAS Enterprise Guide version 8.2 [20]. Two-sided 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for PPA and NPA were determined using the Wilson score method [21].

PPA and NPA in the Overall Cohort

Of the 3403 episodes, 13 had an algorithm-determined positive comparator status on day N, 1765 had a negative comparator status, and 1625 had an “undetermined” comparator status. For two of the episodes with a positive comparator status at day N, they had missing data for symptom status on the date of positive test result and therefore were not included in the PPA analysis based on FDA’s feedback. After removing these two episodes, as well as the 1625 episodes with an “undetermined” comparator status, the final sample size for the PPA and NPA calculations was 1776 samples among 737 unique individuals. Resultant data are shown in Table 3. All 11 samples with a positive comparator status were positive on the candidate test, leading to a PPA of 100% (11/11; 95% CI 74.1, 100). Of 1765 samples with a negative comparator status, 1762 were negative on the candidate test while 3 were positive, leading to a NPA of 99.8% (1762/1765; 95% CI 99.5, 99.9).

Discussion

Based on RWD from the 2020 NFL Surveillance Program, this study demonstrated that the clinical performance of the candidate test was adequate for the routine diagnostic setting to proactively test individuals who are asymptomatic, allowing timely public health interventions. While the NFL population mainly consisted of young, male individuals, which could be viewed as a potential limitation of the generalizability of the study, results were consistent when stratified by age and sex, similar to a previous report [8]. Since the candidate test is using the same well-established sample collection for any patient, and was validated on an adequate proportion of low positive specimens as part of the EUA authorization, there is no biological or medical reason to believe that the candidate test would perform differently by age or sex, further supporting the generalizability of study findings. Findings from this study and the aforementioned TUAH study collectively provided the “totality-of-evidence” that led to FDA clearance for the expanded claim of this test for the qualitative detection of SARS-CoV-2 in the asymptomatic population in February 2025.

As regulators across the globe expand decision-making to data sources beyond clinical trials, these data provide a strong example of the possibilities of real-world data to enable impactful decisions. Over the past few years, RWD has been increasingly accepted by regulators for product approval or label expansion, and experts have called for a broader framework to assess RWD in regulatory decision-making in light of its unique nature compared to traditional clinical trials [22]. However, the successful use of RWD for IVD is still relatively rare, with only eight examples presented by FDA during 2012–2019 [23]. This study represents a significant breakthrough using RWD for regulatory clearance of an IVD, and the ability to leverage two existing datasets collected during the pandemic represents an innovative and efficient method of evidence generation without having to conduct a new study.

To our knowledge, this is the largest population-based cohort with longitudinal follow-up for the evaluation of COVID-19 assays. The test data prospectively collected through the NFL COVID-19 Monitoring Program, along with behavioral protocols and contact tracing, allowed for early identification of infection and mitigation of spread within facilities [13, 24], and enabled a real-world evaluation of the candidate test performance. Because a test result was required for entry into club facilities, there is very little missing data, and data quality was monitored. Based on the FDA’s feedback, a rigorous algorithm was developed to construct the comparator status for each sample; whenever applicable, the algorithm incorporated the clinical adjudication completed as part of the NFL COVID-19 monitoring program. The clinical adjudication was thorough and provided strong evidence for the true infection status, and it was a unique component of the NFL program, which would rarely be available from a commercial RWD source. Of note, the clinical adjudication was made based on all information available, including the candidate test result, and it was reasonable to suspect that dependency on the candidate test result may have biased the clinical adjudication results in some episodes. However, using the algorithm largely mitigated this concern, as in many episodes the comparator status was determined solely based on the independent comparator test result (e.g., there were two episodes where the sample was negative on Aptima, and the final comparator status was considered negative despite the positive clinical adjudication result). It would only remain a concern if the clinical adjudication agreed with the candidate test result but differed from the comparator test result, and the clinical adjudication was used as the final comparator status. This was true for only two episodes within row 2 of Table 2, where the clinical adjudication was negative and concordant with the negative candidate test result, despite the positive comparator test; however, the results remained unchanged in a sensitivity analysis when these episodes were considered as “undetermined” and excluded from analyses (results not shown). Overall, the breadth and scale of these data are unlikely to be repeated, and they provide unprecedented value to generate evidence demonstrating the real-world performance of COVID-19 tests. It is of note that, while antigen tests are valuable for rapid diagnosis, particularly in decentralized and at-home scenarios, they have reduced sensitivity compared to PCR, particularly in asymptomatic individuals or those early in the course of infection. This study demonstrates that the high analytical performance of the Cobas SARS-CoV-2 test is maintained in this key asymptomatic population, which is essential for this use case and future scenarios where high-throughput screening and high sensitivity are fundamental.

A potential limitation of this study is that the symptom status was not collected for those individuals who tested negative. However, given the strict behavioral protocols in place including mandatory symptom reporting and close medical observation [13], we can assume that the overwhelming majority of negative test results were among individuals who are asymptomatic. In addition, per the FDA’s feedback, when the comparator status was positive (i.e., the subject had a higher probability of being symptomatic), those with missing symptom status were not included in the PPA/NPA calculations, providing further reassurance that the subjects were truly asymptomatic.

Conclusion

Based on data collected as part of an occupational testing protocol, this study provides strong evidence supporting the real-world performance of the candidate test in an asymptomatic population. The rigorous study design enabled the generation of regulatory-grade evidence that contributed to the claim expansion of the candidate test in asymptomatic testing.
