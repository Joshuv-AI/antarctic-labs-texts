Triangulating the link between depression and liver disease: An integrative analysis of genetic, observational, and research trend data
Authors: Zhongwen Feng, Yinglian Wang, Qingliu Lu, Xuefeng Jin, Yunyun Qin, Yixuan Jiang, Xiaoyu Chen, Kefeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationship between major depressive disorder (MDD) and liver diseases is complex, with causal links poorly understood. This study aimed to disentangle causal, mediated, and confounded links between MDD and liver diseases.MethodsWe integrated three approaches: a trend analysis of 2004 to 2024 publications; a two-sample Mendelian randomization, or MR, analysis assessing genetic causal effects of MDD on liver diseases; and an observational analysis of the NHANES 2007 to 2020 cohort using survey-weighted regression and formal causal mediation analysis to evaluate clinical pathways.ResultsMR analysis provided genetic evidence for a causal effect of MDD on NAFLD (OR = 1.233, 95% CI: 1.015–1.496), chronic HBV (OR = 1.607, 95% CI: 1.155–2.235), and viral hepatitis (OR = 1.515, 95% CI: 1.053–2.180), but not chronic HCV. In NHANES, the clinical MDD-NAFLD association (AOR = 1.35) became non-significant after adjusting for BMI (AOR = 1.07, P = 0.500), with formal mediation analysis revealing that BMI significantly mediates 80.70% of this risk. Furthermore, MDD showed no independent association with advanced liver fibrosis (P = 0.500). Conversely, a strong observational association was found for chronic HCV (AOR = 2.52) despite the null MR finding; however, this was entirely attenuated after adjusting for illicit drug use (AOR = 1.59, P = 0.072). No significant association was found for chronic HBV.ConclusionMDD increases liver disease risk via distinct pathways. The genetic MDD-NAFLD link appears largely mediated by obesity clinically, manifesting primarily as steatosis rather than direct fibrogenesis. The strong MDD-HCV association reflects socio-behavioral confounding, while the causal MDD-HBV link remains an under-recognized risk. These findings underscore the importance of managing obesity in psychiatric care and integrating mental health screening in hepatology.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) and liver diseases represent two of the most prevalent and disabling global health conditions, both contributing substantially to morbidity, mortality, and health system burden. According to recent estimates, chronic liver disease, including cirrhosis and liver cancer, continues to cause millions of deaths per year worldwide, with rising incidence particularly in metabolic-associated liver disease driven by obesity and metabolic syndrome [1]. At the same time, depression is one of the leading causes of years lived with disability, affecting an estimated more than 300 million people globally [2]. Traditionally considered distinct clinical entities, accumulating evidence indicates that depression and liver diseases frequently co-occur, with complex bidirectional interactions that remain incompletely understood.

The comorbidity between depression and liver diseases may arise from multiple convergent biological and behavioral pathways. Systemic inflammation, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation, autonomic imbalance, metabolic dysfunction, and gut–liver axis alterations have all been implicated as mechanistic bridges linking MDD with hepatic pathology [3,4]. Behavioral factors associated with depression, such as physical inactivity, alcohol consumption, poor diet, smoking, and disrupted sleep, further exacerbate metabolic and hepatic vulnerability [5,6]. Conversely, chronic liver disease can increase psychological stress through symptom burden, impaired quality of life, and neuropsychiatric consequences of hepatic encephalopathy or antiviral therapy [7]. This reciprocal relationship underscores the necessity of considering depression and liver diseases not in isolation, but as interrelated components of a shared pathophysiological network.

Despite growing clinical awareness, research on the comorbidity between liver disease and MDD remains fragmented. Most existing studies are observational, limited by confounding, reverse causation, and regional research silos. For example, early investigations predominantly focused on hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, where depression frequently emerged as a treatment-related adverse effect of interferon-based regimens [8,9]. More recent studies have shifted attention toward Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD) and Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Fatty Liver Disease (MAFLD), reflecting the global epidemiological transition from viral to metabolic liver diseases [10]. However, no comprehensive synthesis has mapped the intellectual landscape of this evolving field or quantitatively assessed causal relationships at the genetic level.

To address these gaps, integrative approaches are needed. Bibliometric analysis provides a powerful tool for systematically characterizing global research trends, knowledge structures, and collaborative networks within a field, thereby illuminating both strengths and underexplored areas [11]. In parallel, Mendelian randomization (MR) leverages genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer causal relationships between exposures and outcomes, overcoming major limitations of conventional observational designs [12]. Applying MR to liver disease and MDD enables rigorous evaluation of whether genetic liability to depression causally influences the risk of specific liver diseases, thereby advancing mechanistic understanding and informing prevention strategies. However, to bridge the gap from genetic liability to clinical practice, it is crucial to triangulate these genetic findings with evidence from large-scale, independent observational cohorts. This combined approach allows for a comprehensive assessment of both causal inference and real-world associational relevance.

In this study, we employed a tripartite approach, combining bibliometric mapping, a two-sample MR analysis, and an assessment of clinical associations in an independent observational cohort to provide a comprehensive overview and robust causal inference regarding this comorbidity. First, we conducted a comprehensive bibliometric analysis across Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed from 2004 to 2024, identifying global publication trends, research hotspots, and collaborative patterns. Second, we employed MR to test the potential causal effects of genetic susceptibility to MDD on major liver diseases, including NAFLD, chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, viral hepatitis, and primary biliary cholangitis. Third, we sought to examine the real-world clinical associations corresponding to these genetic findings by assessing the link between MDD and liver diseases in a large, nationally representative US cohort (NHANES). By integrating these three complementary methodologies, our study aims to (1) delineate the research landscape and intellectual evolution of liver disease and MDD, (2) provide genetic evidence on causal pathways linking MDD and liver diseases, (3) assess the clinical relevance of these findings in a large, real-world population, and (4) highlight future research priorities to guide mechanistic studies, clinical practice, and public health interventions.

Materials and methods

Bibliometric data collection

On June 10, 2025, a comprehensive literature search was conducted across three major acknowledged databases—Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC), Scopus, and PubMed—to identify global studies published between 2004 and 2024 examining the comorbidity between liver disease and MDD. The detailed search strategy is presented in Supplementary Table S1. Records retrieved from all databases were consolidated, and duplicate entries were systematically removed prior to bibliometric analysis.

Bibliometric analysis

To analyze the acquired data, we employed software such as the Bibliometrix package (v4.3.3) within the R environment (v4.2.2), alongside VOSviewer (v1.6.17) and CiteSpace (v6.3. R1) software. Scientometric visualizations and knowledge mapping were enabled by these toolkits. To ensure methodological rigor, data extraction and analytical procedures were independently executed by two researchers under a dual-reviewer protocol.

VOSviewer was employed to generate visualizations of national collaboration networks, bibliographic coupling analysis of journal, and keyword co-occurrence. Meanwhile, the Bibliometrix R package was utilized not only for data curation and duplicate removal but also to identify potentially relevant journals. CiteSpace was primarily applied to detect citation bursts, facilitating the identification of temporally significant reference surges within the extensive literature. Furthermore, Impact Factors (IF) for journals were obtained from the 2024 Journal Citation Reports (JCR).

Mendelian randomization analysis

We conducted a two-sample MR analysis to assess the potential causal effect of MDD on liver diseases. MR exploits genetic variants robustly associated with the exposure as instrumental variables (IVs), leveraging publicly available Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) summary statistics to minimize confounding and reverse causation inherent in observational designs. MR relies on three core assumptions: (1) Relevance, whereby selected IVs are strongly associated with the exposure; (2) Independence, meaning IVs are not correlated with any confounders of the exposure–outcome relationship; and (3) Exclusion Restriction, which requires that IVs affect the outcome only through the exposure—and not via alternative pathways [13].

Eligible exposure and outcome datasets were obtained from publicly available GWAS sources, including the GWAS Catalog, Neale Lab, IEU OpenGWAS, and PheWeb. A genome-wide association meta-analysis was conducted using summary-level data on liver disease and MDD (see Supplementary Table S2 for detailed information). To maximize SNP inclusion and allow broader causal inference, SNPs associated with the exposure at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−7) were selected as instrumental variables (IVs). Linkage disequilibrium clumping (r2 < 0.001, window size = 10,000 kb) was performed to ensure IV independence. Given the relatively lenient significance threshold, an F-statistic greater than 10 was applied to confirm instrument strength, as the F statistic quantifies the association between SNPs and the exposure, with higher values indicating stronger instruments [13]. Palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies were excluded to avoid strand ambiguity. Ultimately, 66 SNPs were retained as valid IVs for analyses with NAFLD, chronic HBV, and viral hepatitis (Supplementary Table S3). For the primary biliary cholangitis outcome, five palindromic SNPs were removed, leaving 39 SNPs as valid instruments (Supplementary Table S4). Exposure and outcome data were harmonized based on effect alleles, and all analyses were conducted on the merged dataset. The primary MR analysis employed the random-effects inverse variance weighted (IVW) method to estimate the causal effect of MDD on liver disease. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q test, while robustness was evaluated through complementary methods including MR-Egger regression and maximum likelihood estimation. Horizontal pleiotropy was examined using the MR-Egger intercept and the MR-PRESSO global test, and the Steiger test was applied to assess the directionality of causality.

To account for multiple hypothesis testing across the three primary outcomes, statistical correction was performed using both the stringent Bonferroni method and the false discovery rate (FDR) (Benjamini–Hochberg procedure). Corrected P values were reported alongside unadjusted results to ensure robust causal inference. All analyses were conducted using the “TwoSampleMR”, “MendelianRandomization” and “MR-PRESSO” packages in R.

Observational analysis in an independent cohort

To assess the clinical associations corresponding to the MR findings in an independent clinical cohort, we conducted a cross-sectional analysis using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2007 to 2020 (pre-pandemic). The study population included adult participants aged ≥18 years. The primary exposure, MDD, was defined as a Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) score ≥10 [14]. The main outcomes included: (1) NAFLD, defined by a Fatty Liver Index (FLI) ≥ 60, with individuals with excessive alcohol consumption or viral hepatitis infection excluded [15]; (2) chronic HBV was explicitly defined as isolated hepatitis B surface antigen positivity; (3) chronic HCV was defined as hepatitis C virus RNA positivity. (4) viral hepatitis was defined as a broader composite category encompassing positive markers for either HBV or HCV infections. We used the survey package in R software to assess the OR and 95% CI for the association between MDD and liver outcomes, using survey-weighted logistic regression.

All models accounted for the complex, multistage sampling design of NHANES and were adjusted for key covariates, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, poverty income ratio, and history of diabetes. Additionally, to assess the robustness of this association in individuals with higher symptom burden, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by repeating the logistic regression models using a more stringent cutoff (PHQ-9 ≥ 15), which represents moderately severe to severe depression. Furthermore, given the strong association between MDD and obesity observed in our baseline data (see result 3.3.1 Table 3) and the reliance of the FLI score on BMI, we conducted a critical sensitivity analysis for NAFLD by adding BMI (BMXBMI) as an additional covariate to the primary adjusted model (Model 1) to create Model 2. To robustly quantify the mechanistic pathway linking MDD to NAFLD, a formal causal mediation analysis was performed using the mediation package in R. We estimated the Average Causal Mediation Effect (ACME) and Average Direct Effect (ADE) using 500 Monte Carlo simulations with robust standard errors. Second, to evaluate liver fibrosis independent of adiposity while avoiding rare-event convergence biases, the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index was log-transformed and analyzed continuously using survey-weighted multiple linear regression. Finally, to explicitly address potential socio-behavioral confounding in chronic HCV, a targeted sensitivity analysis (Model 3) was conducted in the 2007-2016 sub-cohort, further adjusting for a history of illicit drug use (cocaine, heroin, or methamphetamine).

Results

Bibliometric analysis of the liver disease and MDD research landscape

Overview

From 2004 to 2024, a total of 1122 valid records were identified. Over this 21-year period, global publications on this comorbidity demonstrated a fluctuating upward trajectory, peaking in 2024. This growth was marked by significant surges in 2017 and 2021, interrupted by subsequent stabilization phases (Supplementary Fig. S1A). USA (n = 390) and China (n = 110) dominated global output, collectively accounting for 44.6% of total publications. However, both exhibited low international collaboration rates. Notably, the United Kingdom (n = 42) ranked fifth in output but achieved the highest Multiple Country Publications (MCP) ratio (11.9%) among the top five nations, underscoring an inverse relationship between publication volume and collaborative efficiency (Supplementary Fig. S1B, Table S5).

Co-authorship network analysis (Supplementary Fig. S2A) demonstrates that the USA functions as the central hub for most high-output nations despite its low MCP ratio. Temporally stratified analysis (Supplementary Fig. S2B) reveals that USA research productivity peaked predominantly before 2018, whereas post-2020 scholarly output shifted toward emerging contributors including China, India, and South Korea.

Journal analysis and visualization

To identify core journals, we analyzed productivity and citation networks. As presented in Supplementary Fig. S2C, PLOS ONE (n = 23), American Journal of Gastroenterology (n = 16), and Liver International (n = 16) emerged as the most productive journals. Supplementary Fig. S2D highlights Hepatology (1767 citations), New England Journal of Medicine (941 citations), and The Lancet (765 citations) as the most influential journals based on co-citation analysis. Bibliographic coupling further confirmed that PLOS ONE, American Journal of Gastroenterology, and Liver International demonstrated the strongest thematic interconnections. Collectively, these findings establish these journals as pivotal platforms for scholarship on this comorbidity, characterized by high-volume publication and strong thematic linkage.

Bursts of references and keyword analysis

To identify research frontiers and thematic shifts, we utilized CiteSpace to detect the top 15 citation bursts (Fig. 1), with corresponding titles cataloged in Supplementary Table S6. Analysis of these bursts revealed a clear temporal evolution. The most prominent early bursts, from both Scopus and WoSCC, focused heavily on HCV treatment, with topics such as "Peginterferon alfa-2a plus ribavirin" and "Boceprevir" dominating the landscape. In sharp contrast, the most recently emerged citation bursts highlight a clear thematic pivot towards NAFLD epidemiology and burden. Two-sample MR of MDD and liver diseases

Primary IVW analysis

We employed a two-sample MR approach to investigate the genetically predicted causal association between MDD and liver diseases. The IVW analysis revealed significant causal relationships for several liver conditions. Specifically, MDD was causally associated with an increased risk of NAFLD (OR = 1.233, 95% CI: 1.015–1.496; P = 0.034), viral hepatitis (OR = 1.515, 95% CI: 1.053–2.180; P = 0.025), and chronic HBV infection (OR = 1.607, 95% CI: 1.155–2.235; P = 0.005). The primary IVW analysis also suggested a potential association with primary biliary cholangitis (OR = 1.498, 95% CI: 1.007–2.229; P = 0.046). However, this finding was considered spurious and not carried forward for correction, as subsequent sensitivity analyses identified significant horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept P < 0.05; see Sec 3.2.3) and failed to confirm causal direction (Steiger test P = 0.278; see Sec 3.2.4). Multiple testing correction

Considering the issue of multiple hypothesis testing, we applied Bonferroni and FDR corrections. Following the removal of primary biliary cholangitis, corrections were applied across the three remaining primary outcomes. The association between MDD and chronic HBV infection remained statistically significant (P = 0.005 < 0.0167). However, the associations with viral hepatitis (P = 0.025 > 0.0167) and NAFLD (P = 0.034 > 0.0167) did not survive this stringent correction. In contrast, after FDR adjustment (Benjamini–Hochberg procedure), all three associations remained statistically significant. The recalculated FDR-adjusted P-values were: chronic HBV (PFDR = 0.015), viral hepatitis (PFDR = 0.0375), and NAFLD (PFDR = 0.0375). These findings suggest that the MDD–HBV relationship is highly robust, while the associations with NAFLD and viral hepatitis are also supported under the widely accepted FDR framework. Detailed adjusted P values are provided in Supplementary Table S8.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted extensive sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of causal estimates (Table 2). Cochran's Q tests showed no significant heterogeneity for NAFLD, viral hepatitis, chronic HBV infection, or primary biliary cholangitis (P > 0.05), indicating consistent effect estimates across IVs. Horizontal pleiotropy assessed by MR-Egger intercepts was minimal for NAFLD (intercept = 0.026, P = 0.059), chronic HBV (intercept = 0.020, P = 0.490), and viral hepatitis (intercept = 0.004, P = 0.868), whereas primary biliary cholangitis exhibited a marginally significant pleiotropy signal (intercept = −0.088; P < 0.05). Causal directionality

In Table 2, Steiger's tests demonstrated that genetic predisposition to MDD precedes the development of NAFLD and chronic HBV, with P-values of 2.37 × 10−124 and 5.05 × 10−118, respectively. This supports a unidirectional causal effect from MDD to these liver outcomes. Directionality could not be confirmed for viral hepatitis due to unavailable variant-level variance data, and the test for primary biliary cholangitis was non-significant (P = 0.278), indicating uncertain causal direction.

Summary of MR analysis

Forest and scatter plots illustrating genetically predicted causal associations between MDD and liver diseases, as well as subtype-specific risks, are presented in Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4. Detailed results of sensitivity analyses are provided in Supplementary Fig. S5 and Table 2. Taken together, these analyses reinforce the robustness of primary findings for NAFLD, viral hepatitis, and chronic HBV infection, whereas the association with primary biliary cholangitis warrants caution due to extreme MR-Egger estimates, marginal pleiotropy, and non-significant Steiger test results.

Observational analysis of the NHANES cohort

Baseline characteristics

The analysis included a weighted population of 147.9 million adults. Participants with MDD (weighted N = 13.88 million, 9.4% of the cohort) were more likely to be female (65.6% vs. 48.2%), had a lower poverty income ratio (Median 1.5 vs. 3.2), higher BMI (30.6 vs. 28.5 kg/m2), larger waist circumference (101.3 vs. 96.9 cm), and a higher prevalence of diabetes (15.4% vs. 8.2%) compared to the non-MDD group (P < 0.001). The MDD group also had a significantly higher prevalence of NAFLD (23.5% vs. 18.0%), chronic HCV (2.5% vs. 0.7%), and viral hepatitis (2.7% vs. 0.9%) (P < 0.001). No significant difference was observed for chronic HBV prevalence (0.4% vs. 0.3%, P = 0.5). The detailed baseline characteristics are presented in Table 3.

Association between MDD and liver diseases

The results of the survey-weighted logistic regression analyses are summarized in Table 4. In our primary adjusted model (Model 1, adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, poverty income ratio, and diabetes), MDD remained significantly associated with an increased risk of NAFLD (Adjusted OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.17–1.56, P < 0.001). However, in our pre-specified sensitivity analysis (Model 2), after additionally adjusting for BMI, this association was no longer significant (Adjusted OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 0.89–1.28, P = 0.500). To further elucidate this attenuation, a formal causal mediation analysis was conducted. The analysis robustly confirmed that BMI acts as a primary mechanistic bridge, significantly mediating 80.70% of the total effect of MDD on NAFLD (ACME = 0.0361, P < 0.001; ADE = 0.0086, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S9). Discussion

Principal findings

In this study, we employed a tripartite approach—combining bibliometric analysis, MR analysis, and independent observational analysis—to investigate the complex relationship between MDD and liver diseases. The bibliometric analysis revealed a rising, albeit fragmented, global research trend, with a notable thematic shift from viral hepatitis towards metabolic liver diseases like NAFLD. Our MR analysis provided robust genetic evidence supporting a causal effect of MDD on several liver conditions, most notably chronic HBV infection, NAFLD, and viral hepatitis.

Significantly, we examined these genetic findings in a large, independent US-based cohort to disentangle the true pathways. This observational analysis quantitatively disentangled the link between MDD and NAFLD: the association was significant in the primary model but disappeared after adjusting for BMI. Our formal causal mediation analysis proved that this link is primarily mediated by obesity (80.70%), and our FIB-4 analysis confirmed the absence of independent fibrogenesis. Moreover, we successfully unmasked the strong observational association between MDD and chronic HCV, demonstrating it to be entirely driven by confounding from illicit drug use. The strong causal signal for chronic HBV identified in the MR analysis was not replicated in the NHANES cohort, a divergence that highlights the complementary nature of these methodologies.

The MDD-NAFLD axis: adiposity as the mechanistic bridge

The integration of MR and observational data provides a more nuanced understanding than either method alone. The findings for NAFLD highlight this complexity. Our MR analysis supported a genetic causal link, suggesting MDD liability increases NAFLD risk. However, our NHANES analysis revealed that this association is critically dependent on obesity.

The biological and behavioral pathways linking MDD to liver disease are multiple and convergent. Our clarified finding for NAFLD is supported by well-established mechanisms. Systemic low-grade inflammation, a hallmark of depression, promotes hepatic steatosis and fibrogenesis [[16], [17], [18]] through pro-inflammatory cytokines that activate hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) [19]. Chronic inflammation also induces insulin resistance and lipotoxicity, which further exacerbate steatosis, oxidative stress, and HSC activation [20]. Dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and autonomic imbalance may further aggravate insulin resistance [21], amplify gluconeogenesis [22], and impair hepatic microcirculation [23]. Furthermore, dysregulation of the gut-liver axis in depression—including gut dysbiosis and increased intestinal permeability—may augment hepatic inflammation via endotoxemia and pro-inflammatory signaling [[24], [25], [26]].

In parallel, behavioral factors common in MDD, such as physical inactivity, poor diet, alcohol use, and disrupted sleep, can exacerbate adiposity and metabolic dysfunction [27,28]. Pharmacological effects may also contribute. While some antidepressants have neutral metabolic profiles, others are associated with weight gain or metabolic derangements [29,30], and long-term use has been linked to metabolic syndrome features [31]. Crucially, our MR estimates capture the genetic liability to depression itself, not treatment exposure, highlighting etiologic pathways intrinsic to the disorder. This behavioral and pharmacological pathway, by driving adiposity and metabolic dysfunction, provides a direct biological explanation for our NHANES finding that the MDD-NAFLD link was completely attenuated after adjusting for BMI. Instead of treating this attenuation as a null finding, our formal causal mediation analysis precisely quantified this pathway, revealing that BMI significantly mediates exactly 80.70% of the total effect of MDD on NAFLD. This mathematically confirms that the genetic liability to MDD drives liver steatosis primarily through these adiposity-promoting pathways.

To further delineate the boundaries of hepatic injury, we analyzed the continuous Log-FIB4 index. The lack of a significant independent association with advanced fibrosis indicates that the hepatic impact of MDD predominantly manifests as early-stage metabolic steatosis rather than direct structural fibrogenesis. However, the detection of a highly significant direct effect independent of BMI underscores that the residual ∼19.3% risk points toward the intrinsic biological overlaps mentioned above, such as MDD-induced systemic low-grade inflammation or gut microbiota dysbiosis, which can directly promote hepatic steatosis even in the absence of overt obesity.

Disentangling viral hepatitis: confounding vs. hidden causality

Our study revealed intriguing discrepancies regarding viral hepatitis. The most striking discrepancy emerged regarding chronic HCV. Our MR analysis found no genetic evidence for a causal link from MDD to chronic HCV. This stands in stark contrast to our NHANES base models, which revealed a very strong observational association. By hypothesizing that this clinical link is heavily confounded, we integrated a targeted sensitivity analysis. Strikingly, after adjusting for illicit drug use, the MDD-HCV association was completely attenuated. This perfectly explains the MR null finding and proves that the real-world clinical link is driven by shared socio-behavioral risk factors, such as intravenous drug use, rather than intrinsic biology. This finding highlights the strength of using MR to disambiguate causal relationships from those driven by shared environmental or behavioral factors.

Conversely, the MR analysis identified a strong causal link for chronic HBV, which likely drove the association found for the broader viral hepatitis category. In stark contrast, the NHANES analysis showed no significant association with chronic HBV. This divergence is likely attributable to the very low prevalence (0.3-0.4%) of chronic HBV in the US general population, which severely limited the statistical power. Therefore, the MR finding likely represents a true "hidden" causal effect. For chronic viral hepatitis, depression-related immune modulation may impair viral clearance, resonating with our MR signal for chronic HBV [32]. Furthermore, depression-related cognitive deficits may also undermine vaccination uptake and antiviral treatment adherence in real-world clinical settings.

Contextualizing findings within the bibliometric landscape

Our bibliometric analysis provides critical context for interpreting these multi-modal results. The clear thematic evolution from HCV and interferon-related depression to NAFLD explains the current research landscape. First, the strong observational link between MDD and HCV aligns with the historical research focus. However, our drug-use sensitivity analysis suggests this is a confounded association rather than a causal one, reflecting the shared risk factors (e.g., intravenous drug use) prevalent in early HCV-focused literature. Second, the field's recent pivot to NAFLD is supported by our MR analysis. Crucially, our NHANES mediation analysis clarifies this pathway, showing the true research focus should be on the MDD-obesity-NAFLD axis, specifically targeting weight gain and psychopharmacological metabolic side effects. Third, the bibliometric data highlights a critical research gap: the robust MR finding of a causal link between MDD and chronic HBV suggests that this specific comorbidity is a critically under-studied and overlooked area of risk.

Clinical and public-health implications

Our findings support routine bidirectional vigilance. The robust genetic link between MDD and NAFLD—coupled with our observational finding that this link is 80.70% mediated by BMI—warrants a stronger focus on metabolic management in psychiatric patients. While systematic screening using indices like FIB-4 is useful to rule out severe complications, our results imply that managing obesity and metabolic syndrome should be a primary intervention target to break the causal chain from MDD to NAFLD [33,34]. Conversely, hepatology clinics should consider proactive detection and management of depression in patients with NAFLD to improve adherence to lifestyle modifications.

The strong observational link to chronic HCV reinforces the need for integrated mental health support and substance abuse in HCV care pathways. The causal evidence for chronic HBV, despite the null finding in NHANES, suggests that in HBV-endemic regions or high-risk populations, depression management could be a potential target for improving long-term viral control and vaccination uptake. At the health-system level, our bibliometric evidence of low multinational collaboration highlights a critical opportunity for global consortia to design care pathways that bridge hepatology and mental health, particularly to test interventions in diverse populations.

Strengths and methodological considerations

This study's primary strength is the triangulation of three distinct methodologies: bibliometric mapping, genetic epidemiology, and independent observational analysis. Specifically, the integration of causal mediation analysis and drug-use sensitivity modeling allowed us to explicitly quantify mechanistic pathways and eliminate socio-behavioral confounding.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the GWAS datasets were predominantly of European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of the MR findings. Second, the NHANES analysis provided crucial real-world context, but its cross-sectional design cannot establish temporality. We measured MDD symptoms and NAFLD indicators at the same time point. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility of reverse causation, where hepatic or metabolic dysfunction might contribute to depressive symptoms. Third, the statistical power of the NHANES analysis was limited for low-prevalence conditions like chronic HBV, which likely explains the divergence from the MR results. Fourth, our NAFLD definition in NHANES relied on the FLI, a non-invasive proxy score. This index is heavily dependent on BMI and waist circumference, which are themselves strongly correlated with MDD, as seen in our baseline data. This limitation is highlighted by our sensitivity analysis, where the MDD-NAFLD association disappeared after adjusting for BMI, confirming that the FLI proxy is capturing a link mediated by adiposity. However, our robust mediation results confirm that this proxy successfully captured the adiposity-driven pathways integral to the disease's early etiology. Finally, due to data availability, our HCV sensitivity model controlling for drug use was restricted to the 2007-2016 cohorts, yet it provided sufficient power to validate the confounding hypothesis.

Future research directions

Three lines of inquiry appear particularly urgent. First, mechanistic studies should continue to interrogate the gut–brain–liver axis. Second, the divergent findings for viral hepatitis must be untangled. Furthermore, both MR and large-scale observational studies must be replicated in HBV-endemic populations to validate the causal signal we identified and assess its real-world clinical relevance. Third, implementation science is needed to translate these insights, testing whether evidence-based depression management can reduce NAFLD incidence and improve viral hepatitis control in diverse clinical settings.

Conclusions

This study maps two decades of scholarship on this comorbidity and provides critical, triangulated evidence on its complex etiology. We provide robust genetic evidence (MR) supporting MDD as a causal risk factor for NAFLD. However, our causal mediation analysis in NHANES demonstrates that this clinical link is 80.70% mediated by obesity, manifesting without independent fibrogenesis. Furthermore, we highlight a robust causal link for chronic HBV identified through MR, and prove that the strong observational association for chronic HCV in NHANES is driven by illicit drug use confounding. These findings underscore the need to integrate mental and hepatic health, justifying depression screening in liver disease care and metabolic/obesity surveillance in psychiatric practice.
