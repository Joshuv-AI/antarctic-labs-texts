Oral magnesium supplements and insulin resistance in individuals with diabetes and pre-diabetes: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Aryana Amiri, Fateme Seighali, Mohammad Sadra Gholami-Chahkand, Behrooz Jannat, Niloofar Seighali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes is a common health challenge. Previous studies have established that diabetes is often associated with low circulating magnesium status. As evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on the effect of magnesium supplementation on insulin resistance remains inconsistent, and the influence of baseline status is not fully understood, this systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the effects of magnesium supplements, compared with placebo, on insulin resistance in individuals with diabetes and pre-diabetes.MethodsA comprehensive search based on the PRISMA 2020 guidelines was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus until 24 April 2026. Randomized controlled trials with a population of individuals with diabetes and pre-diabetes that received any magnesium supplements compared to a placebo group, evaluating insulin and the HOMA-IR index as the primary outcome, were included in our study. Secondary outcomes were serum magnesium level, HbA1c, and fasting blood glucose (FBG). We pooled data using a random-effects model to estimate changes in outcomes. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on dosage (> 250 and ≤ 250 mg/day) and follow-up duration (under or over 12 weeks). Meta-regression, publication bias, and sensitivity analysis were performed as well.ResultsFifteen RCTs involving 1,085 participants met the criteria. Overall, the oral magnesium supplementation did not result in a statistically significant change in insulin levels (MD = − 1.73; 95% CI: −3.73 to 0.27; p-value = 0.09) and HOMA-IR (MD = − 0.74; 95% CI: −1.58 to 0.09; p-value = 0.08) in people with diabetes. Similar non-significant findings were observed in individuals with prediabetes (p > 0.05). However, meta-regression analysis demonstrated that baseline insulin level and HOMA-IR index significantly modified the intervention effect (p = 0.01), suggesting differential responsiveness across patient subgroups. No significant associations were observed for baseline HbA1c, fasting blood glucose, magnesium levels, age, or BMI. Subgroup analysis based on doses and duration of follow-up did not change the significance of the result.ConclusionMagnesium supplementation does not appear to improve insulin resistance in individuals with diabetes or prediabetes significantly. However, its effects may depend on baseline insulin status, highlighting the need for targeted interventions in individuals with higher insulin resistance. Future well-designed trials with larger sample sizes are needed to clarify baseline insulin effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40795-026-01424-y.Keywords: Magnesium, Supplement, Diabetes, HOMA-IR, Insulin resistance, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Diabetes is a major global health challenge and is projected to affect approximately one in ten people worldwide by 2045 [1]. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a risk factor leading to comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease, blindness, kidney failure, and nontraumatic leg amputations [2–4]. The underlying mechanism of hyperglycemia is based on β-cell impairment and insulin resistance [5].

Magnesium is an essential mineral with different roles in 300 biochemical reactions and vital functions, including glucose regulation, muscle contraction, myocardial electrical activity, and blood pressure [6, 8]. and insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells [9]. However, previous studies demonstrated that hypomagnesemia is related to hyperglycaemia and insulin resistance, and a higher risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, and chronic kidney disease is estimated [10–12, 14]. Individuals with diabetes have a higher prevalence of hypomagnesemia than those without diabetes [15]. Hypomagnesemia in individuals with diabetes is associated with insulin resistance, retinopathy, poor glycaemic control, and hypertension [16].

Evidence from randomized controlled trials evaluating magnesium supplementation has been inconsistent, with some studies reporting improvements in insulin levels and homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) [17, 18, 20], while others show no significant effects [21, 23]. These discrepancies may be explained by differences in study populations, baseline magnesium level, dosage, and intervention duration.

However, several systematic reviews and meta-analyses were conducted to evaluate the influences of magnesium supplements on the development of T2DM [24, 25]. A meta-analysis focusing especially on insulin resistance by assessing the insulin level and HOMA-IR is relatively limited. Thus, the effects of magnesium supplementation improving the parameters related to insulin resistance in T2DM require additional investigation.

Given these inconsistencies, a systematic review and meta-analysis are warranted to provide a comprehensive synthesis of current evidence. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the effects of magnesium supplementation on insulin levels and HOMA-IR in patients with diabetes and pre-diabetes, offering a clearer understanding of its potential role in improving insulin sensitivity.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted according to PRISMA 2020 guidelines [26]. (Supplemental material, Table S1). Our study protocol has been registered at PROSPERO with the following code: CRD420261371610.

Search strategy

We performed a comprehensive search in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, Cochrane Central Register of controlled Trials (CENTRAL) and ClinicalTrials.gov site up to 24 April 2026, using the search strategy with the combination of following key terms: (“magnesium supplement”) AND (“diabetes mellitus”), alongside with their equivalent terms, without restrictions on language or publication date (Supplemental material, Table S2).

Eligibility criteria

Title-abstract screening was performed by two reviewers (A.A. and F.S.). Discrepancies were resolved by the third reviewer (N.S.). Randomized controlled trials with people with diabetes and prediabetes who received any formulation of oral magnesium supplements compared to a placebo group evaluating insulin and the HOMA-IR index were included in our study. There was no restriction on the language or date of the published studies. Case report studies, case series, letter-to-editor articles, review studies, meta-analyses, and commentary studies without any relevant data were excluded. Two authors (A.A., and F.S.) extracted data on study characteristics such as Author, year, country, study design, population, magnesium status of patients before starting the trial (hypomagnesemia and normomagnesemia), number of patients in case and control group, duration of follow-up (weeks), magnesium formula, dose (mg/day), route of administration, mean baseline Age, mean baseline BMI, proportion of female participants, and mean pre- and post-intervention data for serum magnesium level (mg/dl), fasting blood glucose (FBG) (mg/dl), HbA1c (%), insulin (µIU/ml), and homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) index onto a dedicated excel spreadsheet. Studies were primarily included based on reporting insulin-related outcomes. As a result, data extraction of secondary outcomes such as HbA1c, FBS, and magnesium level was restricted to a subset of included trials and may not represent the full body of available evidence. Discrepancies in extraction were resolved through discussion and mutual consensus.

Quality assessment

The quality assessment of the included studies was performed via the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [27] by two reviewers (N.S. and F.S.) independently, and studies were classified as low risk, some concerns, or high risk. Any disagreements were resolved by the third author (B.J.).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using Stata 18.0 (StataCorp, TX, USA). The pooled effect size was expressed as mean difference (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for continuous outcomes. Given anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity, a random effects model was applied, and the between-study variance was estimated using the restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) method. Heterogeneity across studies was quantified using the I² statistic, with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, predefined subgroup analyses were conducted based on magnesium dose (≤ 250 mg/day vs. >250 mg/day) and intervention duration (≤ 12 weeks vs. >12 weeks). Furthermore, univariate meta-regression was performed to examine the influence of continuous covariates: mean age and baseline body mass index (BMI) for all outcomes. For the outcomes of insulin and HOMA-IR, separate meta-regression models were fitted including dose, duration, baseline magnesium level, fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), baseline insulin, and baseline HOMA-IR as potential moderators. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using the leave-one-out approach, sequentially omitting each study to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates. Publication bias was evaluated by visual inspection of funnel plots, complemented by Egger’s regression asymmetry test. In case of suspected asymmetry, the trim and fill method was applied to adjust for potential missing studies and to recalculate the pooled effect size.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

A systematic search across PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, CENTRAL, and clinicaltrial.gov retrieved 6,979 records after duplicate removal. Through screening based on title and abstract, 67 studies were identified for full-text screening. Finally, a total of 15 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comprising 1,085 patients were included in our study (Fig. 1). Of them, 555 patients received oral magnesium supplement and 530 patients received placebo. The studies were grouped into patients with diabetes and pre-diabetes; 11 studies with 785 patients with diabetes [17, 18, 20, 22, 23, 28–31, 34]. and 4 studies with 300 patients with pre-diabetes were included [19, 21, 35, 36]. Seven of the included studies used magnesium oxide [19, 21, 29, 31, 32, 34, 36], three of them used magnesium chloride [17, 20, 33], one magnesium sulphate [22], one magnesium lactate [23], and one used a mixture of magnesium gluconate, oxide, and lactate [18]. One of the studies included in meta-analysis in form of two effect sizes, as it had two intervention groups receiving magnesium doses of 20 and 50 mg/day [17]. Additional characteristic data of each included study are provided in Table 1. The quality assessment of the included RCTs was done by the RoB2 tool. Thirteen studies were judged to be at low risk of bias [19–21, 23, 28–30, 33, 35, 36], whereas two studies were assessed as some concerns [18, 35]. Primary outcome

Patients with diabetes

Insulin

After pooling the data of nine effect sizes for insulin, meta-analysis demonstrated magnesium supplementation did not alter the insulin level significantly compared to placebo (MD=-1.73; 95% CI [-3.73, 0.27]; p-value = 0.09) (Fig. 2.A); whereas doses ≤ 250 mg/day showed a borderline significant reduction in insulin level (MD=-2.36; 95% CI [-4.76, 0.04]; p-value = 0.05) (Fig. 2.B). However, the subgroup of follow-up duration over 12 weeks had a single study, but it showed a significant decrease in insulin level in the magnesium supplement group compared to placebo (MD=-1.3; 95% CI [-2.51, -0.09]; p-value = 0.04). The findings for the > 12-week insulin subgroup were derived from a single study and should therefore be interpreted cautiously (Fig. 3). Doses over 250 mg/day or duration lower than 12 weeks did not change the insulin level significantly (p-value = 0.23 and 0.12, respectively).

Subgroup analysis of insulin level changes in diabetic patients receiving oral magnesium supplementation compared to placebo based on duration of follow-up

HOMA-IR

Overall meta-analysis of eleven studies showed magnesium supplementation non-significantly decreased HOMA-IR in patients with diabetes compared to placebo (MD=-0.74; 95% CI [-1.58, 0.09]; p-value = 0.08) (Fig. 4.A). Subgroup analysis based on dose did not show significant differences between doses under and over 250 mg/day (p = 0.07 and 0.14, respectively) (Fig. 4.B). Also, subgroup analysis based on duration of magnesium supplementation demonstrated a non-significant decrease in both groups of under and above 12 weeks follow-up without between-group differences (p = 0.09 and p = 0.13, respectively) (Figure S1).

Individuals with pre-diabetes

Insulin

Meta-analysis of three included studies demonstrated that patients in the magnesium supplement group compared to the placebo group experienced a non-significant reduction (MD = 0.70; 95% CI [-4.10, 5.49]; p-value = 0.78) (Figure S11).

HOMA-IR

Pooled data of four studies in this analysis showed a non-significant decrease in HOMA-IR in individuals with pre-diabetes receiving magnesium supplementation compared to the placebo group (MD=-0.53; 95% CI [-1.46, 0.4]; p-value = 0.27) (Figure S12).

Meta-regression

Meta-regression analysis was performed for patients with diabetes, because the number of studies involving individuals with prediabetes was limited. The meta-regression showed significant modifying effects of mean baseline HOMA-IR and mean baseline insulin level on HOMA-IR index, mean baseline insulin level on insulin level, and age on magnesium level (Table 2). The analysis revealed that individuals with higher baseline insulin levels or higher baseline HOMA-IR index may experience lower decrease in HOMA-IR after magnesium supplementation (coefficient= -0.26 and − 0.42, respectively; p = 0.01 for both). Also, patients with diabetes with higher baseline insulin level may experience lower decrease in insulin level after receiving magnesium (coefficient= -0.72, p < 0.0001). Furthermore, analysis revealed that magnesium level of older patients with diabetes may increase more than younger patients (coefficient = 0.01, p = 0.006). Meta-regression didn’t show any significant association between age, BMI, duration of follow-up, baseline Mg level, baseline FBG, and baseline HbA1c with HOMA-IR or insulin level.

Meta-regression analysis for outcomes of diabetic population

Secondary outcome

Patients with diabetes

Magnesium level

The pooled data of eleven effect sizes showed a significant increase in serum magnesium level in the magnesium supplementation group compared to the placebo group (MD = 0.14; 95% CI [0.06, 0.28]; p-value < 0.001) (Figure S2). All subgroup analyses based on dose and duration showed a significant increase (p-value < 0.04) except for the subgroup of doses over 250 mg/day (MD = 0.12; 95% CI [-0.05, 0.29]; p-value = 0.18) (Figure S3 and S4, respectively).

HbA1c

Meta-analysis of ten pooled effect sizes showed a significant reduction in HbA1C level in patients with diabetes receiving magnesium supplementation compared to the placebo group (MD= -0.41; 95% CI [-0.73, -0.09]; p-value = 0.01) (Figure S5). Also, patients who received magnesium doses under 250 mg per day experienced a significant reduction (MD= -0.49; 95% CI [-0.88, -0.11]; p-value = 0.01), but doses over 250 mg/day did not change HbA1c significantly (p = 0.45) (Figure S6). Patients who received the intervention for more than 12 weeks showed a significant reduction in HbA1c compared to placebo (MD= -1.06; 95% CI [-2.08, -0.05]; p-value = 0.04); whereas the duration under 12 weeks did not alter HbA1c significantly (p = 0.09) (Figure S7).

Fasting Blood Glucose (FBG)

The analysis of 12 effect sizes showed a non-significant decrease of FBG in the magnesium group compared to placebo (MD= -6.23; 95% CI [-13.01, 0.55]; p-value = 0.07) (Figure S8). The subgroup analysis based on dosage demonstrated pooled data from patients with diabetes of 5 effect sizes with magnesium supplementation dose over 250 mg/day experienced significant reduction in FBG compared to placebo group (MD= -3.54; 95% CI [-6.92, -0.15]; p-value = 0.04) (Figure S9). Whereas patients with doses lower than 250 mg/day did not experience significant change in FBS compared to placebo (p-value = 0.11). Although magnesium supplementation with duration under 12 weeks did not show a significant effect (p-value = 0.21), the analysis demonstrated a significant decrease in FBG after 12 weeks in the magnesium group compared to the placebo group (MD= -4.38; 95% CI [-6.08, -2.68]; p-value < 0.001) (Figure S10).

Individuals with pre-diabetes

Magnesium level

After pooling the data on magnesium level, only two effect sizes had data. The analysis showed a non-significant increase in magnesium level after magnesium supplementation compared to placebo in individuals with prediabetes (MD = 0.71; 95% CI [-0.33, 1.75]; p-value = 0.18) (Figure S13).

Fasting blood glucose

Pooling the data of four studies showed a non-significant reduction in FBG in individuals with prediabetes in the group receiving magnesium compared to placebo (MD=-1.03; 95% CI [-3.86, 1.8]; p-value = 0.47) (Figure S14).

HbA1c

Meta-analysis of four included studies revealed that individuals with prediabetes receiving magnesium supplements compared to placebo experienced a non-significant reduction in HbA1c level (MD= -0.13; 95% CI [-0.34, 0.08]; p-value = 0.21) (Figure S15).

Publication bias

According to quantitative measurements using Egger’s test and visual inspection of the funnel plots, the analysis showed there was no evidence of publication bias in the primary or secondary outcomes for diabetic and individuals with prediabetes (p-value > 0.05) (Figure S16-S25).

Sensitivity analysis

Leave-one-out analysis showed removing every single study did not change the trend and significance of the overall analysis for HbA1c and magnesium level (Figure S26 and S27, respectively), whereas removing Sadeghian et al., 2020 [29] changed the significance of FBG, HOMA-IR index, and insulin analysis from non-significant to significant (Figure S28 and S29, respectively). Also, removing Navarrete-Cortes et al., 2014 [23] and Solati et al., [22] changed the result of FBG (Figure S30) and insulin significance, respectively. Based on the sensitivity analysis, these three studies, especially Sadeghian et al., 2020 [29], may be the source of heterogeneity.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, we evaluated the effects of magnesium supplementation on insulin resistance and glycemic outcomes in individuals with diabetes and prediabetes. The findings indicate that magnesium supplementation does not significantly improve primary indices of insulin resistance, including insulin levels and HOMA-IR. In contrast, significant benefits were identified for secondary outcomes, particularly serum magnesium levels and HbA1c, suggesting that magnesium may improve glycemic control through mechanisms not fully reflected by conventional insulin resistance markers. However, our findings suggest that magnesium supplementation may not be universally effective, but could be beneficial in individuals with higher baseline insulin resistance, which may explain inconsistencies in previous meta-analyses.

Our subgroup analyses for Mg supplementation effects on insulin levels in the present study demonstrated that lower doses of Mg (≤ 250 mg/day) were associated with a borderline reduction in insulin level, suggesting that moderate supplementation may be more effective than higher doses. Fong et al. [37] also suggested that higher magnesium usage could lead to lower circulating insulin concentration. This observation may be attributed to the saturable nature of intestinal magnesium absorption and the tight regulation of magnesium homeostasis [38].

Magnesium supplementation resulted in a significant increase in serum magnesium concentrations. Razzaghi et al. [31] in a 12-week trial, concluded a definite rise in serum Mg level in the magnesium group despite placebo.

Furthermore, a significant reduction in HbA1c was observed, particularly in studies with longer intervention durations (> 12 weeks) [20, 21, 33, 34]. Since HbA1c reflects long-term glycemic control, this finding indicates that magnesium supplementation may exert cumulative metabolic benefits over time [39]. Similarly, reductions in FBG were more evident in longer-duration studies, highlighting the importance of prolonged intervention. Maqrashi et al. [40], in a systematic review of 23 trials, concluded that Mg supplementation significantly decreases FBG and also increases serum Mg levels.

The discrepancy between improvements in HbA1c and the lack of significant changes in insulin resistance indices may suggest that magnesium acts through indirect or complementary pathways [41]. Magnesium plays a crucial role as a cofactor in over 300 enzymatic reactions, including those involved in glucose metabolism and insulin signaling [42]. It is essential for insulin receptor autophosphorylation and the activation of downstream pathways responsible for glucose uptake via GLUT4 transporters. Additionally, magnesium deficiency has been linked to increased oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation, both of which impair insulin signaling and contribute to metabolic dysfunction [43]. Restoration of magnesium levels may therefore improve metabolic homeostasis without producing large measurable changes in HOMA-IR or circulating insulin levels.

It is also important to acknowledge that HOMA-IR may not fully capture the heterogeneity of magnesium-related insulin resistance, particularly given that the dynamics and kinetics of magnesium differ between prediabetes and diabetes. Serum magnesium reflects only approximately 1% of total body magnesium, and normal serum values do not preclude intracellular depletion [44]. Studies have demonstrated that intracellular free magnesium concentrations are significantly reduced in type 2 diabetes even when serum magnesium appears within the normal reference range, and that this intracellular depletion directly impairs insulin receptor tyrosine kinase activity and downstream signaling [45, 46]. The failure of HOMA-IR to distinguish between true hypomagnesemia and functional magnesium deficit may therefore contribute to the attenuated and heterogeneous responses observed across the included trials.

Beyond HOMA-IR, emerging evidence highlights the potential utility of alternative surrogate markers of insulin resistance in the context of magnesium research. The Triglyceride-Glucose (TyG) index, calculated from fasting triglycerides and glucose, has been validated as a reliable and cost-effective indicator of insulin resistance, with demonstrated superiority in predicting prediabetes and metabolic syndrome progression [47]. Given that magnesium deficiency is independently associated with elevated triglycerides and reduced HDL cholesterol, the TyG index may provide a more metabolically sensitive framework for detecting magnesium-related dysglycemia than HOMA-IR alone [48, 49]. Similarly, the Single Point Insulin Sensitivity Estimator (SPISE), which incorporates triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, and BMI, has shown superior predictive accuracy for insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome compared to HOMA-IR in multiple cross-sectional studies [50, 51]. Future trials evaluating magnesium supplementation should consider incorporating these indices alongside HOMA-IR to more comprehensively characterize the metabolic impact of intervention.

Meta-regression analysis in this study revealed that higher baseline insulin levels and HOMA-IR were associated with smaller improvements following supplementation. This suggests that individuals with more severe insulin resistance may be less responsive to magnesium intervention or may require longer treatment durations. Furthermore, age was positively associated with changes in serum magnesium levels, indicating that older individuals may experience greater magnesium repletion, possibly due to lower baseline levels or altered absorption.

In prediabetic populations, magnesium supplementation did not significantly affect insulin resistance or glycemic outcomes. Previous observational studies have suggested that higher magnesium intake is associated with a reduced risk of developing type 2 diabetes, indicating a potential preventive role that may not be fully captured in short-term clinical trials [52].

This study has several strengths, including adherence to PRISMA guidelines, inclusion of only randomized controlled trials, and comprehensive subgroup and meta-regression analyses. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Significant heterogeneity across studies may have influenced the pooled estimates due to differences in magnesium formulations, dosage, duration, and baseline patient characteristics. Additionally, the relatively small number of studies in certain subgroup analyses limits statistical power. The findings from subgroup and meta-regression analyses should be interpreted with caution, as they were exploratory in nature and intended primarily to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity. The meta-regression analyses should be interpreted cautiously because they were based on a limited number of studies and involved the evaluation of multiple potential moderators, increasing the possibility of chance findings. In addition, these analyses relied on study-level aggregate data; therefore, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of individual-level treatment responses. Consequently, the meta-regression findings should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Also, it should be mentioned that the restriction to insulin-reporting trials may have limited the comprehensiveness of secondary outcome analyses; however, this approach ensured consistency in the primary objective of the study. It should be noted that there was clinical heterogeneity among the included populations. Participants were recruited from a range of diabetes-related clinical settings, including diabetic foot ulcers, diabetic nephropathy, hemodialysis, hypomagnesemia, and normomagnesemia. Such variability may influence responses to magnesium supplementation and could limit the clinical interpretability and generalizability of the pooled estimates. This baseline clinical setting should be considered in future studies. Furthermore, although insulin and HOMA-IR were selected as primary indices of insulin resistance based on their consistent reporting across included trials and their established use in RCT settings, it is acknowledged that C-peptide was not systematically reported across the included studies and could not be incorporated into the primary analysis. Given its potential to more accurately differentiate between prediabetic and diabetic states, future trials evaluating magnesium supplementation are encouraged to include C-peptide as a complementary outcome measure. Although some subgroup and meta-regression analyses suggested potential factors that may influence treatment response, the overall pooled effects on insulin and HOMA-IR were not statistically significant. Therefore, the evidence remains insufficient to conclude that magnesium supplementation leads to clinically meaningful improvements in insulin resistance.

Conclusion

In conclusion, magnesium supplementation appears to improve serum magnesium levels and glycemic control, particularly HbA1c, in individuals with prediabetes, but does not significantly reduce insulin resistance as measured by HOMA-IR or insulin levels. The effectiveness of supplementation may vary according to dosage, duration, and baseline metabolic status; however, the current evidence remains insufficient to demonstrate a consistent effect on insulin resistance. Further large-scale, well-designed randomized controlled trials are warranted to clarify the role of magnesium in insulin resistance and to identify populations most likely to benefit.
