TNF as a mediator of metabolic inflammation and body-brain interaction in obesity-driven neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration
Authors: Chih Hung Lo, Jialiu Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBody–brain interaction (BBI) plays a critical role in coordinating the communication between peripheral organs and the brain, contributing to the comorbidity of metabolic disorders and neurological disorders. In the context of obesity, one of the key mediators driving systemic and neuroinflammatory responses is the soluble form of tumor necrosis factor (TNF), which primarily signals through TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1) to regulate inflammation and cell death. In this review, we examine how TNF/TNFR1-mediated metabolic inflammation in obesity disrupts cellular homeostasis across multiple organ systems, including the brain. In peripheral tissues, TNF is overproduced and secreted by activated macrophages, leading to lipid dysmetabolism, insulin resistance, and metabolic dysfunction in key cell types such as adipocytes and hepatocytes. Elevated circulating TNF also increases the permeability of the blood–brain barrier, enabling peripheral inflammatory mediators to infiltrate the brain and activate glial cells, thereby amplifying neuroinflammation. Within the brain, TNF induces metabolic and autolysosomal dysfunction in neurons, resulting in elevated reactive oxygen species, accumulation of toxic protein aggregates, and impaired insulin signaling, contributing collectively to neuronal death and the progression of neurodegeneration. We further highlight the metabolic-inflammatory crosstalk within the BBI as a potential therapeutic target, focusing on anti-inflammatory strategies that modulate TNF/TNFR1 signaling. Lastly, we provide future perspectives on the implications of body–brain axes, cell type–specific mechanisms, and disease comorbidities in the context of obesity.Keywords: TNFR1 signaling, Body-brain interaction, Metabolic inflammation, Necroptosis, Blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability, Therapeutic targeting

Introduction

Neurological disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and ischemic stroke, are increasingly linked to chronic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction. Metabolic syndromes - including obesity, type 2 diabetes (T2D), atherosclerosis, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), have emerged as major risk factors contributing to both the onset and progression of these neurological conditions (Amorim et al., 2022; Le Thuc and García-Cáceres, 2024; Ly et al., 2023; Procaccini et al., 2016; Takahashi et al., 2007). These syndromes are characterized by excessive nutrient intake and abnormal lipid accumulation in peripheral tissues, which promote systemic lipid dysmetabolism, insulin resistance, metabolic disturbances, and persistent low-grade inflammation (Boden-Albala et al., 2008; Freeman et al., 2014; Sripetchwandee et al., 2018; Takahashi et al., 2007) that extend beyond the body, significantly impacting brain function (Asimakidou et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2025a; Zeng and Lo, 2025). At the cellular level, such metabolic disruptions contribute to neuroinflammation, impaired autolysosomal degradation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and central insulin resistance, all of which accelerate neuronal dysfunction and degeneration (Lo, 2024; Miller and Spencer, 2014; Schmitt and Gaspar, 2023; Sripetchwandee et al., 2018). These interdependent processes converge into the notion of the metabolic-inflammatory axis, describing the complex, bidirectional relationship between metabolic dysregulation and chronic inflammation. This axis not only fuels disease progression systemically but also plays a pivotal role in body-brain interaction (BBI), further aggravating neuropathology (Hachiya et al., 2022; Sui and Pasco, 2020; Yin et al., 2017; Zeng and Lo, 2025). The growing recognition of the metabolic-inflammatory axis provides new opportunities for therapeutic intervention in neurological disorders.

Multiple evidence has suggested a strong correlation between obesity and neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration, potentially acting through BBI and this has been primarily attributed to the transmission and propagation of inflammatory cytokines from the peripheral to the central nervous system (CNS) (Miller and Spencer, 2014; Salas-Venegas et al., 2022). Amongst the inflammatory cytokines, the soluble form of tumor necrosis factor (TNF), primarily interacting with TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1), emerges as a crucial mediator of inflammation across various systemic chronic inflammatory conditions as well as neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration through BBI (McCoy and Tansey, 2008; Shamansurova et al., 2020; Siegmund and Wajant, 2023). TNF cytokine binds to TNFR1 at the extracellular domain of the receptor and triggers a structural alteration in the receptor-ligand arrangement, facilitating the recruitment of receptor-interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1) and TNFR1-associated death domain (TRADD), leading to the assembly of downstream complexes I and II (Fig. 1A) (Kucka and Wajant, 2021; Lo, 2025a). Complex I activates the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway, responsible for initiating the expression of cytokines and genes that are responsible for survival and inflammation. On the other hand, complex II instigates cell death signaling, including apoptosis and necroptosis (Kucka and Wajant, 2021). The fate of the cell, whether it undergoes apoptosis or necroptosis, is determined by the activity of caspase-8, including its expression level, phosphorylation state, and interaction with cellular degradation systems like the ubiquitin-proteosome system or the autolysosomal network (Ye et al., 2023). Disruption or absence of caspase-8 leads to non-cleavage of RIPK1, resulting in subsequent phosphorylation of RIPK1, RIPK3, and pseudokinase mixed lineage kinase domain-like (MLKL), initiating necroptosis (Ye et al., 2023). Phosphorylated MLKL (pMLKL) forms oligomers and translocate to insert into the cellular membrane, forming pores and causing the release of intracellular molecules, which are characteristic features of necroptotic cell death (Dhuriya and Sharma, 2018; Samson et al., 2020).

Under physiological and homeostatic conditions, TNF is produced at low levels in the brain and plays essential roles in regulating synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis, learning, and memory (Kleidonas et al., 2022; Mygind et al., 2021; Yli-Karjanmaa et al., 2019b). In 11–13 months-old TNF knockout male mice, reduced spatial learning and memory were observed (Mygind et al., 2021). TNF deficiency also led to a decreased number of proliferating cells and microglia, along with an increased number of neurons in the developing neocortex at embryonic day 13.5 (E13.5), postnatal day 7 (P7), and in adult TNF knockout mice. Furthermore, long-term inhibition of TNF using etanercept in adult C57BL/6 mice reduced the number of proliferating cells in the granule cell layer of the dentate gyrus and impaired spatial learning and memory (Yli-Karjanmaa et al., 2019b). Consistent with these findings, another study demonstrated that low concentrations of TNF enhanced excitatory synaptic strength without affecting inhibitory neurotransmission in mouse organotypic entorhinal-hippocampal tissue cultures (Kleidonas et al., 2022).

Under pathological conditions such as ischemia-reperfusion injury and heart failure, TNF displays dose- and time-dependent effects, providing cytoprotection during transient exposure but contributing to tissue damage and dysfunction when exposure is sustained (Kleinbongard et al., 2011, 2010). In vascular disease models, TNF plays a central role in driving chronic inflammation associated with atherosclerosis. For instance, in low-density lipoprotein (LDL)-deficient mice, systemic inflammation is attenuated following treatment with anti-TNF monoclonal antibodies, demonstrating the cytokine’s contribution to atherogenesis (Oberoi et al., 2018). Furthermore, in LDL receptor–deficient mice with macrophage-specific deletion of IκB kinase 2 (IKK2), a key activator of NF-κB signaling, atherosclerosis paradoxically worsens, characterized by increased lesion area, necrosis, and plaque complexity, alongside diminished levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Kanters et al., 2003). These findings underscore the delicate balance of TNF-mediated immune regulation in atherosclerotic plaque formation and progression. Importantly, atherosclerosis, often a consequence of metabolic syndrome, is a well-established precursor to ischemic stroke (Boden-Albala et al., 2008; Takahashi et al., 2007), and shared inflammatory pathways further support mechanistic continuity between these conditions. Together, these studies support a unifying role for chronic TNF/TNFR1 signaling in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis and ischemic stroke. They highlight how systemic metabolic inflammation and vascular dysfunction converge on the brain, reinforcing the relevance of targeting the metabolic-inflammatory axis in preventing and treating both cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease.

Beyond the brain, obesity is also associated with heightened peripheral inflammation, characterized by increased macrophage activation and infiltration of immune cells, particularly within adipose tissue depots (Cancello et al., 2006; Russo and Lumeng, 2018). In addition, a study demonstrated that in both obese mice and humans, the majority of macrophages in white adipose tissue (WAT) are localized around dead or dying adipocytes (Cinti et al., 2005). In the short term, obesity triggers the overproduction and secretion of adipokines and proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF, from these macrophages, contributing to inflammation, insulin resistance, and mitochondrial and autolysosomal dysfunction (Fig. 1B) (Arden et al., 2024; Ju et al., 2019; Sadeghi et al., 2023). Long-term effect of obesity results in impairments and death of primary functional cells such as adipocyte and hepatocyte as well as neurons in the brain (Fig. 1B). Besides active inflammation, necroptotic cell death can also lead to the formation of the damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which results in the bursting of the cells and the release of toxic factors and more cytokines. Peripheral proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF, have been shown to cross the BBB (Huang et al., 2021; Varatharaj and Galea, 2017). Once in the brain, these cytokines can interact with local resident immune cells or glial cells to exacerbate the inflammatory processes in the brain (Sun et al., 2022). This mechanism of obesity-induced neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration has been implicated in AD (De Sousa Rodrigues et al., 2019; Plantone et al., 2024; Sethi and Hotamisligil, 2021), PD (Amin et al., 2022; Batarseh and Al Thaher, 2023; Cavalheiro et al., 2022), and multiple sclerosis (MS) (Correale and Marrodan, 2022; Fresegna et al., 2020; Marrodan et al., 2021; Schreiner and Genes, 2021). Importantly, TNF/TNFR1 signaling could negatively modulate metabolic activities such as mitochondrial and autolysosomal functions as well as insulin resistance which further leads to impaired synaptic plasticity, neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative phenotypes, as well as memory and cognitive deficits (Asimakidou et al., 2024; Bomfim et al., 2012; Lourenco et al., 2013; Maldonado-Ruiz et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2023). Furthermore, early and reversible changes in hypothalamus (McLean et al., 2019a; Thaler et al., 2013) and hippocampal proteome (McLean et al., 2019b) that are associated with immune response and inflammation in HFD fed obese mice indicate the feasibility of targeting both peripheral and central inflammation to attenuate obesity-induced neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration.

Although evidence has shown that TNF mediates the connection between disorders in the peripheral and central nervous systems through BBI (Quarta et al., 2022; Salas-Venegas et al., 2022; Sethi and Hotamisligil, 2021; Uranga and Keller, 2019), it remains unclear of the exact mechanisms of TNF production and transmission and its implications on the brain in obesity. In this review, we will explore how the increase in TNF production and secretion arises under obesity condition, and how it affects the peripheral organs and enters the brain to induce neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. We also propose the role of the metabolic-inflammatory axis in driving disease progression and explore potential therapeutic strategies to mitigate its impact. Finally, we present future perspectives on the implications of various body-brain axes, highlighting cell-type-specific mechanisms and BBI-mediated comorbidity of metabolic and neurological disorders associated with obesity.

Obesity-induced TNF overproduction and secretion from peripheral organs

Metabolic inflammation is characteristic of obesity-related metabolic disorders, associated with increased risk of development of T2D, MASLD, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), or cardiovascular disease. Metabolic inflammation is a state of chronic, low-grade inflammation that can be driven by a range of adverse dietary factors (Charles-Messance et al., 2020). Metabolic inflammation can be detected by routine clinical laboratory testing, indicated by an elevated high-sensitivity c-reactive protein test, which reflects increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF (Tilg et al., 2024). Metabolic inflammation in obesity affects major organs including the adipose tissue, liver, pancreas, heart, and brain (Tilg et al., 2024). In obese diabetic patients, serum TNF levels were significantly higher than in nonobese diabetic patients (Alzamil, 2020). In addition, patients under conditions of severe obesity produce elevated levels of TNF, together with several other cytokines and adipokines (Jonkers et al., 2002). One contribution to this lies in the different adipocyte composition in obese versus lean patients.

In obese patients, cell types of innate and adaptive immunity have been shown to infiltrate the obese adipose tissues (Cancello et al., 2006). Soluble TNF is largely produced by adipocytes and macrophages in the body and binds to TNFR1 and TNFR2 (H S Schipper et al., 2012a). Under obesity condition, there is mitochondrial dysfunction and increased oxidative stress in the adipose tissue (Li et al., 2023). The adipose tissue secretes adipokines and cytokines including adiponectin, resistin, TNF, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (Arnoldussen et al., 2014; Scheja and Heeren, 2019). Adipose tissue macrophages (ATM) are responsible for almost all TNF expression in the adipose tissue (Fig. 2A) (Weisberg et al., 2003). The continuing and dynamic interplay among various adipokines and cytokines further propagates inflammation in the periphery (Vachliotis and Polyzos, 2023a). Using a mouse model with TNF deletion specifically in the ATM, it was shown that TNF deletion significantly reduced adipose tissue TNF gene expression and circulating TNF, and downregulated genes linked to the NF-κB signaling pathways (Aladhami et al., 2021). Interestingly, TNF deletion in macrophage had no effect on hindering the development of obesity, hepatic lipid accumulation, or improving glucose metabolism or insulin sensitivity, suggesting that macrophage-derived TNF is not a causative factor for the induction of obesity-associated metabolic dysfunction (Aladhami et al., 2021).

In obese patients, cell types of innate and adaptive immunity infiltrate adipose tissues (Cancello et al., 2006). Soluble TNF is produced largely by adipocytes and macrophages, acting through both TNFR1 and TNFR2, as reviewed in (Henk S Schipper et al., 2012b). While TNFR1 primarily mediates pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic responses, TNFR2 has been increasingly recognized for its role in immune regulation and tissue repair (Fischer et al., 2020). Elevated serum TNFR2 and adipose TNFR2 expression have been reported in human obesity, including in obese Hispanic women and other cohorts (Connor et al., 2022; Good et al., 2006; Hotamisligil et al., 1997; Mohamed-Ali et al., 1999; Rönnemaa et al., 2000). Genetic studies in animal models suggest that TNFR1 ablation typically reduces metabolic and inflammatory pathology, whereas TNFR2 deletion often worsens outcomes (Fischer et al., 2020). TNFR2 is also implicated in obesity-related metabolic regulation (Liu et al., 1998), though its specific role in HFD models remains underexplored. Notably, TNFR2 agonists have shown therapeutic potential in autoimmune disease models (Fiedler et al., 2023), raising the possibility of future applications in metabolic diseases where immune homeostasis is disrupted.

Beyond its role in adipose inflammation, TNF/TNFR1 signaling exhibits depot-specific differences across WAT and brown adipose tissue (BAT), as well as between regional WAT depots (e.g., subcutaneous vs. visceral). Functionally, WAT primarily stores and mobilizes energy and secretes endocrine signals, whereas BAT specializes in energy dissipation via β-oxidation coupled with thermogenesis (Cinti, 2006). Obesity in both rodents and humans is associated with increased numbers of apoptotic and necrotic adipocytes in both WAT and BAT (Cinti et al., 2005; Strissel et al., 2007). TNF impairs insulin sensitivity in both tissue types (Nieto-Vazquez et al., 2008), but BAT appears to be more sensitive to TNF-induced dysfunction and apoptosis compared to WAT (Nisoli et al., 2006). Interestingly, TNF expression is more abundant in WAT compared to BAT under HFD conditions, suggesting depot-specific expression and function (Aquilano et al., 2019). TNF has been shown to modulate BAT thermogenic capacity in conflicting ways, either through upregulating uncoupling protein (UCP) expression in some studies (Coombes et al., 1987; Masaki et al., 1999), or downregulating UCP1 and β3-adrenergic receptor expression in other studies (Nisoli et al., 2000; Porras et al., 2002; Valladares et al., 2001). WAT is distributed in distinct anatomical regions including subcutaneous, visceral (intra-abdominal), and smaller periorgan depots (Wajchenberg, 2000). Human adipose tissue displays region-specific differences in secretory function that may influence systemic metabolic homeostasis. For instance, although leptin, angiotensinogen, IL-6, and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 show clear regional expression differences between subcutaneous and visceral fat, no consistent site-specific differences in TNF production have been observed (Arner, 2001). Nevertheless, it remains plausible that regional variations in adipokine profiles, including TNF and its receptors, contribute to the distinct inflammatory and metabolic outcomes associated with central (visceral) versus peripheral (subcutaneous) obesity.

The adipose tissue also communicates with other organs such as the liver, pancreas, and intestines via endocrine activities (Tilg et al., 2024). Among them, the liver constitutes a major metabolic organ that is most affected by adipose tissue metabolism (Azzu et al., 2020). In response to obesity and metabolic stress, the liver releases hepatokines such as TNF, angiopoietin-like protein 3, fibroblast growth factor 21, selenoprotein B and follistatin (Stefan et al., 2023). These hepatokines affect glucose and lipid metabolism and have pro-inflammatory and immunomodulatory activities (Stefan et al., 2023). In human MASLD/MASH patients, it was also shown that there is a higher level of TNF circulation in the blood stream (Gómez-Hurtado et al., 2020). Interestingly, in obese patients, compared to peripheral fat tissues, hepatic tissues of patients with MASH showed an elevated increase in the expression of both TNF and TNFR1 mRNA levels (Crespo et al., 2001). Elevated TNF is also observed in different HFD mouse models. In wild type (WT) mice, 12 weeks of HFD raised phosphorylated p65 and TNF levels (Hu et al., 2025), while 16 weeks of HFD further increased phosphorylated p65 and reduced IκB expression, indicating NF-κB activation in the liver (Wang et al., 2024). A study using S467A knock-in mice, where p65 serine 467 was replaced with alanine to reduce p65 synthesis and TNF-induced NF-κB activation, examined the effects of NF-κB inhibition on liver inflammation. After 12 weeks of HFD, S467A mice showed lower body weight, increased locomotor activity and energy expenditure, and reduced hepatic TNF levels, but no improvement in glucose metabolism or insulin sensitivity (Riedlinger et al., 2017).

In the presence of liver steatosis, TNF is produced by resident hepatic cells, Kupffer cells, and infiltrating immune cells (Polyzos et al., 2009), where Kupffer cells are the major contributor of TNF under diet induced MASH (Su, 2002). In addition, the progression of MASH is partially dependent on Kupffer cells producing TNF (Nakagawa et al., 2014; Todoric et al., 2020). Silencing NF-κB in Kupffer cells reduced IL-1β expression and improved hepatic insulin sensitivity in obese mice, indicating a role for liver macrophage-derived IL-1β in obesity-induced liver dysfunction (Tencerova et al., 2015). However, direct IL-1β silencing in Kupffer cells did not improve insulin sensitivity, suggesting that NF-κB, which is downstream of TNFR1 signaling pathways, regulates hepatic insulin sensitivity independently of IL-1β (Morgantini et al., 2019). In co-cultures of Kupffer cells and hepatocytes or supernatant transfer experiments, TNF secretion by Kupffer cells is necessary and sufficient to induce steatosis in hepatocytes, independent of the challenge of hepatocytes with elevated fatty acid levels. In addition, this study further shows that free fatty acid (FFA) induced lipotoxicity can trigger release of TNF from Kupffer cells, indicating that Kupffer cells function as the primary sensor for FFA overload, and transmit the information to the hepatocyte via TNF secretion (Diehl et al., 2020). Hepatocytes that are stressed by lipotoxicity can also contribute to TNF production via DAMPs or pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) (Alisi et al., 2011). Hepatocyte-specific TNFR1 ablation have been shown to reverse steatosis, liver injury and MASH (Wandrer et al., 2020). In the liver of TNFR1 and TNFR2 double knockout (KO) mice after feeding on methionine- and choline-deficient (MCD) diet which lacks essential nutrients for lipid metabolism and liver health, the number of activated hepatic Kupffer cells recruited was significantly decreased with hepatic induction of TNF being significantly suppressed (Tomita et al., 2006). In mutant mice (RIPK1K45A/K45A), where RIPK1 lacks enzymatic activity, 24 weeks of HFD resulted in a reduced MASH phenotype, hepatic cell death, and inflammation compared to WT mice (Tao et al., 2021).

On the other hand, a study reported that TNFR1 signaling disruption in TNFR1 KO mice upon HFD feeding leads to an accelerated progression from simple steatosis to a more severe phenotype with many MASH features, pointing out a key role of TNFR1 in MASLD progression (Lambertucci et al., 2018). In another study, it was shown that livers of TNF and TNFR1 KO mice fed with MCD diet showed equivalent steatohepatitis (dela Peña et al., 2005). Interestingly, NF-ĸB activation was enhanced in hepatic nuclear fractions from MCD dietary feeding in TNF and TNFR1 KO mice compared with respective controls. Blockade of NF-κB transcriptional activation substantially protected against development of steatohepatitis, with significant reductions in liver injury and hepatic inflammation. This indicates in the MCD dietary model of steatohepatitis, NF-κB is activated early and is an important proinflammatory mediator of lesion development, and steatohepatitis may occur independently of TNF synthesis and TNFR1 activation (dela Peña et al., 2005).

Among the different types of cell death mechanisms in MASLD/MASH, necroptosis is considerably present in both human and experimental models of MASLD/MASH (Afonso et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). Hepatic levels of RIPK3 are particularly increased in MASLD patients carrying the variant of the lipid droplet lipase patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing protein three (Afonso et al., 2021), an established risk factor for altered hepatic metabolism. This suggests a link between necroptosis associated signalling pathways, MASLD pathogenesis, and lipid metabolism dysregulation in human (Afonso et al., 2024). Enhanced levels of key players involved in necroptosis induction have been found in both the liver and serum of MASLD patients, correlating with disease severity (Gautheron et al., 2014; Majdi et al., 2020; Saeed et al., 2019). Serum markers of necroptosis, such as liver TNF, RIPK3, and pMLKL were increased in MASLD patients (Afonso et al., 2015). Increased RIPK3 was also observed in MASH patients which correlated with hepatic inflammation and fibrosis (Afonso et al., 2021). In mice with 18 weeks of HFD feeding, TNF, RIPK3, and MLKL expression were increased in the liver (Afonso et al., 2015). In addition, functional studies in primary murine hepatocytes established the association between TNF-induced RIPK3 expression, activation of necroptosis and oxidative stress (Afonso et al., 2015). This indicates that necroptosis is increased in the liver of MASLD patients and in experimental models of MASH. In other studies, it was shown that the loss of MLKL prevents metabolic dysfunction and hepatic steatosis upon HFD (Saeed et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2019). In mice fed on HFD for 12 weeks, there is increased expression of RIPK3 as well as pMLKL, an effector of necroptotic cell death, in the liver. HFD also increased hepatic steatosis and TNF levels in mice (Roychowdhury et al., 2016).

In summary, these studies provide evidence on how obesity and lipid overload in primary functional cells such as adipocyte and hepatocyte could trigger inflammation through TNF/TNFR1 signaling and ultimately leading to cellular impairments and death (Fig. 2B). In addition to affecting the adipose tissues and the liver, obesity also has detrimental effects on other organs. For example, obesity leads to fat accumulation in the pancreas, leading to increased expression of cytokines including TNF and fibrosis (Donath, 2013; Mathur et al., 2007; Smits and van Geenen, 2011). In the gastrointestinal tract, obesity results in increased expression of TNF in mice colons (Moreno-Navarrete et al., 2010). Interestingly, under obesity condition, myokines, including myostatin and irisin, are mainly released by skeletal muscle, which attenuate inflammation and increase glucose metabolism and fatty acid oxidation (Boström et al., 2012).

Elevated circulating TNF and BBB hyperpermeability

Due to obesity-induced inflammation in the adipose, liver, pancreas, and other organs, there is increased TNF in the blood stream (Fig. 2C) (Arnoldussen et al., 2014; Gómez-Hurtado et al., 2020; Scheja and Heeren, 2019). It has been reported that TNF is elevated in the blood of obese human patients (Alzamil, 2020; Jonkers et al., 2002). A critical component of the cerebrovascular system is the BBB, which strictly regulates the exchange of substances between the blood and the brain. This dynamic barrier ensures the stability of brain microenvironment, protects against neurotoxins, and facilitates proper neuronal function. However, in pathological states such as obesity, which leads to increased serum TNF levels, this pivotal barrier can be compromised, leading to various defects associated with neurological disorders (Fig. 3A) (Corti et al., 2022; Feng et al., 2024). Understanding the intricate relationship between obesity, inflammation, and BBB dysfunction therefore becomes a matter of paramount importance.

After TNF enters the brain, it interacts with local resident immune cells or glial cells to exacerbate neuroinflammation. (B) Stimulation of TNFR1 signaling by excessive TNF induces various metabolic defects including impairments in mitochondrial and autolysosomal functions as well as insulin resistance through the metabolic-inflammatory axis. Ultimately, this results in TNFR1-mediated neuronal dysfunction and necroptosis, leading to neurodegeneration. The schematic is created using BioRender.

HFD mice had increased expression of inflammatory cytokines including TNF in the median eminence, preceding changes in other circumventricular regions. This led to an early loss of the structural organization of the median eminence β1-tanycytes (Ramalho et al., 2018). An hour of exposure to HFD could already increase BBB permeability (Leidmaa et al., 2024). In mice treated with a TNF inhibitor, HFD-induced BBB dysfunction and behavioral deficits were mitigated, suggesting that inflammatory signaling was critical for inducing these pathological changes (de Paula et al., 2021). In AD mice, obesogenic diet enhances BBB permeability, disrupts innate and adaptive immune cell populations in the blood, promotes gut dysbiosis, and enhanced trafficking of immune cells to the CNS through soluble TNF-dependent mechanisms (Herrick et al., 2020). TNF has also been shown to induce leukocyte adhesion to cerebral vessels through the upregulation of adhesion molecules such as vascular cell adhesion molecule (VCAM) and intercellular adhesion molecule (Pober and Cotran, 1990) and can further contribute to BBB disruption (de Vries et al., 1996). Furthermore, BBB hyperpermeability, accounted by an increase in hippocampal albumin content as well as a decrease in claudin-5 protein levels and collagen IV immunostaining, was also observed together with an upregulation of VCAM-1 (de Aquino et al., 2019).

Among the several adipokines and cytokines produced by the adipose tissues and periphery, TNF, IL-6, and leptin have been shown to be more predominantly able to cross the BBB (Arnoldussen et al., 2014). In a cognitive continuum group from normal to dementia conditions, it was found that TNF levels were significantly correlated with Mini-Mental State Examination scores, while IL-6 and leptin showed no association with global cognition or function. This indicates the TNF is the more critical cytokine causing changes in the brain (Magalhães et al., 2018). Transport of TNF across the BBB occurs via receptor-mediated transcytosis involving TNF receptors TNFR1 (p55) and TNFR2 (p75) expressed on brain endothelial cells (Banks et al., 2001; Pan and Kastin, 2007, 2002; Simone et al., 2006). In mice, circulating TNF has been shown to cross the BBB via a specific, saturable transport mechanism (Gutierrez et al., 1993). To elucidate the role of TNF receptors in this process, (Pan and Kastin, 2002) examined TNF influx in receptor knockout mice. In mice lacking either p55 or p75, transport of 125I-TNF into the spinal cord was significantly, but not completely reduced, with minimal effects observed in the brain. However, in double-knockout mice lacking both receptors, TNF entry into both brain and spinal cord was entirely abolished. These findings indicate that both TNFR1 and TNFR2 contribute synergistically to TNF transport across the BBB and that neither receptor alone is sufficient to mediate full transcytosis (Pan and Kastin, 2002). In a different study, administration of radioactively labeled TNF in the jugular vein in both WT mice and a mice model of AD, shows that the hypothalamus had the fastest (1.73 microl/g/min) rates of uptake, while the parietal cortex had the slowest (0.189 microl/g/min) rates, a regional difference of about 9-fold. Within these two regions, there are no differences in the rate of transport between both strains of mice, suggesting region-specific transport. The authors also showed that transport was higher for the occipital cortex, midbrain, and striatum in aged AD mice. These results show blood-borne TNF enters some regions of the brain much more readily than others and TNF transport into some brain regions of the AD mice is increased at an age when learning and memory are impaired (Banks et al., 2001). However, it remains unclear whether obesity alters the transport of these cytokines across the BBB, and if so, how it affects TNF transcytosis (Rhea et al., 2017).

In addition to propagating through the blood stream, cytokines such as TNF have the capacity to transmit signals across the BBB through stimulation of the afferent vagus nerve (Guilherme et al., 2019; Watkins et al., 1995; Zanos et al., 2018). Cholinergic vagal efferent fibers stimulate enteric neurons that inhibit the release of inflammatory cytokines TNF, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-18 from peripheral macrophages (Guilherme et al., 2019; Watkins et al., 1995; Zanos et al., 2018). Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) has been a way to attenuate inflammation propagation from the peripheral to the brain. Chronic VNS reduced food intake, body weight gain, epididymal fat pad weight, and inflammation in adult male rats fed on HFD. In another study, plasma TNF was increased after 12 weeks of HFD feeding in rats, and rats after 12 weeks of VNS treatment had significantly decreased TNF levels in both plasma and brain (Chunchai et al., 2016). The underlying mechanisms of VNS induced weight loss have been proposed to be due to the stimulation of vagal afferent fibers which provided the satiating effects via a gut-brain feedback mechanism, leading to lower food consumption and subsequent weight loss (Bray, 2000; Ritter et al., 1989).

TNF-mediated neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration under obesity conditions

The detrimental effects of TNF in the brain are illustrated by the presence of glial activation or neuroinflammation (Fig. 3A) as well as metabolic dysfunction and neuronal death or neurodegeneration (Fig. 3B) (Clark et al., 2010; Gonzalez Caldito, 2023; Hennessy et al., 2017; Rohm et al., 2022). In neuronal and microglia cells such as N2a or BV2 cells, short-term exposure to lipotoxicity induced by palmitate results in increased expression of apoptotic and inflammatory genes (Nakandakari et al., 2019). In mice and rats with short-term HFD feeding between 3 and 21 days, there is increased fasting glucose and insulin resistance indicative of metabolic dysfunction (Nakandakari et al., 2019). In the brain of these mice, there is presence of gliosis and neuroinflammation (Guyenet et al., 2013; C.-J. Xu et al., 2021) together with elevated cytokines including TNF and IL-1β (Nakandakari et al., 2019). There are dysregulated pathways such as the activation of extracellular signal-regulated kinases 1 and 2, p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase, phosphorylated c-Jun N-terminal kinase, caspase-3, and cleaved caspase-3, as well as upregulation of AD-related markers including Aβ, phosphorylated tau, Bax, protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B and presenilin-1 (Guyenet et al., 2013; Nakandakari et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2021). Another study demonstrated that mice with 3–6 weeks of HFD feeding illustrated depressive-like behavior, impaired brain structures, dysregulated synaptic plasticity, increased neuronal cell death, and aberrant inflammatory responses with elevated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and reduced expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines (Wu et al., 2018). In a study comparing the effects of cytokines in mice fed on HFD, TNFR1 deficient mice are protected from obesity-induced inflammation in the brain while IL-10 deficient mice demonstrate increased inflammation (Morari et al., 2014). These data indicate the significant detrimental role of TNF/TNFR1 signaling in obesity-induced neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration.

While short-term HFD feeding in mice triggers early neuroinflammation and neuronal dysfunction (Fig. 3A), long-term HFD feeding exacerbates these conditions with propagation of neuroinflammation as well as more pronounced metabolic defects and neuronal loss in different brain regions (Fig. 3B). In the context of acute brain injury, such as ischemic stroke, HFD has also been shown to worsen neuroinflammation and outcomes. HFD enhances TNF-driven infiltration of peripheral macrophages and leads to depletion of neuroprotective microglia within the infarct core, exacerbating astrocytic scarring and neuronal death (Fifield et al., 2019; Langdon et al., 2011; Li et al., 2013). These animals also display pronounced microgliosis and astrogliosis, correlating with increased infarct volumes and poorer post-stroke recovery. Transcriptomic analyses reveal that these neuroinflammatory changes emerge specifically after stroke onset: while no differences in inflammation or infarct size are observed at early time points (10 h post-injury), HFD-fed animals exhibit strong upregulation of inflammatory gene expression by day 3 (Peterson et al., 2021). Specifically, HFD treatment upregulated disease-associated markers in both microglia and astrocytes, including microglia-specific markers such as Csf2ra and Adam8, and astrocyte-specific markers such as C4b and Gdpd2 (Lin et al., 2023) with phenotypic spectrum in a region-specific manner (Baufeld et al., 2016). The complement system, including C4b, has been implicated in the progression of AD by promoting inflammation and contributing to neuronal damage (Ziabska et al., 2021). Studies in animal models further suggest that inhibiting components of the complement cascade may reduce Aβ accumulation and improve cognitive function (Hansen et al., 2017). The ADAM family of zinc metalloproteinases, including Adam8, plays a beneficial role in proteolysis by enhancing Aβ degradation and reducing its production in AD (Gough et al., 2011). In HFD mouse, Adam8 expression is significantly elevated compared to controls (Horakova et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2013). In a genetic model of obesity, there is an increase in the activation and number of glial cells, along with reduction in the number of neurons (Andrade et al., 2023). Interesting, inhibition of NF-κB signaling in astrocytes prevents acute HFD-induced astrocyte activation (Buckman et al., 2015), while its inhibition in microglia reduces obesity-induced adiposity and body weight but paradoxically worsens HFD-induced glucose intolerance (Douglass et al., 2023), highlighting cell-specific differences.

Besides inducing and propagating inflammation, TNF also contributes to obesity-induced metabolic perturbations by impairing mitochondrial and autolysosomal functions (Obara-Michlewska, 2022; Quick et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2025b). Altered regulation of ubiquitin-proteasome system was also observed in WT and TNFR1 deficient mice fed with HFD (Ignacio-Souza et al., 2014), further exemplifying the crosstalk between inflammation and protein degradation systems (Asimakidou et al., 2024; Gómez-Díaz and Ikeda, 2019; Kocaturk and Gozuacik, 2018; C. Xu et al., 2021). These defects further lead to neuronal cell death and progressive loss of neurons as well as memory and cognitive dysfunction in HFD mice (Alkan et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022). Progressive neuronal loss and cognitive impairments are prominent in HFD fed mice. In mouse brains, neuronal loss can be seen in the midbrain (Bittencourt et al., 2022; Jang et al., 2017) and the hippocampal regions (Alkan et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2024), along with elevated cytokine level including TNF, insulin resistance, synaptic dysfunction, and cognitive impairments which can be alleviated with treatments that reduced inflammation (Liu et al., 2014).

Recently, specific regulated cell death such as TNF/TNFR1-mediated necroptosis has been shown in neurons under lipotoxic condition with treatment of palmitate as well as in the hippocampus of HFD induced T2D-like mice (Preeti et al., 2023). These mice exhibited metabolic impairments, including insulin resistance, autolysosomal, and mitochondrial dysfunctions in the hippocampus. There were also BBB leakage, neuronal necroptosis, glial activation, as well as tau and amyloid oligomer accumulation, leading to impaired behavioral performance in the mice (Preeti et al., 2023). Importantly, administration of necrostatin-1 (Nec-1), an inhibitor of RIPK1 downstream of TNFR1 signaling, restored metabolic function, reduced neuronal necroptosis and glial activation, and improved cognition in T2D-like mice (Preeti et al., 2023). To support the presence of TNF/TNFR1-mediated neuronal necroptosis under obesity condition, elevated pMLKL levels have been shown in motor neurons in the spinal cord (Qiang et al., 2024), dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra (Song et al., 2024), hypothalamus (Li et al., 2021), and cortex (Yawoot et al., 2022). While several studies have demonstrated the attenuation of neuroinflammation and neuronal necroptosis to restore metabolic functions (Jinawong et al., 2020; Preeti et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2015), emerging evidence suggests that rescuing metabolic defects can also mitigate neuroinflammation and necroptosis (Li et al., 2021; Lo et al., 2023), highlighting the bidirectional interplay of the metabolic-inflammatory axis (Fig. 3B).

With metabolic defects and presence of neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration, specifically necroptosis in some cases, HFD fed mice illustrate similar phenotypes as AD mice. Importantly, long-term HFD exposure leads to the increased expression and accumulation of phosphorylated tau and Aβ in the hippocampus and a significant increase in TNF in the brain tissues and in blood circulation (Busquets et al., 2017; Niu et al., 2016). The presence of metabolic dysfunction could further exacerbate the neurotoxicity of these toxic protein aggregates. HFD feeding has been shown to exacerbate existing conditions of amyloid pathology, metabolic defects, neuroinflammation, neuronal loss, and cognitive impairments in APP/PS1 (Ettcheto et al., 2016; Thériault et al., 2016) and 3xTg-AD (Sah et al., 2017; Vandal et al., 2014) mouse models of AD and 6-hydroxydopamine (Morris et al., 2010; Sharma and Taliyan, 2018) and 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (Bousquet et al., 2012) induced mouse models of PD. These AD and PD mouse models have been associated with TNF/TNFR1 mediated necroptosis (Janelsins et al., 2008; Leem et al., 2023; Regoni et al., 2024) and the presence of HFD would contribute to increased neurotoxicity. A study compares the effects of HFD on both WT and APP/PS1 mice and shows insulin signaling dysregulation and mitochondrial dysfunctions in the brain, leading to hippocampal-dependent memory and cognitive deficits, independent of amyloid pathology (Petrov et al., 2015), although other studies suggest that amyloid pathology plays a detrimental role under obesity condition (Petrov et al., 2015). It is important to note that although HFD could induce changes in both WT and AD mouse models, the change in the WT depends on the time fed on HFD and in some instances may not have as serious phenotypes as AD mice (Petrov et al., 2015). Hence, it remains important to dissect the disease mechanisms of neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration that are associated with obesity as compared to those that arise from toxic protein aggregates carrying familial AD mutations.

Moreover, aging itself is a critical modulator of TNF expression, with implications for both baseline inflammation and disease susceptibility. Aging is associated with a state of chronic, low-grade inflammation, termed “inflammaging”, in which TNF is a central cytokine (Franceschi et al., 2000). In both humans and rodents, circulating TNF levels increase with age even in the absence of disease (Bruunsgaard et al., 2003; Chung et al., 2009), contributing to insulin resistance and cognitive decline (Michaud et al., 2013). In mice, elevated TNF expression has been observed in aged adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, and the brain, often in association with increased macrophage infiltration or microglial activation (Lumeng et al., 2011; Niraula et al., 2017). Human studies similarly show that TNF levels correlate positively with age and are associated with frailty, cognitive impairment, and poor metabolic outcomes (de Magalhães et al., 2017; Franceschi and Campisi, 2014).

While the studies discussed in the above sections have summarized the detrimental effects of TNF in inducing neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration, it remains crucial to elucidate the origin of the elevated TNF levels in the brain. Some studies have provided evidence that there is no increase in the mRNA level of TNF/TNFRs in the hippocampus (de Sousa Rodrigues et al., 2017) and cortex (Mondal et al., 2020) of the brain under obesity condition. While still unclear, this suggests that there may not the presence of de novo production or elevation of TNF as well as its cognate receptors. Instead, neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration likely result from peripheral TNF infiltration and its negative consequential effects. On top of TNF, there is also an increase in lipid content, especially palmitate, in the brain and cerebrospinal fluid of overweight and obese humans (Melo et al., 2020). It is unclear whether these excess lipids are produced de novo in the brain or are transported from the peripheral to the brain. Nonetheless, from a mouse model with intracerebroventricular infusion of palmitate, it is illustrated that activated microglia and TNF mediate palmitate-induced insulin resistance, synaptic plasticity, and cognitive impairments in the brain (Melo et al., 2020). Furthermore, it has been shown that acute and chronic HFD feeding in mice dynamically modulate TNF expression alongside astrocyte activation, indicating a time-dependent shift between neurotrophic and neurotoxic effects of lipids (Dalvi et al., 2017). Altogether, these studies exemplify the significance of TNF/TNFR1 signaling and downstream pathways in regulating metabolic-inflammatory axis within body-brain interaction in obesity.

Therapeutic targeting of TNF/TNFR1 signaling in obesity

Therapeutic targeting of TNF/TNFR1 signaling pathway represents an important approach in restoring functional body-brain interaction through modulating the immune system and mediating inflammation to influence both peripheral and CNS processes. This can be enabled by BBB-penetrating therapeutics or peripheral targeting strategies that modulate TNF/TNFR1 signaling pathway. Direct targeting of TNF ligand includes the use of the BBB-penetrating XPro-1595 therapy (MacPherson et al., 2017; Steed et al., 2003) or the conventional anti-TNFs (Ou et al., 2022). A seminal study has demonstrated that targeting soluble TNF using XPro-1595 is effective in lowering the risk of obesity or T2D associated behavior deficits or AD-like symptoms (De Sousa Rodrigues et al., 2019). Neutralization of soluble TNF with XPro1595 remedies dysregulated brain immunomodulatory and neuroinflammatory genes expression and prevents anxiety-like behavior and sociability deficits present in mice with high-fat high-carbohydrate diet consumption (De Sousa Rodrigues et al., 2019). Importantly, selective targeting of soluble TNF restores both peripheral and central insulin signaling (De Sousa Rodrigues et al., 2019). Furthermore, inhibition of TNFR1 downstream signaling molecule phosphorylated RIPK1 by Nec-1 promotes metabolic functions, attenuates neuroinflammation, and exerts neuroprotection in T2D-like mice (Preeti et al., 2023). Another study elucidates the link between peripheral inflammation and cognitive dysfunction under obesity condition. The study suggests that HFD fed mice exhibits neuroinflammation and BBB impairment in the hippocampus which aggravate synaptic and metabolic dysfunction in the brain (de Paula et al., 2021). Mice administered with infliximab, an anti-TNF monoclonal antibody, demonstrate improved behavioral characterization and more intact BBB integrity (de Paula et al., 2021). The connection between peripheral inflammation and cognitive impairments is further supported by the generation of AD/TNF mice (5XFAD/Tg197) that develop inflammatory arthritis and AD features such as amyloid deposits induced by human TNF expression (Paouri et al., 2017). Although this is not a mouse model with obesity condition, the presence of inflammatory arthritis exacerbates neuroinflammation and increased infiltrating immune cells as well as compromised neuronal integrity and altered amyloid pathology. Importantly, these phenotypes in the mice were reversed with treatment of infliximab (Paouri et al., 2017). As anti-TNFs are not known to be able to cross the BBB, their ability to rescue neuroinflammation and brain pathology likely stems from reducing peripheral to brain inflammatory signaling, highlighting the role of systemic inflammation in brain disorders. In contrast, BBB-penetrating therapeutics like XPro-1595 provide widespread anti-inflammatory effects in both the body and brain.

Building on the evidence linking peripheral inflammation to cognitive dysfunction, several studies have explored the role of TNF inhibition in ischemic stroke models. In stroke models, anti-TNF treatment consistently reduces infarct volume. For instance, TNF binding protein (TNFbp), a PEGylated dimeric form of soluble TNF receptor I, significantly decreased cortical infarct size when administered intracranially within 60 min after permanent middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) in mice. TNFbp also reduced DNA fragmentation at the infarct margin and provided sustained neuroprotection for up to two weeks post-stroke, suggesting therapeutic potential if brain delivery challenges can be overcome (Nawashiro et al., 1997). In C57BL/6 mice subjected to MCAO, topical, but not intracerebroventricular, administration of the soluble TNF inhibitor XPro1595 significantly reduced infarct volume at both 1 and 3 days post-stroke. Etanercept showed no effect. XPro1595 also modulated microglial activation, increasing expression of phagocytic markers (P2ry12 and Trem2) and decreasing Cx3cr1, supporting the neuroprotective role of solTNF-selective inhibition (Yli-Karjanmaa et al., 2019). Aging is another factor that exacerbates ischemic stroke outcomes. Aged mice (18–20 months) exhibited larger infarcts, worse motor deficits, and greater blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption after transient MCAO compared to young mice (3 months). Elevated TNF levels were observed at baseline and post-stroke in aged mice. Pre-treatment with the TNF–neutralizing antibody adalimumab restored infarct size, neuromotor function, and survival to levels seen in young mice. Similarly, in human stroke patients, higher plasma TNF levels correlated with age and poorer neurological outcomes. These findings implicate TNF–driven inflammaging in age-related stroke vulnerability and suggest TNF inhibition as a promising strategy in elderly populations (Liberale et al., 2021). Metabolic disturbances such as hyperglycemia also aggravate stroke outcomes through a self-reinforcing cycle of inflammation. The TNF receptor inhibitor R-7050 exhibits neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and antihyperglycemic effects. In a rat model of permanent cerebral ischemia, R-7050 pretreatment reduced infarct volume, neurological deficits, edema, oxidative stress, and caspase-3 activation. It also preserved the tight junction protein ZO-1 and reversed TNFR1, NF-κB, and IL-6 activation in both cortical tissue and TNF–treated endothelial cells. R-7050 further ameliorated metabolic disturbances such as hyperglycemia, elevated corticosterone, free fatty acids, CRP, and FGF-15, and restored insulin signaling in skeletal muscle, highlighting its therapeutic potential in stroke via both inflammatory and metabolic pathways (Lin et al., 2021). Additionally, systemic inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis worsen stroke outcomes. In a TNF–dependent RA mouse model, ischemia/reperfusion injury resulted in larger infarcts, greater BBB breakdown, and increased microglial and macrophage infiltration. These changes were accompanied by reduced tight junction proteins (claudin-5 and occludin) and enhanced oxidative stress. Treatment with the TNF inhibitor Infliximab reversed these pathological features and restored stroke outcomes to baseline, reinforcing the role of TNF in stroke exacerbation under inflammatory conditions (Bonetti et al., 2019). The use of different therapeutics as tools could potentially delineate the contribution of TNF/TNFR1 signaling to the disease mechanisms underlying obesity-induced neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. Across the body-brain interaction, it is interesting to note that inhibition of TNFR1 downstream IKKβ/NF-κB signaling pathway in the brain by compound or genetic modulation has been shown to attenuate obesity and restore insulin signaling (Benzler et al., 2015). The role of TNF/TNFR1 signaling in the brain, particular in the hypothalamus, has been shown to play an important role in impairing thermogenesis as well as insulin secretion and signaling (Arruda et al., 2011). TNFR1 KO mice fed with HFD had elevated oxygen consumption reflective of increased thermogenesis as well as improved insulin signal transduction in peripheral tissues such as the liver and skeletal muscles (Arruda et al., 2011). To further demonstrate that this effect stems from direct attenuation of hypothalamic inflammation, the study injected anti-TNF infliximab intracerebroventricularly into the brain and showed that immunoneutralization of TNF in HFD mice fully restored thermogenesis impairment and insulin signaling in liver and muscle, indicating the brain to body role of interaction through TNF (Arruda et al., 2011). It is also important to note that TNFR1 inhibition such as through anti-human TNFR1 (Wandrer et al., 2020) or downregulation of TNF expression and level (Vachliotis and Polyzos, 2023a; Xu et al., 2024) are widely adopted strategies for treatment of obesity, MASLD, and their associated peripheral inflammation and cell death, which could reduce circulating TNF to reach the brain. Furthermore, treatment of sodium-glucose cotransporter two inhibitors to obese mice inhibits microglia activation in hypothalamus, leading to improved insulin and glucose homeostasis as well as reduced triglyceride and fat content (Barreto-Vianna et al., 2016; Naznin et al., 2017). These studies demonstrate the brain to body control of metabolic-inflammatory axis and emphasize the importance of the bidirectional modulation within the body-brain interaction (de Paula et al., 2021; Maldonado-Ruiz et al., 2017).

Targeting TNF/TNFR1 signaling pathway to modulate body-brain interactions holds promise for treating obesity-induced metabolic, inflammatory, and neurodegenerative disorders. While developing effective BBB-penetrant TNF or TNFR1 inhibitors remains challenging, targeting TNF/TNFR1 signaling in the periphery has shown feasibility in modulating neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. The use of new TNFR1 receptor-specific competitive (Chen et al., 2017; Lo et al., 2021, 2017; Saddala and Huang, 2019; Vunnam et al., 2023) or allosteric modulators (Lo, 2025b; Lo et al., 2020, 2019; Murali et al., 2005; Zeng et al., 2024) would be beneficial in modulating the metabolic-inflammatory axis within body-brain interactions, including MASLD, T2D, and obesity as well as related neurological diseases such as AD and related dementias. In the clinical setting, circulating TNF has been evaluated as a biomarker for therapeutic response in obesity-related metabolic disorders, as reviewed in (Vachliotis and Polyzos, 2023b). However, there remains a significant lack of clinical trials directly testing anti-TNF or TNFR1-targeting therapies in these patient populations. Most interventions to date have targeted upstream or downstream mechanisms that indirectly reduce TNF levels, rather than specifically antagonizing TNF or TNFR1 (Vachliotis and Polyzos, 2023b). Observational studies in humans, particularly patients with autoimmune diseases receiving anti-TNF therapy, have provided some indirect evidence for therapeutic benefit in comorbid metabolic conditions, including improvements in insulin sensitivity and systemic inflammation (Papaefthymiou et al., 2022; Schramm et al., 2008). More recently, studies have reported promising outcomes from anti-TNF therapy in mitigating obesity-associated metabolic complications in clinical cohorts (Lim et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2022; Shafrir et al., 2025). However, these findings are often confounded by the complex comorbidity profiles of autoimmune disease patients (Tang et al., 2020; Villumsen et al., 2023) and have not yet been extended to investigations of neuroinflammatory or neurodegenerative outcomes. This is primarily due to soluble TNF neutralization or global TNF blockade having potential off-target side effects by dysregulating TNFR2-mediated immune responses, which are essential for maintaining homeostasis (Gogulescu et al., 2024; Gonzalez Caldito, 2023). Hence, this warrants further investigation of the efficacy of anti-TNF and TNFR1-targeting therapies in future clinical trials involving patients with comorbidities in obesity-related metabolic and neurodegenerative disorders. The most promising strategy appears to be therapeutics with TNFR1 receptor-specific inhibition and BBB-penetration capability (Fischer et al., 2020; Li et al., 2025), which enable TNFR2 to continue to perform its normal function in immune regulation (Ortí-Casañ et al., 2019; Papazian et al., 2021).

Importantly, the existence of the metabolic-inflammatory axis and crosstalk opens new avenue for targeting inflammation through modulating metabolic pathways. For example, increasing insulin sensitivity reduces TNF mediated inflammation by restoring metabolic balance and mitigating chronic immune activation as well as suppressing NF-κB activation (Chang et al., 2021). In addition, restoring insulin signaling improves autolysosomal functions and reduces stress-induced TNF secretion, thereby maintaining cellular homeostasis (Sadeghi et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2023). Furthermore, resolving autolysosomal, and mitochondrial dysfunction has been shown to attenuate inflammation (Cheong et al., 2025; Vest et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2023) potentially through promoting autophagic turnover of damaged mitochondria, improving mitochondrial membrane potential, and reducing reactive oxygen species (Lo et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2023). As reviewed in other papers, other anti-inflammatory therapeutic agents such as polyphenols and flavonoids (Jeon et al., 2012), or probiotics that induce microbiome shifts (Ansari et al., 2023), as well as lifestyle changes such as fasting (van den Burg et al., 2025) and exercise (Kang et al., 2016), could exert positive effects on metabolic functions and reduce neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration (van Dijk et al., 2015; Zeng et al., 2025a; Zeng and Lo, 2025). Finally, when considering the targeting of TNF–TNFR1 signaling in both the body and brain, sexual dimorphism must be considered. Male mice on a high-fat diet (HFD) exhibit elevated levels of TNF, whereas in female mice—both intact and ovariectomized—TNF levels remain relatively unchanged following HFD exposure (K.-H. E. Chen et al., 2021b; Lainez et al., 2018; Lainez and Coss, 2019). Estrogen appears to play a protective role by downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and preserving metabolic and cognitive functions, suggesting that anti-TNF interventions may have sex-specific outcomes (Green et al., 1999; Vegeto et al., 2001).

Summary and future perspectives

Obesity is a complex, multifactorial condition that drives chronic low-grade inflammation and metabolic dysfunction throughout the body. These disruptions extend beyond peripheral tissues, significantly impacting brain health and increasing the risk of neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration through the BBI and the metabolic-inflammatory axis. Obesity affects all organs, and it is important to examine all body-brain axes and delineate the contribution of different cell types that could be involved in perturbing metabolic homeostasis and inflammatory response. While different body-brain axes are present including but not limited to liver-brain axis and gut-brain axis, they appear to be centrally connected through the vagus nerve (Ma et al., 2025). The vagus nerve has been proposed to play a key role in the body-brain circuit to regulate inflammatory responses as well as spreading and propagation of toxic protein aggregates across the peripheral and central nervous systems (C. Chen et al., 2021a; Chen and Mor, 2023; Jin et al., 2024; Molinas et al., 2023). New innovative therapies should be designed to tackle disorders associated with pathological body-brain interactions (Höke, 2006).

Most obesity-based BBI studies utilize HFD-fed mice or rats, with the severity of neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration varying based on diet type, feeding duration, and region-specific pathological changes in the brain (Guillemot-Legris and Muccioli, 2017). In addition, obesity often coexists with vascular comorbidities such as atherosclerosis, insulin resistance, and endothelial dysfunction, all of which are known to compromise cerebral perfusion and BBB integrity. Chronic metabolic inflammation associated with obesity contributes to increased circulating levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress, and matrix metalloproteinases, which can exacerbate BBB breakdown and promote leukocyte infiltration into the brain parenchyma - hallmarks of both stroke pathology and progressive neurodegeneration. Moreover, heterogeneity within and between organs and cell types in obesity needs to be better resolved through single-cell and spatial multi-omics technologies to pinpoint key pathological drivers and improve therapeutic targeting (Deng et al., 2024; Junaid et al., 2025; O’Connor et al., 2023a, 2023b; Preedy et al., 2024). Furthermore, we propose that more human postmortem samples reflecting obesity-induced neurological disorders, or comorbid presentations of obesity, T2D, and MASLD with neurodegenerative disease, are critically needed. Such samples should include well-documented clinical histories and longitudinal symptom data to facilitate understanding of BBI pathology in humans - a current limitation in the field. Mining existing human multi-omics datasets related to obesity, T2D, MASLD, and neurological diseases may also yield novel hypotheses regarding BBI dysfunction that remain underexplored. Lastly, given the frequent comorbidity and inter-individual variability in human patients, future studies should incorporate detailed multi-omics profiling and consider personalized medicine approaches to simultaneously address metabolic and neurological disease burden.
