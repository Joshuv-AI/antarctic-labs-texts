Background

Scale-up is an understudied concept in implementation science, with few existing empirical studies that explicitly focus on the process of scale-up. To date, most implementation science studies have focused on initial adoption of evidence-based practices within systems or settings, with less inquiry into rigorous, theory-driven strategies for large-scale implementation of evidence-based practices to benefit more people and to foster policy and program development on a lasting basis [1]. Assessing scale-up and scalability, however, is crucial for ensuring sustainability of complex interventions within low-resource settings.

One setting where scale-up has been understudied is the U.S. prison system. Incarcerated individuals have greater burden of chronic disease than the general population, including more hypertension, diabetes, high cholesterol, and obesity [2–4]. Fifty percent of those in prison have at least one chronic physical health condition, and disease burden will likely increase as the prison population continues to age. Due to high incarceration rates and longer sentences, the proportion of older adults in prisons has increased significantly over the last 2 decades. From 1999 to 2016, the number of individuals in U.S. state and federal prisons ages 55 + increased 280% [5]. Further, the increase in the population of imprisoned older adults has far outpaced the growth of the overall prison population. In 1990, only 2 states housed at least 1000 incarcerated individuals ages 55 +; by 2009, that number increased to 28 states. The cost of incarcerating older adults in the U.S. is 9 times higher than the cost of incarcerating their younger counterparts, largely due to health care costs. In 2013, the Federal Bureau of Prisons spent $881 million on the care of incarcerated individuals aged > 50 [6]. In Texas, adults ages 55 + make up 46.7% of all incarcerated individuals with 3 or more chronic health conditions, and these incarcerated individuals are prescribed an average of 7 classes of medications [7]. Despite this, prison health care services primarily focus on infectious diseases, mental illness, and substance abuse, largely neglecting chronic physical health conditions.

Since the 2010 s, several state prison systems in the U.S. have begun small pilot programs using The Chronic Disease Self-Management Program (CDSMP), a 6-week evidence-based program developed to support self-management for people with chronic illnesses [8]. It promotes self-efficacy for self-care through weekly workshops, action planning, and leader support. At least 10 randomized trials conducted in non-correctional settings have linked CDSMP to improvements in disease management, self-efficacy, exercise, pain, and depressive symptoms [9]. CDSMP is also linked to a reduction in health care spending, emergency department visits and hospitalizations [10].

CDSMP has been delivered in state corrections systems using 3 approaches: 1) bringing external community-based CDSMP leaders into the correctional setting to lead the program, 2) training internal correctional staff as program leaders, and 3) training incarcerated individuals to serve as peer leaders. To date, however, there has been no research into efficient and effective strategies for scaling up CDSMP within state corrections systems using any of these approaches. It is important to understand the processes needed to bring CDSMP into widespread use within state prison systems, where space, time, and staff resources are limited. Both qualitative and quantitative data are needed to develop and test effective strategies for scaling up complex interventions.

There are pros and cons associated with community, staff, and peer-led programs: CDSMP agency leaders are of little cost to correctional departments, but it may be difficult to integrate them into the correctional setting. Staff leaders are already familiar with the prison setting, but their time to deliver the intervention may be limited and costly. Peer leaders have shared experiences with program participants and can use this to provide support at low cost; however, there may be security and administrative concerns with this approach.

The purpose of this study is to evaluate and develop an approach to maximize the scalability of CDSMP among older adults in state correctional systems. Although scale-up is an understudied concept in implementation science, several conceptual models are frequently mentioned in the literature, including the Scaling Up Management Framework. Developed by Kohl and colleagues [11, 12], this framework was designed to accelerate the spread of evidence-based practices by providing practical guidelines that practitioners can follow to carry out the tasks needed for effective scale-up. It uses a three-step, ten-task process that involves 1) developing a scale-up plan, 2) establishing the pre-conditions for scaling up, and 3) implementing the scaling-up process. We will rely on this framework to develop a CDSMP scale-up intervention for correctional settings. Development and testing of this scale-up intervention is important both for prisons and for implementation science generally. Several leading implementation science researchers have called for more research on scalability and scale-up [13, 14].

Guided by the Scaling up Management Framework, we will assess ways to maximize the scalability of CDSMP within state correctional settings and develop and refine CDSMP scale-up strategies for these settings. This study does not focus on making any adaptations to the CDSMP itself. Instead, we aim to adapt the implementation strategies, as approaches used in community settings may not translate well in correctional environments. We also recognize that the implementation approach needed for a small pilot may differ from that needed for system-wide scale-up. Our work centers on refining implementation strategies that address these realities, while maintaining the integrity of the intervention. Specifically, we will do the following: 1) quantitatively and qualitatively describe factors influencing scalability among current and former CDSMP facilitators (n = 60) within state prisons to inform scale-up strategy development and 2) develop and refine a manualized approach for successful CDSMP scale-up within a state prison. We will then test the scale-up manual in a future randomized implementation trial. This pilot project begins a line of research that will develop and test use of implementation science approaches to scale-up and sustain evidence-based physical health interventions within state correctional settings.

Methods

Implementation framework

Our study utilizes the Scaling Up Management (SUM) Framework (Fig. 1), which was developed to improve the scalability of health improvement solutions [11, 15]. Although the framework has been applied to interventions in maternal and child health, youth development, and family planning (among others), it has not been previously tested in research. Thus, our work will be the first application of the framework in research. This framework has been cited in several implementation science publications that specifically identify existing scalability frameworks [13, 16].

The Scaling Up Management (SUM) framework

The framework includes three main steps (Fig. 1): 1) developing a scaling up plan, 2) establishing the preconditions for scaling, and 3) implementing the scaling up process. The Framework articulates tasks or strategies that may go with each step. We will use this framework to guide our assessments for this study. This project will focus primarily on Step 1 (Developing a Scaling up Plan), which includes creating a vision, assessing scalability, filling information gaps, and preparing a scaling up manual in preparation for a full-scale randomized trial of the effectiveness of the manual in a subsequent implementation study.

Overview of the Chronic Disease Self-Management Program (CDSMP)

CDSMP is an evidence-based program designed to empower individuals with the skills and knowledge needed to manage their own chronic health conditions [8]. Initially developed at Stanford University, it is now a licensed program product managed through the Self-Management Resource Center [17]. The program promotes disease-related self-efficacy and teaches self-care behaviors through weekly workshops, action planning, and leader support. The intervention is led by 2 leaders who have chronic health conditions or live with a person with a chronic health condition. The program consists of six 150-min sessions conducted over a 6-week period. Session topics include exercising for strength, flexibility, and endurance; medication use; effective communication with family/friends/health professionals; nutrition; decision-making; and how to evaluate new treatments. Weekly workshops include interactive activities like discussions, action-planning practice, behavior modeling, problem-solving techniques, and decision-making. Participants can practice exercise and relaxation techniques during the sessions. Typically, 12 to 16 individuals participate in each workshop. The program is fully manualized. All participants receive copies of the participant manual at program enrollment. CDSMP has been evaluated across numerous populations, including samples of community-dwelling older adults in the United States, China [18], the Netherlands [19], and the United Kingdom [20]. The program has also been successfully utilized in low resource communities, which often face structural barriers that impact disease management (e.g. poverty, limited access to healthy foods, environmental barriers to physical activity). It has been used successfully in ethnically diverse groups, including African-American older adults [21, 22]. Participation in CDSMP has been associated with improvements in disease related self-efficacy, health-related quality of life, aerobic exercise, and communication with physicians [9].

Study design and participants

We will use a mixed-methods approach to assess scalability, using both quantitative survey data and qualitative interviews. This study does not meet the World Health Organization (WHO) definition of a clinical trial. Study enrollment began in 2024 and is expected to continue through early 2026. Study participants will include 20 external agency leaders who have led prison CDSMP groups, 20 prison staff who have led or implemented prison CDSMP groups, and 20 incarcerated individuals from U.S. state prisons who have led or participated in CDSMP in prison. To date, CDSMP has been implemented in some form in at least 10 state prisons around the country. We will use several approaches for recruiting participants. To recruit external agency leaders, we will reach out to organizations currently and previously funded by the National Council on Aging to do programming within state prisons. We have already identified several such organizations who have expressed interest in study participation. We will use snowball sampling to recruit additional external agency leaders. To recruit correctional staff involved in CDSMP programming, we will leverage the study investigators’ existing relationships with state prisons around the country, as two of the study investigators have conducted research in correctional settings for more than 15 years. We have already identified staff from several state correctional departments who are willing to participate in the project and to provide us with access to their prison populations. We will include correctional employees at all levels, including administrators, officers, and healthcare professionals. Incarcerated participants will be recruited from state facilities throughout the United States where CDSMP has been previously conducted. These facilities will be in both urban and rural settings and vary in security level, population size, and healthcare infrastructure. We will work with prison staff to identify potentially eligible participants based on prison records and staff knowledge of prior CDSMP participation. We will then approach them privately regarding study participation. Study staff obtaining consent will clarify the nature of the study and possible alternatives upfront. Prior to enrolling participants in the research, they will fully explain the study procedures, risks, benefits, and alternatives to participants, emphasizing that participation has no impact on the other services they receive at the prison, the terms or length of their confinement, or any other community services that they receive post-release. We will offer to read all consent information aloud as needed.

Research procedures

CDSMP external agency leaders & prison staff participants

We will recruit 20 CDSMP community agency leader participants and 20 correctional staff participants for this study. Participants will be (1) at least 18 years of age, (2) English-speaking, and (3) current or former users of Chronic Disease Self-Management programming within a correctional setting. CDSMP community agency leader participants will include university researchers, health department employees, and staff from community-based organizations, among others. Correctional staff participants will include correctional administrators, officers, and healthcare staff (e.g. physicians, nurses, social workers, therapists).

After completing the informed consent procedures, CDSMP external agency leaders and prison staff participants will complete electronic copies of quantitative measures. They will then be scheduled for either in-person or videoconference qualitative interviews. Qualitative and quantitative data will then be used to develop the scalability manual outlined in Aim 2.

Incarcerated participants

Incarcerated participants will be English-speaking men and women ages 45 + who are current or former CDSMP participants within a correctional CDSMP program. After informed consent, incarcerated participants who have taken part in CDSMP programming will participate via in-person or videoconference interviews (at the discretion of the prison).

Quantitative measures

We will collect demographic data from all participants. Additionally, we will collect information from staff participants about their facility, how and when they implemented CDSMP, and the status of the program within their facility. We will utilize 2 measures to assess dimensions of CDSMP scalability. The first is the Intervention Scalability Assessment Tool [23, 24], a tool developed to help policymakers and practitioners evaluate the suitability of health interventions for scale-up. It assesses 10 domains using 19 items rated on a Likert scale 0 (not at all) to 3 (to a large extent). Scores can be calculated across multiple participants by summing the total score by domain from each participant and averaging across participants. The second measure is the Scalability Checklist [11], a tool used to identify actions that can simplify scale-up. It utilizes a 3-point scale to assess scalability across seven domains. Both scales measure aspects of the organizational and social contexts. Together, the two measures assess 15 non-overlapping constructs (Table 1). For simplicity, we will choose 2–3 core items reflecting each of the 15 domains. We will report psychometrics for the modified scales as they perform in the study. Additionally, we will summarize positive results, challenges, and recommendations within each checklist domain. We will make these modified tools available publicly after the study concludes.

Quantitative analyses

Assessing scalability to inform scale-up manual development

We will calculate summary scores for each of the 15 non-overlapping constructs represented by the ISAT and the Scalability Checklist. We will use these scores to identify domains where focused implementation strategy development will be needed prior to scale-up of CDSMP in correctional settings. We expect that the highest scale-up readiness scores will be in the domains recognizing magnitude of the problem (high chronic disease prevalence & poor disease self- management in the correctional setting) and intervention effectiveness. We expect that the lowest scores will be in the domains of implementation infrastructure and support for change. To be considered ready for scale-up, our goal is to have scores of at least 2 (on a 0 to 3 scale) in each of the domains of our quantitative measures. In domains where scores are low (< 2 out of 3), we will use qualitative interviews to query participants about strategies to include in our scale-up manual to address these barriers/concerns. Incarcerated individuals will be able to skip any domains that they deem are not relevant to them.

Results will inform scale-up manual development. We expect that the primary implementation mechanism for scale-up will be the realignment and mobilization of resources within the correctional system and sustainability of funding, as both human and financial resources have been previously identified as implementation barriers for correctional systems [25, 26].

Assessing differences in scalability by facilitator type and facility type

We will use multiple linear regression to evaluate how facilitator type (CDSMP external agency leader, prison staff member, incarcerated individual), and facility type (male versus female; public versus private) are associated with scalability in each quantitative domain. Differences based on these characteristics will be addressed in the scale-up manual. We envision that the manual will include a decision tree that correctional systems can use depending on their desired facilitator type (external, internal, or incarcerated CDSMP facilitators), and/or facility type (male versus female; public versus private).

Power

Given our sample size, the effect size Cohen’s d = 0.91 is detectable as statistically significant in comparisons of leader groups (i.e., external vs. internal vs. incarcerated CDSMP facilitators) on quantitative measures with power of 0.80 in two-sided tests at 0.05 level of significance. If the observed effect sizes are smaller, statistical significance will not be reached, but the magnitude of the effect sizes will be used to gauge practical significance of the differences. In Cohen’s classification, d = 0.2 is small, d = 0.5 is medium, and d = 0.8 is large, with d = 0.5 commonly used as a universal cut-off for clinical significance of health status measures [27]. The ability to detect medium to large effects is informative for manual development in this pilot study.

Qualitative methods

After the surveys are completed, we will conduct semi-structured qualitative interviews with participants. Interviews will last roughly one hour and will be conducted either by phone or videoconference. We are interested in identifying the organizational features needed for successful scale-up. Participants will be queried regarding organization, technical, and process factors critical to small-scale success; organizational capacity to expand operations and deliver services on a larger scale; ideal partners for scale-up; policy changes needed for broad scale-up; intermediate organizations needed for scale-up; ideal strategies for scaling up; who should perform key functions; ways to minimize complexity/cost; perceived benefit for relevant stakeholders; any documentation of processes or cost-effectiveness; unique characteristics of the organization crucial for successful replication; funding to support scale-up; advantage of CDSMP over other practices; necessary supervision and quality control mechanisms; and any accountability measures or incentives crucial for success. We will also ask specifically about differences in scalability of CDSMP based on factors such as leader type (community agency versus prison staff versus peer leader) and facility gender segregation (facilities housing men versus women). Delivery of an evidence-based practice as intended by its developers is crucial in implementation science research, as omitting or changing any of its core components may render it ineffective. We expect that the primary implementation mechanism for scale-up will be the realignment and mobilization of resources within the correctional system and the sustainability of funding. Thus, we will deliberately include questions related to mobilization of resources and sustainment of funding in our qualitative interview guide.

Qualitative analyses

Interviews will be recorded and deidentified prior to transcription & analysis. Two team members will independently code the interview transcripts. We will use the Scaling Up Management Framework as a deductive coding framework and then identify inductive codes as they arise. Qualitative data will be analyzed in line with our goals, objectives, and key research questions. Data will be coded using Taguette, a free and open-source tool for qualitative analysis of text data. Coding results will then be compared for consistency. When interrater agreement is not achieved, the 2 coders will meet to discuss findings and to reach final agreement.

Data monitoring plan

Each study participant will be issued an ID number that will be used for data collection. Quantitative survey data to be collected from staff study participants will be collected electronically and will only be identified with the study ID number of the participant. The codes that link the name of the participant and the study ID will be kept confidential in secured electronic files on password-protected computers only accessible to the research team. We will use REDCap for quantitative data collection (i.e., research staff will enter data directly into REDCap during data collection). REDCap is a HIPAA compliant, secure web-based clinical research application designed to store sensitive information. In circumstances where direct electronic data capture is not possible, the study team will use paper-based study forms. Completed study forms will be stored in a locked drawer in the PI’s locked office. Standard data checking procedures will include checking forms for missing data and examination of key variables for skewness, variability, missing data, and outliers.

Study interviews will be audio-recorded using an encrypted digital voice recorder. All recordings will be stored on password protected University servers. After interviews are complete, recordings will be de-identified, encrypted and sent to a third-party transcription service. All communications between servers will be encrypted via industry best-practices.

Data access

Access to the final study dataset will be limited to the research team members listed on the approved institutional review board (IRB) protocol. De-identified data may be shared with collaborators under a data use agreement, in accordance with ethical and regulatory guidelines. No identifiable information will be shared outside of the research team.

Development of scale-up manual

Results from our mixed-methods study will be used to develop a written CDSMP scale-up manual for use by state correctional systems. This manual will use participant feedback and scalability scores to identify barriers, facilitators, and strategies for implementation, organized according to the Scaling Up Management Framework.

We will use implementation mapping processes to develop our scale-up manual (Fig. 2). First, (1) we will summarize study findings related to barriers and facilitators of scalability. Then (2) we will identify our implementation outcomes and performance objectives specific to each type of stakeholder (e.g. CDSMP instructors, correctional staff, etc.) From there, (3) we will develop our implementation intervention manual, with attention to specific strategies for addressing barriers. These strategies will draw on existing theoretical models of scale-up [14, 28, 29]. We will then (4) create protocols, processes, documents, and other materials to guide implementation, based on the strategies identified.

Implementation mapping

When the draft manual is developed, we will re-interview study participants (i.e. CDSMP agency staff, correctional staff) to obtain feedback on its clarity, usefulness, feasibility, and acceptability. We will also re-administer our quantitative scalability measures based on approaches described in the manual. We will iteratively refine the manual until we reach a target score of 2 out of 3 in each of our scalability domains.

Protocol modifications communication plan

Any important modifications to the study protocol (e.g. changes to eligibility criteria, outcome measures, or analytic approaches) will be promptly communicated to all relevant stakeholders. This includes submitting amendments for review and approval by the institutional review board (IRB). All study team members will be informed of protocol changes via written communication and team meetings. If modifications impact study participants or their involvement, they will be notified in a timely and appropriate manner, including re-consent when necessary. Protocol changes that affect the interpretation of study outcomes will be transparently reported in any resulting publications.

Dissemination plan

Findings from this study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journal publications, conference presentations, and community reports. Authorship eligibility will follow the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) guidelines, which require substantial contributions to the conception or design of the work; or the acquisition, analysis, or interpretation of data; drafting the work or revising it critically for important intellectual content; and final approval of the version to be published. All listed authors will meet these criteria. No professional writers will be used in the preparation of this manuscript. In addition to academic outlets, we will share results with key stakeholders (e.g. correctional health administrators and participants) as appropriate. Summaries will be tailored to meet the needs of diverse audiences and may include infographics, reports, oral presentations, and poster presentations to prison advisory boards or healthcare staff. We are committed to ensuring that the results are accessible and actionable for those involved in the care and support of older adults with criminal legal system involvement.

Discussion

This study is innovative in several ways. First, it focuses on scale-up, an understudied area of implementation science. Most implementation science studies focus on initial adoption of evidence-based practices within systems or settings, with little development and evaluation of rigorous, theory-based strategies for scale-up. This project assesses, develops, and manualizes a plan to scale up an evidence-based practice in a specific kind of setting, as recommended by numerous implementation science researchers [12, 13]. This pilot study sets the groundwork for a subsequent randomized trial of scale-up strategies. Additionally, this will be the first scientific evaluation of CDSMP implementation within a correctional setting. Although CDSMP has been used in correctional settings, no prior implementation research has been conducted on the use of CDSMP in these settings. Further, very few prison-based studies focus on physical health. Most health-related interventions conducted in correctional settings focus on mental health [30, 31] or substance abuse [32]. When physical health interventions are conducted, they largely address infectious diseases (e.g. HIV, tuberculosis) [33]. Our project will be one of only a few studies addressing chronic physical health in a correctional setting.

This project assesses scalability across domains, identifies likely implementation strategies for addressing each area of scalability, and iteratively develops a scale-up manual based on this data. We will test our manual in a subsequent randomized R01 implementation trial comparing use of the scale-up manual to implementation as usual within state prisons. Primary implementation outcomes of that trial will include: (1) scale-up (the extent to which CDSMP is institutionalized within a correctional system’s ongoing, stable operations), (2) sustainment, and (3) penetration (how well CDSMP is integrated into a correctional system, extent to which it is used by incarcerated individuals with chronic health conditions). We hypothesize that facilities randomized to use our scale-up manual will achieve greater scale-up, sustainment & penetration than those randomized to implementation as usual. Randomization will take place at the facility level. Facility-randomized trials are necessarily large. Therefore, we will prepare for this much larger trial during the data collection period by using the networks of our interviewees to develop relationships with multiple correctional systems that can serve as sites for our R01 randomized implementation trial.

Final products of the current study will include: (1) the scale-up manual to be tested in a subsequent implementation trial supported by the R01, (2) the decision tree within our scale-up manual describing scale-up approaches based on facilitator and facility type, (3) feasibility tests of research procedures to be used in the implementation trial (e.g. feasibility of recruitment, pilot-testing of study assessments), (4) a manuscript that describes our process for manual development, (5) a mixed method manuscript describing scalability and ways to improve it within each domain; and (6) a manuscript adding to the psychometric literature on both our quantitative measures.

As prison populations continue to age, chronic disease burden within prisons will continue to increase, which contributes to skyrocketing costs. Understanding how to expand evidence-based chronic disease programs within prisons is crucial for reducing disease-related morbidity and mortality among incarcerated individuals. This line of research will: (1) identify and test scale-up strategies for evidence-based chronic disease management programs in prisons, and (2) strengthen the evidence base on scale-up, an understudied area of implementation science.
