Global variation of plant nitrogen acquisition strategies and their responses to decreasing nitrogen availability
Authors: Jiaqiang Liao, Jinhua Mao, Jinlong Peng, Yiqi Luo, Mianhai Zheng, Lei Song, Chen Chen, Lingli Liu, Shuli Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plants employ three N acquisition strategies to support productivity: root uptake from soils, symbiotic fixation, and resorption from senescing leaves. However, their global variation and coordination remain poorly understood. Here, we compile 3,193 field observations and use machine learning to map the three strategies and quantify their relative contributions. We estimate that global nitrogen uptake, fixation, and resorption amount to 559.2 ± 56.5 (mean ± standard deviation), 125.5 ± 7.2, and 279.3 ± 5.0 Tg N yr−1, respectively, representing 58%, 13%, and 29% of total plant N acquisition. Their global variations are primarily determined by temperature and water conditions. Nitrogen uptake dominates globally, especially in warm, humid low–latitude regions, while resorption is more prevalent in arid and cold environments. In contrast, fixation contributes relatively little, with some hotspots in tropical regions and northern high latitudes. Notably, we find significant shifts in N acquisition strategies—from uptake to fixation and resorption—along gradients of decreasing nitrogen availability. These results suggest a spatially coordinated adjustment of plant nitrogen acquisition strategies in response to changing N conditions and offer insights into how coordinated nitrogen strategies shape terrestrial nitrogen cycling and its coupling with the carbon sink.

Introduction

Nitrogen (N) is essential for plant growth in terrestrial ecosystems1. However, elevated carbon dioxide concentration under global change has stimulated plant photosynthesis, thereby increasing N demand and driving progressive N limitation, in which greater N sequestration in organic matter and constrained soil N supply jointly reduce N availability worldwide2–4. Therefore, the ability of plants to acquire sufficient N to sustain productivity has profound implications for terrestrial carbon uptake and climate–carbon feedbacks5. Plants have evolved various strategies for acquiring N, including root uptake from soils, symbiotic fixation, and resorption from senescing leaves6–8. While these three strategies collectively determine total plant N acquisition, how they vary globally to meet plant N demand remains unclear.

The cost–benefit hypothesis of N acquisition is used to explain changes in plant N acquisition strategies. Plants require resource investment to acquire N, with more N acquisition entailing higher resource costs9,10. Of the three N acquisition strategies, N uptake is the most basic and cost–effective approach8. This approach enables plants to absorb soil N directly through their roots, but its effectiveness depends on the N availability in the soil. In contrast, N fixation represents an “external source” strategy, in which N–fixing plants convert atmospheric N into biologically available forms, thereby reducing their dependence on the soil11. However, this process requires a large investment of resources in symbiotic nodules12. In addition to increasing N inputs, plants can enhance their N acquisition through N resorption from senescing leaves. This is considered a conservative strategy13,14, but it also requires energy to transport nutrients15. Given the trade–offs between N acquisition and resource investment, it can be hypothesized that plants may dynamically adjust their N acquisition strategies to adapt to varying N availability across different environments, thereby optimizing resource allocation to meet N demand.

Recent empirical studies have shown that plant N acquisition strategies respond strongly to environmental variation. For example, higher temperatures can accelerate soil N mineralization and nitrification, increasing N availability and thereby facilitating direct N uptake by roots16. In contrast, cold environments slow microbial activity and N turnover, often making resorption a more reliable source of N14. Broad precipitation gradients further modulate N acquisition, with higher precipitation supporting enhanced N transport and microbial processing, whereas arid climates constrain these processes by limiting water inputs and reducing the delivery of N to roots. This also suppresses symbiotic N fixation through resource limitation17. Under dry conditions, plants commonly increase their dependence on N resorption18. Although previous studies have examined the impact of environmental factors on N acquisition, we still lack an integrative understanding of how different factors collectively drive global variations in the dominance of different N acquisition strategies.

Despite growing interest in plant N acquisition, most field and estimation studies focus merely on individual strategies. Research simultaneously observing all three N strategies remains scarce, making it difficult to quantify their relative contributions and to reveal the mechanisms underlying the changes in these strategies across different environments. While process–based models can simulate all three N acquisition processes, they often rely on simplified representations of these mechanisms, which introduce uncertainties when compared with empirical observations19. Recently, machine learning techniques enable us to use abundant data for more accurate modeling of ecological processes in a unified framework20. As global observational data on plant N acquisition processes grow16,21–25, this offers a promising opportunity to explore the contributions of different N acquisition strategies to total N acquisition.

Here, we construct a comprehensive global database of plant N acquisition strategies (including N uptake, fixation, and resorption), comprising 3193 field observations from 607 sites across most continents and ecosystems worldwide (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Note 1). The specific questions addressed by this study are how different plant N acquisition strategies vary globally, how their relative contributions differ, and what factors drive their dominance. First, we develop predictive models for each N acquisition strategy, using machine learning techniques based on observations of plant N acquisition and environmental variables related to climate, soil and vegetation. Using these model outputs, we estimate total plant N acquisition as the sum of N uptake, fixation, and resorption for each grid cell. We then quantify the relative contributions of each N acquisition strategy to the total N acquisition on a global scale. Results

Global rates of plant N uptake, fixation, and resorption

Random forest models were constructed to quantify global plant N uptake, fixation, and resorption, which explained 68, 56, and 47% of their total variation, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 1–3 and Supplementary Note 2). The global average N uptake rate was 48.6 ± 4.9 (mean ± standard deviation) kg ha−1 yr−1, and the average rates of N fixation and resorption were 11.0 ± 0.7 kg ha−1 yr−1 and 25.3 ± 0.5 kg ha−1 yr−1, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). Relative contribution of N uptake, fixation, and resorption to total N acquisition

Globally, plant N uptake dominated across most regions, particularly in low- and mid-latitude regions (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). N resorption was dominant in Central Asia, northern Europe and North America, as well as in Central Oceania. In contrast, N fixation showed more localized but notable contributions, particularly in some tropical regions (e.g., parts of Africa) and high-latitudes hotspots in the Northern Hemisphere. In addition, plant N acquisition strategies varied distinctly across ecosystems (Supplementary Fig. 6). Broadleaf forests exhibited the highest proportional reliance on uptake (63%) and the lowest on fixation (13%). Mixed forests showed a slightly lower uptake contribution (48%) and a comparatively higher reliance on fixation (15%) and resorption (37%). In contrast, needleleaf forests displayed a distinct strategy, with the lowest uptake proportion (36%) and the highest dependence on both fixation (22%) and resorption (42%). Finally, savannas, shrublands, grasslands, and tundra ecosystems showed a broadly similar pattern, with uptake accounting for ~56–59% of total N acquisition, resorption contributing ~20–28%, and fixation representing a smaller fraction (~ 12–18%).

Environmental variation was captured by two dominant axes that together explained 85% of the variance among climate, soil, and vegetation variables (Supplementary Fig. 7). The first axis (PCA1) primarily reflected temperature gradients, showing strong positive loadings for mean annual temperature, maximum and minimum temperatures (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 8). Along with the environmental gradient, PCA1 showed a significant positive correlation with N uptake contribution and negative correlations with N fixation and resorption contributions (Fig. 4b–d and Supplementary Table 2). Thus, in warmer environments, plants prioritized N uptake over fixation or resorption. PCA2 was positively correlated with N uptake contribution and negatively correlated with N resorption contribution (Fig. 4f, g and Supplementary Table 2), whereas its relationship with N fixation was weak (Supplementary Fig. 7). Together, these gradients showed that colder and drier climates promote greater dependence on N resorption.

Associations between ecosystem N availability and plant N acquisition strategies

Natural abundance of δ15N was used as an integrated indicator of ecosystem N status (Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Note 3). Field validation confirmed that both soil and leaf δ15N closely correlate with the degree of N limitation across sites, with lower δ15N values indicating lower N availability and stronger N limitation (Supplementary Fig. 10). Across global grids, significant correlations were found between the relative contributions of different plant N acquisition strategies and N availability, reflected by both soil δ15N and leaf δ15N (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 3). Higher soil δ15N was associated with an increased contribution of N uptake but a reduced contribution of resorption and fixation. Among these, N fixation was more sensitive to soil δ15N changes than resorption, with its contribution approaching zero at the highest soil δ15N levels (Fig. 5a). Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study provides a comprehensive, data-driven global assessment of plant N acquisition strategies and their dominant environmental drivers by integrating in situ observations with machine learning techniques. By explicitly quantifying the relative contributions of N uptake, fixation, and resorption to the total plant N acquisition, we move beyond previous large-scale studies that have inferred N acquisition strategies indirectly from single traits, such as foliar N concentration, N:P stoichiometry26, or mycorrhizal type27, or implicitly assumed that these traits uniquely represent dominant N acquisition pathways28. Our results reveal distinct biogeographic patterns in the dominance of different N acquisition strategies and demonstrate that their relative contributions shift systematically along environmental gradients. Notably, these emergent global patterns closely track ecosystem N availability, providing rare empirical validation of long-standing cost–benefit theory regarding plant N acquisition. Together, our findings establish a quantitative framework showing how plants adjust their N acquisition strategies in response to declining N availability across diverse environments.

Environmental conditions, particularly climate factors, are the main determinants of plant N acquisition strategies at the global scale. The relative contribution of N uptake is strongly associated with temperature conditions (i.e., mean annual temperature (MAT), maximum temperature (Tmax), and minimum temperature (Tmin)) (Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Fig. 8). Three potential mechanisms could explain the increased dominance of N uptake under warmer conditions. First, higher temperatures promote root growth, significantly increasing fine root biomass and enhancing N uptake29,30. Second, plant N uptake is closely linked to soil N turnover, which directly supplies extractable N. A suitable increase in temperature can stimulate net N mineralization and nitrification, producing more ammonium and nitrate, thus facilitating N uptake31,32. Third, rising temperatures may inhibit N fixation in tropical regions as nitrogenase activity decreases when temperatures exceed optimal levels33–35. Furthermore, environments with higher MAT are less conducive to N resorption14,36. Overall, these factors combine to make N uptake the dominant N acquisition strategy in regions at low latitudes.

In contrast, the contribution of N resorption to total plant N acquisition increased under colder and drier conditions (Fig. 4c, g). In these environments, microbial activity is reduced, and soil N cycling is slowed, shifting the primary source of plant–available N from N mineralization to N resorption18. High leaf N resorption further reduces litter N concentrations, which in turn decreases litter N mineralization and potentially reduces soil N availability, thereby limiting N uptake37. Consistent with this, our findings revealed a negative relationship between N resorption and uptake contributions on a global scale (Fig. 4). Moreover, plants exhibit increased N resorption and reduced N fixation under water–limited conditions17, which is consistent with our findings that drought increases the contribution of N resorption, whereas N fixation responds minimally (Fig. 4e, f and Supplementary Fig. 7). Overall, plants tend to adopt conservative nutrient strategies in drier and colder environments by increasing leaf nutrient resorption rather than relying on N uptake and fixation38.

The contribution of N fixation to total plant N acquisition is more frequently higher at high latitudes, even though N–fixing plants are dominantly distributed in low latitudes and potentially have a large capacity for N fixation39,40. This discrepancy can be attributed not only to the inhibitory effect of high temperatures on N fixation, as demonstrated by our findings (Fig. 4d) and prior studies35,41, but also to the cost–benefit trade–offs associated with various N acquisition strategies10,15. In low-latitude regions, despite the higher abundance of N–fixing plants42, the benefits of N fixation may be insignificant as plants can acquire sufficient N at a lower cost through uptake10. In addition, many tropical N–fixing species possess a facultative fixation strategy, enabling them to reduce costs by downregulating N fixation43. Conversely, at higher latitudes, N fixation becomes more cost–effective under more severe N limitation39. Studies have also shown that N–fixing bacteria and their host plants are increasingly adapting to cold climates, enabling them to fix N at rates comparable to those in warmer zones44,45. Therefore, the high contribution of N fixation in high-latitude regions stems from the sustained capacity for fixation, whereas uptake and resorption are reduced.

The preference of N acquisition strategies closely corresponds to ecosystem N status (Figs. 2 and 5). Our results support the expectation that, as N availability decreases (indicated by lower δ15N), the relative contributions of N fixation and resorption increase while plant N uptake becomes less dominant. This shift occurs because, when N is abundant, N uptake is a competitive strategy due to its lowest cost9; however, under N limitation, N fixation and resorption become more important by increasing N sources and reducing N losses18,46, respectively. The global patterns observed here are consistent with results from local experiments. For example, elevated carbon dioxide and warming enhance plant growth and N demand while potentially reducing soil N availability in the long term, thereby decreasing N uptake rate2,47. Conversely, both N fixation and resorption tend to intensify in response to these global changes48–50. These results reveal a scalable interaction between plant N acquisition strategies and N availability, providing valuable insights for cross-scale modeling of plant N cycling.

Notably, we found that plant N acquisition strategies exhibit different sensitivities to soil δ15N versus leaf δ15N (Fig. 5). The contribution of N uptake to total N acquisition is primarily influenced by soil δ15N rather than leaf δ15N, reflecting its belowground acquisition pathway. Conversely, N resorption is negatively correlated with soil δ¹⁵N but positively correlated with leaf δ15N. Higher soil δ15N typically reflects greater soil N availability, in which case plants reduce their reliance on internal N conservation through resorption18. However, during N resorption, the preferential export of the lighter isotope (δ14N) to the stem leads to δ15N enrichment in the remaining leaf tissue, resulting in a positive relationship between leaf δ15N and N resorption51. These patterns highlight the need to differentiate δ15N signals from above- and belowground ecosystem components when using δ15N as an indicator of ecosystem N availability. Together, our results underscore the complementary roles of different plant N acquisition strategies along the N availability gradient and demonstrate how these strategies help plants optimize N acquisition in different environments. Specifically, global N availability is projected to continue declining due to CO2–induced progressive N limitation, in which increased plant N demand outpaces soil N supply3,4,52. Under this scenario, adaptive shifts in plant N acquisition strategies could help alleviate N limitation and enhance plant growth and terrestrial carbon sequestration5.

This study has several broader implications for understanding plant nutrient economics and terrestrial carbon–nitrogen interactions. First, by integrating N uptake, fixation, and resorption within a unified, observation-informed framework, our analysis moves beyond pathway-specific perspectives and reveals that plant N acquisition is a coordinated strategy system shaped by environmental constraints. Second, the global patterns identified in this study provide empirical evidence to support the long-held theoretical expectation that plants adjust their N acquisition strategies according to the N status of the ecosystem5,10. By demonstrating systematic shifts from uptake-dominated strategies under higher N availability to increased reliance on fixation and resorption under stronger N limitation, our findings provide a perspective on how plants maintain function through strategic trade-offs in resource-limited environments. This is consistent with the concept of progressive release from N limitation48. Third, our results provide an empirical basis for evaluating how well current models capture the balance between N uptake, fixation, and resorption. Such pattern-based constraints are essential for interpreting model behavior and improving confidence in simulations of plant N cycling across diverse ecosystems.

Nevertheless, uncertainties remain due to the limited number of site-level observations that simultaneously quantify uptake, fixation, and resorption. Despite this limitation, our estimates fall within the ranges reported by independent methods in previous studies (Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Note 4) and show acceptable predictive performance, supporting the robustness of the large-scale patterns identified here. Second, scaling species-level measurements to ecosystem fluxes may introduce uncertainty, even when based on dominant species. To address this, our approach follows established practices in global synthesis, providing an internally consistent framework for comparing N acquisition pathways. This framework is further supported by model uncertainty analysis and cross-study comparisons (Supplementary Notes 2 and 4). Third, plant N acquisition is defined here from a functional and nutrient economics perspective. Leaf N resorption represents an internal recycling pathway that substantially supports photosynthesis, rendering it functionally comparable to N uptake and fixation in sustaining plant productivity. However, plants can also resorb N from other senescing organs (e.g., fine roots and stems)53,54, which are not included here due to the scarcity of global observations. Therefore, our estimates likely represent a conservative assessment of the total contribution of resorption to whole-plant N acquisition. Expanding coordinated, multi-pathway observations and incorporating community-wide data will be essential to further constrain these estimates. Finally, while our findings highlight the primary role of climate factors in predicting large-scale plant N acquisition strategies, it is also important to consider the influence of other factors, such as mycorrhizal associations. For example, our results (Fig. 4a) and those of recent studies7 suggest that plants associated with arbuscular mycorrhizae tend to rely more on uptake, whereas those associated with ectomycorrhiza tend to rely more on resorption. Furthermore, ongoing anthropogenic disturbances and community succession are likely to be altering ecosystems and influencing plant N acquisition55. Therefore, we call for future research to explore the temporal dynamics of different N acquisition processes to enable more accurate assessments.

In summary, this study represents a comprehensive attempt to quantify plant N acquisition strategies and examine how these strategies change with environmental variations. Globally, the dominant strategy shifts from N uptake in low-latitude regions to N resorption and N fixation in high-latitude regions. Temperature and water conditions are the primary environmental factors regulating global variation in plant N acquisition strategies. Using natural δ15N for verification, our analysis reveals significant correlations between plant N acquisition strategies and N availability, indicating a flexible adjustment of these strategies to changing N conditions. As N limitation is expected to become more widespread in the future3, it is crucial to consider such trade-offs between plant N acquisition strategies in response to N availability within Earth system models. This will improve predictions concerning the impacts of N limitation on the land carbon sink and guide future climate change projections.

Methods

Our analysis was conducted in five stages: (1) compiling global databases of plant N uptake, fixation, and resorption, along with 36 associated environmental covariates (related to climate, soil, and vegetation); (2) training machine learning models at the site level with environmental covariates; (3) applying the models globally to estimate plant N uptake, fixation, and resorption, and calculating their relative contributions to total plant N acquisition; (4) conducting a principal component analysis (PCA) to identify the main environmental drivers of the relative contributions of different N acquisition strategies; (5) examining the relationship between the relative contributions of plant N acquisition strategies and indicators of N availability (δ15N).

Data collection

We systematically collected data from peer-reviewed articles before September 2025 that measured plant N uptake rates, N fixation rates, and N resorption efficiency. Literature searches were conducted using Web of Science and Google Scholar. We also compiled data from publicly available databases18,21,56,57. This equation assumes that total N in senescing leaves is partitioned between resorbed N and N remaining in litter. Leaf litter N content was obtained from the EC-Earth3 model under the historical scenario and represents the annual N flux in litterfall. Only leaf N resorption was considered, because leaves account for the largest and most dynamic N pool in plants, and their N resorption represents a major component of plant-controlled N recycling at the ecosystem level.

We limited all datasets to field measurements from terrestrial ecosystems, selecting only control treatment data and excluding other experimental treatments. The final dataset contained 3193 records from 607 sites distributed globally across different ecosystems, including 54 sites for N uptake rates, 211 sites for symbiotic N fixation rates, and 342 sites for N resorption efficiency (Fig. 1). The number of observations for each N acquisition strategy was: uptake (n = 913), fixation (n = 493), and resorption (n = 1787).

We collected 36 environmental covariates based on their theoretical and empirical relationships with plant N acquisition strategies, encompassing climate, soil, and vegetation characteristics. Climatic variables (e.g., mean annual temperature, precipitation) were included due to their influence on plant growth, N acquisition, microbial activity, and the N availability16,18. Soil properties (e.g., soil pH, organic carbon content) were chosen because they regulate N transformation processes, such as mineralization and nitrification, which are critical for plant N acquisition63,64. Vegetation characteristics (e.g., leaf N content, net primary productivity) were incorporated to account for variations in N demand and acquisition efficiency across species and communities9,27. We also considered the basal area of N-fixing plants to reflect the coverage of N-fixing species in ecosystems42. All covariates were available as global gridded datasets and were used to develop spatially explicit models of plant N acquisition. Details of all variables, including descriptions, units and source information are provided in Supplementary Table 5.

Spatial modeling and global prediction

We developed spatially explicit models of plant N uptake, fixation and resorption, respectively, using a random forest approach. To minimize multicollinearity among predictors, we first conducted a variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis and retained only those variables with VIF values less than 10 (Supplementary Fig. 12). After that, we applied the bootstrap algorithm for sampling, randomly dividing the dataset into 80% training data and 20% test data using fivefold cross-validation. During the training process, a set of hyperparameters was iterated over to select the optimal ones, such as the number of trees (ranging from 1 to 2000) and the minimum number of observations per leaf (ranging from 5 to 500). We systematically traversed each parameter to identify the optimal model configuration, selecting the one with the highest R2 for each of the N uptake, fixation, and resorption models, respectively. Finally, the best–performing models, which exhibited the highest R2 were used for global map predictions. All model performances were well aligned with the testing databases (with R2 values of 0.68 for uptake, 0.56 for fixation and 0.47 for resorption) in our study (Supplementary Fig. 1).

To ensure that these spatial predictions can be meaningfully interpreted for global N cycling assessments, two complementary uncertainty analysis approaches were implemented (Supplementary Note 2). First, to characterize spatial patterns of predictive confidence, 100 bootstrap samples were created by resampling the training dataset with replacement. Using the optimized random forest configuration, 100 global prediction maps were produced for each N acquisition process. Final global prediction patterns were shown as the ensemble mean of the 100 maps. The pixel-wise coefficient of variation (CV, standard deviation divided by mean) and 95% confidence intervals were then calculated. A lower CV denotes higher model certainty. Overall, CV values of different models were low, with most pixels exhibiting CVs below 0.3, suggesting relatively low prediction uncertainty (Supplementary Fig. 2). N uptake exhibited the greatest uncertainty, likely reflecting its higher spatiotemporal variability and stronger dependence on rapidly changing soil and environmental conditions. In contrast, fixation and resorption rates may be more physiologically constrained by symbiotic associations and internal nutrient cycling, respectively, resulting in lower uncertainty. Spatially, higher uncertainty was observed in northern and central Asia and North America for uptake and fixation—regions characterized by sparse observations and low predicted rates—whereas resorption uncertainty was highest in tropical regions. These regions, therefore, warrant caution when interpreting the predicted spatial patterns.

Second, to assess the extent of model extrapolation beyond observed environmental conditions, we compared the range of predictor values at each grid cell with those represented in the training dataset. For each covariate, we recorded the minimum and maximum values of sampled data, and the proportion of pixels exceeding these limits was computed. The resulting map delineated interpolation (within range) versus extrapolation (outside range) areas. Locations with low interpolation (high extrapolation) indicate limited environmental representation in the model predictors and were primarily in regions that are consistently underrepresented in observational data65. This spatial extrapolation assessment indicated that the majority of global land areas fell within the environmental space covered by the observations, implying limited extrapolation risk at the global scale (Supplementary Fig. 3). For uptake, extrapolated regions (blue) were mainly located in African savannas and Arctic tundra—ecosystems poorly represented in the observational dataset. Thus, predictions in these areas should be interpreted with caution. Fixation and resorption models showed minimal extrapolation overall, though small Arctic areas exhibited elevated extrapolation for resorption.

Finally, the total plant N acquisition was estimated by summing the three N acquisition strategies, and the relative contribution of each strategy was calculated as the proportion of uptake, fixation, or resorption to the total. By applying consistent data processing, predictor selection, and modeling protocols across all three pathways, our framework enables internally comparable and spatially explicit assessments of global plant N acquisition.

Environmental driver analysis

To identify the dominant environmental factors influencing variation in the relative contributions of different plant N acquisition strategies, we adopted a two–step framework combining principal component analysis (PCA) and Shapley Additive Explanation (SHAP) approach66. Because the relative contributions of three strategies are derived from separate predictive models and are inherently interdependent (sum is 100%), their responses to environmental conditions cannot be directly inferred from the drivers of individual process rates. Therefore, rather than focusing on individual predictors, we first transformed the original environmental variables into a reduced set of orthogonal environmental gradients using PCA. This provides a clearer and more interpretable basis for assessing how broad environmental conditions regulate shifts among N acquisition strategies.

Specifically, PCA was applied to reduce the dimensionality of 36 covariates and extract the two most important principal components (PCA1 and PCA2), which together captured ~85% of total environmental variation (Supplementary Fig. 7a). We then examined how these environmental gradients relate to the modeled relative contributions of N uptake, fixation, and resorption. At the 0.5° grid scale, Pearson correlations were calculated between PCA1 or PCA2 and the relative contribution of each N strategy (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 7b). Correlation coefficients (|r|) below 0.1 were considered to indicate negligible associations of limited ecological relevance67.

To further interpret the environmental meaning of each PCA axis, we used SHAP analysis to assess the effect of each environmental factor on these PCA-derived components. While PCA loadings characterize the linear relationships between each variable and the principal components (Supplementary Table 6), SHAP analysis can further capture non-linear effects, interactions among predictors, and observation-level variability (Supplementary Fig. 8). We used the fastshap package in R to calculate the Shapley values, which quantify the contribution of each variable to the model predictions. The importance of each variable was then assessed by summing the absolute values of its Shapley values across all observations. A positive Shapley value indicates a positive effect on the principal component axis, while a negative value suggests a negative influence. The magnitude of the absolute Shapley value reflects the strength of the variable’s impact, which corresponds to the key factors identified by the PCA loadings (Fig. 4a, e and Supplementary Table 6).

N availability information from soil δ15N and leaf δ15N

To independently evaluate whether the relative contributions of plant N acquisition strategies co-vary with ecosystem N status, we used the natural abundance of N isotopes (δ15N) as an integrative proxy for N availability. The δ15N signal reflects cumulative fractionation processes during N inputs, transformations, and losses, and is widely used as an indicator of ecosystem N cycling openness and relative N limitation at regional to global scales3. High δ15N values in both soil and plants indicate greater N availability68,69, as ecosystems with high N availability tend to exhibit greater N loss via gaseous emissions or leaching, which preferentially remove 14N and enrich the remaining soil in 15N. In addition, plants in high N availability environments rely less on mycorrhizal fungi, which prefer to transfer 14N to plants, leading to higher δ15N enrichment in plants.

Therefore, we used two independent global δ15N datasets to assess the relationships between ecosystem N availability and plant N acquisition strategies. Soil δ15N data were derived from a product based on 5887 field measurements, with a model accuracy R2 = 0.9270. Leaf δ15N data were obtained from satellite-based retrievals linking leaf spectral signatures with field δ15N observations71. Both datasets were resampled to 0.5° resolution to match our global prediction maps of N acquisition strategies (Supplementary Fig. 9). For each grid cell, we extracted the relative contributions of N uptake, fixation, and resorption, along with soil and leaf δ15N values. We then conducted Pearson correlation and linear regression analyses to assess the relationships. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.001 (***).

To validate the reliability of δ15N as an indicator of N availability, we compared δ15N values with field-based evidence of nutrient limitation compiled by ref. 72, which assessed N limitation as the ratio of N to phosphorus resorption efficiencies. Larger ratios represent stronger N limitation, and we found that both soil and leaf δ15N declined with increasing N limitation (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Note 3). This cross-validation supports the reliability of using δ15N as a large-scale indicator of N availability.
