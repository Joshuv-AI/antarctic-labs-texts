Reduced Vascular Complications After Catheter Ablation Applying Sonography‐Guidance and Modified Postprocedural Care
Authors: Niklas Schenker, Julius Obergassel, Charlotte Asbeck, Marc D Lemoine, Djemail Ismaili, Ilaria My, Katharina Govorov, Fabian Moser, Jan Riess, Moritz Nies, Paulus Kirchhof, Feifan Ouyang, Bruno Reissmann, Andreas Rillig, Andreas Metzner, Laura Rottner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundVascular access and postprocedural access site management remain the leading causes for postprocedural complications following interventional electrophysiological (EP) procedures.MethodPatients were consecutively included in a prospective registry (TRUST, NCT05521451). Two vascular access and groin‐site management protocols that changed as an institutional standard at a cut‐off date (August 15, 2022) were compared. Group I used conventional access with an 8‐h pressure bandage and bed rest. Group II utilized ultrasound‐guided access, figure‐of‐8 suture, a 2‐h pressure bandage, and 6 h total bed rest. Minor complications included hematoma, bleeding, AV fistula, or pseudoaneurysm not requiring intervention. Major complications involved any incident necessitating intervention (transfusion, surgery, interventional therapy) or retroperitoneal hematoma.ResultsA total of 1672 procedures in 1501 patients (median age 65 (IQR 56.74) years, 38% female) were analyzed. Clinical features were balanced with the exception of a higher body mass index (median 27 [24;31] vs. 26 [24;30], p = 0.011) and more frequent arterial hypertension (63% vs. 56%, p = 0.003) in Group I. Total vascular complication rate was 9% (69/780 procedures) for Group I, and 4% (40/892 procedures) for Group II (OR 0.48 [95% CI: 0.32–0.73], p < 0.001). Major complications occurred in 30/109 (28%) cases, of which 20/780 (3%) occurred in Group I and 10/892 (1%) in Group II (OR 0.43 [95% CI: 0.18–0.97], p = 0.042). Hematomata were most frequently observed in both groups (Group I: 46/69, 67%; Group II: 30/40, 75%).ConclusionVascular complication rates in EP‐procedures were significantly reduced by implementing a novel institutional groin‐site management standard including ultrasound‐guided vascular access and modified post‐interventional care.

Introduction

Better outcomes of catheter ablation for cardiac arrhythmias and extended indications in guidelines lead to increasingly performed electrophysiology (EP) procedures [1]. Peri‐procedural complications can inevitably occur. Vascular complications, most of them non‐lethal, are the most frequently observed complications, leading to prolonged hospitalizations and subsequent interventions or surgery for complication management [2]. Uninterrupted anticoagulation, simultaneous antiplatelet therapy, intraprocedural systemic anticoagulation, and the complexity of the ablation procedure may increase the bleeding risk [3, 4, 5].

Conventionally, femoral puncture for vascular access was guided by anatomical landmarks, although interpatient variability of femoral vessel location, with the femoral artery crossing above the femoral vein in two‐thirds of patients, is high and complicates venous access [6]. Ultrasound‐guided puncture was first introduced for placing jugular central venous catheters and has become routine on many occasions [7, 8]. Ultrasound‐guided insertion of small diameter venous catheters reduces procedure times, inadvertent arterial punctures, and overall puncture attempts, as well as vascular complications compared to a conventional approach [9, 10]. Newer evidence supports the use of ultrasound‐assisted puncture in EP procedures [11, 12, 13], but most analyses originate from single‐center, retrospective datasets limited to atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation procedures; therefore, they do not represent the full spectrum of EP procedures.

As interventional electrophysiology moves towards same‐day‐discharge, demands on efficient vascular access and postprocedural groin‐site management are growing. Ultrasound‐guided vascular access and postprocedural vascular closure by figure‐of‐8‐suture could render vascular access for ablation procedure more effective and safer. The aim of this prospective study was to assess the impact on vascular complication rates of introducing ultrasound‐guided femoral vascular puncture and of a simple, efficient modified post‐interventional groin‐site management strategy as a center‐wide standard for all EP procedures.

Methods

Study Design

The present study is a secondary analysis of the “Long‐term Outcome and Predictors for Recurrence after Medical and Interventional Treatment of Arrhythmias at the University Heart Center Hamburg” (TRUST; NCT05521451) study, an investigator‐initiated, single‐center, prospective clinical cohort study including and acquiring long‐term follow‐up information on patients treated for cardiac arrhythmias or at high risk for cardiac arrhythmias. The study design was described earlier [14]. The study protocol and all legal documents of the registry were conferred within the ethics committee of the General Medical Council Hamburg, Germany in 2020 and 2021 (2020‐10 066‐BO‐ff).

For the present study, 1501 consecutive patients undergoing any interventional EP or ablation procedure around August 15th 2022, a cut‐off date when the institutional standard for vascular access and postprocedural groin‐site management was changed, were sampled from the registry. All peri‐procedural complications were routinely collected prospectively in the registry. Additionally, all documentation within the electronic health record, especially all clinical notes and future visits, were manually reviewed by trained staff to identify not‐yet registered but clinically documented access site complications for this analysis. All identified cases (prospective registry plus manually reviewed electronic health record), as well as statistical outliers and implausible data, were adjudicated and evaluated by trained electrophysiologists (NS, JO, LR). The study aimed to compare the incidences of minor and major groin‐site complications between these two groups.

Endpoints and Definition of Complications

The primary outcome was occurrence of any minor or major vascular complication observed during hospitalization and up to 30 days after discharge. Hematoma, bleeding, or pseudoaneurysm of the femoral artery without need for intervention (e.g., transfusion, thrombin injection, surgery) were classified as minor complications. Minor vascular complications requiring interventional or surgical management, or transfusion, and all arteriovenous or lymphatic fistula, abscess formation and retroperitoneal hematomata were defined as major complications. Decision on transfusion or surgical interventions was made using interdisciplinary clinical decision making, in line with recommendations by the “Bleeding Academic Research Consortium” (BARC).

Vascular Access for Electrophysiological Procedures

All patients gave written informed consent to the procedure. Catheter ablation procedures were, in general, performed under deep sedation using midazolam, fentanyl, and propofol. Vital parameters were continuously monitored.

Procedures in patients on anticoagulation were performed without interrupting oral vitamin K anticoagulation with a target international normalized ratio between 2 and 3 on the day of the procedure, or by omitting the morning dose in patients on direct oral anticoagulants. In left atrial or left ventricular procedures, intravenous heparin was administered aiming for an activated clotting time of at least 300 s throughout the procedure.

Usually, the right femoral vessels were used to gain vascular access. Only in cases of individual anatomical variants, vascular access was obtained left sided and/or via left subclavian vein. Depending on the target arrhythmia and ablation modality, the inserted sheath differed in size and number. Table 1 gives detailed sheath configurations routinely used in this cohort of patients. Invasive blood pressure monitoring was performed via a small (4F) arterial line at the discretion of the operator, typically for ventricular tachycardia ablation in patients with severely reduced left ventricular function.

Conventional Versus Ultrasound‐Guided Approach for Vascular Access

Analysis Group I

Before August 15, 2022, conventional vascular access was obtained using the modified Seldinger technique, simple anatomical landmarks and palpation of the femoral arterial pulse. After the procedure all sheaths were removed, and the puncture sites were manually compressed until hemostasis. A pressure bandage was applied and bedrest was prescribed for at least 8 h.

Analysis Group II

On August 15, 2022, ultrasound‐guided vascular puncture was introduced. A linear array ultrasonography probe was connected to a mobile echocardiography machine (GE Vivid S70N, General Electric, Boston, Massachusetts, USA) and covered with a sterile sleeve. After placement of the transducer at a 90‐degree angle to the femoral vein—displaying the vessels in short axis—the femoral vein was differentiated from the artery by compression and/or Doppler sonography, and vascular cannulation was performed under direct ultrasound visualization, targeting the anterior vessel wall in a 35° angulation at the level of the common femoral vein, while carefully avoiding low puncture sites or bifurcation (Figure 1). All operators were appropriately trained by experienced vascular medicine specialists to qualify for ultrasound‐assisted vascular puncture. The new postprocedural management included vascular closure by a figure‐of‐8 suture and a compression bandage for 2 h, followed by bedrest for another 4 h.

Ultrasound‐guided vascular access. (A) The operator holds the transducer while puncturing with the other hand. (B) The optimal site for puncture with the vein and artery visualized side‐by‐side (A: Artery; V: Vein).

In both groups, arterial punctures were mostly closed utilizing a collagen plug vascular closure device (ANGIO‐SEAL, Terumo Interventional Systems), but the use of arterial closure devices was left to the discretion of the operator. Postprocedural management did not differ between patients with and without arterial closure device implantation.

Postprocedural Management and Follow‐Up

In patients on DOAC, one dose for the day of the procedure was given 6 h after the procedure. Before discharge, patients underwent clinical examinations, including inspection, palpation, and auscultation of the puncture sites. Vascular doppler ultrasound was carried out to rule out vascular complications in case of clinical abnormalities. All examination results were documented in the electronic patient record by institutional standard. Postprocedural inpatient monitoring was planned for one night. After discharge, all patients were instructed to return to our emergency department in cases of any abnormalities or complications. For a period of 30 days after hospital discharge, each clinical visit for a vascular complication was followed up.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics for patient demographics, medical history, and procedural parameters are presented as absolute numbers and percentages, medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), or means with standard deviation (SD), depending on distribution which was determined through Q‐Q plots and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Univariate comparisons for incidences of minor and major vascular complications between study groups (I: conventional group; II: new groin‐site management protocol) and between procedure groups (AF/AT: AF and AF‐related atrial tachycardia (AT); PVC/VT: premature ventricular contractions (PVC) and ventricular tachycardia (VT) ablation, Other: non‐AF‐related supraventricular tachycardia, cavotricuspid isthmus ablation, left atrial appendage occluder implantation, right ventricular stimulation, atrioventricular node ablation) were performed using appropriate tests based on variable type and group count. Non‐parametric tests were used for non‐normal, non‐transformable variables. To assess residual confounding, exploratory multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed for the primary outcome. Potential confounding covariates were identified through univariate screening and clinical plausibility, followed by feature selection using Lasso regression. The resulting model included age, sex, body mass index, study group (reference: Group I), procedure classification (reference: other), and the CHA2DS2‐VASc‐Score. The latter was maintained as a composite variable to aggregate multiple clinical covariates, thereby preserving model stability given the low event rate. In a first sensitivity analysis, the CHA2DS2‐VASc‐Score was split into its components to determine which specific clinical factors drove the predictive effect. Subsequently, interaction terms for the treatment group and selected covariates were assessed within the original multivariable model. Finally, the use of oral anticoagulation at baseline was tested for its relevance as a confounder, with procedure classification excluded in this specific model to avoid multicollinearity. A two‐sided significance level of p ≤ 0.05 was pre‐specified. All statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.12 with Pandas and Scikit‐learn; graphs were plotted using Matplotlib.

Primary Outcome: Occurrence of Vascular Complications

A total of 109 vascular complications in 1672 EP‐procedures (7%) were observed, thereof 69/780 (9%) in Group I and 40/892 (4%) in Group II (OR 0.48 [95% CI: 0.32–0.73], p < 0.001; Figure 3). This relates to a relative risk of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.37–1.03) in Group II. In 30/109 (28%) cases, groin site complications were classified as major with an incidence of 20/780 (3%) in Group I and 10/892 (1%) in Group II (OR 0.43 [95% CI: 0.18–0.97], p = 0.042; Figure 3).

Anticoagulant use was not different between patients with (78/109 (72%)) compared to patients without (1107/1562 (71%); p = 0.965) complications. However, the rate of anticoagulant use at admission in patients experiencing severe major complications (25/30 (83%)) was numerically but not significantly higher compared to patients experiencing minor complications (53/79 (67%); p = 0.149).

Similarly, combined use of anticoagulants and antiplatelet therapy at admission was similar between study groups (Group I: 25/707 (4%); group 2: 20/794 (3%); p = 0.320). In complicative procedures, 7/109 (6%) received a combined therapy of an anticoagulant and an antiplatelet agent compared to 46/1509 (3%) of patients undergoing non‐complicative procedures (p = 0.088). A similar pattern was observed with respect to complication severity with 3/27 (10%) patients undergoing procedures with a major complication receiving both an anticoagulant and an antiplatelet agent compared to 4/75 (5%) of patients undergoing procedures with minor complications (p = 0.616).

The share of procedures with more than 3 sheaths inserted was higher in the group of patients experiencing vascular complications (17/109; 16%) compared to the group without complications (97/1563; 6%; p < 0.001). There were no differences for the percentage of procedures with more than 3 sheaths inserted comparing minor (11/79; 14%) to severely complicative (6/30; 20%; p = 0.555) procedures. Equally, any arterial puncture was performed in 24/109 (22%) procedures in which a complication occurred while only in 148/1563 (6%) of procedures in which no complication was observed (p < 0.001). There were 16/79 (20%) minor complicative procedures compared to 8/30 (27%) severely complicative procedures (p = 0.643) in which any arterial access was achieved. Predictors of Vascular Complications

Univariate predictors of any type of vascular complications were procedure type (AF‐ and AT‐related versus PVC‐ or VT‐related versus SVT and other procedures; p = 0.013), presence of arterial hypertension (p = 0.046), and presence or a history of heart failure (p = 0.010). The total number of sheaths was not different between patients with and without any groin complication (3 [2, 3] vs 3 [2, 3]; p = 0.076).

To account for potential confounding multiple patient‐ and procedure‐related potential predictors were included in an exploratory multivariable analysis. Age, sex, study group (conventional versus novel institutional groin‐site management protocol including ultrasound‐guided vascular access), CHA2DS2‐VASc‐Score, body mass index, any arterial puncture and procedure classification were selected as features. In line with the univariate results, belonging to Group II was an independent protective factor (OR 0.42 [95% CI: 0.28–0.62], p < 0.001) with respect to the occurrence of the primary outcome. Further strong, independent predictors were insertion of any arterial sheath (OR 3.79 [95% CI: 1.60–8.96], p = 0.002) and a higher CHA2DS2‐VASc‐Score (OR 1.12 [95% CI: 1.06–1.42], p = 0.005). Besides, age (OR 0.97 [95% CI: 0.96–0.99], p < 0.001) and body mass index (OR 0.42 [95% CI: 0.28–0.62], p < 0.001) were significant predictors (Figure 4, Table S1). To further explore the effects of clinical circumstances and risk factors, CHA2DS2‐VASc‐Score was split into its components. In addition to the beforementioned predictors, arterial hypertension (OR 1.71 [95% CI: 1.07–2.72], p = 0.025) was identified as the driver behind the predictive effect of CHA2DS2‐VASc‐Score (Table S2).

As an additional sensitivity analysis, we used the originally selected features age, sex, study group, CHA2DS2‐VASc‐Score, body mass index, any arterial sheath insertion and procedure classification and fitted an additional logistic regression model including interaction terms with the study group. A significant interaction with the treatment was only observed for higher body mass index (OR 1.07 (95% CI: 1.00–1.14); p Interaction = 0.047) suggesting that the protective effect of the novel institutional groin‐site management strategy may be attenuated in obese patients. However, the novel institutional groin‐site management strategy still retained a strong overall protective effect (OR 0.05 [95% CI: 0.00–0.62], p = 0.019) across the spectrum of body mass indices (Table S3).

As a third sensitivity analysis, the effect of anticoagulant use at baseline was explored in a multivariable analysis without interaction terms and excluding the procedure classification variable to avoid multicollinearity (Table S4). Herein, any anticoagulant use at admission was not an independent predictor of the primary outcome (OR 1.37 (95% CI: 0.81–2.30); p = 0.238).

Discussion

This study is one of the largest prospective registries, not limited to certain EP procedure types, to investigate the effects on vascular complication rates of a novel institutional standard for vascular access and peri‐procedural groin‐site management. The main results are:

Introduction of a novel institutional standard that included ultrasound‐guided vascular access, venous closure with a figure‐of‐8‐suture and reduced pressure‐bandage‐ and bed rest time was associated with a reduced overall (9% vs. 4%, OR 0.48 [95% CI: 0.32–0.73], p < 0.001) and major (3% vs. 1%, OR 0.43 [95% CI: 0.18–0.97], p = 0.042) vascular complication rate.

Introduction of a novel institutional standard that included ultrasound‐guided vascular access, venous closure with a figure‐of‐8‐suture and reduced pressure‐bandage‐ and bed rest time was associated with a reduced overall (9% vs. 4%, OR 0.48 [95% CI: 0.32–0.73], p < 0.001) and major (3% vs. 1%, OR 0.43 [95% CI: 0.18–0.97], p = 0.042) vascular complication rate.

Incidence of Vascular Complications

The incidence of vascular complications in patients undergoing catheter ablation varies according to procedure type, and is reported to be 3%–5% in patients undergoing VT‐ [15, 16] and therefore higher compared to patients undergoing AF‐ablation with 1%–2% [2, 17]. More recent analyses focusing on the impact of ultrasound‐guidance on vascular complications during ablation procedures found even higher overall complication rates of up to 8% [11, 13]. Heterogeneous definitions contribute to the variations of reported incidences of vascular complications. In line with the two previous analyses, this study considered a wide range of complications in its primary endpoint, especially including mild hematomas, persistent bleeding, conservatively treated femoral pseudoaneurysms and AV fistulas, which explains the higher overall vascular complication rate observed here (6.5%). Furthermore, shortened compression time and earlier mobilization may increase the likelihood of detected minor access site hematomas, but their relative rate remained similar in both study groups (62% in Group I vs. 70% in Group II) and overall minor complications, and more importantly, major complications were significantly reduced applying ultrasound guided vascular puncture, figure‐of‐eight suture and shortened compression‐ and bed rest times.

Two cases of retroperitoneal hematoma occurred only in Group II. One patient needed surgery for an active bleeding of a small venous side branch causing a relevant hemoglobin drop and a retroperitoneal hematoma, but could be discharged shortly after surgery recovery, while the other patient was managed conservatively with prolonged application of compression bandage and showed no active bleeding thereafter. Both cases highlight that ultrasound guided access is an important tool to reduce vascular complications, but is not without its limits. Close proximity of arterial and venous vessels and small side branches that might be overseen can result in accidental vascular perforation. However, the low number of these cases is promising and overall results support the changed institutional workflow.

Identifying Target Groups for Ultrasound‐Guided Vascular Access

This study showed an effective and significant reduction of the overall peri‐procedural vascular complication rate through implementation of a novel institutional standard that includes ultrasound‐guided vascular access and a modified post‐interventional groin‐site management. Beyond the reduction of the overall vascular complication rate, the novel institutional standard also effective reduced the incidence of major vascular complications (Figure 3). These results, in line with further studies [11, 12, 18, 19], support ultrasound‐guided vascular access in combination with figure‐of‐8‐suture closure of femoral veins. It additionally supports shortening of the postprocedural groin compression time regarding safety aspects.

Previous studies have suggested that older, female, and obese patients are particularly prone to vascular complications [11, 12] and it is therefore reasonable to assume that these patients may benefit most from ultrasound‐guided puncture. In our analysis, age and body mass index were significant predictors with only small effect sizes. However, arterial punctures and a higher CHA2DS2‐VASc‐Score were strongly and independently associated with a higher risk for vascular complications. Interestingly, the protective effect of the novel institutional groin‐site strategy was slightly less pronounced in patients with a higher body mass index in our trial, with a 7% odds increase for each unit increase in body mass index in the novel institutional standard group, relative to the conventional group (Table S3).

The total number of sheaths did not differ between groups with and without any vascular complication. This is consistent with results from randomized trials comparing irrigated radiofrequency (RF) current ablation with single‐shot devices, the latter requiring large‐bore sheath access [20, 21].

Generally, the novel groin management may be most beneficial in patients at highest risk of vascular complications, including elderly and obese patients and those with multiple comorbidities (CHA2DS2‐VASc‐Score p Interaction = 0.094; Table S2). Overall, the sensitivity and subgroup analyses reinforce the robustness of the observed protective effect of the novel institutional standard against vascular complications across various patient characteristics, with a potential need for extra vigilance in patients with higher body mass index. While patients on hemodialysis are at high risk for bleeding in various clinical situations, the number of patients on hemodialysis included in this analysis was too low to observe any differences in the primary outcome.

Impact of Peri‐Procedural Groin Management With Focus on Same‐Day Discharge

The implementation of ultrasound‐guided vascular access is associated with negligible additional costs and resource use. Besides, ultrasound‐guided puncture was shown to reduce overall and inadvertent vascular attempts [12], can shorten procedure times, reduce the length of in‐hospital stay, and therefore improve efficiency. Unlike expensive vascular closure systems, which are highly debated and showed significant but marginal benefits in a recent study [22], venous closure using a figure‐of‐8 suture does not involve any relevant additional costs. Future studies will focus on whether a compression bandage is still needed after an inconspicuous ultrasound‐assisted venous puncture and closure with a figure‐of‐8 suture, as well as how long patients should be monitored in hospital after ablation procedures.

Strengths and Limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this present study is the largest prospective cohort study to report on the impact of a novel groin‐site management standard on vascular complication rates. Systematic implementation of a novel groin management and comparison to an established, standardized conventional groin management approach, prospective data capture, systematic evaluation of all groins prior to discharge and capture of complications up to 30 days are strengths of the analysis. Our strategy includes not only ultrasound‐guided vascular access but also a standardized postprocedural management with figure‐of‐8 suture venous closure which reduced postprocedural groin compression times. A limitation is that this analysis cannot differentiate between these two components.

While the data set was collected prospectively and the switch from one strategy to another occurred on a given day, this falls short of a randomized study and opens the possibility for residual confounding. Explorative multivariable analyses were conducted to get an understanding of potentially confounding factors. The novel institutional standard, however, remained predictive across several adjusted sensitivity analyses. Further validations in controlled trials and in multi‐center settings are desirable.

Follow‐up was, however, limited to information collected in routine clinical care and late vascular complications may not have been recognized.

The population treated here is predominantly of Caucasian ethnicity. The setting of the study is a large ablation referral center. Results may differ in other ethnicities and in other types of centers.

Conclusion

This large prospective cohort study demonstrates that implementation of a novel institutional standard for groin management, including ultrasound‐guided vascular access and optimized postprocedural care, reduces vascular complication rates in unselected patients undergoing EP‐procedures. The novel protocol shortened compression time and did not require relevant additional resources. Future research and randomized controlled trials should focus on further refining protocols, particularly for high risk subgroups, and evaluating their impact on same‐day discharge rates.
