Holodiastolic Murmur following Blunt Chest Trauma: Rapid Clinical Deterioration due to Aortic Valve Perforation
Authors: Evan S. Offord, Offdan D. Narvaez-Guerra, Jeannine Jeha, Michelle Nabi, Michael Mankbadi, Gerard Aurigemma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Blunt traumatic injury to cardiac structures may have devastating consequences, and given the high morbidity and mortality of cardiac trauma, appropriate diagnosis and management are crucial to prevent poor outcomes. Although the vast majority of blunt thoracic trauma cases result in injury of the aortic root or the thoracic ascending aorta with concomitant damage of the aortic valve (AV) leaflets resulting in severe aortic regurgitation (AR), which can manifest in cardiogenic shock, it is exceedingly rare for a traumatic AV rupture to present with an intact ascending aorta.1,2 Herein, we present a case of a torn AV with intact ascending aorta and its resultant management and repair following a traumatic fall from a tree stand as a representative example of presentation and management of this rare clinical presentation following blunt cardiac trauma (BCT).

Case Presentation

A 68-year-old man with a medical history of essential hypertension, major depressive disorder, and benign prostatic hypertrophy presented to our institution after sustaining a fall. According to patient report, on the day of admission they fell 20 feet from an elevated platform, striking the ground with the left side of their chest. They were found down by family 2 hours after their fall, primarily complaining of left-sided chest pain. They had no surgical history, and their medications included lisinopril and sertraline. Family history was noncontributory. They denied recreational drug use.

The patient was hemodynamically stable on presentation, with a blood pressure of 126/53 mm Hg (pulse pressure 73 mm Hg) and heart rate of 82 bpm. Patient height and weight were recorded as 175.3 cm and 93.6 kg, respectively. Physical exam revealed tenderness to palpation of the left lateral chest wall and left upper and lower abdominal quadrants. A III/IV blowing diastolic murmur, best heard at the left lower sternal border, was present. Laboratory tests were significant for troponin I 0.71 ng/mL, creatine kinase 1941 U/L, lactic acid 7.6 mmol/L, hemoglobin 12.8 g/dL, and creatinine 2.1 mg/dL. A 12-lead electrocardiogram showed normal sinus rhythm with incomplete right bundle branch block.

Computed tomography confirmed the presence of left sixth and seventh rib fractures, a small left apical pneumothorax, and a small retroperitoneal hematoma in the left psoas muscle. Treatment was started for acute kidney injury secondary to traumatic rhabdomyolysis. On day 4 of admission, due to worsening hypoxia, the patient required noninvasive positive pressure ventilation, as well as afterload reducing agents as a temporizing measure. An invasive coronary angiogram revealed no significant coronary artery disease, and cardiac catheterization showed left ventricular end-diastolic pressure of 28 mm Hg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure 21 mm Hg, and cardiac index of 2.8 L/min/m2 by thermodilution method. The patient was then optimized for cardiothoracic surgery. On admission day 7, a transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) performed prior to surgical intervention showed tear and prolapse of the right coronary cusp, perforation of the left coronary cusp, and perforation of the NCC, with evidence of descending aorta diastolic flow reversal, consistent with severe AR (Figures 2 and 3, A, Figure 4, Figure 5, Figure 6, Videos 2-4). Severity of AR was supported by additional TEE measurements including an effective regurgitant orifice area of 0.65 cm2, pressure half-time (PHT) of 188 ms (Figure 2, A), left ventricular outflow tract stroke volume of 88 mL, regurgitant volume of 72 mL, and regurgitant fraction of 82%. Anatomical findings were confirmed during surgical exploration. The AV was noncalcified; the right coronary cusp was partially torn from the commissure with the left coronary cusp, both right and left cusps had tears in the body of the respective leaflet, and there was perforation of the NCC (Figure 3, B). Discussion

Blunt cardiac trauma may present as one of many injury mechanisms, including cardiac contusion, commotio cordis, valvular injury, aortic dissection, aortic transection, electric conduction system injury, pericardial injury, ventricular septal or free wall rupture, and coronary artery dissection.1,2 Although BCT is present in only a small fraction of trauma patients, outcomes are poor, accounting for a quarter of all traumatic fatalities, and thus it is crucial to detect and treat these conditions as they occur.2 The clinical consequence of valvular injury during BCT is well documented, with primarily regurgitant pathology; however, the relative incidence of the different cardiac valves being damaged is less clear in the literature. While some describe the AV as the most common site of damage, followed by the mitral valve, tricuspid valve, and pulmonic valve, others have found the mitral valve to have the highest incidence at 1.25%, followed by the aortic (0.93%), the tricuspid (0.13%), and finally the pulmonic valve (0.02%).3 As demonstrated, one consistent finding is that left-sided valves are seen as the most vulnerable.2, 3, 4, 5 This relative increase in the frequency of left-sided valvular injury may also be ascribed to their presentation, with left-sided valves generally having a more acute clinical presentation with cardiogenic shock and pulmonary edema, as opposed to right-sided valvular injury with later-onset sequelae of right ventricular dysfunction.5

Damage can manifest as an avulsion of the valve cusps, commissures, or shredding of the leaflets.6 During BCT, intrathoracic pressure may acutely elevate, which could translate into a rapid increase in intraventricular pressure within the closed left-sided heart cavities if trauma occurs during diastole. Aortic pressure may also increase due to a rise in intra-abdominal pressure. Both mechanisms of pressure increase put the AV at risk of rupture.5,7,8 Further, an underlying diagnosis of essential hypertension, especially if uncontrolled, may lead to left ventricular hypertrophy and reduced ventricular compliance, increasing the baseline left ventricular end-diastolic pressure and theoretically amplifying the risk of AV structural failure with the concomitant increase in intraventricular pressure from BCT. Although our patient carried a diagnosis of hypertension, blood pressure is documented as well controlled in the outpatient setting and they did not have evidence of left ventricular hypertrophy on electrocardiogram or TTE/TEE. Overall, this injury mechanism is consistent with the presentation of our patient, with valvular injury high on our initial differential following trauma survey. Further, approximately half of blunt cardiac injuries have concomitant thoracic aorta injury; however. the reported cases of traumatic AV rupture without an injured aorta are exceedingly rare in the literature, and management of a damaged thoracic aorta may require emergent surgical intervention.2,9 Thus, our case may serve as a representative example of presentation and management of a BCT patient with an isolated AV rupture without aortic involvement.

Presentations of acute AV rupture range from asymptomatic and incidental by echocardiogram months following the trauma to fulminant cardiogenic shock. This variability in presentation makes initial diagnosis challenging and requires a high level of suspicion. Our patient presented with a new diastolic murmur, and although initially hemodynamically stable, quickly required noninvasive positive pressure ventilation. Auscultating a new diastolic murmur, in our case AR, is often the first clinical sign of AV rupture. Acute, severe AR can manifest as a short-duration murmur due to rapid equalization of pressure between the aorta and left ventricle, as well as a high-pitch “cooing dove” murmur, classically described in AV leaflet perforations.10 Further, the elevated filling pressures are notable in this case. We hypothesize that the acute AR led to dramatic increases in left ventricular filling pressures because the regurgitant volume could not be accommodated by the normal-sized left ventricle acutely. There were likely, in addition, effects of pericardial restraint. Diagnosis of AR is further characterized by time course (acute vs chronic) and severity (mild, moderate, severe). Chronic AR classically presents with left ventricular remodeling in the form of eccentric chamber dilation, hypertrophy, and aortic root dilation to accommodate the reversal in diastolic blood flow.11 Our patient's normal left ventricular end-diastolic volume index (reference, 3.0-7.9 mL/m2), left ventricular internal diameter in diastole (reference, 3.7 to 5.6 cm), and aortic root diameter of 3.7 cm at the sinus of Valsalva (reference, 2.9 to 3.7 cm), as well as 3.2 cm at the sino-tubular junction (reference, 2.4 to 3.2 cm), suggest a lack of left ventricular remodeling and an acute rather than chronic etiology, consistent with BCT.

The use of echocardiography may also aid in classifying the severity of AR, through qualitative, semiquantitative, and quantitative methods.11 The presence of holodiastolic flow reversal (end-diastolic velocity >20 cm/sec) in the descending aorta is a crucial qualitative measure for defining severe AR.11 This flow reversal is shown in Figure 2, B, although with limitations in our patient given the relatively high scale of the measurement likely underestimating the apparent diastolic velocities. In addition, the observed low-flow velocity may be multifactorial, potentially reflecting a reduced low cardiac output state in this specific patient. In such cases, peak diastolic velocities in the descending aorta can fall below the typical >20-60 cm/sec range, which is likely the case in our patient. The PHT as measured by Doppler echocardiography is a semiquantitative measure for classifying severe AR, with our patient (Figure 2, A) meeting severe AR criteria (<200 msec).11 Lastly, our patient meets the severe AR criteria for critical quantitative measures in the effective regurgitant orifice area (≥0.3 cm2), regurgitant volume (>60 mL), and regurgitant fraction (≥50%).11 Thus, both the TTE and TEE quantitative measurements, as well as the clinical history, point to this case being an acute and severe AR precipitated by BCT and valvular perforation.

Echocardiography remains the reference standard as the best initial imaging modality in traumatic cardiac injury with a new cardiac murmur.12,13 Compared with TEE, TTE has lower sensitivity (47% vs 93%) and higher specificity (91% vs 87%) for detecting blunt cardiac injury.14 Diagnostic accuracy of both modalities for detecting AV injury in BCT has not yet been studied, likely due to its rarity. It should be noted that in polytrauma patients with poor acoustic windows due to mechanical ventilation, chest tubes, and/or other injuries, TEE overcomes the technical limitations of TTE and ought to be considered as an imaging modality. Although rare, similar cases do exist in the literature and further demonstrate the utility of TEE in these AV rupture cases.15

Conclusion

Our patient presented following a traumatic fall, initially with stable hemodynamics, with further investigation revealing an AV rupture requiring surgical intervention. Prompt diagnosis and treatment is key to preventing poor outcomes, with our case serving as a representative study as to patient presentation, clinical diagnostics, and management of AV rupture with intact ascending aorta, an exceedingly rare but potentially lethal injury that must be considered in trauma patients.
