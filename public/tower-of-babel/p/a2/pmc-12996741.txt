Pharmacokinetic Comparison Between a Fixed‐Dose Combination of Atorvastatin/Fenofibrate 20/145 mg and the Corresponding Individual Components
Authors: Seungri Lee, Juyoung Khwarg, Sungyeun Bae, Dong‐Min Park, Heejung Park, SeungHwan Lee, Kyung‐Sang Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCo‐administration of atorvastatin and fenofibrate has demonstrated clinical benefits in patients with dyslipidemia. To improve convenience and adherence, a fixed‐dose combination (FDC) of the two agents has gained interest. This study aimed to compare the pharmacokinetics (PKs) and safety of an FDC of atorvastatin/fenofibrate 20/145 mg with the corresponding individual components. A randomized, open‐label, single‐dose, two‐sequence, two‐treatment, four‐period full replicated crossover study was conducted. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the two sequences and received FDC or individual components. PK parameters were estimated using a non‐compartmental method. The geometric mean ratio (GMR) and its 90% confidence interval (CI) of the FDC to the individual components were calculated using mixed effect model. A total of 36 participants completed the study. The GMRs (90% CIs) for maximum plasma concentration and area under the time‐concentration curve from zero to the last measurable point were 1.1038 (0.9985‐1.2202) and 1.0148 (0.9745–1.0567) for atorvastatin, 1.0032 (0.9261‐1.0867) and 0.9882 (0.9520–1.0258) for 2‐OH atorvastatin. For fenofibric acid, the corresponding values were 0.9896 (0.8810‐1.1116) and 0.9871 (0.8869‐1.0986), respectively. The FDC of atorvastatin/fenofibrate 20/145 mg showed comparable PK profiles to the corresponding individual components, supporting its potential as an alternative therapeutic option for dyslipidemia with convenience.

Dyslipidemia is a metabolic disorder characterized by abnormal lipid profiles, including elevated low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), increased triglycerides (TG), and reduced high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C). 1 Among the various types of dyslipidemia, a particularly prevalent and clinically relevant form is combined dyslipidemia, characterized by the concomitant elevation of LDL‐C and TG levels, often accompanied by decreased HDL‐C level. 2 It is frequently observed in individuals with metabolic syndrome and is a major risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, which remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. 3

Atorvastatin, a 3‐hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl‐coenzyme A (HMG‐CoA) reductase inhibitor, effectively reduces cholesterol levels by inhibiting the conversion of HMG‐CoA to mevalonate which is the rate‐limiting step in cholesterol biosynthesis. It is recommended at an initial dose of 10 mg once daily, with titration up to 80 mg daily based on the patient's LDL‐C levels. Atorvastatin is rapidly absorbed, with a median time to maximum plasma concentration (tmax) of 1‐2 h, predominantly metabolized by CYP3A4 to its main active metabolite, 2‐OH atorvastatin, and eliminated with a half‐life of 14 h. 4 However, as monotherapy, atorvastatin has limited efficacy in lowering triglycerides (TG) and increasing HDL‐C, particularly in patients with combined dyslipidemia, suggesting the need for additional therapeutic strategies to achieve comprehensive lipid control. 5 , 6

Fenofibrate, a peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor alpha (PPARα) agonist, is a prodrug converted in vivo to fenofibric acid, the active form. It modulates gene expression involved in lipid metabolism and apolipoprotein synthesis, leading to significant reductions in TG and elevations in HDL‐C. 6 , 7 For combined dyslipidemia, the initial dose of fenofibrate is 145 mg once daily. 8 Fenofibric acid reaches its peak concentration within 6‐8 h. Following absorption, fenofibrate is primarily excreted in urine as metabolites, mainly fenofibric acid and its glucuronide conjugate. Fenofibric acid has a half‐life of 20 h, allowing once daily dosing.

Co‐administration of statins and fenofibrate has shown enhanced lipid‐modifying efficacy with no significant pharmacokinetic drug interaction observed. 9 , 10 Various studies have demonstrated that, compared to statin monotherapy, adding fenofibrate to statin therapy led to reduction in major cardiovascular events and improved lipid control, especially in patients with features of metabolic syndrome or persistently elevated TG levels despite statin monotherapy. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14

Administering these agents separately may compromise long‐term adherence, particularly in patients requiring multiple medications for metabolic syndrome. 15 Given the chronic nature of dyslipidemia and the need for lifelong therapy, developing a fixed‐dose combination (FDC) may provide a more patient‐friendly regimen, thereby improving compliance and potentially enhancing clinical outcomes. 16 Despite the therapeutic potential of statin‐fibrate combination therapy, no FDC formulation of atorvastatin and fenofibrate is currently available. The development of such an FDC is expected to improve both adherence and overall treatment outcomes.

This study aimed to compare the pharmacokinetics (PKs) and safety of a new FDC of atorvastatin/fenofibrate 20/145 mg with the corresponding loose combination to generate regulatory evidence in healthy Korean participants.

Methods

The study protocol was approved by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of H Plus Yangji Hospital (KCT0010222). The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Korean Good Clinical Practice (KGCP) guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent before screening.

Study Population

Healthy Korean participants aged 19 years or older with a body mass index (BMI) between 18.0 and 30.0 kg/m2 were recruited. Participants were excluded if they had a history of gastrointestinal surgery that could affect drug absorption (except for simple appendectomy or polypectomy), or any gastrointestinal disease. Participants with clinically significant hepatic or renal impairment, including elevated liver enzymes, creatinine phosphokinase levels (>3 × upper limit of normal), were also excluded.

Study Design

A randomized, open‐label, single‐dose, two‐sequence, two‐treatment, four‐period full replicated crossover study was conducted (Figure 1). Participants were randomized to one of the two treatment sequences: TRTR or RTRT, where T represents the test formulation and R represents the reference formulation. The test formulation was the FDC of atorvastatin/fenofibrate 20/145 mg (AD‐218, Yuhan Corporation) under development, and the reference formulation was the corresponding loose combination of atorvastatin tablet (Lipitor 20 mg, Viatris Inc.) and fenofibrate tablets (Fenowell 145 mg, Yuhan Corporation). Each participant received a single oral dose of either formulation twice, according to their assigned sequence, with a 14‐day washout periods. All treatments were administered under fasted conditions with 150 mL of water. Food and beverages other than water were restricted for 4 hou post‐dose, and standardized meals were provided at 4 and 10 hours after dosing.

Study design. Loose combination: Co‐administration of atorvastatin 20 mg tablet and fenofibrate 145 mg tablet. FDC: FDC of atorvastatin/fenofibrate 20/145 mg tablet. FDC, fixed‐dose combination.

PK blood samples for atorvastatin and its metabolite 2‐OH atorvastatin were collected in all four periods at pre‐dose, 0.17, 0.33, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, and 48 hours post‐dose. Fenofibric acid was sampled only during the first and second periods at pre‐dose, 0.5, 1, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5, 5, 6, 8, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post‐dose. The blood samples (4 mL) were collected in EDTA‐K2 tubes, centrifuged at 4°C, 3000 rpm for 10 min, and stored at −70°C until bioanalysis.

Bioanalytical Methods

The plasma concentrations of atorvastatin and 2‐OH atorvastatin were measured by ultra‐performance liquid chromatography (ACQUITY UPLC System; Waters, Milford, MA) coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (Xevo TQ‐XS MS; Waters, Milford, MA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source in positive ion mode. Atorvastatin‐d5 and 2‐OH atorvastatin‐d5 were used as an internal standard (IS). Chromatographic separation was carried out on a C18 column (2.1 × 50) maintained at 30°C ± 5°C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% (w/v) ammonium acetate in distilled water and acetonitrile, delivered with a time‐dependent gradient at a flow rate of 0.4–0.6 mL/min. Quantification was carried out using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The mass transitions monitored were m/z 559.45 → 440.45 for atorvastatin, 564.45 → 445.45 for atorvastatin‐d5, and m/z 575.45 → 440.45 for 2‐OH atorvastatin, 580.45 → 445.45 m/z for 2‐OH atorvastatin‐d5. The calibration curve was linear over the concentration range of 0.0500 to 100 ng/mL. Both intra‐ and inter‐batch precision (% relative standard deviation, %RSD) and accuracy (% deviation from the theoretical mean, % DMT) were within ±15.0%, satisfying the standard criteria for bioanalytical method validation.

The plasma concentration of fenofibric acid was measured by same UPLC‐MS system. Fenofibric acid‐d6 was used as an IS. Chromatographic separation was carried out on a C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm) maintained at 30 °C ± 5 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% (w/v) ammonium acetate in distilled water and acetonitrile, delivered using a time‐dependent gradient at a flow rate of 0.4–0.6 mL/min. Quantification was performed in MRM mode, with the mass transition of m/z 319.00 → 233.00 for fenofibric acid and 325.10 → 233.00 for fenofibric acid‐d6. The calibration curve was linear over the concentration range of 50.0 to 50,000 ng/mL. Both intra‐ and inter‐batch precision (%RSD) and accuracy (%DMD) were within ± 15.0%, satisfying the standard criteria for bioanalytical method validation.

Pharmacokinetic Analysis

For PK analysis, participants who completed up to second period were included in the analysis of fenofibric acid, while those who completed all four periods were included in the analysis of atorvastatin and 2‐OH atorvastatin. PK parameters were calculated using non‐compartmental analysis with Phoenix WinNonlin (version 8.5; Certara, NJ). The analysis was performed based on the actual times of blood sample collection. The maximum observed plasma concentration (Cmax) and tmax were determined directly from the observed plasma concentration‐time profiles. The area under the concentration‐time curve (AUC) from zero to the last measurable point (AUClast) was calculated using the linear trapezoidal linear interpolation method. The AUC from 0 to infinity (AUCinf) was calculated as AUClast + Clast/λz, where Clast represents the last measurable concentration and λz is the terminal elimination rate constant, derived as the slope of the terminal phase of the log‐transformed plasma concentration‐time curve using linear regression. Metabolic ratio was determined by comparing systemic exposure between the metabolite and the parent drug, calculated as AUCinf,metabolite/AUCinf,parent.

Safety Assessment

Participants who received at least one dose of the study drugs were included in the safety assessment. Safety was assessed by monitoring adverse events (AEs), vital signs (blood pressure, pulse rate, and body temperature), 12‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG) and clinical laboratory tests (hematology, blood chemistry, coagulation, and urinalysis). All AEs were classified according to treatment group and coded by system organ classes and preferred terms using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA version 25.0).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables, and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. A mixed effect model was applied to log‐transformed Cmax and AUClast to calculate the geometric mean ratios (GMRs) and corresponding 90% confidence intervals (CIs) for the FDC compared to the loose combination. The intra‐subject coefficient of variation (CV, %) of atorvastatin and 2‐OH atorvastatin was calculated using the within‐subject standard deviation (SWR) of the log‐transformed parameters, according to the formula: CV (%) = 100×(e(SWR)2−1). For analytes with intra‐subject variability exceeding 30%, scaled‐average‐bioequivalence limits were calculated to account for the high variability. Scaled bioequivalence limits were then determined using the regulatory constant k = 0.760 and the formula: [LowerLimit,UpperLimit]=exp(±k·SWR). These widened criteria were applied only to the Cmax, while the conventional acceptance range of 0.8–1.25 was maintained for AUC parameters regardless of variability.

The sample size was determined based on the maximum intra‐subject CVs of atorvastatin, which was reported as 42.2% for Cmax.17 Under the assumption of no PK difference between the FDC and the loose combination, a T/R ratio of 0.90 was applied for sample size estimation. Based on a replicated crossover design, a minimum of 30 participants was required to detect a 20% difference in PK parameters with 80% power at a two‐sided significance level of 5%. Considering potential dropouts, the total number of participants was set at 40.

Results

Study Dispositions and Demographics

A total of 41 participants were randomized, of whom one withdrew consent before receiving the first dose of the study drug. Accordingly, 40 participants received the study drug, 39 completed up to period 2 and 36 participants completed the entire study (Figure S1). The mean ± SD for age, body weight, height, and BMI of the 40 enrolled participants were 29.55 ± 7.74 years, 68.85 ± 9.43 kg, 171.14 ± 5.85 cm, and 23.48 ± 2.69 kg/m2, respectively. Demographic characteristics, including age, body weight, height, BMI, and alcohol consumption were similar between the sequences (Table S1).

Pharmacokinetics

The overall plasma concentration‐time profiles of atorvastatin and 2‐OH atorvastatin were comparable between the FDC and loose combination (Figure 2). Atorvastatin and 2‐OH atorvastatin rapidly reached peak plasma concentrations, and the log‐linear plots exhibited parallel elimination phases between the formulations, indicating similar disposition kinetics. Consistently, PK parameters of atorvastatin and 2‐OH atorvastatin were comparable between the FDC and loose combination (Figure 3, Table 1). For atorvastatin, the GMRs and 90% CIs of Cmax and AUClast were 1.1038 (0.9985–1.2202) and 1.0148 (0.9745–1.0567), respectively. For 2‐OH atorvastatin, the corresponding values were 1.0032 (0.9261–1.0867) and 0.9882 (0.9520–1.0258), respectively. The intra‐subject CV for Cmax in the loose combination was 41.4% for atorvastatin, corresponding to a widened acceptance range of 0.7392–1.3530 for atorvastatin (Table 2).

In the case of fenofibric acid, the overall plasma concentration‐time profiles were also comparable between the FDC and the loose combination (Figure 2). It exhibited a delayed tmax, consistent with slower absorption. The log‐linear terminal phases were parallel between formulations, indicating similar disposition kinetics. Accordingly, PK parameters of fenofibric acid were comparable between the two formulations (Table 1). The GMRs and 90% CIs of Cmax and AUClast were 0.9896 (0.8810–1.1116) and 0.9871 (0.8869–1.0986), respectively (Table 2).

Safety

A total of 7 treatment emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were reported in 6 (15.0%) participants in the FDC group and 5 TEAEs were reported in 4 (10.3%) participants of the loose combination group (Table 3). All TEAEs were resolved without clinically significant sequelae, and no serious adverse events were reported throughout the study. The incidence and severity of TEAEs were comparable between the FDC and the corresponding loose combination.

Discussion

Combined dyslipidemia remains a key risk factor for cardiovascular disease. As atorvastatin has limited effects on TG and HDL‐C, co‐administration with fenofibrate offers complementary lipid‐modifying benefits. 18 To improve adherence and convenience, the first such FDC of atorvastatin/fenofibrate 20/145 mg in Korea is currently under development. This study compared the PKs and safety of this FDC with the corresponding loose combination, providing regulatory‐quality data supporting formulation bridging in the Korean population.

The atorvastatin and fenofibrate doses used in this FDC were 20 and 145 mg, respectively. Atorvastatin is approved at doses ranging from 10 to 80 mg, depending on therapeutic goals and patient condition. The 20 mg dose is one of the most commonly prescribed strength in clinical practice and provides a well‐established balance between efficacy and safety when co‐administered with fenofibrate. 19 In Asian populations, atorvastatin exposure has been reported to be higher than that observed in Western populations, indicating that comparable lipid‐lowering efficacy may be achieved at lower doses. 20 , 21 However, the higher systemic exposure also warrants careful consideration of safety, as an increased risk of statin‐associated myopathy has been reported, particularly when co‐administered with fibrates according to the product label. 4 , 19 Taking these efficacy and safety considerations together, the 20 mg strength was selected as an appropriate fixed dose for FDC development. The 145 mg dose of fenofibrate was chosen as it is the standard approved strength commonly used in clinical practice.

Atorvastatin is classified as a highly variable drug. 17 To address high intra‐subject variability of the atorvastatin, a 2 × 2 × 4 full replicated crossover design was employed. Under the assumptions of 80% statistical power, a two‐sided significance level of 5%, and a T/R ratio of 0.9, the required sample size for a conventional 2 × 2 design was estimated to be 71 participants. In contrast, the replicated design reduced the minimum required sample size to 30, while maintaining equivalent statistical power, thereby enhancing operational feasibility and offering ethical advantages by minimizing unnecessary exposure to investigational treatments.

To further minimize participant burden, blood sampling for fenofibric acid was conducted over only two periods rather than four periods. This modified sampling strategy was considered sufficient to characterize fenofibric acid exposure under both treatment conditions. In addition to fewer sampling periods, the blood volume collected for fenofibric acid was also reduced, improving participant safety while maintaining the integrity of the pharmacokinetic evaluation.

The intra‐subject CV (%) of atorvastatin Cmax observed in this study was 41.4%, indicating high variability. In accordance with European Medicines Agency (EMA) and MFDS, the reference‐scaled bioequivalence approach can be applied for highly variable drugs. 22 , 23 , 24 Based on the estimated within‐subject standard deviation for atorvastatin Cmax, the acceptable bioequivalence range could have been scaled to 0.7392–1.3530. However, as the GMRs and 90% CI for AUClast and Cmax for atorvastatin already fell within the conventional bioequivalence criteria, the scaled bioequivalence approach was not required, demonstrating the FDC and loose combination exhibited comparable PK profiles under the standard bioequivalence criteria.

In this study, the PK analysis included both parent drugs and their active metabolites in accordance with regulatory and scientific principles. Regulatory guidelines recommend using the parent compound as the primary analyte for bioequivalence evaluation, unless active metabolites significantly contribute to the efficacy or parent drug concentrations are too low to quantify. 22 , 24 , 25 Atorvastatin undergoes extensive hepatic metabolism, primarily via CYP3A4, producing active hydroxylated metabolites, among which 2‐OH atorvastatin is the primary form. These metabolites possess lipid‐lowering activity comparable to the parent compound. 4 , 26 The metabolic ratios of 2‐OH atorvastatin in this study were 1.17 for the FDC and 1.18 for the loose combination, indicating systemic exposure of the metabolite was comparable to that of the parent drug in both formulations. The 90% CIs for both the parent and metabolite fell within the conventional bioequivalence range, confirming comparable exposure between formulations. Inclusion of the active metabolite thus provides additional support for overall PK similarity. For fenofibrate, it is rapidly hydrolyzed by esterases to the active metabolite fenofibric acid, and unchanged fenofibrate is generally not detected in plasma. 27 Accordingly, in this study, the PK of fenofibric acid was compared between the two formulations. This analyte selection strategy for both drugs aligned with their metabolic profiles and regulatory expectations.

This study has several limitations. First, the PK evaluation was conducted exclusively in healthy Korean participants, and therefore potential ethnic differences in the PK parameters of atorvastatin or fenofibrate cannot be fully excluded. However, while systemic exposure may vary across populations due to differences in drug disposition, such variability is unlikely to significantly affect the assessment of relative PK equivalence between formulations in a crossover design. Given that this FDC is being developed for regulatory approval and clinical use in Korea, the inclusion of healthy Korean participants was considered suitable for the study objectives. Second, this study assessed the PKs and safety of the FDC in healthy volunteers, but did not include an evaluation of lipid‐lowering efficacy. To further evaluate the lipid‐lowering efficacy and long‐term safety of the FDC, a phase III clinical trial (NCT05400317) is currently ongoing in patients with combined dyslipidemia who are stabilized on atorvastatin 20 mg.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that the FDC of atorvastatin/fenofibrate 20/145 mg is pharmacokinetically equivalent to the corresponding loose combination. The availability of an FDC could potentially enhance treatment adherence and prescribing convenience, which may in turn facilitate broader use of combination therapy in clinical practice.
