Enhancing a diverse population: the American Thoracic Society Mentoring Program for Underrepresented in Medicine
Authors: Kimberley C W Wang, Kimberly Lawrence, Juan P Wisnivesky, Alejandro A Diaz
Published: 2026

Introduction

Workforce diversity in pulmonary, critical care, and sleep medicine remains a major challenge due to the persistently low numbers of underrepresented in medicine (URM) professionals and trainees entering these fields.1-3 This lack of representation not only limits the breadth of perspectives and innovation within the profession but may also contribute to healthcare disparities among minoritized patient populations.4 Mentorship and networking are ­crucial for the career development and success of early-stage professionals and trainees.5,6 Additionally, a URM mentorship program could promote equity by reducing structural barriers (eg, increasing networks and role models), advancing career development (eg, enhancing leadership opportunities and research productivity), fostering belonging (eg, mentor-mentee relationships could buffer isolation and discrimination), and encouraging advocacy. However, junior URM professionals often face limited opportunities due to the scarcity of senior URM mentors.7

Program creation and development

To address this critical gap, American Thoracic Society (ATS) Health Equity and Diversity Committee (HEDC) members created a mentoring program in 2021 designed to support URM junior members (Figure 1). The program’s primary goal is to foster career development, professional networking, and provide structured mentorship through a 2-year program.8 The program is sponsored and overseen by the ATS HEDC, spanning all 14 society assemblies, providing a unique opportunity for participants to build a supportive professional community across a wide range of disciplines. While many participants are pursuing academic careers in clinical practice, research, and medical education, the program also supports URM mentees exploring careers in government, non-governmental organizations, and industry.

Program participation

Each junior participant is matched with a senior mentor based on shared areas of interests in clinical practice, medical education, research, scholarship, and work and life balance. Typically, the mentees were asked to focus on at least two of those areas to facilitate matching. Since the program’s inception, 28 mentors (64% female) from 26 institutions in United States and Latin America have participated, each typically mentoring one to two mentees per year. The ATS staff coordinates monthly mentor-mentee virtual meetings to ensure engagement and relationship-building, and the pairs are encouraged to meet in person at the ATS International Conference. Notably, 13 mentors have remained in the program for multiple years, underscoring their commitment and mutual benefits of these relationships. Mentors gain valuable insight and forge connections with other research or education groups with shared interests. The program also includes a web-based seminar series covering essential topics for career development, including networking strategies, career tracks, overcoming barriers in academic medicine, funding opportunities, curriculum development, and leadership (Table 1). The webinar topics were designed based on feedback from ATS HEDC and mentee surveys. To remain active in the program, mentees are expected to attend at least four of the monthly office hours and one live webinar.

To date, 62 mentees (63% female) from 56 institutions in the North and South America, Europe, Africa, Asia, and Oceania have enrolled. Participants self-identified as Black, Asian, White, biracial/multiracial, and Hispanic and span various stages of training, including residents, fellows, and postdoctoral researchers. Only one mentee has withdrawn from the program prematurely.

Program satisfaction and improvements

To evaluate the program’s impact, the ATS HDEC is starting to use participant surveys to assess engagement, mentee development and impact on their career. Preliminary results from a 2023-2024 survey (n = 15; 50% response rate) showed that 73% of mentees were very satisfied with their mentoring relationship, and 80% reported receiving practical advice and honest feedback that supported their career growth. For example, one mentee received advice on interviewing and then successfully matched in their desired specialist program. Others were provided access to resources and guidance in research grants. Beyond supporting individual participants, the program also benefited the ATS by identifying and cultivating emerging leaders from diverse backgrounds, ­fostering innovation in the field, and establishing a platform that promotes equitable dialogue and inclusion. For example, some mentees have subsequently been awarded the designation of Fellow of the ATS, a designation of academic distinction, and assumed leadership roles, including serving as chairs of Assembly committees.

Over the past 4 years, the core structure of the program has remained consistent, with incremental improvements enhancing its impact. For example, beginning in the 2023-2024 cycle, the number of webinars was increased to 4 per year with fresh topics based on participant surveys (Table 1). Additionally, efforts to expand the mentoring pool have been successful. While mentors were primarily drawn from the HEDC, the program now engages the broader ATS membership through targeted marketing and outreach on social media platforms.

Key lessons and challenges

Several lessons have emerged from this initiative. First, it is feasible and impactful to implement a society-wise mentoring program that connects URM members. Second, the program offers a valuable and safe space for conversations about professional growth, careers goals, and challenges. Finally, by spotlighting emergent leaders from diverse backgrounds, the program helps fulfills one of the core values of diversity initiatives—visibility and recognition.

Despite these advancements, regular attendance at mentor meetings and webinars has posed a challenge. Many participants face competing demands, such as clinical responsibilities, and international members struggle with time zone differences. To address this, the program now provides a calendar of events in advance and asks the prospect participants to commit to attending a minimum number of mentoring meetings and webinars. It also allows mentor and mentees to schedule their meetings independently when they cannot attend those organized by ATS staff. Another ongoing issue is ensuring optimal mentor-mentee matching. While the program achieves a high rate of alignment based on shared interests, occasionally there is no suitable mentor that matches the research subject for a particular mentee. To improve engagement and effectiveness, the program will launch an evaluation survey to measure the mentoring program’s impact, identify areas for enhancement, guide resource allocation, and inform future program design. To foster lasting mentoring relationships beyond the completion of the program, ATS HDEC hosts an annual reception during the ATS International Conference, providing mentors and mentees with a valuable opportunity to reconnect and network.

Looking ahead

In 2023, the ATS Pediatrics Assembly published the results of their diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) Needs Assessment Survey.9 One of the recommendations for supporting trainees was mentorship and sponsorship, which includes protecting URM faculty time for mentorship and scholarly oversight of URM trainees and facilitating networking and sponsorship for trainees, and to dedicate concrete resources to DEI efforts. Those survey results further highlight the importance of ATS URM Mentoring Program continuity. Based on our participant satisfaction survey, there is no doubt that URM mentoring programs are a critical strategy not only to foster individual growth and professional advancement, but also to address systemic barriers rooted in historical and structural exclusion. We also acknowledge that the development and implementation of URM mentoring programs at the medical society level should be informed by rigorous investigation—an unmet need that should be prioritized. With support from ATS staff and HEDC members, the ATS URM Mentoring Program stands as model to sustained commitment to equity, inclusion, and professional growth. It offers a solid framework for supporting URM professionals and represents a vital step towards a more diverse and inclusive future in pulmonary, critical care, and sleep medicine.
