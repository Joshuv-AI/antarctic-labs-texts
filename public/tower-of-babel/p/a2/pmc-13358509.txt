Real-time thoracic duct visualization in lung cancer surgery: feasibility of indocyanine green fluorescence imaging via inguinal lymph node injection
Authors: Jian-Ping Huang, Yuan Chen, Yi-Zhuo Hu, Yan-Ming Zhu, Hao Ding, Qi-Fan Li, Yuan Cui, Jun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChylothorax from thoracic duct (TD) injury is a serious complication of lung cancer surgery. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of real-time TD visualization using indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence imaging via inguinal lymph node injection during uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) lobectomy, with the primary endpoint being the success rate of TD identification.MethodsIn this retrospective feasibility study of 54 T1N0M0 non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients undergoing uniportal VATS lobectomy, ICG was injected into superficial (n=23) or deep (n=31) inguinal lymph nodes. The primary endpoint was the success rate of TD visualization; secondary endpoints included time to fluorescence appearance, signal-to-background ratio (SBR), chyle test positivity, chest tube duration, and hospital stay. Near-infrared imaging visualized the TD, assessing success rate, time-to-visualization, SBR, and chylothorax.ResultsTD visualization was successful in 96.3% (52/54) of cases. While success rates were similar between groups, deep injection yielded marginally faster visualization and higher SBR. Imaging revealed TD anatomy and variations, particularly near right lymph node stations. Right-sided surgeries had a non-significantly higher chyle test positivity and longer chest tube duration.ConclusionsDeep inguinal lymph node injection of ICG is a feasible approach for real-time TD visualization, with comparable outcomes to superficial injection. This technique may aid anatomical mapping and potentially help reduce iatrogenic TD injury, although direct evidence of clinical benefit requires further study.Keywords: Indocyanine green (ICG), thoracic duct (TD), video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Thoracic duct (TD) visualization using indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence via inguinal lymph node injection was highly feasible, with an overall success rate of 96.3%.

• Injection into deep inguinal lymph nodes yielded a marginally faster visualization time and a higher signal-to-background ratio compared to superficial injection, though the difference was not statistically significant.

• ICG imaging effectively revealed detailed TD anatomy and its variations, particularly identifying vulnerable small branches around right-sided mediastinal lymph node stations (2–4 and 7).

What is known and what is new?

• Iatrogenic TD injury during lymph node dissection is a primary cause of postoperative chylothorax. ICG fluorescence imaging is a promising tool for real-time TD visualization, but its application protocol in lung cancer surgery is not standardized.

• This study provides comparative data on two injection sites (superficial vs. deep inguinal lymph nodes), demonstrating that deep injection is a technically straightforward and effective approach. It confirms the high feasibility and utility of ICG-guided TD mapping specifically in uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery lobectomy for non-small cell lung cancer.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Preoperative deep inguinal lymph node injection of ICG offers a reliable method for intraoperative TD identification. Mastering this technique may aid anatomical mapping and potentially help reduce iatrogenic TD injury during lung cancer surgery.

• This enhanced visualization is expected to reduce iatrogenic TD injury, especially during right-sided mediastinal lymph node dissection where the TD is in close proximity, thereby potentially lowering the incidence of postoperative chylothorax. We recommend considering the adoption of this technique as a preventive strategy in radical lung cancer resections.

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most common malignant tumors, ranking first in both incidence and mortality (1). Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for more than 85% of cases, and approximately 30% of these cases are still in the early, operable stage at diagnosis (2). Radical pulmonary resection is the preferred treatment for such patients, including anatomical lung resection and mediastinal lymph node dissection or sampling. The surgical outcomes and control of complications impact the patient’s 5-year survival rate and postoperative quality of life (3). The thoracic duct (TD), the body’s largest lymphatic return channel, originates from the cisterna chyli (at the T12–L2 level), ascends along the right anterior aspect of the spine to the level of the fifth thoracic vertebra where it turns left, and finally drains into the left venous angle. This anatomical structure runs closely alongside the bronchovascular bundle, vagus nerve, and mediastinal lymph nodes in the hilar region (4). It is noteworthy that the anatomical variation rate of the TD is as high as 20–40% (5). This significantly increases the risk of injury to the main TD and its branches during mediastinal lymph node dissection in lung cancer surgery. Multiple clinical studies have confirmed that the incidence of TD injury after radical lung cancer surgery ranges from 2.5% to 5.4% (6-8).

Postoperative chylothorax resulting from injury to the TD and its branches is a serious complication after lung surgery. Chylothorax is defined as the accumulation of triglyceride-rich lymphatic fluid in the pleural cavity, typically caused by TD or branch injury, whereas ordinary pleural effusion is serous or hemorrhagic exudate. The two entities differ markedly in etiology, biochemical composition, and clinical management. Significant pleural effusion is closely associated with prolonged chest tube drainage, extended hospital stays, and increased hospitalization costs (9). Furthermore, chylous fluid leaking from a TD injury is rich in lymphocytes, proteins, and long-chain triglycerides, with a daily drainage volume potentially exceeding 2,000 mL. This not only leads to rapid onset of hypoproteinemia and lymphocytopenia in patients but may also cause fatal consequences such as acute respiratory failure and sepsis (10). Treatment for chylothorax includes conservative management and surgical intervention. First-line conservative management consists of a low-fat, medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) diet. When conservative treatment fails (e.g., daily drainage exceeding 1 liter or development of severe metabolic derangements), interventional radiology (such as TD embolization) or surgical ligation of the TD is indicated. Surgical methods are usually necessary when pleural fluid drainage exceeds 1 liter per day or conservative treatment fails. The primary surgical approach is TD ligation, with a success rate exceeding 90% (11,12). If the injury site is identified, direct ligation with non-absorbable sutures should be performed. If the injury site cannot be located, the preferred method is supradiaphragmatic ligation between the aorta and azygos vein. The challenge of this procedure lies in the identification of the TD. If the TD cannot be identified, only traditional mass ligation above the diaphragm can be performed, which may increase the risk of iatrogenic injury and make it difficult to confirm successful ligation. Therefore, intraoperative identification of the TD and its branches is the best preventive measure to avoid injury and enable timely intervention if injury occurs. In 1952, Kinmonth first reported visualizing the TD by injecting lipiodol contrast agent via the dorsalis pedis lymphatic vessel, pioneering visualization research of the lymphatic system (13). In 2009, Okuda et al. identified various courses and branches of the TD using magnetic resonance thoracic ductography (14). However, these TD imaging techniques have limitations such as radiation exposure, long imaging time, risk of contrast agent allergy, difficulty in imaging the surgical field, and low success rates, making it difficult to achieve intraoperative imaging and real-time identification of the TD during surgery.

Indocyanine green (ICG) binds to plasma proteins, resulting in an 8- to 10-fold increase in fluorescence intensity and a tissue penetration depth of 5–10 mm, making it suitable for real-time intraoperative imaging of the TD (15). Since Ashitate et al. first demonstrated near-infrared fluorescence imaging of the TD in animal models in 2011, ICG has emerged as a promising tracer for lymphatic mapping (16).

Currently, TD imaging technology has found some applications in thoracic surgery, achieving preliminary success in reducing the incidence of postoperative chylothorax in esophageal cancer surgery (17). However, it remains unclear whether it can similarly reduce the incidence of postoperative chylothorax in lung cancer surgery. To date, no study has systematically evaluated the feasibility of ICG-guided TD visualization specifically during uniportal VATS lobectomy with systematic mediastinal lymph node dissection, nor has the comparison between superficial and deep inguinal lymph node injection been reported in this context. This study aims to investigate the role of ICG TD imaging in radical lung cancer surgery and to evaluate the TD imaging effect of inguinal lymph node injection of ICG. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0178/rc).

Methods

General information

Patient clinical data

We retrospectively collected and analyzed data from patients who underwent uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) lobectomy + mediastinal lymph node dissection performed by the same surgical team at The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between May 2023 and October 2024.

Inclusion criteria were as follows:

Patients undergoing uniportal VATS lobectomy + mediastinal lymph node dissection, with postoperative routine pathology indicating pTNM stage T1N0M0;

Patients undergoing uniportal VATS lobectomy + mediastinal lymph node dissection, with postoperative routine pathology indicating pTNM stage T1N0M0;

No history of other malignant tumors and no prior any anti-tumor related treatments;

No history of other malignant tumors and no prior any anti-tumor related treatments;

All patients aged 18 years or older, with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status score of 0–1;

All patients aged 18 years or older, with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status score of 0–1;

ICG TD imaging procedure

ICG was provided as 25 mg powder and reconstituted in 10 mL of water, resulting in a solution containing 2.5 mg of ICG per milliliter. After general anesthesia was established and before surgical incision, while the patient was still in the supine position, one inguinal lymph node on each side was selected under ultrasound guidance, and 1 mL of ICG was injected into each bilateral inguinal lymph node. Bilateral injection was performed to increase total lymphatic uptake, shorten the time to visualization, and compensate for possible unilateral lymphatic hypoplasia or previous groin surgery that may impair lymphatic drainage. Surgery commenced approximately 10–15 minutes later, after the patient was positioned in the lateral decubitus position. Among them, 23 patients received injections into the superficial group of inguinal lymph nodes, and 31 patients into the deep group. The superficial group nodes are located superficial to the fascia lata (i.e., the iliotibial band of the thigh; superficial inguinal nodes lie superficial to this fascia), while the deep group nodes are located around the femoral vein. Typical lymph nodes are oval, with regular contours, clear boundaries, a central hyperechoic medulla with blood flow signals, surrounded by a thin and uniform hypoechoic cortex. Superficial inguinal lymph nodes are located superficial to the fascia lata and are numerous (mean 10.7 per patient in this study, size range 2.1–13.6 mm). Deep inguinal lymph nodes are located around the femoral vein, fewer in number (mean 1.2 per patient), but have relatively stable sizes (range, 2.7–7.1 mm). In patients with small superficial nodes, maintaining the needle tip within the lymph node medulla can be technically challenging; deep injection may offer greater ease in such cases. Criteria for successful injection: needle entry into the lymph node medulla, no blood aspiration upon withdrawal, feeling significant resistance during ICG injection, and observing gradual enlargement of the lymph node medulla and decreasing echogenicity on ultrasound. Surgical procedure

Preoperative examination and preparation

Patients deemed suitable for surgery after initial assessment were admitted. The attending physician collected a detailed history and performed a physical examination, completing preoperative tests such as computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography-CT (PET-CT), abdominal ultrasound, vascular ultrasound, and pulmonary function tests. After all results were available and absolute surgical contraindications were excluded, the surgical plan was determined. Subsequently, the surgical plan and associated risks were communicated to the patient and family. Surgery proceeded after patient and family consent and signing of the surgical informed consent form. Blood preparation and skin preparation were done one day before surgery. A soft diet was advised the night before surgery, and estazolam could be used if necessary for sleep. Fasting and water restriction were followed as per anesthesiologist’s orders preoperatively.

Surgical plan

Surgery was performed by a professional thoracic surgery team using uniportal VATS for radical lung cancer resection. The uniportal VATS incision was made at the intersection of the mid-axillary line and the fourth or fifth intercostal space, approximately 3–5 cm long, extendable as needed. After general anesthesia, double-lumen endotracheal intubation was performed in the supine position for single-lung ventilation on the healthy side. The patient was then placed in the lateral decubitus position with the operative side up, and a jackknife position was used to expand the intercostal space. The standard procedure was uniportal VATS lobectomy + systematic lymph node dissection. For left-sided surgeries, stations 5, 7, 9, 10, 11, and 12 were routinely dissected. For right-sided surgeries, stations 2–4, 7, 9, 10, 11, and 12 were routinely dissected. All resected surgical specimens were sent to the pathology department for histopathological examination. After chest closure, a chest tube was placed through the incision. The patient was transferred to the thoracic surgery intensive care unit for perioperative management.

When fluorescence signals revealed the TD or its branches within or adjacent to the dissection field (particularly around right stations 2–4 and station 7), the operating surgeon modified the dissection strategy as follows: (I) blunt dissection or preservation of periductal adipose tissue was prioritized over sharp dissection; (II) when a fluorescent lymphatic vessel was adherent to a target lymph node, fine electrocautery was used instead of sharp transection; (III) after completion of lymphadenectomy, the surgical bed was re-examined under fluorescence mode to detect any visible chyle leak, which would prompt immediate clip ligation or suturing.

Surgery-related indicators were recorded in detail in the surgical record, including surgical approach, extent of resection, intra-thoracic findings, anatomical and operational difficulties, operation time, intraoperative blood loss, TD imaging time, and whether fluorescence persisted throughout the surgery. Photographs of the TD were taken in both white light and fluorescence modes.

Perioperative management

Postoperative patients routinely received analgesic, nebulized inhalation, expectorant, anti-inflammatory, acid-suppressing/gastric protective, prophylactic antibiotic, nutritional support, fluid replacement, and other symptomatic supportive treatments. Chest X-ray was reviewed on the first postoperative day, and blood tests and biochemistry on the second day. Patients were encouraged to mobilize early, perform breathing exercises, and cough expectoration to accelerate recovery. The characteristics and volume of pleural drainage were recorded daily. A chyle test was performed on pleural fluid sampled on the second postoperative day. The test was performed on postoperative day 2 using 5 mL of pleural drainage fluid centrifuged at 1,200 rpm for 5 minutes; the supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of ether, and after stratification, the aqueous layer was stained with Sudan III and examined microscopically for fat globules. A positive test was defined as the presence of orange-red fat globules under microscopy or a triglyceride concentration >1.24 mmol/L (110 mg/dL), in accordance with international consensus. Relevant indicators were reviewed periodically to assess inflammation and internal environment. Bedside or upright chest X-rays were reviewed regularly to assess the thoracic cavity. If the X-ray showed good re-expansion of the operated lung, no air leak upon coughing, unobstructed thoracic drainage with low volume and not dark color, the chest tube could be removed. Specifically, the chest tube was removed when daily drainage volume was less than 200 mL, the fluid was clear (non-chylous, non-hemorrhagic), no air leak was observed, and chest X-ray confirmed satisfactory lung re-expansion. After tube removal, patients were advised to rest in bed for two hours. If no significant discomfort was reported and examination results were normal the next day, discharge could be processed after evaluation by a thoracic surgeon. Patients were instructed on precautions before discharge, and subsequent treatment plans were assessed by a multidisciplinary team.

Data collection and statistical analysis

Basic information, surgical process, and postoperative outcomes of the 54 patients were recorded and compared, divided chronologically into “Preoperative and general information”, “Intraoperative and perioperative information”, and “Postoperative and follow-up information”. This included age, gender, smoking history, underlying diseases (hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, etc.), pulmonary function GOLD classification, and imaging findings. Intraoperative and postoperative data collected included ICG injection site (superficial or deep inguinal lymph nodes), time to appearance of imaging (from ICG injection to intraoperative imaging appearance), surgical method (uniportal VATS left upper, left lower, right upper, right middle, or right lower lobectomy), operation time, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative tube removal time, postoperative complication rate, postoperative chyle test results, and discharge time. The primary endpoint of this study was the success rate of TD visualization, defined as clear identification of the TD or its branches under near-infrared fluorescence imaging during surgery. Secondary endpoints included time to fluorescence appearance, signal-to-background ratio (SBR), positivity rate of the postoperative chyle test, chest tube duration, and hospital stay.

Use of assessment scales

Preoperative clinical staging and postoperative pathological staging of NSCLC patients were performed according to the 8th edition of the UICC TNM classification. Patient general health status before cancer treatment was evaluated using the ECOG performance status scale. Complications were assessed using the Clavien-Dindo classification system. Pulmonary function was evaluated using the GOLD classification method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0. For continuous data, normality was tested first. If normally distributed, data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using t-tests; if not normally distributed, data were expressed as median (range) and compared using non-parametric tests. Categorical data were expressed as number (percentage) and compared using chi-square tests; Fisher’s exact test was used if the sample size was less than 5. Fluorescence intensity of the TD was quantified based on intraoperative recorded images using image analysis software (ImageJ v1.54f). The SBR was defined as the fluorescence intensity in the region of interest on the TD divided by the fluorescence intensity of an adjacent region of the same size and shape without the duct. Each image was assessed three times to reduce error. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013) and was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (No. 2024633). All patients provided written informed consent.

Results

Patient general information and clinicopathological characteristics

A total of 54 patients were enrolled between June 2023 and October 2024. TD imaging was observed intraoperatively in 52 patients after ICG injection into the inguinal lymph nodes. Regarding the two patients with failed visualization, one patient had a history of right inguinal hernia repair, and bilateral inguinal nodes were poorly visualized on ultrasound with no fluorescence signal after injection; the other patient had a body mass index of 32.5 kg/m2, possibly due to excessive subcutaneous fat impairing ultrasound localization or slow lymphatic return. A total of 54 patients were enrolled between June 2023 and October 2024. TD imaging was observed intraoperatively in 52 patients after ICG injection into the inguinal lymph nodes. The baseline characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1 (individual patient data are provided in Table S1). Inguinal lymph nodes are further divided into superficial and deep groups. Superficial nodes are located below the inguinal ligament, superficial to the fascia lata, and are numerous. Deep nodes are located deep to the fascia lata, near the femoral vein and canal. To clarify the effect of inguinal lymph node location on TD imaging, patients were divided into superficial and deep groups based on injection site. There were no significant statistical differences between the two groups in terms of gender, age, body mass index (BMI), underlying diseases, pulmonary function, smoking history, etc. (Table 2). The average bilateral superficial lymph node count per patient was 10.7, while the deep lymph node count was 1.2; the average size of superficial group nodes was 5.5 mm (range, 2.1–13.6 mm), and the deep group average size was 4.4 mm (range, 2.7–7.1 mm). The results showed a successful imaging rate of 95.65% in the superficial group and 96.77% in the deep group, with no statistical difference in success rate between the two (P=0.68). We further analyzed the intraoperative and postoperative outcomes of patients in the superficial and deep injection groups. As shown in Table 3, there were no statistical differences in the time to appearance of imaging and SBR between the superficial and deep groups within the ICG group, although the deep group’s imaging time (9.80±1.50 minutes) was slightly shorter than the superficial group’s (11.82±1.68 minutes), and the deep group’s SBR was slightly higher than the superficial group’s (51.13±24.15 vs. 45.27±36.12). There were no statistical differences between the two groups in imaging success rate, positive chyle test rate, operation time, intraoperative blood loss, grade I postoperative complications, postoperative tube removal time, and postoperative discharge time.

Discussion

Among iatrogenic chylothorax, radical lung cancer surgery is one of the common causes, with literature reporting a postoperative chylothorax incidence of about 1–4% (18). Postoperative chylothorax may be related to injury of its branches, which can easily occur during mediastinal lymph node dissection (19). This suggests that systematic mediastinal lymph node dissection is the main cause of postoperative chylothorax. Additionally, lymph node metastasis of the tumor may increase pressure within the lymphatic system, thereby increasing the risk of chylothorax (6). Due to the loss of electrolytes, proteins, and lymphocytes, chylothorax causes serious problems, adversely affecting wound healing and immunity. It is an uncommon but relatively serious complication after general thoracic surgery, especially after systematic lymph node dissection in lung cancer treatment (10,20). Therefore, preventing the occurrence of chylothorax is particularly important. Intraoperative identification of the TD and its branches is the best preventive measure to avoid injury and enable timely intervention if injury occurs. Several studies have shown that ICG TD imaging technology, capable of real-time intraoperative imaging, can reduce the incidence of chylothorax after esophageal cancer surgery (21). However, there is no standardized protocol for ICG injection site, and the role of this technology in radical lung cancer surgery has not been evaluated. The primary contribution of this study is to demonstrate the feasibility of ICG-guided TD visualization specifically in the setting of uniportal VATS lobectomy with systematic mediastinal lymph node dissection, and to provide the first comparative data between superficial versus deep inguinal lymph node injection routes.

There are four main routes for ICG administration: (I) subcutaneous injection in the interdigital web spaces; (II) injection into the mesentery; (III) subcutaneous injection in the groin; (IV) injection into the inguinal lymph nodes (22). Interdigital and groin subcutaneous injections have drawbacks such as long time to appearance of imaging and low success rates (23). Mesenteric injection is only applicable during the abdominal phase of esophageal surgery (24). Inguinal lymph node injection has a high success rate, is convenient to perform, and has a short imaging appearance time. Inguinal lymph node injection appears to be the most successful area for ICG administration (25). Inguinal lymph nodes are further divided into superficial and deep groups. Our results showed successful imaging rates of 95% for the superficial group and 97% for the deep group, with no statistical difference in SBR. There were no statistical differences in time to imaging appearance and SBR between the two groups, although the deep group’s imaging time was slightly shorter and its SBR slightly higher. In clinical practice, when using ultrasound to explore inguinal lymph nodes, it was found that some patients had small superficial inguinal lymph nodes, making intranodal injection slightly more difficult. In such cases, injecting ICG into the deep inguinal lymph nodes is more convenient. In our experience, superficial nodes were too small in some patients leading to one puncture failure in the superficial group, whereas deep node puncture had a higher success rate and better operator consistency. Thus, the clinical value of this comparison is to guide selection: both routes are acceptable when superficial nodes are favorable, but deep injection is a more reliable fallback otherwise.

Using ICG tracing technology, we explored the course of the TD in lung surgery. On the left side, the TD courses posterior to the aorta and is usually visible only above the aortic level. It continues ascending close to the spine above the aortic arch, where fluorescence disappears. Fluorescence reappears near the left venous angle in most patients, where the TD drains into the venous system. The TD was not observed even after dissection of left station 7 and 5 lymph nodes. On the right side, the TD was observed posterior to the esophagus in almost all patients. Gently pushing the pleural tissue near the esophagus forward with gauze better exposed the TD. At the level of the azygos vein arch, the TD was often located posterior-lateral to the azygos vein. In about half of the patients, the TD could not be observed above the azygos vein level due to its deep location, beyond the penetration depth of fluorescence. Previous studies have reported fluorescent TDs coursing above the azygos arch, deep to stations 2–4 lymph nodes, in two-thirds of patients, which is consistent with our observations. Meanwhile, we observed various TD variations, such as numerous small TD branches around right stations 2–4 lymph nodes (Figure 1A), a lymphatic vessel near right station 7 lymph nodes (Figure 1B), lymphatic vessels arranged along the intercostal spaces, and the TD giving off a relatively large branch crossing the left subclavian artery (Figure 1C,1D). Furthermore, we observed that one patient (1/29, 3.4%) had numerous small TD branches around the right stations 2–4 lymph nodes (Figure 2). When dissecting stations 2–4 lymph nodes, injury to such branches is inevitable. If chyle leakage occurs after injury and goes undetected, it may lead to postoperative chylothorax.

The absence of statistically significant differences between superficial and deep inguinal lymph node injection in this study may be attributable to the relatively small sample size and insufficient statistical power to detect small effect sizes. Alternatively, once ICG enters the lymphatic circulation, its transport kinetics may be similar regardless of the injection site, and differences in local injection characteristics could be offset by lymphatic flow velocity. Therefore, neither injection route is superior in terms of imaging outcomes, and the choice between superficial and deep injection can be individualized based on ultrasound assessment of node size and accessibility.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this is a retrospective, single-arm feasibility study without a control group. Therefore, no causal conclusions can be drawn about the reduction of chylothorax or TD injury. Second, the sample size is relatively small. The lack of statistically significant differences between the superficial and deep injection groups may be due to insufficient power. Third, the clinical utility of intraoperative TD visualization remains to be demonstrated in prospective comparative studies. Specifically, we need to assess whether it leads to modifications in surgical dissection or reduces complications. Fourth, fluorescence visualization above the azygos vein was often suboptimal. This limited the ability to identify the TD in the upper right mediastinum, where it is most vulnerable during station 2–4 lymph node dissection. Fifth, the potential impact of high BMI on imaging success and quality was not systematically analyzed. In the present study, one patient with failed visualization had a BMI of 32.5 kg/m2, and excessive subcutaneous adipose tissue may have hindered ultrasound-guided localization of inguinal lymph nodes or delayed lymphatic transit of ICG. Furthermore, increased mediastinal fat can attenuate near-infrared fluorescence signals, reducing the effective tissue penetration depth. Since our cohort consisted predominantly of non-obese Asian patients (mean BMI approximately 23–24 kg/m2), the feasibility of this technique in obese populations remains unknown. Future studies should specifically evaluate the relationship between BMI, injection success, and fluorescence quality.

Compared with the application of ICG lymphangiography in esophageal cancer surgery, TD visualization in lung cancer surgery faces distinct challenges. In esophageal surgery, TD injury typically occurs during mobilization of the thoracic esophagus, whereas in lung cancer surgery, injury predominantly occurs during right-sided mediastinal lymph node dissection (particularly stations 2–4 and 7), where the TD and its small branches are in close proximity to the lymph nodes. In the present study, patients undergoing right-sided resections had significantly longer chest tube duration (P=0.048) and a higher but not statistically significant rate of chyle test positivity (6/29 vs. 2/23) compared to left-sided resections, suggesting a trend toward higher risk of TD injury on the right side. Potential mechanisms include a more superficial course of the TD in the right mediastinum (often located posterior-lateral to the azygos vein), a greater number of small branches around right stations 2–4 lymph nodes (observed in 3.4% of patients in this study), and the more extensive lymphadenectomy performed on the right side.

Systematic mediastinal lymph node dissection is essential for accurate pathological staging and improved survival in lung cancer surgery. However, it inevitably increases the risk of TD injury and postoperative chylothorax. The technique described herein does not aim to replace or reduce lymphadenectomy; rather, it helps surgeons identify the TD and its variant branches in real time, allowing them to modify dissection techniques (e.g., blunt dissection, preservation of periductal tissue) to avoid ductal injury while maintaining complete lymph node clearance.

This technique offers several advantages over conventional lymphangiography. Lipiodol lymphangiography requires pedal lymphatic puncture, is technically demanding, time-consuming, involves radiation exposure, and cannot be performed intraoperatively. Magnetic resonance thoracic ductography is radiation-free but cannot be used during surgery. ICG fluorescence imaging provides real-time, high-resolution intraoperative images without radiation exposure, with a short time to visualization (approximately 10–15 minutes). However, this technique also has limitations. Fluorescence visualization above the azygos vein is often suboptimal because the TD lies deeper at this level, exceeding the effective penetration depth of ICG fluorescence (5–10 mm), which limits the ability to identify the upper course of the TD during station 2–4 lymph node dissection.

This study is an exploratory feasibility study without a control group; therefore, no conclusions can be drawn regarding the reduction of chylothorax incidence. Future prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to compare postoperative chylothorax rates, chest tube duration, and hospital costs between ICG-guided and conventional surgery groups. Furthermore, optimization of patient selection strategies (e.g., restricting use to right-sided resections, patients with low body mass index, or those with minimal mediastinal fat) may improve the cost-effectiveness of this technique and represents an important direction for future research.

Conclusions

In summary, our study confirms the feasibility of TD tracing using ICG in lung cancer surgery. Injection of the tracer into the deep group of inguinal lymph nodes is technically simpler, may potentially help reduce iatrogenic injury, but direct evidence is lacking. Mastering the ICG tracing method can visualize the normal and variant courses of the TD. This helps reduce iatrogenic injury to the TD during lymph node dissection, particularly in right-sided lung cancer surgeries, thereby helping to prevent postoperative complications such as chylothorax. Due to the low incidence of chylothorax (1–4%), routine use is not recommended for all lung cancer surgery patients. However, it may be considered for high-risk populations (e.g., those undergoing right-sided surgery, with prior chest radiotherapy, or with significant lymph node enlargement) or as a training tool for surgeons.
