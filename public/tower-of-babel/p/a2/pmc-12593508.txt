Targeting the extracellular matrix with Tenascin-C-specific CAR T cells extends survival in preclinical models of glioblastoma
Authors: Jana de Sostoa, Eliana Marinari, Martin Pedard, Valérie Widmer, Suzel Davanture, Karl Schaller, Stephanie Tissot, Michele De Palma, Benita Wolf, Gertraud Orend, Valérie Dutoit, Denis Migliorini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlioblastoma (GBM) is an aggressive brain tumor associated with poor outcome and limited treatment options. Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells targeting cell surface antigens were shown to induce tumor regression in patients with GBM, although efficacy was transient. To broaden the range of tumor-restricted antigens, we developed CAR T cells targeting Tenascin-C (TNC), a secreted extracellular matrix protein that is overexpressed in GBM and plays a critical role in tumor progression.MethodsSecond-generation CAR T cells were engineered to target the alternatively spliced fibronectin type III (FNIII)-D domain of TNC using a single-chain variable fragment isolated from the R6N antibody and coupled to a CD28 costimulatory domain. TNC-CAR T cells were evaluated in vitro for antigen specificity, activation, and cell proliferation using TNC-expressing patient-derived GBM cell lines cultured as adherent cells or as neurospheres. Reactivity toward purified TNC protein, tumor supernatant, and ex vivo patient tumor samples was also assessed. Cytotoxic CAR T-cell activity was tested against TNC-positive and TNC-negative GBM cell lines, including bystander effects mediated by secreted TNC. In vivo efficacy and safety were determined in NOD scid gamma mice bearing patient-derived GBM tumors.ResultsTNC-CAR T cells demonstrated activation when exposed to TNC-positive GBM cells, cell-derived supernatants, or purified TNC protein. They exhibited potent cytotoxicity against TNC-expressing, GBM-derived adherent cells and neurospheres, and induced bystander killing of TNC-negative cells in the presence of either TNC-secreting cells or purified TNC. In vivo, TNC-CAR T cells efficiently infiltrated tumors, triggered cancer cell apoptosis, and significantly extended survival of mice bearing patient-derived GBM, with no evidence of off-tumor toxicity. Notably, TNC-CAR T cells were activated exclusively in the presence of tumor samples and showed no reactivity toward patient-derived non-tumor tissues.ConclusionsTargeting the alternatively spliced FNIII-D domain of TNC with CAR T cells offers a promising therapeutic approach for GBM. TNC-CAR T cells demonstrated specific tumor recognition, robust antitumor activity and the ability to induce bystander effects mediated by secreted TNC. Their efficacy in preclinical models, combined with a favorable safety profile, underscores their potential for clinical translation.

Background

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most prevalent and highly aggressive malignant primary tumor in the central nervous system (CNS),1 with a 5-year survival rate in adults of less than 5% and a median survival of about 15 months. Standard of care, comprising surgical resection, radiotherapy, and temozolomide-based chemotherapy, leads to only a transient response, and has remained unchanged for the past two decades.2 3 To date, various immunotherapeutic approaches, including immune checkpoint inhibitors, oncolytic viruses and tumor vaccines, have been tested in GBM; however, phase III clinical trials failed to demonstrate significant clinical benefit.4 In contrast, recent studies have highlighted the potential of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy for brain tumors.59 In these trials, IL13Rα2-specific CAR T cells,5 epidermal growth factor receptor variant III (EGFRvIII)-specific CD3/EGFR BITE-secreting CAR T cells8 or bivalent IL13Rα2/EGFR-specific CAR T cells6 9 showed minimal toxicities and strong, although transient, tumor response in patients with recurrent GBM. In addition, GD2-targeting CAR T cells were able to induce neurological benefit and tumor shrinkage in pediatric patients with glioma.7 However, heterogeneous and unspecific antigen expression10 as well as the strong immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME)11 are challenges still to be addressed for CAR T cells to be fully efficient. In this regard, using tumor-restricted antigens is required to prevent on-target off-tumor toxicity.

Our laboratory has previously identified novel targetable antigens relevant to GBM,12 among which Tenascin-C (TNC) is a pivotal player in GBM pathogenesis. TNC is a large glycoprotein of the extracellular matrix (ECM) that plays multiple roles in malignant gliomas, including neovascularization, proliferation, adhesion, migration, and immunomodulation.1315 Importantly, TNC exhibits a dynamic expression pattern; it is predominantly expressed during embryonic development, undergoes downregulation in adulthood, and is re-expressed under pathological conditions such as tumorigenesis, inflammation, and tissue repair.1315 Furthermore, while TNC is encoded by a single gene, hundreds of TNC isoforms are generated by alternative splicing of the TNC transcript, in which up to nine alternative splice fibronectin III (FNIII) domains (A1, A2, A3, A4, B, AD2, AD1, C and D) may be integrated.16 While small isoforms predominate in adult tissues, tumorigenesis is characterized by the predominant expression of large TNC isoforms, which incorporate one or more of the alternatively spliced FNIII domains.14 Elevated TNC levels, particularly the expression of large isoforms, have long been recognized as promising targets for therapeutic intervention.17 In the context of GBM, the release of TNC molecules by tumor cells and their binding to the cell surface highlights the potential of CAR T cells to target and eliminate these cells. In this regard, targeting the alternatively spliced FNIII-D domain of TNC using the R6N antibody coupled to an immunocytokine resulted in specific tumor targeting and enhanced survival of SMA-497 glioma-bearing mice.18 Here, in order to develop CAR T cells targeting a GBM-restricted antigen, we generated TNC FNIII-D-specific CAR T cells encompassing a single-chain variable fragment derived from the fully human R6N antibody18 that recognizes human and mouse TNC, enabling testing in both human and mouse systems.

Results

Identification of an alternative splicing isoform of TNC restricted to GBM

We and others12 13 19 previously identified TNC (figure 1A) as a GBM-associated protein, including through peptidome analysis of 32 GBM samples.12 Subsequent validation of protein expression was conducted in a cohort of 250 primary or recurrent GBM samples.12 To further explore TNC expression in GBM and healthy brain tissues, we first analyzed The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) dataset. We observed significant overexpression of TNC in GBM samples compared with healthy brain (figure 1B). In addition, TNC expression was, on average, higher in grade IV compared with grade II and III glioma (figure 1C). Single-cell RNA-seq data from nine patients with GBM showed that TNC was predominantly expressed by neoplastic astrocytes and, to a lesser extent, by vascular cells, with minimal expression by other cells within the TME20 (figure 1E and F). In order to identify tumor-restricted TNC isoforms, we investigated exon-level inclusion of the FNIII-D domain (located in exon 17) using TCGA SpliceSeq and observed 86% inclusion in GBM and 82% in low-grade glioma (LGG), whereas this splicing variant was not observed in healthy brain (figure 1D). Among the >500 TNC isoforms reported,16 we detected 32 isoforms containing alternatively spliced FNIII domains in GBM and selected four representative isoforms—containing the alternatively spliced FNIII domains AD1–D; A2–AD2 and C; A2–B; and A1–A3, B–AD2—that together contain every FNIII domain (figure 1D, online supplemental figure 1A) for further analysis. Isoforms including the A2, A3, A4, B, AD2, or C domains showed expression in normal brain, whereas domains D, AD1 and A1 exhibited GBM-restricted splicing. At the protein level, we detected strong expression of the FNIII-D domain in human GBM samples (figure 1G and H). We also observed robust and tumor-restricted expression of TNC FNIII-D in tumors isolated from NOD scid gamma (NSG) mice following intracranial injection of the patient-derived TNC-positive Ge518 GBM cell line previously established in our laboratory12 (online supplemental figure 1B).

Generation and characterization of human TNC-specific CAR T cells

We next generated TNC-targeting CAR T cells recognizing the alternatively spliced FNIII-D domain. The scFv incorporated into the CAR construct was derived from the previously reported fully humanized anti-TNC antibody R6N.18 The TNC-CAR construct additionally comprised the short IgG4 hinge, the CD28 transmembrane domain and the CD28 costimulatory and CD3ζ signaling domains (TNC_28z, online supplemental figure 2A). As illustrated in online supplemental figure 2B, we achieved >80% transduction of TNC_28z CAR in T cells. TNC-CAR T cells demonstrated robust ex vivo expansion, and consistently displayed 88–96% viability across donors and conditions (online supplemental figure 2C,D). The majority of non-transduced (NTD) and TNC-CAR T cells expressed the chemokine receptors CXCR3, CCR2, and CCR5 known to be involved in tumor T cells homing/retention21 (online supplemental figure 2E). As a stem-cell-like CAR T-cell phenotype has been demonstrated to correlate with improved antitumor efficacy,2225 we evaluated the composition in T cell memory subsets, as well as expression of the T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3) and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) activation/exhaustion markers (online supplemental figure 2F-K). Flow cytometry analysis revealed variable CD4:CD8 ratios depending on the donor (online supplemental figure 2F,K) whereas both NTD and TNC-CAR T cells predominantly consisted of central memory (TCM) cells, with smaller proportions of effector memory (TEM) cells (online supplemental figure 2G,K). CD127, a predictive marker of T-cell stemness,26 was consistently expressed across multiple donors (online supplemental figure 2H,K). Finally, while neither NTD nor TNC-CAR T cells expressed PD-1, both exhibited high levels of TIM-3 (online supplemental figure 2I-K).

We next assessed antigen-specific TNC-CAR T-cell function using GBM cell lines generated in our laboratory as targets. Ge518 and Ge1302 cell lines expressed both the short TNC isoform (TNCs) as well as the TNC FNIII-D isoform (TNC_D) at the RNA level, whereas the Ge738 cell line did not (figure 2A). Intracellular TNC protein expression was confirmed by flow cytometry in the Ge518 and Ge1302 cell lines (figure 2B) and by ELISA in the Ge518 cell line (figure 2C). Then, to first investigate whether TNC-CAR T cells were able to bind soluble TNC, both NTD and TNC-CAR T cells were co-cultured with purified recombinant human TNC (rhTNC) or Ge518-derived supernatant. TNC binding to the surface of TNC-CAR T cells was higher in the presence of rhTNC than cell supernatant (figure 2D) and higher on CAR T cells with high TNC-CAR expression (TNC_28zHigh). Importantly, no binding was detected on NTD T cells.

We then evaluated the activation and proliferation capacity of TNC-CAR T cells. NTD and TNC-CAR T cells were co-cultured with increasing amounts of rhTNC or Ge518-derived supernatant. After 72 hours, only TNC-CAR T cells exhibited dose-dependent activation, as observed by expression of the activation markers CD25 and CD69 (figure 2E). In addition, CD8+ and CD4+ TNC-CAR T cells were able to specifically proliferate when co-cultured with TNC-expressing Ge518 and Ge1302 cell lines, but not with the TNC-negative Ge738 cell line (figure 2F). CD8+ TNC-CAR T cells also proliferated when co-culture with rhTNC (figure 2G). These results suggest that TNC-CAR T cells are activated specifically when exposed to TNC, whether expressed by patient-derived GBM cell lines or present in a soluble form.

TNC-CAR T cells mediate antitumor activity against patient-derived GBM cells in vitro

We next investigated TNC-CAR T cell-mediated cytotoxicity in vitro. We first incubated green fluorescent protein (GFP)-expressing Ge518, Ge1302, and Ge738 cell lines with NTD or TNC-CAR T cells at different effector:target (E:T) ratios and followed tumor cell growth over time. TNC-CAR T cells were able to inhibit the growth of TNC-positive Ge518 and Ge1302 cell lines, whereas growth of the TNC-negative Ge738 cell line was not affected (figure 3A). Consistent with these observations, TNC-CAR T cells exhibited significant killing of TNC-positive but not TNC-negative cell lines (figure 3B). Moderate growth inhibition and cytotoxicity were observed also when Ge518 cells were co-cultured with NTD T cells, which might be attributed to nutrient competition or increased sensitivity of the Ge518 cell line to T cells. We then assessed cytokine and cytotoxic molecule secretion using the 13-plex human CD8/NK panel. TNC-CAR T cells coincubated with TNC-positive Ge518 and Ge1302 cells secreted elevated levels of interleukin (IL)-6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, soluble FasL, interferon (IFN)-γ, granzyme B (GzB), perforin and granulysin compared with TNC-CAR T cells incubated with TNC-negative Ge738 cells (figure 3C). We further evaluated co-expression of GzB, IFN-γ, IL-2, and TNF-α in CD4+ and CD8+ TNC-CAR T cells either alone or after 24-hour co-culture with Ge518 cells. As shown in figure 3D, CD4+ and CD8+ TNC-CAR T cells co-expressed up to four effector molecules, with CD4+ cells displaying particularly high multifunctionality. These results demonstrate that TNC-CAR T cells can mount a robust, antigen-dependent and polyfunctional response.

We then investigated changes in CAR T-cell phenotype post-antigen exposure, by determining the proportions of TN, TEM, TCM, and TEMRA cells within NTD and TNC-CAR T cells before and after co-culture with TNC-positive or TNC-negative cells (figure 3E). After 72 hours of co-culture with TNC-positive tumor cells, TNC-CAR T cells showed increased proportions of TEM cells, which were not observed after incubation with TNC-negative tumor cells, consistent with an antigen-driven T-cell activation trajectory.

Finally, to recapitulate the in vivo setting in which tumor cells grow in three-dimensional (3D) structures, we assessed the cytotoxic activity of TNC-CAR T cells using neurospheres. We generated GFP+ neurospheres from the NS970.2, NS904, and GBM6 patient-derived cell lines, by seeding dissociated cells and culturing them for 72 hours to allow the formation of a single neurosphere per well before adding NTD or TNC-CAR T cells. As shown in figure 3F and G, co-culture with TNC-CAR T cells, but not NTD T cells, induced rapid and robust lysis of NS904 and GBM6, and to a lesser extent NS970.2 cells, consistent with their lower TNC expression (online supplemental figure 3A). These results were replicated using T cells from a different healthy donor (online supplemental figure 3B,C). Notably, CD19_28z (CD19)-CAR T cells, which express a CAR unable to recognize GBM cells, failed to inhibit tumor cell growth. In summary, these results show that TNC-CAR T cells can effectively overcome structural barriers posed by 3D tumor architectures likely to be observed in vivo.

TNC-CAR T cells mediate contact-dependent bystander killing

Several studies have demonstrated that CAR T cells can mediate bystander killing of antigen-negative cells.2729 To assess this, mCherry-expressing TNC-positive Ge518 or Ge1302 cells were mixed with GFP-expressing TNC-negative Ge738 cells and cultured in the presence of NTD or TNC-CAR T cells (figure 4A). In this setting, TNC-CAR T cells were able to inhibit the growth of both TNC-positive and TNC-negative tumor cells (figure 4B,C). This bystander effect was further observed in cytotoxicity assays (figure 4D). These results suggest that TNC-CAR T cells can induce bystander lysis of TNC-negative cells when co-cultured with TNC-positive cells.

To investigate whether TNC-CAR T cell-mediated bystander killing was due to binding of TNC to neighboring antigen-negative cells, we co-cultured individual Ge518, Ge1302, and Ge738 cells with NTD or TNC-CAR T cells in the absence or presence of rhTNC (figure 4E). We observed a significant increase in the killing of TNC-negative Ge738 cells when rhTNC was added, suggesting that bystander cytotoxicity was at least partly due to TNC binding to TNC-negative cells. To confirm this finding, we co-cultured TNC-negative Ge738 cells with NTD or TNC-CAR T cells in the absence or presence of rhTNC and observed tumor growth inhibition only in the presence of rhTNC (figure 4F).

To further define the mode and kinetics of killing, we performed spinning-disk live imaging of CD19- or TNC-CAR T-cell interaction with the Ge518 and Ge738 cell lines (figure 4G–K). When co-culture with Ge518 cells, TNC-CAR T cells formed fewer (figure 4H) and more stable (figure 4I) contacts while CD19-CAR T cells made frequent and shorter contacts. In addition, TNC-CAR T cells polarized lytic granules toward the immune synapse (figure 4J) and killed targets rapidly (figure 4G and K), typically within 10–60 min of initial contact, whereas control CD19-CAR T cells made non-synaptic contacts and did not kill (figure 4G and K). These observations support direct, contact-dependent, immune-synapse–mediated killing of TNC+ targets. In the case of co-culture with Ge738 cells, in which rhTNC was available exogenously to enable interaction, TNC-CAR T cells were able to form more stable contacts with tumor cells than CD19-CAR T cells (figure 4I). There were also trends towards increased numbers of TNC-CAR T cells polarizing lytic granules toward the immune synapse (figure 4J) and increased target cell killing (figure 4G and K) as compared with CD19-CAR T cells. These data suggest that Ge738 killing in the presence of rhTNC is partially mediated by perforin/granzyme-dependent synapse-mediated cytotoxicity, potentially complemented by cytokine-receptor or death-receptor (Fas/FasL) pathways. Altogether, these findings highlight the potential of targeting extracellular TNC with CAR T cells as a strategy to eliminate TNC-negative cells in proximity to TNC-secreting tumor cells.

TNC-CAR T cells exhibit antitumor efficacy in patient-derived GBM xenografts

We then evaluated the efficacy of TNC-CAR T cells in a patient-derived GBM xenograft. Ge518 cells were implanted orthotopically in NSG mice followed by intracranial transfer of NTD or TNC-CAR T cells on day 7 (figure 5A). Treatment with TNC-CAR T cells resulted in significant tumor regression (figure 5B,C) and extended mouse survival (figure 5D) compared with NTD T cell-treated mice. However, all tumors relapsed shortly after CAR T-cell transfer. To investigate whether this was due to antigen escape, we collected tumors at the end of the experiment and evaluated TNC expression by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR). While a mild decrease in TNC expression was observed in the TNC-CAR T cell-treated mice compared with NTD T cell-treated mice, TNC expression was conserved in all resected tumors (figure 5E). We also examined expression of TNC FNIII-D using immunofluorescence analysis and detected TNC-positive tumor nests within TNC-negative tumor areas in all evaluated TNC-CAR T cell-treated mice (figure 5F), although TNC expression was often lower than in NTD T cell-treated mice (figure 5G). We repeated this experiment with smaller cohorts of mice and sacrificed them on day 21, when the tumors had already relapsed. Tumor cells isolated from NTD or TNC-CAR T cell-treated mice were collected and exposed to NTD or TNC-CAR T cells and IFN-γ was measured in the supernatant (online supplemental figure 4A,B). We observed strong activation of TNC-CAR T cells when cultured with resected tumors, regardless of whether they were derived from NTD or TNC-CAR T cell-treated mice (online supplemental figure 4C), indicating that there was no significant antigen loss in this model.

To investigate potential changes in expression of TNC isoforms in response to CAR T-cell therapy, we used NSG mice carrying intracranial Ge518 tumors and sacrificed them on day 4, 7, and 10 post-T cell transfer (online supplemental figure 5A). The levels of the TNC isoforms containing the alternatively spliced domains were analyzed by qRT-PCR. Given the interaction of TNC with other ECM proteins, we also assessed expression of the human FN1 and COL6A1 genes. Human Tenascin-R (TN-R) and Tenascin-W (TN-W) were also assessed in light of their importance in cancer.13 Additionally, we evaluated human T-cell infiltration by analyzing expression of the human CD4 and CD8 genes. As shown in online supplemental figure 5B, no significant alterations were observed in the content of TNC isoforms containing the A1, A2, A3, A4, B, AD2, AD1, C, and D domains over the 10 days following T-cell infusion. TN-R and TN-W transcripts were not detected. Interestingly, while not statistically significant, FN1 and COL6A1 expression showed a slight decrease in TNC-CAR T cell-treated mice compared with NTD T cell-treated mice, suggesting that targeting TNC may influence expression of other ECM molecules, resembling the TNCKO condition.30 31 We also noted a trend of elevated CD4 and CD8 gene expression in TNC-CAR T cell-treated groups compared with NTD T cell-treated groups.

Having ruled out antigen or isoform loss as a reason for tumor relapse, we tested whether a second CAR T-cell infusion improved mouse survival (online supplemental figure 6A). NTD and CD19-CAR T cells showed no difference in survival, confirming the specificity of the TNC-CAR effect (online supplemental figure 6B,C). However, administration of two TNC-CAR T-cell doses did not extend survival compared with a single dose, likely due to the aggressive nature of the Ge518 tumor model.

Finally, to substantiate our findings, we tested TNC-CAR T cells in additional patient-derived GBM xenografts. Patient-derived GBM632 (figure 5H) and BT93533 (figure 5I) cells were implanted intracranially into NSG mice, followed by intratumoral administration of either CD19-CAR or TNC-CAR T cells on day 9 (GBM6) or 21 (BT935). In both models, TNC-CAR T-cell treatment significantly prolonged survival compared with CD19-CAR T cells.

TNC-CAR T-cell therapy is improved on combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors

To further investigate the mechanisms underlying tumor relapse, we examined brains from Ge518 tumor-bearing mice 7 days after injection of NTD or TNC-CAR T cells using multiplex immunofluorescence staining (figure 6A). In TNC-CAR T cell-treated mice, we observed significant infiltration of CD3+ CD8− (CD4+) and CD8+ human T cells within the tumor, in contrast to low or absent T cells in NTD T cell-treated mice (figure 6A and B). Over 75% of TNC-CAR T cells expressed at least one exhaustion marker, with 20–55% of them co-expressing PD-1, TIM-3, and lymphocyte activation gene-3 (LAG3) (figure 6A, C and D), indicative of an exhausted T-cell phenotype. These findings suggest that tumor-intrinsic factors drive early CAR T-cell exhaustion, potentially impairing their long-lasting antitumor efficacy. Importantly, TNC-CAR T cell-treated tumors exhibited widespread apoptosis, as indicated by cleaved caspase 3 (CC3) expression spatially overlapping with Ge518 tumor cells (figure 6E, online supplemental figure 7). In contrast, limited apoptosis was observed in tumors of NTD T cell-treated mice as well as in the surrounding brain tissues of TNC-CAR T cell-treated mice, suggesting that TNC-CAR T-cell activity was localized to the tumor site. Notably, TNC expression levels remained unchanged at this time point (figure 6F).

To investigate drivers of CAR T-cell dysfunction, murine CD11b+ cells were isolated from untreated Ge518 tumors at day 15 post-implantation for flow cytometry and bulk RNA-seq analysis, whereas human Ge518 cells were profiled by bulk RNA-seq (online supplemental figure 8A). Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis of the murine CD11b+ flow cytometry data showed presence of microglia and macrophages, including CD206+MHC-II− immunosuppressive subsets (online supplemental figure 8B). Bulk RNA-seq of murine CD11b+ myeloid cells revealed expression of immunosuppressive programs, including M2-like macrophages, checkpoint ligands and TAM, as well as resident microglia (online supplemental figure 8C). In the human tumor cell fraction, comparison of Ge518 tumors with the matched Ge518 cell line showed higher expression of genes associated with immune suppression and myeloid recruitment (eg, ENTPD1, CEACAM1, NECTIN2, SPP1, SERPINE1, TGB1, COL3A1) in tumors (online supplemental figure 8D). Together, these data suggest that an immunosuppressive microenvironment contributes to early CAR T-cell exhaustion in GBM.

In light of this, we evaluated whether combining TNC-CAR T cells with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) targeting TIM-3, PD-1, and LAG-3 could improve therapeutic efficacy. Ge518 tumor-bearing mice received intratumoral injections of either CD19-CAR or TNC-CAR T cells, followed by intracerebroventricular/intraperitoneal administration of the triple ICB combination or corresponding isotype controls (figure 6G). As shown in figure 6H, TNC-CAR T cells combined with ICB significantly extended survival compared with TNC-CAR T cells infused with control antibodies, whereas no survival benefit was observed when CD19-CAR T cells were used. Overall, these results pinpoint early T-cell exhaustion as a potential key contributor to tumor resistance or relapse in this model. We further confirmed that TNC-CAR T-cell antitumor activity was dependent on TNC expression by showing that treatment of the mice bearing TNC-negative Ge738 cells did not improve survival compared with treatment with NTD T cells (online supplemental figure 9A-D). As expected, neither TNC nor CC3 were expressed in tumors post-T-cell treatment (online supplemental figure 9E).

Preclinical safety of TNC-CAR T cells

Safety is a primary concern when considering CAR T-cell products for clinical application. To evaluate the potential clinical translation of TNC-CAR T cells, we assessed their toxicity and specificity. No significant body weight loss was observed following TNC-CAR T-cell treatment in the experiments shown above, suggesting a favorable safety profile (online supplemental figure 10A,B). To then assess specific recognition of ex vivo human GBM samples, we incubated NTD or TNC-CAR T cells with single-cell suspension of patient-derived GBM samples as well as of samples obtained from patients with arteriovenous malformation (AVM), epilepsy, as well as autopsy samples. As shown in figure 7A, 10 out of 13 GBM samples elicited robust activation of TNC-CAR T cells, whereas no activation of NTD cells was observed. Conversely, TNC-CAR T cells were not activated when co-cultured with any of the control samples (AVM, epilepsy, and autopsy). Finally, in another ex vivo setting using organotypic slices from freshly resected GBM samples, we showed that TNC-CAR T cells but not NTD or CD19-CAR T cells were activated when co-culture with organotypic slices from two different patients (figure 7B and C). Collectively, these findings suggest that TNC-CAR T cells are a safe and promising therapeutic candidate for GBM.

Discussion

This study is the first to demonstrate that the FNIII-D domain of TNC, an ECM molecule, can be effectively targeted by CAR T cells in the context of GBM. TNC-CAR T cells exhibited strong binding, activation, and proliferation when exposed to both purified TNC and conditioned medium from patient-derived TNC+ GBM cells. This resulted in selective and efficient killing of these cells as well as GBM neurospheres in vitro. In vivo, TNC-CAR T cells displayed significant antitumor activity, extending mouse survival, likely due to tumor cell apoptosis.

GBM exhibits profound interpatient and intrapatient antigen heterogeneity, posing challenges for the development of antigen-based therapies. In addition, selecting GBM-associated targets is particularly difficult due to the sensitivity of the brain to off-target activity.34 In previous work, Dutoit et al12 identified TNC as one of several tumor-associated peptides in GBM, highlighting it as a promising target. TNC, bearing 14 EGF-like domains and 8 universal FNIII domains, has the potential to generate numerous variants through alternative splicing, which can add up to 9 additional FNIII domains.14 We focused on targeting the alternatively spliced FNIII-D domain, a decision supported by RNA-seq analysis of GBM and LGG samples, along with TCGA SpliceSeq data. This analysis revealed minimal detection of the isoforms containing the FNIII-D domain in healthy tissues, while it was significantly represented in GBM. Immunofluorescence analysis across multiple human GBM samples further corroborated these findings, emphasizing the potential of TNC as a tumor-restricted antigen.17 In this regard, as the R6N scFv used in this study recognizes both the mouse and human TNC FNIII-D domain,18 we were able to evaluate the safety profile of TNC-CAR T cells in both species. Preclinical data revealed that intracranial delivery of TNC-CAR T cells did not lead to noticeable neurological symptoms, suggesting a favorable safety profile. Moreover, we show that TNC-CAR T cells are not activated when exposed to non-tumor human CNS samples. These results align with those of Nadal et al,18 who provided strong evidence for the expression of FNIII-D across several cancer-related tissues, with no detectable expression in healthy counterparts. Moreover, this study, along with others15 35 36 suggests that TNC targeting could benefit not only GBM but also other cancers, including breast, ovarian, skin, uterine, gastric, renal, and pancreatic cancers. Gan et al recently highlighted the critical role of TNC in the initiation of breast cancer metastasis to the brain, further supporting the therapeutic potential of TNC-CAR T cells across various tumor types.35

TNC has been targeted with antibodies, nanobodies, peptides, small RNAs, and aptamers, some of which have shown promising preclinical and clinical responses.15 17 However, CAR T cells may offer unique advantages over these approaches. CAR T cells can persist within the TME, continuously recognizing and eliminating antigen-expressing cells, potentially achieving durable responses without requiring repeated infusions. Additionally, as demonstrated here and in other studies,28 29 37 CAR T cells can induce a bystander effect, both by secreting cytokines and inflammatory molecules and, in the case of TNC-CAR T cells, by binding to TNC on the surface of adjacent, non-TNC-secreting cells, thereby broadening their cytotoxic reach beyond TNC-expressing cells. Importantly, as shown in this study and elsewhere,38 TNC is not expressed by healthy brain cells and off-tumor toxicity is thus not to be expected. Finally, CAR T cells can be engineered to further enhance their activity and persistence and can be combined with immune modulators, as shown here, or other approaches. Collectively, these features underscore the potential of CAR T cells as a powerful approach for TNC targeting in GBM.

A recent study by Wickman et al39 investigated CAR T cells directed against the onco-fetal FNIII-C TNC domain. In a diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma model, C-domain-targeted CAR T cells induced transient tumor regressions with a moderate survival extension—an outcome broadly comparable to what we observe with FNIII-D–targeted CAR T cells in GBM. Meaningful cross-study comparisons are limited by (1) the different tumor types (pediatric glioma vs adult GBM), (2) potential differences in epitope density and biological roles between FNIII domains, and (3) distinct scFvs and manufacturing processes. Nevertheless, the two studies strengthen the case for TNC as a tumor-restricted target while underscoring the need for optimization to achieve durable clinical benefit.

To date, few other CARs have been developed to target ECM components in solid tumors.4045 Consistent with the findings of the present study, Martín-Otal et al41 and Wagner et al40 developed CAR T cells targeting the fibronectin extra domain A (EDA) and extra domain B (EDB) splice variants, respectively. In addition to improving survival in mouse models, these studies showed that targeting fibronectin affected the tumor vasculature and induced substantial changes in the TME.41 These studies collectively underscore the potential of ECM molecules as effective targets for CAR T-cell therapy. We further demonstrate that targeting an ECM molecule can enhance CAR T-cell cytotoxicity via bystander activity when the target binds to neighboring antigen-negative cells. Although ECM-targeting CAR T-cell therapies are still in early stages of development, studies suggest that they may help to overcome biochemical and physical barriers imposed by the ECM in solid tumors.30 31 46 However, further research using immunocompetent models is necessary to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the potential of TNC targeting within the TME.

Treatment with TNC-CAR T cells successfully extended mouse survival in three xenograft models, although tumors eventually relapsed. In this regard, a strength of the current study is the use of several patient-derived GBM cell lines that likely more accurately represent the heterogeneity and genetic complexity of patient tumors compared with established cell lines such as U87MG or U251MG. Analysis of potential tumor escape mechanisms revealed minimal changes in expression of TNC isoforms following CAR T-cell therapy, including the FNIII-D domain. This finding suggests that antigen downregulation is unlikely to be a major contributor to relapse. Instead, the limited antitumor effect is likely driven by multiple factors, particularly the highly immunosuppressive GBM TME, as demonstrated in the present study, which hampers CAR T-cell activity and persistence.47 48 Multispectral immunofluorescence analysis, together with the in vivo ICB experiment suggests that early exhaustion of infiltrated T cells may limit treatment efficacy.

In summary, our findings provide evidence that targeting the TNC FNIII-D domain with CAR T cells holds promise for GBM therapy. As recent studies by Watson et al49 50 observed increased TNC expression following radiotherapy linked to fibrotic scar formation and tumor relapse, TNC-CAR T cells could synergize with radiotherapy to prevent relapse. While these results are encouraging, further optimization of CAR T-cell function, including strategies to mitigate T-cell exhaustion and overcome the challenges of the TME, will be crucial. Future research, including combination therapies or circuit-based CAR designs (eg, SynNotch, tandem CARs, bispecific engagers incorporating other tumor-associated antigens), will be key to refining this approach and exploring its potential for treating other solid tumors, paving the way for phase I clinical trials.
