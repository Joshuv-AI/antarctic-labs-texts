Landmark-guided deformable image registration for accurate cumulative dose evaluation in lung reirradiation
Authors: Marija Popovic, Marco di Francesco, Neil Kopek, Georges Al Makdessi, Monica Serban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Reirradiation, Lung, Dose accumulation, Dose mapping, Deformable image registration, Rigid image registration

Introduction

Managing recurrent, metastatic, and new primary lung cancers, may require lung reirradiation (reRT). Despite the recognized clinical utility of this approach, significant challenges remain due to the lack of standardized methods for dose reporting across multiple radiotherapy courses and for evaluating the biological effects of prior treatments [1], [2], [3], [4]. Effective and safe lung reRT planning is limited by insufficient data on cumulative organ-of-interest (OOI) dose guidance, their recovery over time, and guidance on how to account for the time interval between treatments [5], [6]. While emerging consensus guidelines aim to standardize reRT practice [1], [7], significant variability remains in cumulative dose calculation and reporting, complicating efforts to correlate dose with patient outcomes and OOI tolerances. Three-dimensional mapping of dose across radiotherapy courses onto a single reference image enables the assessment of cumulative OOI dose. This is valuable for biological evaluation and individual risk modeling; however, it depends critically on the accuracy of the image registration [8].

In current clinical practice, lung reRT most commonly relies on rigid image registration (RIR) due to its simplicity, speed, and robustness. According to a recent survey by Willmann et al., 71% of clinicians reported using RIR for lung reRT [9]. Deformable image registration (DIR) can improve registration accuracy when accounting for anatomical changes such as those caused by respiratory motion, tumor response or progression, and surgical interventions [10], [11]. Despite its advantages, clinical adoption of DIR in reRT remains limited. This is primarily due to non-standardized commissioning practices and evolving quality assurance tools [12], [13], [14], [15], which undermine clinical confidence in DIR’s reliability and accuracy for treatment planning decisions.

This study aimed to develop and evaluate a robust lung reRT dose accumulation workflow using DIR tools within a commercial treatment planning system. The approach was built upon the image registration quality assurance recommendations of the American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM) Task Group 132 (TG-132) [16]. While TG-132 did not address DIR-based dose accumulation, its quality assurance principles provided a foundation for evaluating registration accuracy in our reRT workflow. Using digital phantom data and clinical lung reRT cases, we assessed the impact of commercially available DIR and RIR methods on cumulative dose assessment.

Materials and methods

Computational tools and DIR evaluation framework

The DIR was performed within the Velocity imaging informatics platform (version 4.1, Varian Medical Systems, 2022), specifically using the intensity-based algorithm with a B-spline transformation model and mutual information as the similarity metric [17]. Two DIR approaches were evaluated. The first, Deformable Multipass (DMP), applied three iterations of intensity-based deformation following initial rigid auto-alignment. The second, landmark point guided deformation (LPGD), utilized manually defined landmark points to guide the deformable registration following initial rigid alignment.

The regions of interest (ROI) for the deformation were systematically varied (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Three ROIs (ROI1-3) were defined based on the full scan and lung volume, and three additional ROIs (ROI4-6) were defined using Boolean unions of the 10% isodose lines from the previous and reRT treatments. The 10% isodose threshold was selected based on the International Commission of Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU) definition of irradiated volume, which considers this region to receive a dose that is clinically relevant for normal tissue effects [18].

ROI6 adds a 5-slice (12.5 mm) buffer beyond the combined 10% isodose boundary with axial lung conformity. ROI definitions include overlapping lung regions to ensure complete anatomical coverage of the irradiated volume.

The performance of DIR was evaluated using qualitative visualization (checkerboard, blended views, and difference maps) by two board-certified medical physicists with over 10 years of clinical experience, and using quantitative metrics recommended by TG-132 [16], with target tolerances provided in brackets (Supplementary Table S1): target registration error (TRE, 2–3 mm), Dice similarity coefficient (DSC, 0.80–0.90), mean distance to agreement (MDA, 2–3 mm), Jacobian determinant (JD, no negative values), and consistency metric (2–3 mm).

DIR validation on digital phantom

The accuracy of DIR was evaluated on a digital phantom (4DCT 6 from DIR-Lab, University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center), a four-dimensional (4D) CT dataset with 300 landmark points distributed throughout the lungs [19]. End-inhale and end-exhale phases were registered. The coordinates of the landmark points were converted into a Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) structure set using MIM (version 7.1.6, Cleveland, OH). The 300 landmarks supplied with the phantom were used exclusively for quantitative evaluation of registration accuracy. Additional landmark points were required to guide LPGD. These guiding landmarks were placed on clearly identifiable anatomical features, typically bronchiole bifurcations, within 2 cm of the peripheral lung, and preferentially along the posterior border where respiratory motion is greatest. The number of guiding landmarks was increased from 4 to 10 in steps of 2 to assess the effect on deformation accuracy. The landmark placement method is illustrated in the Supplementary Fig. S1.

DIR validation on clinical datasets

This retrospective study was reviewed by the institutional Research Ethics Board (MUHC REB study 2022-8245) and included 21 clinical lung reRT cases anonymized prior to analysis. Cases were selected where the first treatment targeted the central lung, and reRT irradiated lung volume, either ipsilateral or contralateral, that involved overlapping OOIs (Fig. 2). Both DMP and LPGD were evaluated across ROI1-6. For ROI4-6 derived from the 10% isodose volumes, three configurations were evaluated: ROI4 matched the cranio-caudal extent of the 10% isodose, allowing axial extension beyond the body contour; ROI5 used the same cranio-caudal extent but was laterally constrained to the lung volume; and ROI6 added a 12.5 mm cranio-caudal buffer beyond the 10% isodose and was also laterally constrained to the lung. When the 10% isodose extended beyond the lung, the buffer was applied only within the lung. This 12.5 mm buffer extended ROI6 to the 1–2% isodose region. For LPGD, up to 10 landmark pairs were manually identified per case by a single observer (Supplementary Fig. S1) following the same anatomical criteria as in the phantom study. The complete workflow is presented in the Supplementary Fig. S2. Forty additional landmarks within the combined 10% isodose volume were used for DIR accuracy assessment with TRE metrics in 18 of 21 clinical cases. In the remaining three cases, severe anatomical changes between scans prevented landmark identification and, thus, TRE metrics could not be calculated. Inter-observer landmark variability, an indicator of robustness of the homologous landmark identification process, was assessed in five clinical cases by a radiation oncologist and a medical physicist.

Impact of image registration strategy on dose volume metrics

To evaluate the potential impact of image registration on clinical decision-making, DIR-assisted cumulative dose distributions were compared with current institutional clinical standard RIR-based summation in 21 clinical cases (Fig. S3). For 18 cases, LPGD within ROI6 was used as the preferred method, while DMP was applied in three cases where landmark identification was not feasible.

Doses from the first and reRT courses were converted voxel-wise to equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQD2) using the linear-quadratic model with an α/β ratio of 3 Gy for OOIs. The dose from the first course was mapped to the reRT planning CT image and then summed with the reRT dose to provide volumetric cumulative equieffective dose.

Clinical OOIs were contoured by a radiation oncologist when deemed at risk of exceeding clinical tolerance with reRT. Cumulative OOI dose metrics derived using DIR were compared with the institutional standard, which uses EQD2 summation from RIR focused on the clinical target and adjacent OOIs (Supplementary Fig. S3). They were also evaluated against published consensus-based dose guidance (Table 3.5 from [1]).

Results

For the digital phantom, DMP accuracy improved as the ROI was reduced from the full scan volume to the lung-focused ROI. However, the mean TRE values for ROI1, ROI2 and ROI3 remained beyond the TG-132 tolerance, being 5.6 ± 0.3 mm, 4.7 ± 0.2 mm, 3.6 ± 0.2 mm, respectively. Thirty-nine of 300 landmark points exceeded the 5 mm dimensional cut-off (largest was 18.3 mm in Δz), with approximately 90% of failures occurring near the lung-chest wall interface. Qualitative assessment of the deformation vectors indicated most significant deformation within 1 cm from the pleura, with this effect most pronounced near the anterior and posterior aspects of the lung (Fig. 3).

In 21 clinical cases, DMP and LPGD were applied with deformations focused on ROI5 and ROI6. Inter-observer variability in landmark identification was assessed in five clinical cases, in which landmarks were identified independently by a radiation oncologist and a medical physicist, with observed differences of the order of 1–2 mm.

The 40 landmarks used for DIR accuracy assessment could only be identified in 18 cases, where the mean TRE for LPGD within ROI6 met TG-132 tolerances (Supplementary Fig. S4). The maximum dimensional difference in TRE was also reduced with LPGD over ROI6, with most landmarks satisfying the 5 mm tolerance in all directions (Supplementary Table S2).

Agreement between clinically relevant OOI contours was acceptable for the heart, great vessels, lungs, and spinal cord, with DSC values meeting TG-132 tolerances (Supplementary Table S3). The carina exhibited a DSC marginally below the tolerance of 0.8 in three cases, with a minimum DSC of 0.76. The esophagus exhibited low DSC values and failed to meet TG-132 tolerance in 15 of 18 cases. Variability in the contoured extent of the esophagus was observed across treatment courses. The MDA met TG-132 criteria for the carina, heart, lungs, and spinal cord. The great vessels and esophagus exceeded MDA tolerance in one case each.

Cumulative dose distributions generated using DIR were compared with RIR-based summations in all clinical cases, and neither DIR- nor RIR-based dose summation method consistently yielded higher or lower dose-volume histogram (DVH) metrics across all OOIs (Table 1). In the most extreme case maximum doses to the heart and carina reached 80 Gy and 60 Gy, respectively. At the cohort level, lung volume dose metrics exhibited statistically significant differences between approaches. On average, DIR-assisted summation resulted in a critical volume receiving 12.5 Gy (CV12.5 Gy) that was larger by 100 cm3 (p = 0.01), a lower mean dose (by 0.6 Gy, p = 0.001), and a minimally lower V20 Gy (0.5% difference, p < 0.001).

Discussion

Lung reRT cases require an individualized approach when assessing cumulative OOI dose metrics. This study showed that reliance on RIR alone may misrepresent cumulative dose in some clinically relevant structures, which could lead to compromised treatment decisions such as reduced prescription dose or compromised target coverage at reRT. The workflow proposed in this study offers a clinically feasible approach for DIR-based dose summation, with incorporated TG-132-based validation and landmark guidance to substantiate the accuracy of the registration. As a result, cumulative dose estimates better reflect the true anatomical deformation over time between two radiotherapy courses, supporting more informed treatment decisions in lung reRT.

In this study, DIR-assisted cumulative dose distributions demonstrated notable differences compared to RIR-assisted summation. In nine cases (50% of those with verifiable TRE), at least one organ exhibited a dose difference ≥10 Gy between DIR- and RIR-assisted summation. In two cases, DIR-assisted summation reduced heart dose to within published tolerance levels, whereas RIR-assisted dose exceeded these levels. DIR-assisted summation also resulted in statistically significant reductions in lung dose metrics, CV12.5 Gy, mean dose, and V20 Gy. These patient-specific differences are consistent with findings reported by Nix et al. [28] for pelvic reRT using the Anaconda DIR algorithm and with observations of Senthi et al. [10] in lung reRT. The latter study used a modified B-spline algorithm in Velocity (version 2.8, Varian Medical Systems, 2013) and reported improved spatial registration accuracy compared to RIR, particularly in patients with large anatomical changes. The reported spatial differences between DIR- and RIR-assisted transfer of dose clouds mirror cumulative dose variations reported in the present study. Although cumulative dose differences were not quantified in their study, the reported spatial discrepancies suggest the potential for clinically meaningful dose variation.

In the digital phantom DIR validation, both DMP and LPGD showed improved performance within the focused ROI3. The deformation pattern observed with DMP near the lung-chest wall interface reflects its inability to adequately capture the independent motion of the lung relative to the chest wall (lung sliding). This limitation arose from the algorithm’s design, which enforces smooth, continuous deformations across the image volume, a constraint that prevents the algorithm from accurately modeling the discontinuous motion that occurred at tissue interfaces such as the pleural surface. In contrast, LPGD with guiding points placed near the chest wall effectively addressed this challenge by allowing the deformation to be constrained at the lung boundary, thereby better capturing the independent motion of the lung and chest wall.

On the digital phantom, consistency metrics demonstrated stable deformation in both forward and reverse directions. Qualitative assessment identified a small subregion in the lower posterior right lung with localized differences in the reverse deformation; however, this had minimal impact on overall registration quality. Should similar discrepancies arise within clinically relevant regions, additional local landmarks could be introduced to better constrain the deformation. The 10-landmark LPGD configuration demonstrated progressively improved accuracy compared to configurations using fewer landmarks. Phantom experiments showed that the 10-landmark LPGD configuration consistently met TG-132 tolerances, leading to its selection as the baseline for subsequent clinical case analyses.

Registration performance in clinical cases improved as the ROI was reduced to the region encompassed by the 10% isodose lines. Additional improvement was achieved by adding a superior/inferior buffer to the bounding box (ROI6), allowing inclusion of anatomical features that may have shifted between scans. The 10-point LPGD approach within ROI6 represented a clinically feasible, patient-specific strategy for lung reRT when anatomical distortions do not preclude landmark identification. Although manual landmark initialization was time-consuming, landmark placement was generally straightforward and reproducible, as points were typically selected at distinct bronchiole bifurcations in the low-dose lung periphery. The observed trend of improved accuracy with increasing landmarks showed that the benefit of added points plateaued after roughly ten well-distributed pairs, once TG-132 tolerances were met. In practice, if visual inspection reveals a localized misregistration, additional landmarks may be selectively placed in that region to improve alignment.

The proposed method prioritizes strategic landmark placement rather than exhaustive sampling, while allowing for targeted additional landmarks near critical structures adjacent to steep dose gradients. This pragmatic approach is consistent with recent guidance emphasizing the need to balance geometric accuracy with practical considerations [21], [22]. Beyond the threshold of TG-132 tolerance level, other uncertainties, such as contouring variability and dose calculation errors, are likely to dominate. Finally, in cases with significant anatomical distortion where reliable landmarks cannot be identified, DMP- or RIR-assisted deformations may serve as less optimal but practical alternatives [20].

The findings of this study support earlier reports [16], [20] demonstrating that DIR accuracy can be improved by tailoring the focus region of registration to the clinically relevant region. In current clinical practice, this is typically achieved by focusing RIR on the target and nearby OOIs. In this study, using the irradiated region defined by the 10% isodoses provided accurate registrations, with quality metrics well within TG-132 tolerances. This approach may be suitable in most cases, though patient-specific considerations may justify extending the ROI beyond this volume to include additional clinically relevant tissues.

This study focuses on landmark-based DIR approaches, and the decision not to pursue structure-guided DIR was based on two considerations: manually contoured OOIs that are easily identifiable were unlikely to improve the performance of this intensity-based algorithm, while OOIs that are not easily identifiable could be susceptible to contouring variability—an issue well-documented for thoracic OOIs and potentially amplified in the reRT setting [23], [24]. The approach developed in this study can be transferred to other DIR algorithms or platforms but should be validated before clinical use. Once established, the workflow is straightforward and reliable. We note that intensity-based algorithms perform less well in sites with low soft-tissue contrast or large anatomical changes (e.g., abdomen, pelvis, bowel), where alternatives such as biomechanical model–based DIR may be preferred.

A limitation of the proposed approach is its reliance on manually identified landmarks; their identification introduces variability dependent on image quality, anatomical conspicuity, and user experience. Inter-observer differences between a radiation oncologist and a medical physicist were 1–2 mm across five lung cases, consistent with uncertainties reported for established lung landmark validation datasets POPI [25] and DIR-Lab [26]. While these differences fell within TG-132 tolerances, manual landmark placement remains inherently subjective and susceptible to variability.

A further limitation concerns the relationship between geometric accuracy and reliable dose accumulation. While TG-132 provides quantitative criteria for registration accuracy, it does not address how geometric uncertainty propagates to dose. Consequently, low TRE does not guarantee dosimetric accuracy, particularly in regions with steep dose gradients. Small geometric shifts in high-gradient areas, such as those encountered in stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) where gradients may reach 12%/mm [27], can lead to substantial local dose errors, whereas similar errors in low-gradient regions have minimal impact. Future work could include a focused sensitivity analysis in selected high-gradient cases to quantify the impact of registration uncertainty on calculated dose-volume metrics by perturbing landmarks or deformation fields within observed TRE ranges. Dose mapping uncertainties, as summarized by Murr et al. [8], may influence outcomes, particularly when cumulative dose is used to re-optimize treatment plan in iterative planning approaches.

In summary, this study presents a validated, clinically feasible workflow for DIR-assisted dose accumulation in thoracic reRT, addressing a recognized gap in standardized cumulative dose reporting across radiotherapy courses. By demonstrating that landmark-guided DIR within targeted isodose-defined regions meaningfully improves cumulative dose estimates compared to RIR, this work supports more informed, individualized reRT planning. As consensus guidelines for reRT continue to evolve, validated DIR workflows of this kind are essential to enable accurate dose-outcome correlations and ultimately improve the evidence base for safe and effective lung reRT.
