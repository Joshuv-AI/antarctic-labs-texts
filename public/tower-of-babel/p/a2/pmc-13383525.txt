The role of health literacy in medication adherence among people living with multiple sclerosis: a cross-sectional study in Isfahan, Iran
Authors: Mohammad Mehdi Kanani, Shahla Abolhassani, Fatemeh Nazari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultiple Sclerosis (MS) is a chronic demyelinating disease of the central nervous system with a growing prevalence. While various disease-modifying treatments (DMTs) are available, adherence to DMTs has remained a challenge in the management of MS. Among the previously known factors associated with adherence to DMTs, health literacy as an essential factor in health-related behaviors and a contributor to treatment adherence in other chronic diseases, has not been studied. This study aims to determine the role of health literacy in medication adherence among people living with MS.MethodsThe current study is a multicenter cross-sectional study conducted in two centers affiliated with the Isfahan University of Medical Sciences. A total of 317 participants were recruited using random systematic sampling. The participants were assessed for eligibility via the phone call. Data was then collected via a form for demographic and disease-related information, a revised version of the Multiple Sclerosis Treatment Adherence Questionnaire (rMS-TAQ), and the Health Literacy Questionnaire for Multiple Sclerosis patients (MSHLQ). Statistical analysis included descriptive statistics, Spearman’s correlation, and stepwise linear regression. Data analysis was carried out by IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.Results76.3% of participants were adherent (doses missed = 0). The health literacy score in MSHLQ was moderately associated with medication adherence score in rMS-TAQ (Spearman’s rho = 0.553, p < 0.001). A final regression model was found significant (F(3, 316) = 43.877, p < 0.001), which predicted adherence score in rMS-TAQ based on total health literacy score, the score in the ‘knowledge of caring for the disease’ subscale of MSHLQ, and income.ConclusionHealth literacy is associated with better adherence to DMTs. Disease knowledge may play a mediating role in the relationship between health literacy and medication adherence in people living with MS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04985-4.

Background

Multiple Sclerosis (MS) is a chronic demyelinating disease of the central nervous system that constitutes a growing burden of non-traumatic neurologic disabilities in younger people [1]. Aside from physical disability, people with MS may experience mental disorders, including depressive symptoms and anxiety, and cognitive impairments, including information processing speed, attention, learning, and memory that substantially add to the burden of MS, especially given the young age of onset of the disease [2]. Evidence suggest that MS may have deeper neuropsychiatric burden as it is shown to affect cortical structures in the frontal and temporal lobes, and lingual and parahippocampal gyri, consistent with a range of the sensory and cognitive symptoms [3]. It has been estimated that there are 2.8 million people diagnosed with MS as of 2020, and the prevalence of MS has increased consistently since 2013 in every world region. With a prevalence of 90 cases per 100,000 people, Iran has more cases of MS than any other nation in the Eastern Mediterranean region [4]. Isfahan, located at the center of Iran, is thought to be the most MS-prevalent province in Iran with 183.96 cases per 100,000 people in 2021 [5].

Despite the advances in immunotherapies, there is still no curative treatment for MS; however, there are various immunomodulating agents known as disease-modifying treatments (DMTs) available for all phenotypes of MS that effectively prevent symptom relapses and slow down the disease progression [6]. Nonetheless, non-adherence to DMTs has remained a significant challenge in the management of MS. According to a meta-analysis, the range of twelve-month binary non-adherence was 10.8–47.0% for oral DMTs and 22.6–53.0% for injectable DMTs [7]. Observations indicate better adherence to intravenous DMTs, though discontinuation risks persist [8]. It is a substantial challenge in caring for people with MS as non-adherence is associated with poorer clinical outcomes, higher relapse rate, higher disability scores, and higher disease-related costs, and lower cognitive function [9–11]. This issue necessitates the study of factors that potentially contribute to treatment adherence.

Health literacy is a known factor in health decision-making and health-related behaviors that has gathered growing attention in the health literature [12, 13]. It can be defined as ‘the ability of an individual to obtain and translate knowledge and information in order to maintain and improve health in a way that is appropriate to the individual and system contexts.’ [14] Dehghani has analyzed concept of health literacy in MS in the literature, and concluded that there are four attributes of the health literacy in the context of MS – health information evaluation, understand disease and its related issues, reading skills, and capacity to use knowledge. In this model, health literacy is a dynamic concept that improves self-care, promotes well-being and health, and decreases health service utilization [15].

Since its introduction in 1990, health literacy has evolved from an individual set of skills applied in health behaviors to a social determinant recognized as a public concern [16]. Given the importance of health literacy in health-related behaviors, it would be reasonable to consider it as a potential contributing factor in treatment compliance.

The role of health literacy in medication adherence has been studied in several chronic diseases. Higher health literacy is associated with better medication adherence among people living with type II diabetes [17–19], hypertension [20, 21], heart failure [22], chronic kidney disease [23], hepatic cirrhosis [24], and stroke [25]. While most studies have found a positive correlation, others have reported either a negative relationship [26, 27], or no significant relationship at all [28]. A systematic review revealed only half of the included recent studies found a significant positive relationship. Therefore, the current body of evidence is not conclusive [29].

However, the relationship between health literacy and adherence to DMTs among people living with MS has remained unclear. We found only one study to examine this relationship in people with MS which could not establish a conclusion due to lack of variability in the data [30]. It indicates a gap in the literature regarding the role of health literacy in medication adherence in general and in the field of MS management in particular. Furthermore, no study to our knowledge has investigated this relationship in the Iranian context, where cultural and healthcare system factors may shape health literacy and adherence behaviors differently than in other settings. Therefore, this study aims to address this dual gap by examining the role of health literacy in medication adherence among Iranian adults living with MS, thereby offering novel insights for both MS care and cross-cultural health literacy research.

Objectives

The current study aims to determine whether there is a relationship between health literacy and medication adherence among people living with MS. We hypothesize that higher health literacy is significantly associated with better adherence to medication adherence.

Methods

Study design

This study employs a descriptive, cross-sectional design to examine the link between health literacy as the independent variable and adherence to DMTs as the dependent variable. The data collection was conducted between May 2024 and November 2025. The data analysis was finalized by the end of November 2025.

Study settings

The study was conducted in two selected hospitals affiliated with Isfahan University of Medical Sciences in Isfahan, Iran. They are located in the Isfahan main city. Both centers have outpatient ‘MS clinic’ wards. People living with MS are often referred to these centers for follow-up, medication prescription, and medication administration. The data collection was conducted between May 2024 and November 2025. The data analysis was finalized by the end of November 2025.

Therefore, 317 participants are the minimum sample size required. We did not consider an attrition rate as the study design is not longitudinal.

Recruitment

After the study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, the research team received a letter of introduction from the Vice Chancellery for Research. This letter presented the primary investigator, on behalf of the team, to the Vice Chancellery for Therapeutic Affairs. This department is responsible for the registry of people diagnosed with MS in Isfahan. We then presented the purpose of the study and research procedure to request access to the contact information of people diagnosed with MS. Following approval from the affiliated security department, we were granted limited access to contact information. A list of all patients was subsequently retrieved with no specific order (e.g., not alphabetical or based on the year of diagnosis).

We assigned each patient a unique numerical code. starting from 1, in an Excel file. The list contained a total of 11,895 registered patients diagnosed with MS according to a neurologist report. Recruitment began in May 2024 using the random systematic sampling. Based on the assigned numerical codes, we contacted patients using a calculated sampling interval (k) of 37, which was derived by dividing the total number of registered patients by the required sample size.

The contact was a phone call made to interview the registered patients and assess them for eligibility. Willing, eligible patients were invited to an in-person meeting in the selected centers for obtaining written consent and data collection. For patients unable or unwilling to participate in an in-person meeting, the same procedure was conducted online.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria for participants were: (1) MS diagnosis according to McDonald’s criteria, (2) ability to give and giving informed consent to participate, (3) a minimum age of 18, (4) ability to read and write, (5) self-reported physical and mental health to fill out the questionnaires, (6) having no history of other chronic diseases, (7) an expanded disability status scale (EDSS) score less than 8.5, (8) taking the prescribed DMT for at least six months, and (9) absence of relapse for at least one month. Participants unwilling to participate or continue to participate were excluded from the study. Participants with incomplete responses, with even one item skipped, would also be excluded.

Outcomes and materials

Adherence to DMTs, which is often synonymous with treatment adherence in the context of MS, is the outcome of interest and the dependent variable in this study. Use of the term ‘adherence’ in the current research complies with World Health Organization definition which describes adherence as “the extent to which a person’s behaviour. corresponds with agreed recommendations from a health care provider.” [32] The health literacy is the hypothesized predictor and the independent variable of the study. Both variables are the primary outcomes that researchers intended to quantify in the study.

Demographic and clinical characteristics served as the secondary, background variables of the study. These included age, gender, education, marital status, employment, income, insurance coverage, disease pattern (i.e., phenotype or clinical course), disease duration, and EDSS.

Instruments and data collection

The demographic and clinical data of participants were collected using a form developed by the researchers. Participants were asked to select their level of income adequacy from three options: (1) ‘Not adequate at all,’ (2) ‘Relatively adequate,’ or (3) ‘Totally adequate.’ Therefore, income was evaluated qualitatively. The qualitative assessment was preferred due to concerns that some participants might have privacy issues when reporting their true income, which could introduce potential bias. The EDSS score of participants was either obtained from patients’ records or from physical examination and history taking. The EDSS is a useful measure of disability in people with MS first introduced by Kurtzke as an extension of and complement to Functional Systems Score [33].

Medication adherence was measured by an enhanced version of the Multiple Sclerosis Treatment Adherence Questionnaire (rMS-TAQ). Initially developed in 2011 by Wicks et al. using an online community [31], MS-TAQ quantifies adherence to DMTs in three subscales – a 13-item ‘Barriers’ with each item representing a barrier to adherence that is rated “not important at all (0)” to “extremely important” (3) in missing a dose, a 10-item ‘Side Effects’ that with each item corresponding with a known DMT side effect is rated in a Likert scale from “never” (0) to “all or nearly all of the time” (4), and finally a 7-item ‘Coping Strategies’ with each item describing a method to cope with DMTs side effects using a yes (1) or No (0) rating. All three subscales have a 28-day timeframe.

To validate the construct of MS-TAQ, the developers used the missed dose ratio (MDR) to see if each subscale is correlated with the MDR. The findings revealed that the ‘Barriers’ subscale demonstrated a significant positive correlation with the MDR, whereas the ‘Coping Strategies’ subscale showed a significant negative correlation. The ‘Side Effects’ subscale was not significantly correlated with MDR. The reliability analysis also showed mixed findings – although the internal consistency of both ‘Barriers’ and ‘Side Effects’ subscales was acceptable, a low Cronbach’s alpha for the ‘Coping Strategies’ subscale was found [31]. Some translations of MS-TAQ have shown better psychometric performance [34].

The introduction of new DMTs with dosing intervals more than the original 28-day timeframe and new side effects makes it necessary to enhance the original version of MS-TAQ for new applications. Therefore, we re-translated the MS-TAQ to Persian and used a similar researcher-built questionnaire [35] and an updated literature review to enhance the original version. Then, the face validity was evaluated for clarity of items both qualitatively and quantitatively by interviewing ten patients diagnosed with MS. After minor changes in text style, the content validity was then assessed by a panel of twenty faculty members of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences.

The final scale, renamed as revised the Multiple Sclerosis Treatment Adherence Questionnaire (rMS-TAQ), maintains the same subscales with significant modifications. The original 28-day timeframe is replaced with the term ‘recently,’ which can be adopted for all types of DMTs regardless of dosing intervals. The number of items in each subscale changes with some original or newly added items removed. The scoring changed to a Likert scoring for all items, ranging from 0 to 4. The 18 items of the ‘Barriers’ subscale and the 15 items of the ‘Side Effects’ are negatively rated from 4 to 0 while the 7 items ‘Coping Strategies’ subscale is rated from 0 to 4. The new scoring allows a ‘whole scale’ scoring summing all 40 items. It could not be used in the original version.

Preliminary reliability of the instrument was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha by distributing rMS-TAQ to ten patients. The Cronbach’s alphas for ‘Barriers,’ ‘Side Effects,’ ‘Coping Strategies,’ and the whole scale were 0.73, 0.87, 0.92, and 0.89, respectively. An early report1 of psychometric properties of rMS-TAQ has been previously presented in 11th International Annual Congress on Research and Technology of Iranian Medical Sciences Students [36].

However, we used the same definition of binary adherence in the original MS-TAQ, with non-adherence to oral or injectable DMTs defined as MDR ≠ 0. We also adopted the definition of non-adherence to intravenous DMTs suggested by Alonso et al., which considers a dose missed if the patient has delayed DMT administration more than 15 days [37].

Health literacy was measured by the Health Literacy Questionnaire for Multiple Sclerosis patients (MSHLQ). It is a 22-item health literacy scale developed specifically for people with MS. MSHLQ measures health literacy of people with MS in four subscales, including ‘Appraisal of health Information, ‘Ability to search health information,’ ‘Knowledge of caring for the disease,’ and ‘Successful functioning in health conditions.’ The scale is developed by Dehghani and Keshavarzi in 2017 by reviewing the literature, existing questionnaires on health literacy, and interviewing MS patients in the field. The construct validity of this questionnaire was assessed using exploratory factor analysis and confirmed by known-groups validity. It was also found reliable with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.94 [38].

Study procedure

The diagram shown in Fig. 1 summarizes the study procedure. During recruitment process, a phone call was made to interview each registered patient. During the phone calls, the primary investigator introduced the research team and explained a summary of the study procedure and objectives. If the contacted person were willing, they would be assessed for eligibility criteria. Willing, eligible patients were invited to an in-person meeting in which a written informed consent was obtained and the data were collected. The meetings were planned to take place in either of the two selected centers. For patients unwilling to participate in an in-person meeting, an online version of the consent form and data collection instruments was sent.

STROBE flow diagram of the study procedure

To collect data, the participants were asked to complete a printed copy of the demographic and disease information form, the MSHLQ, and the rMS-TAQ. Participants were informed that their responses will be de-identified and they will be free to refuse to participate without expecting any change in their access to care services. They were also free to contact the primary investigator in case that a question was raised.

The collected data were then de-identified and entered into IBM SPSS Statistics version 24 for data analysis. The authors have followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement guideline to write the current article (Additional file) [39].

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to describe demographic and clinical data. Spearman’s correlation was used to test the bivariate correlation between primary outcomes of the study. Stepwise linear regression was used to test the research hypothesis. As the current literature is inconclusive about factor associated with medication adherence and data from the Iranian context are particularly limited, a theory-driven approach to variable selection was not possible.

There was no missing data in the final analysis since the participants were rigorously screened to exclude incomplete responses. During data collection, if a participant – whether enrolled in online or in-person data collection – failed to complete all questionnaire items, they were asked to do so. Participants were excluded from the final analysis if they were unwilling to complete the questionnaire at the data collection session or, in the case of the online data collection, were unavailable for follow-up via telephone or text message. Therefore, there was no item-level missing data in the final data set. All data analyses and descriptive statistics were carried out by IBM SPSS Statistics version 24. All analyses were two-tailed, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 317 participants returned the questionnaires and were included in the final analysis, making the response rate of the survey 56.9%. The demographic data are detailed in Table 1. The mean age of participants was 40.39 ± 8.77, ranging from 19 to 78. The majority of participants were female (76.0%), indicating a 3:1 ratio. Participants were mostly married (74.1%), with a diploma degree (33.1%), covered by insurance (98.4%), and a relatively adequate income (51.7%). Homemakers made up 48.6% of the employment status.

Binary adherence and adherence scores in rMS-TAQ

Of 317 participants, 242 (76.3%) participants were adherent, meaning they did not have a single missed dose in the past 28 days for oral and injectable DMTs or did not delay medication administration for more than 15 days for intravenous DMTs. It makes the non-adherence rate 23.7%.

The mean scores of the subscales ‘Barriers,’ ‘Side Effects,’ and ‘Coping Strategies’ were 41.32 ± 15.75, 48.99 ± 9.38, and 7.65 ± 4.53, respectively. The mean of rMS-TAQ total score was 97.98 ± 19.54. The rMS-TAQ scores are normally distributed according to Kolmogorov-Smirnov test results (D(317) = 0.43, p = 0.200).

Health literacy

The mean of MSHLQ total score was 76.43 ± 13.68 (median [IQR], 78.00 [20.00]). The mean score for subscales ‘Appraisal of health Information,’ ‘Ability to search health information,’ ‘Knowledge of caring for the disease,’ and ‘Successful functioning in health conditions’ was 16.60 ± 3.75 (17.00 [5.00]), 19.37 ± 4.60 (20.00 [7.00]), 23.41 ± 5.20 (24.00 [7.00]), and 17.03 ± 4.08 (18.00 [6.00]), respectively. The scores of none of the subscales were normally distributed.

Correlation analysis

As the health literacy scores were not normally distributed, we used Spearman’s correlation test for bivariate correlation analysis (Table 4). The health literacy scores in each subscale of MSHLQ were moderately associated with the adherence score in rMS-TAQ, except for the subscale ‘appraisal of health Information’ that showed a weak yet significant correlation (Spearman’s rho = 0.325, p < 0.001). The strongest correlation was between the adherence score and the total score of MSHLQ (Spearman’s rho = 0.553, p < 0.001).

To control for background variables that could potentially have confounding effects in the relationship between primary outcomes, stepwise linear regression was used. Apart from each subscale of MSHLQ and its total scores, the entry variables included gender, education (transformed to a dummy variable where ‘higher education’ = 1 and ‘diploma or non-graduate’ = 0), income (‘adequate or relatively adequate’ = 1, ‘inadequate’ = 0), insurance coverage, and EDSS score. Based on the Spearman’s correlation results, we excluded age (Spearman’s rho = 0.002, p = 0.968) and disease duration (Spearman’s rho = − 0.004, p = 0.946).

Before proceeding to interpret the regression analysis, the functional form of the relationship between rMS-TAQ scores and MSHLQ scores was assessed through visual inspection of the scatterplot. We assumed the linearity of the relationship was justified. The visual inspection of the P-P plot revealed that the residuals were normally distributed. The casewise diagnostics revealed no significant outliers with standardized residuals greater than 3 in absolute value. The collinearity diagnostics and statistics showed no evidence of multicollinearity.

The final regression model, summarized in Table 5, significantly predicted total adherence score in rMS-TAQ based on total health literacy score in MSHLQ, the score in the ‘knowledge of caring for the disease’ subscale of MSHLQ, and income (F(3, 316) = 43.877, p < 0.001). The model explains 29.6% of the variance in the adherence score.

Discussion

The main objective of the study was to determine whether health literacy is associated with medication adherence among people with MS. The association between health literacy and medication adherence was significant, which confirms our expectation in the study hypothesis. Each subscale of health literacy in the current study was also significantly correlated with medication adherence. However, the correlations were moderate except for one domain of health literacy that was weakly associated with adherence. This domain of health literacy corresponds with the patients’ ability to evaluate health information [38].

The findings are overall consistent with prior studies that found a positive relationship between health literacy and medication adherence among people living with other chronic diseases. The similar weak-to-moderate correlation was found in most of the previous studies [17, 19, 20, 23, 40].

The regression model also supported the study hypothesis. However, the percentage of explained variance by the model (R2) is relatively low. The value of R2 in the current study is lower than some of the similar studies with different variables as predictors of adherence [17, 23, 41], while higher compared to some other studies [40].

We speculate that the part of the total variance not explained by the model may be related to the Dunning-Kruger effect. Initially described in 1999, the Dunning-Kruger effect is a cognitive bias that posits people with limited competence in a particular domain tend to overestimate their abilities due to a dual burden: they lack the metacognitive skills to accurately assess their performance while simultaneously making flawed judgments [42].

In the context of health literacy, Canady and Larzo have confirmed the impact of the Dunning-Kruger effect. In their study, the participants with lower health literacy reported equal or greater confidence in health knowledge than individuals with higher health literacy while exhibiting more problematic engagement in health behaviors [43]. This potential cognitive bias may have two implications for the findings of the current study. First, people with limited health literacy may have reported greater adherence than their actual compliance. The overestimation of adherence in self-reported methods compared to direct methods is not uncommon [43]. Second, people may have reported their health literacy greater than the actual set of health-related skills and competencies they have. In other words, participants may either overestimate their health literacy skills, including knowledge of caring for the disease, or overestimate their adherence to treatment. Consequently, some participants with high self-rated literacy skills may have poor adherence, while others with limited skills may report high self-rated adherence. Both possibilities would contribute to high variance in medication adherence and health literacy that is not fully explained by the model which may have diminished the explanatory power of the regression model. As this study did not include a measure of participants’ actual performance, further studies are required to address the potential role of Dunning-Kruger effect in the context of medication adherence and health literacy in people with MS.

Among all domains of health literacy, the only dimension that remained in the final model was ‘knowledge of caring for the disease.’ This dimension represents the patients’ knowledge of health, self-management, disease and its provocative factors, and the level of confidence they feel about their knowledge. These findings highlight the importance of knowledge as a component of health literacy in line with previously explored frameworks of health literacy [12]. Considering it was the only domain of health literacy with similar predictive power of the total score of the health literacy, suggests it could be a key intervening variable and potentially a mediating factor – a hypothesis that requires testing in future longitudinal research. This is particularly important in the context of MS because it is believed that 43% to 70% of people living with MS suffer from cognitive impairment which limits their ability to acquire and process information [44].

In addition to our main findings, we described the demographic and clinical information of people with MS. In line with the latest data reported in a previous study conducted in Isfahan, the majority of participants were female with a similar ratio, although the age and disease duration in the current study have slightly increased. This is perhaps because of the exclusion of people aged less than 18 in the current study, which excludes all cases of pediatric MS [5]. In contrary to previous studies, education and, more surprisingly, gender were not predictors of adherence to DMTs in the current study [45]. A possible reason may be the introduction of new DMTs that have not been studied much in the past literature. The impact of income on medication adherence in this study has been similarly reported in people with type II diabetes [17, 46].

Based on the definition of binary adherence (MDR = 0 as adherent), 76.3% of the participants were adherent to treatment. This finding is consistent with most existing studies in the literature on binary adherence in people with MS. For example, in a study in Latin America in 2022, 78.1% of all participants were adherent, with similar percentages observed by country: 79.7% in Argentina and 76% in Ecuador [37]. A similar adherence rate, 72%, was reported in Poland in 2022 [47]. This finding falls within the range of values calculated in a meta-analysis in 2021 using other definitions of adherence such as pharmacy refill data, including proportion of days covered (PDC) or medication possession ratio (MPR) [7]. Therefore, it can be said that the rate of binary adherence in people with MS in the present study is consistent with that of most MS patients in other regions.

Implications

The results of the study highlight the role of health literacy as a social determinant of health. As the non-adherence to DMTs is directly associated with poorer health outcomes, health literacy would be a target for public health interventions. Group educational programs, especially in the referral centers of the patients and MS communities, are cost-effective interventions for all healthcare settings, especially developing countries. There are limited yet promising interventions such as peer-led education [48, 49], online courses [50], and short films [51].

The current study provides evidence for the potential of educational programs to be effective interventions against non-adherence to DMTs and the associated disease burden and costs. Furthermore, there is evidence that educational interventions may increase the cognitive reserve of people living with MS [52]; therefore, educational programs not only may improve adherence to DMTs via health literacy but may also delay cognitive impairment. Nurses play a leading role in many of these programs [48, 53] as they have both theoretical knowledge and patient education competencies required for the programs. Therefore, we believe there are opportunities for nurse researchers in this matter.

Another implication of the findings is that, given the predictive role of income in adherence to DMTs, special attention to low-income communities is required for both policy-makers and healthcare personnel. The low-income people tend to have lower academic performance and to be less educated [54], which is, in turn, a potential barrier to educational interventions.

Limitations and strengths

This study is prone to common survey biases, including recall bias and social desirability bias [55]. All the measures to minimize the environmental disturbances and stress were considered by the researches in an effort to mitigate the risk of recall bias, including planning both phone interview and data collection based on participants’ preferences. To avoid social desirability bias, all participants were informed about the de-identification process and the confidentiality of data. No personal information was required to complete the questionnaires.

The 56.9% response rate is a study limitation that introduces a risk of non-response bias. We attempted to mitigate this by sending one reminder to participants who did not return the questionnaires or returned incomplete questionnaires. The non-response bias may limit the generalizability of our results to the broader population of people with MS.

Data were collected via both online and paper-based, in-person surveys. In the online version, the participants were required to complete all items. In the paper-based version, the lead author reviewed each returned questionnaire for completeness at the time of collection and invited participants to complete any skipped items. Any case with at least one missing response was excluded from analysis. This resulted in the exclusion of 128 participants which may be a potential source of selection bias.

Another challenge was to find a unified operational definition for adherence to all DMTs. Although we used the definition suggested by Alonso et al. for a missed dose of intravenous DMTs, this definition does not seem to have an evidence-based basis [37]. It means that MDR for intravenous DMTs can only be 0 or 1, which causes heterogeneity in the data related to MDRs. We believe a unified definition of adherence or a missed dose appears to be impossible due the unique diversity in DMTs compared to medications available for other disorders. Additionally, the psychometric performance of rMS-TAQ may deem insufficient as the preliminary reliability analysis was limited to only ten participants. Until the full psychometric assessment of rMS-TAQ, the results should be interpreted carefully.

The risk of bias would be very low if the adherence to DMTs was assessed by direct approaches, like the database of the insurance companies or a medication event monitoring system. Future studies may find these methods more insightful compared to questionnaire-based assessment.

The limited explanatory power of the regression model, evidenced by the low R², may reflect the absence of potential factors known to influence adherence in a number of studies [7, 45]. Notably, we did not assess potential contributing factors such as depression, cognitive impairment, or social support. Although we attempted to control for these variables by excluding individuals with self-reported chronic illnesses, it may not have sufficiently accounted for these factors, particularly if participants were unaware of or did not report underlying cognitive or mental health conditions. Future research incorporating these factors is needed to address this limitation.

Finally, the assessment of income in this study was very abstract. The interpretation of ‘adequacy’ of income may vary from person to person. However, given the highly personal nature of income, it would be hard to find a direct way to collect these data. Future studies are recommended to use percentile-based questions to collect more reliable data regarding income.

The strengths of the study include the random sampling method that differs from the convenience, non-random sampling approaches in most of the previous studies [19, 22, 26, 28]. The random systematic sampling improves the generalizability of the results. Additionally, in contrast to other studies conducted in Iran [19, 22], the current study is a multicenter study in which recruitment was not limited to a single center. We have also included all types of DMTs in the study; a large number of previous studies of medication adherence in the field of MS have been limited to oral or injectable DMTs [7, 47, 56].

Conclusion

The findings suggest that higher health literacy is associated with better medication adherence among people living with MS. The study provides the empirical evidence on the relationship between health literacy and medication adherence among Iranian adults living with MS. Our findings add to literature regarding the link between health literacy and medication adherence in chronic diseases in general, and people with MS in particular. To our knowledge, it is also the first study to examine this link in Iranian context.

Among dimensions of health literacy, the ‘knowledge of disease and care for the disease’ was found to be the strongest predictor for medication adherence. However, the correlation is moderate, and the model could explain relatively low percentage of the total variance of medication adherence.

Future studies are needed to elaborate more details about the relationship between health literacy and medication adherence in people living with MS, the potential mediating role of disease knowledge, and to overcome the limitations of this study.
