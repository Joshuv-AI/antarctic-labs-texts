Joint association of nutritional and inflammatory status, and sedentary behavior on mortality in US adult cancer survivors: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Tongzhe Zhao, Cheng Yang, Xueli Yang, Long Liang, Yunxiang Qi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe interplay between nutritional and inflammatory status and sedentary behavior is intricate, and both are associated with the prognosis of cancer survivors. However, the combined effect of these factors on the mortality rate of cancer survivors remains unclear.MethodsWe selected cancer survivors from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2007–2018). The Naples Prognostic Score (NPS) was used as an integrated marker of inflammatory and nutritional status, while sedentary behavior was quantified based on self-reported daily sitting time. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were employed to examine the independent and combined associations of NPS and sedentary behavior with mortality outcomes in this population.ResultsAmong 2,411 cancer survivors followed for a median of 71 months, 554 deaths occurred (cancer, 190; non-cancer, 364). High NPS correlated with higher all-cause, cancer-specific, and non-cancer mortality. Longer sedentary time was independently linked to higher all-cause and non-cancer mortality, with each additional hour of sitting per day conferring a significant rise in risk. Survivors exhibiting both high NPS and ≥ 6 h/day of sedentary behavior faced the greatest mortality risks: all-cause (hazard ratio [HR] 4.44, 95% CI: 2.66–7.42), cancer-specific (HR 1.92 95% CI: 1.11–3.32), and non-cancer (HR 2.81, 95% CI: 1.76–4.50).ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that nutritional and inflammatory status and sedentary behavior collectively influence mortality risk in cancer survivors. Patients exhibiting concurrent poor nutrition, systemic inflammation (high NPS), and sedentary behavior may be at higher risk of death.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15176-8.Keywords: Cancer survivors, Sedentary behavior, Inflammation, Nutrition, Naples prognostic score, Mortality, NHANES

Introduction

Cancer remains a critical global public health challenge, with nearly 20 million new cases reported worldwide in 2022, yet mortality rates have steadily declined due to therapeutic advancements, lifestyle improvement and healthier nutritional intake [1, 2]. The United States alone recorded approximately 18.1 million cancer survivors in 2022 [3]. This growing survivor population highlights the urgent need to identify key risk factors that shape long-term outcomes.

Nutritional and systemic inflammatory status of cancer patients significantly influences survival outcomes [4]. Established biomarkers, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI), advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI), and prognostic nutritional index (PNI), capture these physiological states and correlate strongly with disease progression and survival across multiple malignancies [5–8]. However, such continuous variables usually rely on sample-specific thresholds for categorization, resulting in a standard heterogeneity gap in their analysis in pan-population cancer survivors. The Naples prognostic score (NPS) combines serum albumin (ALB), total cholesterol (TC), NLR, and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR) to evaluate systemic inflammation and nutritional status simultaneously [9]. Originally validated in colorectal cancer [9], NPS has since demonstrated a significant predictive prognostic value across diverse malignancies [10–14]. Unlike continuous metrics, NPS employs consistent 0–4 scoring criteria across studies, making this standardized metric particularly useful for assessing nutritional-inflammation status and long-term prognosis in cancer survivors.

Persistent sedentary behavior is strongly associated with cancer progression and long-term adverse outcomes [15–17]. Notably, US cancer survivors spend an estimated 62% of their waking hours (approximately 8.7 h per day) in sedentary activities, a significantly greater duration and with fewer interruptions than individuals without a cancer diagnosis [18]. The relationships among nutrition, inflammation, and sedentary behavior are complex [19–23], and these conditions often co-occur in cancer survivors [24–26]. Such coexistence may amplify adverse effects on recovery and long-term outcomes, yet their combined impact on survival has received relatively little attention to date.

This cohort study, based on a large nationally representative sample from the United States, evaluated the independent and combined impacts of nutritional and inflammatory status (NPS) and sedentary behavior on mortality risk among cancer survivors. Additionally, it assessed the predictive value of NPS for all-cause mortality in this population.

Methods

Data source and study population

The primary exposure, outcome variables, and key covariates were collected during the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007–2018 cycles. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and all NHANES protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board. Figure 1 presents the participant selection process.

Study flow diagram. NHANES, National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey; NPS, Naples prognostic score

Diagnosis of cancer

The NHANES collected cancer history data through in-person interviews, where participants reported whether they had received a cancer diagnosis, their age at each diagnosis, and the cancer type. Self-reported cancer diagnoses have shown reliability in prior research [27]. We calculated years since diagnosis by subtracting the age at first cancer diagnosis from the participant’s current age. Based on previous studies, we grouped cancers into obesity-related (including breast, hematologic, colorectal, esophageal, brain, gallbladder, liver, kidney, pancreatic, thyroid, gastric, ovarian, and uterine cancers) and non-obesity-related types [28, 29].

NPS assessment

The NPS was calculated based on four components: ALB, TC, NLR, and LMR. For each component, one point was assigned if the following conditions were met: ALB < 40 g/L, TC ≤ 180 mg/dL, NLR ≥ 2.96, or LMR ≤ 4.44; otherwise, the component score was 0. The overall NPS was obtained by summing these four component scores, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 4 [9]. Based on previous studies, participants were categorized into three groups based on total scores: low (score of 0), intermediate (scores of 1, 2), and high (scores of 3, 4). Definitions and computational methods for additional nutritional and inflammatory markers appear in Supplementary Table S1.

Daily sitting time & leisure-time physical activity assessment

Daily sitting time and Leisure-Time physical activity (LTPA) were assessed using the World Health Organization’s Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) [30]. The questionnaire assessed daily sitting minutes in a typical day, which we converted to hours per day (h/d) and classified into four sedentary time categories (0 to < 4, 4 to < 6, 6 to 8, and ≥ 8 h/d) based on previous research [31, 32]. Respondents also documented weekly frequency and duration of moderate- and vigorous-intensity leisure activities. We computed total LTPA by summing moderate-intensity activity minutes with double the vigorous-intensity minutes [31]. Participants were then classified as physically active (≥ 150 min/week) or inactive (< 150 min/week) according to the 2018 US Physical Activity Guidelines [33].

Mortality assessment

Mortality data and causes of death were obtained from the National Death Index (NDI) using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) coding system. All-cause mortality encompassed deaths from any origin, while cancer-specific mortality included only fatalities attributed to malignant neoplasms (ICD-10 C00-C97), with noncancer mortality comprising all remaining cases. The follow-up period was defined as the interval from the date of the initial interview to either the date of death or December 31, 2019.

Assessment of demographic factors and covariates

Sociodemographic, lifestyle behavior, and chronic comorbidities covariates were considered in this study. Sociodemographics and lifestyle behaviors included age, sex, race and ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, and others [American Indian/Native Alaskan/Pacific Islander, Asian, multiracial]), education attainment (less than high school, high school or equivalent, and above high school), marital status (not married and Married/living with partner), family income poverty ratio (< 1.3, 1.3 to 3.5, and > 3.5), body mass index (BMI) categories (< 25, 25 to < 30, and ≥ 30), smoking status (never, former, and current), alcohol use (never, former, and current), and LTPA (< 150 MET-min/wk and ≥ 150 MET-min/wk). Chronic comorbidities (hyperlipidemia, diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease [CVD]) were identified based on self-reported diagnoses or medication use, and laboratory results, as detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Following NHANES analytic guidelines, all analyses in this study incorporated sample weighting, clustering, and stratification to ensure national representation of the US population. Baseline characteristics were summarized by NPS categories. Continuous variables are presented as weighted mean (SD), and categorical variables are presented via counts (weighted frequencies). Continuous variables were compared using the t test if normally distributed or the Kruskal–Wallis test if non-normally distributed, and categorical variables were compared using the χ² test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models evaluated independent and joint associations between NPS, daily sitting time, and mortality outcomes (all-cause, cancer-specific, and non-cancer). Final models were adjusted for demographic factors (age, sex, race and ethnicity, education, and family income poverty ratio), BMI, smoking status, alcohol use, LTPA, and chronic comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, CVD), along with cancer type diversity and time since initial diagnosis. For all-cause mortality, NPS was categorized into three groups (0, 1–2, 3–4). For cancer-specific and non-cancer mortality, participants were grouped into two categories (0–1, 2–4) to ensure adequate sample size in the reference group and obtain stable hazard ratio estimates. Predictive performance of the NPS for all-cause mortality was evaluated using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves (time-ROC) and corresponding area under the curve (time-AUC). Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analyses illustrated differences in survival probabilities across groups defined by combined NPS and sedentary time. Sensitivity analyses included: excluding participants who died within the first 24 months of follow-up, to minimize reverse causation. Multiple imputation was performed using the “mice” package in R software, incorporating all covariates into the imputation model. Participants with missing primary exposure or outcome variables remained excluded. Multivariable Cox regression analyses were then repeated on the imputed datasets.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software 4.4.2 and DecisionLinnc1.0 software [34]. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

From 2007 to 2018, the study enrolled 2,411 cancer survivors (weighted population: 18,324,849; weighted mean age: 62.7 years; weighted male proportion: 43.9%), with participant characteristics stratified by NPS in Table 1(at the end of the document text). Male cancer survivors and survivors with older age or insufficient LTPA were more likely to have higher NPS compared with their counterparts. Additionally, participants with higher NPS more frequently had hypertension, diabetes, and CVD. Notably, sedentary behavior further influenced the distribution of NPS: among cancer survivors with daily sedentary time of less than 6 h, the proportion of low NPS survivors was 6% higher than that of survivors with daily sedentary time of ≥ 6 h, while the proportion of high NPS survivors was 6.6% lower (Table 1, Fig. 2).

Comparison of nutritional or inflammatory indicators for predicting all-cause mortality

To evaluate the prognostic utility of the NPS for all-cause mortality among cancer survivors, we constructed time-ROC (Supplementary Fig. S1) and time-AUC (Fig. 3). Overall, the AUC values of NPS were generally comparable to those of ALI, SIRI, and PNI, and exceeded that of other individual nutritional or inflammatory indicators.

Association between NPS or daily sitting time and mortality

In the separate analysis, During the follow-up median period of up to 71 months, a total of 554 deaths were observed. Of these, 190 were attributed to cancer and 364 to various other causes. In the final adjusted model, both NPS and daily sedentary time showed clear links to mortality among cancer survivors. Compared with those in the low NPS group, those with high NPS exhibited HR of 3.53 (95% CI: 2.47–5.05) for all-cause mortality, 1.80 (95% CI: 1.19–2.72) for cancer-specific mortality, and 1.54 (95% CI: 1.21–1.96) for non-cancer mortality (Table 2). Survivors reporting > 8 h/day of sedentary time showed elevated all-cause (HR 1.74, 95% CI: 1.23–2.48) and non-cancer (HR 2.40, 95% CI: 1.51–3.81) mortality risks compared to those with < 4 h/day (Table 2). Meanwhile, each extra point of NPS was associated with a 38% increase in all-cause mortality, a 43% increase in Cancer-specific mortality and a 35% increase in non-cancer mortality, and each additional hour of sedentary behavior per day was associated with a 7% increase in all-cause mortality and a 9% increase in non-cancer mortality (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Joint association of NPS and sitting time with mortality

In the joint analysis, cancer survivors with high NPS combined with ≥ 6 h/d of sedentary time exhibited the greatest risks for all-cause, cancer-specific, and non-cancer mortality (Table 3; Fig. 4). Specifically, in the final adjusted model, the HR was 4.44 (95% CI, 2.66–7.42) for all-cause mortality, with values of 1.92 (95% CI: 1.11–3.32) and 2.81 (95% CI: 1.76–4.50) observed for cancer-specific and non-cancer mortality, respectively. The KM analysis showed that the group with high NPS and daily sitting time ≥ 6 h/d had lower overall and non-cancer survival probabilities relative to other groups (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Sensitivity analyses

In sensitivity analyses excluding deaths within the first 24 months, the associations of NPS and daily sitting time with all-cause and non-cancer mortality remained robust (Supplementary Table S3). For cancer-specific mortality, however, the associations were attenuated and no longer statistically significant, likely due to the smaller number of cancer deaths after exclusion. Although the hazard ratios remained above 1.0, consistent with the main analyses. In analyses after imputing missing covariate data, the associations were materially unchanged (Supplementary Table S4), supporting the robustness of our findings.

Subgroup analyses

In subgroup analyses, the adverse effects of high NPS (scores 3, 4) were more pronounced in cancer survivors who were younger, male, and had a BMI ≥ 25. Conversely, the link between prolonged sitting and mortality was more pronounced in cancer survivors who were older, female, and had a BMI < 25 (Supplementary Table S4). Both high NPS and sedentary behavior exhibited more pronounced mortality risks in non-obesity-related cancers than in obesity-related cases (Supplementary Table S4). In the combined analysis, cancer survivors with high NPS (scores 2–4) and daily sitting time ≥ 6 h/d faced significantly increased all-cause mortality risk across all subgroups relative to controls (Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion

In this study, we preliminarily validated the predictive power of simple and broadly applicable indicators of nutritional and inflammatory status, represented by the Naples Prognostic Score (NPS). The NPS is a composite index derived from serum albumin, total cholesterol, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR). We discussed the separate and combined effects of NPS and sedentary behavior on mortality among cancer survivors using a nationally representative US cohort. Our findings indicate that high NPS is linked to increased risk of all-cause, cancer-specific, and non-cancer mortality, while prolonged sedentary time is associated with higher all-cause and non-cancer mortality risk. In addition, individuals with both high NPS and sedentary time ≥ 6 h/d faced the highest mortality risks across all categories relative to those with low NPS and sedentary time < 6 h/d.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the joint impact of nutritional status, inflammation, and sedentary behavior on mortality outcomes using a nationally representative cohort of cancer survivors. Prior studies have typically examined these factors independently, with limited attention to their combined effects [35–37]. Several previous studies have addressed the interplay between nutritional or inflammatory markers and physical activity in relation to mortality among cancer survivors [38, 39], but it is important to point out that sedentary behavior has been identified as a health risk factor independent of insufficient physical activity [40–42], and therefore additional consideration of the effect of sedentary behavior is important. We employed the NPS to assess nutritional-inflammatory status, a metric previously linked to poor prognosis across multiple cancer types. In addition, a recent study on community-based cancer survivors showed that high NPS was associated with both higher cancer incidence and mortality among cancer survivors [43], which is well supported by our findings. On this basis, we further validated the predictive ability of NPS for the risk of all-cause mortality in cancer survivors and compared it with nutritional or inflammatory indices commonly used in past studies through time-ROC and time-AUC. The results showed that the predictive performance of NPS was comparable to that of ALI, SIRI, and PNI and superior to other indicators, demonstrating reliable risk predictive ability and consistency at multiple follow-up time points. Unlike continuity indicators such as ALI, SIRI, and PNI — which require sample-specific cutoff values [44, 45] — NPS maintains consistent stratification criteria across studies [9, 11], enhancing its reproducibility and clinical utility.

From the point of view of possible biological mechanisms, NPS works by integrating four indicators, ALB, TC, NLR, and LMR, all of which correlate with the body’s nutritional status and systemic inflammation levels. First, lower levels of serum albumin reflect the reduced protein synthesis capacity and albumin escape caused by malnutrition and chronic inflammation, which are associated with a higher risk of death in cancer patients [46, 47]. Secondly, cholesterol and its metabolism are not only associated with cancer progression but also with immune cell activity [48]. Serum cholesterol is thought to positively regulate the activity of T cells and anti-tumor NK cells [49, 50]. A large meta-analysis further confirmed that high serum cholesterol is associated with better prognosis in cancer [51]. In addition, immune cells have an important role in tumor development. Neutrophils play multifaceted and sometimes contradictory roles in tumorigenesis, growth, and spread, and their increase is usually associated with poor prognosis [52, 53]. Monocytes can differentiate in the tumor microenvironment to differentiate into tumor-associated macrophages that support tumorigenesis, local progression, and distant metastasis [54]. Lymphocytes play an important role in the immune system and anti-tumor immunity [55]. The long-term cumulative effects of sedentary behavior, as a health risk factor independent of physical inactivity, may induce a chronic inflammatory state and metabolic abnormalities through mechanisms such as increased skeletal muscle wasting, insulin resistance, disturbances in lipid metabolism, and the release of pro-inflammatory factors, which may exacerbate the systemic burden of inflammation and overall health depletion in cancer survivors [40]. In addition, sedentary behavior may coexist with psychosocial problems such as anxiety and depression, which may have an indirect effect on mortality risk [56]. More importantly, chronic inflammation and sedentary behavior may mutually amplify adverse health effects through synergistic mechanisms [21, 57]. On the one hand, increased sedentary time leads to increased levels of some inflammatory factors [58, 59]. On the other hand, individuals with inflammation and malnutrition frequently experience fatigue and decreased physical strength, which often leads to reduced physical activity and increased sedentary behavior [21]. Animal experiments have also shown that exogenous inflammatory factors or bacterial products can induce “sickness behavior,” manifested as reduced activity and food intake [22, 60, 61]. These intertwined physiological and behavioral factors may substantially increase mortality risk among cancer survivors.

Interestingly, our subgroup analyses indicated that the effect of sedentary behavior on the risk of death was more pronounced in survivors with a BMI < 25 and non-obese-related cancer survivors. This finding contrasts with the conventional perspective that sedentary behavior contributes to obesity [62] and that obese individuals face higher risks of adverse cancer outcomes [63, 64]. Increasing evidence points to an “obesity paradox” in cancer, where obese patients may experience a survival advantage [65, 66]. This protective effect may be attributed to greater energy reserves, enhanced tolerance to treatment-related adverse effects, and reduced likelihood of cachexia development [65, 67, 68]. Thus, non-obese individuals may be more vulnerable to the detrimental consequences of prolonged sedentary behavior, such as muscle loss, decreased muscle function, and chronic systemic inflammation, under conditions of lack of metabolic reserve, which can lead to a higher risk of mortality. This also suggests that the value of intervention for sedentary behavior as an independent risk factor is equally important in non-obese cancer survivors. Nevertheless, these subgroup findings should be regarded as exploratory given the potential for chance associations due to multiple testing, and should be interpreted cautiously until replicated in future studies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, sedentary time and several covariates, including lifestyle factors, were self-reported and thus prone to recall bias. In particular, sedentary behavior was assessed only as total sitting time without distinguishing between occupational and leisure domains, which may have introduced misclassification and most likely attenuated the observed associations. Second, cancer history was self-reported, which may result in misclassification, although prior studies suggest reasonable concordance with medical records. Third, both NPS and sedentary time were measured only once at baseline, and changes in nutritional, inflammatory, and behavioral status over time could not be captured. Such changes may influence mortality risk, and future studies with repeated measurements are warranted to better clarify these longitudinal effects. Fourth, NHANES does not provide information on cancer stage or treatment, which are important determinants of nutritional and inflammatory status, physical activity, and survival. The absence of these data may have introduced residual confounding. Fifth, although survey weights, clustering, and stratification were applied, weighted estimates in small subgroups such as cancer survivors may be unstable and not fully representative of the broader U.S. population. Finally, although we excluded deaths within the first 24 months in sensitivity analyses, reverse causation cannot be fully ruled out. To clarify our hypothesized framework, a directed acyclic graph (DAG) was included (Supplementary Fig. S4), which also highlights unavailable variables in NHANES such as cancer stage and treatment.

Conclusion

This study suggests that cancer survivors with both poor nutritional and inflammatory status (high NPS) and sedentary behavior may face a significantly increased risk of all-cause, cancer-specific, and non-cancer mortality. These results highlight critical factors that may affect the long-term health and survival of this population. At the same time, the study demonstrates the importance of integrating nutritional and inflammatory status and sedentary behavior when developing strategies to enhance long-term outcomes for cancer survivors.
