Please show restraint: excessive focus on catastrophic events can lead to greater psychological trauma
Authors: Huaqiang Liu, Zhensong Lan, Qi Zhao, Fafang Deng, Xuefang Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study explored the relationship between attention, negative emotions, empathy, mindfulness, and psychological trauma in catastrophic events to reduce psychological trauma in the public.MethodsA total of 526 adults in Guangxi Province, China, were investigated using self-rating scales for post-traumatic stress disorder, emotion, and empathy, as well as a five-factor self-rating mindfulness scale and a catastrophic incident concern questionnaire.ResultsThe results revealed the following: (1) Emotion significantly mediated attention and psychological trauma. (2) Empathy had a significant mediating effect on attention and psychological trauma. (3) Mindfulness moderated the relationship between negative emotions and psychological trauma, amplifying the impact of emotions on trauma rather than buffering it.ConclusionsWhen people pay attention to catastrophic events, they should remain sensible and restrained, and avoid excessive emotional involvement to reduce the excessive psychological trauma caused by the event.

Background

On 21 March, 2022, a devastating aviation disaster occurred in Mengshan County, Guangxi Province, China, claiming the lives of 133 passengers. This tragedy not only brought immense grief to the families of the victims but caused widespread sadness in the public psyche. After experiencing catastrophic events, excessive stress disrupts people’s adaptive mechanisms, breaks the balance of the body, leads to human dysfunction, and quickly results in psychological stress trauma, that is, psychological stress disorders [1]. It is widely believed that only personal experience of a catastrophic event can cause psychological trauma. However, in the information age, new media has brought people closer to catastrophic events and victims off the screen, and harmful information overload may also increase the public’s emotional involvement [2]. Given that our negative preferences have evolutionary advantages, when we receive huge amounts of media information, these preferences lead us to indulge in negative emotions [3]. Notably, individuals with high empathy are more sensitive to the emotions of others [2]. Under the influence of empathy, successive bad news will gradually break through the defence line and sadness, regret, pain, and powerlessness will directly impact individuals, causing secondary psychological trauma. Mindfulness, a kind of positive consciousness, has gradually become the focus of attention. Mindfulness may be used to eliminate negative emotions caused by empathy during catastrophic events and to avoid secondary psychological trauma in people who are sensitive to such events. Therefore, exploring the factors and mechanisms that influence public attention to catastrophic events in psychological trauma, including the positive effect of mindfulness on public awareness of psychological trauma, holds both theoretical and practical significance. Further research is necessary to prevent and intervene in avoiding and adjusting psychological trauma when coping with catastrophic events.

Relationship between attention, emotion, and psychological trauma

With information overload and excessive attention paid to catastrophic events, people lose their sense of security and control over their environments [4]. Individuals who pay excessive attention to the adverse cues of traumatic events and catastrophize their harmful effects experience higher levels of depression [5]. Studies have documented that with rapid changes in the mode of information dissemination, the impact of catastrophic events on the public far from the scene is gradually increasing [6]. Public emotions about a disaster depend on the form of the images they see to some extent, and they respond differently to different types of disaster information [7].

Psychological trauma refers to a psychogenic mental disorder that occurs or is delayed after an individual experiences or witnesses an unusual catastrophic threat. It mainly manifests as repetitive experience symptoms, avoidance symptoms, negative cognitive and emotional change symptoms, and increased alertness symptoms [8]. Some scholars have established that the morbidity of public psychological trauma increases after catastrophic events [9], and the lifetime prevalence rate is high [10]. There is a relationship between concerns about catastrophic events and the public morbidity associated with psychological trauma. The public is more susceptible to emotional connections after witnessing a traumatic event, which increases their vulnerability to traumatic stress symptoms [11]. Studies have shown that trauma and disaster events are becoming increasingly prominent among the public. Moreover, the different emotional responses of the public to disaster events play a particular role in regulating the symptoms of psychological trauma, such as positive and negative emotions, which control and enhance shock responses, respectively [12].

Based on this, we formulate the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1 (H1)

Attention to catastrophic events may lead to negative emotions and psychological trauma.

Relationship between attention, empathy, emotion, and psychological trauma

Empathy is also a psychological process that aligns a person’s feelings more closely with the situations of others than with their own [13]. Therefore, individuals with strong empathy may have a more robust emotional response to others’ pain. Eckland, Leyro, Mendes, and Thompson [14] determined that the higher the cognitive empathy level, the higher the emotional clarity of individuals under stress. Watkins and Brown [2] stated that individuals with high empathy are more sensitive to others’ emotions. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2 (H2)

Individuals with higher empathy are more likely to experience psychological trauma when paying attention to catastrophic events.

Mindfulness, emotion, and psychological trauma

Mindfulness is the total acceptance of the external and internal awareness of the present moment in a non-judgemental manner [15]. In recent years, many studies have found that mindfulness, as a therapeutic technique, has an excellent therapeutic effect on psychological trauma in clinical and non-clinical individuals [16, 17]. They are vital for improving subjective well-being, regulating negative emotions, and improving depression and anxiety symptoms [18]. Furthermore, some scholars have proposed an interactive relationship between emotional state and psychological trauma; positively improving the former affects the treatment of the latter. Simultaneously, mindfulness can also enhance individual emotional regulation [19]. Based on this, we analysed the relationships between mindfulness in attention, emotion, and psychological trauma to explore the introduction of mindfulness to reduce or regulate psychological trauma caused by individual attention to catastrophic events. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3 (H3)

Mindfulness helps buffer negative emotions, thereby moderating the psychological trauma of the public from focusing on catastrophic events.

Methods

Study participants

Within two weeks of the aviation disaster, 526 residents were randomly recruited online from five cities in the Guangxi Province. First, five cities were selected from among the 12 cities in Guangxi Province. Subsequently, the investigators utilised platforms such as QQ, WeChat chat groups, and public accounts to distribute survey questionnaires in different cities. Finally, valid questionnaires were collected and screened. Before the questionnaire survey, the participants were informed about the contents of the survey, and the investigators obtained their consent. All participants were informed that anonymity would be guaranteed to protect their privacy.

After deleting invalid responses, such as those with similar responses and missing answers, 466 valid questionnaires were obtained, yielding a valid response rate of 88.59%. The participants comprised 150 men (32.2%) and 316 women (67.8%). The participants were primarily aged 18–27 years, accounting for 67.4% of all participants.

Measuring instrument

Post-traumatic stress disorder self-rating scale

The Chinese version of the Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder Self-Rating Scale (PTSD Checklist, PCL-C) was revised and tested [20]. The scale comprises four factors: recurrent responses to traumatic experiences, avoidance responses, social dysfunction responses, and increased vigilance responses, with 17 items rated on a five-point scale. A higher score indicates greater severity of psychological trauma resulting from the past period of attention to a catastrophic event. Cronbach’s α of the scale in this study was 0.97, and the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett test coefficients were 0.96.

Depression anxiety stress scales

A simplified Chinese version of the self-rating emotional scale (Depression Anxiety Stress Scales) revised by Gong et al. [21] was adopted. The scale includes three factors–stress, anxiety, and depression–and comprises 21 questions rated on a five-point scale. The higher the score, the worse the emotional state of the participant. After confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) for the items for which the common factor variances of A1, A2, and A3 were less than 0.50 were deleted, Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.96, and the KMO and Bartlett test coefficients were 0.97.

Empathy self-rating scale

Wu [22] revised the Interpersonal Responsiveness Index based on the one compiled by Davis [23]. The scale comprises four factors–fantasising about empathy, empathy, caring, and personal suffering–with 28 items scored on a five-point scale. A higher score indicates a higher degree of empathy and consideration from the perspective of others. After CFA, items B2, B5, B12, B20, B22, and B24 with common factor variances of less than 0.50 were deleted, and Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.69 and the KMO and Bartlett test coefficients were 0.89.

Five-factor mindfulness questionnaire

The five-factor mindfulness scale (Five Factors of Mindfulness Questionnaire) revised by Deng et al. [3] was used. The scale comprises five factors–observation, description, conscious action, non-judgment, and non-action–with 28 items scored on a five-point scale. A higher score reflects a greater ability to ‘focus on the present, perceive what one is doing, and not criticise or evaluate the things around them. After CFA, items C1, C11, C25, C26, and C33, whose common factor variances were less than 0.50, were deleted. Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.61 and the KMO and Bartlett test coefficients were 0.93.

Catastrophic event concern self-rating questionnaire

An independent variable questionnaire was used to evaluate participants’ degree of attention, follow-up, and participation in catastrophic events. The questionnaire included six items: concern about recent catastrophic events, follow-up, participation in discussions, and evaluation of the degree of concern. It was scored on a five-point scale, with higher scores indicating greater concern regarding recent catastrophic events. After the reliability and validity tests, Cronbach’s α of the questionnaire was 0.85, and the KMO and Bartlett test coefficients were 0.81, indicating that the questionnaire had high reliability and validity.

Data analysis

SPSS Windows software version 25.0 was used for the descriptive statistics and correlation analyses. Amos 23.0, SPSS 25.0, and its plug-in PROCESS v3.5 [24] were used to test the reliability and validity. The specific analysis steps are as follows: [1] Harman’s single factor test was adopted to test the common method bias in the questionnaire, and Cronbach’s α, KMO test, and Bartlett’s sphericity test were used to test the reliability and validity of the scale [2]. The mean and standard deviation of each variable was analysed using descriptive statistics, and the correlation between the variables was tested using Spearman’s correlation coefficient (P < 0.05, representing a significant correlation between two variables) [3]. MODEL 4 and MODEL 89 of the Hayes [24] PROCESS plug-in were used to test the mediating and moderating effects, respectively. Excel was used to draw a simple slope adjustment chart based on the moderating effect results.

If the P-value was less than 0.05, the correlation between the variables was statistically significant, and the null hypothesis was rejected (the correlation coefficient was 0). To avoid Type I errors caused by multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied to the results of multiple correlation analyses [25] (the significance level was adjusted to 0.05/5 = 0.01). After correction, Spearman correlation analysis of the total scores of the variables revealed the following (see Table 1): psychological trauma was positively correlated with attention, emotion, empathy, and mindfulness (rs = 0.172–0.535, ps < 0.001), positively correlated with emotion and empathy (rs = 0.126–0.401, ps < 0.01), and negatively correlated with mindfulness (r=-0. 199, p < 0.001).

In Model M1, the total, direct, and indirect effect of attention on psychological trauma was 0.265 (β = 0.045, t = 5.957, p < 0.001), 0.219 (β = 0.049, t = 4.511, p < 0.001), and 0.046, respectively. In Model M2, the total, direct, and indirect effects of attention on psychological trauma were 0.265 (β = 0.045, t = 5.957, p < 0.001), 0.202 (β = 0.038, t = 5.274, p < 0.001), and 0.064, respectively, the 95% CI was [0.0120, 0.1233] (excluding 0), indicating that emotion had a significant mediating effect between attention and psychological trauma, with an effect size of 24.15%.

Therefore, too much attention paid to catastrophic events is more likely to lead to negative public emotions, which, assuming that H1 is true, leads to psychological trauma. Individuals with strong empathy were more likely to experience psychological trauma, and H2 is also supported.

Considering empathy and emotion as chain intermediary variables, attention as an independent variable, and psychological trauma as a dependent variable, Model M3 is established. The results (see Table 2) indicate that in the mediating path of ‘attention → empathy → emotion → psychological trauma’, the total and direct effect of attention on psychological trauma was 0.268 (β = 0.045, t = 5.983, p < 0.001) and 0.215 (β = 0.042, t = 5.151, p < 0.001), respectively. The indirect effect was 0.047 and the 95% CI was [0.0240, 0.0730] (excluding 0), indicating that empathy and emotion had a significant mediating effect between attention and psychological trauma, with an effect size of 17.60%.

This study further verified the moderating effects of mindfulness on emotions and psychological trauma. Simple slope analysis revealed that, for individuals with lower levels of mindfulness (M-1SD), the positive predictive effect of emotions on psychological trauma was weaker. In contrast, for individuals with higher levels of mindfulness (M + 1SD), the positive association between emotions and psychological trauma was stronger (Fig. 1). This indicates that, as mindfulness levels increase, the predictive effect of emotion on psychological trauma increases. As emotion scores increase, indicating poorer emotional states, mindfulness does not buffer the impact of emotion on psychological trauma but instead amplifies this relationship, particularly when individuals are experiencing negative emotional states. Discussion

Based on the perspectives of empathy and negative emotions, this study constructed a chain mediation model of the impact of catastrophic event attention on psychological trauma.

The primary mechanisms of empathy, cognition, and emotion make the public, as observers, produce personal pain-matching with empathy ability when facing emotional situations [26]. Empathy also causes one to regard other people’s emotions as one’s own in the process of experiencing other people’s actual or inferred emotional states, thus causing the accumulation of negative emotions [27]. An interactive relationship exists between emotions and trauma, and people with greater difficulty in regulating emotions before trauma are particularly likely to develop PTSD or more severe symptoms [28]. Increased mood dysregulation may also be caused by trauma symptoms [29]. Consistent with our results, empathy played a mediating role between attention and trauma, while emotion played a mediating role between empathy and trauma. Empathy and emotion had a significant mediating effect between attention and trauma; empathy had a significant mediating effect between attention and trauma; and attention and emotion had a significant mediating effect between empathy and trauma. The path of ‘attention → empathy → emotion → trauma’ was verified. Some scholars have found that trauma can affect empathy. Symptoms of post-traumatic numbness impair empathy, making it difficult for patients to experience intimacy and tenderness, and leading to negative emotional and cognitive changes [30]. The strong relationship between trauma, empathy, and emotion is clear, but causal evidence is lacking [31].

Mindfulness served as a moderating variable between emotion and trauma, which is consistent with the design of this study [18]. Our analysis confirmed that mindfulness moderates the relationship between emotion and psychological trauma, but not in the expected way. Contrary to some previous studies [32], mindfulness did not alleviate or buffer the impact of emotion on psychological trauma. Instead, it amplified the relationship, such that higher levels of mindfulness were associated with a stronger predictive effect of emotion on psychological trauma. This finding challenges the common assumption that mindfulness always plays a protective role in emotional regulation, as it appears to enhance emotional awareness without necessarily reducing emotional distress or trauma.

Mindfulness had no moderating effect on attention or empathy. Some scholars define ‘mindfulness’ as maintaining a positive state of mind, which includes a present-oriented experience and confident emotional attitude towards the object of attention [33]. However, mindfulness significantly moderated the relationship between emotion and psychological trauma, although it did not alleviate trauma by reducing the intensity of negative emotions. Instead, mindfulness amplified the emotional impact on trauma, suggesting that individuals with higher levels of mindfulness experience greater awareness of their emotional states, which may intensify the relationship between negative emotions and trauma [34]. This finding contrasts with the traditional view of mindfulness as a purely protective emotional regulation technique and indicates that mindfulness may enhance emotional awareness without reducing emotional distress.

Mindfulness is known to improve tolerance of negative emotions by enhancing psychological and physical flexibility [34]. It has been shown to reduce rumination and anxiety, but in this study, mindfulness did not reduce the incidence of psychological trauma [35]. Instead, it intensified the relationship between emotion and trauma. This may explain why mindfulness did not significantly moderate attention or empathy in this study, as its primary role in this context may be related to emotional awareness rather than cognitive or social processes.

Conclusion

This study derived the following conclusions: [1] There are sex differences in the psychological trauma of the public caused by catastrophic events, with women experiencing significantly higher levels of trauma than men [2]. Excessive attention to catastrophic events is likely to induce negative emotions in the public, leading to psychological trauma [3]. Individuals with greater vital empathy are more likely to experience stronger negative emotions and more severe psychological trauma [4]. Mindfulness did not buffer individuals’ negative emotions but instead amplified the impact of these emotions on psychological trauma. Therefore, mindfulness may increase the awareness of emotional states, which can intensify the relationship between negative emotions and trauma, particularly when individuals are exposed to catastrophic events.
