Investigating the role of mimosine-induced genotoxic stress through DNA repair profiling
Authors: Sai Pallavi Pradeep, Suraj Kalaria, Peter M Glazer, Raman Bahal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall molecules that induce DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) are of significant interest for cancer therapy due to their ability to exploit repair vulnerabilities in tumor cells. Mimosine, a non-proteinogenic amino acid derived from Leucaena species, is known to cause cell-cycle arrest and DNA strand breaks, yet its interaction with DNA repair pathways remains poorly understood. Here, we systematically evaluated the genotoxic effects of mimosine in a panel of DNA repair-efficient and deficient cells models. Using neutral comet and γH2Ax assay, we demonstrated that mimosine treatment induces DSBs that persist in cells lacking non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), homologous recombination (HR), and nucleotide excision repair (NER). We established that cells with efficient NHEJ repair pathways can resolve mimosine-induced DNA lesions. Cell cycle analysis shows that mimosine treatment stalls the G1 phase when the NHEJ pathway is active. Cell viability profiling across cancer cells shows greater sensitivity to mimosine in cells with DNA repair deficiency. These findings establish the repair pathway dependencies that drive the mechanism of action of mimosine and its use in cancer treatment therapy.Keywords: MT: clinical applications, mimosine, homologous recombination, non-homologous end joining, nucleotide excision repair, comet assay, cell-cycle arrest, gamma H2Ax assay

Introduction

Identifying naturally derived small molecules that induce DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) has attracted significant interest for therapeutic applications. Small molecules interact with DNA by covalent and non-covalent interactions. Covalently interacting small molecules such as cisplatin and nitrogen mustard bind irreversibly and inhibit replication and transcription of DNA. Non-covalent small molecule interactions include electrostatic interactions, DNA intercalators, and groove binding. In groove binding, small molecules interact with the DNA major or minor grooves via hydrogen or van der Waals interactions. At the same time, intercalation occurs when planar polyaromatic molecules position themselves between nucleic acid base pairs and disrupt the organization of the DNA double helix.1 They induce double-strand breaks via mechanisms such as iron chelation, the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and topoisomerase poisoning.2,3 DSBs are then sensed and repaired through DNA repair pathways that include homologous recombination (HR), non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), base excision repair (BER), and nucleotide excision repair (NER). HR is an error-free double-strand DNA repair mechanism that functions during the S and G2 phases of the cell cycle, with BRCA1/BRCA2 genes responsible for strand repair. NHEJ functions throughout the cell cycle and directly ligates DNA ends with the help of DNA-dependent protein kinases (DNA-PK). NER pathway repairs bulky DNA lesions using the xeroderma pigmentosum group A (XPA) scaffold protein to stabilize and repair the site of the lesion.4 Understanding which DNA repair pathway is activated by a given small molecule helps rationalize the design of combination therapies that exploit synthetic lethality, optimize gene-editing outcomes, and identify potential resistance mechanisms that can develop in cancer therapeutics.

One such molecule of interest is mimosine or b-[N-(3-hydroxy-4-oxypyridyl)]-a-aminopropionic acid, which has demonstrated promising biological activity but remains poorly characterized in the context of DNA repair. Mimosine is a non-proteinogenic amino acid naturally derived from plants belonging to the Leucaena genus. Mimosine is recognized for its ability to chelate metal ions and induce cell-cycle arrest.5 Mimosine has also been observed to induce double-stranded breaks during the G1 phase, inhibiting the initiation of DNA synthesis at replication origins.6 While the formation of double-strand breaks has been explored, the involvement of DNA repair pathways in mimosine-induced double-strand breaks remains understudied.

In this study, we investigated the potential of mimosine to induce double-strand breaks in cancer cell lines. To identify pathways sensitized by mimosine, we employed DNA repair-efficient and deficient cell lines. We assessed the role of the NHEJ pathway using the malignant glioma cell lines M059J and M059K. M059J cells lack the DNA-dependent protien kinase catalytic subunit (DNA-PKcs) holoenzyme, rendering them unable to repair DNA DSBs via the NHEJ pathway, while M059K cells have normal levels of DNA-PKcs.7 The HR pathway was accessed using DLD1 wild-type and DLD1 BRCA2 knockout (KO) cell lines.8 To access the impact on the NER pathways, we used XP20S fibroblast cell line obtained from XPA patients, notable for its deficiency in XPA protein, an essential component of NER. The XP20S cells were transformed with a vector that expresses XPA cDNA, restoring the NER pathway.9 The induction of double-strand breaks after mimosine treatment was evaluated using the comet assay and γH2Ax assay. We evaluated the impact of double-strand breaks on the cell cycle using flow cytometry. Finally, we examined cell viability and analyzed how DNA repair pathways affect cell survival after mimosine treatment.

Results

Mimosine-induced DNA damage detected by the comet assay

To determine whether mimosine impairs DNA repair pathways, we performed neutral comet assays in a panel of DNA repair-efficient and deficient cell lines. To assess the role of DNA-PKcs, we used M059K (DNA-PKcs efficient) and M059J (DNA-PKcs deficient) human glioma cell lines. We treated M059K and M059J with 200, 400, and 800 μM of mimosine for 24 h to assess the dose-dependent effects of mimosine. In DNA-PKcs-deficient M059J cells, mimosine treatment resulted in significantly higher tail moments compared to untreated controls, indicating accumulation of DNA strand breaks. In contrast, DNA-PKcs-efficient M059K cells showed minimal tail moments, similar to those of the control (Figure 1A). We did not observe an increase in tail moment in M059K with higher mimosine doses, indicating effective repair of mimosine-induced lesions (Figure S1A). M059J cells showed a significant dose-dependent increase in tail moment compared to control, suggesting that DNA-PKcs deficiency makes cells more sensitive to mimosine-induced DNA strand breaks (Figures S1B and S1C). We used bleomycin as a positive control (Figure S1D). Next, we explored the role of the HR pathway in mimosine response using BRCA2-deficient and efficient DLD1 cell lines. Treatment of BRCA2-deficient DLD1 (BR) cells and BRCA2-efficient DLD1 cells with 200 μM of mimosine showed a significant increase in comet moment compared to the control (Figure 1B). DLD1 (BR-) and DLD1 cells showed no dose-dependent response, as no significant difference in comet moment between mimosine-treated samples (Figures S2A–S2C). We used bleomycin treatment as a positive control in DLD1 (BR-) and DLD1 cells, and observed significantly higher comet moment in DLD1 (BR-) compared to DLD1 cells (Figure S2D).

To examine the role of XPA in mimosine treatment, we treated XP20S with and without XPA restored. Both XP20S+XPA and XP20S were treated with 200 μM of mimosine and showed a significant increase in the median comet moment compared to untreated controls (Figure 1C). XP20S showed a significant dose-dependent increase in comet moment, whereas functional XPA showed no dose-dependent response to mimosine treatment (Figures S3A–S3C). Bleomycin treatment resulted in comparable tail moments between XP20S+XPA and XP20S cell lines (Figure S3D). Bleomycin was used as a high-potency radiomimetic positive control. It is activated by Fe(II) and oxygen and directly cleaves DNA by free radical production, causing larger comet moments than mimosine under the same conditions.10,11,12

γH2AX assay to detect mimosine-induced DNA damage

We next examined DNA repair foci formation by γH2AX in response to mimosine treatment. γH2AX, a phosphorylated form of the histone protein H2AX, serves as a biomarker for DNA strand breaks. We treated both DNA repair-efficient and deficient cell lines with 150 μM of mimosine for 24 h and assessed γH2AX foci formation. M059J (DNA-PKcs-deficient) cells exhibited higher median foci per cell following mimosine treatment compared to PBS control. Consistent with the comet assay, DNA-PKcs efficient M059K cells showed minimal foci formation comparable to the control (Figure 2A). Bleomycin treatment resulted in a higher median of γH2AX foci in M059J cells compared to M059K cells (Figure S4A). DLD1 and DLD1 (BR-) cells showed a significantly elevated number of γH2AX foci after treatment with mimosine in comparison to control (Figure 2B). DLD1 (BR-) cells showed a significantly higher number of γH2AX foci compared to DLD1 cells treated with bleomycin (Figure S4B).

Furthermore, treatment with mimosine in XP20S+XPA cell lines increased γH2Ax foci relative to the control, with XPA-deficient cells exhibiting a higher number of foci per cell (Figure 2C), reinforcing the role of XPA in NER and its impact on DNA damage accumulation. XP20S+XPA and XP20S cell lines showed similar levels of γH2Ax foci when treated with bleomycin (Figure S4C). These findings suggest that mimosine-induced DNA lesions are repaired in cells with a functional DNA-PKcs pathway and persist in cells lacking efficient HR and NER pathways.

Mimosine-induced cell-cycle arrest analysis

Next, we used cell cycle profiling to assess whether mimosine-induced DNA strand breaks can lead to cell-cycle arrest. Cell lines were treated with M059K and M059J cells were treated with 150 and 800 μM concentrations of mimosine and bleomycin as a positive control and cell cycle progression was assessed using flow cytometry. DNA-PKcs-efficient M059K cells demonstrated G2 phase stalling compared with untreated controls. DNA-PKcs-deficient M059J cells demonstrated a higher mean percentage of cells in the G1 phase relative to the control, suggesting an impaired DNA-PKcs repair mechanism may lead to prolonged cell-cycle arrest at the G1 checkpoint (Figure 3A). Similarly, mimosine-treated DLD1 and DLD1 (Br-) cells showed an increased population of cells arrested in G1 phase, implying that mimosine induces a G1 checkpoint response. However, treatment with bleomycin-induced DNA damage activated G2/M phase checkpoints in all DNA repair-efficient and deficient cell lines (Figure 3B). XP20S+XPA showed a significantly higher percentage of cells in the G1 phase. Furthermore, XP20S cells demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in the G1 phase, suggesting a correlation between mimosine concentration and G1 arrest. Both XP20S+XPA and XP20S cells treated with bleomycin triggered G2/M phase stalling compared to the control (Figure 3C). Mimosine and bleomycin cause cell-cycle arrests in different phases because they act through distinct targets and checkpoint responses. Mimosine synchronizes cells in the late G1/S phase by iron chelation, inhibiting ribonucleotide reductase activity, depleting dNTP pools, and promoting ROS/ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM)-dependent signaling, thereby limiting S phase entry or progression.5,13,14 Cell-cycle arrest is a direct effect of mimosine treatment and occurs independently of DNA repair status. In DNA repair-deficient cell lines, unresolved DNA lesions may persist and trigger secondary G2 checkpoint activation. In contrast, bleomycin induces direct double-strand breaks that activate ATM signaling and trigger G2/M checkpoint arrest.15

Cell viability due to mimosine-induced genotoxicity

We assessed cell viability in DNA repair-efficient and deficient cell lines using the CellTiter-Glo luminescent assay. We treated the cell lines with different doses of mimosine and observed a dose-dependent reduction in cell viability. DNA repair-efficient cell lines had significantly higher IC50 than DNA repair-deficient cell lines (Figure S5), suggesting increased sensitivity to mimosine-induced cytotoxicity. In addition, we also screened other cancer cell lines for their sensitivity to mimosine treatment. We observed a dose-dependent viability reduction in MDA-MB-231, HeLa, HepG2, Hep3B, and A549 (Figures S6A–S6E). MDA-MB-231 and A549 exhibited IC50 values greater than 1 mM, whereas hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines (HepG2 and Hep3B) and HeLa cells showed lower IC50 values, indicating greater sensitivity to mimosine (Figure S6F). Among hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines, Hep3B showed a lower IC50, consistent with its p53-null status that prevents DNA repair pathways from functioning.16 HeLa cells possess a functional p53 gene, but it is degraded by the HPV E6 oncoprotein, leading to a defective G1/S checkpoint.17,18 This contributes to the heightened sensitivity of HeLa cells to mimosine, as the cells continue into S phase despite unresolved DNA damage. Mimosine’s application as an anticancer agent is highly selective and dependent on cell line and may be further leveraged through synthetic lethality approaches.

Discussion

This study aimed to elucidate the DNA damage and repair responses triggered by mimosine, a non-essential amino acid. We demonstrated that mimosine induces double-strand breaks in multiple cancer lines, as evidenced by a significant increase in comet tail moments and elevated γH2Ax foci formation. Mimosine doses (200–800 μM) were selected based on ranges used in prior cell culture studies to measure genotoxic stress, while enabling downstream repair pathways and retaining measurable viability.19 Our findings are consistent with prior reports that mimosine induces double-stranded breaks that can initiate cell-cycle arrest during the late G1 phase.19 Mimosine-induced double-strand breaks impede the initiation of DNA synthesis at replication origins.5 The plausible mechanism involves mimosine’s electrostatic interaction with DNA20 and its role as a potent chelator, binding iron and copper ions, thereby promoting the oxidative generation of free radicals.21 Mimosine has demonstrated variability in the size of double-strand breaks, contingent upon the presence of metal ions. Treatment with iron ions resulted in the generation of random double-strand breaks, while treatment with copper ions produced uniformly sized DNA fragments.22 The variation may arise from the presence of ions and their interactions with the chromatin structure; however, it was demonstrated that mimosine does not have a preferential binding affinity for either ion.6 Using DNA repair-efficient and deficient cell lines, we expanded the current understanding of the cellular response to mimosine. The activation of specific DNA repair pathways by mimosine has important implications for both cancer therapy and gene editing. Our results indicate that cells efficient in the NHEJ pathway, particularly DNA-PKcs, are better able to repair mimosine-induced DSBs. This aligns with the known NHEJ activity during the G1 phase of the cell cycle, consistent with mimosine-induced G1 phase arrest. Our data support a functional repair dependency model, where mimosine-induced DNA lesions do not resolve in a DNA-PKcs-deficient cell line, as evidenced by persistence of DSB markers. Similarly, BRCA2-deficient DLD1 cells showed a moderate increase in DNA damage and γH2AX foci compared to HR-efficient counterparts. These findings suggest that HR also contributes to the repair of mimosine-induced strand breaks, although to a lesser extent than NHEJ. This aligns with the observation that mimosine primarily induces damage during the G1 phase, when HR is less active, further reinforcing the central role of NHEJ in mimosine response. The involvement of NER pathways was evaluated using XP20S fibroblasts lacking the XPA protein. These cells exhibited increased comet tail moments and γH2AX foci formation compared to XPA efficient cell lines, suggesting that NER, while associated with bulky DNA lesion repair, may also play a supportive role in processing mimosine-induced damage through the stabilization or processing of stalled replication forks or intercalated DNA. While γH2AX is widely used as a DNA damage marker, it can also be induced by replication stress,23 and therefore, we interpret γH2Ax results with the neutral comet assay to assess mimosine-induced genotoxicity.

In addition, mimosine has been observed to elevate caspase 9 levels, suggesting a potential pathway for apoptosis induction.24 Beyond its anticancer potential, mimosine has demonstrated antibacterial activity, including inhibition of dermatophytid fungi and bacteria. It has demonstrated antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus and functions as a Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1 (HIF-1) agonist, enhancing the antimicrobial function of human phagocytes.25 Together, these findings underscore mimosine’s multifaceted therapeutic potential as both a DNA-damaging agent and an immunomodulatory compound.

Mimosine is a natural small molecule with metal chelating properties, and its mechanism of inducing DNA damage may involve oxidative stress or interference with DNA replication complexes. The potential for mimosine to act as a direct inhibitor of DNA repair enzymes through the chelation of metal ions remains an important mechanistic consideration. However, our study suggests that repair inhibition is the reason for the observed DNA damage. We observed that the DNA-PKcs proficient cell line (M059K) resolves mimosine-induced DNA lesions, while the DNA-PKcs deficient cell line (M059J) exhibits persistent DNA damage, suggesting the NHEJ pathway remains functional. Further studies, such as mechanistic evaluations of mimosine interactions with DNA and replication forks, are warranted. Our findings advance the mechanistic understanding of mimosine’s anticancer effect and support its potential as a sensitizing agent in combination therapies, particularly in cancers with defective NHEJ repair.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

DLD1 cells, from large intestine colon tissue, were cultured in McCoys media. Both M059J and M059K cells and glial cell lines were cultured in DMEM F-12. XP02S cells, from a xenoderma pigmentosa sample, were cultured in DMEM. HeLa, A549, HEPG2, and Hep3B cells were cultured in EMEM. MDA-MB-231 was cultured in L-15 media. All media was supplemented with 1% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2.

γH2AX foci assay

A total of 50,000 cells were seeded onto a coverslip. Cells were treated with mimosine (100, 400, and 800 mM) and bleomycin (150 mM) for 24 h. NucBlue (Thermo Fisher Scientific; #R37605) was used to stain the nucleus for 25 min at 37°C. To fix the cells, 4% paraformaldehyde was used for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X- in PBS and then incubated with anti-γH2AX antibody conjugated with phycoerythrin (PE; BioLegend; #613412) for 3 h at 37°C. Coverslips were mounted on glass slides and Keyence BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope (USA) was used to capture images. Images were quantified using cell profiler 4.2.6.

Comet assay

DLD1, M059J/K, and XP02S cells were seeded at 50,000 cells/well. Cells were treated with varying concentrations of mimosine (100, 400, and 800 mM) and bleomycin (150 mM) for 24 h. Comet assay was performed as per the standard R&D systems CometAssay kit. In short, low melting agarose was used to suspend cells after which they were set at 4°C. The comet slides were placed in a lysis solution overnight, followed by an electrophoresis solution for 30 min. After running the slides at 21V for 45 min, the slides were placed in acetate solution for 30 min and then a 70% ethanol solution for 30 min. A desiccator was used to dry the samples, after which they were stained with SYBR Gold for 30 min. Images were captured using the Keyence BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope (USA) and then analyzed using ImageJ.

Cell-cycle arrest

A total of 200,000 DLD1, M059J/K, and XP02S cells were seeded on a 12-well plate in triplicates. Cells were treated with mimosine (100, 400, 800 mM) and bleomycin (150 mM) for 24 h. After 24 h, cells were washed with 1× PBS, trypsinized and transferred into 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. Samples were centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 4 min and then washed with 1× PBS. To fix the cells, the pellet was resuspended in 1,000 μL of pure ethanol and incubated at 20°C for 20 min. The cells were centrifuged again and then rehydrated for 15 min with PBS. After a final round of centrifugation, the pellet was stained with 500 μL of FxCycle PI/RNase staining solution for 45 min and then passed through filtered fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) tubes. Cells were analyzed on LSR Fortessa X-20 cell analyzer (BD Biosciences, USA) and FlowJo 10.8 was used to quantify the staining.

Cell viability

DLD1, M059J/K, XP02S cells, HeLa, MDA-MB-231, A549, HEPG2, and Hep3B cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at 10,000 cells/well. Cells were treated with different concentrations of mimosine (0.0625, 0.125, 0.25, 0.5, and 1 mM). After 24 h, cell viability was measured using CellTiter-Glo (Promega, #G9241), and luminescence was recorded using TECAN infinite 200 PRO M-plex microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 (v.10.6). Data are presented as a box and whiskers plot with median (min to max), with the number of replicates specified in the figure captions. We evaluated significant differences between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test (two-tailed). Non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was used to compare multiple groups. IC50 concentrations for mimosine treatments were determined using normalized data. We performed a best curve fitting analysis to compare the 3-parameter logistic (3-PL) model with the 4-PL model. The best-fit curve was selected based on the extra sum of squares F test. We have reported the absolute IC50 and 95% confidence interval (CI) for the selected model. We have reported IC50 as >1 mM rather than an extrapolated value.
