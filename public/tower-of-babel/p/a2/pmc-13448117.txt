Exploring the correlation between psychological indicators and professional sports performance athletes through virtual reality training
Authors: Xu Ran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examines the relationship between psychological indicators and performance outcomes, and explored changes in motor performance during a training period incorporating virtual reality (VR)-based components. The sample consisted of 100 student boxers (65 men and 35 women) aged 18 years or over. A single-group pre–post design was employed over a six-month training period. Data were collected using standardised psychological measures and sports-specific performance tests. Statistical analysis included descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, regression modelling and paired comparisons. The results revealed strong correlations among psychological variables, such as depression, anxiety, and overall mental health (r up to 0.76, p < 0.001). However, the associations between these variables and performance outcomes, as measured by the Sports Achievement Score (SAS), were weak and largely non-significant. Regression models showed limited explanatory power (R² = 0.031–0.080), and psychological variables did not emerge as significant predictors. Significant improvements in motor performance were observed in both hands over the training period (p < 0.001), with moderate effect sizes. However, due to the lack of a control group, it is not possible to attribute these changes specifically to VR training. A small but statistically significant correlation was found between the VR-related variable and anxiety (r = 0.23, p = 0.022). In conclusion, while psychological variables were internally consistent, they showed limited direct relevance for short-term performance outcomes in this sample. Performance improvements were observed during a structured training period that included VR components; however, causal conclusions regarding the effectiveness of VR cannot be drawn. Introduction

The mental health of young people is deteriorating. There is an increasing prevalence of neuropsychiatric problems, particularly stress reactions, and the overall number of people with mental health disorders is steadily increasing1,2. In the domain of sports, success is contingent not solely on skill and ability, but also on the social environment, characterised by relationships with coaches and teammates, as well as recognition within the group3. Psychological well-being exerts a substantial influence on performance. Mental health issues have the potential to hinder optimal performance levels, thereby underscoring the necessity for a comprehensive investigation into the relationship between mental well-being and athletic achievement.

It has been demonstrated that athletes frequently experience a paucity of motivation, anxiety in the aftermath of defeats, and tension during training or competition4. These factors have the potential to inflict harm upon personality and diminish mental resilience. The concept of psychological safety, defined as a state of inner security and well-being, is challenging to attain in the absence of effective mechanisms to prevent, neutralise or manage threats during training and competition3,5. As posited by Mojtahedi et al.6, negative emotions and stress are prevalent in both training and competition. Whilst physical activity is generally considered beneficial for mental health, it has been demonstrated that maximal exertion, injury, exhaustion and emotional pressure can have a detrimental effect, particularly in young athletes7.

Innovative technologies have already become a commonplace feature in educational settings8. These include digital tools, online platforms, artificial intelligence (AI), augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR), cloud systems and the Internet of Things (IoT)8,9. The efficacy, accessibility and appeal of these methods is indisputable. The utilisation of VR technology engenders immersive environments that emulate real-life scenarios, thereby facilitating the management of psychological ailments such as anxiety, phobias, eating disorders, addictions, and panic disorder4. During the period of restrictions imposed by the government in response to the pandemic, the focus of the physical education and sports training sectors shifted towards the use of VR technology4.

Nevertheless, the utilisation of VR and analogous innovative tools to support athletes’ mental health has not been the subject of sufficient academic scrutiny. The problem has both theoretical and practical dimensions, and requires further research. Mental disorders among athletes remain a serious issue, and new methods are required to help cope with them — either as separate courses or integrated into regular training. Virtual reality has been identified as a potentially beneficial tool in this context, as it facilitates the acquisition of experience, the enhancement of skills, and the promotion of psychological well-being through the utilisation of controlled scenarios. Nevertheless, it has been demonstrated that VR is unable to fully replicate real-life situations10.

The present study investigates the relationship between psychological indicators and athletic performance in student-boxers following the integration of virtual reality training.

Literature review

The field of sports psychology has been identified as a central component in the training of athletes across all levels of expertise, ranging from novices to professionals11. Mental health has been demonstrated to exert a direct influence on performance and results. This approach assists athletes in comprehending their emotions, motivations, and behaviours during training and competitive events11.

A plethora of studies have been conducted to ascertain the factors that contribute to the mental resilience of athletes. These factors encompass the ability to compete effectively under pressure, the efficacy of teamwork, the presence of team spirit, and the adeptness of emotion regulation. It is evident that these factors collectively contribute to the concept of psychological safety12,13. Athletes represent a distinct group, characterised by specific demands on both their physical abilities and mental state14. Consequently, there is an increasing emphasis on the provision of support, including regular consultations, training in stress and emotion management, and strategies to enhance morale.

Athletes are subject to a multitude of challenges, including demanding schedules, the pressure to maintain peak physical condition, social isolation, complex interpersonal relationships, fatigue, financial difficulties, criticism, and injuries15. A comprehensive understanding of these factors is instrumental in enabling coaches, psychologists and organisers to formulate optimal training and competition conditions, thereby enhancing both performance and well-being.

Psychologists emphasise the necessity for pragmatic interventions, such as cultivating the capacity to accept both triumphs and setbacks, establishing attainable objectives, fortifying collective bonds, and managing stress2. It has been demonstrated that elite athletes frequently opt for programmes that incorporate psychological support and align with the cultural milieu of their sporting discipline16.

Athletes undergo training regimens that are often more arduous than those followed by the general population, thereby potentially concealing the emergence of certain mental health concerns17. The triggers for this condition are well documented, including participation in sporting activities (particularly among young athletes), chronic stress, and injuries. These issues have the potential to manifest in a serious manner, as evidenced by previous research17. It has been demonstrated that elite athletes frequently exhibit symptoms of mental health conditions at rates that are comparable to, if not higher than, those of the general population. The advent of the pandemic further compounded the existing challenges, introducing hitherto unencountered pressures that exacerbated existing issues3. This necessitates a heightened level of attention, encompassing the provision of additional psychological support, the cultivation of enhanced stress management skills, and a more pronounced emphasis on mental health within the context of daily training routines.

As Batista et al.18 assert, motivation is pivotal in maintaining engagement with sporting activities. The pandemic resulted in the cancellation of competitions, the curtailing of social interaction, and the disruption of numerous avenues for fulfilling fundamental psychological requirements. This has led to an elevated risk of depression and loneliness7. The impact of these factors on mental health is significant.

It is generally accepted that participation in regular, moderate physical activity has a positive effect on mental well-being. However, it is important to note that extensive professional training can, in some cases, have unintended consequences. As asserted by Brito et al.19, a plethora of factors have been demonstrated to have a detrimental effect on mental well-being, including but not limited to: overtraining, injuries, stress, poor recovery, and burnout. The environment surrounding an athlete has the capacity to either facilitate or hinder mental well-being. The impact of the same load can vary significantly between individuals, with its effect being contingent on individual traits and circumstances2. It is incumbent upon coaches to be vigilant for indications of overload and to explore innovative approaches to training that are more efficacious.

Virtual reality (VR) is emerging as a valuable instrument in the field of sports psychology10. The application has been demonstrated to assist with symptoms of anxiety and depression, in addition to other psychological concerns, through the creation of structured and controlled scenarios. Systematic reviews indicate that virtual reality (VR) has the potential to enhance mental well-being in athletes, though outcomes demonstrate variability across different sports and methodologies10. It is evident that both VR and AR have now become prevalent commercial products20. The utilisation of simulators for riding, extreme sports, and fitness experienced a surge during the period of isolation imposed by the global pandemic, with a concomitant positive effect on both physical and mental well-being4,21,22. VR has been demonstrated to offer novel experiences, motivate athletes to engage more deeply, and modify their training-related emotions21.

A study was conducted that examined the impact of virtual reality (VR) fitness on mental and physical health during periods of pandemic lockdown, and it was found that there were significant benefits4. Another study examined VR sports in the Metaverse among young Chinese athletes and demonstrated that mental health and performance anxiety fully mediated the link between VR experience and endurance23. The mental state of the participants had the most significant impact on the outcomes.

Problem statement

Analysis of scientific publications indicates that physical activity generally positively affects mental health10,22. However, intense physical activity performed by student boxers can jeopardize their mental well-being, increasing the symptoms of anxiety and depression due to overtraining, injuries, and burnout7,19. The main motivation for this paper was an interest in the psychological health of student boxers and the impact of mental health on their results in professional sports. The study also rests on the need to investigate the impact of innovative teaching methods (virtual reality) on the mental health of athletes.

The study hypothesises that there is a correlation between mental health indicators and athletic performance; however, it is posited that these effects may be indirect and of limited magnitude. It is further hypothesised that the utilisation of virtual reality (VR) training can enhance motor performance and may exhibit a modest correlation with psychological outcomes. The aim of the present study is twofold: firstly, to examine the relationships between athletes’ psychological indicators and performance outcomes; and secondly, to evaluate the effectiveness of virtual reality training in improving motor performance and its potential association with psychological variables. Research questions: What mental health symptoms are more common in athletes? What psychological symptoms are most prominent in athletes? How does the use of VR technologies affect the mental health and high performance of athletes?

Methods and materials

Participants

The authors conducted the study based on the Faculty of Physical Education and Sports Sciences, Qufu Normal University in China. The study included 100 student-athletes (65 males, 35 females). Three boxing coaches were involved in the training process and evaluation procedures; however, no data from coaches were included in the statistical analyses. In this study, the recruitment method can be defined as purposive sampling based on predefined eligibility criteria (age ≥ 18 years and prior competitive experience). The researchers used statistical data on athletes aged 18 and older who already had sports titles that met the study criteria. Thus, they selected study participants with certain achievements in sports. Presumably, these participants were more stable psychologically, as required by the goals of the study. Participation in the study was voluntary, and some students refused for personal reasons. This fact may also be part of the recruitment process for selecting participants. Participants possessed equal levels of athletic experience, yet their level of experience with technology remained unexplored. All participants engaged with technology during the study, and if any questions regarding technology use arose, they could always seek assistance from their instructors.

Procedure

The present study employed a single-group pre–post design without a control group or randomisation. Therefore, the study does not allow causal inference regarding the effectiveness of the VR intervention. The observed changes in motor performance should be interpreted as improvements that occurred during the training period in which VR technology was implemented, rather than as effects attributable exclusively to VR exposure.

Students had to complete a 6-month training course and prepare for standardized control tests. Appendix 1 contains some examples of the training curriculum. Figure 1 shows the details of the curriculum.

Boxing training curriculum data.

Before training, the boxers visited an online educational psychotherapist to take a survey (Mental Health Inventory, MHI). The survey identified the symptoms of psychological disorders: stress, anxiety, panic attacks, etc. Table 1 describes the indicators used and examples of questions. The researchers compared the athletes’ answers with normal data. Normal data in this context refer to standardized norms for psychological well-being, established through psychological tests and scales. In this case, normal data indicate the level of psychological well-being typical for the population of athletes within a specific age group and sports domain. During the course, every athlete had the opportunity to seek advice from a sports psychologist and psychiatrist, if necessary.

The Mental Health Inventory (MHI) consists of 34 items. The original version of the MHI comprises 38 items. However, this study used a shortened version containing 34 items. Items were excluded during the pilot phase due to redundancy and limited relevance to the athletic population. The items that were retained were chosen to ensure that the core symptom domains were still covered. Four items were removed due to low factor loadings (< 0.40) and redundancy identified during pilot testing. The indicators were measured using a 5-point Likert scale with a range from 1 to 524. The internal consistency of the psychological scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a value of 0.69. This indicates that the scale is sufficiently reliable for exploratory research. Although the MHI is usually used as one unified instrument, this study grouped its items into four symptom domains: psychological discomfort, depression, anxiety and panic. These domains were analysed as separate subscales for exploratory purposes.

The subscales included items with different response formats (4- and 5-point Likert scales) to reflect the heterogeneity of symptom assessment. To ensure comparability, total scores were computed within each subscale without rescaling.

Across all subscales, higher scores indicate greater symptom severity (i.e., poorer mental health). Accordingly, the composite index (MHI_total) represents overall psychological distress rather than positive mental well-being.

The researchers examined the athletic performances and compared the sports achievements of athletes with their mental health before implementing the virtual reality method into the educational process. Athletes started preparation for the standardized tests used in the school curriculum. They used virtual reality technology for training. For the training sessions, a virtual reality system, specifically the PlayStation VR, was utilized. The introduced virtual reality implied a synthesis of the virtual avatar of an athlete with three-dimensional data on human movement. Virtual avatars had regular video functions, such as video storage, fast playback, slow playback, and freeze frame. Thus, first, each athlete recorded a video of their performance of the studied movement. Then the teachers uploaded this video to the VR system. At this stage, they used VR glasses. The motion analysis system animated the virtual avatars, moving three-dimensional motion data extracted from the video into the created three-dimensional model of a virtual person. Then a three-dimensional avatar simulated the movements (technical actions of athletes). This process allowed coaches and athletes to observe the technical movements of athletes in three-dimensional space in real-time. Each teacher had VR glasses paired with the athletes’ VR glasses. Therefore, teachers could connect to any athlete and monitor them. The VR system displayed and reproduced the virtual animation of an athlete and the video captured by the camera on the same screen. It allowed for an intuitive comparison of the movements of athletes.

A detailed diagram of the research design in graphic form is presented in Fig. 2.

Virtual reality glasses used in the experiment.

The VR training was incorporated into regular boxing practice sessions. All participants completed the same set of VR-based tasks, which focused on executing technical skills and analysing movement. The intervention was conducted throughout the six-month training period alongside standard coaching. Although no formal compliance metric was recorded, coaches monitored adherence to the training protocol.

After the course, the administration of the sports school conducted standardized boxing tests for athletes. The athletes’ coaches had to rate the students on the Sports Achievement Scale (SAS) to measure their achievements25. The SAS is a 16-item self-report questionnaire that assesses perceived sporting performance and achievement. It was completed by athletes after the intervention. In the present study, SAS scores were used as a proxy for performance outcomes. The use of psychometric tools, such as MHI and SAS, allows for reliable assessments of athletes’ mental health and their athletic achievements. These tools showed psychological aspects before training and competitions and provided a more objective assessment of the athletes’ achievements. Therefore, MHI and SAS can be useful for coaches and administrators of sports schools.

To collect information about the psychological health of the athletes participating in the study, the researchers applied a survey that included questions about mental health symptoms. Table 1 contains the questions. The authors conducted the survey online, which took about 20–30 min. Data collection took place on the ZOOM platform. All the study participants completed the survey. A sports school psychologist assisted with the survey before the competition preparation course.

Data analysis

The VR_effect variable represents a subjective evaluation of the perceived usefulness and impact of VR training. It is measured on a Likert-type scale from 1 to 10, with higher values indicating a stronger perceived effect. It reflects participants’ overall appraisal of the VR training experience, rather than being an objective performance metric. The statistical analysis was conducted using the R statistical environment (version 4.5.1). The analytical workflow incorporated a combination of descriptive, inferential, and multivariate techniques that were deemed to be appropriate for the structure and scale of the collected data.

Initially, the descriptive statistics were calculated for all the study variables, and these are presented as mean values with standard deviations (M ± SD). This provided an overall characterisation of psychological indicators and performance measures in the sample.

In order to examine gender differences in anxiety levels, Welch’s independent samples t-test was applied, as it does not assume homogeneity of variances. In addition to statistical significance, effect size was estimated using Cohen’s d, thus facilitating the interpretation of the practical magnitude of observed differences.

Pearson’s correlation coefficient was utilised to analyse the associations between psychological variables. A full correlation matrix was computed, accompanied by significance testing and 95% confidence intervals for each coefficient. In order to ensure the robustness of the findings, a supplementary non-parametric analysis was also performed using Spearman’s rank correlation. Given the multiple comparisons involved, p-values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure in order to control for Type I error inflation.

In order to further investigate the relationships between variables, linear regression models were constructed. The baseline model incorporated two predictor variables: overall mental health (MHI_total) and gender, which were used to predict performance outcomes (SAS score). An extended model incorporated additional psychological variables (anxiety, depression) and the VR-related effect indicator. The model fit was evaluated using R² and adjusted R², while the statistical significance of the predictors was assessed via standard t-tests. The impact of the intervention (VR-based exposure) on motor performance was evaluated using paired samples t-tests, which compared pre- and post-intervention measures for both the right and left hand separately. The magnitude of the effects was once more measured using Cohen’s d. In order to take into account any potential deviations from normality, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were additionally performed as a robustness check.

The internal consistency of the psychological scale (Psychological Discomfort, Depression, Anxiety, Panic) was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. The value thus obtained indicated acceptable reliability for exploratory research purposes. It is noteworthy that all statistical tests were two-tailed, and results were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. The assumptions for linear regression (normality, homoscedasticity, and the absence of multicollinearity) were evaluated using standard diagnostic procedures and found to be acceptable.

Ethical principles

The ethical principles of the study were agreed upon before the experiment. The administration of the educational institution approved the study. All student-athletes gave their written consent to participate in the study. The researchers observed the principles of reliability and competence when collecting information. The study followed the principles of confidentiality and respect for the individuals - participants’ responses were kept confidential and anonymous.

Limitations

The limiting factors of the study included the time to study the research problem, which was extensive and required more time to study. Other limitations were the sample size, gender, and age of the participants – the athletes aged 18 and 25 had different indicators of psychological health. At the same time, the survey method was a limitation of the study. Surveys may be limited by insufficient data and do not always provide an opportunity to conduct a deeper analysis. In addition, descriptive studies do not identify cause-and-effect relationships or establish the influence of one variable on another. Nevertheless, they provide valuable information about the current state of the studied issue. Further studies can also consider other factors.

The scores improved noticeably for both hands after the VR training. As you can see in Fig. 7, most participants showed higher results post-intervention than before. Paired t-tests confirmed significant differences for the right hand (p < 0.001) and left hand (p < 0.001). Effect sizes were moderate: Cohen’s d = 0.53 (right) and 0.61 (left). The consistency between parametric and non-parametric tests indicates that the results are robust to potential deviations from normality.

Regression models looking at predictors of sport achievement (SAS score) are shown in Supplementary Table S3. In Model 1, neither MHI_total nor gender significantly predicted SAS, and the explained variance was low (R² = 0.031). In Model 2, the inclusion of additional psychological variables and VR_effect slightly increased the explained variance (R² = 0.080). Among the predictors, only VR_effect reached statistical significance (p = 0.022), while all other variables remained non-significant. Overall, the regression models only explained a small amount of the variance in sports achievement. This suggests that the psychological variables included in the models have limited predictive value for this sample.

The sports achievement score (SAS) showed mostly weak and non-significant correlations with psychological variables, except for a small negative association with panic symptoms. The strongest association was observed between anxiety and MHI_total (r = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.66–0.83, p < 0.001). A weak but statistically significant correlation was found between the SAS scale and the VR_effect scale (r = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.03–0.41, p = 0.022). Adjusting for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate did not substantially alter the pattern of significant results.

We checked the reliability of the psychological scale (distress, depression, anxiety, panic) with Cronbach’s alpha - it came out to 0.69 (Supplementary Table S6), which is acceptable for exploratory work. The average inter-item correlation was 0.35, so the items hang together reasonably well.

Nonparametric correlations (Supplementary Table S7) gave almost the same picture as Pearson’s - the pattern stayed stable, with only small changes in strength. Overall, the psychological variables are connected in a logical way, but they don’t link strongly to anxiety scores. VR_effect showed a weak positive correlation with SAS, indicating that greater VR-related engagement or exposure was modestly linked to higher performance scores. Assumptions of linear regression (normality, homoscedasticity, and absence of multicollinearity) were examined and were not found to be violated.

Overall, the intercorrelations of psychological variables were as expected; however, their associations with sporting achievement were generally weak. Further regression analyses confirmed the limited predictive value of these variables. Although motor performance improved during the study, and VR_effect exhibited a modest correlation with performance outcomes, these results should be interpreted with caution due to the study’s design.

Discussion

This study examined the relationships between psychological indicators, motor performance, and exposure to VR-based training in a group of student boxers. The findings reveal that, while psychological variables demonstrate limited direct association with short-term performance outcomes, they form a coherent internal structure. Meanwhile, measurable improvements in motor performance are observed over the training period. The VR_effect variable is interpreted here as an indicator of VR-related engagement or performance change (see Methods). As higher MHI_total scores reflect higher levels of psychological distress, the observed positive intercorrelations are conceptually consistent.

A key finding of the study is the lack of strong or consistent correlations between psychological factors and performance outcomes as measured by SAS. Although distress, depression, anxiety, and panic were significantly intercorrelated, indicating internal structural coherence consistent with established models of psychological distress, these variables did not meaningfully predict performance in regression analyses. The explained variance remained low (R² = 0.031), suggesting that psychological status contributed minimally to performance variability in this sample. Even after including the VR-related variable, the explanatory power increased only modestly (R² = 0.080), with VR effect emerging as the only statistically significant predictor.

This pattern aligns with recent sport psychology findings indicating that, while psychological variables are important for athletes’ overall well-being, they do not always translate into immediate performance outcomes, particularly in short-term or highly structured training contexts12,13. In combat sports, performance is often more strongly influenced by technical proficiency, reaction timing, and sensorimotor coordination than by baseline psychological state, particularly in controlled training conditions.

At the same time, the observed improvements in motor performance were substantial. Significant pre–post differences were found for both hands (p < 0.001), with moderate effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 0.53–0.61), indicating meaningful gains were made during the training period. However, it is important to note that the study design does not permit the attribution of these improvements to VR training alone. In the absence of a control group, randomisation or an active comparator, alternative explanations must be considered, including general training effects, repeated testing, seasonal adaptation, and coaching influences. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as performance improvements during the training period in which VR was implemented, rather than as evidence of VR’s effectiveness per se.

The VR-related variable (VR_effect) demonstrated a small but statistically significant association with lower anxiety levels (r = 0.23, p = 0.022). Although modest in magnitude, this finding is consistent with previous literature suggesting that immersive technologies may influence psychological states through mechanisms such as increased engagement, perceived competence, and attentional focus22,23. However, the correlational nature of the analysis precludes causal interpretation, and the directionality of this relationship remains unclear.

It is important to note that the weak association between psychological variables and performance observed in this study should be considered alongside recent research on mental fatigue and sport-specific performance26–29. Emerging evidence suggests that mental fatigue can impair perceptual-cognitive processes, reaction time, and decision-making in athletes16,17,26–29. However, these effects tend to be task-specific and context-dependent, often being more pronounced under competitive or cognitively demanding conditions than during structured training phases. In this regard, the relatively weak influence of psychological variables in the present study may reflect the specific training context, in which technical repetition and motor learning dominate performance outcomes.

The prevalence of psychological symptoms observed in this sample (60% reporting at least one symptom) is consistent with previous research indicating a significant mental health burden among young and student athletes3,7,30. This supports the broader consensus that monitoring mental health remains a critical component of athlete support systems2. Nevertheless, the present findings suggest that, within the timeframe of this study, such symptoms did not result in any measurable differences in immediate performance outcomes.

From a training perspective, the observed performance improvements can be understood in the context of the recognised benefits of VR-based environments. VR provides enhanced visuospatial feedback and allows for the repetition of standardised tasks and the controlled simulation of sports-specific scenarios, all of which are known to facilitate motor learning20,21. Similar effects have been reported in other sporting contexts where VR-based interventions have contributed to improvements in technical skills and coordination31–33. In boxing, where precision, timing and motor execution are critical, these mechanisms may be particularly relevant. These findings can also be considered in relation to the literature on mental fatigue26–29, in which psychomotor performance fluctuations are often influenced by fatigue-related perceptual and attentional processes rather than baseline psychological symptom levels16,17.

However, it is important to avoid overstating the psychological impact of VR in the absence of strong empirical evidence. Although a small but significant association was observed between VR effect and anxiety, the current data do not support broad claims regarding the mental health benefits of VR training. Any such effects should be considered preliminary and require further investigation using controlled experimental designs.

Several methodological considerations provide further context for the findings. The relatively low explained variance in the regression models suggests that the current set of variables does not capture the key determinants of performance. Training intensity, coaching quality, motivation, and physical conditioning are factors that may play a more substantial role and should be incorporated in future research. Additionally, while the reliability of the psychological measures (Cronbach’s α = 0.69) was acceptable, it was not high, suggesting moderate internal consistency and potential limitations in measurement.

Finally, the results were robust across parametric and non-parametric analyses, with Wilcoxon tests confirming the direction and significance of performance changes. While this supports the stability of the observed effects, it does not address the underlying causal mechanisms.

In summary, the findings suggest that, in this cohort of student athletes, psychological variables and short-term performance outcomes operate largely independently, while improvements in motor performance are observed during the training period that included VR exposure. However, due to the observational pre–post design, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory.

Future research should prioritise randomised controlled designs, clearer operationalisation of VR-related variables and integration of mental fatigue frameworks, in order to better understand the interaction between psychological state, cognitive load and performance adaptation in combat sports.

Conclusions

This study examined the relationships between psychological indicators, performance outcomes, and exposure to VR-based training in a sample of student boxers.

The findings suggest that, while psychological variables — including depression, anxiety, distress, and panic — form a coherent and interrelated structure, they do not significantly predict short-term performance outcomes as measured by SAS. Regression models had limited explanatory power, suggesting that psychological factors had a minimal direct influence on performance variability within this cohort.

Significant improvements in motor performance were observed over the training period, with moderate effect sizes for both hands. However, due to the single-group pre–post design, these improvements cannot be specifically attributed to VR. Improvements in performance were observed during a training period that included VR-based components.

A small but statistically significant association was found between the VR-related variable and anxiety levels. This indicates a potential, albeit limited, relationship between training experience and psychological state.

Overall, the results suggest that short-term performance outcomes in this sample of student athletes are more closely associated with training-related factors than with baseline psychological status. These findings are exploratory and do not provide causal evidence regarding the effectiveness of VR interventions.

Further research using controlled experimental designs is required to clarify the specific contribution of VR training and to improve our understanding of the interaction between psychological processes and performance in combat sports.
