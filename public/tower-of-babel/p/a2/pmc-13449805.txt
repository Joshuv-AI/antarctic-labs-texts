Plant nutrients predominantly explain variation in pH value of C3 and C4 woody plants in the desert of Xinjiang, China
Authors: Kaijuan Du, Yan Luo, Yanming Gong, Yaxuan Wang, Sining Liu, Jie Yang, Jinze Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a valuable yet understudied plant functional trait, leaf pH plays a key role in indicating plant survival environment, nutritional status and physiological and ecological processes. This trait may exhibit particularly high sensitivity and unique response patterns under the harsh conditions of desert ecosystems, characterized by water scarcity and severe soil salinization. This study investigated the pH of assimilating and non-assimilating branches in C3 and C4 woody plants from the Xinjiang desert, analyzing their responses to biotic and abiotic factors. The results showed that the average leaf pH of desert woody plants was 5.60 ± 0.02. Notably, C4 plants had a significantly higher mean pH (5.67 ± 0.03) than C3 plants (5.56 ± 0.02). Branch type also influenced pH, but the pattern differed between photosynthetic pathways: in C3 plants, assimilating branches had a lower pH (5.51 ± 0.03) than non-assimilating branches (5.64 ± 0.03), whereas in C4 plants, assimilating branches had a significantly higher pH (5.81 ± 0.03) than non-assimilating branches (5.19 ± 0.07). Furthermore, direct comparisons between pathways revealed that assimilating branches of C3 plants were more acidic than those of C4 plants, while the opposite was true for non-assimilating branches. Partial regression analysis indicated that plant nutrient status was the primary factor influencing pH in C3 assimilating and non-assimilating branches, as well as in C4 non-assimilating branches. In contrast, pH variation in C4 assimilating branches was jointly affected by plant nutrients and soil factors. Overall, these findings demonstrate that pH variation in C3 and C4 plants appears to be largely associated with internal physiological regulation and plant functional characteristics. This study provides a novel perspective for understanding desert plant survival strategies and offers a scientific basis for the sustainable management and conservation of desert ecosystems.Keywords: pH value, Photosynthetic pathway, Desert plants, Plant nutrients, Soil factors

Introduction

As adaptive features shaped by long-term evolution, plant leaf functional traits serve as key indicators for analyzing plant ecological strategies and ecosystem functions [1, 2]. These traits, including leaf morphology and nutrient content, systematically reveal plant resource allocation strategies, environmental adaptation mechanisms, and the balance of ecosystem functions [1–5]. Recently recognized as a novel “soft trait”, leaf pH is a functional attribute co-regulated by the relatively stable intracellular pH (of the cytoplasm and vacuole) and the apoplastic pH, which is sensitive to external environmental changes [6–9]. Studies have shown that leaf pH, which is easy to measure, is not only closely linked to various other traits but can also effectively indicate plant living conditions and metabolic status [10, 11]. Consequently, studying leaf pH is crucial for understanding plant physiology and ecosystem nutrient cycling. In this study, “leaf pH” is used as a general term for the pH of photosynthetic organs, which include true leaves in typical woody plants and assimilating branches in leaf-reduced desert species that functionally replace leaves.

Although environmental heterogeneity (such as water stress, soil nutrient status, etc.) significantly influences leaf pH [9, 12, 13], pH homeostasis driven by plant functional differences suggests that leaf pH is associated with phylogenetic conservation and functional strategies [14]. It may form stable, cross-scale pattern through mechanisms such as chemical defense (e.g., phenolic accumulation) or nutrient absorption patterns (e.g., cation enrichment) [10, 14]. Plant functional types (e.g., phylogenetic types, life forms) are dominant factors governing leaf pH variation, with their contribution rate significantly exceeding that of environmental factors across scales [14]. Different photosynthetic pathways, a key functional classification, further reinforce this pattern through distinct carbon metabolism mechanism. In C4 plants, the catalytic activity and oligomerization dynamics of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC), the rate-limiting enzyme for CO₂ fixation, are strictly regulated by cytoplasmic pH [15]. Conversely, the apoplastic pH in C3 plants (e.g., potato, broad bean) exhibits a rapid, asymmetric response to changes in ambient CO₂ concentration [16]. Furthermore, differences in functional types indirectly affect leaf pH by regulating nutrient uptake patterns (e.g., altering nitrogen and phosphorus content). These differences reflect long-term evolutionary adaptations to diverse environments and are closely linked to plant biomass, resource acquisition, and resource use efficiency [5, 17–19].

In drought-dominated desert ecosystems, chronic water deficit triggers a suite of physiological and biochemical response in desert plants. Some xerophytic woody plants undergo leaf reduction, with assimilating branches replacing leaves for photosynthesis. Assimilating branches are specialized leaf-replacement organs that combine support and photosynthetic functions. They typically exhibit a flattened morphology, well-developed palisade tissue, and high water storage capacity, enabling efficient carbon assimilation under extreme water deficit. This organ differentiation phenomenon, in which assimilating branches replace leaves, is considered an important strategy for plants to adapt to long-term arid environments. In this process, different organs may have different responses in water use, ion regulation and acid-base balance. Drought often has a more pronounced effect on leaf pH than salt stress [6]. For instance, under drought conditions, increased cellular Ca2+ can elevate leaf pH. The unique climatic pattern of low rainfall and high evapotranspiration in Xinjiang exacerbates this effect, further limiting desert plants growth [20]. In adaptation, desert woody plants (e, g., Tamarix ramosissima, Reaumuria soongarica and Haloxylon ammodendron) accumulate saline-alkali ion concentrations in saline-alkali and water-deficient environments, leading to increased apoplastic pH. This helps lower water potential and enhance water uptake capacity [8, 21]. Additionally, drought and salt stress may promote high abscisic acid (ABA) concentrations to regulate stomatal closure and prevent excessive water loss [22, 23]. However, research on leaf pH remains insufficient, especially in desert ecosystems. Plants in these regions endure prolonged extreme water stress and poor soil conditions, and their functional types may maintain leaf pH stability through unique chemical defense or nutrient acquisition strategies. Yet, the variation mechanisms and driving factors of leaf pH in plants with different photosynthetic pathways within desert ecosystems are still poorly understood.

As a key physiological index connecting ion balance, organic acid metabolism and carbon metabolism, leaf pH not only reflects the response of plants to water and salt stress, but also plays a key role in maintaining intracellular and extracellular acid-base homeostasis, regulating resource utilization efficiency and coordinating stress adaptation. Therefore, investigating leaf pH is of great significance for revealing the physiological and ecological adaptation mechanisms of plants under drought stress, as well as for informing the sustainable management and ecological restoration of arid zone ecosystems. Based on this, this study takes C3 and C4 woody plants in the desert of Xinjiang, China as the research object, and puts forward and tests the following scientific problems: (1) Are there systematic differences in pH between assimilating and non-assimilating branches of plants with different photosynthetic pathways? (2) Is the variation mainly driven by plant nutrients or soil factors?

Materials and methods

Study sites

In this study, 42 sites in Xinjiang desert area were selected for field investigation (40.24–47.64°N, 80.66–91.33°E), with an altitude range of 270–1451 m (Fig. 1). The study area belongs to the typical temperate continental arid desert climate. In spring and summer, there is high temperature, less rain and more windy weather, and the winter is long and cold. The annual average temperature (MAT) is 5.29–14.35 ℃, and the annual average precipitation (MAP) is 38–226 mm. The soil is mainly gray desert soil, gray brown desert soil and aeolian sandy soil, and the soil salt content is high. The vegetation composition in this area is simple and sparse. Representative shrub species include Tamarix ramosissima, Haloxylon ammodendron, Calligonum leucocladum, Lycium ruthenicum, Reaumuria soongarica, Alhagi camelorum Fisch, Ephedra przewalskii and Halostachys caspica.

Locations of the 42 sampling sites in Xinjiang, China

Field sampling

In the summer of 2019 (July-August), mature green assimilating branches, non-assimilating branches and soil samples were collected in the sample area. Plant information is shown in Table 1, and the formal identification of all plant materials was conducted by the plant expert Shimin Duan (Xinjiang Institute of Ecology and Geography, Chinese Academy of Sciences) and Dr. Yan Luo (an Associate Professor at the Department of Ecology, Xinjiang University, China) according to Flora of China. According to the principle of maturity and health, 3–5 plants were selected for each species (each plant represents a repeat), and their assimilating and non-assimilating branches were collected respectively. For taller woody plants, high-branch scissors were used to collect healthy assimilating or non-assimilating branches on the sunny side of the canopy. At least 50 fresh assimilating or non-assimilating branches were collected from each replicate, and the fresh weight was not less than 20 g. Then the collected were divided into two parts. One part was placed in a plastic bag and transported to the laboratory for short-term storage in a refrigerator at -4 ℃ for the determination of leaf pH value, and the other part was used for the determination of plant nutrient content. Three sample points were selected in each plot, and the surface soil (0–20 cm) was taken by random sampling method and fully mixed, and put into a numbered sealed bag for the measurement of soil indicators. There was no rainfall in the study area 20 days before sampling.

Methods of sample treatment

For frozen plant samples, 3 g of the middle part was cut into pieces. The homogenate was manually ground with a quartz mortar and transferred to a 100 ml centrifuge tube. The mortar was washed with deionized water 3–4 times, and all the liquid was transferred to the centrifuge tube. The plant leaves were mixed with deionized water at a mass ratio of 1:10, shaken for 1 h at room temperature and 250 rpm, and then stood for 30 min until the solid-liquid separation. The pH of the supernatant was measured by a pH meter (SevenExcellence-S975, METTLER TOLEDO, Switzerland) [24]. For unfrozen plant samples, rinse with deionized water in the laboratory, dry at 105 ℃ for 30 min, then dry to constant weight at 65 ℃, grind and store for chemical analysis. For soil samples, they were dried in the shade, ground through an 80-mesh sieve, weighed and bagged for testing.

The content of plant C was determined by potassium dichromate volumetric method, and the content of N in plant and soil samples was determined by Alpkem automatic analyzer (Kjektec System 1026 distilling unit, Sweden). The content of P was determined by molybdate / ascorbic acid blue colorimetry using ICP-OES 7300 DV (Perkin Elmer) instrument. The content of K was determined by ICP-OES 7000 DV (Perkin Elmer). Soil electrical conductivity (EC) was measured by electrode method. The soil samples were mixed with deionized water at a soil-water mass ratio of 1:2.5, shaken at room temperature and 250 rpm for 10 min to fully disperse the soil samples, and allowed to stand for 30 min until solid-liquid separation. The supernatant was subjected to pH determination (SevenExcellence-S975, METTLER TOLEDO, Switzerland). The soil water stress index (Ksoil) was derived from the Global Aridity Index (Global-Aridity) and Global Potential Evapo-Transpiration (Global-PET) Climate Dataset, Global High-Resolution Soil-Water Balance dataset of CGIAR-CSI (http://www.cgiar-csi.org) ).

Data analysis

Excel was used to organize and analyze the data obtained in this study. The independent sample T test in IBM SPSS Statistics 27 software was used to analyze the difference of pH value between assimilating and non-assimilating branches of plants with different photosynthetic pathways, p < 0.05 indicates that there is a significant difference. The effects of biotic and abiotic factors on the pH value of assimilating and non-assimilating branches of plants with different photosynthetic pathways were analyzed by using “dplyr”, “ggplot2” and “broom” packages in R4.3.3 to draw forest maps. The independent and interactive effects of biotic and abiotic factors on the pH values of assimilating and non-assimilating branches of plants with different photosynthetic pathways were analyzed by partial regression analysis using “vegan” package.

Results

pH characteristics of assimilating and non-assimilating branches in desert woody plants

The pH of plants with different photosynthetic pathways was different (Table 2). At the overall level, the average pH of desert woody plants was 5.60 ± 0.02, and the pH of C4 plants (5.67 ± 0.03) was significantly higher than that of C3 plants (5.56 ± 0.02). In the same photosynthetic pathway, the pH of assimilating branches (5.51 ± 0.03) of C3 plants was significantly lower than that of non-assimilating branches (5.64 ± 0.03). The pH of assimilating branches of C4 plants (5.81 ± 0.03) was significantly higher than that of non-assimilating branches (5.19 ± 0.07). In different photosynthetic pathways, the pH of assimilating branches showed a trend of C4 plants > C3 plants (5.81 ± 0.03 and 5.51 ± 0.03), while the pH of non-assimilating branches showed a trend of C4 plants < C3 plants (5.19 ± 0.07 and 5.64 ± 0.03).

Factors affecting pH of assimilating and non-assimilating branches in desert woody plants

As shown in Fig. 2, the pH of assimilating and non-assimilating branches of plants with different photosynthetic pathways was significantly affected by biotic and abiotic factors. In C3 plants, the pH of the assimilating branches was mainly regulated by the nutrient content in the plant. The N content (p < 0.001) and K content (p < 0.01) showed a significant positive effect on the pH of the assimilating branches, while the soil pH showed a significant negative effect (p < 0.001). Other factors had no significant effect on the pH of the assimilating branches of C3 plants (p > 0.05) (Fig. 2-a); The change of pH of non-assimilating branches was also affected by nutrients in plants. K content had a positive effect on it (p < 0.05), while N content showed a negative effect (p < 0.05), and other factors had no significant effect (p > 0.05) (Fig. 2-b). In contrast, the pH of C4 plants was more complexly regulated by biotic and abiotic factors. The pH of assimilating branches was positively affected by various soil factors, including STN (p < 0.001), Ksoil (p < 0.05), STK (p < 0.05) and EC (p < 0.05), and negatively affected by plant C content (p < 0.001) and N content (p < 0.001) and soil pH (p < 0.01), while plant P and K content and STP had no effect on the pH of assimilating branches (p > 0.05) (Fig. 2-c); The pH of non-assimilating branches was mainly positively affected by plant P content (p < 0.001), N content (p < 0.01) and STN (p < 0.05), while soil pH (p < 0.01), plant C content (p < 0.05) and STP (p < 0.05) had negative effects on it, plant K content, EC, STK and Ksoil had no effect on it (p > 0.05) (Fig. 2-d).

The comprehensive response of pH of assimilating branches and non-assimilating branches of desert woody plants to influencing factors

The results of partial regression analysis showed that the combined effects of plant and soil factors on the pH variation of assimilating and non-assimilating branches of C3 and C4 plants were different (Fig. 3). In general, the comprehensive interpretation rate of plant and soil factors on the pH variation of assimilating and non-assimilating branches is 33.97%-67.63%, of which the interpretation rate of plant factors is 16.74%-58.68%, and the interpretation rate of soil factors is 14.69%-36.14%. The overall contribution of the interaction between the two is small. For C3 plants, whether assimilating branches or non-assimilating branches, the explanation rate of plant factors to pH variation is higher than that of soil factors. However, C4 plants showed different regulation characteristics: plant and soil factors had the lowest explanation rate for the pH of C4 plants on assimilating branches, and the influence of soil factors was slightly higher than that of plant factors ; the interpretation rate of non-assimilating branches pH of C4 plants was the highest, and the contribution of plant factors was significantly higher than that of soil factors. From the above results, it can be seen that plant factors are the main factors driving the pH variation for the assimilating and non-assimilating branches of C3 plants, as well as for the non-assimilating branches of C4 plants.

Discussion

Characteristics of pH in assimilating and non-assimilating branches of plants

The changes in leaf pH of desert woody plants may be closely related to their various adaptation strategies to environmental stresses such as drought and salinity [6]. The study found that the average pH of desert woody plants in the overall level was 5.60 ± 0.02, which was generally higher than that in the northern humid area (4.73 ± 0.11), semi-humid area (5.21 ± 0.07) and southwestern humid area (Karst area 5.16 ± 0.08; mountain area 4.83 ± 0.08) [14, 25], which is consistent with the results of Luo et al. [6]. This phenomenon may be attributed to the increase of soluble cation content in desert plant leaves under drought and salt stress conditions. Liu et al. [14] also supported this view. They found that the contents of calcium, magnesium, sodium, iron, total dissolved solids and salinity in plant leaves under drought conditions were significantly higher than those in other areas, a pattern that is consistent with the higher pH of leaves in arid areas than in humid areas [14].

There were significant differences in photosynthetic characteristics between C3 and C4 plants [26–29]. Compared with C3 plants, C4 plants can concentrate CO2 more efficiently through the synergistic effect of mesophyll cells and vascular bundle sheath cells, thereby improving photosynthetic efficiency and reducing water transpiration loss. The results of this study show that the pH of C4 plants is higher than that of C3 plants. Studies have shown that changes in plant transpiration can regulate the acid-base environment of apoplasts by affecting leaf water status and related signaling pathways [9]. Therefore, the lower transpiration rate of C4 plants may cause an increase in apoplastic pH by affecting ion transport or ABA-mediated regulatory mechanisms. However, the specific regulation mechanism of pH difference between different photosynthetic types of leaves remains to be further studied to clarify the internal relationship between photosynthetic physiological characteristics, leaf morphological structure and pH regulation.

As an important physiological trait, leaf pH is closely related to the trade-off relationship between multiple functional traits, and to some extent likely reflects the adaptation strategies of plants to environmental conditions [1, 30, 31]. This study found that the pH of assimilating branches of C3 plants was significantly lower than that of non-assimilating branches, which may be related to the differences between assimilating branches and non-assimilating branches in physiological processes such as photosynthesis, respiration and nutrient absorption. As a special organ for photosynthesis of xerophytes instead of degraded leaves, the assimilating branches show significant adaptability to tissue structure, and improves photosynthetic gas exchange and water use efficiency in water-limited environments by enhancing water storage tissue and optimizing photosynthetic tissue layout [32, 33], its high photosynthetic and metabolic activity may promote the synthesis and accumulation of organic acids, thereby reducing the pH value of leaves [34].

In contrast, the pH of assimilating branches of C4 plants was significantly higher than that of non-assimilating branches. C4 plants fixed CO2 in mesophyll cells by PEPC, and the optimum pH of the enzyme was about 8.0, which had higher requirements for cytoplasmic pH environment. Therefore, as the main photosynthetic organ of C4 plants, the relatively high pH of the assimilating branches may be beneficial to the efficient activity of PEPC and promote the formation and temporary storage of intermediate products such as malic acid or aspartic acid [15]. In plants with different photosynthetic pathways, the pH of assimilating branches of C4 plants was significantly higher than that of C3 plants, while the pH of non-assimilating branches showed the opposite trend, which reflected the intrinsic characteristics of different metabolic pathways and the physiological adaptation model formed by organ function differentiation. This is consistent with the specific results of Mason et al. [35] in the study that different types of plants also have specific changes in their pH. In addition, in order to meet the high ATP demand, C4 plants have evolved higher thylakoid membrane proton conductivity to finely regulate the pH dynamics in chloroplasts. This organelle-scale regulation mechanism may indirectly reflect the higher pH homeostasis at the tissue level to a certain extent [36].

Responses of pH in assimilating and non-assimilating branches of plants with different photosynthetic pathways to influencing factors

The pH of assimilating and non-assimilating branches of plants with different photosynthetic pathways is regulated by multiple factors, and their mechanisms are significantly different. This study found that for C3 plants, the pH of assimilating branches was positively affected by the content of N and K in leaves, which was consistent with the positive correlation between leaf pH and cation content such as N and K found in the overall study of terrestrial plants [14, 37]. The positive effect of plant N and K may maintain the intracellular CO2 concentration by regulating RuBP carboxylase activity and stomatal conductance, thus affecting the release of H⁺ in the carboxylation reaction [4]. The pH of non-assimilating branches of C3 plants is more dependent on the balance of nutrients in the body, and the content of K and N shows the opposite effect, revealing that there is a more complex trade-off between nutrient distribution and pH maintenance in non-photosynthetic organs [38].

In contrast, the regulation of pH in assimilating and non-assimilating branches of C4 plants showed higher complexity. The pH of assimilating and non-assimilating branches was positively affected by STN and negatively affected by plant C content and soil pH. The results were not completely consistent with the conclusion of Liu et al. [14] that the pH of woody plant leaves increased with the increase of soil pH. This difference may be partly due to the differences in photosynthetic pathway types, functional traits and physiological regulation mechanisms of the research objects, thus covering the unique response patterns of C4 plants. Further analysis showed that the positive effect of STN may be involved in the regulation of leaf pH by improving nitrogen supply and enhancing metabolic activity, while the negative effect of soil pH may reflect the regulation of extracellular environment by changes in ion balance and proton kinetics under high pH conditions.

Previous studies have shown that environmental factors (including soil chemical properties and nutrient status) can significantly change the pH of leaf exosomes, which in turn affects ion transport, pH homeostasis maintenance and related signaling pathways (such as ion exchange, proton pump activity, etc.). These changes may eventually lead to differential response of leaf pH value by changing proton balance and ion ratio [39]. In addition, the negative effect of plant C content on leaf pH may be associated with changes in carbon distribution patterns and organic acid metabolic intensity [40]. Organic acid is not only an important product of carbon metabolism, but also a key buffer substance to maintain cell pH homeostasis. Its accumulation may reduce the overall pH of leaves through the dissociation of carboxylic acid groups [41].

Factors affecting plant pH include water stress, plant nutrients, and soil nutrients [9, 12, 13, 16, 42]. We found that plant and soil factors have significant effects on the pH of assimilating and non-assimilating branches of C3 and C4 plants. Among them, plant factors play a dominant role in explaining the pH variation of assimilating and non-assimilating branches of C3 plants and non-assimilating branches of C4 plants. Plant nutrients appear to play a key role in regulating leaf pH by directly participating in cell metabolism and ion homeostasis. The photosynthesis of C3 plants depends on the fixation of CO2 by RuBP carboxylase, and the organic acids (such as malic acid) produced during its metabolism and the transmembrane transport of H⁺/OH⁻ have a direct effect on cell pH [43].

C4 plants achieve CO2 concentration through a typical garland structure, and the carbon fixation process is completed by the coordination of mesophyll cells and bundle sheath cells: PEP carboxylase in mesophyll cells is responsible for initial fixation, and Rubisco in bundle sheath cells completes the Calvin cycle. Therefore, the change of pH in non-assimilating branches of C4 plants largely reflects its intrinsic photosynthetic metabolic characteristics, while nutrient supply further affects pH homeostasis by regulating related enzyme activities and organic acid synthesis [44–46]. In addition, this study found that soil factors have a higher explanatory power for the pH variation of C4 plants assimilating branches. This result is consistent with the conclusion proposed by Li et al. [19] that soil factors have the greatest impact on leaf pH changes, indicating that soil environment plays an important role in regulating the acidity characteristics of plant photosynthetic organs. It should be noted that Li et al. [19] focused on desert herbaceous plants, while this study focused on C4 woody plants (such as Haloxylon ammodendron) with photosynthetic organ specialization. The differences in plant types, photosynthetic organ structure and resource utilization strategies between the two may lead to differences in the mechanism, intensity and ecological significance of soil factors on leaf pH.

Conclusion

This study systematically analyzed the pH characteristics and driving factors of assimilating and non-assimilating branches of C3 and C4 woody plants in Xinjiang desert. The results showed that the pH of assimilating branches of C4 plants was significantly higher than that of C3 plants, while the pH of non-assimilating branches was significantly lower than that of C3 plants. In the same photosynthetic pathway, the pH of assimilating branches of C3 plants was lower than that of non-assimilating branches, while C4 plants showed the opposite trend, indicating that the internal acid-base environment pattern of plants was determined by both photosynthetic type and organ specificity. The study further clarified that the pH variation of C3 plants was primarily associated with nutrient factors in plants, while the pH of C4 plants, especially their assimilating branches, was co-regulated by plant nutrients and soil factors, showing higher sensitivity to environmental factors. These findings provide a theoretical basis for plant functional group allocation and nutrient management in ecological restoration of arid areas. In the future, it is necessary to further explore the influence of internal changes of plants on leaf pH value, so as to deepen the understanding of the adaptability of desert plants.
