Fatal and rare Chandipura virus cases in India: Etiopathogenesis, complications and prevention
Authors: Aminath Waafira, Kannan Subbaram, Razana Faiz, Zeba Un Naher, Punya Laxmi Manandhar, Sheeza Ali
Published: 2024

Dear Editor

The National Institute of Virology in Pune has reported the first case of death in the recent outbreak of the Chandipura virus. The Director of Public Health and Preventive Medicine has issued preventive and control measures regarding the spread of the disease since the death toll has risen above 15, with a total number of reported cases exceeding 29. Since June 2024, a total of 78 suspected cases of Chandipura virus have resulted in 28 deaths, with the majority of cases reported from the state of Gujarat. Since then, sporadic cases have been reported, leading to a lag in preventive and curative measures for the virus until an outbreak in 2003 in central India due to the high case fatality rate above 50 %. There were 329 reported cases with 183 fatalities in Andhra Pradesh due to neurological illness, which was later attributed to the Chandipura virus as the etiology. The outbreak in the state of Gujarat during this period resulted in 26 cases and 18 fatalities, and that in Maharashtra resulted in 118 deaths [1,5].

First discovered in India in 1966, in the city of Pune, the Chandipura virus, belonging to the family Rhabdoviridae, is a negative sense RNA virus encoding several proteins. The P protein and the M protein are important since they play roles in the viral replicative cycle and the morbid nature, respectively. It is an arthropod-borne virus selective to India and has been isolated from humans, sandflies (Phlebotomus spp.), mosquitoes (Aedes aegypti), and hedgehogs [2]. It is transmitted mainly by phlebotomine sandflies, where the virus resides in the salivary gland and is transferred to humans during bites.

Chandipura virus is considered the most important vesiculovirus of public health due to its high case fatality rate. The infection manifests as a sudden onset of high-grade fever with gastrointestinal manifestations including vomiting and diarrhea. Neurological manifestations are the most concerning and dreaded feature of the disease. These include altered mental status, impaired neurological functions resulting in dysarthria, blurring of vision, and loss of balance. It may result in signs of meningeal irritation, namely headaches, photophobia, neck stiffness, and seizures. The signs and symptoms are of great clinical significance due to the rapidly developing acute encephalitis, which either leads to death within 24–48 hours or recovery [3,4].

Rapid diagnostic techniques were introduced due to the rapid progression, including reverse transcriptase PCR and real-time PCR.

The mainstay of treatment is supportive care and prevention of secondary bacterial infections since there is no specific antiretroviral treatment or vaccination against the virus. However, research and clinical trials have been carried out to develop a new recombinant and inactivated vaccine, which was carried out in mice, but human trials are yet to be done.

The prevention strategies are similar to any other vector-borne infection, including the elimination of breeding sites for sandflies and the prevention of bites with protective clothing [5].
