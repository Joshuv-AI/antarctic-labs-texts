Tumor-secreted AGR2 induces dendritic cell dysfunction and impairs immunotherapy efficacy in LKB1-deficient cancer
Authors: Yuxin Yao, Yuejun Luo, Zhanyu Wang, Enzhi Yin, Chengming Liu, Sufei Zheng, Xinfeng Wang, Xiaoya Tang, Nan Sun, Jie He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLKB1 (STK11)-deficient tumors exhibit an immunosuppressive microenvironment that limits the efficacy of immunotherapies such as anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) antibodies. However, the underlying mechanisms driving immune evasion remain unclear. Dendritic cells (DCs), especially conventional DC 1 (cDC1), play a crucial role in antigen presentation and CD8+ T-cell activation, yet their dysfunction in LKB1-deficient tumors has not been well characterized.MethodsTumor-intrinsic LKB1 deficiency was modeled by subcutaneous inoculation of CRISPR/Cas9-engineered Stk11-knockout tumor cell lines into syngeneic mice. DC infiltration and function were assessed through a series of flow cytometry-based in vivo and in vitro assays, including analyses of infiltration, migration, antigen uptake, maturation, and CD8+ T-cell priming. Naïve CD8+ T-cell activation by cDC1s was evaluated via adoptive transfer of CD45.1+ OT-I T cells. Secretome proteomics and functional rescue experiments identified anterior gradient 2 (AGR2) as a key immunosuppressive mediator, and arginase 1 (ARG1) was validated as its functional interactor through liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), co-immunoprecipitation, and imaging flow cytometry. Upstream regulation of AGR2 was investigated by AMPKα/FOXA1 silencing and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) quantitative PCR, revealing a tumor-intrinsic AMPKα–FOXA1–AGR2 axis driving DC dysfunction.ResultsLKB1-deficient tumors exhibited significantly reduced cDC1 infiltration, and cDC1s were functionally impaired in antigen uptake, migration, and naïve CD8+ T-cell priming. AGR2, a secreted protein transcriptionally upregulated via the tumor-intrinsic AMPKα–FOXA1 pathway, was identified as a key mediator of DC dysfunction. Mechanistically, AGR2 interacted with ARG1, stabilizing its expression and promoting ARG1 accumulation in DCs. Restoring DC infiltration and activity through FLT3L-driven expansion and tumor-antigen–specific DC supplementation significantly enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-1 treatment in LKB1-deficient lung adenocarcinoma models.ConclusionOur study identifies profound DC dysfunction—characterized by impaired antigen uptake, migration, and naïve CD8+ T-cell priming—as a key mechanism of immune evasion in LKB1-deficient tumors. This dysfunction is driven by tumor-secreted AGR2, which stabilizes ARG1 in DCs and suppresses CD8+ T-cell activation. Targeting the AMPKα–FOXA1–AGR2–ARG1 axis or restoring DC competence offers a promising strategy to enhance immunotherapy efficacy in LKB1-deficient subtype.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

LKB1 (STK11) is a tumor suppressor frequently inactivated in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), leading to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Dendritic cells (DCs), particularly the conventional type 1 subset (cDC1s), play a pivotal role in priming cytotoxic T cells and orchestrating antitumor immunity. However, the functional state of DCs in LKB1-deficient tumors, and how they contribute to immune evasion in this setting, remains poorly understood.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study demonstrates that LKB1-deficient tumors exhibit significantly reduced cDC1 infiltration and impair cDC1 function, including antigen uptake, migration, and naïve T-cell priming. We identify anterior gradient 2 (AGR2), a secreted protein upregulated via the AMPKα/FOXA1 pathway in LKB1-deficient tumors, as a key mediator of DC dysfunction. Mechanistically, AGR2 is internalized by DCs and binds to arginase 1 (ARG1), enhancing its protein stability and causing its accumulation. AGR2 also suppresses several immune-stimulatory genes in DCs, jointly contributing to impaired DC function. Therapeutic strategies that restore DC function, including tumor-antigen–loaded DC supplementation and FLT3L overexpression, improve the response to immune checkpoint blockade in LKB1-deficient LUAD tumors.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

This study uncovers a previously unrecognized mechanism of dendritic cell dysfunction in LKB1-deficient lung cancer and identifies the AGR2–ARG1 axis as a potential therapeutic target. By demonstrating that restoring DC function can overcome immune resistance, it provides a rationale for incorporating DC-based interventions into immunotherapy strategies for LKB1-deficient LUAD tumors. Moreover, AGR2 may serve as a biomarker or therapeutic target for optimizing immune responses and guiding the development of more effective treatments for LKB1-deficient cancers.

Background

In the last decade, immunotherapy became the first-line therapeutic strategy for late-stage lung adenocarcinoma, one of the most dangerous and prevalent cancers, but the efficacy of immunotherapy demonstrates significant intrinsic heterogeneity.1 Skoulidis et al have identified three distinct subtypes of KRAS-mutant lung adenocarcinoma, from which the STK11 loss of function group (KRAS-mutant LKB1 loss-of-function subtype (KL) group) exhibits unique biological characteristics and worse prognoses, particularly in the context of immunotherapy.2 The mechanisms underlying the poor response to immunotherapy in the KL group tumors warrant further elucidation.

Effective immunotherapy requires (1) efficient tumor antigen presentation, (2) effective priming of naïve T cells by functional antigen-presenting cells (APCs), and (3) a supportive tumor microenvironment.3 4 Given the poor CD8+ T-cell infiltration in LKB1-deficient tumors, we focused on the early steps of antigen-specific T-cell activation, particularly the role of APCs. Among APCs, dendritic cells (DCs) are key “full-time” antigen presenters. DCs are traditionally classified into several subtypes: conventional DC 1 (cDC1), conventional DC 2, plasmacytoid DC, and monocyte-derived DC.5 In the tumor periphery, DCs capture antigens, migrate to tumor-draining lymph nodes (tdLNs), and prime naïve T cells.6 cDC1s are especially critical for activating CD8+ T cells and sustaining their antitumor activity. Disruption of this process can impair immune responses and limit T-cell infiltration.

Tumor-secreted proteins serve as crucial mediators that enable cancer cells to adapt to and manipulate their surrounding microenvironment.7 Identifying secreted proteins involved in immune evasion provides potential prognostic markers and immunotherapeutic targets for cancer treatment. AGR2 (anterior gradient 2) is a member of the protein disulfide isomerase (PDI) family, primarily localized in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), where it plays a key role in protein folding and homeostasis.8 Its high expression in tumors is associated with progression and metastasis in several cancers, including lung, gastric, pancreatic, breast and prostate cancer.915 Unlike typical PDIs, AGR2 can also be secreted into the extracellular space, functioning in a non-cell-autonomous manner.16 On secretion, AGR2 interacts within the tumor microenvironment and modulates signaling pathways that facilitate tumor progression, metastasis, and immune suppression.1012 1619 Despite its known role in promoting tumor malignancy, the impact of tumor-derived extracellular AGR2 on the tumor immune microenvironment remains largely unexplored.

In our study, we comprehensively evaluated the functional state of DCs in KL lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) through a series of in vivo and in vitro experiments. Our findings reveal significant impairments in key DC functions, including antigen uptake, migration, and the priming of naïve CD8+ T cells. Supernatant proteomics analysis reveals a significant upregulation of secreted AGR2 in LKB1-deficient cancer cell lines. Mechanistically, LKB1 deficiency suppresses AMPKα phosphorylation, enhances the occupancy of FOXA1 at the AGR2 promoter, thereby increasing AGR2 expression. Reducing extracellular AGR2 effectively enhances DC function in LKB1-deficient settings. Furthermore, we demonstrate that tumor-derived AGR2 binds to arginase 1 (ARG1) in DCs, promoting its stabilization and accumulation. ARG1 has been reported to suppress DC-CD8+ T-cell activation by depleting arginine and generating harmful metabolites such as kynurenine.2022 Overall, this study provides a comprehensive characterization of DC dysfunction—particularly in cDC1 subsets—in KL LUAD and uncovers the AGR2-ARG1 axis as a key contributor to immunotherapy resistance by impairing DC function in priming CD8+ T cells.

Method

Cancer cell lines culture and infection

LUAD cell lines (A549, H460) were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI) 1640; mouse lines (Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC), Panc02) in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). STK11 overexpression and AGR2 knockdown were achieved via lentiviral transduction; STK11 knockout via CRISPR-Cas9. FOXA1 was silenced transiently with siRNAs. Lentiviruses were packaged in HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Stable lines were selected using puromycin (2 µg/mL) or blasticidin (10 µg/mL). Verification was done by western blot. Oligonucleotide sequences are listed in online supplemental file 5.

Primary cell generation and culture

Murine bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were generated from C57BL/6 femurs and cultured with 2 ng/mL granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) (PeproTech) and 200 ng/mL FLT3L (Bio X Cell). Maturation was induced using 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS). OT-I CD8+ T cells were isolated from OT-I mice (Shanghai Model Organisms Center) using naive Naïve CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL) and activated with anti-CD3 (2 µg/mL) and anti-CD28 (1 µg/mL) in medium supplemented with 100 U/mL interleukin (IL)-2. Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) from healthy donors were processed by Ficoll-Paque; CD14+ monocytes and CD8+ T cells were purified using CD14 and CD8 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotech). Monocytes were differentiated into immature DCs with 50 ng/mL GM-CSF and 20 ng/mL IL-4 (PeproTech).

Animal experiment

Tumor models were established in C57BL/6 mice (Beijing HFK Bioscience) with subcutaneous injection of 2–5×10⁶ tumor cells. Mice were randomized and treated with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) (200 µg, intraperitoneal (i.p.), days 1, 4, 7, 10). OT-I CD8+ T-cell transfer (5×10⁴ cells) was performed prior to tumor implantation. Tumor volume was calculated as (length×width²)/2. Mice were euthanized at endpoint using carbon dioxide inhalation, and tumors/lymph nodes were harvested. Inclusion and exclusion criteria for animals were established a priori. Animals were excluded if they failed to develop measurable tumors after injection. During data analysis, data points were excluded only if technical errors were identified. Investigators were blinded to group allocation throughout the study, including during treatment administration, outcome assessment, and data analysis. Group assignments were coded by an independent researcher who was not involved in the experiment.

Post-sacrifice tissue processing

Tumors were processed using the Tumor Dissociation Kit for Mouse (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, tumors were cut into small pieces and digested with the enzyme mix using the gentleMACS Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec) on the designated program. After digestion, red blood cells were lysed with ACK buffer (Sigma), and the tissue was filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension, which was then used for further analysis. Murine tumors were excised, rinsed in cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), blotted dry, and necrotic areas were removed. Tissue pieces (~0.25 g) were placed on nylon mesh in pre-weighed tubes and centrifuged at 68–2,700 g for 10 min at 4°C. The tumor interstitial fluid collected at the tube bottom was clarified at 10,000 g for 5 min, supplemented with protease inhibitors, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C until use.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry analysis was performed as previously described.23 Single-cell suspensions were stained sequentially with Zombie dye, Fc receptor blocker, and fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against surface markers at 4°C for 30 min. After washing and fixation (IC Fixation Buffer, eBioscience), cells were permeabilized (eBioscience Permeabilization Buffer) and stained for intracellular markers at 4°C for 30 min. TCF1/TCF7 staining used the True Nuclear Transcription Buffer Set. Samples were acquired on a BD LSRII or Cytek NL-CLC3000. Antibody details are listed in online supplemental file 6. The gating strategy for in vivo experiment is shown in online supplemental figure S2.

In vitro dendritic cell assays

Antigen uptake

Tumor cells were labeled with 1 nM CellTracer Violet (Thermo) and co-cultured with DCs.

Migration

Tumor cells were plated in 24-well plates; 2×10⁵ CD11c+ DCs were placed in 5 µm transwell inserts (Corning).

Naïve T-cell priming

CD8+ T cells and DCs were co-cultured in tumor-conditioned medium. Cytotoxicity (interferon (IFN)-γ, granzyme B, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α) and proliferation (CellTracer Violet) were assessed.

T-cell migration

DCs were exposed to tumor-conditioned medium in 24-well plates, and 2×10⁵ CD8+ T cells were added to 5 µm transwell inserts (Corning). Migrated CD3+CD8+ cells were counted.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription-quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNA-Quick Kit (ES Science, #RN001), and 2 µg was reverse transcribed (Tiangen, KR118). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (Tiangen, FP209) and 10 µM primers on a QuantStudio five system (Thermo) under standard cycling conditions (95°C 3 min; 40 cycles of 95°C 5 s, 60°C 15 s). Relative messenger RNA (mRNA) levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method, normalized to GAPDH. Three biological replicates were used. Primer sequences are listed in online supplemental file 4.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors (Thermo, #78425) and centrifuged at 13,000 g for 20 min at 4°C. Protein concentration was measured using the BCA assay (Thermo, #23227). Samples were mixed with 5×loading buffer, boiled at 99°C for 10 min, separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP) -conjugated secondary antibodies (1 hour, room temperature (RT)). Signals were visualized using ECL (Millipore, #WBULS0500) and detected with ImageQuant 350 (GE). Bulk tumor tissue protein was extracted by using T-PER Tissue Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo, #78510).

Immunofluorescence

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with methanol, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Primary antibodies: anti-AGR2 (Abcam, ab209224, 1:500); secondary: (CST, #4313, 1:1000). FOXA1 was stained using fluorescence-conjugated antibody (CST, #44957). Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Abcam, ab104139). Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (PerkinElmer UltraVIEW VoX Spinning Disk Confocal Microscope) and analyzed with ImageJ.

RNA sequencing analysis

BMDCs were treated with 10 µg/mL LPS (Sigma) for 24 hours and then collected. Then, 5×10⁶ BMDCs were seeded into a 6-well dish with 2 mL of conditioned medium collected from a 24-hour culture of tumor cell lines, or with 2 mL of RPMI 1640 containing 1 µg/mL of mouse IgG or mouse AGR2. After washing with precooled PBS, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Thermo) followed by standard protocol. Library construction and sequencing were performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (paired end, 150 bp). Raw sequencing data were quality-checked using FastQC, and reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10, GRCm38) using the STAR aligner (V.2.7.9a). Gene-level read counts were generated with featureCounts (Subread V.2.0.1). Differential expression analysis was conducted using edgeR (V.3.38.4) with a filtering threshold of |log₂ fold change|≥1 and false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction).

Proteomics analysis

Samples were ultrafiltered using 3 kDa Millipore tubes (14,000 g, 15 min), and retentates were buffered (8 M urea, 100 mM TEAB, pH 8.5), reduced with 1 M DTT (56°C, 1 hour), and alkylated with IAM (room temperature, dark, 1 hour). Protein concentration was assessed via Bradford assay. SDS-PAGE (12%) and Coomassie staining were used to check sample integrity. Proteins were digested with trypsin (37°C, 4 hours), followed by overnight CaCl₂ incubation. After acidification (formic acid, pH<3) and C18 desalting, peptides were lyophilized. For liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), 200 ng peptides were reconstituted and analyzed using a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system coupled to a Thermo Orbitrap Astral in DIA mode. Data were processed using DIA-NN (V.1.8.1). Filtering criteria included a precursor mass tolerance of 10 ppm, fragment mass tolerance of 0.02 Da, and a peptide/protein-level FDR threshold of 1%. Differential proteins were identified by fold change and significance (p<0.05, |log₂FC|>1).

ELISA

ELISA was performed using the Human AGR2 ELISA Kit (RedDot Biotech, Cat# RD-AGR2-Hu and Cat# RD-AGR2-Mu) to quantify AGR2 protein levels in cell culture supernatants and tumor interstitial fluid. After treatment, supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 1,000 g for 20 min to remove debris and stored at –80°C until analysis. All reagents and samples were brought to room temperature before use. Standards were prepared by serial dilution, and 100 µL of standards or samples were added to the pre-coated 96-well plate. Following incubation with Detection Reagent A and B, plates were washed and incubated with 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate. The reaction was stopped with Stop Solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Sample concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed using the SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (CST, #9003) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, quenched with 0.125 M glycine, and washed with ice-cold PBS containing protease inhibitors. Nuclei were isolated, chromatin was digested with micrococcal nuclease and lysed by sonication to obtain DNA fragments of 150–900 bp. Equal amounts of chromatin were incubated overnight at 4°C with specific antibodies, control IgG, or a positive control anti-histone H3 antibody, followed by capture with Protein G magnetic beads and sequential low-salt and high-salt washes. Chromatin was eluted, cross-links were reversed at 65°C, and DNA was purified using spin columns. qPCR was performed with locus-specific primers, and results were expressed as percentage of input.

Public single-cell data analysis

Raw single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data (GSE180963) were obtained from GEO. Preprocessing and downstream analysis were conducted using Seurat V.3.1.5. Gene expression matrices were normalized with NormalizeData and ScaleData (unique molecular identifier (UMI) scaled to 10,000, log-transformed). The top 200 highly variable genes were selected for principal component analysis (PCA), and the first 20 principal components (PCs) were used for Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Unsupervised clustering was performed using FindClusters (resolution=0.06), yielding 10 clusters. Marker genes were identified using FindAllMarkers (Wilcoxon test) and annotated via CellMarker and literature. Differentially expressed genes were subjected to pathway enrichment analysis to identify relevant biological processes.

Public data analysis

Gene expression profiles of tumor and normal tissues were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and GTEx datasets. Differential expression analysis was performed using GEPIA2 (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn), which integrates TCGA and GTEx expression data. Statistical significance was assessed using one-way analysis of variance as implemented in GEPIA2. Significantly differentially expressed genes were defined by a cut-off of |log₂ fold change|≥1 and q value<0.01 (adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method). Gene expression values were log₂-transformed transcripts per million (TPM) for visualization purposes. Cox regression was performed with AGR2 expression as a continuous variable. Restricted cubic splines with four knots (5th, 35th, 65th, 95th percentiles) were used to model non-linear effects, and Akaike information criterion (AIC) was applied for model selection. Genome-wide Pearson correlation coefficients with AGR2 were calculated using log2(TPM+1) expression values from TCGA-LUAD, and STK11 was ranked among all genes accordingly. AGR2 risk association data were acquired from the Cancer Immunology Data Engine (CIDE) (https://cide.ccr.cancer.gov/).

Immunoprecipitation

BMDC lysates were pre-cleared with protein A/G beads at 4°C for 1 hour. Recombinant AGR2 (CUSABIO, #CSB-EP001458MO) was incubated overnight at 4°C with BMDC lysates. Anti-MYC antibody (CST, #2278, 1:100) or control IgG (CST, #2729) was then added and rotated overnight at 4°C. Protein A/G beads (25 µL) were added and incubated at room temperature for 1 hour. Beads were washed with lysis buffer three times and pure water before SDS-PAGE or LC-MS/MS. For western blot, MYC-tagged proteins were immunoprecipitated (CST, #2276), and ARG1 was detected using anti-ARG1 (CST, #93668). A light chain-specific anti-rabbit secondary antibody (CST, #45262) was used to reduce IgG heavy chain interference.

LC-MS/MS

Samples stored at –80°C were mixed with 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer and magnetic beads in loading buffer, then heated at 95°C for 15 min. Protein levels were estimated by silver staining against a HeLa standard. On-bead digestion was performed with trypsin (20 ng/µL, overnight), followed by DTT reduction (5 mM, 37°C, 60 min) and IAM alkylation (11 mM, RT, dark, 45 min). A second trypsin incubation (10 ng/µL, 4 hours) was done before peptide acidification and C18 desalting. Peptides were separated on a NanoElute UHPLC system (6–80% B gradient, 20 min, 500 nL/min) and analyzed using a timsTOF Pro 2 in dia-PASEF mode. MS1 range: 300–1,500 m/z; MS/MS: 400–850 m/z in 7 m/z windows. Protein quantification was based on relative intensity. Raw data were analyzed using DIA-NN (V.1.8) against the UniProt Mus musculus database. Search parameters included trypsin digestion (one missed cleavage), carbamidomethylation on cysteine as a fixed modification, and a 1% FDR threshold at precursor and protein levels. Differential proteins were defined as those with fold change >1.5 or <1/1.5 and unadjusted p<0.05.

Image flow cytometry

BMDCs were prepared as previously described. Recombinant AGR2 (0.5 µg/mL) was added to mature BMDCs for 24 hours. Cells were washed, fixed, and permeabilized (eBioscience buffers), then stained with mouse anti-MYC and rabbit anti-ARG1 antibodies in 5% BSA/PBS overnight at 4°C. After washing, secondary antibodies (CST, #4313, #4408; 1:1000) were added for 30 min in the dark. Imaging flow cytometry was performed using the Amnis ImageStream X Mark II, and data were analyzed with IDEAS software for morphology and fluorescence intensity.

Statistical analysis and graphic generation

All data presented in this paper were processed according to the corresponding figure legends. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.9.0. Differences were considered statistically significant at p<0.05. Differential expression analysis, heatmap generation, and volcano plot visualization based on proteomics, RNA-seq, and LC-MS/MS data were performed using R V.4.2.1. Asterisks indicate statistical significance: *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, and ****p<0.0001. All illustrative figures are created in https://BioRender.com. We used the ARRIVE reporting guideline to draft this manuscript, and the ARRIVE reporting checklist when editing, included in ARRIVE-checklist.24 All animals and data points were included; no exclusions were made. Functional assays and ELISA were independently repeated two times, and other molecular experiments were repeated three times with similar results. For all figures, “n” indicates the number of biological samples included in the panels.

Results

KL tumors exhibit dendritic cell dysfunction and resistance to PD-1 blockade

To investigate the impact of LKB1 deficiency on the tumor immune microenvironment, we analyzed scRNA-seq on tumors from KrasG12D/+ mice with conditional knockout of Stk11 (GSE180963). UMAP visualization is shown in figure 1A. Gene Ontology biological process enrichment analysis indicated that the downregulated genes in DC subsets from the KL group were primarily involved in antigen processing, presentation, and immune activation pathways (figure 1B). Similarly, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment of downregulated gene in the KL group analysis further confirmed the suppression of key immune-related pathways in DCs from the KL group compared with the K group (figure 1C). These data suggest that DC dysfunction in LKB1-deficient tumors might underlie the observed immune evasion and resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy. To test this hypothesis, we established CRISPR/Cas9-mediated Stk11 knockout models in LLC and Panc02 cell lines. Tumor growth curves showed that LKB1-deficient tumors continued to grow rapidly despite treatment, indicating resistance to immunotherapy (figure 1A,B and D-G). Consistently, tumor volume and weight measurements further confirmed that tumors in the KL group were significantly larger than those in the K group, even in the presence of anti-PD-1 therapy (figure 1E,F and H,I). Additionally, flow cytometry analysis of tumors suggests that immunotherapy resistance is more likely driven by a paucity of CD8+ T cells (figure 1J,K) rather than a deficiency in their cytotoxic or memory function, as indicated by granzyme B, TNF-α, IFN-γ and CD44 expression (online supplemental figure S1C-I). Since DCs play a crucial role in priming naïve T cells in the tdLNs and recruiting them to the tumor site, we hypothesized that their dysfunction may underlie the reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration observed in LKB1-deficient tumors, ultimately leading to impaired immune responses and resistance to immunotherapy.

Reduced cDC1 infiltration and impaired antigen presentation in the LKB1-deficient tumor microenvironment

To further investigate DC dysfunction in antigen presentation, we designed a series of in vivo and in vitro experiments to assess different stages of the process. First, we analyzed cDC1 infiltration in two subcutaneous tumor models, LLC and Panc02. As key cross-priming cells, cDC1s activate naïve CD8+ T cells, promote their expansion and tumor migration, and sustain cytotoxic activity.5 25 The percentage of cDC1 was significantly reduced in LKB1-deficient tumors in both models (figure 2A,B and online supplemental figure S2A), indicating impaired infiltration.

Next, we assessed antigen uptake by overexpressing ZsGreen in tumor cell lines, as this degradation-resistant fluorescent protein is engulfed by DCs along with tumor antigens (figure 2C).26 ZsGreen+ cDC1s were significantly reduced, reflecting impaired antigen uptake (figure 2D). Additionally, the number of antigen-loaded cDC1 in the tdLNs was also reduced (figure 2E). However, the overall proportion of cDC1 in the tdLNs remained unchanged between groups (figure 2F), suggesting migration occurred but was less efficient for antigen-loaded cDC1s.

To explore migration defects, we examined CCR7 expression, a key chemokine receptor upregulated during activation. CCR7 levels remained unchanged in vitro and vivo (online supplemental figure S1J and K), suggesting that other regulatory mechanisms may influence cDC1 trafficking.

Following antigen uptake, cDC1 should undergo activation and maturation into a functional state. To assess cDC1 maturation, we analyzed activation markers both in vitro (figure 2G) and in vivo (figure 2H–I). However, the expression of CD86 and CD80, key activation markers, remained largely unchanged. Interestingly, I-A/I-E, a critical marker of DC maturation and antigen presentation, was significantly downregulated in vivo (figure 2) but remained unchanged in vitro, suggesting tumor-specific microenvironmental influences.

In vitro, BMDCs co-cultured with tumor cells confirmed reduced antigen uptake (CellTracer Violet+ BMDCs; figure 1J). Migration assays also showed fewer BMDCs reaching the bottom chamber in response to LKB1-deficient tumor cells, consistent with in vivo findings (figure 2K).

Together, these results indicate that cDC1 infiltration, antigen uptake, maturation, and migration are all compromised in the LKB1-deficient tumor microenvironment.

Impaired cDC1-mediated CD8+ T-cell priming in LKB1-deficient tumors

To further investigate the role of cDC1 in naïve CD8+ T-cell priming, we adoptively transferred CD45.1+ OT-I CD8+ T cells into C57BL/6 CD45.2+ mice bearing LLC-OVA or Panc02-OVA tumor cells (figure 3A, online supplemental figure S2B). In LKB1-deficient tumors, the frequency of TCF1+TIM3⁻ OT-I CD8+ T cells, rather than TCF1⁻TIM3+OT-I CD8+ T cells, was significantly decreased in STK11-deficient tumors compared with controls, suggesting impaired formation or persistence of stem-like progenitor CD8+ T cells (figure 3B,C). Importantly, surface presentation of SIINFEKL-H-2Kᵇ complexes on tumor cells and cDC1s was comparable between groups, indicating that antigen processing and presentation were not affected by STK11 loss (figure 3D). Meanwhile, OT-I CD8+ T cells were significantly reduced in both tdLNs and tumors (figure 3E,F), suggesting impaired priming by cDC1 and limited expansion of tumor-specific T cells at this early checkpoint. Despite reduced OT-I CD8+ T-cell numbers, their cytotoxic function remained intact in the LKB1-deficient tumor microenvironment, with granzyme B and TNF-α expression sustained or increased (online supplemental figure S1L and M). This indicates that the primary defect lies in T-cell priming and expansion, rather than effector function after tumor infiltration.

To further explore cDC1 dysfunction, we used an in vitro co-culture model (figure 3G). Together, these results demonstrate that cDC1 within the LKB1-deficient tumors fail to effectively prime CD8+ T cells in tdLNs, resulting in insufficient tumor-specific T-cell generation. The reduced T-cell frequency in tumors likely reflects both upstream priming failure and inadequate local support. These defects collectively promote immune evasion in LKB1-deficient tumors, highlighting the crucial role of cDC1 in orchestrating effective CD8+ T-cell responses against tumors.

Ameliorating dendritic cell function enhances immunotherapy in LKB1-deficient LUAD

To enhance immune responses in LKB1-deficient LUAD, we focused on improving DC function in combination with anti-PD-1 therapy, a key mediator of T-cell activation. We designed two combination strategies with PD-1 blockade to promote the efficacy of immunotherapy.

First, we used Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 ligand (FLT3L), a growth factor known to enhance DC differentiation and function, particularly increasing the infiltration of cDC1s27 (figure 4A). FLT3L was overexpressed in engineered cell lines, which were subsequently administered to tumor-bearing mice in combination with anti-PD-1 therapy. This intervention significantly slowed tumor progression and improved the therapeutic efficacy of PD-1 blockade (figure 4B–E). FLT3L overexpression led to increased infiltration of cDC1s and CD8+ T cells in LKB1-deficient tumors, suggesting enhanced antitumor immune activation (figure 4F,G).

Second, we directly injected antigen-specific DCs (OVA-loaded) into tumor-bearing mice carrying subcutaneous tumors derived from LKB1-deficient, OVA-overexpressing cell lines, in combination with anti-PD-1 therapy (figure 4H). Together, these findings suggest that enhancing DC function is a promising strategy to potentiate immune responses and improve the effectiveness of immunotherapy in LKB1-deficient LUAD, offering a potential means to overcome immune suppression in this subtype.

Upregulated extracellular AGR2 suppresses dendritic cell function in the LKB1-deficient LUAD

To investigate potential factors contributing to DC dysfunction in the LKB1-deficient tumor microenvironment, we used A549 and H460 LUAD cell lines with endogenous STK11 mutations. After overexpressing STK11, we analyzed their secretome profiles to uncover STK11-regulated immunomodulatory proteins (figure 5A; see online supplemental file 1). A heatmap of differentially secreted proteins highlighted AGR2 as one of the most significantly upregulated in the LKB1-deficient group (figure 5B). Among these, we focused on proteins with potential immunomodulatory functions. Using TCGA-LUAD RNA-seq data, we examined correlation between the top five upregulated secreted proteins and immune cell infiltration. Notably, AGR2, an ER-resident protein with emerging extracellular roles in cancer,16 showed a strong negative correlation with DC infiltration across multiple algorithms (figure 5C). Besides its strong negative correlation with DC infiltration, AGR2 was further prioritized based on the following considerations: (1) although several studies have reported its secretory nature, its extracellular function remains largely undefined121517 28; and (2) both its intracellular and extracellular activities have been associated with pro-oncogenic effects, and existing evidence suggests an immunosuppressive role, warranting further investigation into its impact on tumor–immune interactions.1216 2830 Expression analysis confirmed AGR2 upregulation in LKB1-deficient cells (figure 5D). ELISA further showed increased AGR2 secretion in A549 and H460 cells, which was reduced by STK11 overexpression or AGR2 knockdown (figure 5E). To validate these findings in vivo, we examined syngeneic tumors and tumor interstitial fluid (TIF). Immunoblotting of bulk tumor lysates revealed elevated total AGR2 in STK11-deficient tumors (online supplemental figure S1N), indicating increased overall AGR2 abundance within tumor tissues. Consistently, ELISA of matched TIF demonstrated much higher secreted AGR2 levels (online supplemental figure S1O). These results demonstrate that STK11 loss enhances both total and secreted AGR2 in the tumor microenvironment.

We next assessed AGR2 expression patterns and clinical relevance. AGR2 is elevated in various cancers, including breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), LUAD, ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (OV) and other tumor types (online supplemental figure S3A). While dichotomized analysis showed that high AGR2 expression was associated with poor survival (p=0.02, (online supplemental figure S3B), spline modeling revealed a J-shaped relationship, suggesting that both low and high expression levels may be linked to increased risk (online supplemental figure S3C and D). Meanwhile, analysis of the newly released CIDE showed that AGR2 expression was significantly associated with increased risk in immunotherapy-treated cohorts across pan-cancers (p=1.95×10⁻⁵), consistent with an immunosuppressive role of AGR2 (online supplemental figure S3E).31 It is also inversely correlated with STK11 expression in TCGA-LUAD (R=−0.1, p=0.028; online supplemental figure S3F), suggesting regulatory interplay. However, STK11 ranks 3247th among all protein-coding genes based on genome-wide Pearson correlation with AGR2 (online supplemental figure S3G), highlighting that the correlation, while statistically significant, may not be biologically dominant.

To validate AGR2’s functional impact, we performed co-culture experiments using PBMC-derived DCs. Stable AGR2 knockdown cell lines were generated in A549-vehicle and H460-vehicle backgrounds (online supplemental figure S3H). We tested five conditions: vehicle control (Veh), STK11-overexpressing (STK11-OE), AGR2 knockdown in vehicle control (Veh-shAGR2), AGR2 knockdown in vehicle control with recombinant AGR2 (rAGR2) supplementation (Veh-shAGR2+rAGR2), and STK11-OE with rAGR2 supplementation. AGR2 knockdown significantly enhanced DC-mediated phagocytosis of tumor cells (figure 5F,G) and DC migration (figure 5H,I), while rAGR2 reversed these effects. Moreover, in DC–T cell co-culture systems conducted in tumor cell supernatants, AGR2 knockdown restored granzyme B production (figure 5J,K) and proliferation (figure 5L) of T cells, both suppressed by rAGR2. Consistently, addition of rAGR2 to STK11-OE tumor supernatants also diminished DC-mediated T-cell responses. Together, these results demonstrate that tumor-derived extracellular AGR2 suppresses DC function and downstream T-cell activation, contributing to immune evasion in the LKB1-deficient tumor.

To explore the relationship between LKB1 and AGR2, we confirmed that AGR2 mRNA expression was upregulated in LKB1-deficiency cell lines, suggesting transcriptional regulation. Transient FOXA1 knockdown in A549 and H460 cells significantly reduced AGR2 mRNA and protein levels (figure 6A–D and online supplemental figure S3I), implicating FOXA1 in AGR2 transcriptional control. Based on publicly available ChIP-seq data of FOXA1 in A549 cells (online supplemental figure S3J), which revealed two enrichment peaks within the AGR2 locus, we designed ChIP-qPCR primers targeting these sites (PP1 and PP2) (figure 6E).33 The results demonstrated that FOXA1 directly binds to both regions (figure 6F,G).

Given that LKB1 is known to activate AMPKα via phosphorylation, we hypothesized that AMPKα might modulate FOXA1/AGR2 expression. Indeed, pharmacologic inhibition with compound C or genetic knockdown of AMPKα1 (siPRKAA1) enhanced FOXA1 occupancy at AGR2 promoter peaks (figure 6H,I). Given the possibility that extracellular AGR2 may be internalized through endocytosis, we also screened for cytoplasmic proteins. Among the potential AGR2 targets, we identified ARG1, a key marker protein of myeloid cells, including DCs, known for its role in T-cell suppression202234 35 (figure 7B; see online supplemental file 2). To evaluate the intracellular effect of AGR2, imaging flow cytometry was performed, revealing increased intracellular ARG1 accumulation in BMDCs following AGR2 treatment compared with IgG control (figure 7D). To assess whether this effect was post-transcriptional, BMDCs were treated with either cycloheximide (CHX) to block protein synthesis or actinomycin D (ACTD) to inhibit transcription, followed by AGR2 or IgG treatment. Under ACTD treatment, ARG1 expression declined similarly in both groups, suggesting minimal transcriptional regulation; however, under CHX treatment, AGR2 significantly delayed the reduction of ARG1 protein levels compared with IgG, indicating that AGR2 enhances ARG1 stability at the post-translational level (figure 7E).

To determine whether AGR2-mediated DC–T cell suppression depends on ARG1, we added rAGR2 and the ARG1 inhibitor CB-1158 to DC–T cell co-cultures in STK11-OE tumor supernatants. CB-1158 reversed AGR2-mediated suppressive effects, as shown by increased expression of granzyme B in CD8+ T cells (figure 7F,G) and enhanced CD8+ T-cell proliferation (figure 7H). These results demonstrate that ARG1 is a critical mediator of AGR2-induced DC dysfunction in priming T-cell responses. We also performed RNA-seq on BMDCs treated with either recombinant AGR2 or IgG for 24 hours. Typical downregulated genes involved with immune-regulated function in the AGR2 group were displayed (figure 7; see online supplemental file 3). Notably, chemokines and receptors related to DC migration, including Ccr2, Ccl7, Ccl17, Ccl22, and Ccl6, were downregulated after AGR2 treatment.3639 Genes such as Ccr2, Cx3cr1 and Itgae, which are involved in inflammatory signaling and antigen presentation, were also reduced.36 40 41 In addition, transcriptional regulators including FoxM1 and Irf6, as well as several cell cycle-associated genes such as Mki67, Ccnf and Cdk1 were downregulated, indicating suppressed activation and proliferative capacity of BMDCs.42 43 These findings suggest that AGR2 not only stabilizes ARG1 post-translationally but also reprograms the DC transcriptome toward a suppressed state.

Together, these data support a model in which tumor-derived AGR2 impairs DC function by promoting ARG1 accumulation and transcriptional suppression of immune-stimulatory pathways (figure 7J).

Discussion

The KL subtype of LUAD is known for a marked decrease in CD8+ T-cell infiltration and resistance to immunotherapy.44 DCs are essential for initiating adaptive immunity by processing and presenting antigens to T cells. Our study confirms that reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration in KL tumors is partly due to impaired DC function. Specifically, our findings indicate that in the KL environment, the capacity of cDC1s to uptake, migrate, and prime naïve T cells is compromised. Although dysfunction in uptake and migration occurs prior to naïve T cells contact, adoptive transfer of naïve CD8+ T cells ensures sufficient naïve T cells for DCs to prime, highlighting an intrinsic priming defect in DCs in the tdLNs. Moreover, the greater reduction of OT-I CD8+ T cells in tumors than in tdLNs suggests that, beyond impaired priming, the local tumor milieu fails to support tumor-specific T cells. Together, our findings underscore the role of DC dysfunction in mediating immunotherapy resistance in LKB1-deficient LUAD.

DC function has long been recognized as a critical factor in antitumor immunity.5 25 45 46 To address the immunotherapy resistance in LKB1-deficient tumors from the perspective of DCs, we explored strategies aimed at improving DC infiltration and function, including enhancing DC expansion with FLT3L and tumor-antigen-specific DC supplementation. FLT3L, a potent activator of DCs, has shown promise in clinical trials by enhancing intratumoral DC infiltration and antitumor immune responses.4750 Its combination with immunotherapy may improve outcomes in LKB1-deficient cancers. Delivering functional, antigen-loaded DCs helps overcome endogenous DC defects and enhance immune activation. This approach is conceptually similar to DC vaccines that boost tumor-specific T-cell responses and have shown promising effects in preclinical settings.5153

We also found that AGR2 is secreted by STK11-deficient cells and contributes to DC dysfunction. Elevated extracellular AGR2 impairs DC function and may serve as a biomarker for immune resistance.54 A recent study further showed that secreted AGR2 can be internalized by macrophages and suppress their antiviral responses by interfering with innate immune transcriptional programs. This supports a broader immunosuppressive role of extracellular AGR2 across myeloid cells.29 A similar immunosuppressive role was reported in pancreatic cancer, where secreted AGR2 activated cancer-associated fibroblasts to promote desmoplasia and immune exclusion via a reciprocal AGR2–IGF1 signaling loop.28 Another study demonstrated that targeting extracellular AGR2 using a bispecific antibody against AGR2 and PD-1 effectively restored T-cell cytotoxicity and suppressed tumor growth in tumors with high AGR2 expression, reinforcing the potential of AGR2 as a tractable therapeutic target.55 Our study further complements these findings by elucidating the role of secreted AGR2 in impairing DC function within the tumor microenvironment. AGR2 has been reported to be detectable in patient serum and plasma by ELISA,56 and elevated circulating levels have been strongly associated with poor prognosis across multiple malignancies (including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)), 5560 highlighting its clinical translational value as an immune-related prognostic biomarker.

Beyond its secreted form, intracellular AGR2 has been implicated in multiple oncogenic processes. It regulates ER homeostasis, and AGR2 loss induces ER stress-mediated apoptosis, a condition known to alter cytokine production and shape an immune-repressive milieu.61 62 Moreover, AGR2 is both induced by and sustains PGE2–AKT signaling, a pathway well recognized for dampening antitumor immunity.63 Interactome analyses also reveal that AGR2 associates with diverse client proteins, including chaperones, transcriptional regulators, and signaling intermediates, thereby integrating stress responses with oncogenic signaling.11 64 65 Through these mechanisms, intracellular AGR2 could cooperate with extracellular AGR2 to reinforce an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

Mechanistically, AGR2 binds and stabilizes ARG1 in DCs, a known immunosuppressive enzyme that depletes arginine, which is essential for T-cell activation. ARG1 is recognized as a suppression marker for myeloid cells (including DCs) in antitumor immunity.21 22 Prior studies suggest ARG1 and IDO1 can act sequentially to deplete key amino acids and induce immunosuppression via kynurenine production.20 Beyond DCs, ARG1 also plays central roles in the immunosuppressive activity of other myeloid subsets. For example, Veglia et al demonstrated that both polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs) and monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs) rely on arginase-driven arginine depletion and related metabolic pathways to inhibit T-cell function.66 These findings support the rationale that targeting ARG1 in various myeloid-derived populations may provide a combinatorial strategy to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy. In line with this rationale, our results indicate that tumor-derived AGR2 impairs DC function through ARG1 stabilization, and that pharmacological inhibition of ARG1 with CB-1158 effectively reverses AGR2-mediated suppression. CB-1158 is an orally available ARG1 inhibitor that has been reported to enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade.67 Thus, our study provided a potential combinatorial strategy targeting the AGR2-ARG1 axis to restore antitumor immunity in LKB1-deficient cancer.

Mechanistically, FOXA1 transcriptionally regulated AGR2 expression, consistent with prior findings.15 Meanwhile, FOXA1 was found to occupy two distinct binding sites: one upstream (PP2) and one downstream (PP1) of the transcription start site, which suggests that FOXA1 may impact both transcription initiation and post-initiation events. Interestingly, we found that pharmacologic inhibition with compound C markedly increased FOXA1 expression in an STK11-dependent manner, whereas genetic knockdown of AMPKα did not. Nevertheless, both interventions elevated AGR2 expression, consistent with the notion that AMPKα primarily regulates AGR2 by modulating FOXA1 binding activity at the AGR2 promoter rather than altering FOXA1 abundance. This is in line with previous studies demonstrating that AMPK can influence transcription factor–DNA interactions independently of changes in transcription factor expression.68 69 These findings also suggest that compound C exerts additional effects on FOXA1 expression beyond AMPKα inhibition, highlighting that multiple layers of AMPK regulation—including phosphorylation-dependent activity and noncanonical drug effects—converge on the FOXA1–AGR2 axis. FOXA1 knockdown in the vehicle context led to less AGR2 suppression than LKB1 overexpression, indicating the involvement of additional regulatory mechanisms. Moreover, AMPKα is well-known for its role in energy metabolism, and previous studies have shown that metformin treatment or calorie restriction can enhance AMPKα phosphorylation and activity.70 71 These findings highlight a promising clinical strategy to combine metformin or calorie restriction to restore DC function in patients with STK11-deficient tumors.

In summary, our study uncovers a previously unrecognized mechanism by which tumor-intrinsic LKB1 deficiency impairs DC function and promotes immune evasion. Tumor-derived AGR2, transcriptionally upregulated via the AMPKα–FOXA1 axis, drives immunosuppression by binding and stabilizing ARG1 in DCs. This interaction impairs DC function and CD8+ T-cell activation, contributing to a “cold” tumor microenvironment. Notably, restoring DC activity through FLT3L administration or antigen-specific DC supplementation markedly improved the efficacy of PD-1 blockade in LKB1-deficient tumors. These findings highlight the AMPKα–FOXA1–AGR2 signaling pathway in tumor cells and its downstream effect on DC-intrinsic ARG1 stabilization, as a key intercellular axis driving immune resistance. Targeting this pathway or boosting DC function may offer a rational strategy to overcome the immunosuppressive barrier in this clinically challenging tumor subtype.
