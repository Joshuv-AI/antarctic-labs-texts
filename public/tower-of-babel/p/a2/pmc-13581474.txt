Prevalence of Periodontitis in Africa: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis of Epidemiological Studies Up to 2025
Authors: Abdoulaziz Diarra, Hamoun Sabri, Estelle Flore Bandre, Estelle Noëla Hoho Youl, Kevimy Agossa, Olivier Huck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo provide updated and comprehensive estimates of the changes in prevalence of periodontitis in Africa, based on epidemiological data up to 2025.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted through MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science and Google Scholar. Meta‐analyses were performed to estimate the prevalence of periodontitis across population subgroups and diagnostic criteria.ResultsTwenty‐three studies met the inclusion criteria. Among children (< 10 years), prevalence was 1.9% (95% CI: 0.9%–3.9%; I 2 = 77.7%) and among adolescents (10–19 years), it was 11.2% (95% CI: 4.7%–24.3%; I 2 = 98.1%). In young adults (20–25 years), prevalence was 12.8% (95% CI: 2.8%–42.8%; I 2 = 98.9%), in adults (25–49 years), 20.0% (95% CI: 11.9%–31.7%; I 2 = 99.1%) and in older adults (≥ 50 years) 23.1% (95% CI: 12.2%–39.3%). Substantial variation was observed across diagnostic definitions and thresholds. Geographic region and urban versus rural origin emerged as significant modifiers of prevalence.ConclusionEpidemiological studies on periodontitis in Africa often overrepresent younger populations and rely on non‐confident diagnostic criteria, likely underestimating true prevalence. Nevertheless, updated estimates suggest a concerning burden among younger age groups. More comprehensive research is needed to enhance the accuracy of prevalence estimates of periodontitis in Africa.

Introduction

The distribution of periodontitis varies across geographic regions, with differences attributed to a combination of individual factors, environmental conditions and health system determinants, including access to dental care and treatment regimens (Albandar 2024; Liang et al. 2024; Steele et al. 2015; Borrell and Crawford 2012; Nibali et al. 2019). In 2021, 1.07 billion individuals worldwide were affected by severe periodontitis (95% CI: 896.55–1234.84 million).

Periodontitis is more prevalent in Africa compared to other regions of the world (Bouziane et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2022). This disparity is attributed to suboptimal oral hygiene practices, limited access to care and genetic susceptibility (Borrell and Crawford 2012; Nibali et al. 2019).

Africa, which comprises more than 15% of the global population, is characterised by pronounced disparities in wealth, living conditions and access to health care (Karamagi et al. 2024; Nachega et al. 2012). The prevalence of periodontitis has been reported using heterogeneous methodologies and definitions over time (Ke et al. 2023; Savage et al. 2009), leading to considerable variations in estimates (Holtfreter et al. 2015; Ke et al. 2023; Savage et al. 2009). This systematic review aims (i) to provide a comprehensive, updated estimate of the prevalence of periodontitis in Africa, based on epidemiological data up to 2025. The secondary objectives are (ii) to compare prevalence estimates based on different case definitions and (iii) to explore the potential influence of region, age and sex on these estimates. The goal is to present context‐specific evidence on the epidemiology of periodontitis to inform practice and public health initiatives.

Materials and Methods

Protocol Registration and Reporting Format

This systematic review (SR) adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Page et al. 2021) (Appendix S1 for PRISMA checklist). The study protocol has been registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF) (registration DOI: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/TXNJY).

Focused Research Question and Eligibility Criteria

The focused question was, ‘What is the change of prevalence of periodontitis in epidemiological studies conducted in Africa up to 2025?’

Eligibility criteria were established using the CoCoPop framework (Munn et al. 2015; Munn et al. 2018) as recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute for reviews of prevalence and incidence (Munn et al. 2015, 2018). Information Sources and Search Strategy

An electronic search without language restrictions was conducted to identify eligible studies published up to April 2025. MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, Scopus, Web of Science and Google Scholar databases were searched. A manual search was also conducted using bibliographic references of selected articles and previous reviews on periodontal diseases in Africa and global epidemiology. A search algorithm was developed for MEDLINE and adapted for other databases (Appendix S2).

Article Selection Process

Two reviewers (A.D. and K.A.) independently screened the titles and abstracts from identified articles. Full texts were reviewed for studies that potentially met the inclusion criteria or when abstracts lacked sufficient data. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (O.H.).

Data Extraction

Data extraction was carried out by two independent reviewers (A.D. and E.F.B.) using a standardised form. Case definitions were categorised as ‘confident’ or ‘non‐confident’ based on previous studies (Albandar 2014; Muñoz Aguilera et al. 2020; Trindade et al. 2023) (Appendix S3).

Methodological Quality and Risk of Bias Assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed by two independent reviewers (A.D. and E.F.B.) using the ‘Assessing Risk of Bias in Population‐based Prevalence Studies’ tool (Hoy et al. 2012). Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (K.A.).

Data Synthesis and Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and RStudio version 2024.04.2 + 757 (Posit Software, Boston, MA), employing the meta, metafor and dmetar packages. Description of the statistical methods used is presented in the Appendix S5.

Results

Search Results and Study Selection

The initial database search identified a total of 2650 records and 3 references from citation search and grey literature. After removing duplicates and following title and abstract screening, 76 articles underwent full‐text review, and 23 articles were selected for qualitative and quantitative analysis (Figure 1 and Appendix S4).

Flowchart showing included studies selection according to PRISMA guidelines.

Study Characteristics

All included studies were cross‐sectional, including 45,974 participants from 13 African countries. Five studies were conducted in Southern Africa (Domingos et al. 2022; Frencken et al. 1999; Lembariti et al. 1988; Mumghamba et al. 1996; Mumghamba et al. 1995), five studies from Eastern Africa (Albandar et al. 2002; Elamin et al. 2010; Khalifa et al. 2012; Olsson 1978; Tefera and Bekele 2020), nine studies from Western Africa (Adegbembo and El‐Nadeef 1995; Braimoh and Alade 2019; Clauss et al. 2021; Hewlett et al. 2022; Jordan et al. 2011; Okeigbemen et al. 2012; Sheiham 1968; Tobin and Ajayi 2017; Varenne et al. 2004) and four studies from North Africa (Abou El Fadl et al. 2021; Kissa et al. 2022; Kissa et al. 2016; Peeran et al. 2013) (Figure 2). The majority of studies (21/23, 91.3%) used only non‐confident case definitions for periodontitis (Abou El Fadl et al. 2021; Adegbembo and El‐Nadeef 1995; Albandar et al. 2002; Braimoh and Alade 2019; Clauss et al. 2021; Domingos et al. 2022; Elamin et al. 2010; Frencken et al. 1999; Jordan et al. 2011; Khalifa et al. 2012; Kissa et al. 2016; Lembariti et al. 1988; Mumghamba et al. 1996; Mumghamba et al. 1995; Peeran et al. 2013; Tefera and Bekele 2020; Okeigbemen et al. 2012; Olsson 1978; Sheiham 1968; Tobin and Ajayi 2017; Varenne et al. 2004). One study conducted in Ghana (Hewlett et al. 2022) used CDC/AAP 2012 confident periodontal case definitions, and another one conducted in Morocco (Kissa et al. 2022) used both non‐confident and confident periodontal case definitions (Adegbembo and El‐Nadeef 1995; Albandar 2014 and EFP/AAP 2018 case definitions, respectively). More than half of the studies (52.2%) conducted full‐mouth examinations for periodontal diagnosis (Albandar et al. 2002; Clauss et al. 2021; Elamin et al. 2010; Frencken et al. 1999; Hewlett et al. 2022; Khalifa et al. 2012; Kissa et al. 2016; Lembariti et al. 1988; Olsson 1978; Sheiham 1968; Tefera and Bekele 2020) (Table 1).

Meta‐Analysis on Prevalence of Periodontitis

Analyses were restricted to studies published from 2000 onwards. Sensitivity analyses incorporating studies published before 2000 are presented in Appendix S7 (Figures S1–S5; Table S1).

To align with the terminology used in the literature and account for variations in disease classification, prevalence of what was previously termed aggressive periodontitis—now classified as Grade C periodontitis—was evaluated separately. Sensitivity analyses assessing the robustness of these estimates are presented in Appendix S8.

Prevalence of Periodontitis by Age Group

Children (< 10 Years)

Six prevalence estimates from four studies (n = 2637) contributed to the analysis. The pooled prevalence of periodontitis was 1.9% (95% CI: 0.9%–3.9%; I 2 = 77.7%). Prevalence varied by diagnostic threshold: studies using CPI score 3 reported 3.9%, while those using CPI score 4 reported 0.6% (Figure 3A).

Adolescents (10–19 Years)

Five groups from four studies (n = 2703) were included. The overall prevalence was 11.2% (95% CI: 4.7%–24.3%; I 2 = 98.1%). Prevalence varied across diagnostic thresholds, although these differences were not statistically significant (χ2 = 4.2, p = 0.12). Studies using CPI score 3 reported a prevalence of 23.2% (95% CI: 7.5%–53.0%; I 2 = 98.6%), while those using CPI score 4 reported 5.4% (95% CI: 1.5%–17.9%; I 2 = 94.9%). A single study using the Albandar classification reported 7.0% (95% CI: 4.8%–9.8%) (Figure 3B).

Young Adults (20–25 Years)

Three prevalence estimates from two studies (n = 1532) were included. The overall prevalence was 12.8% (95% CI: 2.8%–42.8%; I 2 = 98.9%). Prevalence varied significantly according to the diagnostic criteria applied (χ2 = 183.7, p < 0.001). A study using CPI score 3 reported 38.3% (95% CI: 34.7%–42.0%), while CPI score 4 from the same source yielded 3.4% (95% CI: 2.2%–5.1%). A separate study using the Albandar classification reported 12.1% (95% CI: 7.1%–18.9%) (Figure 3C).

Adults (25–49 Years)

Nineteen prevalence estimates from nine studies (n = 22,117) were included. The global prevalence was 20.0% (95% CI: 11.9%–31.7%; I 2 = 99.1%; τ 2 = 1.8). Diagnostic criteria significantly contributed to the observed heterogeneity (χ2 = 9.2, p = 0.01). Studies using CPI score 3 reported a prevalence of 38.4% (95% CI: 22.5%–57.1%; I 2 = 98.8%), those using CPI score 4 reported 9.5% (95% CI: 4.2%–19.9%; I 2 = 99.3%) and those combining CPI 3 and 4 yielded 19.2% (95% CI: 8.4%–38.1%; I 2 = 77.4%) (Figure 3D).

Among males, the overall prevalence of periodontitis was 17.6% (95% CI: 10.3%–28.6%; I 2 = 99.2%). Again, substantial variation was observed across diagnostic categories. Meta‐Analysis of Periodontitis Prevalence by Toothbrushing Habits

The findings support a dose–response relationship between oral hygiene behaviour and periodontal health, with more frequent brushing associated with lower prevalence of disease, particularly in studies using CPI score 3 (Figure 5). Among these, individuals brushing once daily had a prevalence of 26.2% (95% CI: 21.3%–31.8%) compared to 21.8% (95% CI: 8.9%–44.3%) for those brushing twice daily and 17.5% (95% CI: 4.1%–51.3%) among individuals brushing three or more times per day. For studies applying CPI score 4, once‐daily brushers had a prevalence of 3.6% (95% CI: 3.1%–4.1%), increasing slightly to 4.0% (95% CI: 1.5%–9.7%) for twice‐daily brushing and 5.6% (95% CI: 1.5%–18.5%) for brushing three or more times per day. In the CPI 3 and 4 combined group, the prevalence estimates were 28.1% (95% CI: 26.8%–29.4%) for once‐daily brushing, 25.0% (95% CI: 16.1%–36.7%) for twice‐daily and 23.7% (95% CI: 14.9%–36.5%) for three or more times per day.

Meta‐Analysis of Grade C Periodontitis (Formerly, Aggressive Periodontitis)

A total of four unique studies were included in this analysis. The overall prevalence of aggressive periodontitis—now classified as Grade C periodontitis—was 9.6% (95% CI: 4.7%–18.4%). Age‐stratified analysis included three subgroups: 12–15 years, 16–18 years and 19–25 years. The highest prevalence was observed among individuals aged 19–25 years (14.5%, 95% CI: 4.7%–36.7%; I 2 = 91.9%), followed by those aged 16–18 years (9.1%, 95% CI: 2.4%–28.7%; I 2 = 99.2%) and 12–15 years (6.7%, 95% CI: 1.4%–26.9%; I 2 = 99.2%). Although these estimates suggest a trend towards increasing prevalence with age, the difference across age groups was not statistically significant (p = 0.68) (Figure 6A). Similarly, no statistically significant differences in prevalence were observed between males and females or between urban and rural populations (Figure 6B,C).

Mixed‐Effects Meta‐Regression of Periodontitis Prevalence

A mixed‐effects meta‐regression was performed to examine the influence of study‐level characteristics on periodontitis prevalence. The final model explained 84% of the between‐study heterogeneity (Appendix S8).

Geographical region was a significant modifier. Compared to studies conducted in North Africa (reference category), those from East Africa showed significantly lower prevalence (β = −0.4, SE = 0.2, p = 0.01). No statistically significant differences were observed for studies from West Africa (β = −0.1, SE = 0.1, p = 0.17) or South Africa (β = 0.0, SE = 0.1, p = 0.17).

Sampling frame also influenced prevalence estimates. Studies conducted within a single city reported significantly higher prevalence than those based on national samples (β = 0.7, SE = 0.4, p = 0.04).

In terms of population type, studies conducted exclusively in urban populations (β = −0.6, SE = 0.17, p = 0.001) or in mixed rural–urban populations (β = −0.3, SE = 0.1, p < 0.001) showed significantly lower prevalence compared to those focusing on rural populations.

Study setting was another significant determinant. Hospital‐based studies reported significantly higher prevalence than population‐based studies (β = 0.4, SE = 0.1, p < 0.001).

With respect to the age group, periodontitis prevalence was significantly lower among adolescents aged 10–19 years (β = −0.2, SE = 0.1, p = 0.002) and children under 10 years (β = −0.3, SE = 0.1, p < 0.001) compared to adults aged 25–49 years (reference group). Additional model details are provided in the Appendices S8–S10.

Discussion

Our meta‐analysis estimates the prevalence of periodontitis in African populations during the 21st century to range from ~2% to 20%, varying by age group. Sensitivity analyses incorporating studies published prior to 2000 yielded similar prevalence estimates. This range may appear to diverge from the commonly held perception that periodontitis is more widespread in Africa. Several key demographic and methodological factors need to be considered when interpreting these estimates. Global data indicate that the peak incidence of periodontitis occurs between the third and fourth decades of life (Nascimento, Alves‐Costa, & Romandini, 2024). One important observation in the epidemiological studies included is the overrepresentation of younger populations. However, the proportion of individuals aged over 30 years in our pooled sample was substantially lower than the demographic reality in Africa (Gerland et al. 2022). This underrepresentation may explain the lower prevalence estimates in our analysis, as subgroup analyses show a clear age‐related increase in periodontitis prevalence.

The lower prevalence obtained is also likely influenced by some methodological aspects of the studies. The sensitivity and specificity of periodontitis detection are affected by case definitions, diagnostic tools and examination modes (Holtfreter et al. 2015; Ke et al. 2023; Savage et al. 2009). Indeed, non‐confident definitions can underestimate the prevalence of periodontitis by nearly two‐fold compared to confident ones (Trindade et al. 2023). Most studies conducted in Africa after 2000 still used non‐confident case definitions, while only two recent studies (Hewlett et al. 2022; Kissa et al. 2022) adopted CDC/AAP or EFP/AAP definitions. Furthermore, the use of CPI/CPITN classifications resulted in the majority of studies likely underestimate the prevalence of periodontitis (V. Baelum and Papapanou 1996; Frencken et al. 2017). Nearly half of the included studies also used partial‐mouth examinations, which are known to underestimate prevalence by up to 50% (P. I. Eke et al. 2010). Despite these methodological issues, our estimates are in line with previous findings from Albandar et al., who reported similar estimates for periodontitis in children and young adults in Africa prior to 2000 (Albandar and Tinoco 2002). Likewise, our pooled prevalence of 9.55% (95% CI: 4.71%–18.41%) for what was previously termed aggressive periodontitis—now classified as Grade C periodontitis—is broadly consistent with a recent global review reporting estimates in Africa, ranging from 2% to 7% (Bouziane et al. 2020). Although our findings underscore a concerning burden of disease in younger populations, this estimate remains likely underestimated due to the case definitions used.

Our subgroup analyses identified toothbrushing frequency as a significant confounder, consistent with findings from other global populations (Billings et al. 2018; Lertpimonchai et al. 2017; Schwendicke et al. 2018; Zimmermann et al. 2015). In addition, our meta‐regression also detected geographical variation in prevalence across African regions, aligning with a recent study (Wu et al. 2022). However, the generalisability of this finding is limited by the unequal geographic distribution of the available studies.

The scarcity of recent, reliable epidemiological data on the prevalence of periodontitis in Africa remains a major challenge. It is also important to mention that, while the studies included employed robust sampling strategies, they typically used stratified random cluster sampling in geographically well‐defined but nationally restricted areas. As such, there may still be some differences between the populations sampled and the broader African population (Vibeke Baelum and Scheutz 2002). Given the considerable heterogeneity of the available data, some sources of heterogeneity might not have been accounted for in our analysis. However, given the substantial proportion of heterogeneity explained by the used model (84%), we are reasonably confident in the estimates obtained. Owing to the cross‐sectional nature of the studies included and the heterogeneous temporal and geographical distribution of the data, we did not assess trends in periodontitis prevalence over time. Similarly, the severity of periodontitis was not evaluated in this review. Evaluating periodontitis severity in African populations might be biased, as tracing the origin of tooth loss—whether periodontal or non‐periodontal—can be challenging (Vibeke Baelum and Scheutz 2002). This high heterogeneity also emphasises the potential impact of lifestyle and systemic factors that could affect the significant variance in prevalence across cohorts, suggesting that periodontal diagnosis must be integrated into a broader diagnostic framework of systemic health.

The wide temporal span of the included studies (1968–2022) may have introduced cohort effects, as changes in oral hygiene practices, socioeconomic conditions, oral health awareness and access to dental care over time could have influenced prevalence estimates across study periods. However, meta‐regression incorporating study era (before 2000 versus after and including 2000) as moderator, adjusted for diagnostic criteria and age group, showed no significant association between study year and prevalence estimates (Appendix S7). Stratification by decade was also considered to formally analyse these temporal changes, but it was not feasible due to the small number of studies. Only 1–3 studies were available per decade from the 1960s through the 2000s, which would have produced severely imprecise and potentially misleading estimates. Consequently, the estimates presented in this review should be interpreted as pooled cross‐sectional prevalences aggregated over the study period, and temporal variations in disease burden cannot be entirely ruled out.

Despite these limitations, this systematic review offers valuable insights for public health initiatives and clinical practices in Africa. Clinically, the high prevalence of periodontitis in younger populations underscores the urgent need for improved screening and prevention efforts in this group. Our findings emphasise the critical need for further high‐quality epidemiological studies conducted across diverse regions of Africa. As suggested by Trindade et al. (Trindade et al. 2023), future studies should utilise at least two confident case definitions (e.g., CDC/AAP 2012 and EFP/AAP 2018) to improve the accuracy of prevalence estimates and strengthen future meta‐epidemiological research. Furthermore, the higher prevalence of periodontitis in youth in Africa may reflect increased susceptibility to early, rapidly progressive forms of the disease. Nevertheless, many of the countries included in this study are low‐and middle‐income nations, where disparities in healthcare access may contribute to the high prevalence of oral diseases, particularly periodontitis (Olujitan et al. 2025).

Conclusion

Epidemiological studies on periodontitis in Africa since 2000 are marked by an overrepresentation of younger populations and a continued reliance on non‐confident diagnostic criteria. These factors likely contribute to an underestimation of the true prevalence. Within this context, our updated prevalence estimates suggest a concerning burden of disease among the younger age groups. There is a need for methodologically robust research to capture the true epidemiology of periodontitis in Africa.
