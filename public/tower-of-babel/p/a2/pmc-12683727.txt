When AI Meets Coronary CT: Overcoming Challenges and Enhancing Accuracy in CAD-RADS Reporting
Authors: Weikai Dong, Jiaming Fan, Wei Li
Published: 2025

We read with great interest the recent publication by Min et al. [1] entitled “Large Language Models for CAD-RADS 2.0 Extraction From Semi-Structured Coronary CT Angiography Reports: A Multi-Institutional Study”. We appreciate the authors’ exploration of the use of large language models (LLMs) to perform structured reporting of coronary CT angiography within the Coronary Artery Disease-Reporting and DataSystem (CAD-RADS) 2.0 framework. Although this study makes an important contribution with relative rigor, several considerations regarding its methodology are discussed herein, along with opportunities for improvement in both the methodology and future studies.

First, the use of synthetic reports (although ethical and privacy-preserving) may not capture the heterogeneity and ambiguity observed in clinical reports. As highlighted in previous studies, the application of real clinical datasets is crucial for validating CAD-RADS-related imaging biomarkers. Thus, it is reasonable to expect that anonymized clinical case hybrid datasets will provide a better simulation of practical applications.

Second, the inability to output calcium scoring measures and plaque composition types reduces the interpretability of the model for real-world risk stratification. Recent guidelines outline the importance of CT-derived parameters, such as total plaque volume and high-risk plaque characteristics, in guiding therapeutic action in patients with borderline or non-obstructive disease [23]. Hence, the use of multimodal data inputs, such as coronary calcium quantification and biomechanical stress estimations, may be necessary to increase clinical fidelity and utility as a decision-support tool for LLMs.

Third, the present study characterized the severity of stenosis, plaque burden, and standard modifiers, but did not analyze the clinical implications of discrepancies in the classification of stenosis. For example, the clinical impact of misclassifying a plaque as CAD-RADS 3, rather than CAD-RADS 4A, may significantly affect patient care. Future studies should determine whether misclassifications of LLM translate to clinical errors in decision-making, particularly in patients with intermediate lesions or myocardial bridging, for whom either CT-FFR [4] or dynamic perfusion imaging would help resolve the diagnosis.

Fourth, although the authors also applied chain-of-thought prompting methods in prompt engineering, an error analysis showed that the models failed to correctly apply numerical thresholds and qualitative terms. Thus, the addition of domain-specific training stages and external knowledge graphs may be helpful. As AI-enabled coronary calcium scoring has brought value by standardizing quantitative outputs in cardiac imaging [5], we hypothesize that similar targeted architectural modifications may also enhance the reasoning capability of LLMs in CAD-RADS tasks.

Finally, institution-wise performance disparities show that model generalizability remains a limitation. This observation underlines the need to use diverse training corpora and, possibly, local LLM calibration protocols. In conclusion, this study investigated the automation of structured reporting using LLMs. If future studies with real-world datasets, multimodal input, and decision impact analysis reveal the alignment of model output with the best current practice regarding coronary CT interpretation and care for patients, future work could achieve values similar to real-world findings, as achieved with experimental datasets. We offer suggestions for refining an already exceptional article and eagerly anticipate future insightful contributions from the authors.
