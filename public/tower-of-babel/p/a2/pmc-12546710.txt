Intracranial aneurysm risk stratification in acute ocular motor nerve palsy based on clinical features
Authors: Yang Ding, Xiangyu Ding, Bin Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To develop and validate a clinical-laboratory screening model for rapid differentiation of intracranial aneurysm (IA)-related acute ocular motor nerve palsy (AOMNP) from microvascular ischemia (MI), thereby reducing diagnostic delays and optimizing resource utilization. In this multicenter retrospective study, 62 AOMNP patients from Anhui Provincial Hospital (derivation cohort: IA = 29, MI = 33) and 29 from Suzhou Municipal Hospital (validation cohort: IA = 9, MI = 20) were categorized based on rigorous imaging criteria. Independent predictors of IA were identified via multivariable logistic regression, followed by nomogram construction. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, with model generalizability assessed through external validation. Female sex (adjusted odds ratio [OR] = 7.94, 95% CI: 1.60–40.00), pupillary changes (OR = 10.02, 95% CI: 2.22–45.15), and elevated D-dimer levels (> 0.675 mg/L; OR = 1.58 per 0.1 mg/L increment, 95% CI: 1.05–2.39) emerged as independent predictors of IA. The integrated model demonstrated robust discriminative capacity (AUC = 0.899, derivation cohort), with preserved performance in the validation cohort (sensitivity: 77.8%, specificity: 85.0%, negative predictive value: 89.5%). Implementation of a simplified triage algorithm (female sex + pupillary abnormalities + D-dimer > 0.675 mg/L) prioritized high-risk patients for urgent vascular imaging, potentially reducing unnecessary testing by 73.3% in low-risk subgroups.

Introduction

Acute ocular motor nerve palsy (AOMNP) is a neurological disorder characterized by ptosis, restricted ocular motility, and diplopia, with etiologies spanning microvascular ischemia, intracranial aneurysm (IA), neoplasms, and other risks. Previous studies report that microvascular ischemia is the most common etiology in AOMNP patients over 50 years old1, and that severe headache suggests the presence of causes other than microvascular ischemia in cases of isolated oculomotor nerve palsy2. However, it is critical to note that up to 40% of isolated third nerve palsies are caused by aneurysms3. Given that IA rupture can lead to fatal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), early identification of high-risk patients is paramount. Current clinical guidelines universally recommend neuroimaging for all isolated ocular motor nerve palsy cases4. Nonetheless, in practice, overlapping clinical features (e.g., pain, complete palsy) between microvascular ischemia and aneurysmal palsy3, combined with insufficient clinician awareness of the urgency of imaging, often result in missed or delayed IA diagnoses.

In recent years, triage strategies integrating biomarkers and clinical features have emerged as a research focus. Studies indicate that posterior communicating artery aneurysms (accounting for 64.9% of IA-related AOMNP cases) frequently compress the oculomotor nerve due to anatomical proximity5. Their occurrence correlates significantly with aneurysm diameter (HR = 1.31) and aneurysm wall enhancement (AWE, OR = 6.13)6–8, highlighting the potential value of imaging characteristics in etiological differentiation. However, reliance solely on morphological features may overlook high-risk clinical populations. Thus, there is an urgent need to integrate demographic, clinical, and laboratory indicators to develop comprehensive screening models that optimize imaging precision.

Currently, no consensus exists on discriminative markers for IA-related AOMNP. Prior evidence suggests that pupillary dilation may indicate aneurysmal compression of oculomotor parasympathetic fibers, but its positive predictive value is limited by confounders such as diabetic pupillary abnormalities9. Furthermore, the association between inflammatory markers or coagulation indices (e.g., D-dimer) and IA remains controversial10,11. To address these gaps, this study retrospectively analyzed clinical data from AOMNP patients, systematically comparing demographic profiles, clinical manifestations, and biomarkers between IA and microvascular ischemia cohorts. This retrospective exploratory analysis aimed to develop a hypothesis-generating screening model to inform early clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This multicenter retrospective cohort study aimed to develop and validate a screening pathway for intracranial aneurysm (intracranial aneurysm, IA)-related acute ocular motor nerve palsy (acute ocular motor nerve palsy, AOMNP). This multicenter retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China (Approval No.: 2023-RE-128) and SuZhou Municipal Hosptial of Anhui Province(Approval No. C2025004). All participants provided informed consent and all procedures followed the Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) guidelines. This was a convenience sample of consecutive patients meeting the eligibility criteria during the study period. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for the external validation cohort were identical to those used in the derivation cohort. All patients met predefined clinical and imaging diagnostic criteria, and risk factor assessments followed the same protocol.

The study comprised two phases: Derivation phase: 62 AOMNP patients admitted to Anhui Provincial Hospital (January 2020–June 2023) were stratified into IA (n = 29) and microvascular ischemia (MI, n = 33) groups based on etiology.External validation phase: 29 AOMNP patients from Suzhou Municipal Hospital (IA group: n = 9; MI group: n = 20) were enrolled to validate the generalizability of the screening model. Inclusion criteria: Age ≥ 18 years; Clinically confirmed AOMNP (ptosis, restricted ocular motility, diplopia); IA group: Intracranial aneurysm confirmed by CTA or DSA as the sole etiology, with exclusion of other neurological disorders; MI group: Structural lesions ruled out by cranial MRI/MRA and meeting ≥ 2 microvascular ischemia risk factors (diabetes, hypertension, coronary artery disease, or cerebral infarction history).Exclusion criteria: AOMNP secondary to trauma, infection, neoplasms, myasthenia gravis, or brainstem infarction; Incomplete imaging data.

Data collection

The following data were extracted from electronic medical records:1) Demographics: Age, sex;2) Vascular risk factors: Hypertension, type 2 diabetes, coronary artery disease, cerebral infarction history;3) Ophthalmic examination: Pupillary changes (dilation or loss of light reflex);4) Laboratory parameters: Fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), D-dimer (immunoturbidimetric assay, R-MAX), lipid profile (TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C), renal function (Cr, uric acid), and complete blood count;5) Imaging: IA group confirmed via CTA/DSA; MI group confirmed via MRI to exclude structural lesions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0 (IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA). Two-tailed P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Normally distributed data were analyzed using independent t-tests (mean ± standard deviation), while non-normally distributed data were analyzed via the Mann-Whitney U test (median and interquartile range). Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests (frequency and percentage). Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into multivariate logistic regression (stepwise method) to identify independent predictors, with odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated. A nomogram was developed based on regression coefficients to quantify individual IA riskReceiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to assess diagnostic accuracy (AUC, sensitivity, specificity, optimal cutoff). Calibration performance was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and non-significant p-value indicates good model fit. D-dimer cutoff was determined using the maximal Youden index from the ROC curve of the derivation cohort. To quantify the uncertainty of performance metrics, 95% confidence intervals for AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and negative predictive value were estimated using nonparametric bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) in both derivation and validation cohorts. All bootstrap analyses were performed using SPSS.

Results

General clinical characteristics

The study enrolled 62 patients with AOMNP, including 29 in the IA group and 33 in the MI group. Age did not differ significantly between groups (IA: 62.03 ± 11.68 vs. MI: 63.46 ± 8.30 years, P = 0.580), but the IA group had a higher proportion of females (75.9% vs. 36.4%, P = 0.002). The MI group exhibited more microvascular risk factors (mean 2.33 vs. 1.45 in IA, P < 0.001), suggesting that relying solely on medical history may lead to IA underdiagnosis. Pupillary abnormalities (dilation or light reflex loss) occurred in 75.9% (22/29) of IA patients, significantly exceeding the MI group (18.2%, 6/33; P < 0.001). The MI group showed higher fasting blood glucose (8.16 ± 2.45 vs. 4.67 ± 1.87 mmol/L, P < 0.001) and glycated hemoglobin (7.2% vs. 6.0%, P = 0.002), consistent with its larger diabetic population. IA patients had elevated D-dimer levels (0.76 vs. 0.54 mg/L, P < 0.001), while renal function, lipid profiles, and hematological parameters showed no intergroup differences (Table 1).

Independent predictors of intracranial aneurysm

Variables with univariate significance (sex, pupillary changes, D-dimer) were analyzed via multivariate logistic regression (Fig. 1A). Female sex (OR = 7.94), pupillary abnormalities (OR = 10.019), and elevated D-dimer (OR = 1.584 per 0.1 unit increase) emerged as independent IA predictors. A nomogram integrating these features was developed for individualized IA risk stratification (Fig. 1B).

Diagnostic performance of predictive models

ROC analysis revealed optimal discrimination for the combined sex-pupil-D-dimer model (AUC = 0.899, Fig. 2A) and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test showed no significant lack of fit (P = 0.58). A simplified model (sex + pupils) retained high accuracy (AUC = 0.864, Fig. 2B). D-dimer alone (cutoff: 0.675 mg/L) showed moderate performance (AUC = 0.787; sensitivity = 77.4%, specificity = 76.9%; Fig. 2C), which improved after excluding high-risk patients (females with pupillary changes, AUC = 0.834; Fig. 2D). The proposed screening pathway (Fig. 2E) prioritizes urgent imaging for high-risk patients (females + pupillary changes) and D-dimer-stratified imaging (≥ 0.675 mg/L) for others.

External validation

The screening model was validated in 29 AOMNP patients (IA = 9, MI = 20) from Suzhou Municipal Hospital. Validation and training cohorts showed concordant sex, pupillary, and D-dimer trends based on 1,000 bootstrap replicates (Fig. 3A). The protocol achieved sensitivity = 77.8%, specificity = 85.0%, and negative predictive value (NPV) = 89.5% (Fig. 3B), with a stepwise implementation flowchart (Fig. 3C).

Discussion

AOMNP is a clinically common neurological disorder that significantly impacts patients’ quality of life. Current evidence indicates diverse etiologies for AOMNP, including IA, diabetes, trauma, and infections12. The clinical significance of AOMNP cannot be overstated, as its occurrence may serve as an early warning sign of IA rupture, which can lead to catastrophic complications such as subarachnoid hemorrhage13. Timely neuroimaging to exclude IA is critical for reducing mortality and disability. In this study, retrospective analysis of AOMNP patients from Anhui Provincial Hospital identified female sex, pupillary abnormalities, and elevated D-dimer (> 0.675 mg/L) as key predictors of IA etiology. The derived screening algorithm demonstrated robust diagnostic accuracy in the external validation cohort, underscoring its potential clinical utility for improving outcomes in IA-related AOMNP.

Intracranial aneurysms, defined as localized dilatations of cerebral arterial walls due to congenital or acquired vascular defects14, represent a major neurosurgical concern. Although microvascular ischemia is a prevalent cause of AOMNP, our findings revealed that IA-related AOMNP patients had an average of 1.45 coexisting microvascular risk factors, complicating etiology differentiation based solely on medical history. Epidemiological studies report a 1–5% prevalence of IA, with higher incidence in individuals over 40 years15. Notably, IA exhibits marked sex disparity, with a 1.39-fold higher incidence in females compared to males, potentially attributable to hormonal and vascular structural differences16. Consistent with prior research, our IA cohort had a significantly higher proportion of female patients than the microvascular ischemia group, emphasizing the need for heightened clinical vigilance in females presenting with AOMNP.

The clinical manifestations of AOMNP—ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, and pupillary changes17 — vary between IA and microvascular etiologies. Microvascular ischemia typically spares pupillary size (preserved mydriasis) and manifests as incomplete ophthalmoplegia with gradual progression18. In contrast, IA-induced AOMNP often involves pupillary dilation and light reflex loss due to compression of the oculomotor nerve’s parasympathetic fibers9. The study by Juthamat Witthayaweerasak et al. found that poor pupillary response is an important consideration for early neuroimaging in patients with isolated oculomotor nerve palsy19. Our data confirmed significantly higher rates of pupillary abnormalities in IA patients. Although both groups frequently presented with binocular diplopia, this symptom lacked discriminative value. These findings align with the established pathoanatomical mechanism of IA-related AOMNP, where posterior communicating artery aneurysms compress the nerve’s superomedial pupillomotor fibers.

D-dimer, a fibrin degradation product reflecting thrombotic and fibrinolytic activity20, was elevated in IA patients, particularly those with aneurysmal thrombosis or rupture11. Hemodynamic alterations in IA, such as turbulent flow and stasis, promote intraluminal thrombosis10, potentially explaining the observed D-dimer elevation. Notably, in non-female patients without pupillary changes, a D-dimer threshold of ≥ 0.675 mg/L demonstrated satisfactory diagnostic performance for IA. This highlights D-dimer’s dual role as both a pathophysiological marker and a pragmatic diagnostic adjunct.

Although vascular comorbidities such as diabetes and coronary artery disease may influence D-dimer levels and pupillary responses, our analysis showed that these features remained more pronounced in IA cases compared to MI, despite the latter having more ischemia-associated conditions. This further supports the robustness of our screening model across heterogeneous clinical backgrounds.While our triage strategy (female sex + pupillary changes + D-dimer > 0.675 mg/L) shows promise for optimizing neuroimaging prioritization, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the retrospective design introduces selection bias risks, compounded by the relatively small sample size. Second, pupillary assessment relied on clinical observation rather than standardized tools (e.g., dynamic pupillography), potentially introducing measurement bias. Third, the cost-effectiveness of this algorithm remains uncertain due to the lack of direct comparisons between imaging modalities (CTA/MRA vs. DSA) in detecting IA. Future multicenter prospective studies should validate these findings, refine predictor weights, and integrate molecular or radiomic biomarkers to enhance precision. Additionally, health economic evaluations and long-term outcome analyses are essential to assess the real-world impact of this screening pathway.

In conclusion, this retrospective exploratory study proposes a hypothesis-generating screening model—based on sex, pupillary response, and D-dimer levels—that effectively stratifies IA risk in patients with AOMNP. By facilitating targeted neuroimaging, this approach may reduce IA-related morbidity and mortality through timely intervention, while minimizing unnecessary diagnostic procedures in low-risk individuals.
