Parasympathetic tone activity, heart rate, and grimace scale in conscious horses of 3 breeds before, during, and after nociceptive mechanical stimulation
Authors: Jorge Eduardo Mendoza Flores, Angelica Terrazas, Alma V Lara Sagahon, Monica Aleman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParasympathetic tone activity (PTA) in response to nociceptive stimulus in conscious non‐sedated horses is unknown.ObjectivesStudy PTA, heart rate (HR), and horse grimace scale (HGS) at rest and during mechanical nociceptive stimulation.AnimalsNinety healthy young adult horses (females, males): 30 each of Friesians, Quarter Horses, and Warmbloods.MethodsProspective control study. The study consisted of habituation to equipment (Day 1), baseline recordings (Days 2 and 3), and nociceptive testing applying mild pressure to the metacarpus (Day 4). Parasympathetic tone, HR, and HGS were recorded simultaneously on Days 2 to 4. Each study lasted 30 minutes and was done in triplicate at 3 different time points per day.ResultsBaseline PTA was not different among breeds. It decreased in Warmbloods and Quarter Horses during placement of the stimulus device without stimulation (P < .01). A significant decrease in PTA (P < .001) occurred during nociceptive stimulus (marked in Quarter Horses, intermediate in Warmbloods, and mild in Friesians). Heart rate and HGS increased significantly (P < .001) during the stimulus in all breeds but returned to baseline poststimulation. Friesians required higher pressure (P < .05) to elicit an aversive response to the stimulus.ConclusionsHorses' PTA, HR, and HGS change in response to a mild mechanical nociceptive stimulus with Friesians showing less variation. Stress induced a decrease in PTA in Quarter Horses and Warmbloods but not in Friesians. Friesians appeared to be more tolerant to pain based on PTA, HR, and HGS findings compared with other breeds.Keywords: autonomous, behavior, pain, vagal tone, welfare

INTRODUCTION

Pain is an important welfare issue in veterinary medicine. 1 Pain, especially when mild, can be challenging to detect and therefore can be overlooked. Differences among species (humans, dogs, and rats) 2 , 3 , 4 and temperamental states (humans and horses) 5 , 6 that modify nociceptive perception have been reported. Anecdotally, equine clinicians have noted less expression of pain (stoicism) in certain horse breeds, 7 commonly the Friesian breed. 8 Facial expression scoring scales have been developed to improve the assessment of pain in different species, including horses. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 A published horse grimace scale (HGS) evaluates 6 facial features called facial action units (FAUs) with individual scores (0‐2) depending on the absence or presence of defined clinical manifestations for each FAU for a final maximum score of 12 points, where a higher score means a higher degree of pain. 9

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) regulates visceral, cardiovascular, respiratory, and glandular activities to preserve homeostasis through the interaction of the sympathetic and parasympathetic (vagal) systems. 15 Autonomic assessment has been used to measure states of physical and emotional health in human and veterinary medicine. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Several studies showed that pain causes an important autonomic imbalance. 21 , 22 An increase in parasympathetic tone might produce an important antinociceptive effect by improving analgesic modulation in certain areas of the brain. 23 , 24 Measurement of parasympathetic tone activity (PTA) by heart rate variability (HRV) analysis has been used in dogs, cats, and horses as a method to assess ANS function. 25 , 26 , 27 This measured PTA is similar to the analgesia nociception index (ANI) used in human medicine and is measured by continuously analyzing the influence of the respiratory neural cycle on HRV, filtering out high‐frequency fluctuations (HF‐HRV) between 0.15 and 0.5 Hz which are exclusively mediated by vagal stimulation. 21 , 28 , 29 This procedure provides an estimate of PTA depending on vagal predominance ranging from 0 to 100 (unidimensional, no units). 30 An immediate PTA (PTAi) and mean PTA (PTAm) are calculated in the last 56 seconds and from the average of the last 176 seconds of the recording, respectively. 21 , 28 , 29 Parasympathetic tone monitoring devices were developed to evaluate the analgesia‐nociception balance under anesthesia in dogs, pigs, horses, and humans 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 ; and in unanesthetized dogs and humans. 35 , 36 Parasympathetic tone activity in horses undergoing colic surgery showed efficacy in predicting hemodynamic changes and lower vagal tone. 34 Similarly, higher PTA was recorded when administering intraoperative fentanyl, 37 whereas ketamine showed sympathomimetic properties in horses. 38

Parasympathetic tone activity, HR, and HGS in association with a mechanical nociceptive stimulus and differences by breed have not been studied in the horse. Therefore, our objective was to investigate PTA, HR, and HGS under nociceptive mechanical stimulation in 3 breeds of horses: Friesians, Quarter Horses, and Warmbloods. Our testing hypothesis was that Friesians would maintain a higher PTA at rest and under nociceptive stimulation compared with other breeds, likely explaining the perceived higher tolerance to pain. It was also expected that a nociceptive stimulus would decrease PTA and increase HR and HGS, independent of the breed.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Prospective control study.

Horses and facilities

Ninety horses, healthy based on physical examination, consisting of 30 horses of each breed were included in the study: Friesians (FRI), Quarter Horses (QH), and Warmbloods (WB). The sample size was based on convenience, resulting in 30 horses per group. Friesians consisted of 14 females and 16 intact males with a mean age of 6.2 ± 2.1 years, and approximate weight with a weight tape of 500 to 700 kg. The physical activity of the horses was dressage (n = 25) and reproduction (n = 5). Quarter Horses consisted of 12 females, 11 intact males, and 7 geldings, mean age 4.5 ± 4.8 years, and an approximate weight of 350 to 500 kg. Their physical activity consisted of charreria (national equestrian sport of Mexico, n = 29), and racing (n = 1). Warmbloods consisted of 9 females, 5 intact males, and 16 geldings, mean age 10.7 ± 4 years, approximate weight of 400 to 600 kg, all jumping in active training. Pregnant or lactating females were not included. All 90 horses had no history of illness, and no administration of medications or surgery in the last 30 days before enrollment in the study. The horses were fed according to their usual diet, generally consisting of oat hay 3 to 4 times a day, alfalfa 1 to 2 times a day, commercial balanced feed 3 to 5 kg/day, and free access to water. Horses were housed in 12 different equestrian centers in Mexico City and neighboring towns. The study was performed at the location where horses were housed to avoid stress from transportation, being in an unfamiliar place, and to avoid prolonged time for habituation. 39 All horses were habituated to being tied or restricted in standing stocks according to equestrian facilities management. For the study, horses were restricted within an area adapted to ensure the safety of personnel, horses, and equipment by placing protective panels or confining horses within stocks. Horses were tied loosely in their stall for the duration of each study (30 minutes). One of the authors performed all of the studies (Jorge Eduardo Mendoza Flores) and was assisted by an experienced handler. The study was approved by the Institutional Subcommittee for the Care and Use of Experimental Animals of Postgraduate Degree in Production Sciences and Animal Health of the National Autonomous University of Mexico, protocol #SICUAE.DC‐2022/4‐1. Written owner consent was obtained.

PTA equipment and study design

PTA equipment

The PTA equipment (Mdoloris Medical Systems, Lille, France) was programmed for use in horses 34 , 37 , 38 by attaching 3 crocodile clips to the skin: 1 in the left thoracic area at the level of the olecranon (Figure 1A), and 2 more at the cranial aspect of the right scapula at a distance of 10 cm between these electrodes (Figure 1A). The device records 4 variables: PTAm, PTAi, respiratory energy, and HR. Based on established published protocols, only PTAm and HR data were used for statistical analysis. 34 , 37 , 38 The equipment is set to record data when the signal quality is automatically determined as good by the device.

Nociceptive stimulus

The nociceptive stimulus was generated using equipment reproduced from previous work. 40 In brief, a lightweight device consisting of a pneumatic actuator (resistance syringe with a blunt end attached to a plunger that pushes a pin 1 mm in diameter) was mounted to a polyethylene holder and padded with expanded neoprene to form a bracelet that was wrapped around the metacarpus (Figure 1B) and fixed using velcro tape. To provide the mechanical stimulus, increasing pressure was applied with the plunger until obtaining an aversive response (“nociceptive threshold”). This response was the result of the minimum force in kg/cm2 needed to elicit a clear withdrawal of the limb, hitting the ground with the limb, or attempting to escape, at which time the stimulus was stopped. The pressure (kg/cm2) that generated a nociceptive threshold was recorded each time the test was performed. If there was no aversive reaction at 2 kg/cm2, the process was interrupted to avoid tissue damage from pressure. The apparatus was calibrated using a scale (Torrey model L‐Equation 5/10 Aut., Mexico), applying pressure to push the pin against the scale plate, and comparing the weight recorded on the scale (kg) and the corresponding pressure of the device (kg/cm2). Calibration was done and repeated 10 times before the experiment to ensure reproducible results.

Study design

The study was divided into 3 stages for a total duration of 4 days: Habituation (Day 1), baseline (Days 2 and 3), and nociceptive testing (Day 4; Figure 2A). Each stage was performed at 3 different time points throughout the day from 8 to 9:30 am, 12 to 1:30 pm, and 5 to 6:30 pm on Days 1 to 4. The effects of time of day on PTA, HR, and HGS were investigated from Days 2 to 4. Horses of the same breed were randomly identified by successive numbers (no 1, no 2, and so on until no 30). Three horses in successive order were studied each day as follows: Horse no 1—from 8 to 8:30 am, no 2—8:30 to 9:00 am, and no 3—9 to 9:30 am; same horse no 1—from 12 to 12:30 pm, no 2—12:30 to 1 pm, no 3—1 to 1:30 pm; and finally horse no 1—5 to 5:30 pm, no 2—5:30 to 6 pm, no 3—6 to 6:30 pm. Each study at each time point had a duration of 30 minutes per horse. This protocol was followed for each day of the study (4 days).

Stages

Habituation (Day 1). At 3 different times (8‐9:30 am, 12‐1:30 pm, and 5‐6:30 pm), the horses were placed in the testing area for 10 minutes and handled by the personnel performing the experiment. Short periods of habituation (minutes) have been considered adequate for environmental stressors in horses as shown by a lack of differences in behavior and HRV. 41 Furthermore, the habituation time was not extended to avoid unnecessary time remaining tied or confined in the stocks. To simulate the recording of PTA, horses were exposed to the equipment by placing the crocodile clips on the skin (without recording), the bracelet for nociceptive stimulation placed in the left metacarpus (without stimulation), and the video camera (without recording) at 1 m in front of the horse. Horses appeared calm and no changes in facial expression (eg, ears pulled back, widening of the palpebral fissure, grimace) were noted during this stage. The period of habituation was not extended to avoid unnecessary time remaining tied or confined in stocks.

Baseline (Days 2 and 3)—Horses were instrumented and recordings were carried out without nociceptive stimulation at 3 time periods during the day as described above. Horses remained in the testing area connected to the PTA Index monitor for the recording of PTA. The pressure bracelet was not placed on the horses for this stage of the study. Baseline PTA and HR were monitored continuously for 30 minutes at each time point for each horse under study. During those 30 minutes at each time point, recordings were made every 5 minutes for a total of 6 recordings, and data were reported as the mean. Simultaneous 1‐minute videos (3 videos per time point) were captured for HGS at minutes 5, 15, and 25.

Nociceptive testing (Day 4)—Protocol for testing and recording were performed as described above at 3 time points during the day. For each time point, the horses were connected to the PTA Index monitor for a total of 30 minutes. The pressure bracelet was placed on the metacarpus 10 minutes before the stimulus was applied as described. Time 0 (minute 0) was defined as the time at which the nociceptive stimulus was applied. The PTA and HR values were recorded as follows: 2 prestimulus recordings (10 and 5 minutes before the stimulus [time 0]), 2 during nociceptive testing (time 0 = start of nociceptive stimulus, and time of aversive reaction), and 2 poststimulus recordings (5 and 10 minutes after time 0; Figure 2B). Videos (60 seconds) for HGS were taken as follows: 1 prestimulus video (−5 minutes), during nociceptive stimulation from the start of stimulus to aversive reaction (time 0), and 2 poststimulus video (1 and 5 minutes poststimulus).

Horse grimace scale

A published HGS was used in our study. 9 In brief, the HGS consisted of the evaluation of 6 FAUs: (a) Stiffly backwards ears, (b) orbital tightening, (c) tension above the eye area, (d) prominent strained chewing muscles, (e) mouth strained and pronounced chin, and (f) strained nostrils and flattening of the profile. Each FAU is scored from 0 to 2, where 0 = not present, 1 = moderately present, and 2 = clearly present. 9 For the scoring of HGS, the sum of all 6 FAU was made for a possible minimum score of 0 and a maximum of 12. The higher the score, the higher the manifestation of pain. The HGS score was obtained by recording videos with a camera (Insta 360 GO 2, China) placed in front of the face 1 m away from the horse to obtain different angles of the face. 42 Videos were analyzed by a veterinarian trained in HGS (Jorge Eduardo Mendoza Flores) blinded to the horses' results of PTA and HR. Recordings of HGS were done during the stages of baseline and nociceptive testing as follows. Baseline: Three HGS recordings were made at 5, 15, and 25 minutes of this stage. Nociceptive testing: Four HGS recordings were made at 10 minutes before the nociceptive stimulus, during the nociceptive stimulus (start of stimulus to aversive reaction), and 1 and 5 minutes after the nociceptive stimulus.

Statistical analysis

Normality in the raw data was determined graphically using the “fitdist” function of R package “fitdistrplus.” 43 Analysis was performed using software R (R Core Team, 2023; A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing_. R Foundation for Statistical Computing. Vienna, Austria. https://www.R-project.org/). The variables analyzed in association with PTA, HR and HGS were the effect between groups by breed, age, and sex, and effect within each horse by time, day, and nociceptive stimulus. To analyze PTA and HR, both on baseline days and during nociceptive testing an analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for a factorial experiment with the R Package “ez” (Easy Analysis and Visualization of Factorial Experiments_. R package version 4.4‐0. https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=ez), Fisher's least significant difference was computed to provide 95% confidence intervals (CI). For HGS, the R Package “ordinal” (Ordinal—Regression Models for Ordinal Data. R package version 2022.11‐16. https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=ordinal) and the nonparametric Friedman's test were used. Post hoc analysis P values were adjusted using the Benjamini‐Hochberg method. Normality was determined graphically with the “fitdist” function of R package “fitdistrplus.” 43 Where appropriate, mean, and SD and median and range were presented for normally and not normally distributed data, respectively.

RESULTS

Baseline (Days 2 and 3)

Parasympathetic tone activity

No significant difference (P > .05) in PTA was found by sex, time point (8 am, 12 pm, and 5 pm) or day (Day 2 vs Day 3) or breed. Parasympathetic means on baseline (Days 2 and 3): Friesians PTAm 47 ± 6 (CI, 45‐48.5), Quarter Horse PTAm 44 ± 8.3 (CI, 42.5‐45.5), Warmblood PTAm 43.5 ± 8.7 (CI, 42‐45; Figure 3A). Although not significant (P = .13), the PTAm in Friesians was higher than that of other breeds with a variation (ΔPTA) of 5.5%, and 4.4% higher than Quarter Horses and Warmbloods, respectively (Figure 3A).

Heart rate

Warmbloods had significantly lower HR than other breeds (P = .03). Mares had significantly higher HR (P = .003) than males, independent of breed. No significant difference was found among time points or baseline days (P > .05). Baseline data are shown in Figure 3B.

Horse grimace scale

Horses at rest had a median HGS of 2 (range, 1‐4). No significant differences by breed, sex, age, time, or day were found (P > .05; Figure 3C).

Nociceptive testing (Day 4)

Prestimulus PTAm

No difference by time points or sex was found (P > .05). Significant effects were found by breed in prestimulus values (baseline, −10 to −5 minutes) with Friesians having a PTAm (46.3; CI, 45.1‐47.5; P < .01) higher than Quarter Horses (PTAm, 43; CI, 41.8‐44.2) and Warmbloods (PTAm, 40.9; CI, 39.7‐42.1; Figure 3A). No significant difference was found between PTAm at baseline and prestimulus for all breeds (Figure 3A).

Nociceptive threshold

Significant effect by breed exerting higher pressure to generate a nociceptive threshold was needed in Friesians (0.78 kg/cm2; CI, 0.71‐0.85; P < .05) compared with Warmbloods (0.7 kg/cm2; CI, 0.74‐0.77) and Quarter Horses (0.59 kg/cm2; CI, 0.64‐0.76). No significant differences were found by age, sex, or time of day. Time between the initiation of nociceptive stimulus to manifestations of aversive reaction to the stimulus was recorded and significantly different (P < .001) among breeds: Friesians (mean, 15.3 seconds ± 7.6; CI, 14.4‐16.2), Quarter Horses (10 seconds ± 2.9; CI, 9‐10.9), and Warmbloods (12.4 seconds ± 6.1; CI, 11.4‐13.2).

Poststimulus PTAm

Independent of breed, a significant parasympathetic decrease occurred 1 minute poststimulus (PTAAR 41.5, Δ7%; CI, 40.3‐42.7, Δ7%; P < .001) and at 5 minutes poststimulus (PTAmin5 40.7; CI, 39.5‐41.9; Δ9%; P < .001; Figure 3A). Friesians showed the lowest variation (lowest decrease: ΔPTAAR 4%, ΔPTAmin5 6.6%), intermediate Warmbloods (ΔPTAAR 6.2%, ΔPTAmin5 7.5%), and highest variation in Quarter Horses (ΔPTAAR 12.3%, ΔPTAmin5 16.9%). Values of PTAm at 10 minutes poststimulus were similar to those at baseline independent of breed.

Heart rate during nociceptive testing

No differences by sex, age, or time of day were found (P > .05) during the prestimulus period. Warmbloods had a significantly lower HR (P = .03) than the other breeds at prestimulus recordings (−10 minutes, −5 minutes). The mean HR of all 3 breeds at the time of initiation of the nociceptive stimulus was 36.4 beats/minute (CI, 35.7‐37.1) which increased to 37.6 beats/minute (CI, 36.9‐38.3) at the time of aversive reaction. Although minimal, this HR increase was significant (P < .001; Figure 3B). The mean HR among all 3 breeds during the nociceptive stimulus was not significantly different. After the stimulus, mean HR returned to baseline values 5 and 10 minutes poststimulus (Figure 3B). No significant difference in HR was found at 5 and 10 minutes poststimulus among breeds.

Horse grimace scale during nociceptive testing

Independent of breed, the prestimulus (−10 minutes, −5 minutes) median (range) HGS was 2 (1‐4). A significant increase in median HGS was observed during nociceptive stimulus (HGS 4 [2‐7], P < .001) compared with baseline days and prestimulus. After discontinuation of the nociceptive stimulus, median HGS decreased at 1 (HGS 3 [1‐5]) and 5 (HGS 2 [1‐5]) minutes poststimulus (Figure 3C). No significant differences by breed, sex, age, or time of day were found. A linear association (−0.5, Spearman; P < .001) between PTAm and HGS at the time of aversive reaction was found.

DISCUSSION

We investigated PTA in 3 popular breeds: Friesians, Quarter Horses, and Warmbloods. These 3 breeds of horses had similar PTA with no differences by age, sex, or time of the day of recording when at rest in a familiar environment and under nonstressful or painful conditions. Heart rate at rest was significantly lower in Warmbloods and higher in females independent of breed. At rest and when not stimulated, a low HGS was similar among breeds, age, and sex. Warmbloods and Quarter Horses had significant decreases in PTA upon nociceptive stimulus compared with Friesians, with the largest decrease in Quarter Horses. Likewise, the pressure and time in seconds to trigger an aversive response were significantly higher in Friesians than in the other breeds, and lowest in Quarter Horses. Furthermore, PTA during the nociceptive stimulus was not significantly different from that of the prestimulus period in Friesians. Finally, an inverse correlation between PTA and HGS was identified. A decrease in PTA during the nociceptive stimulus correlated with an increase in HGS, indicating a higher degree of pain. This decrease was less marked in Friesians compared with the other breeds.

Although Friesians had a higher PTAm at rest compared with Quarter Horses and Warmbloods, this difference was not significant (P = .13), perhaps a larger sample size would have been helpful to further investigate this observation. Similarly, others reported no autonomic difference between Standardbreds and Thoroughbreds 44 or between Arabians and Konic horses. 45 In contrast, a retrospective study identified differences in autonomic function among 6 breeds of dogs, with brachiocephalic breeds showing higher vagal tone than non‐brachiocephalic breeds. 26 A significant decrease from baseline to prestimulus PTAm was identified in Quarter Horses and Warmbloods but not in Friesians. This decrease in PTAm likely was related to possible stress associated with the placement of the pressure bracelet on the metacarpus to record during the prestimulus period (Day 4), whereas during the baseline recording (Days 2 and 3) the bracelet was not applied. Another possibility for this observation could be a difference in skin sensitivity among breeds which was not investigated in our study. However, this finding suggests that stressful (not painful or anticipatory response 46 ) situations also alter parasympathetic tone, in this case in Quarter Horses and Warmbloods. Better adaptation to stressful situations because of temperament generating less autonomic alterations has been described in horses. 45 , 47 Therefore, it is possible that Friesians are better adapted or more tolerant to stressful conditions than other breeds.

Parasympathetic tone activity decreased in all breeds upon nociceptive stimulus, however, the decrease in Friesians was not as marked as that observed in Quarter Horses and Warmbloods. This finding indicates that Friesians maintain higher PTAm even in the phase of pain suggesting that Friesians are more tolerant to pain, in support of anecdotal clinical observations. Furthermore, the amount of time and pressure exerted to generate an aversive reaction to a nociceptive stimulus was significantly higher in Friesians compared with Quarter Horses and Warmbloods. Significant variation was found in PTAm after exerting a nociceptive stimulus for all breeds, with Friesians showing less variation. These findings further suggest that Friesians maintain higher vagal tone and therefore are less prone to autonomic changes in the phase of stress and nociceptive stimulus compared with Warmbloods and Quarter Horses.

The reason for the higher parasympathetic tone in Friesians is unknown but a genetic contribution is a consideration. This difference might accompany other breed‐related characteristics such as metabolic status (size, obesity, and energy regulation), 48 performance, 8 , 44 and temperament 45 , 47 in specific breeds. It is not clear whether vagal predominance in Friesian horses generates tolerance (less physiological or behavioral response) to nociceptive stimuli, but research in humans supports the proposal that the parasympathetic system exerts an important antinociceptive role, through projected parasympathetic afferents to specific areas of the brain such as the solitary nuclei, raphe magnus, locus ceruleus, amygdala, and periaqueductal gray substance, responsible for the descending modulation of pain. 23 , 24 , 49 Anecdotally, equine clinicians report stoicism in Friesian horses mainly with gastrointestinal pain. Although our study was a model of somatic nociception through a mechanical stimulus, several studies in humans show that vagal predominance generates less visceral nociceptive sensitivity. 24 Other authors have shown parasympathetic decrease during painful events in horses such as laminitis and gastric ulcers. 50 , 51 However, to our knowledge, there was no evidence in any study that higher vagal predominance can contribute to decreased nociceptive sensitivity in horses. Horses in our study had no evidence of laminitis (no lameness or presence of digital pulses). Although gastroscopy was not performed to investigate gastric ulceration in our horses, the horses had no signs such as aggressive behavior, poor performance, kicking in the air, biting at their flanks, girth sensitivity or overall sensitivity to touch, abdominal pain, weight loss, or diarrhea. Furthermore, the PTAm only decreased during the nociceptive stimulation and returned to baseline values within 10 minutes of discontinuation of the stimulus. Therefore, the decrease in PTAm was unlikely to be attributed to gastric ulcers. In a recent study, pain in horses with gastric ulcers was not detected using the HGS. 52

The ANS can modify cardiovascular parameters such as HR in response to drugs 53 or nociceptive stimuli. 33 At rest during habituation recordings, Warmblood horses had significantly lower resting HR (3.3 beats/min lower) than other breeds, but this difference appeared to be of no clinical relevance for the study. Additionally, these horses were in a high‐level athletic activity (show jumping) which has been reported to result in cardiovascular adaptation (eg, lower resting HR) from training. 54 Independent of breed, mares had a higher resting HR which has been reported previously. 44 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 Despite finding a significant effect in increased HR at the time of aversive reaction to the stimulus, the difference in means was too small, and therefore considered an ineffective indicator of pain in our study, a finding similar to those reported by other authors. 58 , 59 However, modification of PTA or HR does not strictly reflect cardiovascular changes and differences in PTA might not coincide with changes in HR. 31 , 38

A HGS ≤3 has been reported to reflect a horse at rest. 60 In our study, the median HGS was 2 during baseline and prestimulus periods. The median HGS increased to 4 (P < .01) during the nociceptive stimulus generating a physical reaction manifested as a modified facial expression and aversion to the stimulus. Increased HGS has been reported in horses with laminitis, mares with follicular aspiration, and after various surgeries. 9 , 61 , 62 Those studies evaluated horses with acute or persistent pain or both, resulting in higher HGS scores (6‐8) than those reported in our study (HGS of 4) consistent with mild pain that was transitory. 9 , 61 , 62 The median HGS decreased to a baseline score of 2, 5 minutes poststimulus, also suggesting that HGS is a better indicator for mild physical pain than HR.

Limitations of our study included the lack of power calculations for sample size, which could have resulted in under or overestimation of the observed findings. However, this number of horses per group was the only number available to us, and our sample size was close to that used in other studies involving PTA or HR investigation with significant effects. 37 , 38 The sample size used in our study allowed us to detect significant effects. Thus, our results will be useful to determine a power analysis for future research. Another limitation included the number of females and males per breed, which was not homogeneous. However, no significant differences by sex were found when PTAm was evaluated for all breeds combined. Also, age per breed was not homogenous, with Warmbloods being older than the other breeds (WB, 10.7 years ± 4; QH, 4.5 years ± 4.8; FRI, 6.2 years ± 2.1). However, no significant differences in PTAm were found by age for all breeds combined. Our study included horses with various amounts of physical activity, handling, feeding, and housing because horses were client‐owned and the study was performed at a familiar location for the horse to minimize stress. Therefore, the effect of these variables on PTAm is unknown. During the habituation period, the pressure bracelet was not placed to avoid possible stimulation, and therefore horses were not acclimated to the bracelet before the study. Scoring of HGS was only performed by 1 of the authors (Jorge Eduardo Mendoza Flores) who was not blinded to the objectives and hypothesis of the study, which potentially could have resulted in bias. However, PTAm is an objective means of assessing pain for which alterations coincided with changes in HGS in response to a nociceptive stimulus.

In conclusion, Friesians, Warmbloods, and Quarter Horses show similar parasympathetic tone at rest. However, a decrease in parasympathetic tone was induced upon a stressful (nonpainful) stimulus in Quarter Horses and Warmbloods but not in Friesians. A nociceptive mechanical stimulus produced mild PTA variation (decrease) in Friesians, intermediate in Warmbloods, and marked in Quarter Horses. An increase in HGS is an effective method to detect pain even when a nociceptive stimulus of low intensity is applied. This increase in HGS occurred concurrently with a decrease in PTA and increase in HR during nociceptive stimulus. The detected higher parasympathetic tone in Friesians might account for the perceived increased tolerance to pain compared with other breeds. Future work should be done to corroborate this assumption and validate the effectiveness of PTA monitoring.
