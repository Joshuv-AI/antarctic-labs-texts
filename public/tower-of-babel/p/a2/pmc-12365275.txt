Association of new Life’s Essential 8 with biological aging among the national health and nutrition examination surveys
Authors: Xiaoli Chen, Xin Yin, Xin Chen, Yajie Gao, Nan Ye, Xingkang He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) score was updated by the American Heart Association, which constituted cardiovascular health (CVH) and was significantly associated with overall and cardiovascular disease-specific mortality. The aim of this research was to evaluate the connection between LE8, the newly revised indicator of cardiovascular health, and the acceleration of biological age. Participants for this study were selected from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. The LE8 scores, derived from measurements following the American Heart Association definitions, were categorized into three levels: low, moderate, and high, on a scale of 0 to 100. The PhenoAge algorithm was used to compute biological age, while the PhenoAge advancement was used to assess biological age acceleration. Linear and logistic regression analyses were used to examine the association between the LE8 score and biological age. A total of 17,153 individuals with an average age of 47.54 were included in the study. The average LE8 score was 68, and the average Phenotypic age was 44.61. Individuals with higher LE8 scores were found to have a younger Phenotypic age, as demonstrated by their lower PhenoAge scores. After controlling for potential confounding variables, a higher LE8 score was found to be associated with a decrease in PhenoAge advancement (β = − 1.22, P < 0.01) and a lower likelihood of biological aging (OR = 0.65, P < 0.01). Both health behavior and health factor scores were associated with biological aging (both P < 0.01). Each type of LE8 score in the high group was positively associated with biological aging (both P < 0.01). A significant inverse association between LE8 score and biological aging was observed in all subgroups (both P < 0.01). The LE8 and its subscale scores showed a negative association with the likelihood of phenotypic aging. Introduction

The rapid aging of the global population has necessitated a greater emphasis on the study of aging, a multifaceted and intricate process that manifests in biological, molecular, phenotypic, and functional ways1,2. This process, impacted by genetics, biology, and environmental factors, varies significantly between species and individuals3–5. Due to the inability of chronological age to accurately capture the diversity of aging, the concept of “biological age” has recently gained traction in the field of geroscience6,7. Unlike chronological age, which only records the passage of time, biological age measures an individual’s physiological health, taking into account lifestyle, genetics, and overall health to calculate aging speed8–10. Among the various biological aging metrics, Levine et al.’s PhenoAge algorithm is particularly noteworthy for its accuracy and precision11. By computing biological age using blood chemistry-derived measurements, it provides a method for gauging the effects of various exposures on aging and proposes potential anti-aging treatments or preventative programs. Accelerated biological aging, characterized by a quicker rise in biological age compared to chronological age, leads to a greater prevalence of chronic diseases in later life, thus posing a considerable societal dilemma12. This highlights the potential usefulness of treatments or preventative programs targeting aging to defer the severity or onset of most chronic diseases.

Cardiovascular disease, a major factor in both morbidity and healthcare costs, is strongly linked to the aging process13. In 2010, the American Heart Association (AHA) introduced Life’s Simple 7 (LS7), a concept which focused on the significance of cardiovascular health (CVH) for overall wellbeing and longevity14,15. This concept comprised of seven components: body mass index, physical activity, diet, cigarette smoking, blood pressure, blood glucose, and cholesterol14. When these elements were optimized, it was found to result in improved cardiovascular health, increased longevity, and enhanced quality of life16,17. Joyce et al. demonstrated a correlation between elevated LS7 scores and epigenetic age, suggesting that epigenetic age could serve as a valuable biomarker for assessing the risk of cardiovascular disease18. Over the years, accumulating evidence and experience had underscored the value of LS7 as a powerful health construct, but also had revealed its limitations.

Responding to these insights and the need for a more comprehensive assessment, the AHA introduced an updated algorithm in 2022, known as the Life’s Essential 8 (LE8)19. This enhanced metric incorporates sleep health as an additional component and employs a revised scoring system, thereby offering a more comprehensive evaluation of cardiovascular health. Recent studies have demonstrated that individuals with higher LE8 scores, which measure cardiovascular health, are at a lower risk of developing chronic conditions such as diabetes, cancer, and dementia, as well as a diminished risk of cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality20–22. Of note, whether cardiovascular health defined by the LE8 could influence biological age has not been extensively investigated. This study seeks to bridge the gap in knowledge by assessing the association between the LE8 score and adults from a large population in the US. Furthermore, we will investigate the association between individual LE8 scores and biological aging. A better understanding of the molecular and biological processes associated with these relationships may improve our understanding of their intricate relationship with health, potentially offering new methods to detect and prevent age- and lifestyle-related chronic diseases.

Method

This study utilizes data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) conducted between 2005 and 2018. The NHANES is an ongoing survey of the noninstitutionalized civilian population in the United States, utilizing a complex, stratified, multistage probability cluster sampling design. Further information regarding the inception, components, and activities of the NHANES can be found elsewhere23. The NHANES was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and all individuals involved were informed of the details before signing a written informed consent. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. As the initial collection of sleep health information (for LE8) occurred in the 2005–2006 NHANES and C-reactive protein information (for Phenotypic age) was not available from 2011 to 2014, we retrieved participants from five survey cycles, namely 2005–2006, 2007–2008, 2009–2010, 2015–2016, and 2017–2018, respectively. A total of 39,749 adults aged 20 or over were identified as having Phenotypic age biomarkers across the five survey cycles. After eliminating those with missing data on CVH (n = 17,226) or those with missing potential covariates (n = 3204), a total of 17,153 adults aged 20 or older were included in this study, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Definitions of new Life’s essential 8 scores

New Life’s Essential 8 consisted of four health behaviors and four health factors to assess cardiovascular health19. The four health behaviors consist of diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, and sleep, while the four health factors are body mass index, blood lipids, glucose, and blood pressure. Detailed algorithms to compute each metric of the LE8 scores had been previously documented and could be found in Table S119. According to previous studies, individuals with a LE8 score of 80–100 were categorized as having a high CVH group, those with a score of 50–79 as a moderate CVH group, and those with a score of 0–49 as a low CVH group21,24. This definition was used to quantify and classify health behavior and health factor scores for further analysis, based on a set of predefined cut-offs. This study collected dietary information through a self-administered food frequency questionnaire. Participants who supplied dietary data for two days were included in the data analysis, while those with only one day of data were not considered. To evaluate dietary quality at the population level, the Healthy Eating Index 2015 (HEI-2015) was taken into consideration. The final total LE8 score was computed by averaging all eight individual cardiovascular metric scores, in line with AHA recommendations.

Assessment of PhenoAge advancement and acceleration of biological aging

In this study, we utilized the PhenoAge algorithm, which is the most reliable and validated method, to measure biological age based on blood chemistry-derived measures. The PhenoAge algorithm, developed by Dr. Morgan Levine and her team, was a groundbreaking method for determining biological age11. The detailed description of this algorithm has been previously published elsewhere. The PhenoAge algorithm was created using elastic-net Gompertz regression to study the mortality rate of individuals observed in the NHANES III. This algorithm was based on nine blood chemistries, including mean cell volume, red cell distribution width, white blood cell count, lymphocyte proportion, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, creatinine, C-reactive protein, and glucose, in combination with chronological age. Participants with missing values were not included in this analysis. Evidence indicated that the PhenoAge algorithm was a multi-faceted, dynamic, and predictive measure of biological age that could provide valuable insights into an individual’s health and longevity. The ‘BioAge’ R package was employed to calculate biological age values. The PhenoAge advancement was calculated by subtracting Phenotypic age from chronological age. A positive PhenoAge advancement value indicates an advanced state of biological aging and an increased likelihood of diseases and mortality; in contrast, a negative value was determined as Phenotypic deaging, implying a reduced likelihood of diseases and mortality. In contrast, a negative value was identified, indicating delayed biological aging and suggesting a decreased susceptibility to diseases and mortality.

Assessments of covariates

The current study included the following covariates: age, gender, ethnicity, marital status, education level, family income-to-poverty ratio, drinking status, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and general health. In this study, age was stratified into three strata: 20–49 years, 50–69 years, and 70 years or older. Ethnicity was categorized as Non-Hispanic White, Mexican American, Non-Hispanic Black, and Others. Marital status was categorized as married/living with partner, never married, and widowed/divorced/separated. Education levels were categorized as graduate or less, high school, and more than high school. General health information was collected through self-reporting in the questionnaire and was categorized as poor/fair, good, and excellent/very good.

Statistical analysis

To take into account the complex sampling design, we used sample weights, stratification, and clustering to yield estimations that accurately represent the general population. Descriptive statistics were used to provide an overview of the basic characteristics of the participants, with the mean (standard deviation, SD) for continuous variables and the count (percentage) for categorical variables. To assess differences between the different CVH groups, the baseline characteristics were compared using the chi-squared test with Rao & Scott’s second-order correction for categorical variables and ANOVA for continuous variables. Survey-weighted multivariable linear regressions were conducted to examine the relationships between total and single LE8 metric scores (in both continuous and categorical forms) and PhenoAge advancement, after adjusting for age, gender, ethnicity, marital status, education level, family income-to-poverty ratio, drinking status, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and general health. After adjusting for age, gender, ethnicity, marital status, education level, family income-to-poverty ratio, drinking status, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and general health, multivariable logistic regressions were further used to assess the associations between total and single LE8 metric scores and the likelihood of biological aging (Phenotypic age deceleration as the reference). Subgroup analyses and interaction effect tests were conducted to investigate the association between total CVH metric score and biological age, stratified by age group, gender, ethnicity, educational level, marital status, family income to poverty ratio, general health status and survey cycles. To assess the reliability of our results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis that excluded adults with prior cardiovascular disease and diabetes. R software, version 4.2.0 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) was used to perform all analyses, and a two-sided P < 0.05 was taken to be statistically significant.

Results

The analysis was conducted on 17,153 individuals, with an average age of 47.54 years. A detailed selection flowchart was presented in Fig. 1. The average PhenoAge of the participants was 44.61 years, and their PhenoAge advancement was − 2.93 years, which suggests that their biological age was lower than their chronological age by 2.93 years. The baseline characteristics of the participants, estimated by the LE8 score, were divided into three categories of total CVH score (low, intermediate, and high), which were presented in Table 1. The average LE8 score was 68, with 13% being classified as low, 69% as moderate, and 18% as high CVH. Generally, individuals with higher levels of CVH were younger, female, non-Hispanic white, had a higher educational background, were married, had a high ratio of family income to poverty, and had mild drinking habits (all P < 0.001). Those with increased CVH scores had a decrease in PhenoAge and a slower rate of PhenoAge advancement.

Table 2 presented the association between the LE8 score and PhenoAge advancement, as well as biological aging. After controlling for multiple variables, a 10-point increase in LE8 score was significantly linked to a decrease of 1.22 years in PhenoAge advancement (β = − 1.22, 95%CI = − 1.31, − 1.12) and a lower likelihood of Phenotypic aging (OR = 0.65, 95%CI = 0.63,0.67) (Table 2). Compared to the low CVH group, the moderate CVH group showed a decrease of 3.37 years in PhenoAge advancement (β = − 3.37, 95%CI= − 3.85, − 2.89) and the high CVH group showed a further decrease of 5.2 years (β = − 5.20, 95%CI= − 5.70, − 4.70, Table 2). A statistically significant association was observed between higher CVH scores and accelerated PhenoAge progression (P-trend < 0.01). Additionally, moderate (OR = 0.42, 95%CI = 0.37, 0.47) and high (OR = 0.17, 95%CI = 0.15, 0.21) CVH groups had a significantly reduced likelihood of Phenotypic aging (Table 2). To further investigate the association between biological aging and both the health behaviors score and health factors score, we conducted an additional exploration. We observed a similar pattern for both scores; health behaviors score and health factors score were significantly linked to PhenoAge progression and biological aging (Table 2). All individual CVH scores in the high group demonstrated a significant connection to PhenoAge advancement and the likelihood of faster Phenotypic aging (all P < 0.05, Table 3).

In order to confirm the robustness of our findings, we conducted subgroup and sensitivity analyses; the results of these analyses are outlined in the Supplementary Materials (SM). All subgroups showed a significant association between LE8 score and both PhenoAge advancement and the likelihood of biological aging. The interaction effect test was conducted, revealing that age, education level, and general health exhibited interaction effects on the correlation between LE8 and PhenoAge advancement. This suggests that the impact of LE8 on biological aging may vary based on an individual’s age, education level, and general health status. After excluding participants with a history of cardiovascular disease (CVD), the association of LE8 score with PhenoAge advancement (β for a 10-point increase in LE8 score: -0.87; 95% CI − 0.99– − 0.75; β for intermediate: − 1.84; 95% CI: − 2.58, -1.11; β for high: − 3.46; 95% CI: − 4.22, − 2.69) and the likelihood of biological aging remained significant (OR for a 10-point increase in LE8 score: 0.68; 95% CI: 0.64, 0.72; β for intermediate: 0.49; 95% CI: 0.38, 0.62; β for high: 0.22; 95% CI: 0.17, 0.29).

Discussion

In this current representative survey study, we revealed an inverse relationship between the LE8 score and PhenoAge advancement, as well as the likelihood of biological aging. Additionally, the individual LE8 score showed a similar trend. Subgroup analysis further confirmed that the associations were consistent among subgroups and remained unchanged even after excluding individuals with a history of CVD. Our findings support the importance of maintaining an optimal LE8 score in reducing the risk of biological aging. The LE8 score represents key lifestyle factors that significantly contribute to decelerating an individual’s biological aging. It had been established through prior studies that adhering to LS7, which encompassed ideal cardiovascular health, could be a safeguard against the development of aging and aging-related diseases25–27. Pottinger et al. discovered a favorable link between an optimal LS7 score and prolonged lifespan in their cross-sectional analysis28. Lemke et al. provided evidence indicating that a higher LS7 score correlated with a deceleration in biological aging as assessed by the epigenetic clock29. Telomere shortening is a well-established characteristic of senescence and aging, with leukocyte telomere length (LTL) acknowledged as a reliable biological aging indicator30. Previous research had shown a correlation between individuals with above-average cardiovascular health, as defined by Life’s Simple 7 metrics, and longer LTL31. In a similar vein, Gebreab et al. identified a significant link between the Life’s Simple 7 score and leukocyte telomere length specifically in females32. In comparison to Life’s Simple 7, Life’s Essential 8 has incorporated sleep health as a new component of its cardiovascular health metrics, and has also revised the definition and introduced a new quantitative assessment of the same19. In the current study, it was also noted that individuals displaying elevated LE8 scores were less likely to experience biological aging, consistent with findings from previous studies. Carbonneau found that DNA methylation-based epigenetic age scores mediated the associations between the LE8 score and cardiovascular disease, mortality, particularly in individuals with a heightened genetic predisposition towards accelerated epigenetic aging33. The implications for public health were profound, in light of another study’s findings on the correlation between LE8 score and phenotypic aging. This study revealed that a one-year increase in biological aging corresponded to a 9% increase in mortality risk34. In summary, these studies presented convincing evidence that attaining and sustaining optimal cardiovascular health as defined by the LE8 score could yield transformative health advantages, slowed down the aging process, and foster healthy aging.

For the individual components of health behaviors, we observed that diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, and sleep health were all negatively associated with PhenoAge advancement and likelihood of biological aging. In the most recent version of LE8, population-level dietary quality was determined by employing either the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension diet (DASH) or HEI-201519. The findings of this study indicate that maintaining a high-quality diet is connected to a lower chance of accelerated biological aging, consistent with established research. Kresovich et al. found that four dietary indexes, that were based on nutritional recommendations (DASH, HEI-2015, Alternative HEI-2015, and the Alternative Mediterranean diet), had an inverse association with methylation-based measures of biological age35. Abigail and colleagues underscored the significance of a dose-dependent correlation between diet quality and the dual outcomes of longevity and successful aging36. These findings indicated that following healthy dietary patterns might yield beneficial impacts on age-related conditions. It was well-known that engaging in sufficient physical activity can significantly reduce the likelihood of death and aging37–39. In the present study, we validated the updated scoring system for physical activity in LE8 and underscored its significance in reducing the risk of aging. This supported the critical role of physical exercise in retarding the aging process and fostering healthy aging. With the growing popularity of e-cigarettes, tobacco use continued to be a major issue in public health40. Previous studies had established a correlation between smoking habits and aging characteristics41. Our study revealed that nicotine exposure, as assessed by LE8, correlated with accelerated biological aging, underscoring the importance of increasing awareness about this relationship. The AHA made a significant alteration to LE8 by including a measure for sleep in addition to the original 7 CVH metrics in LS719. Previous studies had provided support for a significant association between sleep quality and aging process42. Our findings also showed that an increase in the sleep metric score was associated with a decrease in the likelihood of biological age acceleration, underscoring the importance of preserving good sleep quality in promoting healthy aging. Overall, aforementioned health behaviors appeared to be an effective way to optimize cardiovascular health and consequently stave off aging.

For the individual components of health factors, body mass index, blood glucose and blood pressure had a negative association with PhenoAge advancement and the likelihood of biological aging, whereas blood lipids had a positive association with both PhenoAge advancement and the likelihood of biological aging. Evidence was accumulating that lipid metabolism was a significant factor in the aging process43. Lundgren’s study demonstrated a substantial connection between BMI, insulin resistance, and the rate of aging as determined by GrimAge44. Jové et al. conducted a study to identify a lipidomic signature that is correlated with extreme longevity in humans45. Tam et al. demonstrated a parallel spectrum of phenotypes between obesity and aging46. In line with prior research, individuals with lower BMI and blood lipid levels in current study were found to be more susceptible to phenotypic aging. It was a widely accepted fact that high blood glucose levels can be detrimental to one’s health. It had been revealed by a study that higher glucose levels are connected to a higher biological age and a greater risk of mortality from all causes47. Besides, individuals with diabetes had an increased biological age, regardless of the underlying pathophysiology48. In this study, our findings indicated that a higher blood glucose score had a significant impact on delaying the aging process. Hypertension was a major risk factor for cardiovascular events, and controlling blood pressure was a key step in reducing the risk of cardiovascular events and maintaining cardiovascular health49. Blood pressure tends to fluctuate with age, often characterized by an increase in systolic pressure while diastolic pressure remains stable or decreases. These fluctuations could be associated with the biological aging process. A previous study indicated that elevated systolic blood pressure and pulse pressure were linked to accelerated epigenetic aging50. Likewise, Dintica et al. found a correlation between elevated blood pressure levels during early to mid-adulthood and accelerated brain aging in mid-life51. In line with prior research findings, our study confirms the notable influence of elevated blood pressure on susceptibility to phenotypic aging. The exact biological processes associated with this finding were yet to be determined, and more research was required to validate and investigate this finding.

This study has several strengths, such as the utilization of a large and representative sample of US adults. Additionally, the dose-response relationship between LE8 and biological age was explored, and the minimal amount for the beneficial connection was identified. However, there are some potential limitations to consider. Self-reported questionnaires were used to assess health behavior metrics, which could lead to recall bias and measurement errors. In the current study, only the measurement of biological aging was conducted, focusing on the well-validated and widely recognized PhenoAge marker. However, it is recommended that other measures of biological aging be explored in future research. Additionally, despite adjusting for multiple variables, additional confounding factors, both measured and unmeasured, may have impacted the observed associations. Additionally, the cross-sectional study design does not allow for a causal or temporal relationship between LE8 and biological age to be determined. Lastly, due to the potential for discrepancies between other populations, it is essential to be cautious when attempting to apply these results to other contexts.

Conclusions

In the current study, we demonstrated that a higher LE8 score was linked to a lower PhenoAge advancement and a decreased chance of phenotypic aging. Our study results indicate that LE8 could be a viable and effective way to promote anti-aging. It is essential to conduct further research into the longitudinal and causal relationship between LE8 and the potential for accelerated biological aging.
