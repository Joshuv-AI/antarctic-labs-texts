Extubation failure in very preterm infants: associations with respiratory outcomes in a nine-year cohort study
Authors: Kamal Ali, Talal Aljarbou, Saleh Algarni, Saad Alshareedah, Abadi Ghazwani, Naif Alotaibi, Maather Almutairi, Moudhi Alhumaidi, Nouf Alayyar, Mohammed AImahdi, Musab Alshareef, Ahmed Alwatban, Mohamed Khawaji, Abdulaziz Homedi, Saif Alsaif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExtubation failure is a common event in very preterm infants and has been associated with adverse respiratory and clinical outcomes. However, it remains unclear whether extubation failure independently contributes to subsequent morbidity or primarily reflects underlying respiratory disease severity.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of ventilated infants born at less than 32 weeks of gestation in a tertiary neonatal intensive care unit over a 9-year period. Infants who underwent at least one planned extubation attempt were included. Extubation failure was defined as the need for reintubation within 72 h of planned extubation. Multivariate analyses were performed to identify independent predictors and to evaluate associations with moderate-to-severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), duration of invasive ventilation, length of hospital stay, and infection-related morbidity.ResultsA total of 877 infants were included, of whom 89 (10.1%) experienced extubation failure. The incidence demonstrated a strong gestational age gradient, decreasing from 22.5% at less than 26 weeks to 4.9% at 30–31 + 6 weeks (p < 0.001). In multivariate analysis (n = 858), gestational age remained the only independent predictor of extubation failure among the variables available for analysis (adjusted OR 0.78 per week, 95% CI 0.65–0.94; p = 0.007), while birth weight, Apgar score, antenatal steroid exposure, surfactant requirement, early pulmonary complications, and initial respiratory strategy were not independently associated. Among survivors (n = 811), extubation failure was associated with a higher incidence of moderate-to-severe BPD (50.6% vs. 18.7%; adjusted OR 3.07, 95% CI 1.79–5.24; p < 0.001). Infants with extubation failure also had significantly greater cumulative exposure to invasive ventilation (median 31 vs. 6 days; adjusted ratio 2.01, 95% CI 1.64–2.45; p < 0.001) and longer hospital stay (median 97 vs. 59 days; adjusted ratio 1.40, 95% CI 1.21–1.63; p < 0.001). Infection-related morbidities were more frequent, including late-onset sepsis (37.1% vs. 18.1%, p < 0.001) and ventilator-associated pneumonia (28.1% vs. 10.0%, p < 0.001).ConclusionExtubation failure in very preterm infants was most strongly associated with lower gestational age and with a more adverse respiratory course, increased infection-related morbidity, and greater healthcare utilisation. Gestational age should be interpreted as one component of extubation risk assessment rather than as a standalone determinant of extubation readiness. Overall, these findings indicate that extubation failure identifies infants at increased risk of subsequent respiratory morbidity but should not be interpreted as an independent causal determinant of adverse outcomes.

Introduction

Preterm infants frequently require invasive mechanical ventilation because of surfactant deficiency, respiratory immaturity, and evolving lung disease. Although early extubation is a central objective of neonatal respiratory management, extubation failure remains a common complication among very preterm infants and has been associated with cardiorespiratory instability, prolonged respiratory support, and increased risk of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) [1–3]. Reported extubation failure rates range from 10% to 30%, with the highest rates consistently observed among infants of lower gestational age and birth weight [2, 4–7].

A wide range of clinical factors have been associated with extubation outcomes, including gestational age, birth weight, respiratory severity, ventilatory requirements, and oxygen exposure [8, 9]. Extubation failure has also been associated with prolonged respiratory support, longer hospitalisation, and an increased risk of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) [10, 11]. However, because infants who experience extubation failure are typically more premature and have greater baseline respiratory disease severity, it remains uncertain whether extubation failure independently contributes to these adverse outcomes or primarily identifies infants at higher underlying risk.

We therefore conducted a retrospective cohort study of ventilated infants born at less than 32 weeks of gestation admitted to a tertiary neonatal intensive care unit over a nine-year period. The objectives were to determine the incidence of extubation failure across gestational age strata, identify independent predictors of extubation failure, and evaluate its association with moderate-to-severe BPD, duration of invasive ventilation, and length of hospital stay.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in a tertiary level III neonatal intensive care unit at King Abdulaziz Medical City, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, between January 2017 and December 2025. The study included preterm infants born at less than 32 weeks of gestation who required invasive mechanical ventilation during their admission and underwent at least one planned extubation attempt. Infants without a documented extubation outcome were excluded.

Definitions

Extubation failure was defined as the need for reintubation within 72 h after a planned extubation attempt during the same admission. A 72-hour definition was selected because it has been widely used in neonatal studies of extubation outcomes and captures most clinically relevant extubation failures while reducing the likelihood of including later respiratory deterioration unrelated to the initial extubation attempt [3, 12, 13]. Decisions regarding extubation readiness and reintubation were made by the attending neonatal team according to routine unit practice. Although no formal standardized extubation protocol was used, clinical decisions were based on assessment of ventilatory stability, spontaneous respiratory effort, blood gas parameters, and oxygen requirement.

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) severity was classified as grade 0, 1, 2, or 3, and moderate or severe BPD was defined as grade ≥ 2 in accordance with contemporary classification systems [10]. The composite adverse outcome was defined as death before hospital discharge or moderate-to- severe BPD.

Acute pulmonary complications included pulmonary haemorrhage and pneumothorax. Patent ductus arteriosus treatment was defined as medical or surgical closure during admission. Late-onset sepsis was defined as culture-proven infection occurring after 72 h of life, and ventilator-associated pneumonia was diagnosed according to institutional clinical and microbiological criteria [14]. Severe intraventricular haemorrhage was defined as grade III or IV according to the Papile classification [15], and Periventricular leukomalacia was defined as cystic periventricular leukomalacia diagnosed on cranial ultrasonography, with magnetic resonance imaging used for confirmation when clinically indicated. Length of stay was calculated from birth to hospital discharge.

Data collection

Baseline variables before the first planned extubation

Maternal, perinatal, and neonatal variables were extracted from the institutional electronic neonatal database. Baseline variables included gestational age, birth weight, sex, Apgar score at 5 min, antenatal steroid exposure, mode of delivery, prolonged rupture of membranes, surfactant administration, pulmonary haemorrhage, pneumothorax, respiratory support strategy immediately before the first planned extubation attempt, and time to the first planned extubation.

Post-extubation respiratory course

Variables describing the subsequent respiratory course during the remainder of the hospital admission included cumulative duration of invasive mechanical ventilation, duration of non-invasive mechanical ventilation, duration of high-flow nasal cannula support, exposure to high-frequency oscillatory ventilation, inhaled nitric oxide exposure and duration, and postnatal corticosteroid therapy. Major neonatal morbidities recorded during admission included patent ductus arteriosus treatment, late-onset sepsis, ventilator-associated pneumonia, severe intraventricular haemorrhage, and periventricular leukomalacia. Home oxygen requirement at discharge was also recorded.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was extubation failure following a planned extubation attempt.

Secondary outcomes included bronchopulmonary dysplasia severity, moderate-to- severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia among survivors, cumulative duration of invasive ventilation, length of hospital stay, home oxygen requirement at discharge, major neonatal morbidities, and the composite outcome of death before discharge or moderate- -to-severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarised as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range] according to distribution. Between-group comparisons were performed using Welch’s t-test for approximately normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using χ² tests or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Extubation failure incidence across gestational age strata was compared using χ² testing, and ordered trends across gestational age categories were assessed using a χ² test for trend. Absolute risk differences were calculated for categorical variables, and standardised mean differences and standardised differences were reported for continuous and categorical variables, respectively.

Factors associated with extubation failure were examined using multivariate logistic regression. Only variables available before or at the time of the first planned extubation attempt were considered candidate predictors in Model A. Variables describing the subsequent respiratory course were analysed separately as downstream outcomes and were not entered into the prediction model. Covariates were selected a priori on the basis of clinical relevance and previous literature and included gestational age (per week), birth weight (per 100 g), Apgar score at 5 min, sex, prolonged rupture of membranes, mode of delivery, antenatal steroid exposure, receipt of two or more surfactant doses, pulmonary haemorrhage, pneumothorax, and respiratory support strategy immediately before the first planned extubation attempt. Adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors. Model performance was evaluated by discrimination using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and explained variance using Nagelkerke R².

Associations between extubation failure and subsequent clinical outcomes were evaluated using multivariate regression models. Logistic regression was used to assess moderate-to-severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia among survivors and the composite outcome of death before discharge or moderate-to-severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia, adjusted for gestational age, birth weight, and sex.

For skewed continuous outcomes, including cumulative duration of invasive ventilation and length of hospital stay, linear regression models were fitted using log (1 + days) transformation, and exponentiated coefficients were reported as ratios.

Predicted probabilities of extubation failure across gestational age were calculated from the fitted multivariate logistic regression model using the estimated regression coefficients, while holding the remaining covariates at their observed values. These model-derived predicted probabilities were plotted to illustrate the adjusted relationship between gestational age and the probability of extubation failure.

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Analyses were performed using complete-case analysis, with infants missing one or more variables required for a given multivariate model excluded from that analysis. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p value < 0.05.

Results

Study population

The study population is summarized in Fig. 1. During the study period, 1,259 infants born at less than 32 weeks of gestation were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 382 infants were excluded, including 91 who died before assessment and 291 who did not require intubation/mechanical ventilation. The final study cohort comprised 877 ventilated infants who underwent at least one planned extubation attempt. Extubation failure, defined as reintubation within 72 h of planned extubation, occurred in 89 infants (10.1%), while 788 infants (89.9%) were successfully extubated.

Tables 6 and 7 presents the multivariate models examining factors associated with extubation failure and downstream outcomes. In Model A, gestational age was the only variable independently associated with extubation failure among the variables included in the multivariate model (adjusted OR 0.78 per week increase, 95% CI 0.65–0.94; p = 0.007). Birth weight, sex, antenatal steroid exposure, receipt of two or more surfactant doses, pulmonary haemorrhage, pneumothorax, respiratory support strategy immediately before the first planned extubation attempt, and other perinatal variables were not independently associated with extubation failure after adjustment. The model demonstrated acceptable discrimination (AUC 0.706), good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit p = 0.146), and modest explanatory performance (Nagelkerke R²=0.099).

In Model B, extubation failure was independently associated with moderate-to-severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia among survivors (adjusted OR 3.07, 95% CI 1.79–5.24; p < 0.001), longer hospital stay (adjusted ratio 1.40, 95% CI 1.21–1.63; p < 0.001), and greater cumulative exposure to invasive ventilation (adjusted ratio 2.01, 95% CI 1.64–2.45; p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of ventilated infants born before 32 weeks of gestation, extubation failure occurred in 10% of infants and demonstrated a clear inverse relationship with gestational age, with the highest rates observed in those born before 26 weeks. In multivariate analysis, gestational age was the only variable independently associated with extubation failure among the variables included in the multivariate model, while perinatal characteristics, early pulmonary complications, and respiratory support strategy were not independently associated. These findings suggest that gestational maturity is an important component of extubation risk assessment in very preterm infants. Among survivors, extubation failure was associated with higher rates of moderate-to-severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia, longer duration of invasive ventilation, and prolonged hospital stay. Extubation failure was also associated with greater subsequent use of escalation therapies, including high-frequency oscillatory ventilation, inhaled nitric oxide, and postnatal corticosteroid therapy, consistent with a more severe subsequent respiratory course rather than baseline predictors of extubation failure.

Compared with previously published studies of extubation outcomes in preterm infants, the present cohort included 877 ventilated infants who underwent a planned extubation attempt. This cohort is larger than the 174 extremely preterm infants studied by Manley et al. [12] and the 216 infants analysed by Shalish et al. [3] and is similar in size to the 926 infants included in the SUPPORT secondary analysis reported by Chawla et al. [13]. Manley et al. focused on predictors of extubation success and reported associations between extubation failure and later respiratory morbidity, while Chawla et al. identified factors associated with successful extubation and reported higher risks of mortality and bronchopulmonary dysplasia following extubation failure [12, 13]. Shalish et al. [3], and Li et al. [16] examined the timing of reintubation and its association with composite respiratory outcomes. In addition to examining variables associated with extubation failure, our study evaluated the relationship between extubation failure and respiratory outcomes, duration of invasive ventilation, length of hospital stay, and selected neonatal morbidities within a single cohort of very preterm infants. Although the association between lower gestational age and extubation failure is well recognised, our findings extend previous work by providing contemporary data from a large nine-year cohort managed within a single tertiary neonatal intensive care unit. In addition to confirming gestational age as an important component of extubation risk assessment, our findings further quantify the respiratory burden associated with extubation failure, including prolonged invasive ventilation, greater use of respiratory support, and increased respiratory morbidity within contemporary neonatal practice.

The association between lower gestational age and extubation failure observed in our cohort is consistent with previous reports. Extubation failure occurred more frequently among infants born at the lowest gestational ages, and gestational age was the only variable independently associated with extubation failure after adjustment for other perinatal and respiratory variables. Similar observations have been reported by Chawla et al. and Giaccone et al., who identified gestational age as one of the most consistent factors associated with extubation outcome in very preterm infants [13, 17]. Lower gestational age is associated with reduced respiratory reserve and greater susceptibility to respiratory instability following extubation, which may contribute to the higher rates of reintubation observed in this population [13, 18]. Taken together, these findings suggest that gestational maturity is an important component of extubation risk assessment. However, gestational age alone is unlikely to be sufficient for clinical decision-making, and extubation readiness should continue to be assessed using a broader evaluation incorporating physiological status, ventilatory requirements, and overall clinical stability.

In line with the observed associations between extubation failure and subsequent respiratory course, extubation failure was independently associated with moderate-to-severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia among survivors. This finding is consistent with the concept that repeated or prolonged exposure to invasive ventilation contributes to lung injury through volutrauma, atelectrauma, and oxygen toxicity [19]. Bancalari and Jain have highlighted that cumulative ventilator exposure remains a key driver of evolving BPD even under current definitions [20], and data from large neonatal networks demonstrate a strong relationship between duration of mechanical ventilation and BPD severity [21]. Extubation failure was also associated with longer duration of invasive ventilation and prolonged hospital stay. Previous cohort studies have reported similar associations, linking reintubation events with longer respiratory support trajectories and extended NICU admission [22, 23]. Although causality cannot be inferred from observational data, these findings suggest that extubation failure identifies infants at increased risk of BPD and greater resource utilisation. This association may reflect a more severe underlying respiratory course, with increased exposure to complications such as late-onset sepsis and ventilator-associated pneumonia.

These findings have implications for clinical practice and outcome evaluation in neonatal intensive care. Extubation failure may serve as a useful marker of subsequent respiratory morbidity and resource utilisation, but its interpretation should account for gestational age and underlying illness severity. Strategies aimed at reducing cumulative exposure to invasive ventilation remain central to improving outcomes, while extubation decisions in very preterm infants should be individualised and interpreted in the context of maturity and clinical course rather than used as a standalone performance metric.

This study has several strengths. It included a large cohort of ventilated infants born before 32 weeks of gestation, allowing evaluation of extubation outcomes across a broad range of gestational ages. The dataset contained detailed information on respiratory support and subsequent clinical outcomes, permitting assessment of factors associated with extubation failure as well as its relationship with later respiratory morbidity and resource utilisation. Bronchopulmonary dysplasia was classified using contemporary criteria, and analyses were adjusted for clinically relevant perinatal and respiratory variables.

Several limitations should also be considered. The retrospective design precludes causal inference. Nevertheless, the large cohort size and inclusion of key perinatal and respiratory variables allowed adjustment for several important sources of confounding. Extubation readiness criteria were not standardised and may have varied between clinicians, although decisions were made within a single tertiary neonatal intensive care unit using established clinical practice. While this reflects real-world clinical practice in many tertiary neonatal intensive care units, variability in clinician decision-making may have influenced the timing of extubation and reintubation. In addition, detailed ventilator settings, oxygen requirements, and other physiological markers of extubation readiness at the time of planned extubation were not consistently available, limiting assessment of physiological readiness for extubation. Residual confounding by unmeasured markers of respiratory severity at the time of extubation may have influenced the observed associations. Consequently, the present analysis should be interpreted as identifying factors associated with extubation failure rather than providing a clinically validated prediction model for extubation readiness. Despite these limitations, major respiratory variables and measures of respiratory support throughout the admission were available and included in the analyses. Respiratory management practices may also have evolved during the nine-year study period, including the introduction of less invasive surfactant administration (LISA) late in 2023. However, only 43 infants received LISA during the study period, precluding meaningful assessment of its influence on extubation outcomes. Furthermore, as a single-centre study, the findings may not be fully generalisable to units with different patient populations or respiratory management approaches. The observed associations are, however, consistent with those reported in previous studies of extubation outcomes in very preterm infants.

Conclusion

Extubation failure in very preterm infants was associated with lower gestational age and less favourable respiratory outcomes, including moderate-to-severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia, longer duration of invasive ventilation, and prolonged hospital stay. After adjustment for the available perinatal and respiratory variables, gestational age was the only variable independently associated with extubation failure. Nevertheless, gestational age should be interpreted as one component of a broader clinical assessment, as important physiological markers of extubation readiness were not consistently available. These findings suggest that extubation failure identifies a subgroup of infants at increased risk of subsequent respiratory morbidity and prolonged hospitalisation. Future prospective studies incorporating physiological variables immediately before extubation are needed to develop and externally validate clinically useful prediction models for extubation readiness in very preterm infants.
