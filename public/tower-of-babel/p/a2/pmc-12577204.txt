Flexion gap discrepancy drives femoral rotational adjustment in robot-assisted TKA: a CPAK-Based phenotypic analysis
Authors: Haoyang Liu, Hongxu Li, Mengran Shen, Debo Yue, Bailiang Wang, Jinhui Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study investigates the correlation between femoral component rotational adjustment strategies and coronal alignment parameters in robot-assisted total knee arthroplasty (TKA), and explores differences among Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) subtypes.MethodsA retrospective analysis of 100 patients with varus gonarthrosis who underwent robot-assisted total knee arthroplasty (TKA) was conducted. Intraoperative navigation data quantified medial/lateral flexion-extension gaps, physiological external rotation, and femoral rotational adjustments. Preoperative CT-based 3D reconstruction, CPAK classification (types I/IV/VII), and radiographic parameters (HKA, lateral distal femoral angle [LDFA], medial proximal tibial angle [MPTA]) were analysed using one-way ANOVA and multivariate linear regression to identify predictors of rotational adjustments and subtype differences.ResultsPost-adjustment femoral external rotation angles exhibited a unimodal distribution (4.41°±2.28°), with 52% of cases concentrated in the 3°–5° range. This represented a significant increase from preoperative physiological external rotation (3.49°±2.17°, Δ = 1.39°, p < 0.001). No differences in final external rotation were observed among CPAK subtypes (p = 0.356), but type I required greater adjustments than type VII (Δ = 1.46°, p = 0.020). Intraoperative flexion gap discrepancy strongly predicted rotational adjustments (β = 0.342, p < 0.01). Adjusted cases exhibited smaller preoperative MPTA (83.37°±5.17 vs. 85.49°±3.40, p = 0.018) and larger preoperative flexion gap discrepancies (2.56 ± 3.33 vs. 0.86 ± 1.30 mm, p < 0.01).ConclusionRobot-assisted TKA optimizes femoral rotational alignment through dynamic gap balancing, with flexion gap discrepancy serving as the primary determinant for rotational compensation. CPAK classification provides preoperative identification of phenotypes requiring specific compensatory strategies, particularly type I deformities which necessitate greater external rotation adjustments. Robotic systems minimize alignment outliers and reduce soft-tissue releases, supporting the adoption of personalized, gap-balancing protocols in TKA.Keywords: Robot-assisted total knee arthroplasty, Femoral rotational alignment, CPAK classification, Gap balancing, Dynamic planning, Japan

Introduction

The precision of rotational alignment in femoral component positioning during total knee arthroplasty (TKA) critically impacts postoperative joint stability, patellar tracking, and long-term functional outcomes. Studies have demonstrated that malrotation of femoral components may lead to flexion-extension gap imbalance, accelerated polyethene liner wear, and even revision surgery. Traditional surgical techniques, relying on anatomic landmark-based referencing or gap balancing, remain operator-dependent, with their execution susceptible to subjective variations in surgical experience and limitations in intraoperative dynamic assessment. These technique-dependent shortcomings become particularly pronounced in cases complicated by severe bony deformities [1–3]. Research has demonstrated through the analysis of functional phenotypes that significant variations exist in knee joint morphology and biomechanics across different sexes and racial groups, specifically manifesting as distinct structural and functional characteristics [4–7]. Notably, patients with knee osteoarthritis exhibit substantial individual variability (1–10°) in physiological external rotation of the distal femur. Radiographic analyses indicate that approximately 30% of cases demonstrate external rotation angles exceeding 5 ° or measuring below 1° [8–10], whereas conventional osteotomy guides with preset external rotation values lack intraoperative real-time quantitative adjustment mechanisms. This technical limitation may compromise rotational prosthesis congruence in cases with complex anatomical variants [11].

In recent years, the robotic-assisted system has significantly enhanced osteotomy precision and improved control of gap balancing through three-dimensional preoperative planning, coupled with real-time intraoperative dynamic adjustments [12–14]. However, the existing literature predominantly focuses on the efficacy of robotic-assisted TKA in correcting coronal alignment and enhancing clinical outcomes [11–13], while systematic investigations into preoperative physiological femoral rotation distribution, intraoperative external rotation adjustment patterns, and their correlations with coronal plane malalignment remain insufficient [11, 15]. Notably, robotic-assisted TKA employing functional alignment (FA) strategies achieves superior clinical outcomes through quantitative preoperative measurement of femoral component external rotation angles combined with real-time intraoperative visual adjustments. This approach minimises soft tissue release while restoring native ligamentous tension, thereby optimising joint biomechanics [16]. Thus, in patients with varus knee deformities characterised by medial soft tissue contractures and osseous abnormalities, targeted adjustment of external rotation angles becomes imperative to achieve optimal gap balancing [17–19].

However, a critical gap persists in our understanding.​​ While robotic systems provide the capabilityfor precise adjustment, the principlesguiding these adjustments—specifically, how femoral rotational alignment dynamically interacts with coronal plane anatomy and soft-tissue tension—remain poorly defined. Existing literature on robotic TKA predominantly focuses on coronal alignment accuracy and clinical outcomes [16–18], yet ​​systematic investigations into the quantitative patterns of intraoperative femoral rotation adjustment and their correlation with preoperative coronal alignment parameters (e.g., HKA, LDFA, MPTA) are insufficient [11]. ​​Notably, it is unknown whether and how the Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) classification, which captures phenotypic variations in knee morphology [20, 21].

Therefore, the primary purpose of this retrospective study was to quantitatively investigate the determinants of femoral component external rotation adjustment in robot-assisted TKA for varus knees.​​ We hypothesized that: ​​(1) Intraoperative flexion gap discrepancy would be the primary driver for femoral external rotation adjustment,​​ rather than preoperative anatomical landmarks alone. ​​(2) The magnitude of required adjustment would vary significantly among different CPAK phenotypes,​​ given their distinct bony anatomies and soft-tissue envelopes.

By elucidating these relationships, this study aims to provide a ​​data-driven, personalized framework​​ for femoral rotation planning in robotic TKA. Our findings are anticipated to enhance the rationale for dynamic gap balancing, move beyond a “one-size-fits-all” approach, and ultimately contribute to improving the reproducibility and outcomes of personalized knee arthroplasty. This investigation is necessary because it translates the technological capability of robotics into actionable clinical intelligence, defining the “why” and “how much” behind the adjustment, which has not been comprehensively addressed in previous studies.

Methods

Patients enrollment

This study was conducted as a retrospective consecutive cohort analysis. All patients who underwent Mako robotic-arm assisted unilateral total knee arthroplasty for knee osteoarthritis by the same surgical team at the China-Japan Friendship Hospital Department of Orthopedics and Joint Surgery, between June 2023 and November 2024 were considered for inclusion. Initially, 107 cases presenting with varus alignment, defined as a preoperative hip-knee-ankle angle (HKA) of less than 175°, were identified. After excluding 7 cases due to secondary osteoarthritis (e.g., related to rheumatoid arthritis), incomplete preoperative full-length lower-limb radiographs, or prior surgical history of the ipsilateral limb, a final cohort of 100 consecutive cases was established. The sample size was determined a priori based on a power analysis using an effect size of 0.8, α of 0.05, and β of 0.2, indicating a minimum requirement of 98 cases. Thus, the inclusion of 100 cases exceeded this threshold and was deemed sufficient for statistical reliability. Demographic characteristics are detailed in Table 1. Inclusion criteria comprised:​​ (1) primary knee osteoarthritis, (2) patients with knee varus deformity, and (3) first-time TKA on the affected side. Exclusion criteria excluded:​​ (1) secondary knee osteoarthritis (such as cases associated with autoimmune disorders like rheumatoid arthritis), (2) presence of valgus knee deformity, (3) inadequate full-length lower-limb radiographs, (4) history of prior surgery on the same lower limb, (5) congenital bone dysplasia affecting the lower limbs, or (6) incomplete clinical documentation. In contrast to varus deformities, the compensatory alignment mechanisms in valgus knee deformities are not well characterized. Furthermore, robotic-assisted knee arthroplasty for valgus knees presents considerable difficulties, including complexities in preoperative planning, soft-tissue balancing, and accuracy of bone resection [22]. Moreover, the limited number of valgus cases within our cohort reduces the reliability of subgroup analyses. Therefore, patients with valgus deformities were excluded from this study.

Surgical procedure

Preoperative CT data were imported into the MAKO™ system for 3D modelling and preliminary plan generation, followed by surgeon-modified osteotomy planning and implant sizing under general anaesthesia with tourniquet application on the operative limb. A standard medial parapatellar approach facilitated joint exposure, synovial debridement, and resection of hypertrophic tissue. Navigation arrays and fiducial markers were affixed for ankle registration, followed by bone landmark registration and osteophyte resection [23]. Manual ligament balancing recorded flexion-extension gap metrics, guiding FA-TKA adjustments within validated coronal (± 3°)/sagittal (0°-10° flexion) parameters to optimise bone resection and minimise soft-tissue release. Femoral component rotation was dynamically calibrated (0°-6° external rotation relative to posterior condylar axis; 3° internal to 6° external relative to transepicondylar axis), while tibial alignment permitted ± 3° coronal variance and 0°-5° posterior slope, achieving final HKA of 177°-183° [24, 25]. For varus knees, intraoperative adjustments followed mechanistically validated protocols: medial extension gap tightness prompted femoral varus correction (1.2°±0.8° per mm imbalance), while medial flexion constraints mandated 2.3°-4.1° external rotation to prevent anterior femoral notch formation (p < 0.001 vs. neutral alignment). Combined medial compartment tightness necessitated tibial varus compensation (3.1°±1.2°) with concomitant posterior condylar resection (2.4 ± 0.9 mm) and tibial slope modifications (0.5° posteriorization per 1 mm flexion gap deficit), achieving balanced gaps (< 1 mm differential) with permissible lateral laxity (≤ 2 mm) [26]. The flexion-extension gap discrepancy should not exceed 1 mm, and slight lateral laxity is permissible to accommodate physiological soft tissue dynamics while avoiding over-constraint [27, 28]. The robotic arm then guided the oscillating saw to complete femoral and tibial osteotomies. After installing trial components and verifying appropriate knee stability through range-of-motion testing, definitive prostheses were cemented. Following cement curing, the joint was irrigated, and the incision was closed in layers.

Measurement indicators and patient re-grouping

First, preoperative femoral physiological external rotation angles—including measurements of the posterior condylar angle (PCA) and transepicondylar axis (TEA)—were obtained via CT-based 3D reconstruction. The robotic surgical system simultaneously recorded intraoperative data: pre-adjustment flexion/extension gap measurements after osteophyte removal and manual ligament balancing, final femoral component external rotation angles post-adjustment (relative to PCA and TEA offsets), and adjusted flexion/extension gap values. Differences between pre- and post-adjustment values were calculated, with the flexion gap discrepancy defined as lateral minus medial gap (mm) at 90° knee flexion, and extension gap discrepancy as lateral minus medial gap (mm) at full knee extension (Fig. 1).

Second, preoperative knee morphology was classified using the Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) classification. Arithmetic Hip-Knee-Ankle angle (aHKA), estimating constitutional limb alignment and Joint Line Obliquity (JLO), were calculated as follows: JLO = MPTA + LDFA; aHKA = MPTA − LDFA. All enrolled patients exhibited varus deformity (aHKA < -2°), enabling CPAK categorization: Type I​​: JLO < 180°; Type IV​​: JLO = 180°; Type VII​​: JLO >180° [20]. Furthermore, subgroup analysis of baseline characteristics was conducted based on CPAK classification (Types I, IV, and VII), with comparisons detailed in Table 2. Lower limb alignment assessment utilised preoperative weight-bearing full-length radiographs [29]. Measured parameters included: Pre-LDFA​​: Preoperative Lateral Distal Femoral Angle (°), quantified in the coronal plane; Pre-MPTA​​: Preoperative Medial Proximal Tibial Angle (°), assessed preoperatively. Measurement methodologies are illustrated in Fig. 2. All parameters were independently measured by two junior arthroplasty surgeons, with means calculated from their measurements. If discrepancies exceeded 1°, two senior arthroplasty surgeons remeasured and established consensus values. Interobserver agreement assessed via intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) demonstrated excellent reliability (ICC = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.76–0.91, p < 0.001). Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp.) for demographic data and measurement outcomes. Interobserver reliability was assessed via the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Continuous variables conforming to normal distribution (verified by the Shapiro-Wilk test) were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group comparisons utilised an independent samples t-test for between-group differences and a paired t-test for within-group comparisons. Categorical variables were analysed using the Chi-square (χ²) test. A significance level of α = 0.05 was applied. Pearson correlation coefficients quantified variable associations. A linear regression model with external rotation adjustment as the dependent variable was constructed, employing stepwise regression for variable selection and the variance inflation factor (VIF) for assessing multicollinearity. Histograms and box plots were generated using GraphPad Prism version 8.30 for Windows (GraphPad Software).

Results

Preoperative physiological external rotation angles followed a normal distribution (3.8° ± 1.8°, range: 0°–7.3°), while intraoperatively adjusted femoral rotation angles (4.4° ± 2.3°, range: 0°–11°) exhibited unimodal distribution (Fig. 3), with 52% of cases clustered within 3°–5° and merely 0.8% exceeding 10°. Significant disparity existed between preoperative CT-measured physiological external rotation (3.5° ± 2.2°) and robotically optimised final rotation (4.4° ± 2.3°, paired t-test: p < 0.001). Flexion gap discrepancy decreased from 2.40 ± 2.80 mm pre-adjustment to − 0.02 ± 0.64 mm post-adjustment (P < 0.001), while extension gap discrepancy reduced from 1.54 ± 3.33 mm to 0.25 ± 0.85 mm (P < 0.001).

Subgroup analysis revealed that final external rotation angles showed no intergroup variation (means: 4.5 ± 2.3° [Type I], 3.0 ± 1.1° [Type IV], 4.4 ± 2.0° [Type VII]; F = 1.046, p = 0.356). However, significant disparity existed in rotation correction between Types I and VII (Δ = 1.46°, p = 0.020) (Fig. 4). Discussion

This study provides an initial exploration of quantitative patterns in femoral rotational adjustment and their relationship with coronal alignment parameters in varus-aligned TKAs using a robotic system. Our findings of a physiological external rotation range and minimal outliers align with CT-navigated studies [30] and are consistent with the enhanced soft-tissue quantification capabilities of robotic systems, as reported in conventional TKA cohorts [31]. The effective optimization of flexion-extension gaps observed in our cohort further corroborates the gap-balancing principles emphasized by Scott et al. [24], highlighting the role of dynamic soft-tissue tension in guiding femoral component rotation.

The results demonstrated a statistically significant positive correlation between the magnitude of intraoperative femoral external rotation adjustment and pre-adjustment flexion gap discrepancy in both univariate and multivariate analyses. Notably, the adjusted group exhibited a 2.1° reduction in preoperative MPTA compared to the physiological external rotation preservation group. Additionally, the adjusted group showed a 1.7 mm increase in pre-adjustment mediolateral flexion gap discrepancy. These findings suggest that varus deformity-induced morphological alterations in the proximal tibia (reduced MPTA) synergistically contribute to flexion gap imbalance. Compensatory increases in femoral external rotation are therefore essential to achieve dynamic flexion-extension gap equilibrium [32]. This evidence provides a quantitative rationale for adjusting femoral rotation based on soft-tissue balancing in robotic-assisted TKA, particularly in cases with severe varus morphotypes [23]. Several studies have also indicated that severe varus knees are frequently associated with medial femoral posterior condyle wear and lateral soft tissue contracture [19, 33, 34]. This wear reduces the posterior condylar angle (PCA), which, ​​if the femoral component is aligned solely referencing the worn posterior condylar axis, would predispose to implant internal rotation [35]. However, to achieve a balanced flexion gap, this inherent tendency for internal rotation must be ​​counteracted​​ by applying additional external rotation. This ​​compensatory external rotation​​ is necessary to address the concomitant lateral soft tissue contracture. Thus, the robotic system does not simply ‘add’ external rotation but dynamically calculates the required compensation to neutralize the combined effects of bony wear and soft tissue imbalance, ensuring optimal gap kinematics [36]. Moreover, it is crucial to account for the patient’s native knee phenotype when defining ‘advanced’ deformities, as this aligns with the principles of patient-specific alignment strategies [7]. Based on high-precision osteotomy, osseous adjustments can more effectively compensate for soft tissue deformities, which also aligns with Hungerford’s theory [37].

This study also provides initial evidence on the relationship between CPAK classification and femoral rotational alignment parameters. The trend toward larger external rotation adjustments in CPAK Type I knees may be associated with subtype-specific compensatory mechanisms of the femoral posterior condyle [38], potentially explained by a greater compensatory convexity of the lateral condyle as noted in recent studies [39, 40]. Based on this, we propose a staged compensation protocol for CPAK Type I cases: When preoperative MPTA ≤ 85° and flexion gap discrepancy ≥ 2 mm (mean values in the adjusted cohort: 83.4° and 2.6 mm), the initial external rotation adjustment should be ≥ 3° with a 0.5°–1° incremental reserve to compensate for characteristic posterior condylar bone loss and lateral soft tissue tension abnormalities [41–43]. The significant adjustment difference between CPAK Types I and VII underscores the need for phenotype-aware planning. The unique varus deformity with low joint line alignment in Type I [20, 44] can induce perceptual bias, necessitating proactive adjustments that are facilitated by the precise bone resection and real-time feedback of robotic systems [22, 45]. Notably, no significant differences in rotational parameters were observed between CPAK retention and non-retention groups. This phenomenon reflects a paradigm shift from the “strict anatomical reconstruction” pursued in conventional TKA to robotic-assisted techniques prioritising functional alignment (e.g., restoration of knee kinematic axes), thereby attenuating the direct impact of CPAK classification on postoperative rotational parameters [23, 46, 47].Additionally, robotic-assisted precision soft tissue balancing may compensate for certain anatomical phenotypic differences. However, caution is warranted, as the long-term implications of this compensatory mechanism on implant survivorship require validation through extended follow-up. Future systems could integrate machine learning models to enable dynamic prediction of external rotation angles, potentially overcoming the limitations of empirical surgical approaches.

The limited association between ER adjustment and extension gap parameters underscores that extension phase imbalance is less dependent on femoral rotation, relying more on tibial resection and ligament balancing [37, 44, 48]. The robot-assisted system’s precision primarily optimizes ER-flexion gap congruence, while residual extension discrepancies likely originate from tibial component malrotation or persistent soft-tissue imbalance [12, 49, 50], highlighting areas for continued surgical focus.

This study has several limitations: Its single-centre retrospective design may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. The short-term follow-up prevents assessment of long-term outcomes. The cohort included only varus knees, restricting applicability to valgus deformities. Uneven sample distribution across CPAK subtypes reduced statistical power for sub-group comparisons. Soft-tissue tension was not quantitatively measured, and the use of a specific robotic system may affect the generalizability of findings to other platforms. Despite these limitations, this study provides a crucial quantitative foundation for understanding femoral rotational adjustment in robotic TKA. These identified constraints directly chart the course for future research, including multicenter prospective trials with long-term follow-up, the integration of machine learning for predictive adjustment modeling, and the expansion of this analytical framework to include valgus deformities and other robotic systems.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that in robotic-assisted TKA, ​​dynamic gap balancing is the principal determinant​​ of femoral component rotation. Preoperative flexion gap discrepancy is its key intraoperative predictor. CPAK classification ​​preoperatively identifies phenotypes​​ (like Type I) commonly associated with anatomic variants that require greater compensatory external rotation to achieve balanced gaps. Robotic technology enables this precision, minimizing outliers and soft-tissue releases. Thus, ​​integrating phenotypic anticipation with gap-balanced execution​​ advances personalized TKA.
