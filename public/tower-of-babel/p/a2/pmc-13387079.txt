Initial clinical outcomes of uniportal robotic-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy
Authors: Ting-Fang Kuo, Pei-Ming Huang, Ke-Cheng Chen, Mong-Wei Lin, Shuenn-Wen Kuo, Jang-Ming Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRobotic-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy has improved perioperative outcomes; increased surgical proficiency has promoted interest in uniportal techniques. We aimed to evaluate the perioperative and short-term outcomes of a novel uniportal robotic-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy performed through an intercostal incision using the da Vinci Xi system.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed patients who underwent uniportal robotic-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy between September 2024 and July 2025. The primary outcome was the complete resection rate with microscopically negative margins (R0). Secondary outcomes included perioperative and short-term outcomes, including operative time, lymph node yield, blood loss, conversion rate, postoperative pain scores, opioid requirements, complication rates, chest tube duration, and hospital stay.ResultsTwenty-one patients (median age, 64 years; 86% male) underwent uniportal robotic-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy. Squamous cell carcinoma was the most common histology (81%); 62% had stage III–IV disease, and 71% received neoadjuvant therapy. The McKeown and Ivor-Lewis procedures were performed in 52% and 48% of cases, respectively; complete resection was achieved in 95%. The median console time, lymph node yield, and blood loss were 155 min, 34, and 450 mL, respectively. One patient (5%) required conversion to thoracoscopic surgery. The postoperative pain scores were consistently low. The median chest tube duration and postoperative hospital stay were 5.5 and 15.0 days, respectively. Clavien–Dindo grade II or higher complications occurred in 29% of the patients, with no 90-day mortality.ConclusionUniportal robotic-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy is safe and feasible, with acceptable perioperative and short-term outcomes in patients with esophageal cancer.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00464-026-12826-1.

Radical esophagectomy remains among the standard treatment modalities for most esophageal cancer stages [1, 2]. Minimally invasive esophagectomy (MIE) has demonstrated long-term survival comparable to that of open esophagectomy while offering additional short-term outcome benefits [3, 4]. The uniportal minimally invasive esophagectomy (UMIE) technique was developed to further reduce the incision required for MIE, and its feasibility and effectiveness have been demonstrated [5–7]. Robotic surgical platforms provide enhanced three-dimensional visualization and meticulous instrument control, enabling greater precision during complex operations. Recent randomized controlled trials demonstrated that robotic-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy (RAMIE) resulted in a lower incidence of recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, despite a higher mediastinal lymph node yield, particularly in challenging cases [8, 9]. With increasing proficiency in robotic surgery, uniportal robotic-assisted thoracic and abdominal procedures have become feasible using various robotic platforms, even for complex operations [10–17]. Building on prior experience with UMIE, a novel uniportal robotic-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy (URAMIE) technique was developed using the da Vinci Xi system through the same incision as UMIE [5–7, 11]. This study reports the perioperative and short-term outcomes of the URAMIE approach with emphasis on safety and feasibility.

Materials and methods

Ethics committee approval

This study was approved by the ethics committee of the National Taiwan University Hospital, Taipei, Taiwan (202508087RINE). The requirement for patient consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Study population

Following the introduction of URAMIE at our institution in September 2024, all consecutive patients who underwent URAMIE between September 2024 and July 2025 were retrospectively enrolled. Esophagectomy for esophageal cancer was indicated for early (stage I) esophageal cancer unsuitable for endoscopic resection, locally advanced (stages II–III) disease after neoadjuvant chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy, and selected patients with resectable stage IV disease following neoadjuvant therapy [2, 18–20]. Patients were considered eligible for a single-incision minimally invasive esophagectomy approach (UMIE or URAMIE) if complete resection was deemed achievable, there was no history of major prior thoracic surgery, and one-lung ventilation was feasible, as previously described [5–7]. Among eligible patients, the choice between UMIE and URAMIE was primarily determined by logistical and financial considerations, including insurance coverage, robotic system availability, and operating room scheduling, rather than by oncologic factors or baseline patient characteristics. During the same period, our surgical team performed 73 esophagectomies for esophageal cancer, including 21 cases initially planned for URAMIE and 52 cases initially planned for UMIE. Intraoperative conversion occurred in 1 URAMIE case and 2 UMIE cases.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome was the R0 resection rate. Secondary outcomes included perioperative outcomes (operative time, lymph node yield, blood loss, conversion rate) and short-term postoperative outcomes (pain scores, opioid requirements, complication rates, chest tube duration, and length of hospital stay).

Operative technique

Thoracic phase

A wound protector (Alexis; Applied Medical, Rancho Santa Margarita, CA, USA) was used to facilitate access. Intercostal nerve block with 0.5% bupivacaine was performed for chest wall analgesia. The da Vinci Xi robotic system (Intuitive Surgical, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) was docked from the cranial and dorsal sides of the patient. Three trocars were introduced through the same utility incision and oriented along the dorsoventral axis. The dorsally-positioned trocar accommodated a 0° camera (arm 2). The middle trocar served as the entry site for the left-hand instrument (arm 3), which was typically a fenestrated bipolar forceps. A ventrally placed trocar was used for the right-hand instruments (Arm 4), including the Maryland bipolar forceps, Vessel Sealer Extend, clip appliers, and a needle driver. In this configuration, Arm 1 was deactivated. Throughout the procedure, an assistant (a resident who had performed fewer than five minimally invasive esophagectomies), standing on the ventral side of the patient, introduced instruments as needed, such as a linear stapler.

Before esophageal mobilization and lymph node dissection, the vagus nerve was carefully identified and encircled using a vascular loop to prevent inadvertent injury during dissection (Fig. 2B). The azygos vein was routinely divided using an endoscopic linear stapler (Endo GIA™, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) or a Hem-o-lok® polymer clip (Teleflex Medical, Research Triangle Park, NC, USA) to facilitate upper thoracic esophagus exposure (Fig. 2C, D).

In the Ivor-Lewis approach, the distal esophagus was separated from the stomach at the beginning of the thoracic phase using an Endo GIA stapler. Subsequently, the stapled esophageal stump was used for traction to assist in mobilization, which proceeded cranially to the aortic arch level. The proximal esophagus was subsequently transected using an Endo GIA stapler, thereby completing the transthoracic esophagectomy. In the McKeown approach, the esophagus is generally not transected during the thoracic phase unless dense adhesions are encountered. Dissection began in the pericardial region (Fig. 2E, F), followed by esophagus encircling with a Penrose drain (Fig. 3A), which was later used to apply traction during mobilization from the posterior mediastinum. Esophageal mobilization was performed caudally toward the abdomen and cranially toward the thoracic inlet (Fig. 3B, C).Fig. 3Looping of the esophagus (arrow) with one Penrose drain (A). Adequate lymph node dissection requires proper exposure of the surgical field, which was facilitated by the bedside assistant using a suction device, as illustrated in Fig. 4. During the subcarinal lymph node dissection, the assistant retracted the right main bronchus ventrally to improve visualization (Fig. 4A). For the pretracheal nodes, the azygos vein stump and the superior vena cava were similarly retracted caudally and ventrally, respectively (Fig. 4B). The right recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) lymph nodes were cranially dissected from the dorsal side of the looped vagus nerve until the RLN could be observed (Fig. 4C). Left RLN lymph node dissection via a right-sided approach was technically challenging due to the deep anatomical location. Therefore, effective exposure required close coordination between the console surgeon and the assistant. In the Ivor Lewis approach, the gastric conduit was subsequently delivered into the thoracic cavity. Following the robotic system undocking, intrathoracic anastomosis was performed using a transorally inserted circular stapler anvil (DST Series™ EEA™ OrVil™, Covidien, Minneapolis, MN, USA). The anastomotic site was wrapped with continuous running 3–0 barbed sutures (V-Loc™ 180, polyglyconate; Covidien, Dublin, Ireland). In the McKeown approach, 8-mm and 10-mm Penrose drains were used to encircle the esophagus. Subsequently, the drains were advanced into the cervical and abdominal regions to facilitate esophageal identification during the subsequent cervical and abdominal phases. A 28-French chest tube was placed before wound closure.

Abdominal and cervical phases

A hybrid uniportal laparoscopic approach was adopted in the initial URAMIE cases. The abdominal and cervical phases were performed in the same manner as in UMIE, as previously described [5]. Briefly, a gastric tube was the preferred conduit, and mobilization and reconstruction were performed using an uniportal totally laparoscopic technique. Three-field lymph node dissection was routinely performed, except in cases of adenocarcinoma located below the carina. In the McKeown approach, side-to-side cervical anastomosis with an endo GIA linear stapler was preferred. In patients with a short residual esophageal stump, a hand-sewn anastomosis was performed using 3–0 barbed absorbable sutures. All anastomoses and staple lines were subsequently oversewn with 4–0 absorbable sutures (PDS II; Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA).

Postoperative management

Extubation was typically performed in the operating room. Patients remained in the intensive care unit for ≤ 3 days unless clinically unstable. In the immediate postoperative period, pain was managed primarily with morphine- or fentanyl-based analgesia, most commonly delivered via intravenous patient-controlled analgesia or intravenous administration, depending on the patient’s condition and clinical requirements. Prokinetics commenced on postoperative day (POD) 1. Enteral feeding was initiated after the passage of flatus, after which acetaminophen and tramadol were used for pain control. The drains were removed from POD 1 onward when the output was appropriate, and early ambulation was encouraged.

Outcome measurement and statistical analysis

Data regarding patient demographics, perioperative and short-term outcomes were collected. Esophageal cancer staging was determined according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer 8th edition [21]. Functional status was evaluated using the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance scale, and preoperative risk was assessed using the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [22, 23]. Resection margins were defined as R0 (no microscopic tumor), R1 (microscopic residual tumor), or R2 (macroscopic residual tumor) according to pathological assessment. Complications were reported using the Clavien–Dindo classification [24]. Postoperative pain was assessed using the numerical rating scale, with the highest recorded score for each postoperative day used for analysis. Opioid requirements were converted to morphine milligram equivalents (MME) [25]. Continuous data are presented as medians (interquartile range) or means (standard deviation). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical data are presented as numbers (%). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Twenty-one patients underwent URAMIE during the study period. The patient demographics are presented in Table 1. The median age was 64 years, and 18 patients (86%) were male. The median ASA classification and CCI scores were 3 and 4, respectively. Squamous cell carcinoma was the most common histology (81%), and the tumors were mostly located in the lower esophagus (48%), followed by the middle (33%) and upper esophagus (19%). Most patients presented with locally advanced disease, with 62% of them exhibiting stage III or IV disease. Moreover, 15 (71%) patients received neoadjuvant therapy, including chemoradiotherapy in 14 patients and chemotherapy in 1 patient. The perioperative and short-term outcomes are summarized in Table 2. McKeown and Ivor-Lewis esophagectomies were performed in 11 (52%) and 10 (48%) patients; R0 resection was achieved in 20 of them (95%). The median docking time and the mean console time were 9 and 155 min, respectively. Additionally, the median lymph node yield and blood loss volume were 34 and 450 mL, respectively. One patient (5%) required conversion to thoracoscopic surgery because of inadequate one-lung ventilation. The postoperative pain scores were low, with median values of 2, 0, 2, and 1 on POD 0, POD 1, POD 2, and POD 7, respectively. The median postoperative opioid requirement was 12 MME on POD 0, 32 MME on POD 1, and 16 MME on POD 2. The median chest tube duration was 5.5 days, and the median postoperative hospital stay was 15.0 days. Clavien–Dindo grade II or higher complications occurred in six patients (29%), including persistent air leak in one (5%), anastomotic leak in one (5%), vocal cord palsy in two (10%), pneumonia in three (14%), and pneumonitis in one (5%). No 90-day mortality was observed. Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to describe RAMIE performed through a single intercostal incision using the da Vinci Xi system.

Traditionally, robotic-assisted thoracic and abdominal procedures required more than four incisions, particularly for complex operations such as esophagectomy [26]. With increasing surgeon experience and ongoing robotic platform refinement, the incision number has gradually decreased, paralleling the evolutionary trend observed in thoracoscopic and laparoscopic surgeries [11–14]. Recently, Lee et al. described RAMIE techniques using the da Vinci SP system through a subcostal incision. Conversely, Hadzijusufovic et al. reported both RAMIE and robotic-assisted transcervical esophagectomy using the same robotic system. Although both groups adopted a single-port robotic system, subcostal RAMIE required an additional intercostal assistant port [15, 17]. In our URAMIE approach, which was modified from uniportal robotic-assisted lung resection surgery and thoracoscopic UMIE, only a single intercostal incision with the da Vinci Xi system was required [5, 12]. Importantly, the classic fifth intercostal incision provides a holistic intrathoracic view, allowing the introduction and manipulation of instruments, such as the Endo GIA and suction device, with or without temporary dislodgement of one robotic arm, thereby facilitating the performance of a genuine uniportal approach. Compared to uniportal robotic-assisted lung resection surgery, URAMIE requires less bedside assistance, including lung retraction and Endo GIA passage. In our cohort, the bedside assistant was a resident with limited esophagectomy experience. Furthermore, once the issue of instrument collision was managed as in uniportal robotic-assisted lung resection surgery, the three-dimensional view and precise instrument control facilitated the uniportal approach from a technical perspective [25, 27].

Besides the technical details, Lee et al. reported the outcomes of a 17-patient single-port plus one-port subcostal RAMIE cohort and compared them with those of their multiportal RAMIE and thoracoscopic cohorts. They observed comparable complication rates, shorter chest tube duration, reduced length of hospital stay, and lower peak postoperative pain in the single-port plus one-port group [15]. In that study, the median age of patients was 67 years (vs. 64 years in the present series), with 94% male patients (vs. 86%). The median ASA classification was 2 (vs. 3). All patients had squamous cell carcinoma, whereas squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma accounted for 81% and 19% of cases, respectively, in the present series. Locally advanced disease (≥ stage III) was present in 23% of patients (vs. 62%), and 24% received neoadjuvant therapy (vs. 71%). The operative procedures consisted of the Ivor Lewis and McKeown approaches in 59% and 41% of cases, respectively (vs. 48% and 52%, respectively). The median lymph node yield was 32 (vs. 34), and the R0 resection rate was 94% (vs. 95%). The postoperative pain scores on POD 0, 1, 2, and 7 were 3, 3, 3, and 2, respectively (vs. 2, 0, 2, and 1, respectively). Anastomotic leak, vocal cord palsy, and pneumonia occurred in 6%, 24%, and 18% of patients, respectively (vs. 5%, 14%, and 19%, respectively). The median chest tube duration and postoperative hospital stay were 7 and 13 days, respectively (vs. 5.5 days and 15.0 days). Notably, the present series included a higher proportion of patients with locally advanced disease and prior neoadjuvant therapy. Despite this, our URAMIE approach demonstrated acceptable perioperative and short-term outcomes, supporting its safety and feasibility. These cross-study comparisons are descriptive and should be interpreted with caution.

In previous studies, the abdominal phase of subcostal RAMIE using the single-port robotic system was performed with multiportal laparoscopy; conversely, the present series implemented uniportal laparoscopy [15, 17]. While uniportal laparoscopic gastric tube reconstruction is the standard practice at our institution, we demonstrated the feasibility of performing the abdominal phase with the da Vinci Xi robotic system through a glove port (Nelis Corp., Seongnam, Korea) [11]. During the initial adoption of the robotic system for the thoracic phase, a hybrid laparoscopic approach was employed. However, as familiarity with the robotic system increased during the thoracic phase, we gradually reintroduced its use during the abdominal phase and anticipated further outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a small retrospective single-center cohort, which inherently limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, the single-arm design precludes direct comparison with UMIE or multiportal RAMIE; therefore, no conclusion can be drawn regarding comparative clinical advantage. Third, because URAMIE was introduced during the early adoption phase, some degree of selection bias was unavoidable. Although the choice between UMIE and URAMIE was not based on oncologic or baseline patient factors, it was influenced by logistical and financial considerations. Finally, all procedures were performed by a single surgical team; although this reduced technical heterogeneity, reproducibility in other settings remains uncertain. Further comparative studies are warranted to better define the role of URAMIE.

In conclusion, the URAMIE approach appears to be safe and feasible in patients with esophageal cancer, provided that the surgeon has experience in both uniportal robotic-assisted surgery and esophagectomy.
