Plant growth promotion and pathogen protection by the desert endophyte Pseudomonas grandensis R4-79
Authors: Linah Alghanmi, Ahmed Elhady, Sabiha Parween, Heribert Hirt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDesert ecosystems have limited resources and harsh climatic conditions and cannot support cropping in most farming models. Recently, microbial applications have emerged as promising strategies to enhance plant survival and adaptation in such extreme environments. However, the beneficial microbes and our understanding of their functional roles and adaptation mechanisms in desert ecosystems remain underexplored. This study reports the isolation and characterization of the Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain from the arid environment of Wadi Rum, Jordan, associated with the desert plant Ifloga spicata. The genome of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79, sequenced at 275 × PacBio coverage, consists of a 6.18 Mbp chromosome encoding 5,445 proteins, including gene clusters for siderophores, phenazines, hydrogen cyanide, and phytohormones, as well as secretion systems (T2SS, T4SS, T6SS, and TAT). Genomic and phenotypic analyses revealed that Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 belongs to the genus P. granadensis and exhibits plant growth-promoting attributes and substantial biocontrol potential. Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 effectively suppressed key phytopathogens, including the necrotrophic fungus Botrytis cinerea in vitro, as well as Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 and root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne incognita) in vivo in Arabidopsis and tomato. Introduction

Desert ecosystems are among the most fragile environments on Earth, where water is scarce and soils are not rich in nutrients [1]. These factors not only discourage ecological restoration but also impose a limit on the scope of practicable models for sustainable farming [2]. Conversely, while water resources and minimum vegetation cover were previously sought as the focus for rejuvenation of desert ecosystems, recent findings have emphasized the significance of microbial communities in boosting the desert ecosystem [3, 4]. Microbes, particularly those adapted to arid conditions, hold substantial potential to enhance soil fertility, promote plant growth, and improve the resilience of plants to environmental stresses [3]. However, identifying beneficial microbes, enhancing their growth, and expanding their community diversity continue to be major challenges. Moreover, it is necessary to investigate the functional traits and phylogenomic adaptations of beneficial microbes from desert ecosystems in order to advance ecological and agricultural applications.

Abiotic and biotic stresses make ecological revegetation and farming in arid deserts highly challenging. Plants and crops in these environments are highly susceptible to infestations by parasitic nematodes and insects, as well as infections caused by bacterial and fungal phytopathogens [5, 6]. Meloidogyne incognita is among the most destructive plant-parasitic nematodes, with nearly 100% occurrence in the collected soil samples, particularly in the Sahara and Arabian deserts, often exceeding the economic threshold [5]. Additionally, pathogenic fungal species, including Fusarium spp., Alternaria spp., Pythium spp., Rhizoctonia solani, Phytophthora spp., Sclerotinia spp., and Verticillium spp., are widely distributed and cause significant damage, and can cause losses in agricultural production of up to 50–75% in some cases [7]. The harsh abiotic conditions in deserts, coupled with limited plant and microbial diversity, further exacerbate plant susceptibility to phytopathogens that hinder the establishment of resilient vegetation. Compared to other ecosystems, plant pathogens have a significantly greater impact on arid ecosystems. However, efforts to control these pathogens have received minimal attention [5, 8].

Desert plants depend on mutualistic interactions with specialized microbial species to persist in extreme environments. These interactions are vital for the fitness and survival of both partners. For example, the legume shrub Vachellia jacquemontii in the Thar Desert associates with the nitrogen-fixing bacterium Ensifer, which provides bioavailable nitrogen to support the plant's nutritional requirements [9]. Similarly, despite the extreme aridity of the Atacama Desert, arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi have been reported [10] to potentially play significant roles in phosphorus solubilization, plant stress tolerance, and plant growth promotion.

The isolation and application of microbial strains from native desert plants has emerged as an effective strategy to enhance crop performance under harsh conditions. For instance, the halotolerant Bacillus cabrialesii isolated from the Qatar desert improved tomato seedling growth under salt stress and reduced infections by gray mold disease (Botrytis cinerea) [11]. Similarly, two Klebsiella strains isolated from Parastrephia quadrangularis in the Atacama Desert increased wheat seedling fresh weight by up to 60% under salinity stress [12]. Serratia marcescens isolated from Capparis decidua in India’s Thar Desert enhanced wheat tolerance to salinity stress and reduced the severity of fungal disease caused by Fusarium graminearum [13]. Additionally, Pseudomonas argentinensis SA190 isolated from Indigofera argentea in Saudi Arabia's Jizan region improved the drought tolerance of Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings [14].

In an attempt to identify beneficial bacterial strains from the Arabian desert to support arid farming and ecological restoration, the ability of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 to promote plant growth and suppress various phytopathogens was tested. This strain was isolated by our group from the desert plant Ifloga spicata in Wadi Rum, Jordan. In this work, we conducted whole-genome sequencing to determine its taxonomic classification and explore its biochemical potential to promote plant growth and inhibit pathogens. We also characterized the phenotypic properties of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 and assessed its ability to inhibit several phytopathogens such as the bacterial pathogen Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000, the root-knot nematode (Meloidogyne incognita), and the fungal pathogen Botrytis cinerea.

Materials and methods

Root collection and isolation of bacterial strains

Root samples of the desert plant Ifloga spicata were collected from Wadi Rum, Jordan. To study the root endophytic bacteria, the root tissues were washed to remove soil debris and surface-sterilized by immersion in 70% ethanol for 30 s, followed by treatment with 2% sodium hypochlorite for 5 min and thorough rinsing with sterile distilled water. The sterilized roots were macerated in a solution containing 0.8% saline and serially diluted from 10⁻2 to 10⁻5. Each dilution was plated in duplicate on a variety of culture media, including Tryptone-Yeast (TY), R2A, LB agar, and TSA. Bacterial colonies were selected based on distinctive morphological characteristics and pigmentation, followed by further purification to obtain individual strains. The purified bacterial strains were suspended in a 25% glycerol-LB solution and stored at − 80 °C to serve as reference stocks.

Genomic DNA extraction and identification of strains

To identify the bacterial strains, cells were streaked from 25% glycerol stock onto LB agar and incubated at 28 °C overnight. Single bacterial colonies were inoculated into 10 mL of LB broth in a 50 mL Falcon tube and incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. Genomic DNA was extracted using the Genelute Bacterial Genomic DNA Kit (Sigma-Aldrich). The 16S rRNA gene was amplified using Taq DNA Polymerase PCR Master Mix (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and universal primers 27 F (5’-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3’) and 1492R (5’-TACGGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3’). The amplified PCR products were purified using ExoSAP-IT (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and sequenced via Sanger sequencing at the Core Labs, KAUST, Saudi Arabia. Strain identification was performed by comparing the sequences to NCBI’s GenBank database using BLAST. Strains were sorted and grouped into collections based on their genus-level taxonomy, including Pseudomonas spp., Bacillus spp., Enterobacter spp., rhizobial strains, and others.

Genome sequencing and annotation of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79

After screening 86 microbial strains for plant growth promotion activity, Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain was selected for its high ability to promote plant growth. The strain was further analyzed to characterize its genetic features and its plant growth-promotion potential using in silico, in vitro, and in vivo approaches.

To analyze the genome of the Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain, the total genomic was shipped to Novogen Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd. (Singapore). Genomic sequencing and assembly were performed using Single-Molecule Real-Time (SMRT®) sequencing on the PacBio Sequel II/IIe platform. The whole genome assembly was performed using the Hierarchical Genome Assembly Process (HGAP). Genome polishing and circularization were carried out using Arrow (v2.3.3) and Circlator (v1.5.5), respectively. To assess the quality of the genome assembly, quantitative measurements were obtained using BUSCO (v4.0.2) (Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs, https://busco.ezlab.org). Genome annotation was performed for coding genes, repetitive sequences, non-coding RNAs, and pseudogenes using the NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline (PGAP). PGAP identifies structural annotations by comparing open reading frames (ORFs) against libraries containing protein hidden Markov models (HMMs), representative RefSeq proteins, and proteins from well-characterized reference genomes. For genomic regions lacking HMM or protein evidence, GeneMarkS-2 + was used to generate ab initio coding region predictions and to determine start sites for ORFs based on available HMM evidence.

Phylogenetic and pangenome analysis

The genome of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 was taxonomically analyzed using the Type (Strain) Genome Server (TYGS, https://tygs.dsmz.de). Closest type strains were identified via MASH for whole-genome comparison and 16S rDNA BLAST BLAST against TYGS database. Intergenomic distances were calculated using GBDP with the d5 formula, and digital DNA-DNA hybridization (dDDH) values were computed via GGDC 3.0. Pseudomonas sp. CT364 was included as an additional strain due to its beneficial properties. A whole-genome-based phylogenetic tree at the protein level was constructed and visualized using iTOL. Pan-genome analysis was performed using Roary v3.6.0 [15] with a 90% identity threshold. Genes associated with plant growth promotion were subsequently identified using the Plant-associated Bacteria database [16].

Gene mining for potential PGP traits

Protein-level annotation of plant growth-promoting traits (PGPTs) was performed using BLASTp and HMMER searches against the PLaBAse server. This platform hosts a curated PGPT database comprised of 6,900 defined traits and over 6.9 million proteins and enables precise identification and functional annotation of individual proteins associated with plant-beneficial activities. The identified locus was further mined based on an identity of ≥ 40% and ≥ 70% query coverage against each PGPT gene.

Sequence accession number

The WGS of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 has been submitted to NCBI under Bioproject-PRJNA708169, Biosample-SAMN18220753, Genome accession-CP071650-CP071651.

Phenotypic and biochemical properties of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79

Strain culture

Cells of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain were revived from a 25% glycerol stock stored at − 80 °C by streaking onto King’s B agar (Sigma-Aldrich) and incubation at 28 °C overnight. Then, a single pure colony was inoculated into 10 mL of sterilized LB broth (Lennox L Broth Base, Invitrogen) in a 50 mL Falcon tube (Fisher Scientific) and incubated overnight on an orbital shaker (Innova 42, New Brunswick) at 28 °C/220 rpm. A fresh culture was prepared by adding 5 mL of LB broth (Lennox L Broth Base, Invitrogen) to a 15 mL tube (Fisher Scientific), followed by the addition of 500 μL of bacterial culture. The mixture was incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 220 rpm on an orbital shaker for 45 min to allow the culture to reach the exponential phase. The optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was then measured and adjusted to 0.2 using a spectrophotometer (BioPhotometer, Eppendorf).

Electron microscopy

The morphology of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 was visualized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). A 5 µL aliquot of bacterial cells (OD600 = 0.2) was deposited onto carbon/Formvar-coated TEM grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences) and incubated for 2 min to allow adhesion. The grids were washed with distilled water and stained with 1% uranyl acetate for 1 min. Imaging was performed using a Titan ST (Thermo Fisher Scientific) transmission electron microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV. For scanning electron microscope (SEM), bacterial samples were fixed overnight at 4 °C in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4), washed three times in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) to remove residual fixative, and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide in water for 1 h at room temperature, followed by three washes with distilled water. The samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (25%, 50%, 75%, 95%, and 100%; 10 min each). Critical point drying was performed using a Leica Microsystems critical point dryer to preserve structural integrity. The dried samples were sputter-coated with a 6 nm layer of platinum to enhance conductivity and imaged using a Zeiss Merlin Gemini II field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) operated at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV and a beam current of 30 pA.

Motility assays

Motility test was conducted using Motility Test Media (SIGMA-ALDRICH) in solid agar form. A 10 μL aliquot of an overnight bacterial culture (OD600 = 0.2) was carefully inoculated onto the center of the motility agar plate. The plate was incubated at 28 °C for 48 h to allow bacterial growth and potential motility. The motility of the Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain was assessed by observing the spread of bacterial growth from the point of inoculation. A clear zone of growth extending beyond the initial inoculation point would indicate bacterial motility, whereas the absence of such spread would suggest the strain is non-motile. The results were recorded as the extent of the growth diffusion from the inoculation site.

Siderophore production

The blue agar CAS media assay described by Brian et al. (1990) was performed to assess the siderophore production potential. A 10 μL aliquot of the overnight bacterial culture was inoculated onto the CAS agar plate, which contains a blue iron complex. The plate was incubated at 28 °C for 48 h. Siderophore production is indicated by a yellow-orange halo around the bacterial growth, caused by the chelation of iron from the CAS complex, which results in a color change in the surrounding agar.

Phosphate solubilization

The ability to solubilize phosphate was tested using Pikovskaya’s medium (HiMedia); 10 μL of overnight bacterial culture was spotted on PVK medium, then incubated at 28 °C for two days. Formation of a halo zone indicates that the bacteria were able to solubilize phosphate.

IAA production

To detect indole acetic acid (IAA) production, 3 mL of LB broth (Lennox L Broth Base, Invitrogen) enriched with 5 mM L-tryptophan was inoculated with 100 μL of overnight bacterial culture. The next day, the culture was centrifuged for 2 min at 7000 rpm, and 0.5 mL of supernatant was mixed with 1 mL of Salkowski reagent (10% 0.5 M FeCl3 + 35% perchloric acid), and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Formation of a pinkish to red color was a positive indication of IAA production.

Biofilm formation

To check bacterial ability to form biofilm, 200 μL of diluted overnight bacterial culture was added to each well of a 96-well plate and incubated overnight at 28°C. The liquid culture was removed by inverting the plate, followed by two washes with water to eliminate excess liquid. Each well was treated with 125 μL of 0.1% crystal violet solution and incubated for 15 min. After rinsing 3–4 times with water, the plate was inverted and left to dry overnight on a paper towel. The following day, 125 μL of 30% acetic acid was added to each well, incubated for 15 min. Next, biofilm quantification was performed using a plate reader at 630 nm (Infinite M200 PRO, TECAN).

Effects of abiotic stresses on the growth of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79

Salinity stress

To assess salinity tolerance, 20 mL of LB broth (Lennox L Broth Base, Invitrogen) was supplemented with sodium chloride (NaCl) at 50 mM, 100 mM, 150 mM, and 200 mM. The media was inoculated with 1 mL of bacterial culture (OD600 = 0.21). Bacterial growth was monitored by measuring the OD₆₀₀ at hourly intervals. Two biological isolates were used for each concentration.

Drought stress

The drought stress was assessed using polyethylene glycol (PEG 6,000) [17]. PEG was mixed with LB broth to obtain 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% solutions, then 20 mL of LB media was inoculated with 1 mL of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 (OD600 = 0.21). The OD600 was measured every hour for five hours. Two biological replicates were assessed for each concentration.

Heat stress

To evaluate heat tolerance, 1 mL of an overnight Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 culture (OD₆₀₀ = 0.21) was added to 20 mL of LB broth. The cultures were incubated at 28 °C, 37 °C, 45 °C, or 55°C. Bacterial growth was monitored by measuring optical density (OD₆₀₀) every hour for five hours. The experiment was performed with two biological replicates.

Effects of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 against Pseudomonas syringae (Pst) DC3000 infection of Arabidopsis thaliana

Pst DC3000 seed germination assays

We assessed Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0 (wild type) survival of Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0 (wild type) seeds when infected by Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 and Pst DC3000. Seeds of A. thaliana were surface sterilized with 70% ethanol containing 0.05% Triton X-100 and shaken for 10 min, followed by three washes with absolute ethanol. The sterilized seeds were placed in a 2 mL Eppendorf tube containing 1 mL sterilized water (H₂O) and kept at 4 °C for two days to ensure uniform germination. In square Petri dishes (12 × 12 cm), 50 mL of Murashige and Skoog (MS) Basal Salt Mixture containing 0.9% agar (½ MS agar) at pH 5.8 (Murashige and Skoog, 1962; M5524, Sigma Aldrich, Germany) was mixed with 100 µL of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 cells (OD₆₀₀ = 0.2) alone, 100 µL of DC3000 cells (OD₆₀₀ = 0.2) alone, or 100 µL of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 and DC3000 cells together (OD₆₀₀ = 0.2), and ½ MS agar media alone as a mock. A. thaliana seeds were spotted individually onto the ½ MS agar plates, at approximately 36 seeds per plate. The plates were placed in growth chambers (Percival Scientific Inc., USA) vertically (~ 75° angle to the horizontal) and incubated for 16 days at 23 °C with a 16/8 h (light/dark) photoperiod. Ability of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 to suppress Meloidogyne incognita infection

We evaluated the effects of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 both in vitro and in vivo against the root-knot nematode (Meloidogyne incognita, RKN). In a greenhouse experiment, two-week-old tomato seedlings (Moneymaker cultivar) were grown in ½-liter pots filled with sand supplemented with 1 g of Osmocote per liter. The plants were treated with Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 both via soil drenching or foliar spray at an OD600 of 0.2. Control plants received no bacterial application. Positive control plants were treated with Pseudomonas fluorescens strain SBW25 or the nematicide Velum® Prime (Fluopyram 41.5% active ingredient; Bayer CropScience). Each pot was drenched once with 10 μL of Velum® Prime diluted in 50 mL of water. After 5 days of bacterial treatment, the pots were infected with 300 s-stage juvenile RKN (J2s) to assess the bacterial strain's potential to inhibit RKN. Three weeks post-infection, the plants were sampled, and the roots were washed to remove soil. Root galls were counted, and plant biomass was measured. The experiment was repeated twice with a total of n = 21–16 replicates.

In an analogous experiment, tomato seedlings were grown in 2 L pots filled with peat moss supplemented with 1 g of Osmocote per liter. The bacterial-treated and untreated plants were prepared as described above, but were not infected with any pathogens to quantify the plant growth-promoting effects of the Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain on tomato plants. The plants were maintained under greenhouse conditions (25°C, 16-h photoperiod) and watered every 5 days. This experiment was performed once, with 30 to 40 replicates per group.

Growth inhibitory effect of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain on the pathogenic fungus Botrytis cinerea

A dual-culture assay was conducted to assess the antifungal activity of the Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain against Botrytis cinerea. A 1 cm2 plug of B. cinerea mycelium, grown on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA), was placed at the center of a fresh PDA plate. Three individual spots of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 culture were inoculated on the same plate, each positioned 1.5 cm away from the fungal plug. The plates were incubated at 25 °C for 7 days, after which inhibition of fungal growth was assessed and visualized. The experiment was performed with three biological replicates.

Results

Genome assembly, annotation, and phylogenomic

Genomic analysis of the Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain using PacBio technology revealed a single chromosome of 6,175,612 base pairs containing 5473 genes and gene clusters with no plasmids. The annotation pipeline generated 5445 protein-coding sequences (CDs), 96 exons, 73 tRNAs, 67 pseudogenes, 19 rRNAs, nine riboswitches, and one ncRNA (Supplementary Table 1 A).

Functional analysis of the Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 genome was performed using the Blast KOALA platform. Genome mining revealed the presence of several pathways related to metabolism, genetic and environmental information processing, cellular processes, and organismal systems. In addition, the Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain possesses different systems, such as ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, phosphotransferase system (PTS), and bacterial secretion systems for membrane transport. The ABC transporter can transport minerals and organic ions, oligosaccharides, polyols, lipids, monosaccharides, phosphate and amino acids, peptides and nickel, metallic cation, iron-siderophore, and vitamin B12. The PTS includes N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, trehalose, fructose, and nitrogen regulation. The Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain has three secretion systems (type 2, type 4, and type 6), twin arginine targeting (TAT), and Sec-SRP.

Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 also produces secondary metabolites, such as hydrogen cyanide (HCN) and phenazine.

Phylogenetic analysis based on whole-genome sequences revealed that Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 is most closely related to Pseudomonas granadensis LMG 27940 and Pseudomonas granadensis CT364, which form a well-supported clade with high bootstrap values (> 0.99; Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 1B). This indicates a strong evolutionary relationship and suggests that R4-79 likely belongs to the same species cluster as these two P. granadensis strains. The close grouping further supports the species-level classification of R4-79 within the P. granadensis lineage.

Pan-genome analysis of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 was performed alongside closely related strains from two phylogenetic clades: Clade I (Pseudomonas granadensis LMG 27940 and P. granadensis CT364) and Clade II (P. crudilactis UCMA 17988 T, P. germanica FIT28, and P. tensinigenes ZA 5.3; Fig. 2). These 608 unique genes were screened for plant growth-promoting traits (PGPTs) using the PLaBAse resource, and 125 genes met the mapping criteria of ≥ 40% identity and ≥ 70% query coverage. The functional classification of these PGPTs showed enrichment in categories such as competitive exclusion (57%), colonization of plant systems (17%), stress control (17%), bioremediation (6%), biofertilization (1%), phytohormone production (1%), and putative functions (2%; Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table 2).

Morphology, characterization, and growth of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79

Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 is a gram-negative, rod-shaped bacterium equipped with lophotrichous flagella (Fig. 4C-D). The colonies exhibit a mucoid and glossy appearance that suggests the production of extracellular polysaccharides (EPS) or formation of a biofilm (Fig. 4A). Additionally, the colonies display spreading with irregular edges, which indicates motility. These observations were further supported by a motility assay, which demonstrated swarming behavior on agar plates (Fig. 4F) and a biofilm formation assay confirmed the capability of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain to produce biofilm (Fig. 4E).

Next, we investigated the plant growth-promoting traits of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 in vitro, including phosphate solubilization, siderophore, and IAA production. The bacterium was capable of solubilizing phosphate, as indicated by formation of a clear zone on PVK medium (Fig. 4B). The R4-79 strain exhibited a fluorescent yellow-green color on King B agar, which suggests potential production of the siderophore pyoverdine. To confirm this, the chrome azurol sulfonate (CAS) agar assay was performed. The Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain showed yellowish growth on CAS agar, further indicating siderophore production (Fig. 4G). Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 also produced IAA, as confirmed by the color change to pinkish in the IAA assay (Fig. 4H).

The growth of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 was evaluated under various abiotic stresses, specifically salinity, drought, and heat. Under salinity stress, the strain exhibited strong tolerance across a range of NaCl concentrations (50 mM to 200 mM), with no significant reduction in growth compared to the non-stressed control (Fig. 5A). Similarly, drought stress imposed using increasing concentrations of PEG 6000 (10–25%) resulted in a moderate reduction in the growth rate; however, Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 maintained viability and continued to grow under all tested conditions (Fig. 5B). In contrast, heat stress had a more pronounced effect on bacterial growth. At 37 °C, Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 remained viable and continued to grow, albeit at a slower rate compared to the control. However, growth was significantly inhibited at the higher temperatures of 45 °C and 55 °C (Fig. 5C), indicating the strain has lower thermotolerance under more extreme heat stress conditions.

Growth was assessed in LB broth containing different concentrations of PEG 6000. C Heat stress. Cultures were incubated at 28 °C, 37 °C, 45 °C, or 55°C. AntiSMASH analysis revealed the presence of siderophore-producing gene clusters in Pseudomonas sp. R4-79, as well as BGCs for potential antimicrobial compounds such as hydrogen cyanide (HCN), fragin, and lokisin (Supplementary Table 3). Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 possesses the genes to produce quorum-sensing molecules such as toxoflavin and riboflavin. Additionally, functional analysis revealed that the Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 genome possesses the same biofilm-associated genes found in Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 suppresses the pathogenicity of Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 (Pst) in Arabidopsis thaliana

Pst DC3000 seed germination assays

We have tested non-colonized and colonized plants with Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 under the biotic stress of Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 (Pst) in Arabidopsis thaliana. Mock-inoculated plants had a survival percentage of 83.34%, whereas plants colonized with the Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain showed full survival (100%). In contrast, infection with Pst DC3000 completely inhibited the growth of A. thaliana immediately after germination, and no plants survived. However, A. thaliana plants infected with Pst DC3000 but then treated with the Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain exhibited a high survival rate of 86.11% (Fig. 6A). The Pseudomonas sp. Discussion

Pseudomonas granadensis exhibits dual growth promotion and disease suppression capability

The application of beneficial microbes from arid environments, such as the Arabian desert, may significantly enhance sustainable farming practices and ecological restoration in water-limited regions. Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 demonstrated substantial plant growth-promoting effects and pathogen suppression. The main goal of the study was to obtain a bacterium with PGP activity and evaluate its capability to suppress phytopathogens.

Phylogenetic analysis showed that the genome of the Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain is very similar to that of Pseudomonas granadensis strain LMG 27940, which was previously isolated from soil [30]. Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 is the third strain reported under the P. granadensis species and the first to be isolated from an arid environment, specifically in Wadi Rum, Jordan. Notably, another strain within the same clade, P. granadensis CT364, exhibits significant plant growth-promoting potential, including the ability to enhance rooting in olive cuttings more effectively than synthetic auxin (IBA), as well as the ability to solubilize phosphate and produce siderophores. All these parameters may have led to the a greater root length of canola or mung bean seeds and mung bean cuttings, respectively, in gnotobiotic conditions [31]. Similarly, Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 exhibited robust plant growth-promoting activity in tomato plants, as evidenced by increased shoot length and fresh weight compared to uninoculated controls. These beneficial effects of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 may be attributed to its functional traits, including phosphate solubilization, siderophore production, and the synthesis of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), which have been previously linked to PGP activity [32–34]. For instance, phosphate-solubilizing bacteria can allow the use of phosphate fertilizer to be reduced by up to 50% without significantly reducing crop yield [33]. In addition, Pseudomonas that produce siderophores are used in agriculture to increase crop yield, improve plant nutrition, and control plant diseases [34].

Diseases caused by phytopathogens significantly impact agriculture by reducing crop yield and the quality of production, as well as causing economic losses. We found that Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 significantly suppressed multiple plant pathogens, including Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 (Pst), the root-knot nematode M. incognita, and Botrytis cinerea. In addition to siderophore production, the secretion of secondary metabolites by Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 may play a key role in inhibiting pathogens. Furthermore, we also found the genes related to hydrogen cyanide (HCN) and phenazine production in Pseudomonas sp. R4-79. HCN production can confer several benefits in plant–microbe interactions [35], and phenazine has been show to exhibit a wide spectrum of activity against numerous plant pathogenic bacteria and fungi [36]. Moreover, several Pseudomonas strains can outcompete pathogens for nutrients and niche space through rapid colonization, siderophore production, and biofilm formation [37].

Root colonization is the critical first step in plant-bacteria interactions. The bacteria must be able to detect the root, move toward the root, and then establish an attachment to access plant-derived nutrients. Turnbull et al. tested motile and non-motile Pseudomonas fluorescens SBW25, and showed that motility significantly enhanced the ability of Pseudomonas fluorescens to survive in soil and to adhere to wheat roots [38]. Nevertheless, the ability to form biofilm represents a crucial mechanism for colonizing and supporting plant growth. Li et al. demonstrated biofilm formation by plant-beneficial bacteria not only facilitates efficient root colonization, but also contributes to improved plant resilience against biotic and abiotic stresses [39]. Thus, the ability of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 to exhibit motility and form biofilms may contribute to effective root colonization.

Plant tolerance to harsh conditions plays a central role in agriculture to support plant health under abiotic stresses. Our data indicate the desert-isolated bacterium Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 exhibits strong tolerance to multiple abiotic stresses, including salinity and drought. Its ability to survive and grow under these harsh conditions indicates ecological adaptation and suggests that the strain could be potentially used to promote plant resilience in arid and semi-arid environments. The tolerance of Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 to salinity and drought might be related to IAA production. Several studies have shown IAA-producing bacteria can stimulate plant growth and crop yields under salinity and drought stresses [4]. In addition, the formation of biofilms by bacteria may help to protect plants against unpredictable environmental conditions. For instance, the biofilm formation ability of twenty plant growth-promoting bacteria increased at higher salt concentration [40]. Moreover, Wang et al. demonstrated that colonization with the biofilm-forming Bacillus amyloliquefaciens 54 improved drought tolerance in tomato plants [41]. These results suggest that Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 might represent a plant-growth-promoting and biocontrol option that could help to control a broad range of phytopathogens that attack crops grown in arid environments.

Genetic features related to host-microbe interactions and pathogen suppression

Exploring the genetic features of beneficial bacteria is essential to understand their role in plant–microbe interactions. KEGG-based functional annotation of the Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 genome revealed key traits linked to plant growth promotion, stress tolerance, and pathogen suppression including ABC transporters, PTS system, and bacterial secretion systems. For instance, in P. fluorescens Pf-5, ABC transporters are implicated in the secretion of antimicrobial metabolites, such as pyoluteorin, that exhibit biocontrol activity against soilborne pathogens and improve rhizosphere colonization. In particular, the pltI and pltJ genes that promote pyoluteorin export are both necessary for pyoluteorin production and efficient secretion [42]. Moreover, Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 possesses a PTS, which serves as a sugar transport system that uptakes and phosphorylates sugars, such as glucose and fructose, maintains bacterial viability by tuning the fluxes of carbons. In P. putida, the PTS system not only enables metabolic plasticity, but also plays important roles in both bioremediation and metabolic engineering [43]. On the other hand, KEGG analysis by BlastKOALA showed that Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 possess various secretion systems, including Type II (T2SS), Type IV (T4SS), and Type VI (T6SS), and arginine targeting (TAT). Such secretion systems allow the translocation of proteins, toxins, DNA, and small molecules. In the PGPB Dyella japonica, the T2SS plays a crucial role in suppressing MAMP-triggered immunity (MTI) in plants, and thus promotes root colonization. Mutations in the key T2SS components GspD and GspE in Dyella japonica MF79 led to the loss of the ability to inhibit MTI [44]. Moreover, T4SS is involved in DNA conjugation systems and the secretion of protein complexes involved in bacterial competition and host manipulation. For example, the T4SS of P. putida IsoF protects tomato plants against Ralstonia solanacearum by competitive exclusion and disrupting biofilm formation [45]. T6SS injects effector proteins into both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells and is involved in competition and pathogen suppression processes. For instance, in P. putida KT2440, the isogenic triple mutant (ΔT6SS) lost the ability to suppress the pathogenic bacteria Xanthomonas campestris compared to wild-type P. putida KT2440 [46, 47].

Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 also possesses the metabolic machinery to produce secondary metabolites, such as hydrogen cyanide (HCN), that can provide various advantages in plant–microbe interactions, especially suppression of a broad spectrum of phytopathogens [35]. For example, HCN produced by Pseudomonas chlororaphis O6 has been shown to exert suppressive activity against the root-knot nematode Meloidogyne hapla [48]. Furthermore, HCN suppressed mycelial growth of the fungal pathogen Phytophthora infestans, with inhibition rates of 57% to 80% [49]. Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 strain also has the genes to produce the secondary metabolite phenazine. Pseudomonas sp. LBUM223 produces phenazine-1-carboxylic acid (PCA), which plays a significant role in controlling potato common scab caused by Streptomyces scabies [50].

Conclusions

In this study, we isolated Pseudomonas sp. R4-79 and analyzed its functional and genetic makeup. Analysis of functional traits revealed that the R4-79 strain is capable of producing IAA and siderophores, forming biofilm, phosphate solubilization, and is motile. Moreover, the bacterium showed tolerance to salinity and drought stresses as well as inhibiting phytopathogens. Analysis of genomic features revealed the presence of genes that contribute to biofilm formation, disease suppression, plant growth-promotion, root colonization, secretion systems, and stress tolerance. These results indicate that Pseudomonas granadensis R4-79 holds potential as a plant growth-promoting bacterium (PGPB) and also as a biocontrol agent. Therefore, P. granadensis R4-79 may represent a valuable potential agent to increase plant immunity and ecological restoration in dry ecosystems. Future studies should address its effect on abiotic stresses and optimize its application in field conditions to fully harness its capabilities.
