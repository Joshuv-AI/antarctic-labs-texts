Scleral image analysis via artificial intelligence to evaluate inflammatory status in inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Ying Li, Pinyi Huang, Lei Wang, Shuijiao Chen, Guanghui Lian, Fujun Li, Xiaowei Liu, Fanggen Lu, Yani Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAmong the extraintestinal ocular manifestations of patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), episcleritis is associated with the inflammatory activity of IBD. The use of artificial intelligence to evaluate scleral images may help establish a non-invasive method for assessing the degree of inflammation. This study aims to provide a new approach for evaluating IBD inflammation by analyzing scleral images.MethodsWe collected scleral images from patients with IBD across different inflammatory states and from controls. Then, we analyzed the images using the MASK R-CNN and HRNet method to build models for assessing the degree of inflammation.ResultsA total of 942 scleral images were collected and divided into 5 groups: the control group, inflammatory Crohn’s disease (ICD) group, CD in remission (RCD) group, inflammatory ulcerative colitis (IUC) group, and UC in remission (RUC) group. In the training set, the accuracy and F1-score for discriminating between IBD and non-IBD controls, inflammatory IBD and IBD in remission, ICD and RCD, IUC and RUC were 99.02%, 97.99%, and 98.44%, 98.34%, and 99.04%, 98.98%, and 100%, 100%, respectively. In the test set, the accuracy, F1-score and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) were 95.59%, 92.5%, 92.9%, and 85.1%, 81.2%, 85.8%, and 74.2%, 72.5%, 77.9%, and 92.9%, 91.1%, 91.3%, respectively. The inflammatory ulcerative colitis group exhibited the most prominent scleral congestion, which was correlated with disease severity.ConclusionIn this small-sample exploratory study, we propose that the evaluation of ocular manifestations in patients with IBD may serve as a non-invasive method for assessing inflammatory activity, especially in patients with UC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00384-026-05190-y.Keywords: Inflammatory bowel diseases, Scleral image, Inflammation, Artificial intelligence

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which includes Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), is systemic inflammatory gastrointestinal tract disorders with a rising incidence worldwide [1]. Given the unclear etiology of IBD and its highly heterogeneous clinical manifestations, accurate assessment of disease activity is critical for guiding treatment decisions. Endoscopic examinations and novel biomarkers (e.g., microRNAs) have been applied to the diagnosis of IBD and the assessment of disease activity. However, these approaches are invasive and demonstrate limited efficacy [2, 3]. Further research is therefore needed to improve the evaluation of IBD severity.

Extraintestinal manifestations (EIMs) are common in IBD, affecting up to 40% of patients [2]. They involve multiple organ systems and may precede the onset of gastrointestinal symptoms [3]. Ocular manifestations occur in 4–29% of patients with IBD, with episcleritis, scleritis, and uveitis being the most frequent [4]. In clinical practice, episcleritis correlates more closely with IBD activity than other ocular EIMs, appearing during flares and resolving with effective intestinal treatment [5]. Assessing the degree of scleral congestion may represent a non-invasive approach for evaluating inflammatory activity.

In our previous study, we used regional annotation networks and neural networks to analyze scleral images from patients with overt and occult jaundice, and established a promising diagnostic model [6]. Episcleritis is characterized by painless hyperemia of the conjunctiva and sclera, and often correlates with IBD inflammatory activity. However, scleral congestion is difficult to evaluate accurately by the naked eye. The combination of artificial intelligence and scleral image analysis may help identify and stratify inflammatory severity in patients with IBD.

In this study, we collected scleral photos from IBD patients and controls, performed hierarchical training, established a neural network model, and verified its efficacy. This may provide a novel non-invasive method for evaluating inflammatory severity.

Methods

Participants

This study was reported in accordance with the STARD 2015 guidelines for diagnostic accuracy studies [7]. A total of 120 participants were consecutively recruited from Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, between February 2022 and May 2023. Patients with incomplete data were excluded (n = 5). The enrolled subjects were 115, including 97 patients with IBD (78 with CD, 19 with UC) and 18 non-IBD controls. Patients with CD were stratified into inflammatory (n = 31) and remission (n = 47) groups; patients with UC were stratified into inflammatory (n = 6) and remission (n = 13) groups. The control group comprised 18 non-IBD and no inflammatory status (4 with intestinal tuberculosis, 5 with unexplained intestinal ulcers, 2 with intestinal lymphoma, 3 with Behcet’s disease, and 4 healthy volunteers). The study flowchart is shown in Fig. S1.

Definition

CD patients with a Crohn’s Disease Activity Index (CDAI) score ≥ 150 were categorized to an inflammatory group, CD patients with a CDAI score < 150 were categorized to a remission group. UC patients with a Mayo score ≥ 3 were categorized to an inflammatory group; UC patients with a Mayo score < 3 were categorized to a remission group. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, Changsha, China (NO.202110145). All subjects provided written informed consent. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Data collection

This is a retrospective study. In this study, scleral images were collected from participants after the endoscopic test was completed. IBD patients were enrolled under the supervision of both a gastroenterologist and an ophthalmologist, who had full access to all clinical data, yet they were blinded to each other’s examination results. Images were acquired under good lighting conditions, at the same location, same morning time point, and from the same distance upon admission.

Key anatomical landmarks, including the limbus, corneal limbal ring, and medial/lateral canthi, were fully exposed in all images. Photographs were taken using an iPhone 13 (Apple Inc., Cupertino, CA, USA) with Live Photos disabled and default settings. The entire procedure, including explanation and image acquisition, was completed within five minutes.

Data normalization was applied during analysis to minimize confounding effects. Images were obtained at 96-dpi resolution and transferred to a Dell XPS 8930 server for subsequent image processing and machine learning. Representative images are shown in Fig. 1.

Data analysis

Analyzing data consists of two phases: model training and data inference. In the model training phase, a Mask R-CNN [8]-based sclera neural network detection and segmentation model (NNDetSeg) and four HRNet-based IBD recognition neural network classification models (NNCls) were trained using a subset of the data [9]. The overall model structure is presented in Fig. 2. In the data inference phase, test images that were not used in the training phase are processed with the trained models to predict whether the images belong to IBD inflammatory cases.

The overall structure of the models in this study

The sclera segmentation model is a Mask R-CNN model with ResNet50 backbones pretrained on the COCO dataset. The pretrained model can detect and segment 80 categories of objects, including people, cats, and cars. However, it cannot detect and segment the sclera by default. The data collected in 2.3 were used to finetune the Mask R-CNN model to detect and segment sclera regions from the input images.

The IBD recognition models are HRNet models with HRNetV2p-W18 backbones pretrained on the ImageNet dataset [10]. The pretrained model can recognize a wide range of objects from various domains such as animals, vehicles, household items, and more. However, it cannot recognize IBD not presented in the ImageNet dataset. Therefore, we used the data collected in 2.3 to finetune HRNet to identify IBD using segmented sclera images. Four IBD recognition models were trained to identify IBD and non-IBD patients, inflammatory IBD and IBD in remission, RCD and ICD, RUC and IUC, respectively (Fig. 3).

Experimental setting

To validate the effectiveness of the computer model in medical applications, we designed experiments consisting of four task groups. Each task group was divided into training, validation, and test sets, with participant-based stratification at a ratio of 7:2:1 to ensure that participant images did not overlap between sets. This stratification strategy aimed to facilitate the model's learning of specific task features comprehensively and enable reliable evaluation on unseen data. Throughout the experiments, we emphasized the balance of datasets and diversity of participants to ensure the model’s broad generalization capabilities. The training set was employed for learning model parameters, while the validation set played a crucial role in preventing overfitting. Subsequently, the model’s performance on the test set was analyzed. This analysis aimed to provide a thorough understanding of the model's performance in various medical tasks, offering results interpretations based on interpretability. Within our experiments, special attention was given to the evaluation metrics of accuracy and F1-score, which are crucial for assessing the model’s performance in medical contexts. Confusion matrix, as shown in the table below, is a tabular representation used to evaluate model performance in classification problems. Accuracy representing the proportion of correctly classified samples, holds significance in medical applications by indicating the model’s ability to correctly identify patients with diseases or abnormalities. In Eq. (1), T represents the total number of correct predictions, and N represents the total number of test samples. A high accuracy implies the model's reliable discrimination of health statuses, reducing misdiagnosis rates, and enhancing the precision of clinical decision-making.

Simultaneously, the F1-score, defined in Eq. (2), which integrates precision and recall, holds particular significance in a medical context, especially when addressing imbalanced class distributions common in medical datasets. In Eqs. (2)–(3), P and R denote precision and recall, respectively. Within the field of medical image analysis, a high F1-score indicates the model’s well-balanced performance in correctly identifying patients with conditions by balancing the trade-off between false negatives (missed positive cases) and false positives (misclassified negative cases).

To provide a more comprehensive assessment of the model's clinical utility, we extended our evaluation to include sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and the area under the curve (AUC).

Sensitivity measures the proportion of actual positive cases correctly identified by the model, essentially reflecting its ability to avoid missed diagnoses. Specificity (Eq. 6) quantifies the proportion of actual negative cases correctly identified, indicating the model’s ability to minimize false alarms. High sensitivity is vital for screening to ensure that patients are not overlooked, while high specificity is crucial to prevent unnecessary anxiety and intervention among healthy individuals.

Complementing these are the PPV and NPV, defined in Eqs. (7) and (8). PPV represents the probability that subjects with a positive screening test truly have the disease, while NPV indicates the probability that subjects with a negative test are truly healthy. These metrics are directly related to the diagnostic reliability in practical clinical setting.

Finally, the AUC was utilized to evaluate the overall classification performance. The AUC provides an aggregate measure of performance across all possible classification thresholds, where a value of 1.0 indicates a perfect classifier and 0.5 implies random guessing. A high AUC value demonstrates the model’s robust ability to distinguish between benign and malignant cases regardless of the threshold selection.

This robustness is crucial for minimizing the risks of both overlooking and misdiagnosing abnormalities, thereby enhancing the reliability of medical image diagnostics.

Demographic and clinical characteristics of participants

A total of 115 participants were enrolled in this study. All patients underwent ocular image acquisition after endoscopic examination, and no medical interventions were administered in the interim. No adverse events occurred among participants during the study. In clinical practice, white blood cell (WBC), platelet, hemoglobin, C-reactive protein (CRP), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) are often used to evaluate the IBD disease severity [11]. These indicators were collected and analyzed in our study. Compared with the RCD group, patients in the ICD group present with lower hemoglobin, and higher platelet, CRP, ESR, and CDAI score. In addition, compared to the RUC group, the IUC group presents with lower hemoglobin, and higher platelet, CRP, ESR, and Mayo score. The detailed results were shown in Table 1.

Scleral image analysis provides utility in identifying inflammatory activity levels within lBD

Different inflammatory phenotypes of IBD require distinct therapeutic strategies. Clinically, IBD activity is commonly evaluated using clinical manifestations, blood tests, and endoscopy. Many patients with active IBD present with episcleritis and scleral congestion. In this section, we used artificial intelligence to investigate the association between scleral congestion and inflammatory status. A total of 37 patients with active IBD and 60 patients with IBD in remission were enrolled. We collected 306 scleral images from the active IBD group and 487 scleral images from the remission group. In the training set, the model achieved an accuracy of 98.44% and an F1-score of 98.34%. In the test set, the accuracy, F1-score, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and AUC were 85.1%, 81.2%, 83.2%, 86.2%, 84.5%, 79.8%, and 85.8%, respectively (Table 2).

Scleral image analysis contributes to classifying lBD subtypes with different inflammatory states

Although UC and CD are both classified as inflammatory bowel diseases, they differ considerably in clinical manifestations and anatomical distribution. Whether overall differences exist in ocular manifestations during active inflammation remains unclear. In this section, we separately analyzed CD and UC at different inflammatory stages. A total of 31 patients with active CD and 47 patients with CD in remission were enrolled, with 254 scleral images from the active group and 386 scleral images from the remission group. In the training set, the model achieved an accuracy of 99.04% and an F1-score of 98.98%. In the test set, the accuracy, F1-score, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and AUC were 74.2%, 72.5%, 74.4%, 76.1%, 73.8%, 76.2%, and 77.9%, respectively (Table 2).

We also collected 52 scleral images from 6 patients with active UC and 101 scleral images from 13 patients with UC in remission. In the training set, the accuracy and F1-score were both 100%. In the test set, the accuracy, F1-score, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and AUC were 92.9%, 91.1%, 88.3%, 87.6%, 88.7%, 84.5%, and 91.3%, respectively (Table 2).

Scleral image analysis shows potential for distinguishing patients with lBD from non-lBD controls

We further applied artificial intelligence to analyze scleral images from patients with IBD and non-IBD controls. A total of 97 patients with IBD and 18 non-IBD controls were enrolled, with detailed demographic and clinical characteristics presented in Table 1. Overall, 942 scleral images were collected (149 from controls, 793 from IBD patients). In the training set, the model achieved an accuracy of 99.02% and an F1-score of 97.99%. In the test set, the accuracy, F1-score, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and AUC were 95.5%, 92.5%, 95.3%, 91.1%, 90.6%, 89.7%, and 92.9%, respectively (Table 2). Ocular manifestations may represent a potential biomarker for distinguishing IBD from non-IBD controls.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the feasibility of using ocular extraintestinal manifestations to assist in evaluating IBD disease activity. We found that an AI-based neural network model analyzing ocular congestion achieved good performance in differentiating active from remission UC. However, its performance was relatively poorer in distinguishing active versus remission CD. The model also performed well in differentiating patients with IBD from non-IBD based on ocular features. This model may be particularly suitable for the preliminary assessment of inflammatory activity in patients with UC.

When compared with healthy individuals, patients with IBD exhibit differences in some ocular parameters. In previous studies, corneal parameters in patients with IBD were assessed using Pentacam, which revealed thinner corneas than in controls [12]. A prospective cohort study of patients with IBD and non-IBD control subjects evaluated visual acuity, slit-lamp examination, and lacrimal output assessment. Patients with IBD frequently exhibited dry eye symptoms, which were associated with 5-ASA use [13]. Patients with IBD also demonstrated reduced blood flow in the ophthalmic and central retinal arteries, as analyzed by ocular color Doppler ultrasonography [14]. In the present study, we compared scleral congestion between patients with IBD and controls. The model achieved good discriminative performance, likely due to the adequate enrollment of patients with active IBD. Further studies including more non‑IBD subjects will be performed to validate the model.

We next stratified patients with IBD for comparative analysis. Non-invasive and simple approaches for assessing IBD activity have been widely explored, including fecal calprotectin (CP) testing [15] and intestinal ultrasound [16]. Fecal CP reflects intestinal neutrophil infiltration but cannot distinguish specific etiologies; for instance, levels are markedly elevated in Salmonella infection [15]. Intestinal ultrasound relies on specialized equipment and skilled operators, limiting its widespread use [17]. In this study, we investigated the association between scleral congestion and disease activity based on relevant extraintestinal manifestations. A neural network model analyzing scleral images effectively differentiated active from inactive IBD, particularly in patients with UC. Despite only 19 patients with UC were included, the model performed well, possibly because scleral congestion is more prominent in active UC than in active CD. The underlying mechanism remains unclear but may be associated with intestinal immune response translocation.

Artificial intelligence is increasingly being applied in the pathogenesis, diagnosis, and prognosis of IBD. Ofer et al. used machine learning-based gene prioritization to identify 67 novel IBD-associated risk genes [18]. Takenaka et al. constructed a deep neural network using 40,758 colonoscopic and 6885 biopsy images to assess UC, achieving 90.1% accuracy for endoscopic remission and 92.9% for histological remission [19]. Our previous study developed a fused correlation neural network (FCNN) to differentiate CD from intestinal tuberculosis, offering a non‑invasive diagnostic tool [20]. Carter et al. proposed a machine learning framework to identify intestinal wall thickening on CD ultrasound [21], while Stidham et al. used semi‑automated image analysis to evaluate small bowel stenosis and morphology in CD [22]. Ophthalmology is a pioneer in AI translation and has provided valuable experience for other medical fields. AI has been successfully applied to diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma, and other ocular disorders with high diagnostic performance. To our knowledge, no studies have yet applied AI to evaluate ocular manifestations in IBD. In the present study, we focused on ocular manifestations in IBD and achieved good performance in stratifying inflammatory activity. This approach is convenient and non‑invasive. In the future, ocular assessment may serve as a preliminary screening tool or be combined with other biomarkers for more comprehensive evaluation of IBD activity.

Owing to the constraints of sample size, misclassification may occur in this study. Nevertheless, this is an exploratory study, and further external validation and longitudinal follow-up are required to verify its reliability and predictive value. Although smartphone imaging is convenient, it has inherent technical limitations compared with professional ophthalmic photography, which may affect the detection of subtle ocular signs. Future multicenter, longitudinal studies with larger and more diverse cohorts, as well as optimized imaging protocols, are needed to address these limitations.

In this study, the method demonstrates significant potential for evaluating inflammation in IBD, serving as a complementary tool rather than a replacement for existing multimodal assessments. As a cost-effective and non-invasive approach, this may facilitate early disease detection. Even primary care institutions can perform preliminary screening for IBD activity using scleral imaging. As an adjunct to current diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, it allows rapid identification of disease activity and may help guide clinical decision-making. Given the rising incidence of IBD extraintestinal manifestations, using AI to evaluate ocular features for assessing IBD inflammation represents a promising future direction.
