Extreme Genetic Diversity and Signatures of Balancing Selection in Plasmodium vivax Blood-Stage Antigens
Authors: Alison Paolo N Bareng, Myo T Naung, Eli Martin, Somya Mehra, Jacob E Munro, Shannon Takala Harrison, Alyssa E Barry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKnowledge of the genetic diversity of Plasmodium vivax antigen vaccine candidates can offer valuable insights into antigens targeted by host immunity and their utility as vaccine candidates. We previously catalogued the genetic diversity of DBP, MSP1, and members of the RBP family. Here we report the diversity of 14 additional P vivax vaccine candidates to identify polymorphic domains and immune selection, to guide selection of alleles for vaccine development.MethodsA total of 289 of 353 publicly available P vivax whole genome sequences from Asia, the Americas, and Oceania passed a stringent variant-calling pipeline. Measures of diversity and balancing selection were calculated both across linear gene sequences and 3-dimensional (3D) proteins.ResultsThe blood-stage antigens AMA1, CyRPA, RBP1a, and P41 exhibited high genetic diversity and signatures of balancing selection on both linear and 3D structures. These signatures were consistent across all endemic regions, suggesting that these polymorphic loci are under immune selection. In contrast, CSP, TRAP, MSP4, MSP9, MSP10, GAMA, S12, ARP, S25, and S28 antigens showed low to intermediate genetic diversity and variable patterns among countries. Haplotype networks identified common variants that may represent antigenically distinct clusters. Importantly, sequences from the vaccine strain Sal-1 have extremely low global frequencies.ConclusionsAMA1, CyRPA, RBP1a, and P41 are under strong immune selection, while other antigens show limited diversity. Current vaccine formulations based on Sal-1 may have limited efficacy. Our results provide a framework for vaccine developers to select more common variants.Keywords: Plasmodium vivax, antigen diversity, vaccine design

Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax malaria are a major threat to global health, with P vivax proving particularly difficult to control and eliminate [1]. P. vivax liver stage dormancy, preference for reticulocytes, low-density parasitemia, and maturation of gametocytes prior to clinical symptoms favors transmission before diagnosis and treatment [2]. Furthermore, despite global reductions in P vivax cases through effective malaria control [3], challenges remain due to drug and insecticide resistance and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency (G6PD) in endemic countries. Development of a highly effective P vivax vaccine would overcome these issues, reduce the P vivax burden, and support global malaria elimination efforts [4].

Clinical trials conducted in Africa have resulted in World Health Organization approval of the P falciparum vaccines RTS,S [5] and R21 [6], which are based on the circumsporozoite protein (CSP). This provides support for the development of a malaria vaccine; however, P vivax vaccine research lags behind P falciparum with only 3 antigens—Duffy binding protein (DBP), apical membrane antigen 1 (AMA1), and CSP—being formulated as vaccine candidates, and CSP being the only antigen tested in clinical trials [7].

Malaria antigens harbor polymorphisms that may lead to strain-specific immunity [8–12]. These polymorphisms, including single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and variable numbers of tandem repeats, allow the parasite to evade previously acquired immune responses [13]. Resulting “gaps” in the host antibody repertoire may render the host susceptible to malaria upon infection with novel variants. Transmission may occur prior to new antibodies developing as it takes about 2 weeks for the host to produce new functional antibodies against the infecting strain [11]. A single-strain vaccine may therefore offer little to no protection against other antigenically distinct or emerging variants. This diversity is a major limitation for P vivax vaccines [2, 8, 14], considering its greater genetic diversity and more complex biology than P falciparum [15–17 ].

To advance P vivax vaccine development, we aimed to define the global genetic diversity of P vivax antigens by conducting a comprehensive population genetic analysis, leveraging publicly available whole genome sequence data from the MalariaGEN and Broad Institute P vivax community sequencing projects [18, 19]. Our findings provide a framework for selection of variants for vaccine development and for functional and structural studies on P vivax antigens and host immune responses.

METHODS

Selection of P vivax Antigens

An analysis of the diversity of serosurveillance biomarkers has been completed, including the leading P vivax vaccine candidates, DBP, merozoite surface protein 1 (MSP1), and some reticulocyte-binding protein (RBP) and Tryptophan Rich Antigen (TRAg) family members [20]. Fourteen additional P vivax antigens were analyzed in this study—namely, CSP, thrombospodin-related anonymous protein (TRAP), AMA1, cysteine-rich protective antigen (CyRPA), 6-cysteine protein P41 (P41), reticulocyte-binding protein 1a (RBP1a), 6-cysteine protein P12 (S12), merozoite surface protein 4 (MSP4), merozoite surface protein 9 (MSP9), merozoite surface protein 10 (MSP10), GPI-anchored micronemal protein (GAMA), asparagine-rich protein (ARP), ookinete surface protein P25 (S25), and ookinete surface protein P28 (S28). These antigens were selected based on prior evidence highlighting their immunogenicity and potential as P vivax vaccine candidates [7].

Antigen Gene Sequences

Plasmodium vivax whole genome sequences from the 2016 release of the MalariaGEN Plasmodium vivax Genome Variation Project [18] and the Broad Institute P. vivax genome sequencing project [19] were downloaded from the European Nucleotide Archive (Supplementary File 1). Reads were aligned to the PvP01 reference genome (version 1, PlasmoDB release 50: 15 December 2020), and variant calling was performed using GATK (version 4.0.12.0) with adaptations for haploid genomes. A 2-pass workflow was implemented to allow base quality score recalibration using internally derived high-confidence variants [10].

Only the variants within the 14 nuclear chromosomes were retained for further analysis. Variants from nonnuclear contigs and regions of extreme variability (eg, subtelomeric, centromeric, and hypervariable domains) were excluded based on predefined blacklists (Supplementary Figure 1; https://github.com/paolobareng/pvp01_blacklisted_regions.git). Further site- and sample-level filtering was applied to remove low-quality variants and low-confidence genotypes. The complete filtering criteria and thresholds are detailed in Supplementary Figure 1.

Sample complexity was assessed using the within-host diversity metric (FWS) calculated via the moimix R package (https://github.com/bahlolab/moimix). Samples with FWS > 0.80 (monoclonal or low-complexity multiclonal infections) were retained for downstream analyses. Gene regions corresponding to 14 P vivax antigens (Supplementary Table 1) were extracted from the final variant dataset and converted to FASTA format for population genetic analysis (https://github.com/paolobareng/Pvivax_vaccine) (Supplementary Figure 1).

Population Genetic Analysis

VaxPack (https://github.com/BarryLab01/vaxpack) was used to calculate traditional measures of diversity, such as Nei's nucleotide diversity (π) [21], haplotype diversity, number of polymorphic/segregating sites, and the number of synonymous and nonsynonymous SNPs. We measured the π on each antigen dataset for both whole antigen gene sequence as a single metric and in a 2-dimensional sliding window approach (window length = 100, step size = 3). In contrast, each antigen's haplotype diversity was measured by its entire coding sequence.

Tajima's D statistics were computed along each gene for populations with polymorphisms and at least 15 sequences, using a window size of 100 and step size of 3 [10, 22]. We calculated Tajima's D through distinct populations rather than assessing overall sequences as a single metric, as population structure could violate the assumptions to identify different selection patterns. Positive values of Tajima's D indicate balancing selection where alleles are maintained at intermediate frequencies in response to higher chance of evading host immune pressure, whereas negative values indicate purifying or directional selection, suggesting an excess of rare alleles.

Haplotype relationships and their geographic distribution were visualized through construction of haplotype network plots based on the Templeton, Crandall, and Sing inference from PoPArt software (version 4.8.5) restricting to nonsynonymous polymorphisms detected across the full-length gene sequences [23]. Data were referenced by location to determine geographic distribution and frequency of haplotypes clusters in different countries.

Mapping Population Genetic Features Using 3D Protein Modeling

AlphaFold2 [24] was used to predict in silico 3-dimensional (3D) structures of the selected P vivax antigens using default parameters. We selected antigens exhibiting substantial diversity and signatures of balancing selection, and those with resolved crystal protein structures in the Protein Data Bank (AMA1, CyRPA, RBP1a, P41, TRAP, S12, S28, and S25). The AlphaFold2-predicted structures were chosen based on confidence scores and were used for “spatial mapping” of π and Tajima’s D using a modified BiostructMap python package [25]. The π and D values were mapped onto the modeled protein structures. A 15 Å radius was used for each sliding window, and the results were visualized using PyMol version 2.4.0 (2015, https://pymol.org/2/, accessed date: March 2022) [26].

RESULTS

Sequence Data Quality and Filtering

The data analysis included 353 whole genomes obtained from P vivax isolates collected in 18 countries from Africa, Asia, Oceania, and the Americas (Supplementary Figure 1). The sequences were then filtered down to n = 289 (81.9%) through strict variant and sample quality control measures removing countries with <4 genomes and samples with high multiplicity of infection as measured by the FWS statistic (Supplementary Figure 1). FWS estimates that 70.9% (n = 205/289; FWS > 0.95) of the filtered sequence dataset consists of single-clone infections, 19.3% (56/289; 0.80 < FWS ≤ 0.95) are multiclonal infections, and 9.6% (28/289; FWS ≤ 0.80) have undetermined clonality. The final dataset used for analysis included 261 sequences from low-complexity infections derived from 11 countries (Supplementary Figure 1; Supplementary File 1).

Higher Genetic Diversity in Merozoite Antigens

The blood-stage antigens displayed the highest and most consistent genetic diversity among countries for the analyzed parasite antigen genes (Figure 1; Supplementary Table 2). Specifically, AMA1, CyRPA, and P41 demonstrated high nucleotide and haplotype diversity whereas several merozoite antigens, such as RBP1a, S12, and MSP9, exhibited intermediate nucleotide diversity but high haplotype diversity (few polymorphisms, but many combinations forming distinct, rare, and novel haplotypes), likely due to recombination events. Conversely, other merozoite surface antigens, including MSP4, MSP10, GAMA, and ARP, exhibited both low nucleotide and haplotype diversity (Figure 1; Supplementary Figure 2; Supplementary Table 2).

The preerythrocytic antigens (CSP and TRAP) have lower genetic diversity than the blood-stage antigens. Unlike P falciparum CSP, P vivax CSP displayed limited variability, with only 1 nonsynonymous polymorphism detected at the C-terminal region, exclusively observed in Southeast Asian isolates from Thailand, Myanmar, and Malaysia. Meanwhile, the full-length sequence of TRAP exhibited high haplotype diversity despite having only intermediate nucleotide diversity. Similarly, the gametocyte antigen genes S25 and S28 showed low nucleotide diversity across different populations (Figure 1; Supplementary Table 2).

Diversity Hotspots Are Associated With Known Functional Domains

We calculated nucleotide diversity across the length of each of the genes to identify specific antigen domains driving variability. To provide additional insight from physicochemical properties, we further analyzed nucleotide diversity in the context of 3D protein structural models. Both linear sequence and protein structure analyses revealed varied distribution of polymorphism along the gene. Some domains exhibited low diversity while others displayed extreme variability, particularly around known important functional domains (Figure 2A–D; Supplementary Figure 3A–D; Supplementary Table 2).

AMA1 polymorphic hotspots were concentrated in domains I and II (Figure 2A), where the RON2 hydrophobic binding residues and the putative erythrocyte binding sites are located (Supplementary Figure 4A). Similarly, high sequence variability was found in the C-terminus of P41 (Figure 2B), likely due to its exposure to immune defenses as evidenced by antiparallel arrangement of P41 to S12. For RBP1a, region III exhibited greater diversity than region II (Figure 2C), possibly due to extracellular exposure subjected to host antibody attacks. CyRPA showed diversity hotspots with higher frequency of nonsynonymous mutations than synonymous mutations in exon 1, which may play a critical role in merozoite invasion and be a target of host immunity (Figure 2D). Despite overall low diversity, functionally important domains within S12, TRAP, S25, and S28 (Supplementary Figure 4) exhibited higher diversity relative to other domains in these antigens (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Figure 3A–D).

Balancing Selection Is Consistent Across All Populations for Merozoite Antigens

Tajima's D was used to identify evidence of host immune (balancing selection) pressure along the length of the gene. Overall, our findings showed that polymorphic regions within cyrpa (exons 1 and 2), ama1 (domains I and II), rbp1a (regions II and III), p41 (C-terminus), and s12 (P41 interacting sites) genes have high positive Tajima's D across majority of the populations (Figure 3; Supplementary Figure 6A for s12). The observed similarities in diversity and balancing selection patterns across countries indicate that these domains could be targets of consistent selective forces such as host immunity. Conversely, several antigens displayed geographically variable patterns of balancing selection, with MSP9, MSP10, GAMA, and S28 (EGF3 and EGF4) exhibiting positive Tajima's D in Asian isolates and MSP4 and S25 (EGF1 and EGF2) having slightly positive peaks of Tajima's D detected mostly in Latin American isolates (Supplementary Figure 5 for MSP4, MSP9, MSP10, and GAMA; Supplementary Figure 6C and 6D for S28 and S25). There was no significant departure from neutrality identified in CSP, TRAP (VWA domain), and ARP, with Tajima's D value close to zero (Supplementary Figure 5). A summary of Tajima's D findings is listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Given the substantial deviations from neutral distribution observed in genetically variable domains of cyrpa, ama1, rbp1a, and p41, Tajima's D was calculated with integrated spatial coordinates to map these measures of selection onto the 3D protein structure (Figure 4) showing balancing selection “hotspots,” consistent with the linear sequence analysis (Figure 3). Furthermore, hotspots of balancing selection on these antigens map close to important host receptor binding interfaces, consistent with them being targets of immunity.

The polymorphic face of domains I to II of AMA1 displayed the highest Tajima's D across its protein structure. However, positive signals of Tajima's D were also detected on the “silent face” of the protein, where the conserved AMA1 Loop 1a residues are located (Figure 4A). Furthermore, balancing selection hotspots were identified in the N-terminal of the AMA1 protein, but this was distinctly observed in the Papua New Guinean (PNG) population (Figure 4A, encircled).

For cyrpa, strong evidence of balancing selection was observed in the highly polymorphic exon 1 and a specific segment within exon 2 in most populations (Figure 3D). Although these polymorphic regions appear distant in the linear sequence, they are spatially close in the protein structure (Figure 4D).

All populations consistently showed balancing selection hotspots in the C-terminus of P41, with some countries displaying significant Tajima's D values (Figures 3B and 4B). Moreover, the polymorphic tail region (RIII) of RBP1a (Figures 3C and 4C) and the putative interacting sites of P41 in S12 antigen (Supplementary Figure 4B) showed positive Tajima's D values, except for the Mexican population (Supplementary Figure 3A).

Geographically variable patterns of Tajima's D were detected in the genes for TRAP, S25, and S28 (Supplementary Figure 6B–D). The polymorphic VWA region of TRAP, where the metal ion–dependent adhesion site binding ligand is located (Supplementary Figure 4D), was not under selection in most countries, except for PNG and Mexico. Asian countries (Thailand and Cambodia) showed evidence of balancing selection in the thrombospondin type I repeat of TRAP, while South American countries (Peru and Colombia) showed balancing selection evidence in the 2 extensible beta ribbons (Supplementary Figure 6B). The EGF3 to EGF4 domains of S28 displayed positive balancing selection in Thai and Cambodian isolates but not in Central and South American isolates. Conversely, the EGF1 and EGF2 domains of S25 displayed positive Tajima's D, exclusively in Latin American isolates (Supplementary Figure 6C and 6D).

Global Distribution of Antigen Diversity

Antigens such as TRAP, P41, S12, RBP1a, MSP9, AMA1, S25, and S28 show regional clustering patterns with diversity (haplotypes) of P vivax antigens unevenly distributed across parasite populations (Figure 5). CSP, MSP4, MSP10, GAMA, and ARP had a more homogenous distribution of haplotypes throughout the geographic regions, as shown by clusters of similar haplotypes present in multiple countries. Interestingly, CyRPA appears to be less geographically structured and more diverse, as demonstrated by multiple rare haplotypes (Figure 5).

Reference alleles Sal-1 and PvP01 are indicated by dashed circles and boxes, respectively. The Sal-1 vaccine strain for most antigens, except S25 and S12, exhibit extremely low prevalence (<1%) in the global population. Abbreviation: PNG, Papua New Guinea.

The haplotype network plots showed that the vaccine strain allele Sal-1 and reference allele PvP01 were found in low frequencies and clustered with other sequences from the region of these isolates’ geographical origin in the Americas [16], and PvP01 in Papua Indonesia [17] (Figure 5).

DISCUSSION

Given the early stage of P vivax vaccine development, it is critical to review the extent and geographic distribution of P vivax diversity to guide vaccine design. We previously catalogued the diversity of 11 P vivax antigen candidates [20] and now extend this to 14 additional P vivax antigens. Our findings show higher diversity in merozoite antigens (AMA1, CyRPA, P41, RBP1a, MSP9), likely driven by adaptation to host immune responses, whereas other merozoite antigens show lower diversity (S12, MSP4, MSP10, GAMA, and ARP) and preerythrocytic (CSP, TRAP) and gametocyte (S25, S28) antigens show variable diversity across populations. For some merozoite antigens (CyRPA, AMA1, P41, RBP1a), diversity and balancing selection signals clustered in specific regions, consistent with patterns reported for P vivax serosurveillance antigens (DBPII, MSP5, RBP2a, RBP2b) [20], indicating that immune selection pressure is likely the main driver of this gene region–specific variability.

Among the blood-stage antigens, CyRPA showed extensive variability and evidence of balancing selection pressure as previously described [27, 28 ], contrasting with P falciparum where CyRPA has limited diversity and is neutrally evolving [10]. This difference might be due to the distinct role of CyRPA in P vivax, as opposed to P falciparum, where CyRPA forms a complex network with PfRh5 and PfRiPR [29]. Plasmodium vivax CyRPA has demonstrated a change in function [30] and might have a larger role in merozoite invasion exposing it to the host immune system [27, 28]. Several serological studies reported high immunogenicity and antigenicity of P vivax CyRPA in different populations [31, 32] indicating its possible crucial role in P vivax biology and merozoite invasion.

P41, particularly at the C-terminus, showed high balancing selection across all populations, aligning with earlier reports of its robust immune response, making it a strong vaccine candidate. AMA1's domains I and II, where the RON2-binding residues are located, displayed high Tajima's D values, suggesting that these polymorphisms are important immune targets. However, the recently discovered RON2-independent invasion pathway of AMA1 highlights the complexity of the P vivax invasion process, as the direct binding of AMA1 to erythrocyte occurs in the conserved region of the protein called Loop 1a [33, 34]. The discovery of the highly conserved P vivax AMA1 Loop1a functional region emphasizes an alternative vaccine design approach, in addition to focusing on polymorphic regions. Moreover, the balancing selection hotspot in AMA1's N-terminal region, exclusively observed in the PNG population, is noteworthy, as it could potentially indicate localized human genetic adaptation.

Preerythrocytic antigen genes showed limited diversity including the leading antigen vaccine candidate, CSP, with a notable polymorphism exclusively seen in Southeast Asia. This location-specific CSP polymorphism may be related to differences in transmission patterns and vector distribution among populations. The gametocyte antigens S25 and S28 also showed limited diversity, which are likely attributed to their limited expression in the human host [35 ]. Interestingly, the merozoite surface antigens such as MSP4, MSP10, GAMA, and ARP are relatively conserved, which might be due to a variety of factors, such as the necessity to maintain function, possible low gene expression, and minimal antibody access to these antigens [36].

Mapping key diversity parameters over 3D protein structures provided insights into antibody-mediated selection pressure. Nonsynonymous polymorphisms clustered around host–pathogen binding interfaces, consistent with previous investigations. These studies revealed that the critical binding residues in AMA1 domain I [37], CyRPA exon I [27, 28], region III of RBP1a [38, 39], C-terminus of P41 [40, 41], and VWA domain in TRAP [42], have limited variability due to functional and structural constraints, while frequent polymorphisms can be found around the binding radius, possibly due to the immense selection pressure exerted by host antibodies.

Comparative analysis of balancing (immune) selection hotspots across countries revealed similar selection patterns in AMA1 domain I, CyRPA exon I, RBP1a region III, and P41 C-terminus, with minor differences in Mexican isolates, suggesting common evolutionary pressure from host functional immune responses. Mutations in these hotspots could facilitate parasites to escape host immune recognition or result from host genetics factors, such as the erythrocyte receptor specificity, or unique red blood cell (RBC) variants as seen in the AMA1 N-terminal region in the PNG population (Figure 4A). RBC polymorphisms in PNG confer protection against severe malaria symptoms [43], and thus we speculate that the significant Tajima's D value at the AMA1 N-terminus exclusively detected within PNG could be a local parasite adaptation to the unique host genetics. The distinct selection patterns observed in Mexico are likely due to recent bottlenecks from declining transmission due to control strategies [44]. In addition, differences in P vivax transmission and population histories between the Old World (Asia) and New World (the Americas) would explain continental diversity and selection pattern variations. The lack of population structure in domains with balancing selection signatures may be from immune escape polymorphisms or inconsistencies in the meta-population data, such as sampling times and sample size. Nevertheless, most analyzed countries share diverse regions aiding in evaluating vaccine antigen candidates against P vivax.

The low frequency of the Sal-1 isolate allele, particularly for the diverse blood-stage antigens AMA1, CyRPA, RBP1a, and P41 where prevalence was <1%, suggests that vaccines based on the Sal-1 strain (or any single-strain–based vaccine) may not induce broad responses against all circulating alleles. To address antigenic variability, careful selection of alleles guided by findings from this analysis are warranted, similar to our previous study [20 ], where diagnostic biomarkers were chosen from the most prevalent haplotype. However, practical constraints of antigen production, formulation, and cost remain [12]. Furthermore, sequence diversity does not always correlate with strain-specific immunity [45], so clinical evaluation is necessary to better understand the implications of sequence diversity for vaccine efficacy.

One limitation is the number of sequences and timeframes of sample collection across different populations. Sample numbers are extremely low for Malaysia (n = 6), Vietnam (n = 9), Myanmar (n = 9), Indonesia (n = 5), and Brazil (n = 4). In Thailand, some sequences were obtained from samples collected in the mid-2000s, while others were collected in 2013 [18, 19]. Parasite populations are subject to demographic events over time, such as population bottlenecks or expansions, which could distort underlying genetic patterns. The precision of population genetic measures could be improved with greater sample sizes and more temporally consistent datasets. Another limitation is the exclusion of repeat regions and indels. While these are an important source of genetic diversity, they are difficult to interpret, particularly in regions with repetitive sequences or alignment ambiguity. Finally, the analysis does not include data from Africa. Incorporating whole genome sequencing data from these regions in future work will be critical to obtain a more complete picture of global antigenic diversity.

Designing an effective P vivax vaccine requires understanding the diversity and evolution of the candidate antigens. Insights into the diversity of these antigens will inform strain-specific immunity, as observed in P falciparum vaccine studies [8, 9, 46]. Our findings provide critical data on global and population-level diversity of the 14 P vivax antigens, identifying key antigens and important antigen regions for vaccine design. Consistent immune selection signals across diverse populations highlight AMA1, CyRPA, P41, and RBP1a as immune targets. Future studies could identify specific polymorphisms contributing to P vivax immune escape to determine serotype profiles for each antigen.
