Next-generation inhibitors of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro overcome the deficiencies of Paxlovid
Authors: Gan Luo, Gang Wang, Chongbing Liao, Baisen Zhong, Lianfeng Fan, Jue Zhang, Jie Rao, Junjie Zhang, Ziqiao Wang, Man Luo, Yan Yan, Lu Lu, Wei Xu, Fan Wu, Xihui Gao, Wuyuan Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt remains elusive to design peptidomimetic inhibitors of SARS-CoV-2 main protease (Mpro) refractory to multiple deficiencies of Paxlovid (ritonavir-boosted nirmatrelvir), pertaining mainly to E166X mutations-conferred drug resistance and inherent pharmacokinetic limitations to nirmatrelvir. We identify via virtual screening an iso-quinoline P1 moiety in place of the traditional γ-lactam and design iso-quinoline-containing inhibitors with high affinity for Mpro and its nirmatrelvir-resistant E166X mutants. Further optimization at P4 cultivates distinctive peptidomimetic inhibitors with drastically improved pharmacokinetic properties and significantly enhanced antiviral efficacy independent of ritonavir. Two such inhibitors, FD3-32 and FD3-36, also potent against SARS-CoV-1 and MERS-CoV Mpro, are more effective as a monotherapy regimen than Paxlovid in reducing viral loads in vivo and protecting infected male mice from acute lung injury. Here, we report the discovery of next-generation SARS-CoV-2 Mpro inhibitors that overcome the deficiencies of Paxlovid, promising efficacious antivirals critical for mitigating the current and future pandemics of coronaviruses.Subject terms: Structure-based drug design, SARS-CoV-2, X-ray crystallography, Mass spectrometry

Introduction

Within a short period of two decades, the world has already succumbed to three major pandemics of coronaviruses with grave human suffering, i.e., severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 1 (SARS-CoV-1) in 20031, Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV) in 20122, and, particularly, SARS-CoV-2 that causes coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19)3. Tens of millions of people have died worldwide from COVID-19 since December 20194, and many more patients who survived SARS-CoV-2 infection have, without timely antiviral intervention, suffered long COVID – a collection of long-lasting symptoms such as fatigue, shortness of breath, chest pain, brain fog, etc5. The ritonavir (RTV)-boosted oral antiviral nirmatrelvir (NTV), i.e., Paxlovid, developed by Pfizer as a peptidomimetic inhibitor of SARS-CoV-2 main protease (Mpro or 3CLpro), has saved millions of lives globally and is chiefly responsible, along with mRNA vaccination, for largely mitigating the COVID-19 pandemic.

SARS-CoV-2 Mpro is a cysteine protease that processes viral polyproteins important for the assembly and replication of the positive-sense single-stranded RNA β-coronavirus3,6–9. Mpro operates the Cys145-His41 catalytic dyad in its active cleft to cleave protein substrates at the specific site of GlnP1-XxxP1’7–9; this P1-restricted substrate specificity differs from that of known human cysteine proteases8,10, making SARS-CoV-2 Mpro an ideal drug target for antiviral therapy10. Numerous Mpro inhibitors of different structural classes have been reported11,12, among which NTV, a rationally designed peptidomimetic covalent inhibitor with a γ-lactam ring (formed intramolecularly between the α-amino and side-chain amide groups of Gln) at P1 position13, and ensitrelvir (ETV), a non-covalent small-molecule inhibitor derived through structure-based virtual screening14, are clinically proven to be highly effective antivirals for the treatment of SARS-CoV-2 infections.

Despite the huge initial success achieved by Paxlovid in mitigating the COVID-19 pandemic, two major deficiencies of the antiviral are emerging, including evolved drug resistance and adverse clinical effects posed by RTV. The γ-lactam ring at P1 is a general molecular signature of most peptidomimetic covalent inhibitors of Mpro as it supersedes the Gln side chain with reduced conformational entropy and, thus, improved binding affinity for the viral protease15,16. In fact, a network of three H-bonds formed by the P1 residue, Gln side chain or γ-lactam ring, with the three residues F140, H163 and E166 lining part of the S1 binding pocket is strictly maintained in both peptide substrate-Mpro and NTV-Mpro complexes (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. S1), structurally validating the “canonical” γ-lactam-for-Gln design strategy. When a six-membered δ-lactam ring was introduced at P1 of nirmatrelvir, a stronger H-bond formed between the lactam NH and E166 side chain carboxylate, largely responsible for several-fold enhancement of inhibitory and antiviral activity17. While this high-fidelity H-bonding network involving the side chains of H163 Nε2 and E166 Oε2 ensures recognition specificity in both catalysis and inhibition, it invites escape mutations at these two positions for the virus to develop drug resistance. A recent study has shown that an H163A mutation completely abolishes the catalytic activity of Mpro due to mutation-induced conformational changes18, leaving E166 all the more vulnerable to either laboratory-evolved or clinically selected mutations for potential resistance19–22. In fact, drug-resistant variants of the virus featuring E166A or E166V mutation in Mpro, often in combination with M49L, L50F and/or L167F compensatory mutations to partially improve replicative fitness19, exhibit much-reduced sensitivity to NTV and/or ETV due mainly to the loss of electrostatic interactions afforded by E166 side chain19,23–27. Although the prevalence of E166X mutations is relatively low in clinical strains21, pre-emptive and sharply focused efforts are warranted to develop peptidomimetic inhibitors of Mpro with a “non-canonical” P1 moiety to maximally circumvent drug resistance conferred by E166 mutations.

Adverse clinical effects posed by RTV are perhaps a more imminent threat to the future success of Paxlovid, which brought in for Pfizer $5.7B revenues in 2024 alone28. RTV is an HIV-1 protease inhibitor clinically used along with other drugs for the treatment of HIV-1 infection29,30. Coincidentally, RTV also potently inhibits cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4), an enzyme in the liver that metabolizes NTV primarily through oxidation of the γ-lactam ring13, and P-glycoprotein (P-gp), a multidrug efflux transporter in the plasma membrane that diminishes intracellular NTV31. While co-administration of RTV significantly improves the therapeutic efficacy of NTV against SARS-CoV-2 infection by suppressing hepatic metabolism and drug efflux32, it poses serious concerns of drug-drug interactions with multiple medications even after withdrawal of Paxlovid33,34, thus adversely affecting the treatment of other diseases31,34,35. For this reason, earnest efforts are ongoing to develop efficacious antiviral monotherapy against SARS-CoV-2 infection without RTV36,37.

In this work, we initially set out to tackle evolved drug resistance to NTV through the discovery of a distinctive class of peptidomimetic inhibitors against SARS-CoV-2 Mpro and its E166X mutants. We find that iso-quinoline in place of the γ-lactam ring at P1 largely overcomes drug resistance instigated by E166X mutations. Additional modifications at P4 improve pharmacokinetic properties of these inhibitors, which leads in a monotherapy to enhanced in vivo antiviral efficacy compared with NTV (with or without RTV). Further, these inhibitors are potently active against Mpro from SARS-CoV-1 and MERS-CoV, promising a next generation of broad-spectrum antivirals with far-reaching implications for clinical intervention of future pandemics of sarbecoviruses and merbecoviruses.

Results

Identification of pyridine/iso-quinoline derivatives at P1 via virtual library screening

To discover an alternative class of P1 groups in replace of γ-lactam, we first validated our docking protocol using ETV as a quality control (Supplementary Fig. S2), followed by virtually screening of a commercial library of ~ 3,000,000 compounds from four Enamine collections (advanced, functional, hts and premium) against the catalytic cleft of Mpro, from which 10,847 hits were obtained after binding energy-based refinement. Hit compounds were further divided into ten clusters based on Morgan fingerprints38 and projected into a two-dimensional (2D) chemical space for abundance and distribution (Fig. 1b). The compound with the strongest binding energy from each cluster was selected (Supplementary Fig. S3), and its 2D chemical structure presented in Fig. 1b. Nine out of these ten representative compounds contained a heterocycle P1 moiety (colored in hot pink), among which four compounds comprised the same pyridine group at P1 (Fig. 1b). This finding prompted us to conduct a substructure refinement search for aromatic nitrogen heterocycles, which led to the identification of pyridine and its derivatives (2066/10847) in 19% compounds from the above 10,847 hits (Fig. 1c). While pyridine and its derivatives displayed a high probability density in the 2D chemical space as monocyclic replacements for γ-lactam, iso-quinoline and its derivatives at 0.42% (46/10847) stood out as a bicyclic aromatic nitrogen heterocycle at P1 with the highest probability density. Of note, pyridine at P1 had been previously reported for some non-covalent inhibitors of Mpro, and the crystal structures of their complexes with the enzyme determined (Supplementary Fig. S4)39–41. In a non-peptidic inhibitor scaffold discovered by the COVID Moonshot team, isoquinoline in place of pyridine at P1 was found to substantially boost Mpro inhibition through expanded hydrophobic interactions42. Unfortunately, these non-covalent inhibitors failed to achieve sufficient therapeutic exposure in vivo due to high clearance and low bioavailability42.

To dissect the preference of Mpro for pyridine/iso-quinoline derivatives at P1, we analyzed in a molecular docking study six representative compounds from the substructure refinement search (Supplementary Fig. S5). As shown in Supplementary Fig. S5, the aromatic pyridine/iso-quinoline ring occupied the same position as γ-lactam, with its nitrogen atom (as opposed to the carbonyl oxygen atom in γ-lactam) accepting an H-bond of ~ 3.0 Å from H163 Nε2, while leaving F140 O and E166 Oε2 in a “unbound” state due to the lack of additional H-bond acceptors/donors as previously seen (Supplementary Fig. S4)39–41. To interrogate further, we designed and synthesized 29 non-covalent inhibitors of Mpro comprising a pyridine/iso-quinoline moiety (for the S1 pocket) linked to an aromatic (for the S2 pocket) (Supplementary Fig. S6a). Most of these compounds exhibited dose-dependent inhibition of wild-type (WT) Mpro with low micromolar Ki values and highly similar docking modes in the catalytic cleft as predicted (Supplementary Figs. S6b, c, S7). Importantly, iso-quinoline and its bicyclic derivatives at P1 were generally superior to pyridine and its monocyclic derivatives as evidenced by their significant differences in Ki values (Supplementary Fig. S6a, b). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation studies of six representative synthetic compounds docked in the active site of Mpro confirmed the presence of a highly stable H-bond donated by H163 Nε2 to the nitrogen atom of both monocyclic (FD6-7 and FD6-8) and bicyclic derivatives (FD6-10, FD6-31, FD6-68, and FD6-69) at P1 (Fig. 1d, e, Supplementary Figs. S8a, S9). Moreover, while both types of derivatives interacted favorably with H163, H164 and M165 of Mpro, residues 140–145 collectively contributed more binding energy to the bicyclic P1 moieties than to the monocyclic counterparts (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. S8b), thereby explaining the superiority of iso-quinoline over pyridine at P1.

Structural validation by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and X-ray crystallography

We expressed 15N-labeled Mpro in E. coli (Supplementary Fig. S10) and determined its 2D NMR spectra in the presence and absence of some pyridine/iso-quinoline-derived inhibitors. As shown in Fig. 2a, b, the 1H-15N TROSY-HSQC (transverse relaxation optimized spectroscopy-heteronuclear single quantum correlation) spectrum of either apo or inhibitor-bound Mpro displayed generally well-dispersed resonance peaks, except for the broadening of some in the center, as reported previously (Supplementary Figs. S11, S12)43,44. In the absence of a more comprehensive NMR study of our Mpro with limited spectroscopic data, we only made partial assignments according to the published spectra (Supplementary Figs. S11, S12)43,44. The chemical shifts assigned in this study alongside the previously reported values are listed in Supplementary Table S1, accompanied by calculated combined chemical shift deviations (CCSD) as a measure of assignment reliability/ambiguity. For clarity, the assignments were further grouped and differentially colored based on the degree of resonance peak crowding and/or overlapping in Supplementary Table S1. Addition of each of the four inhibitors FD6-7, FD6-10, FD6-31 and FD6-68 to Mpro at a molar ratio of 2:1 invariably induced significant alterations in chemical shifts of some resonance peaks assigned to residues in the catalytic cavity, including V42, T45, F134, G170, V171, T175, V186, and Q192 (Fig. 2c–f). The resonance peaks of some residues located in the S1 pocket such as F140, L141, N142 and E166 became much sharper upon inhibitor binding (Supplementary Fig. S13), indicative of strengthened S1 interactions with pyridine/iso-quinoline derivatives at P1. Of note, FD6-10 with bicyclic iso-quinoline at P1 exerted a much stronger influence on both the sharpness and chemical shifts of those resonance peaks than did FD6-7 with monocyclic methylpyridine, whereas FD6-31 with iso-quinoline and FD6-68 with naphthyridine behaved largely the same (Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Fig. S13), thus corroborating the findings from MD simulation studies and inhibitory activity measurements.

To validate their mode of inhibition at atomic levels, we solved the crystal structure of Mpro in complex with FD6-31 at 1.96 Å resolution (Supplementary Table S2). As shown in the electron density map of FD6-31 occupying the catalytic cavity of Mpro (Fig. 2g), the iso-quinoline moiety embeds itself in the S1 pocket, while the chloro-phenyl group packs against the S2 pocket. As predicted, the N atom of iso-quinoline accepts an H-bond of 2.9 Å from H163 Nε2, an indispensable interaction further strengthened by an aromatic heterocycle (π-π) stacking between the imidazole and iso-quinoline rings (Fig. 2h). Additional stackings form between the iso-quinoline ring and the peptide bond (amide-π) of L141-N142 as well as the side chain (sulfur-π) of C145, which, along with a crystalline water molecule (W1) bridging the phenyl moiety of iso-quinoline with N142 N via two H-bonds, contribute collectively to the preferred recognition of iso-quinoline by the S1 pocket (Fig. 2h). In the S2 pocket, the chloro-phenyl moiety of FD6-31, sandwiched between the side chains of M49 and M165, packs its aromatic ring against H41 (π-π stacking), with its Cl atom in intimate contact with H41 Nδ1, M165 Cε and D187 Cβ (Fig. 2i). An H-bond of 3.0 Å formed between G143 N and the carbonyl O of FD6-31, hydrophobic interactions between C145 Sγ and the heterocyclic linker of FD6-31, and a crystalline water molecule (W2)-mediated indirect contact between the carbonyl O of FD6-31 and C145 N, further stabilize the non-covalent inhibitor in the catalytic cavity of Mpro (Fig. 2j). Of note, the co-crystal structure largely corroborated the docking model of the FD6-31-Mpro complex (Fig. 2k), attesting to the validity of computational efforts. In fact, we further conducted refined MD simulations and computational alanine scan analysis based on the co-crystal structure of FD6-31-Mpro complex (Supplementary Fig. S14), and the results, while entirely consistent with the structural findings, reinforced the energetic importance of C145, H163, and the peptide bond of L141-N142. Finally, comparison of the crystal structures of Mpro in complex with NTV and with FD6-31 underscored some critically important differences between γ-lactam and iso-quinoline in the S1 pocket (Fig. 2l). The former is mainly stabilized by an intricate network of triangulated H-bonds with F140 O, H163 Nε2 and E166 Oε2, whereas the latter only engages H163 Nε2 for binding (Fig. 2l). Despite fewer H-bonds iso-quinoline forms, its aromaticity affords compensatory π stacking interactions absent in the binding of γ-lactam, contributing not only to its energetic preference by the S1 pocket, but more importantly, to its selection as a non-canonical P1 moiety independent of resistance-prone E166 mutations.

Structure-to-activity relationships (SAR) of peptidomimetic inhibitors with iso-quinoline at P1 against SARS-CoV-2 Mpro and three critical E166X mutants

Inspired by the above findings, we set out to test the proof-of-concept of designing peptidomimetic covalent inhibitors against wild-type (WT) and NTV-resistant Mpro through substituting isoquinoline for γ-lactam. A fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET)-based enzymatic assay was employed to determine the Ki value of each inhibitor45,46. To this end, we initially replaced γ-lactam of NTV with pyridine, yielding a weakened inhibitor (FD3-5) against WT Mpro with a Ki value of 29.0 nM, 6-fold higher than that of NTV (Ki = 5.10 nM) (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. S15 and Supplementary Table S3). However, iso-quinoline replacement of γ-lactam improved inhibitory activity of FD3-6 over NTV by 40%, resulting in a Ki value of 3.08 nM (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. S15 and Supplementary Table S3). This initial study functionally confirmed the superiority of iso-quinoline over pyridine, which was also verified in the scaffold of inhibitor GC376 (Fig. 3a), cementing the use of iso-quinoline as an invariant P1 moiety in subsequent extensive SAR investigation aimed at further optimizing subsite interactions involving P2, P3 and P4.

We chemically synthesized 15 additional derivatives of FD3-6 (FD3-11 to FD3-28, Fig. 3b) by introducing cyclo-propyl-spiro-proline in place of dimethyl-cyclo-propyl-proline moiety at P2, cyclohexyl in place of tert-butyl at P3, and 13 different groups in place of trifluoromethyl at P4. While both cyclo-propyl-spiro-proline at P2 and cyclohexyl at P3 as well as most substituents at P4 were functionally deleterious (Fig. 3b), difluoro-chloromethyl (FD3-22), cyclopropyl (FD3-24) and difluoro-cyclopropyl (FD3-25) at P4 improved inhibitory activity by 2-3 folds as compared with trifluoromethyl in FD3-11 (Fig. 3b). The P4 position of GC376 backbone was also interrogated through replacing benzyl (FD3-8) with indolyl derivatives, and the resultant inhibitors FD3-9, FD3-30 and FD3-31 all functionally benefited from an enlarged aromatic substitution.

Given the SAR results, the P2 and P3 moieties were reverted to those of NTV in the next round of inhibitor design, which incorporated difluoro-chloromethyl and difluoro-cyclopropyl substituents at P4, yielding compounds FD3-32 and FD3-33 (Fig. 3c). FD3-32 and FD3-33 potently inhibited WT Mpro with respectively Ki values of 0.513 and 1.23 nM, roughly 5 ~ 10-fold stronger than NTV (Supplementary Fig. S15 and Supplementary Table S3). A slight increase in the ring size at P4 from difluoro-cyclopropyl (FD3-33) to difluoro-cyclobutyl (FD3-34, Ki = 0.983 nM) marginally improved inhibitor activity (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. S15 and Supplementary Table S3). Interestingly, while replacement of one fluorine atom with Cl in the trifluoromethyl group substantially increased the inhibitory activity of FD3-32 over FD3-6, introduction of bromine at the same position had little effect on FD3-36.

A critical test of the proof-of-concept of the design of compounds FD3-6, FD3-32, FD3-33, FD3-34 and FD3-36 lies in their enhanced ability to inhibit E166X mutants of Mpro. We expressed in E. coli three reported E166X mutants of Mpro resistant to ETV and/or NTV (Supplementary Fig. S16), i.e., M49L/E166A23, L50F/E166A/L167F24, and L50F/E166V24. Added to the panel of inhibitors for comparison was Ibuzatrelvir (ITV) – a next-generation monotherapeutic peptidomimetic inhibitor under clinical development by Pfizer against SARS-CoV-2 Mpro36. As shown in Fig. 3d, e, while NTV, ITV, and ETV in particular, lost much of their inhibitory activity against the M49L/E166A mutant, registering respective Ki values of 224 nM, 477 nM, and 71.3 μM, FD3-6 (8.62 nM), FD3-32 (2.18 nM), FD3-33 (1.76 nM), FD3-34 (10.6 nM) and FD3-36 (4.65 nM) all maintained potent inhibition of the enzyme with Ki values in the low nanomolar concentration range (Supplementary Table S4). With respect to the NTV/ETV-resistant triple mutant L50F/E166A/L167F, although all our compounds exhibited somewhat reduced inhibition to double-digit nanomolar Ki values, they were still 15 ~ 43-fold stronger than NTV and ITV, and 168 ~ 400-fold better than ETV (Fig. 3d, e and Supplementary Table S4). The double mutant L50F/E166V is particularly resistant to NTV24, which inhibited the enzyme in our assays with a Ki value of 25 μM, representing a 5000-fold reduction in inhibitory activity (Fig. 3d, e and Supplementary Table S4). However, our designed inhibitors FD3-32, FD3-33, FD3-34 and FD3-36 faired 23 ~ 150-folder better than NTV, with FD3-33 being the strongest (Ki = 167 nM). Taken together, these data biochemically validate the inhibitor design strategy for tackling E166 mutation-conferred drug resistance through substituting a non-canonical moiety, iso-quinoline, for the canonical γ-lactam group at P1 – an iterative process also facilitated by the optimization of P4.

The importance of H163 for Mpro inhibition by FD3-32 and FD3-36

H163 in the S1 pocket of Mpro forms via its imidazole ring a critical H-bond with the sidechain of Gln at P1 of the substrate (Fig. 1a)8, thus important for catalysis. Tran et al. previously reported that an H163A mutation completely inactivated Mpro by inducing in the active site a conformational change confined by a newly formed disulfide bridge between the catalytic thiol group of C145 and neighboring C11718. Given that the iso-quinoline ring at P1 of our inhibitors is also H-bonded to H163 (Fig. 2h), we produced an H163A mutant and characterized its inhibition by NTV, ITV, FD3-32 and FD3-36 to better understand the functional importance of H163 for Mpro inhibition (Supplementary Fig. S17).

As shown in Fig. 3f, g, the H163A mutation markedly reduced enzymatic activity (kcat/Km) by a whopping 600-fold (Fig. 3g) but did not fully obliterate it, contrasting the previous report18. Since the catalytic activity of a cysteine protease depends much on the use of a strongly reducing agent in the assay buffer to keep the catalytic thiol group from being oxidized or forming a disulfide bond47, this observed discrepancy may likely be attributable to differences in experimental conditions pertinent to reducibility. Importantly, the 600-fold decrease in enzymatic activity mostly came from the loss of the turnover number or kcat, but not from Km, suggesting that during catalysis, H163 likely formed a much stronger H-bond with P1 Gln of the substrate in the transition state than in the ground state.

While important for catalysis, H163 was also crucial for Mpro inhibition by NTV, ITV, FD3-32 and FD3-36 as it formed the critical H-bond with the γ-lactam or iso-quinoline ring at P1 (Fig. 1a and Fig. 2h). As shown in Fig. 3h, a huge reduction of ~ 4 orders of magnitude in inhibitory activity was observed for these Mpro inhibitors against the H163A mutant, registering decreases in Ki of 40,000-fold for FD3-32 and of 4000-fold for FD3-36 (Supplementary Table S4). Of note, although these Mpro inhibitors can theoretically induce mutations at position 163 conferring resistance, it is likely to be difficult for drug-resistant H163X mutants to emerge due to its greatly reduced catalytic activity or replication fitness. Nevertheless, adaptive laboratory evolution of SARS-CoV-2 in the presence of FD3-32 and FD3-36 is warranted in future studies to rigorously survey the landscape of resistance for our inhibitors to attest their ability or lack thereof to overcome additional Mpro mutations that may arise.

Mode of action of FD3-32 and FD3-33 against Mpro

Given their potent inhibitory activity against Mpro and its NTV-resistant mutants, FD3-32 and FD3-33 were selected for an in-depth study to decipher their mechanisms of inhibition at the molecular level. We characterized 15N-labeled WT Mpro in the presence and absence of the inhibitors and NTV by NMR spectroscopy. As shown in the 1H-15N TROSY-HSQC spectra (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. S18a, b), addition of FD3-32, FD3-33 or NTV to Mpro induced, as expected, notable shifts of resonance peaks assigned to the residues in the catalytic cavity (Fig. 4b, c and Supplementary Fig. S18c–f). As was the case with non-covalent inhibitors (Supplementary Fig. S13), binding of FD3-32, FD3-33 or NTV to Mpro invariably sharpened resonance peaks of the residues located in the S1 pocket, including F140, L141, N142, E166 and L167 (Fig. 4b, c and Supplementary Fig. S18c–f). However, the degree and extent of chemical shifts caused by our peptidomimetic inhibitors and NTV varied significantly, as evidenced by changes in chemical shift of the three sets of residue(s) V171 and Q192, G170 and F185, and M49 (Fig. 4b, c and Supplementary Fig. S18c–f). These findings illustrate that FD3-32 or FD3-33, in comparison with NTV, while occupying the same orthosteric site in Mpro, differentially induced conformational perturbations not only to local residues composed of the S1 pocket, but also to adjacent residues located in a β-hairpin, an α-helix and a loop motif surrounding the S2, S3 and S4 pockets (Fig. 4d). MD simulations based on the crystal structures of Mpro (apo state) and NTV-Mpro and on the docking complexes of FD3-32/FD3-33-Mpro (Supplementary Fig. S19) corroborated the NMR results by generating ternary phase diagrams for analysis of the mobility, as characterized by statistical dispersion of the MD ensemble, of each of those three structural elements. As shown in Fig. 4e, while inhibitor binding to Mpro differentially increased the mobility of the α-helix (aa 45–51), binding of FD3-32/FD3-33 constrained the conformations of the β-hairpin (aa 166–172) and the loop (aa 185–193) more strongly than that of NTV, indicative of enhanced P1-S1 and P4-S4 interactions involving our inhibitors.

Using ESI-TOF mass spectrometry, we detected covalent complexes of Mpro formed with our peptidomimetic inhibitors upon mixing (Supplementary Fig. S20), validating the mechanism of covalent inhibition. We further compared NTV and FD3-33 in their ability to form covalent complexes with Mpro and its three resistant mutants, M49L/E166A, L50F/E166A/L167F and L50F/E166V, as the molar ratio of inhibitor to enzyme was progressively raised from 0.039:1 to 5:1. As shown in Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. S21, the abundance of enzyme-inhibitor complex relative to free enzyme on mass spectrometry was, as expected, dependent on the concentration and potency of inhibitor, qualitatively confirming the supremacy of FD3-33 over NTV in covalent inhibition of both wild type and mutant enzymes. Of note, we could not quantify these interactions as the covalent bond of thioimidate formed by the catalytic thiol group of the enzyme and the cyano group of the inhibitor is known to be acid labile and readily breaks down reversibly under LC-MS conditions.

Drug-resistant mutations of Mpro marginally reduced the thermal stability of the enzyme as characterized by melting temperature (Tm) measured in a protein thermal shift assay48–50 (Fig. 4g), likely contributing to its impaired replicative fitness. While binding of FD3-33 and NTV to WT Mpro substantially improved the enzyme’s stability, the effect of our inhibitor was much more pronounced (Tm from 53.5 to 75 °C) than that of NTV (Tm from 53.6 °C to 65.8 °C) (Fig. 4h), indicative of a stronger binding affinity of FD3-33 for WT Mpro than NTV. Similarly, while NTV binding showed rather limited influence on the stability of the three resistant mutants of Mpro with ΔTm only ranging from 0.9 °C to 3.5 °C (Fig. 4h), FD3-33 binding resulted in significant Tm shifts by 12.8 °C for L50F/E166A/L167F mutant and 12.5 °C for M49L/E166A mutant (Fig. 4h). The smallest Tm shift of 1.4 °C seen with L50F/E166V mutant was likely attributed to FD3-33 being the least potent inhibitor against L50F/E166V mutant than against WT Mpro and the other two mutants. Taken together, these findings fully elucidate at the molecular and structural levels the mechanisms of action by which our designed inhibitors decimate the function of Mpro and its drug-resistant mutants.

In vitro and in vivo antiviral efficacy, pharmacokinetic and safety profiles of FD3-32 and FD3-33

We first subjected our peptidomimetic inhibitors FD3-6, FD3-32, FD3-33 and FD3-34 to a cytotoxicity assay using human lung epithelial cell A549, mouse macrophage J774A.1, transformed human liver epithelial-2 (THLE-2) cell and African green monkey kidney epithelial cell Vero E6, and none of them impacted the viability of these different cell lines at the highest concentration of 50 μM tested (Supplementary Fig. S22). FD3-32 and FD3-33, along with NTV, were subsequently evaluated in an in vitro antiviral efficacy assay using Vero E6 cells infected with SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.5.2 (100 TCID50). While FD3-32 and FD3-33 showed remarkable antiviral activity with respective IC50 values of 0.794 nM and 9.17 nM as determined by immunofluorescence staining of SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Fig. S23), NTV was drastically less active against the virus with an IC50 of 1530 nM (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Fig. S23), a reduction in activity by 2-3 orders of magnitude. RT-qPCR was also used to quantify viral nucleic acids in cellular supernatants, yielding IC50 values of < 0.0256 nM, 0.944 nM and 30.2 nM for FD3-32, FD3-33 and NTV, respectively (Fig. 5c), largely consistent with the immunofluorescence staining results.

To understand why FD3-32 and FD3-33 were orders of magnitude more active than NTV in vitro despite a modest improvement in their inhibitory activity against Mpro, we compared FD3-32 and NTV in an in vitro infection assay where a P-gp efflux inhibitor, CP-100356, was used13,51. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S24, while CP-100356 treatment had little effect on the antiviral activity of FD3-32 against SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.5.2 infection on Vero E6 cells, it enhanced far less potent NTV activity by at least 30-fold. These results indicate that the supremacy of FD3-32 and FD3-33 resides largely in their ability to evade P-gp-mediated efflux detrimental to NTV.

To prepare FD3-32 and FD3-33 for in vivo efficacy studies, we initially assessed their acute toxicity in healthy male BALB/c mice by orally administering (p.o.) low (100 mg/kg), medium (300 mg/kg), and high (900 mg/kg) doses of NTV, FD3-32 or FD3-33. All mice challenged with these compounds showed 100% survival with healthy alterations in body weights within 7 days post administration (Supplementary Fig. S25a). None of the three compounds showed any adverse effects on the liver and kidney (Supplementary Fig. S25b, c), indicative of pharmacologically acceptable safety profiles. We then evaluated their single-dose pharmacokinetic profiles in healthy male BALB/c mice through oral gavage (20 mg/kg) or intravenous injection (i.v.) (2 mg/kg). Compared with NTV, FD3-32 exhibited a ~ 2-fold increase in the volume of distribution (Vd) and a ~ 3-fold reduction in clearance (CL), translating into a prolonged i.v. half-life (t1/2) of ~ 1.18 h (as opposed to ~ 0.230 h for NTV) (Supplementary Fig. S26 and Supplementary Table S5). Although FD3-33 retains the iso-quinoline ring at P1 as FD3-32, its difluoro-cyclopropyl (as opposed to trifluoromethyl or difluoro-chloromethyl) moiety at P4 led to a ~ 2-fold and a ~ 5-fold higher clearance than NTV and FD3-32, respectively, ensuing a rapid elimination (i.v. t1/2 = ~ 0.150 h) of FD3-33 with a comparable Vd to either NTV or FD3-32 (Supplementary Fig. S26 and Supplementary Table S5). Notably, despite NTV’s longer p.o. t1/2 than those of FD3-32 and FD3-33, both lead compounds exhibited a substantially higher peak plasma concentration (Cmax) than NTV following oral administration, contributing to ~ 56% and ~ 170% improvement in oral bioavailability over NTV (Supplementary Fig. S26 and Supplementary Table S5).

It is worth noting that the elimination t1/2 is an intrinsic pharmacokinetic parameter of a drug dependent only on CL and Vd and should not be influenced in linear pharmacokinetics by dose and the route of administration. However, the apparent p.o. t1/2 of FD3-32, FD3-33 or NTV in our pharmacokinetic studies was significantly longer than its i.v. t1/2, suggesting several possible scenarios. First, high oral doses (relative to low i.v. doses), which are typical in pharmacokinetic studies, may result in a high concentration in the systemic circulation that leads to elimination saturation. Second, poorly soluble oral drugs are often associated with slow absorption, leading to a prolonged p.o. t1/2 in so-called flip-flop kinetics. Third, if drug concentrations fall below the quantification limit early, as was the case with our tested compounds, inaccuracy in i.v. t1/2 measurements may ensue, thus yielding a falsely short elimination t1/2. Presumably for some of these reasons, the reported ratios of the apparent p.o. t1/2 of NTV and its derivatives to their corresponding i.v. t1/2 swing wildly from as low as 0.5 to as high as 5.513,37. Improved data analysis aided by contemporary pharmacokinetic modeling approaches is clearly warranted to address this limitation in our pharmacokinetic studies to better understand the disparity between i.v. t1/2 and p.o. t1/2 for this class of Mpro inhibitors.

Humanized male C57BL/6S mice were intranasally infected with Omicron BA.5.2 for 2 h, followed by orally administered drug treatment for 3 days with NTV at 200 mg/kg twice daily, and with FD3-32 or FD3-33 at the same dose once daily (Fig. 5d). Of note, the dosage of FD3-32 or FD3-33 was half as much as that of NTV due to our inhibitors’ superior oral bioavailability and in vitro bioactivity. In addition, RTV was orally co-administered at 50 mg/kg to simulate NTV’s clinical application scenarios (Fig. 5d). As shown in Fig. 5e, f, treatment with FD3-32/FD3-33 dramatically decreased viral RNA load in both bronchus and lung tissues as quantified by RT-qPCR, whereas NTV treatment failed to reduce viral burden to a level significantly different from that of the vehicle control group. Interestingly, despite a high level of viral load in the brain of vehicle-treated mice, all three compounds showed excellent protective effects as they largely lowered RNA copies to the limit of detection (Fig. 5g). These results were supported by immunohistochemical (IHC) and immunofluorescent (IF) analyses of SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein in the lung (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Fig. S27a), where both FD3-32 and FD3-33 were more effective than NTV in controlling viral infection (Fig. 5i), coinciding with different degrees of tissue damage in the lung revealed by H&E staining (Fig. 5h). In fact, while histopathological analysis indicated severe organ injury in the vehicle control group as evidenced by large exudation and alveolar membrane thickening, treatment with FD3-32 or FD3-33, and with NTV to a much lesser extent, relieved the pathological alterations (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Fig. S28), despite insignificant differences in lung injury scores between vehicle and NTV-treated mice (Fig. 5j). These findings were further corroborated by semi-quantification of positive areas of SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein in the IF images of the lungs. As shown in Fig. 5i, NTV, despite suppressing viral RNA load without statistical significance (Fig. 5f), was efficacious in significantly reducing the expression of the viral protein in the lung. However, FD3-32 and FD3-33, once again, manifested more potency than NTV in the same in vivo setting.

Encouraged by these results, we then asked the question of whether compound FD3-32, due to its superb antiviral efficacy in vitro and in vivo, could be used in a monotherapy without the aid of RTV. Three groups of humanized male C57BL/6S mice (n = 6) were challenged with Omicron BA.5.2 to establish infection, followed by oral administration, twice daily for 3 days, of saline as a vehicle control, FD3-32 at 200 mg/kg, and NTV at 200 mg/kg plus RTV at 50 mg/kg (Fig. 5k). Quantification of viral RNA by RT-qPCR in the bronchus, lung and brain tissues showed that FD3-32 and NTV + RTV at the same dosage were largely comparable in activity against SARS-CoV-2 infection (Fig. 5l–n). While FD3-32 significantly reduced viral load in the bronchus and brain, but not in the lung, NTV + RTV significantly lowered viral load in the brain and lung, but not in the bronchus. We further analyzed diseased tissues using IF and H&E staining and quantified SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein in the lung. As shown in Fig. 5o, p, FD3-32 caused a pronounced reduction in the expression of SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein in the lung, with comparable efficacy to NTV + RTV (Supplementary Fig. S27b). Interestingly, histopathological analysis showed infection-stemmed tissue damage, characterized by large exudation and alveolar membrane thickening, was less severe in the FD3-32 treatment group than in the NTV + RTV treatment group (Fig. 5o, q and Supplementary Fig. S29, S30). Collectively, these in vitro and in vivo results demonstrate the pharmacological supremacy of FD3-32 over NTV in the treatment of SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Pharmacokinetic profiles and in vitro and in vivo antiviral efficacy of FD3-36

Data on NTV, FD3-32 and FD3-33 suggest that the P4 position exerted a strong pharmacokinetic influence on these Mpro inhibitors (Supplementary Fig. S26 and Supplementary Table S5). In light of this observation, we further interrogated the impact of different haloalkanes at P4 on their pharmacokinetic properties as well as in vitro and in vivo therapeutic efficacy through comparison of FD3-32 (a difluoro-chloromethyl group at P4) and FD3-36 (a difluoro-bromomethyl group at P4) with ITV as a control (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Table S6). As shown in Supplementary Table S5, S6, the substitution of the Br atom for Cl at P4 improved oral pharmacokinetic parameters of FD3-36 over FD3-32, as evidenced by increases of 99% in p.o. t1/2, 101% in total drug exposure (AUC and Cmax) and 99% in oral bioavailability, albeit their comparable CL, Vd and i.v. t1/2 values. A much more striking improvement came from FD3-36 over ITV (Supplementary Table S6), where the former showed a 5-fold longer i.v. t1/2 than the latter (1.09 h as opposed to 0.216 h), but a comparable value in p.o. t1/2 (3.22 h versus 3.19 h). More importantly, the total exposure of FD3-36 in tested mice was higher than that of ITV, as indicated by 6-fold and 50-fold differences in AUC for i.v. and p.o. administration, respectively (Supplementary Table S6). In addition, a higher Cmax of orally administered FD3-36 versus ITV represented an 8-fold improvement in oral bioavailability (Supplementary Table S6).

To ascertain the translation of superior pharmacokinetic properties into potent therapeutic efficacy, we subjected FD3-32, FD3-36, Paxlovid and ITV to various in vitro and/or in vivo antiviral activity assays. Both FD3-36 and FD3-32 were at least three orders of magnitude more potent than ITV against Omicron BA.5.2 infection in vitro on Vero E6 cells, as evidenced by the IC50 values of 1235 nM, 0.565 nM and 0.177 nM for ITV, FD3-32 and FD3-36, respectively (Fig. 6b, c and Supplementary Fig. S31). We further compared FD3-36 as a monotherapy with the combination of NTV and RTV with respect to their in vivo antiviral efficacy, where humanized male C57BL/6S mice were initially challenged with Omicron BA.5.2 to establish infection, followed by oral administration, twice daily for 3 days, of saline as a vehicle control, FD3-36 alone at 200 mg/kg, and NTV at 200 mg/kg plus RTV at 50 mg/kg (Fig. 6d). Treatment with FD3-36 more effectively lowered viral load in the bronchus and lung, but not in the brain, than with NTV + RTV, as quantified by RT-qPCR (Fig. 6e–g). Further analysis of the lung using IF and H&E staining revealed a remarkable protective effect of FD3-36, as compared with NTV + RTV (P = 0.0295) (Fig. 6i and Supplementary Fig. S32), on infection-stemmed inflammatory tissue damage, coinciding with its potent inhibition of the expression SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein (Fig. 6h, i). Consistent with the previous results (Fig. 5), large exudation and alveolar membrane thickening were observed in the vehicle control group, which was not significantly alleviated by treatment with NTV + RTV (Fig. 6h, j and Supplementary Fig. S33, S34), contrasting the powerful protective effect of FD3-36. These in vitro and in vivo results collectively demonstrate the pharmacological supremacy of FD3-36 over Paxlovid (and even FD3-32) in the treatment of SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Broad-spectrum inhibition of Mpro of SARS-CoV-1 and MERS-CoV. The canonical P1-S1 interaction involving a P1 Gln residue is evolutionarily conserved across different species of coronaviruses52, suggesting our inhibitors may be of broad-spectrum against Mpro’s of different viral origin. SARS-CoV-1 and SARS-CoV-2 Mpro share a 96% sequence identity and are only ~ 50% identical to MERS-CoV Mpro52. We thus expressed and purified recombinant Mpro of SARS-CoV-1 and MERS-CoV and quantified their inhibition by the clinical candidates NTV, ETV and ITV and our selected leads FD3-6, FD3-32, FD3-33, FD3-34 and FD3-36 (Supplementary Fig. S35 and Fig. 6k). While the inhibitory activity of the three clinical candidates significantly weakened from low nanomolar Ki values for SARS-CoV-2 Mpro to approximately 30 ~ 40 nM for SARS-CoV-1 Mpro, FD3-6, FD3-32 and FD3-36 still maintained their potent, single-digit nanomolar inhibition of both main proteases (Ki, 1.5-3.5 nM), with FD3-33 and FD3-34 performing somewhere in the middle pack (Ki, ~ 12 nM) (Fig. 6l and Supplementary Table S7). FD3-6 and FD3-34 stood out to be the most potent inhibitors of MERS-CoV Mpro, with Ki values at ~8 nM, whereas ETV dropped two orders of magnitude in Ki from its inhibition of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro to of MERS-CoV Mpro (Fig. 6l and Supplementary Table S7). Otherwise, FD3-32, FD3-33 and FD3-36 showed modestly decreased but comparable inhibitory activities with NTV and ITV, with their Ki values clustering between 15 to 23 nM (Fig. 6l and Supplementary Table S7). These results largely validate our iso-quinoline-derived peptidomimetic inhibitors as potential broad-spectrum antivirals against sarbecoviruses and merbecoviruses.

Discussion

The P1-S1 interaction dictates the recognition of proteases by their substrates and substrate-inspired canonical inhibitors. For this reason, when designing inhibitors of a protease for therapeutic use, scientists have always tempted, conveniently, to graft the P1 residue (or derivatives) from its natural substrate(s) to an inhibitor scaffold for the purpose of maintaining “optimal” inhibition. In the case of most clinically used covalent inhibitors designed against SARS-CoV-2 Mpro, including NTV13, Simnotrelvir53, Leritrelvir (RAY1216)37, and Ibuzatrelvir36, the P1 residue is invariably γ-lactam. This traditional design likely harbors their susceptibility to E166X mutations-conferred drug resistance, and may contribute to their limited antiviral efficacy as well, due to hepatic metabolism and drug efflux instigated by suboptimal P1 (and P4) moieties.

Drug resistant variants of SARS-CoV-2 have emerged both clinically and under selective pressure in the laboratory54–57, albeit their relatively low prevalence according to the Global Initiative on Sharing Avian Influenza Data (GISAID) database26,54,58. These variants exhibit different levels of resistance, through naturally occurring mutations such as E166X and M49X, to Mpro inhibitors in clinical use (e.g., NTV and ETV)23,24,26,58. Structurally, E166 is required for Mpro to recognize canonical P1 groups such as γ-lactam and, consequently, its mutation significantly attenuates NTV binding. E166 mutations also impair the catalytic activity of Mpro, necessitating compensatory mutations such as L50F and L167F to restore the replicative fitness of the virus19.

In principle, one rational approach to mitigating NTV resistance conferred by E166X mutations in SARS-CoV-2 Mpro is to replace the canonical γ-lactam at P1 of NTV with a non-canonical moiety that is independent of interactions with E166 and yet sacrifices little or no binding energy. We have achieved this simple objective in this study by identifying iso-quinoline as a replacement for γ-lactam. Iso-quinoline at P1 of FD3-32 and FD3-36 structurally occupies the S1 binding pocket of Mpro similarly to γ-lactam of NTV, differing mainly in the H-bonding network involving E166 – the very residue conferring NTV resistance when mutated. Iso-quinoline forms extensive π stacking interactions with S1-lining residues to compensate for lost H-bonds, gaining, instead, not only stronger inhibition of Mpro but, more importantly, substantially enhanced inhibitory activity against NTV-resistant variants of Mpro harboring E166X mutations.

Of note, iso-quinoline at P1 did not fully restore the Ki values of our leads for L50F/E166A/L50F and L50F/E166V mutants to their inhibitory levels for WT Mpro. This is not totally surprising as iso-quinoline resulted from virtual screening against WT Mpro, not its NTV-resistant mutants. Subtle structural differences in the substrate-binding pocket necessarily exist, beyond the P1-S1 pair, between WT Mpro and L50F/E166A/L50F-Mpro or L50F/E166V-Mpro, likely explaining the reduced inhibitory activities of our leads against these two mutant enzymes. In fact, L50F/E166V-Mpro was substantially more resistant than L50F/E166A/L50F-Mpro to inhibition by NTV and our inhibitors, largely because of a steric clash between the tBu group at P3 and the relatively bulky side chain of Val166 (as opposed to a smaller Ala166)27. However, this steric clash is absent in ETV, where a favorable van der Waals contact was instead gained by the P1 and Val16627, attributing to significantly less resistance of L50F/E166V-Mpro than L50F/E166A/L50F-Mpro to ETV inhibition.

While it is by design that potent inhibitory activity against Mpro and its NTV-resistant E166A/V mutants is achieved for FD3-32 and FD3-36 as an impetus of this study, their improved oral bioavailability and metabolic stability have been attained with a certain degree of serendipity. Through the optimization of P1 and P4, FD3-32 and FD3-36 effectuated superior pharmacokinetic profiles and were far more active both in vitro and in vivo than NTV against Omicron BA.5.2 infection. FD3-32 and FD3-36, each in a monotherapy, more effectively controlled viral infection and disease manifestation in mice than NTV co-administered with RTV, raising hopes that treatment with FD3-32 or FD3-36 alone could alleviate significant clinical burden associated with the use of RTV.

In the absence of RTV, NTV exhibited limited in vitro activity against Omicron BA.5.2 infection with a low micromolar IC50 despite low nanomolar inhibitory activity (Ki) against Mpro, likely attributable to highly expressed P-gp on Vero cells51,59. In sharp contrast, our leads, FD3-32, FD3-33 and FD3-36, were 2 ~ 3 orders of magnitude more active than NTV in vitro despite a merely 1.5 ~ 10-fold improvement in their inhibitory activity against Mpro, suggesting that these three inhibitors with iso-quinoline at P1 may have markedly retarded P-gp-mediated drug efflux detrimental to NTV activity. Indeed, a P-gp efflux inhibitor did significantly improve NTV antiviral activity in vitro while exerting no impact on FD3-32, underscoring the important role of the P-gp efflux pump in dictating the therapeutic efficacy of these inhibitors by differentially regulating their cellular transport. Since inhibition of P-gp usually improves absorption and bioavailability of orally administered drug molecules60, retarded efflux likely explains enhanced oral bioavailability, in vivo drug expose and antiviral activity of the three leads seen in this study. It is also plausible that the substitution of iso-quinoline for γ-lactam at P1, as well as the difluoro-chloromethyl or difluoro-bromomethyl group at P4, may have partially reduced the susceptibility of FD3-32 and FD3-36 to CYP3A4-mediated degradation in the liver, as evidenced by an extended i.v. t1/2, thus further contributing to their antiviral activity.

Even though FD3-36 in our self-contained study exhibited a superior pharmacokinetic profile and a potent antiviral activity, where the second-generation drug candidate ITV from Pfizer was rapidly eliminated in healthy BALB/c mice and was four orders of magnitude weaker in vitro than FD3-36, we did not directly compare their in vivo antiviral efficacy. Nor could we compare objectively the parameters of FD3-36 with those of ITV published in the literature as the experiments were performed under different conditions pertaining to crystalline form, dosage, solvent and animal species, etc36. Of note, the two clinical candidates, ITV and RAY1216, were evaluated for their antiviral efficacy in the presence of a P-gp inhibitor36,37, further attesting to the existing challenge posed by P-gp-mediated drug efflux to the development of efficacious mono-therapeutics against SARS-CoV-2 infection.

In conclusion, we leveraged in silico screening for non-covalent inhibitors of Mpro coupled with extensive MD simulation, SAR and structural studies, cultivating the development of peptidomimetic covalent inhibitors of Mpro and its NTV-resistant E166X mutants with superior pharmacological properties. Our inhibitors structurally bypass the obligating H-bond of NTV formed with E166, showcasing their powerful potential as next-generation peptidomimetic covalent inhibitors of Mpro for the treatment of NTV-resistant SARS-CoV-2 variants conferred by E166X mutations. Moreover, these inhibitors function independently of the use of RTV and are, thus, of critical importance in a monotherapy for the treatment of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Being potently active against Mpro of SARS-CoV-1 and MERS-CoV, they may be developed clinically as broad-spectrum antivirals for the treatment of infection by sarbecoviruses and merbecoviruses of pandemic potential. Future studies on the therapeutic efficacy of FD3-32 and FD3-36 against infection by NTV-resistant clinical strains of SARS-CoV-2 and other emerging variants of interest or concern of coronaviruses are warranted.

Methods

Ethics statement

Animal studies were conducted under the authorization of the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of the School of Basic Medical Sciences at Fudan University (approved number: 20210315-001). All animal experimental procedures strictly adhered to the International Ethical Guidelines and the National Institutes of Health Guide concerning the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Cell lines and virus

Human lung epithelial cell A549, mouse macrophage J774A.1, transformed human liver epithelial-2 (THLE-2), and the African green monkey kidney epithelial cell Vero E6 cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) containing 10% FBS and 1% Pen/Strep, and incubated at 37 °C under 5% (v/v) CO2 atmosphere. SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.5.2 variant came from and was conducted in a Biosafety Level 3 (BSL3) Laboratory at Shanghai Medical College, Fudan University.

Enzymatic assays

A fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET)-based assay was adopted to quantitatively determine the inhibitory activity of compounds against Mpro and its mutants, with an internally quenched fluorogenic peptidyl substrate Dabcyl-KTSAVLQSGFRKME-Edans (Beyotime) using wavelengths of 340 nm and 490 nm for excitation and emission, respectively19,61. To determine enzymatic parameters, Mpro were initially diluted in assay buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4) to specified concentrations. Specifically, the working concentrations were 100 nM for wild-type (WT) SARS-CoV-2 Mpro and its 15N-labeled protein, as well as SARS-CoV-1 Mpro, 400 nM for MERS-CoV Mpro, 500 nM for three mutants including M49L/E166A, L50F/E166A/L167F and L50F/E166V, and 1600 nM for the H163A mutant of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro. Different concentrations of fluorescent substrate were added into the diluted Mpro. Fluorescence intensities were kinetically monitored by a Spark® multifunctional plate reader (Tecan). Initial velocities were referred as the slopes calculated from fitting the linear portion of the curves to a straight line. A standard curve was generated using Edans, then the time-dependent changes in fluorescence signals of each sample were converted to the catalytic velocity using the unit of nM/s. Parameters including Km, kcat and Vmax were calculated using nonlinear regression fitting by GraphPad Prism 8.0 according to the Michaelis-Menten equation.

For determining inhibition constants (Ki), Mpro were diluted in assay buffer to working concentrations (100 nM for WT Mpro of SARS-CoV-1 and SARS-CoV-2, 400 nM for MERS-CoV Mpro, L50F/E166A/L167F and L50F/E166V mutants, and 200 nM for M49L/E166A mutant, as well as 1600 nM for H163A mutant). Different concentrations of compounds were then added into the diluted Mpro, and incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. Fluorescence intensities were kinetically monitored by a Spark® multifunctional plate reader (Tecan) after addition of fluorescent substrate (10 μM). The initial velocities were referred to as the slopes calculated from fitting the linear portion of the curves to a straight line. Enzymatic activities were characterized by percent velocities in the presence of different concentrations of compounds. Ki values were obtained via fitting to the Morrison equation by GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Cytotoxicity assays

Cell lines including A549, J774A.1, THLE-2 and Vero E6 were adopted to evaluate in vitro cytotoxic profiles of tested compounds. Specifically, these cells were plated in 96-well plates with a cell density of 20,000 cells per well, respectively, and incubated at 37 °C overnight. Tested compounds with concentrations ranging from 0.195 to 50 μM were added into the cell medium, and the untreated cells cultured in fresh medium were set as control groups. After 24 h incubation, 10 μL of WST-1 (Beyotime) was added into each well and incubated for 60 min at 37 °C. The optical density for each well was determined by a microplate reader at 450 nm (OD450). The assay was biologically repeated for three times. Percent survival (%) of cells was calculated relative to control groups.

In vivo acute toxicity assays

Healthy 8- to 12-week-old male BALB/c mice (17 ~ 22 g) were orally administered with test compounds (NTV, FD3-32, and FD3-33) in a low (100 mg/kg), medium (300 mg/kg), or high (900 mg/kg) dosage, respectively. Mice were clinically observed for 7 days after administration, and the body weights were recorded every day. At the end of the experiment, blood samples collected from three mice per group were subjected for detection of alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), creatinine (CREA), and urea nitrogen (BUN).

Pharmacokinetic analysis

NTV, FD3-32, FD3-33, ITV and FD3-36 were subjected to pharmacokinetic analysis via intravenous injection (i.v.) or oral administration (p.o.), respectively. 8- to 12-week-old male BALB/c mice were administered with a single dose of 20 mg/kg (i.v.) and 2 mg/kg (p.o.) compounds in saline containing 5% DMSO, 20% HP-β-CD, and 15% HS-15. Blood samples were collected at specified time points (0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 24 h for i.v for all tested compounds.; 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 24 h for p.o. for NTV, FD3-32 and FD3-33; 0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 24 h for p.o. for ITV and FD3-36) pose-dose, and subsequently centrifuged at 3000 × g at 4 °C for 10 min. Blood samples retrieved from healthy mice were diluted in saline to prepare standard curves by adding specified concentrations (5, 10, 20, 50, 200, 1000, 2000, 5000, 10000 ng/mL) of tested compounds. Supernatants of blood samples were analyzed by LC-MS/MS to determine plasma drug concentrations. A Waters Acquity Premier UPLC-Xevo TQ Absolute equipped with an ACQUITY Premier BEH C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm) were employed for separation and quantification of tested compounds with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min with mobile phase A (0.2% formic acid in ddH2O) and mobile phase B (acetonitrile). Gradient: 0-0.5 min, 10% solvent B; 0.5–1.2 min, 10–90% solvent B; 1.2–1.7 min, 90% solvent B; 1.7-1.8 min, 90-10% solvent B; 1.8–2.5 min, 10% solvent B. The average plasma concentration and standard deviation at each time point were calculated, and PKSolver was employed to fit the pharmacokinetic parameters using a non-compartmental model62.

Cellular antiviral assays

Vero E6 cells were plated in 96-well plates with a cell density of 20,000 cells per well, and cultured overnight at 37 °C in DMEM medium plus 10% FBS and 1% Pen/Strep. SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.5.2 variant (100 TCID50) was then added in the presence or absence of NTV, FD3-32, FD3-33, ITV, or FD3-36 at specified concentrations in DMEM medium plus 2% FBS and 1% Pen/Strep, and the resultant cell plates were placed at 37 °C and cultured for 48 h. For characterizing the sensitivity of tested inhibitors to P-gp-mediated efflux, cellular antiviral assays were additionally conducted in the presence and absence of 2 μM CP-100356 (a standard P-gp inhibitor)13,51. Supernatants were collected from Vero E6 cell culture for RT-qPCR analysis using specific primers of SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein genes. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.2 % Triton X-100 for 30 min, which were then stained with rabbit anti-SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein antibody (Sino Biological) and Rhodamine-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Proteintech). After immunofluorescence staining, expression and distribution of nucleocapsid protein in Vero E6 cells were imaged by BZ-X810 Fluorescence Microscope (KEYENCE). Semi-quantification of immunofluorescence images was performed by Image J.

In vivo antiviral assays

All animal experiments involving SARS-CoV-2 infection were performed within a certified animal biosafety level 3 (BSL3) laboratory at Shanghai Medical College, Fudan University. All animal studies were conducted under the authorization of the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of the School of Basic Medical Sciences at Fudan University (approved number: 20210315-001). Ten to fourteen-week-old male humanized C57BL6/S mice (CAG-hACE2-IRES-Luc-Tg) were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms, and then housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility of the BSL3 laboratory in Fudan University in a 12 h light-dark cycle, under the condition with ad libitum access to irradiation-sterilized rodent diet and aseptic water. Mice were divided into four groups (six mice per group) with initial anesthetization by 20 μL/g avertin (Nanjing Aibei Biotechnology), and subsequently challenged intranasally with ~ 5000 PFUs of SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.5.2 in a total volume of 40 μL DMEM. An additional three mice without infection were defined as a mock group. Therapeutic candidates (NTV, FD3-32, and FD3-33) plus RTV were formulated by dissolving in 0.5% methylcellulose solution containing 2% Tween-80 to satisfy the requirements of oral administration. After a 2 h infection, mice were orally administrated with 200 mg/kg NTV plus 50 mg/kg RTV twice a day (b.i.d.), or 200 mg/kg FD3-32/FD3-33 plus 50 mg/kg RTV once a day (q.d.). All mice were sacrificed after three-day treatments; lung and brain were collected for RT-qPCR assay to quantify loads of viral RNA; owing to time limit of BSL3, only three brain tissues from the NTV group had been collected for quantifying viral RNA. Besides, parts of lung tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and further submitted to immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analysis for visualize/quantify the distribution and expression of SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein. H&E staining was conducted to show the histopathological alterations of lung tissues.

To demonstrate the therapeutic efficacy of FD3-32 as a monotherapy, we additionally performed an in vivo antiviral assay. Ten to fourteen-week-old male humanized C57BL6/S mice (CAG-hACE2-IRES-Luc-Tg) were divided into three groups (six mice per group) with initial anesthetization by 20 μL/g avertin (Nanjing Aibei Biotechnology), and subsequently challenged intranasally with ~ 5000 PFUs of SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.5.2 in a total volume of 40 μL DMEM. Therapeutic candidates, including FD3-32 alone or NTV plus RTV, were also formulated by dissolving in 0.5% methylcellulose solution containing 2% Tween-80. After a 2 h infection, infected mice were orally administrated with 200 mg/kg FD3-32 alone, or 200 mg/kg NTV plus 50 mg/kg RTV, or vehicle with equal volume, twice a day (b.i.d.). All mice were sacrificed after three-day treatments; bronchus, lung and brain were collected for RT-qPCR assay to quantify loads of viral RNA. Besides, tissues of the lung were additionally fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and were further submitted to immunohistochemical and immunofluorescent analysis of SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein. H&E analysis was also performed to present the histopathological alterations of lung tissues from our tested mice. The same protocols were employed for determining the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of FD3-36 monotherapy against Omicron BA.5.2 infection, in comparison to NTV co-administrating RTV. Due to the unexpected death of two mice before infecting the virus, the data from 5 mice in the FD3-36 and NTV + RTV groups were ultimately collected. Histopathological lung injury was assessed and scored on a 5-point scale based on the exudation and alveolar membrane thickening: 0, minimal damage; 1, mild damage; 2, moderate damage; 3, severe damage; and 4, maximal damage63.

Computational methods

Molecular docking

Virtual screening

A crystal structure of SARS-CoV-2 Mpro in complex with a non-covalent inhibitor X77 (PDB ID: 6W63) was employed for all docking calculations in this study. The crystal structure was then prepared via protonation at pH 7.0 by Epik and PROPKA in Maestro. According to a previous publication that used the same crystal structure (PDB: 6W63) for virtual screening61, histidine residues including His41, His163, His164, His172, and His246 were modeled as Nε-protonated, whereas His64 and His80 were modeled as Nδ-protonated. Crystallographic waters within 3.0 Å from the ligand were retained otherwise, removed from the structure. And then the structure was submitted to energy-minimization optimization with the OPLS4 force field. Receptor Grid files were generated by applying a cube box with a center of inherent ligand and a size of 20 Å × 20 Å × 20 Å, by using Receptor Grid Generation. Approximately 3 million molecules from the Enamine library (comprising the Advanced, Functional, HTS, and Premium collections, downloaded in December 2021) were initially filtered using Lipinski’s rule of five. A high throughput docking campaign was conducted using Schrödinger2021-2 by module of Virtual Screening Workflow on AMD CPU with 128 cores, 256 threads, which used CPU core time of ~ 120 h. The van der Waals scaling was set to 0.8 for the nonpolar atoms of the ligand, and the partial charge cutoff was set to 0.15. Three-level docking algorithms with differential accuracy, including HTVS, SP and XP, were applied to perform docking calculations, with 10% compounds reserved after each step’s computation. The final output of hit compounds after multiple docking was re-scored by Prime MM-GBSA to calculate binding free energy.

Induced fit docking

Predicted docking poses of our synthetic inhibitors in complex with Mpro were generated by the Schrödinger software package. The receptor was prepared using conditions consistent with those of virtual screening. The LigPrep module was employed to calculate the molecular mechanic parameters of our compounds with the OPLS4 force field, and generate states at pH 7.0 by Epik. Non-covalent docking was performed by using the Induced Fit Docking module. Receptor Grid files were generated by applying a cube box with a center of inherent ligand and a size of 20 Å × 20 Å × 20 Å. The van der Waals scaling was set to 0.5 for the nonpolar atoms of the receptor and ligand, and the maximum number of poses was set to 20. Multi-thread parallel calculation was employed for each individual compound by assigning 20 AMD CPU cores.

Covalent docking

Complexes of our peptidomimetic covalent inhibitors with Mpro were predicted by using the Covalent Docking module of the Schrödinger software package. Preparation of receptor and ligands was conducted as previously described. Mass of the center of the inherent ligand was adopted as the centroid of a cube box with a size of 20Å × 20Å × 20Å. Reaction type between the -SH of C145 and -CN of our compounds was set to nucleophilic addition to a triple bond. ~20 AMD CPU cores were assigned to perform the covalent docking per compounds.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

Crystal structures of Mpro in complex with NTV (PDB ID: 7RFS) and peptidyl substrate (PDB ID: 7DVP)8,13, the co-crystal structure of FD6-31/Mpro complex and those docking complexes of our synthetic compounds binding to Mpro were initially imported to PyMOL for removing hydrogens. Chemical structures of ligands were created by Chem3D, were then optimized, and the partial atomic charges were calculated by AM1-BCC method via Antechamber module in Amber20 to obtain the molecular mechanics parameters of small molecules64. For complexes of covalent inhibitors, parameters of the non-canonical bond formed in the corresponding reactive site should be manually added to the force field files. The molecular mechanic parameters from ff19SB and GAFF2 force field were then assigned to these complexes, which were subsequently neutralized by adding potassium/chlorine counter ions and solvated in a cuboid box of transferable interatomic potential with three points model (TIP3P) water molecules with solvent layers 12.0 Å between solute surface and box edges, via LEaP module65,66. In parallel with the volume of the box, specific counts of potassium/chlorine ions were added to achieve the physiological concentration (150 mM) of KCl. Overall, system setup parameters for all-atom MD simulations involved in this study were provided in Supplementary Table S8. All MD simulations were conducted by using Amber20. The SHAKE algorithm was employed to restrict all covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms. The pmemd.MPI module was used to perform the minimization, heat, density and equilibration of these solvated complexes through parallel computation with assignation of 64 AMD CPU cores. The solvent and ions were optimized using 10000 steps of steepest descent minimization and 10000 steps of conjugated gradient minimization with protein backbone restrained by 25 kcal mol−1Å−2. The systems were further undergone three steps of energy minimizations by 5000-step steepest descent and 5000-step conjugated gradient minimizations with restrain of backbone Cα atoms by 25, 10, and 5 kcal mol−1Å−2, respectively. Last, the systems were fully relaxed by 10000-step of steepest descent and 10000-step conjugated gradient minimizations without any restraint, thus to remove unfavorable contacts. After minimizations, the systems were heated from 0 K to 300 K in 160-ps timescale and maintained 300 K for 40 ps, by using Langevin dynamics at a constant volume, with a time step of 2 fs. A 200-ps density equilibration was then performed to optimize the heated system, which was subsequently undergone two steps of NPT (T = 300 K, P = 1 atm) equilibration with or without backbone restrain for total 200 ps. Finally, these systems were respectively submitted to a 100-ns NPT (T = 300 K, P = 1 atm) production MD simulation with isotropic pressure scaling by using the pmemd.cuda module. Production simulations for each setting were run triplicate. MD trajectories were analyzed by employing CPPTRAJ to extract the RMSD and monitor hydrogen bonds. Conformations at specific frames were exported by using CPPTRAJ and visualized by PyMOL. MMPBSA.py.MPI module was used to calculate the gibbs free energy change (ΔG) between ligands and Mpro according to the MM/GBSA approach, and the ΔG was further decomposed into the contributions of each residue in Mpro67. Besides, MMPBSA.py.MPI was also used to run alanine scanning to calculate the ΔΔG after the single mutation of residues in Mpro. All production simulations in this study were run for three times to obtain three independent trajectories. All quantitative data in MD simulations were analyzed and visualized by GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Cheminformatics

Enamine library computationally screened against Mpro finally identified a molecular set containing 10847 candidates. To further elucidate the chemical features of these compounds to bind the receptor, chemical spaces were explored via an extensive effort of RDKit. The molecular set in SDF file was initially imported and adjusted the order of individual compound randomly. Morgan fingerprints were then computed in a radius of 2, and the number of bits was set to 204868. Tanimoto similarity was calculated using Morgan fingerprints as input; and Tanimoto distance represented as 1-Tanimoto similarity, was used to construct a distance matrix that subsequently transferred to numpy array68. Compounds were thus clustered to ten groups based on the array of Tanimoto distances by adopting Ward method, and were further visualized as a two-dimensional (2D) chemical space by principal components analysis (PCA). The compounds with the best MM/GBSA binding free energy among each group were automatically picked out. RDKit was also employed to search the compounds with substructures of specified aromatic nitrogen heterocycles through matching the simplified molecular-input line-entry system (SMILES) string. 2D chemical spaces were rendered by Seaborn to present the density of molecular distributions, in which the compounds with specified aromatic nitrogen heterocycles were scattered. Potential candidates in scattered compounds exhibited favorable fitting to Mpro were picked out in terms of the interactions with H163, H41, and G143.

Expression and purification of SARS-CoV-1, SARS-CoV-2, and MERS-CoV Mpro and SARS-CoV-2 Mpro E166X mutants

The DNA sequences encoding Mpro of SARS-CoV (nsp5, NCBI RefSeq NC_004718.3), MERS-CoV (nsp5, NCBI RefSeq NC_019843.3), and SARS-CoV-2 (GenBank: MN908947.3) was synthesized with codon optimization for expression in E. coli by TSINGKE, and constructed into the vector of pET28a-SUMO by homologous recombination. The M49L/E166A mutation plasmid was constructed based on the above plasmid of wild-type Mpro as template, with the mutation primers as following:

F-M49L: AGATCTGCTGAATCCGAATTATGAAG;

R-M49L: CAGCAGATCTTCACTCGTGC;

F-E166A: CACATGGCACTGCCGACCG;

R-E166A: CAGTGCCATGTGATGCATATAACAAAAGCTC.

Expression vectors of SARS-CoV-2 mutants, including L50F/E166V and L50F/E166Q/L167F were synthesized by DETAIBIO and then constructed into the vector pET30a by homologous recombination. Abovementioned vectors were respectively transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3). A colony of the bacteria was inoculated into 10 mL of LB broth containing 100 μg/mL kanamycin, and cultured overnight at 200 rpm, 37 °C. The overnight inoculum was sub-cultured with a ratio of 1:100 for ~ 150 min until OD600 ≈ 0.6–0.8, then 1 mM IPTG was added for induction of protein expression and incubated overnight at 180 rpm and 16 °C. The bacteria were resuspended in the binding buffer (Tris/HCl pH 8.4 + 20 mM Imidazole) equivalent to one-thirtieth of the volume of the raw induced bacterial suspension, and then lysed by ultra-high pressure microjet homogenizer at ~ 1050 bar and 6 °C for 15 min. After centrifuging the bacterial lysate at 12000 × g for 20 min, the fusion protein existed in the supernatant of the bacterial lysate. The fusion protein was purified by a Ni-NTA column (His-Trap FF5ml) with a protein purifier (BIO-RAD NGC Quest 10plus). Before purification, 10 CVs (column volumes) of the binding buffer were used to equilibrate the column at 5 mL/min, and then 15 mL of the sample was loaded at 3 mL/min. After that, 15 CVs of binding buffer were used to wash the non-specific miscellaneous proteins at 5 mL/min. Finally, the protein was eluted by the elution buffer (Tris/HCl pH 8.4 + 500 mM Imidazole) at a 0–60% gradience for 13 CVs. The final products of mutants L50F/E166V and L50F/E166A/L167F were obtained after eluting of the Ni-NTA column. To remove the His6-Sumo tag at the N-terminus of Mpro and Mpro M49L/E166A mutant, the fusion protein was cut by the Sumo Protease (Smart-Lifesciences, China) as instructed by the manufacturer overnight at 4 °C. And the resultant samples were dialyzed overnight at 4 °C against 4 L of fresh binding buffer, the His6-SUMO tag and SUMO protease were both removed when the sample was passed through the Ni-NTA column (His-Trap FF5ml). These final products of proteins were verified by LC-MS, and were concentrated up to about 8 mg/mL, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at − 80 °C until used.

15N-labeled SARS-CoV-2 Mpro expression and purification

A colony of the E. coli BL21(DE3) (pET28a-SUMO-Mpro) was inoculated into 10 ml M9 broth containing 100 μg/mL kanamycin, using 15N-NH4Cl (1 g/L) as nitrogen source, and cultured at 200 rpm, 37 °C overnight. The expression was induced at 180 rpm 37 °C for 3 h and then 16 °C overnight. After centrifuging the bacterial lysate at 12000 × g for 20 min, the fusion protein existed in the supernatant of the bacterial lysate. Before purification, 10 CVs (column volumes) of the binding buffer were used to equilibrate the column at 5 mL/min, and then 15 mL of the sample was loaded at 3 mL/min. After that, 15 CVs of binding buffer were used to wash the non-specific miscellaneous proteins at 5 mL/min. Finally, the protein was eluted by the elution buffer (Tris/HCl pH 8.4 + 500 mM Imidazole) at a 0–60% gradience for 13 CVs. Then they were added into the dialysis bag, and by dialyzing the sample overnight at 4 °C against 3 L of fresh binding buffer with 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, The His6-SUMO tag and SUMO protease were both removed when the sample was passed through a HisTrap FF5ml column (Cytiva). The flow-through containing the native Mpro was dialyzed (cut-off 6–8 kDa) twice at 4 °C against 4 L of NMR buffer (100 mM NaPi, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 2 mM DTT, pH 6.8). The protein was verified by LC-MS, the products were concentrated up to about 8 mg/mL, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at − 80 °C until used.

Electrospray ionization time-of-flight (ESI-TOF) mass spectrometer assay

To validate the covalent-inhibition mechanism, purified wild-type (WT) Mpro (15 μM) were mixed with FD3-6, FD3-9, FD3-32 and FD3-33, respectively, at a molar ratio of 4:1 (Mpro:inhibitor), and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Besides, for characterizing dose-response relationships, purified WT Mpro, M49L/E166A mutant, L50F/E166V mutant and L50F/E166A/L167F mutant (15 μM) were incubated with NTV or FD3-33 at molar ratios ranging from 1:5 to 1:0.039 (Mpro:inhibitor), respectively, and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. All samples were prepared with a single biological replicate and were then injected into LC-MS system (Agilent HPLC Infinity II 1290 system coupled with a TOF 6230 detector) by using XBridge® Protein BEH C4 column (2.1 mm × 150 mm, 3.5 μm) for separation with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min with solvent A (0.1% (v/v) TFA in Milli-Q water) and solvent B (0.1% (v/v) TFA in acetonitrile). The initial gradient of 5% solvent B was maintained for 2 min, followed by a gradual increase to 95% for 15 min and maintained for 2 min, and finally back to 5% solvent that maintained for 2 min. Samples went through column were automatically detected by ESI-TOF real time to reflect the relative abundances of Mpro as well as the covalent complexes of Mpro with inhibitors. The exact mass was calculated by deconvoluting molecular ion peaks in ESI-TOF spectra. MS parameters: Gas Temp, 300 °C; Drying Gas, 5 L/min; Nebulizer, 40 psi; Sheath Gas Temp, 325 °C; Sheath Gas Flow, 11 L/min; VCap, 3500 V.

Crystallization, data collection, structure determination, and refinement

SARS-CoV-2 Mpro protein (16 mg/mL) was preincubated with 100 mM FD6-31 (in 100% DMSO) for a molar ratio of approximately 1:3, for 12-18 h at 4 °C. The complex was then passed through a 0.22 μM cellulose-acetate spin filter and set up for crystallization using a crystallization robot (Art Robbins Instruments). Each drop consisted of 0.6 μL of protein solution and 0.6 μL of reservoir solution (0.1 M Bis-Tris 6.5, 20% PEG5000MME) and was equilibrated against 80 μL of reservoir solution at 16 °C for 5–7 days to allow crystals to grow. Large crystals were flash cooled in liquid nitrogen after being passed through a cryoprotectant consisting of a reservoir solution containing 20% ethylene glycol. The diffraction data were collected using a single crystal at 100 K with a wavelength of 0.979 Å at beamline BL10U2 of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) and were integrated and scaled using HKL2000. The structure was solved by molecular replacement using the Mpro complex structure (PDB code: 7VIC) as the search model in the program Molrep. Iterative refinements of the structure model were carried out using Coot and Refmac5. The stereochemical quality of the final model was assessed using MolProbity. The statistics of the data collection and structural refinement are listed in Supplementary Table S2. The coordinate and structural factors of the Mpro-6-31 complex have been deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) under the accession code 9L13.

Compounds were titrated from a stock solution to protein at a molar ratio of 2:1. All samples were prepared with a single biological replicate. Differential scanning fluorimetry

Thermal-shift profiles of WT Mpro and mutants in the presence and absence of inhibitors were determined by employing a fluorescence-based thermal shift assay as previously reported48–50. Purified WT Mpro and resistant mutants, including M49L/E166A, L50F/E166V, and L50F/E166A/L167F, were diluted in buffer containing 40 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.4) and 150 mM NaCl to a final concentration of 10 μM. For characterizing the influence of inhibitors to thermal profiles, NTV and FD3-33 were added to each Mpro in a final concentration of 10 μM, respectively, and incubated at 25 °C for 10 min. After addition of SYPRO Orange dye (Sigma-Aldrich, final concentration = 20 ×), the melting curves and melting temperatures (Tm) were thus determined by detecting fluorescent intensity (RFU) of SYRPRO Orange at 490/530 nm excitation/emission wavelengths with heating from 25 °C to 95 °C with an increment of 0.5 °C (Bio-Rad CFX Connect Real-Time PCR System, FRET mode). The -d(RFU)/dT was plotted against temperature (T), and the minimal signal of the peak was used to define Tm.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Data are presented as mean ± SD or mean ± SEM as specified. The statistical comparisons between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed by two-sided one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA. Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05. The Ki values were calculated by fitting to the Morrison equation, while the IC50 values were obtained by fitting to the four-parameter dose-response model in GraphPad Prism 8.0.
