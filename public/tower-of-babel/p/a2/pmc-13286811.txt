Oxidation Products of S‐Adenosyl Methionine Probed by Infrared Multiple Photon Dissociation Spectroscopy
Authors: Jean‐Xavier Bardaud, Yining Jiang, Carine Clavaguera, Chantal Houée‐Levin, Giel Berden, Jos Oomens, Debora Scuderi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractS‐adenosyl methionine (SAM) is a key biomolecule in cellular processes, acting as a primary methyl donor, having different roles in enzymatic reactions and being used as a scavenger of hydroxyl radicals. Due to the role played by SAM and its antioxidant properties, the one electron oxidation products of SAM produced by gamma radiolysis, which can mimic the oxidation effect produced by hydroxyl radical in the oxidative stress process, have been investigated in this study by collision‐induced dissociation tandem mass spectrometry (CID‐MS2) and infrared multiple photon dissociation spectroscopy (IRMPD). We have revealed the modified sites in the molecule and characterized the 3D structure of the oxo‐forms of SAM in the gas phase. Both CID‐MS2 and IRMPD experiments, when coupled with quantum chemistry calculations have shown that only the adenine moiety of the molecule is oxidized, protecting the methionine from oxidation.Keywords: density functional theory (DFT) calculations, gamma radiolysis, infrared multiple photon dissociation (IRMPD) spectroscopy, OH‐induced oxidation, tandem mass spectrometry

Introduction

S‐adenosyl methionine (SAM) is a key metabolite found in living organisms, participating in their normal functioning [1]. It is composed of an adenosine cation bonded to the sulfur atom of methionine (Figure 1). SAM is synthetized in the human body by the reaction of the methionine amino acid with adenosine triphosphate. As a principal biological methyl donor, SAM transfers the methyl group from the methionine moiety to various acceptors in living organisms via transmethylation reactions [2, 3]. As a result of the methylation reaction, SAM is converted to S‐adenosylhomocysteine (SAH), which is subsequently hydrolyzed by the SAH hydrolase forming adenosine and homocysteine [1].

Chemical structure of SAM(H)+ with the nomenclature used for the adenosine part.

Even if the majority of known SAM dependent biological reactions involve methylation, regioselective transfer of all three alkyl substituents of the SAM sulfonium has been reported and may represent components of more complex biosynthetic cascades [4]. In particular, in addition to alkyl transfer, several other types of SAM‐dependent reactions catalyzed by enzymes have been established including α‐deprotonation as well as β‐elimination concomitant with elimination of a neutral disulfide. The radical S‐adenosylmethionine enzymes, which are classified as a superfamily enzymes, can catalyze radical reactions through the formation of an iron–sulfur cluster and SAM, to initiate a diverse set of radical reactions, in most or all cases via generation of a 5′‐deoxyadenosyl radical intermediate, which can then initiate a broad range of radical‐mediated transformations [5].

Moreover, SAM is widely used for pharmaceutical purposes to treat liver disorders [6] and depression [7, 8]. It acts as a precursor for the formation of cysteine and glutathione (GSH) and it has recently been pointed out that deficiency of SAM, perhaps secondary to lack of sufficient B12 and folate, may contribute to the development of Alzheimer's disease as SAM plays an important role in the inhibition of the oxidative stress, protecting cells against amyloid‐induced cellular injury [9]. Therefore, growing attention is focusing on the use of SAM as a neuroprotective agent, as it has been shown that SAM was more effective than GSH in scavenging • OH. Indeed, the beneficial effect of SAM could include direct radical scavenging as well as acting as a precursor for intracellular GSH [10].

Various analytical methods for the analysis of SAM and SAH in tissues have been previously reported, including liquid chromatography (LC) with UV, fluorescence and electrochemical detection, cation exchange chromatography and capillary electrophoresis methods. More sensitive LC–MS and LC–MS/MS methods enable quantification of SAM and SAH present in plasma or cerebrospinal fluid at low nanomolar concentrations have also been developed, requiring an extraction step and sample volumes ranging from 250 µL to 1 mL or with minimal sample requirements and with sufficient sample throughput, respectively. Stability of both metabolites during sample preparation and storage of tissue samples was studied, demonstrating that preanalytical steps are critical for obtaining valid data of SAM and SAH in tissues [11].

Due to the biological versatility of SAM, the aim of the present work is to employ collision‐induced dissociation‐tandem mass spectrometry (CID‐MS2) and infrared multiple photon dissociation (IRMPD) spectroscopy to investigate its structure in the gas phase and the effect of reactive oxygen species (ROS) on SAM, which could affect its biological functions.

It is well known that radiation chemistry is widely applied in the investigation of chemical reaction involving free radicals. As free radicals are often highly reactive species, they react fast with the surrounding substances and thus produce reversible or irreversible modifications. These free radicals may also be generated from normal metabolic processes in the human body or by harmful substances in the environment, and contribute to unexpected pathogenesis or accelerate the aging process. The study of their reactions is therefore of great chemical and biological research significance. Among all the categories of free radicals, ROS are the most important species.

Gamma radiolysis is employed in this study as a tool to generate free radicals for initiating oxidation reactions in solution [12].

CID‐MS2 is a key methodology to study biomolecules, with the electrospray ionization (ESI) being one of the preferred ionization techniques. It is well recognized as an efficient and highly versatile analytical tool, however, its potential has long been limited by the structural information it can provide. For this reason, the combination of CID‐MS2 with IRMPD spectroscopy has become a powerful tool for 3D structural characterization of molecular ions in the gas phase. Exploiting a highly intense and widely tunable free electron laser (FEL), IRMPD spectroscopy allows for the derivation of the vibrational fingerprint of molecular ions under tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) conditions. Because resonant absorption of IR photons depends on molecular vibrations, the subsequent fragmentation event is highly structure specific, revealing characteristic functional groups of the molecule [12]. To our knowledge, such a sensitive analytical and spectroscopic method has not yet been employed to reveal the structure of SAM and its oxidation products in the gas phase. IRMPD spectroscopy has previously been employed to investigate the one‐electron oxidation of biomolecules in the gas phase [13], with the aim to obtain direct information on the intramolecular interactions in the biomolecule and to reproduce experimental data with quantum‐chemical calculations.

In previous studies performed at the CLIO facility in Orsay, we have investigated the oxidation products formed from linear dipeptides containing methionine. It has been shown that the sulfoxide signature, characteristic of the oxidation of the methionine residue, can be observed near 1000 cm−1 in the IRMPD spectrum of the oxidation products of valine–methionine, tyrosine−methionine, and methionine−methionine dipeptides [14]. In lysine−methionine dipeptide [15], we were able to unravel the mechanism involved in the formation of the sulfoxide. The sulfoxide and sulfone have been clearly identified as final products of the oxidation of S‐methyl‐glutathione (GS‐Me) [16]. Decarboxylation or hydrogen loss are the major oxidation pathways in GS‐Me, while they have not been observed in tryptophan–methionine and methionine–tryptophan dipeptides. Interestingly, tryptophan is oxidized in the dipeptide methionine–tryptophan, while this is not the case for the reverse sequence.

Cyclic peptides have been investigated as well to understand the relationship between the structure of the 2‐center 3‐electron (2c−3e) free radical formed in the first step of the oxidation process and in the final products [17]. More recently, the •OH‐induced oxidation products of DNA nucleosides and nucleotides have been structurally characterized by collision‐induced dissociation, tandem MS, and results have shown that the addition of one oxygen atom occurs on the nucleobase moiety [18]. The gas‐phase geometries of +16 mass increment products have been addressed by CID‐MS2, which suggests that the addition of the oxygen atom occurs on the nucleobase moiety. The gas‐phase geometries of +16 mass increment products have also been investigated by IRMPD spectroscopy and quantum‐chemical calculations. We showed that a carbonyl group is formed at the C8 position after C oxidation of 2’‐deoxyadenosine and its monophosphate derivative. For 2’‐deoxycytidine and its monophosphate derivative, the oxygen atom is added to the C5 position to form a C‐OH group.

In this work, final compounds produced by γ radiolysis are characterized by high‐resolution Fourier‐transform ion cyclotron resonance (FT‐ICR) MS. CID‐MS2 experiments have been carried out to obtain sequencing information on the oxidation products by comparing the fragments of oxidized and nonoxidized molecules. IRMPD spectroscopy in combination with quantum chemistry calculations is used to provide direct information on the geometries of the native and oxidized products. The spectral signature of modified SAM is revealed by comparing the IRMPD spectra of the nonoxidized and oxidized molecules.

Methods/Experimental Section

Materials

SAM was purchased from Aldrich‐Sigma (purity > 98%) and used as received without further purification. To prevent the solution from deteriorating, all the samples were stored in the freezer (−20°C) before and immediately after the gamma irradiation and used the same day to perform MS and IRMPD spectroscopy experiments. We did not observe any degradation, as shown in spectra reported in Figure 2, and previous studies reported on the stability of SAM in acid solution confirmed that no degradation was observed for up to a few days [19]. Water came from a Millipore system. The sample was first dissolved in distilled water to make a concentration of 10−3 M as a stock solution. The solutions were diluted in a 50%:50% (v:v) MeOH:H2O mixture with 0.1% formic acid to enhance protonation in positive ion mode after gamma radiolysis and before performing IRMPD experiments. Concentrations were varied in the range of 10−4–10−6 M, depending on the specific requirements of different experiments.

γ‐irradiation was carried out by using the panoramic 60Co‐source IL60PL Cis‐Bio International (France) at Paris‐Saclay University (Institute of Physical Chemistry, Orsay, France). Samples were irradiated with a dose of 606 Gy. The dose rate was equal to 25.45 Gy.min−1 by Fricke dosimetry [21] and then corrected by the decay of the source over time. This dose rate has been chosen according to previous studies performed on similar molecules of biological interest [13] and to optimize the signal of the ion at m/z 415, corresponding to the oxidized product of SAM. These conditions allowed an easy selection of the m/z 415 ion in our experimental conditions to perform IRMPD spectroscopy. Higher doses will affect the parent ion signal too much, inducing an extensive fragmentation in the solution, reducing the intensity of the parent ion and not affecting the intensity of the +16 product. Lower doses will not produce enough product ion at m/z 415 to be mass selected in our experimental conditions.

MS2‐CID Analysis

After irradiation, the freshly irradiated samples were diluted in 50/50 water/methanol containing 0.1% formic acid to a concentration of 100 µM for MS‐based analysis. Nonirradiated solutions (with the same molecules dissolved in water and purged by N2O) at the same concentrations were prepared as reference. A 7 T hybrid FT‐ICR MS (APEX‐Qe Bruker) in Orsay, France, was used to identify all species generated in the irradiated solution [22].

SAM was delivered into the gas phase by an ESI source with a flow rate of 120 µL.h−1 in positive mode. Typical extraction voltage was −4500 V and the desolvation temperature was 200°C. A dry gas flow was fixed at 8 L.min−1 and the nebulizer pressure was set to 1.5 bar. Ions were accumulated, transferred through ion transfer optics, thermalized in a quadrupole‐hexapole (Qh) interface and later extracted out toward the FT‐ICR cell, which was maintained under a high vacuum (<10−9 mbar) at room temperature. Mass spectra of the species were then recorded with an appropriate accumulation time depending on the abundance of interested species, typically 1 s.

To process the MS2‐CID operation, target masses were first selected using an isolation window of 2 Da by resonant radiofrequency (RF) in FT‐ICR MS. MS2‐CID was then carried out by introducing argon gas to interact with selected ions (precursors) in a collision chamber. This raised the energy of the ions through kinetic‐to‐internal energy conversion, causing them to fragment and become thermalized by colliding with the buffer gas. Fragments were then pulse‐extracted and detected in FT‐ICR cell.

IRMPD Spectroscopy

IRMPD is a process that involves the successive absorption of multiple photons and the dispersion of energy in the molecule via intramolecular vibrational redistribution (IVR) [23]. At absorption wavelengths of the (mass‐selected) ion, the internal energy reaches and exceeds the fragmentation threshold, and fragment ions are observed in the mass spectrum recorded [24, 25].

In this work, the IRMPD spectroscopy experiments have been carried out both at theFEL for infrared eXperiments (FELIX) Laboratory in Nijmegen (Netherlands) in the 800–2000 cm−1 energy range and at Centre Laser Infrarouge d’Orsay (CLIO) in Orsay, France in the 2900–3800 cm−1 energy range.

The IRMPD experiments at the HMFL‐FELIX laboratory were performed using a Bruker AmaZon quadrupole ion trap MS [26] coupled with the free‐electron laser FELIX [27]. The AmaZon ion trap is equipped with an ESI source. Ions are infused using a syringe pump at a rate of 120 µL/h, assisted by a pressurized nebulizing N2 gas heated to 200°C. The spray voltage is 3–5 kV. Two 3 mm diameter holes have been drilled in the ring electrode of the trap, allowing the IR beam to enter the trap and interact with the ion cloud for laser spectroscopy.

The FELIX IR‐FEL runs at 10 Hz pulse repetition rate and covers wavelengths over the entire fingerprint region. Macropulses of 5–10 μs consist of a train of ∼5 ps micropulses, spaced by 1 ns. The energy of each macropulse reaches up to ∼150 mJ, while the spectral width is ∼0.5% of the central wavelength. Five neutral‐density IR attenuators (of 3, 5, 8, 10, and 10 dB used in adjustable combinations) provide attenuation levels to adapt the pulse energy to the studied systems [24], such as to prevent saturation of the IRMPD signal resulting from full depletion of the precursor ion population. A laser energy meter is placed at the exit of the optical pathway from the AmaZon MS to record the pulse energy after passing through the ion trap.

At the CLIO facility, ion spectra were obtained by using a 7 T hybrid FT‐ICR MS (Bruker, APEX‐Qe) [22] coupled to an OPO/OPA system (LaserVision) for the hydrogen stretching region (2900–3800 cm−1). Ions were isolated, thermalized and pulse extracted to the FT‐ICR cell (<10−9 mbar) to interact with the IR laser. For these experiments, mass‐selected ions were trapped and irradiated for 4 s with OPO/OPA radiation (10 Hz, 40 pulses). Within the relevant frequency range, the time‐averaged laser power lies between 0.1 and 0.2 W. An auxiliary CO2 laser (Universal Laser System, 10 W, cw operation, centered at λ = 10.6 µm) was added to enhance the fragmentation. To perform these experiments 3 ms CO2 laser pulse was synchronized with each OPO/OPA pulse. Under these conditions, the bandwidth in the IRMPD spectra is approximately 10 cm−1 at room temperature. The IRMPD spectra were obtained by plotting −ln(PF+P) as a function of the IR wavenumber, where P represents the intensity of precursor ion mass peaks and F the summed intensity of IR induced fragment ions. The spectral intensity was multiplied by the attenuation factor selected and linearly corrected for fluctuations in the laser power over the frequency scan range. The frequency of the FELIX laser was calibrated with a grating spectrometer with an accuracy of ±0.02 μm.

Computational Details

The spectral assignment of protonated SAM(H)+ and oxidized SAM (SAM(O)H+) was achieved by comparing the experimental IRMPD spectra with calculated vibrational spectra. To explore their conformational landscapes, SAM(H)+ and SAM(O)H+ were subjected to iterative metadynamics sampling combined with the genetic crossover method, as implemented in the Conformer Rotamer Ensemble Sampling Tool (CREST) [28]. This procedure included additional geometry optimization of the resulting conformer ensembles. Conformer energies were calculated using the semiempirical extended tight‐binding method (GFN2‐xTB) [29]. To select the most representative structures, a clustering algorithm based on principal component analysis was applied to identify the 10 most representative conformers. The lowest‐energy conformers with distinct structures were then selected. The same procedure, was applied using a clustering algorithm to determine the 10 most representative conformers. This process yielded a final set of around 35 unique structures for both SAM(H)+ and SAM(O)H+ (Figures S1 and S2, respectively).

To improve the accuracy of energy estimations, the geometries of the characteristic conformers of SAM(H)+ and SAM(O)H+ were reoptimized at the B3LYP‐D3(BJ)/6‐311+G(d, p) level. The corresponding IR absorption spectra were then calculated using the same method (Figures S3 and S4, respectively). Frequency‐dependent scaling factors of 0.98 (for ν < 2000 cm−1) and 0.955 (for ν > 2000 cm−1) were applied to correct for anharmonicity effects and basis set incompleteness. All density functional theory (DFT) static calculations were carried out using the Gaussian 16 package [30]. Additionally, single‐point energy calculations were performed for the lowest‐energy conformers at the DLPNO‐CCSD(T)/cc‐pVTZ level using the ORCA program package [31]. These electronic energies then were used in combination with DFT thermal corrections to compute relative Gibbs energies.

Results and Discussion

MS and MS2‐CID Spectra

Figure 2 shows a comparison of the mass spectra of N2O‐saturated SAM solutions before and after irradiation.

The most intense peak at m/z 399 corresponds to nonoxidized SAM: SAM(H)+. New peaks are observed in the mass spectrum following radiolysis‐induced, at m/z 415 and 417, respectively. The m/z 415 species can be attributed to the addition of oxygen to SAM(O)H+ (M + 16). The CID‐MS2 fragmentation spectra (Figure S5 and Table S1) confirm that oxidation occurs on the adenosine moiety. Indeed, diagnostic fragments at m/z 136 and m/z 152 are observed following CID of the molecular ions SAM(H)+ and SAM(O)H+, respectively. The peak at m/z 136 corresponds to protonated adenine (AH+), whereas the fragment at m/z 152 can be attributed to the protonated oxidized adenine species (A(O)H+). Furthermore, a diagnostic fragment at m/z 163 is assigned to the moiety containing the nonoxidized methionine residue.

Previous studies have shown that adenine moiety oxidation mainly occurs at two distinct positions: addition of •OH on C5 or on C8 [32] (following the numbering in Figure 1). Due to the m/z value of the final oxidized product the C8‐oxidized form is the only form observed in the present study, which is consistent with a previous study on nucleosides containing adenine [18]. Consequently, the C8‐oxidized form can be attributed to SAM(O)H+, whose structure we assume to be as shown in Figure 3.

Putative chemical structure of SAM(O)H+. The modification induced by oxidation are indicated in red.

Since only oxidation of the adenine moiety can produce a + 18 mass increment, the peak at m/z 417 can be assigned to the (Fapy‐Ade)MetH+ species (see proposed structure in Figure S6), in which the oxidative modification of adenine leads to the formation of the Fapy‐Ade moiety [33, 34, 35]. Nevertheless, the intensity of the +18 mass increment peak was too low to allow mass selection and subsequent CID fragmentation using our MS. Furthermore, in the irradiated SAM solution, no decarboxylation or dehydrogenation products were detected in our experimental conditions.

It is noteworthy that earlier studies have reported a higher rate constant for the reaction of OH radicals with the methionine residue alone (k(OH + Met) = 8.5 × 109 M−1·L·s−1) [36] than for the reaction with adenosine alone (k(OH + adenosine) = 5.8 × 109 M−1·L·s−1) [37]. Based on these values, one would hypothesize that methionine would react preferentially with OH radicals. In contrast, our results demonstrate that the oxidative modification occurs on the adenine moiety rather than on the methionine residue. One possible explanation is that the sulfur atom of methionine experiences steric hindrance due to its linkage to the adenosine moiety, which could limit its accessibility to OH radicals and impede the formation of >S•—OH radicals in the methionine side chain [36]. This steric constraint would make oxidation of the methionine residue less favorable than oxidation of the adenine unit.

Another plausible hypothesis is that methionine acts as a pro‐oxidant by forming a methionine radical cation (Met•+). It is then possible that an intramolecular electron transfer (IET) from the adenine moiety to Met•+ would generate the adenine radical cation, thereby initiating oxidation at the adenine site under kinetic control, as already observed for sulfur‐centered radicals of N‐acetyl‐methionine [38].

The high‐frequency region exhibits a discernible discrepancy between the two spectra. Some bands are common to both, notably those at 3661, 3556, and 3430 cm−1, which correspond to the area of the X—H stretching vibrations (X = C, O, or N). It is noteworthy that new bands appear in the spectrum of SAM(O)H+ at 3491 cm−1 and several others below 3400 cm−1. These observations indicate the presence of new gas‐phase conformers, leading to shifts in the vibrational bands, as no new X—H chemical bonds are formed. Hence, oxidation leads to the formation of additional conformers in the gas phase.

For both SAM(H)+ and SAM(O)H+, a preliminary assignment of the three common bands can be proposed. The band at 3661 cm−1 may originate primarily from the stretching vibrations of one of the two hydroxyl groups in the adenosine sugar, as suggested by their presence in the spectrum of protonated adenosine [39].

The band near 3556 cm−1 is markedly more intense than in the spectrum of protonated adenosine [39]. This suggests that it is not exclusively composed of the O—H stretching contribution from the sugar hydroxyl group. Rather, its dominant component likely arises from the O—H stretching vibration of the carboxylic acid group in the methionine residue. The blue‐side shoulder of this feature may still include a contribution from the sugar O—H stretching mode, consistent with the weaker signal observed in protonated adenosine.

The band around 3430 cm−1 may, at least in part, arise from N m H2 stretching of the group located on the adenine moiety of adenosine [39], as also indicated by its appearance in the spectra of deoxyadenosine and deoxyadenosine monophosphate [18].

The fingerprint region shows fewer differences between the two species. Notably, the characteristic thioether band near 1000 cm−1 is absent, confirming that oxygen addition did not occur on the sulfur atom [40].

According to earlier calculations [18], several bands can be tentatively assigned. Compared with the sharp band at 1770 cm−1 in SAM(H)+, the spectrum of SAM(O)H+ exhibits an additional feature at 1724 cm−1. The 1770 cm−1 band can be assigned to the C=O stretching mode of the carboxylic group, while the extra band at 1724 cm−1 in SAM(O)H+ likely reflects the presence of an additional carbonyl group generated upon oxidation by γ‐radiolysis as suggested by the MS and MS2‐CID spectra part.

Interestingly, the intense band centered at 832 cm−1 in both SAM(H)+ and SAM(O)H+ was not previously reported in the IRMPD spectra of protonated adenosine or methionine, suggesting that it may arise from interactions between the Met residue and the adenosine moiety.

Various spectral differences are observed upon oxidation, although none is particularly pronounced. The weak band at 640 cm−1 increases in intensity after oxidation, while the weak band at 759 cm−1 disappears.

All of these preliminary hypotheses and tentative assignments can be further examined and refined through comparison with theoretical chemistry calculations.

Theoretical Results and Band Assignment

The molecular nomenclature used in this work is summarized in Figure 1. Subscripts m and b refer, respectively, to the NH2 group located on the methionine residue and on the nucleobase adenine moiety.

All theoretical investigations were carried out on the oxidized form generated by modification at the C8 position of adenine as discussed above. Based on the known proton affinities of the carboxylate group compared to the amino group, it is reasonable to restrict the analysis to protonation occurring on the carboxylate group of the methionine rather than on the amino sites on the adenosine moiety. In addition, the presence of bands associated with the O—H stretching vibration of the carboxylic acid unequivocally rules out the existence of zwitterionic structures in the gas phase.

For clarity, vibrational bands in the IRMPD spectra are labeled alphabetically and are directly compared to the corresponding calculated transitions. Relative Gibbs free energies (in kJ/mol) are reported at the B3LYP‐D3(BJ)/6‐311+G(d, p) level, and, in parentheses, the values are corrected by DLPNO‐CCSD(T)/cc‐pVTZ single‐point electronic energies. An energy‐based ranking index is also included in the structural nomenclature.

Across all calculated conformers, a recurrent folded geometry is observed, arising from an interaction between the methionine and the adenine moieties. This overall shape is primarily dictated by a structuring contact between the positively charged sulfur atom and a nearby electronegative atom, O or N (red dashed lines in Figure 5). In several conformers, this interaction is further reinforced by additional hydrogen bonds (black dashed lines in Figure 5), which contribute to stabilizing the folded arrangement. Together, these hydrogen‐bonding and S+…O/N electrostatic interactions stabilize the overall geometry and shape the recurrent structural organization within the conformational ensemble.

The most stable conformer, SAM_1, shows good agreement with the experimental spectrum. In this structure, the sulfur atom interacts with two nitrogen atoms, one from the methionine moiety and one from the adenosine unit. This geometry indicates that the hydrogen of the methionine carboxylic acid does not participate in any hydrogen bonding, which is consistent with the presence of the experimental G and R bands, respectively, the C—O7″ and CO7″—H stretching modes.

In contrast, in SAM_2, the COOH group forms a hydrogen bond with a nitrogen atom of the adenosine moiety. This interaction induces a shift of the CO7″ stretching vibration, which can be associated with the experimental H band and accounts for the observed broadening of this feature toward 1100 cm−1. SAM_3 can nonetheless be considered present in the gas phase, as it also accounts for the F, G and H bands broadening.

As indicated above, the sulfur atom participates in structural interactions that promote folded and compact geometries between the methionine and adenine moieties. However, in contrast to SAM(H)+, the oxygen introduced upon nucleobase oxidation can form additional interactions with the methionine sulfur, as illustrated by the contacts highlighted in red in Figure 6. These novel interactions have been shown to reshape the intramolecular network, thereby accounting for the observed differences in the conformational landscape between SAM(H)+ and SAM(O)H+.

The first structure, SAMO_1, allows us to assign all the peaks present in the fingerprint region of the experimental spectrum of SAM(O)H+. The high‐frequency region proves more complex in terms of assignment and, as we have already indicated, reveals the presence of other isomers. First, it is noteworthy that, in common with SAM, this structure exhibits the greatest stability with a free carboxylic acid group that is also found in the SAMO_6 conformer. The two bands, D’ and P’, which are also found in the SAM(H)+ spectrum (G and R), can be attributed to the C—O7″ and CO7″—H stretching modes and are specific to the free carboxylic acid function.

The SAMO_2 and SAMO_11 conformers then allow us to attribute the experimental K’ and L’ bands, respectively, to the redshifted CO7″‐H stretching modes. The observed shifts can be primarily linked to the presence of a hydrogen bond between the carboxylic acid group and the amine function present in each conformer. In the case of SAMO_11, the vibrational frequency is modified by an additional interaction between sulfur and the carboxylic acid function.

The J’ and O’ bands are directly linked to base oxidation: the former is linked to the C=O stretching vibration, and the latter to the N7—H stretching vibration that is formed when adenosine is oxidized. The complete absence of band J’ from the SAM spectrum clearly indicates that the oxidation occurs on the adenosine, as has already been observed [18].

It is therefore possible to confirm the structure proposed in Figure 3 for the +16‐oxidation product of SAM(H)+.

Moreover, among the conformers that can be assigned for SAM(O)H+, none exhibits a hydrogen‐bond interaction between the carboxylic acid group and the adenosine moiety, unlike what is observed in SAM_2. This absence of COOH···adenosine hydrogen bonding provides a consistent explanation for the disappearance of the H band, which is present in the spectrum of SAM(H)+ but not in that of SAM(O)H+.

Conclusion

In this work, a multifaceted approach involving CID‐MS2, IRMPD spectroscopy and quantum chemistry calculations has been carried out to shed light on the structures of the oxo‐forms of SAM generated after OH radical‐induced oxidation in N2O saturated solution. The aim was to characterize SAM oxo‐forms.

SAM is a natural compound that is synthetized in homocysteine metabolism, it is the primary methyl donor and the precursor of GSH. SAM is employed in liver injury. Its therapeutic effect is attributed to its capability to enhance GSH storage, and its antioxidant properties can be related to decreasing the production of ROS species.

γ‐radiolysis has been employed to simulate the effect of ROS on SAM during oxidative stress. The main product generated in N2O‐saturated solution of SAM after γ‐radiation corresponds to the oxygen‐addition: +16 mass increment product. Another product corresponding to the addition of an 18 mass increment to the SAM has been revealed by high resolutionMS, but its intensity was quite low, and it was not possible to isolate it and to perform CID‐MS2 or IRMPD spectroscopy experiments.

The results of CID‐MS2 on the M + 16 oxidation product revealed that the nucleobase moiety is oxidized under our experimental conditions, while the sulfur atom remains intact. The hydroxyl radicals formed during the oxidative stress are consumed from the solution.

It is widely acknowledged that the different types of ionizing radiations on DNA can cause two types of effects: (1) direct effect, ionization triggered by the loss of one electron (‐e‐) through interaction of radiation with DNA itself and (2) indirect effect, i.e. interactions of ionizing radiation with the solvent, creating water free radicals such as OH radicals.

The process of oxidation of adenine derivatives induced by OH radicals results in the 8‐oxoAde, Fapy‐Ade, and hypoxanthine as the final products, contingent upon the location of the addition of the hydroxyl group on the adenine moiety [31, 32, 34, 41]. Thanks to these previous studies, as illustrated in Figure 7, it is possible to propose a pathway for the formation of the oxidized products of SAMH+ (1). The 8‐oxoAde‐MetH+ (3) (SAM(O)H+) and (Fapy‐Ade)MetH+ (4) are formed by an intermediate radical, 8‐hydroxy‐7,8‐dihydroaden‐8‐yl radical for the adenine part (2), through addition of •OH to the C8 position and one‐electron oxidation or reduction process. Another possible pathway is the addition of •OH at the C5 position, giving rise to 4,5‐dihydroaden‐4‐yl radicals (5). By loss of water, (5) can transform into strong oxidizing adenine‐N6‐yl radicals (6). Deamination of (6) leads to the final product (7). These oxo‐products have not been observed in the case of SAM in our experimental conditions. This is probably due to the fact that the addition of •OH to the C5 position at a short time scale forms a less stable intermediate or a species that converts at longer time scale into oxidation at the C8 position.

Possible pathways for the formation of oxidation products of adenine moiety in SAMH+.

By combining the IRMPD spectra with DFT calculation, the signature of the C=O bond formation in SAM(O)H+ at ∼1725 cm−1 has been identified for the first time in the +16 SAM oxo‐product. This observation confirms the structure proposed in the Figure 3 where the oxidation occurred in the adenine moiety by formation of a new carbonyl group at the 8‐position. The protonation site was shown to be at the carboxylate group of the zwitterionic SAM molecule for both SAMH+ and SAM(O)H+. The presence of the intense band at ∼3490 cm−1 corresponding to the N7–H vibration of the adenosine moiety showed a radiolysis‐induced oxidation.

IRMPD spectroscopy provides specific diagnostic signatures to identify the oxidation products, which has been applied here for the first time to determine the structure of products resulting from oxidative damage of SAM. Indeed, IRMPD spectroscopy, when combined with quantum chemical calculations, is a powerful structural tool that can provide direct information on geometric details on the modified sites of model systems and can also help to obtain information on the oxidation mechanism.
