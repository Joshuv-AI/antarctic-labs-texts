Effect of abdominal adipose tissue glucose uptake on brain aging
Authors: Weizhao Lu, Yanhua Duan, Kun Li, Zhaoping Cheng, Jianfeng Qiu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAbdominal adipose tissue (AT) mass has adverse effects on the brain. This study aimed to investigate the effect of glucose uptake by abdominal AT on brain aging.METHODSThree‐hundred twenty‐five participants underwent total‐body positron emission tomography scan. Brain age was estimated in an independent test set (n = 98) using a support vector regression model that was built using a training set (n = 227). Effects of abdominal subcutaneous and visceral AT (SAT/VAT) glucose uptake on brain age delta were evaluated using linear regression.RESULTSHigher VAT glucose uptake was linked to negative brain age delta across all subgroups. Higher SAT glucose uptake was associated with negative brain age delta in lean individuals. In contrast, increased SAT glucose uptake demonstrated positive trends with brain age delta in female and overweight/obese participants.DISCUSSIONIncreased glucose uptake of the abdominal VAT has positive influences on the brain, while SAT may not have such influences, except for lean individuals.Highlights Higher glucose uptake of the visceral adipose tissue was linked to decelerated brain aging.Higher glucose uptake of the subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) was associated with negative brain age delta in lean individuals.Faster brain aging was associated with increased glucose uptake of the SAT in female and overweight and obese individuals. Keywords: abdominal adipose tissue, brain age, positron emission tomography, subcutaneous adipose tissue, visceral adipose tissue

BACKGROUND

Obesity, a widespread issue in modern society, leads to various health issues such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and certain cancers. 1 , 2 Abdominal obesity, a common type of obesity, refers to the accumulation of excess fat in the abdominal area. 3 Abdominal visceral adipose tissue (VAT) and subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) are two major forms of abdominal adipose tissue (AT), with excessive VAT conferring heightened risk for developing various health issues. Although SAT expansion is relatively less harmful, it is still associated with a low risk of metabolic disorders. 4 Therefore, understanding the impact of VAT and SAT on health is crucial for developing effective strategies for obesity management.

Over the past decades, neuroimaging research has established links among abdominal VAT, SAT, and the brain. 5 , 6 , 7 Recent investigations have explored the relationships among VAT distribution and brain structure and function, and have revealed that higher levels of VAT are related to decreased cortical thickness of the whole brain, parietal, temporal, and insula lobes; 8 , 9 reduced gray matter volume in the temporal lobe, cingulate gyrus, and hippocampus; 9 , 10 white matter microstructural alterations; 11 and decreased brain function. 12 Additionally, VAT accumulation has been implicated in cognitive decline and emotional processing deficits, 11 , 12 and may potentially lead to Alzheimer's disease. 13 On the other hand, SAT has been shown to have a more complex relationship with the brain. Previous studies have revealed no significant associations between SAT and brain structural and perfusion characteristics including volumes, cortical thickness, gray and white matter microstructural integrity, and cerebral blood flow. 6 , 7 However, one study suggested that abdominal SAT depots might have a protective effect on the female brain against inflammation. 14

Compared to traditional brain measures, brain age distills various brain characteristics such as volumes, cortical thickness, fractional anisotropy, and functional network properties from multiple magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sequences into a single estimate per individual, and has emerged as an effective biomarker for assessing individual brain health. 15 In addition to brain characteristics from MRI, a recent study has demonstrated the feasibility of using 18F‐flurodeoxyglucose (18F‐FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) for brain age estimation. 16 Using brain age as an indicator, Beck et al. have reported that higher fat distribution contributed to accelerated brain aging both cross‐sectionally and longitudinally. 17 Schindler et al. have demonstrated that higher AT volume post‐menopause is linked to older brain age. 18 However, most previous studies have focused on the mass and distribution of VAT and SAT and the effect of AT glucose uptake profiles on brain age has been seldomly explored. In this study, it was hypothesized that glucose uptake of the SAT and VAT might affect the brain age. 18F‐FDG total‐body PET/computed tomography (CT) was used to depict glucose uptake for the VAT and SAT. Multivariable regression analysis was used to assess the effect of VAT and SAT glucose uptake on brain age.

METHODS

The outline of the study is presented in Figure 1. Total‐body 18F‐FDG uptake images were acquired via total‐body PET scan. Brain standardized uptake value normalized by lean body mass (SUL) was calculated from the raw PET images. Brain age was estimated using features from the brain SUL map using a machine learning model. Finally, the effects of abdominal SAT and VAT glucose uptake on brain age were evaluated using a multivariable regression model (Figure 1A). Before the splitting of training and test set, we first calculated the needed sample size for the regression analysis (See Supplementary Methods 1 in supporting information). Thereafter, all participants were randomly assigned to a training set and a test set with a ratio of 7:3. To address other confounding factors such as age, sex, and body weight status, we divided the test set into relevant subgroups (Figure 1B).

Subjects

This cross‐sectional study was approved by the institutional review board of Shandong First Medical University. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants were recruited at the First Affiliated Hospital of Shandong First Medical University from December 2020 to July 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 20 and 90 years; (2) fasting blood glucose levels within the normal range of 3.9 to 7.8 mmol/L; (3) no hypermetabolic lesions such as tumors detected in the brain, abdomen, or liver via medical imaging examination; (4) self‐reported absence of diseases such as diabetes, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases; (5) self‐reported absence of mental illnesses, head trauma, and other conditions that may affect brain metabolism. A total of 325 subjects were finally included, with an average age of 57.94 ± 13.49 years (20–86 years), including 220 males and 105 females.

Postprandial glucose (PPG) concentration, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, hip circumference, and waist‐to‐hip ratio were recorded for all enrolled participants.

Image acquisition and processing

PET images were collected using the uEXPLORER PET/CT system (United Imaging Healthcare). After fasting for 6 hours, participants were injected with 18F‐FDG based on their body weight (0.08 mCi/kg). Sixty minutes after injection, a total‐body PET/CT scan was performed for 10 minutes. PET images were reconstructed using time‐of‐flight and point spread functions with a matrix size of 192 × 192, slice thickness of 2.886 mm, voxel size of 3.125 × 3.125 × 2.886 mm3, three iterations and 20 subsets, Gaussian filter (full width at half maximum = 3 mm), and necessary corrections. Additionally, high‐resolution abdominal CT scan was performed with the following parameters: voltage = 120 kVp, matrix = 515 × 512, slice thickness = 1.5 mm, and voxel size of 0.9766 × 0.9766 × 1.5000 mm3.

Brain SUL maps were calculated from the raw PET images using the following procedures: (1) calculation of standardized uptake value maps and (2) calculation of lean body mass according to the equations proposed by Hume (Supplementary Methods 2 in supporting information). 19 Lean body mass was then used to normalize the standardized uptake values to finally yield total‐body SUL maps.

Additionally, brain SUL maps were extracted from the total‐body SUL maps. Thereafter, Statistical Parametric Mapping 8 (SPM8) was used for the processing of brain SUL maps: the extracted brain SUL maps were spatially registered to the Montreal Neurologic Institute (MNI) template and were resampled into a voxel size of 3 × 3 × 3 mm3. Then, the SUL map was smoothed using a 6‐mm full width at half maximum Gaussian kernel.

VAT, SAT, and liver delineation

We used the 3D Slicer software (version 4.6.2) to segment abdominal VAT and SAT on the SUL images with the assistance of CT images. We aligned the abdominal CT images with the whole‐body SUL images. The CT value range for AT was set from −150 to −50 Hounsfield units, 20 and by setting the AT CT range in the 3D Slicer software, we obtained the masks of VAT and SAT in the corresponding SUL images (Figure S1 in supporting information). In addition, we used the contour function of ITK‐SNAP software (version 3.6.0) to semi‐automatically outline the liver mask on the whole‐body SUL images.

Maximum (SULmax) and mean SUL (SULmean) values were extracted from the whole‐body SUL images for both VAT and SAT. Mean SUL values of the liver were extracted as background signals. SULmax and SULmean of the VAT and SAT were further standardized by dividing SULmean of the liver, to obtain relative SULmax (rSULmax) and relative SULmean (rSULmean) values of the VAT and SAT.

Brain age prediction

We first calculated the needed sample size for the regression analysis (see Supplementary Methods 1). Then, to train the machine learning model and to maintain the sample size, we set a ratio of 7:3 to randomly split all 325 participants into a training set and a test set, resulting in the training set containing 227 participants and the test set containing 98 participants. We used stratified sampling from two age groups (young/middle‐aged, and senior adult) to ensure that the age distribution in the training set and test set was similar, as shown in Figure S2 in supporting information.

Feature extraction and selection

Mean SUL values were extracted from 166 regions across the entire brain as the features using the Automated Anatomical Labelling 3 (AAL3) atlas on the brain SUL map (the brain regions are listed in Table S1 in supporting information). 21 The effects of age and sex on the SUL values were removed from the extracted features using linear regression. Subsequently, the features were normalized to a range between 0 and 1 using min‐max scaling. The normalized features were subjected to feature selection. Feature selection was performed in the training set using the sequential backward elimination (SBE) method. 22 , 23 After feature selection, corresponding features were also discarded in the test set to ensure that the same selected features were used for all participants.

Age prediction via machine learning

In the current study, we applied a support vector regression (SVR) model with a radial basis function (RBF) kernel for age estimation based on the selected brain SUL features. It is worth mentioning that SVR with an RBF kernel was chosen based on the performance comparison to six other commonly used models (see Supplementary Methods 3 in supporting information). The SVR model was trained using the training set and tested in the independent test set. Furthermore, age‐dependency bias was removed as described in a previous study. 24

To evaluate the model performance, we calculated mean square error (MSE), normalized MSE (nMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and the correlation between predicted age and chronological age. Brain age delta was calculated by subtracting the chronological age from the predicted brain age in the test set, with positive values (expressed in years) indicating accelerated brain aging, and negative values indicating decelerated aging.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.0.4). Comparisons of continuous demographic and clinical variables between the training set and test were performed via two‐sample Student t test. Comparison of categorical variable (sex) between the training set and test was performed via a chi‐square test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To address other confounding factors such as age, sex, and body weight status, we divided the test set into relevant subgroups. Two subgroups were generated based on age: young/middle‐aged adult group (age < 60 years, n = 53) and senior adult group (age ≥ 60, n = 45). 25 Two subgroups were generated based on sex: male group (n = 66) and female group (n = 32). As for degree of fatness, lean group (BMI < 24.9 kg/m2, n = 63) and overweight/obese group (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, n = 35) were generated (as shown in Figure 1B) according to the World Health Organization BMI classification.

Comparisons of continuous demographic and clinical variables between different subgroups were performed via two‐sample Student t test. Comparisons of categorical variable (sex) between different subgroups were performed via a chi‐square test. Comparisons of rSUL values between VAT and SAT in each subgroup were performed using two‐sample Student t test. Comparisons of brain age delta and VAT and SAT glucose uptake measures between each two subgroups separated by age, sex, or BMI were performed by Student t test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all statistical analysis.

In addition, a linear regression model was used to evaluate the role of AT glucose uptake measures (rSULmax, rSULmean of the VAT and SAT) as well as other physiological indexes (BMI, PPG, waistline, hipline, and waist–hip ratio) in affecting brain age delta in the test set, controlling for age, sex, and body weight. Furthermore, linear regression was also performed in each subgroup to assess the role of abdominal AT glucose uptake measures in affecting brain age delta. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Furthermore, in the test set, the young/middle‐aged adults and senior adults subgroups were well matched for sex, PPG, height, and waist‐to‐hip ratio, while the young/middle‐aged adults subgroup demonstrated higher body weight, BMI, waistline, and hipline (Table S2 in supporting information). Brain age prediction results

In the training set, the MAE, MSE, and nMSE between chronological age and predicted age were 3.09, 22.99, and 0.01 respectively. The model showed a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.92 between chronological age and predicted age (Figure S3A and Table S5 in supporting information). When applying the model to the test set, the model achieved MAE, MSE, and nMSE between chronological age and predicted age of 3.71, 19.45, and 0.01, respectively. The Pearson correlation coefficient between chronological age and predicted age was 0.94 (Figure S3B).

In addition, the correlation value between brain age delta and actual age in the test set decreased to near zero (r = 0.033) after the estimated brain age was corrected from age‐dependency bias, indicating that the derived brain‐age delta was free from age dependency.

Comparisons between VAT and SAT glucose uptake

We compared the glucose metabolism between VAT and SAT, and the results are presented in Figure S4 in supporting information. As can be seen from the figure, VAT glucose uptake is significantly higher than that of SAT (P < 0.001). A similar phenomenon exists in different age, sex, and body weight subgroups (Figure S4B–D).

Comparisons of adipose tissue metabolic profiles between different subgroups

When the participants were separated by age, young/middle‐aged adults demonstrated lower SAT rSULmax (T = 2.553, P = 0.012), lower SAT rSULmean (T = 2.572, P = 0.012) compared to senior groups. However, the two groups did not differ significantly in brain age delta (T = 1.773, P = 0.080), VAT rSULmax (T = 0.585, P = 0.560), VAT rSULmean (T = 0.525, P = 0.601; Figure 2). When separated by sex, female participants exhibited significantly lower SAT rSULmax (T = 3.526, P < 0.001) and lower SAT rSULmean (T = 3.364, P = 0.001). Males and females demonstrated no significant distinctions in terms of brain age delta (T = 0.520, P = 0.604), VAT rSULmax (T = 0.118, P = 0.907), and rSULmean (T = 0.659, P = 0.512; Figure 3). Finally, when separated by BMI, lean and overweight/obese groups did not differ in brain age delta (T = 0.141, P = 0.888), VAT rSULmax (T = 0.323, P = 0.748), and VAT rSULmean (T = 0.458, P = 0.648). However, the lean subgroup exhibited higher SAT rSULmax (T = 3.703, P < 0.001) and SAT rSULmean (T = 3.607, P < 0.001) compared to the overweight/obese subgroup (Figure 4).

Effects of VAT and SAT glucose uptake and other risk factors on brain age delta

In the test set, increased VAT rSULmean and rSULmax were significantly associated with decelerated brain age delta (β = −6.94 years for rSULmean and β = −3.56 years for rSULmax). SAT rSULmean and rSULmax also demonstrated negative trends with brain age delta (Figure 5A). In terms of the young/middle‐aged adult and senior groups separated by age, decreasing brain age delta was significantly associated with VAT rSULmean and rSULmax in both young/middle‐aged adults (β = −6.09 years for rSULmean and β = −3.43 years for rSULmax) and senior adults (β = −9.12 years for rSULmean and β = −3.66 years for rSULmax; Figure 5B). In terms of participants separated by sex, both rSULmax and rSULmean of the VAT were significantly linked with younger brain age (β = −7.08 years for rSULmean and β = −3.23 years for rSULmax) for the male group. Only rSULmax of the VAT was linked with younger brain age for the female group (β = −4.79 years). In addition, SAT glucose uptake showed opposite trends with brain aging between the two subgroups separated by sex (Figure 5C). In terms of lean and overweight/obese participants, there were significant effects of VAT rSULmean and rSULmax on brain age delta in the lean group (β = −5.43 years for rSULmean, and β = −3.45 years for rSULmax). There was also a significant effect of SAT rSULmean on brain age delta (β = −14.80 years) in the lean group. In contrast, only rSULmax and rSULmean of the VAT were related to decelerated brain aging for overweight/obese participants (β = −11.45 years for rSULmean and β = −5.18 years for rSULmax), and rSULmax and rSULmean of the SAT showed positive trends with brain age delta (Figure 5D).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we evaluated the impact of glucose uptake by abdominal VAT and SAT on brain age using total‐body PET/CT imaging. We found that higher glucose uptake of VAT, rather than SAT, was associated with decelerated brain aging. After stratifying the study sample based on age, sex, and body weight status, we further discovered that SAT glucose uptake had a greater impact on the brains of lean individuals. By contrast, glucose metabolism by SAT might affect brain aging in the female and overweight/obese participants in an inverse direction.

The association between AT and the brain is a topic of growing interest. 26 Several studies have attempted to establish a correlation between AT profiles and brain age markers. 5 , 6 , 7 Recent studies have reported associations between VAT distribution and the brain, and have demonstrated the effect of higher VAT level and distribution on brain structure and function. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Additionally, researches have also revealed that VAT distribution, rather than SAT, contributed to faster brain aging. 19 , 20 However, the effect of glucose uptake by VAT and SAT on the brain has rarely been investigated. In this study, using the state‐of‐the‐art total‐body PET/CT, we investigated the association between glucose uptake by VAT and SAT and brain age delta in an adult cohort.

First, this study revealed that VAT glucose metabolism is significantly higher than SAT, regardless of age, sex, or BMI, which is consistent with previous research findings. 27 , 28 The increased VAT glucose metabolism may be due to underlying differences in stromal metabolic activity between VAT and SAT. 27 Additionally, our study has revealed that increased glucose uptake by VAT, rather than SAT, is associated with decelerated brain aging. Liu et al. have reported that VAT is more strongly associated with insulin resistance than SAT. 29 The underlying mechanisms may be that VAT adipocytes are more metabolically active compared to SAT, and increased VAT mass may potentially evoke the lipolytic process, 30 which in turn releases more free fatty acids into the portal vein and results in the inhibition of the insulin sensitivity and hepatic insulin resistance. 31 Furthermore, one study showed that macrophages accumulated in VAT, and macrophages were an independent factor of insulin resistance. The specific mechanism may be related to inflammatory cytokines secreted by macrophages. 32 In line with previous findings, the positive effect of VAT glucose uptake on brain aging may be due to the fact that VAT is more closely related to insulin sensitivity than SAT, which is consistent with previous findings of decreased glucose uptake in prediabetes and diabetes patients compared to healthy controls. 33

Most previous studies have investigated the influences of VAT and SAT on the brain in middle‐aged and senior adults, but few studies have demonstrated the effects in young adults. Previous studies have reported decreased SAT and increased VAT distribution in humans during aging. 34 , 35 In this study, we have revealed that for young/middle‐aged adults, glucose uptake of VAT was linked to decelerated brain aging. Although young/middle‐aged adults exhibited greater body weight, BMI, hipline, and waistline compared to senior adults, given that the two groups did not have significant waist‐to‐hip ratio and VAT glucose uptake, the negative associations between VAT glucose uptake and brain age delta might suggest that higher glucose uptake of VAT may benefit the brain for young and middle‐aged adults. In terms of senior adults aged ≥ 60 years, higher glucose uptake of VAT may be beneficial to the brain. 36 Moreover, waist‐to‐hip ratio contributes to accelerated brain aging, suggesting that central adiposity as an indicator of visceral fat might be the component mainly responsible for accelerating brain aging, which accords well with a previous study. 24

In humans, fat distribution is significantly different between males and females, with a “pear‐shaped” distribution in females, and an “apple‐shaped” distribution in males, 37 , 38 and this is in line with the current result of lower waist‐to‐hip ratio of females compared to males. The sex difference in SAT and VAT is well established: VAT is more closely associated with insulin resistance and insulin secretion in females, whereas SAT and VAT are associated with insulin resistance to a similar extent. 38 Our current finding has revealed that VAT glucose uptake was more closely linked to decelerated brain aging, which is consistent with well‐established results. In addition, a previous study has reported that SAT in female mice is protective against neuroinflammation; 14 thus, higher SAT glucose uptake may lead to neuroinflammation in the female brain. This may give a potential explanation of the opposite contributions of SAT on brain aging and the different glucose uptake of SAT between female and male subgroups, and further suggests sex differences in SAT glucose uptake.

Lean and overweight/obese groups differed in the effects of AT glucose uptake on brain aging, as both groups exhibited positive effects of VAT glucose uptake on brain aging, whereas the overweight/obese subgroup demonstrated adverse effects of SAT glucose uptake on brain aging. In addition, the overweight/obese subgroup also exhibited lower SAT rSUL than the lean subgroup. The results seem interesting, because it is commonly known that visceral obesity increases risk of cognitive decline in humans, but subcutaneous adiposity does not. 6 A recent study reported that beige adipocytes are indispensable for the neuroprotective and anti‐inflammatory effects of subcutaneous fat in obese mice. 39 In other words, SAT may play a protective role in the overweight and obese. 40 Therefore, higher glucose uptake by SAT may generate an adverse effect on the brain.

This study has several limitations that need to be addressed. First, the accelerated and decelerated brain aging conditions were developed from the cross‐sectional data, which should be interpreted with caution. Longitudinal data are needed to further prove the current findings. Second, the absence of diabetes was based on participants’ self‐report, not the oral glucose tolerance test. Third, we only extracted abdominal AT from abdominal CT images, and evaluated the effect of abdominal AT on brain aging; the effect of AT in other parts of the body on brain age delta was not assessed due to limited conditions.

In conclusion, we collected whole‐body metabolic images using total‐body PET/CT imaging. Based on PET images of the brain, we constructed a brain age prediction model and evaluated the impact of glucose absorption by abdominal VAT and SAT on brain age. We found that increased VAT glucose uptake was accompanied by decelerated brain aging, a phenomenon also observed in all subgroups regardless of age, sex, and degree of fatness. However, in the lean group, SAT metabolism had a significant impact on decelerating brain aging, while in the female and overweight/obese group, SAT metabolism accelerated brain aging. This study uncovered the significance of abdominal AT metabolism on the brain and the differences of VAT and SAT. The underlying mechanisms require further exploration.
