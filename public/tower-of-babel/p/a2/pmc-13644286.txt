Innovative design of a novel combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implant for severe atrophic edentulous dentition: a finite element analysis
Authors: Yantai Tang, Huimin Nie, Zhonghao Liu, Wenjuan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study proposes a novel combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implant to rehabilitate severely atrophic edentulous mandibles. We aimed to elucidate its biomechanical behavior through finite element analysis.MethodsUsing CBCT data from an edentulous patient, we reconstructed a 3D mandibular model. We designed intraosseous fixtures tailored to the residual bone volume and fused them with superstructure abutments and a customized subperiosteal titanium mesh using 3-matic software. Four finite element models were assembled, varying in implant design and abutment count. These models were subjected to four loading conditions: anterior vertical, unilateral molar vertical, bilateral molar vertical, and unilateral molar lateral occlusions. Biomechanical performance was assessed by evaluating the maximum and minimum principal stresses in the peri-implant bone and the von Mises stresses in the prosthetic components.ResultsUnder simulated masticatory loads, the novel combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implant exhibited stress levels well within the elastic range of the material, avoiding plastic deformation. Notably, the six-abutment configuration proved superior under posterior loading, reducing peak von Mises stress in screws by roughly 33% and in implants by 25.5% relative to ultra-short implants.ConclusionsThe proposed combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implant exhibits superior biomechanical stability for severe mandibular atrophy scenarios. By significantly mitigating stress concentrations, particularly in the six-abutment setup, this design presents a viable clinical alternative to overcome common prosthetic rehabilitation challenges.

Introduction

Restoring edentulism via implant-supported prostheses is widely recognized as a predictable and effective treatment modality [1, 2]. Yet, standard protocols often necessitate extensive bone augmentation when addressing severely resorbed mandibles [3]. This requirement poses significant hurdles for the edentulous demographic, predominantly comprising elderly individuals who may be physically compromised. Consequently, such invasive surgeries can undermine long-term success rates [4–6]. While short implants offer an alternative [7], meta-analyses suggest their long-term stability often lags behind standard fixtures [8], posing risks of pathological fractures in atrophic mandibles [9]. Hence, there is a critical need for a rehabilitation strategy that ensures stability while minimizing treatment duration and surgical trauma.

The concept of subperiosteal implants, originally introduced by Gustav Dahl in the 1940s, was specifically aimed at managing severe mandibular atrophy [10]. Unlike endosseous fixtures, these devices sit atop the bone surface, enveloped by the periosteum, offering a less invasive profile. Historically, these customized implants yielded favorable functional outcomes [11–16]. The advent of additively manufactured subperiosteal implants (AMSJI) has largely resolved early fixation issues [13, 16], further enhancing biocompatibility and optimizing the metal framework’s footprint to boost osseointegration [17].

Despite these advancements, AMSJIs are not devoid of complications. Dimitroulis et al. [18] highlighted implant exposure as a prevalent issue in a review of 21 atrophy cases. Moreover, although short-term survival is promising, soft-tissue complications persist [19, 20]. A critical mechanical vulnerability of subperiosteal implants is their instability under lateral forces [21], which often triggers fixation screw (titanium nail) loosening and subsequent micromotion. To mitigate this, our study introduces a “hybrid” design leveraging residual alveolar bone for lateral resistance. We hypothesize that anchoring a root-form intraosseous component into the subperiosteal framework will generate a cross-arch stabilization effect. This design aims to dissipate occlusal forces—especially lateral vectors—more efficiently into the alveolar bone, thereby shielding fixation screws from peak stresses.

The aim of this study was to analyze the biomechanical properties of this combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implant using finite element analysis (FEA) and to establish a reference for the clinical management of patients with severely atrophic edentulous mandibles.

Materials and methods

Construction of geometric models

Four groups of three-dimensional finite element models were established (Fig. 1), considering two parameters: implant design and the number of abutments. The groups included a combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implant with four abutments (CI-4), a combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implant with six abutments (CI-6), an ultra-short implant with four abutments (USI-4), and an ultra-short implant with six abutments (USI-6).

The edentulous mandible was simulated by reconstructing cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) data using commercial modeling software (Mimics 21.0, Materialise Group; Geomagic Wrap 2017, 3D Systems), with a cortical bone thickness of 2 mm, representing cortical bone (Type III) [22]. The prosthetics were designed and generated using exocad DentalCAD 3.1 (Exocad GmbH) and Geomagic Wrap 2017 (3D Systems).

The combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implant included a subperiosteal titanium mesh base, intraosseous implants, abutments, screws, and titanium nails. These components were modeled using UG NX 10.0 software (Siemens PLM Software). The titanium mesh structure was designed to cover the alveolar ridge around the abutment using 3-matic 13.0 software (Materialise Group). A porosity of 65% and a pore size of 0.45 mm were selected for the mesh. These values were chosen based on previous findings [23] indicating that this specific range facilitates optimal nutrient transport and bone tissue ingrowth while maintaining sufficient mechanical strength. A band structure with a 1-mm thickness was designed around the edges of the titanium mesh (Fig. 2). These two components were combined to form the subperiosteal titanium mesh-based structure. To simplify calculations, the titanium nails were designed without threads, and five nails were placed in the buccal region of the mandible (Fig. 1a and b).

For the control groups, ultra-short implants were modeled as generic standard implants with dimensions (length: 4 mm; diameters: 4.1 mm anteriorly and 5.3 mm posteriorly) representative of commercially available systems, using UG NX 10.0 software. The dimensions of the upper screws and abutments were identical to those in the combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implant group, while the lower screws had a length of 6 mm and a diameter of 1.8 mm (Fig. 1c and d).

The models were meshed using 10-node tetrahedral elements (SOLID187) to accommodate the complex geometry of the mandible and implants. A mesh convergence test was conducted by varying the global element size from 0.5 mm to 2.0 mm. The final mesh size was set at 0.8 mm for the implant components and 1.0 mm for the bone, where the variation in peak stress results was less than 5%. The interfaces between the bone and the implants (both ultra-short and combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implants) were defined as “ties” to simulate complete osseointegration [29]. The interfaces between the prosthetic components (prosthesis-screw, prosthesis-abutment, abutment-implant, etc.) were defined as frictional contacts with a friction coefficient (µ) of 0.3 to simulate realistic micromotion [28, 30, 31]. The interfaces comprising the screw-retained mechanism were modeled as ‘bonded’ to simulate tight mechanical locking and preload. Fixed constraints were applied to the bilateral condyles and the lower border of the mandible to prevent rigid body motion and simulate mandibular stability during occlusion [27, 28, 30, 31]. These load magnitudes were selected to simulate realistic clinical scenarios: 100 N and 150 N represent normal physiological mastication forces for implant-supported prostheses, 300 N simulates maximal or parafunctional occlusal forces in edentulous patients, and the 105 N oblique load reflects lateral excursive shear forces [30, 31].

Stress analysis

The FEA process was completed using ANSYS Workbench 19.2 software (Ansys Inc.). Maximum and minimum principal stresses were measured to assess tensile and compressive stresses in the peri-implant bone. While bone remodeling is primarily strain-dependent, a maximum principal stress limit of 140 MPa was utilized as a threshold to evaluate the risk of cortical bone physical failure and compressive overload [24–26]. The von Mises stress was recorded for each implant component. The yield strength of the titanium alloy used was 897 MPa [32]. To ensure long-term clinical safety and minimize the risk of fatigue failure, a safety factor of 2.0 was applied. Therefore, a conservative threshold of 50% of the yield strength (approx. 450 MPa) was used as the design limit to assess biomechanical safety [32].

Figures 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 and 13 depict the stress distributions, and Figs. 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 and 19 illustrate the peak stress values. Comparisons are based on percentage differences in peak stress magnitudes, as statistical significance is not applicable to deterministic FEA. Under unilateral oblique force (105 N) applied to the posterior region, all components reached their maximum stress levels. Both the maximum and minimum principal stresses in the bone remained below the yield strength of 140 MPa. Similarly, the von Mises stresses within each implant component stayed within the material’s allowable limits. However, the abutments and screws in the ultra-short implant groups experienced higher stresses, indicating a greater risk of hardware failure during prolonged use.

Stress in bone

Compressive stress in the bone covered a wider range than tensile stress (Figs. 4 and 5). In the combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implant group, the distribution of tensile stresses was more decentralized compared to the ultra-short implant group, where stress was concentrated at the implant-bone junction. Increasing the number of abutments in the combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implant reduced both tensile and compressive stresses in the surrounding bone during posterior loading (Figs. 14 and 15).

Stress in implants

In the combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implants, stress concentrations were observed at the connection point between the abutment and the subperiosteal titanium mesh base (Figs. 6 and 7). For ultra-short implants, stress was primarily distributed in the cervical region. The peak von Mises stress in the six-abutment design was consistently lower than in the four-abutment design. Specifically, under posterior loading, increasing the number of abutments reduced von Mises stress peaks by approximately 25.5% in the combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implant group and 15.5% in the ultra-short implant group (Fig. 16).

Stress in screws

The von Mises stresses in the screws were mainly concentrated on the unthreaded rods (Figs. 8 and 9). The combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implant groups exhibited lower peak stresses in the screws compared to the ultra-short implant groups across all loading modes. Under posterior loading, increasing the number of abutments reduced stress peaks by an average of 33% in the combined intraosseous-subperiosteal hybrid implant group and 26.3% in the ultra-short implant group (Fig. 17).

Stress in abutments

In the ultra-short implant groups, stresses were mainly distributed at the abutment-implant junction (Figs. 10 and 11), reaching a maximum peak of 400.58 MPa—the highest among all models. Increasing the number of abutments decreased these peaks by an average of 27.5% (Fig. 18).

Stress in titanium nails

As illustrated in the enlarged stress maps (Fig. 12A, B) and the high-magnification stress maps (Fig. 13), the von Mises stress peaks in the titanium nails were located in the peripheral nail regions. Although stress peaks increased with the number of abutments, they remained substantially lower (2.28–10.39 MPa) compared to other implant components (Fig. 19).

Discussion

The utilization of additively manufactured subperiosteal implants has gained traction for treating severe jaw atrophy [12, 13, 24, 26, 33]. Nevertheless, ensuring long-term mechanical stability remains challenging, particularly when lateral forces jeopardize the integrity of fixation screws. Furthermore, traditional designs often suffer from limited osseointegration surface areas [34]. Addressing these pitfalls, our study developed a novel combined intraosseous-subperiosteal implant. By integrating a titanium mesh base with intraosseous fixtures and a screw-retained superstructure, we aimed to optimize stress distribution. Recent systematic reviews and consensus reports emphasize the re-emergence of customized subperiosteal implants as a predictable, patient-specific digital solution for severe jaw atrophy [19, 20, 35]. Driven by advancements in CAD/CAM and additive manufacturing, these graft-less alternatives bypass the morbidity associated with extensive bone augmentation [12, 13]. However, while short-term clinical survivability is promising, ensuring long-term mechanical stability remains challenging, particularly when non-axial lateral forces jeopardize the integrity of fixation screws [21]. To mitigate this specific vulnerability, our mesh incorporates a band structure for rigidity and specific porosity (65%) to facilitate vascularization [36], adhering to optimal osseointegration parameters [17, 37].

To enhance stability under lateral forces—a known weakness of subperiosteal implants [34, 38]—we added an intraosseous component. The superstructure design was inspired by the All-on-4 [39] and All-on-6 [40] concepts. Our results indicate that the new design creates a larger and more uniform contact area with the bone, facilitating improved stress distribution. In contrast, the ultra-short implant group showed stress concentration at the implant neck, a site prone to resorption [41], which aligns with previous findings [41–44].

The highest stress concentrations in the new design were observed at the junction between the mesh base and the abutment, suggesting a potential area for future optimization. The low stress on the titanium nails (2.28–10.39 MPa) is likely attributed to the assumption of 100% osseointegration, which shields the fixation screws from excessive load. It must be noted that in clinical scenarios with partial bone loss or fatigue, this stress shielding might diminish, potentially increasing the load on fixation screws.

Comparative studies, such as Wagner et al. [45], found that stresses in short implant components were higher than in standard implants, consistent with our findings. Generally, a higher crown height-to-implant length ratio increases leverage, resulting in greater forces on the abutment and screw [46]. Our ultra-short implant models (4 mm length) confirmed this, experiencing the highest stresses under oblique loading [47, 48]. Consistent with Pandey et al. [49] and others [50, 51], we found that the six-abutment configuration provided more favorable biomechanical behavior than the four-abutment design by offering more stress transfer pathways.

Clinical implications

This combined implant design offers a ‘graft-less’ solution for elderly patients with Cawood-Howell Class V-VI atrophy. By engaging the residual basal bone via the intraosseous component, it may provide immediate stability that pure subperiosteal implants lack, potentially shortening treatment times. To mitigate the impact of lateral forces, it is advisable to appropriately reduce the inclination of tooth cusps in the final prosthesis [52].

Limitations and future research

This study has limitations. First, the uniform 2 mm cortical bone thickness represents an idealized “best-case” computational scenario. It does not reflect regional anatomical variations; clinically, the posterior atrophic mandible often presents with significantly thinner cortical plates than the anterior region. Consequently, the actual stress concentrations in the posterior bone may be higher than those predicted by our idealized model. Second, material properties were assumed to be isotropic and homogeneous. While variations in bone quality would alter absolute stress values, the relative advantage of the new design is expected to be maintained or amplified in softer bone due to its wider stress distribution. Third, this was a static analysis assuming 100% osseointegration. Future studies will focus on: (1) topology optimization of the mesh structure to minimize stress shielding, (2) dynamic fatigue testing according to ISO 14,801, and (3) in vivo animal studies to assess osseointegration at the intraosseous-subperiosteal junction.

Conclusions

A novel customized combined intraosseous-subperiosteal implant was developed for severely atrophic edentulous mandibles. This design demonstrated superior biomechanical properties compared to ultra-short implants. Notably, the six-abutment configuration reduced von Mises stress on the screws by approximately 33%. This design represents a biomechanically superior alternative for minimizing hardware failure in severe atrophy cases.
