Operationalizing transparency regarding the use of health data for research: a cross-sectional survey of citizens’ perspectives
Authors: Annabelle Cumyn, Jean-Frédéric Ménard, Emmanuel Bilodeau, Roxanne Dault, Jean-Philippe Morissette, Marie-Pier Domingue, Félix Camirand Lemyre, Denis Boutin, Jean-François Ethier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSecondary use of health data can accelerate discovery and enable personalized care, yet social acceptability relies on autonomy, transparency, and trust, especially in the context of security breaches and concerns about misuse. In Quebec, as in many Western settings, there is no formal mechanism to inform individuals about such use, underscoring the need for practical solutions. This quantitative cross-sectional study assessed whether transparency mechanisms increase trust in the use of health data for research; examined their perceived priority and acceptability; and explored effects across population subgroups.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional telephone survey among 366 adults in Quebec (Canada) selected via stratified random sampling. The sample included a random selection of adult household members based on age. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and exploratory multivariate logistic regressions were performed.ResultsMore than half of respondents were unaware that their health data may be used for research. Trust was higher for public-sector than private-sector researchers. Overall, 76% indicated that access to information would increase their trust, particularly younger adults (OR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.10–3.57). Over 90% judged implementing transparency mechanisms within five years as important, with stronger support among those aware of current data use (OR = 4.63, 95% CI: 1.33–16.20). A secure website was considered acceptable by 75% of respondents as a transparency mechanism. Individuals with high trust in public-sector researchers (OR: 2.06, 95% CI: 1.08–3.94) and younger adults (OR: 2.05, 95% CI: 1.13–3.71) were more likely to consider it important. Exploratory analyses suggested that awareness of current data use for research and pre-existing trust predict increased trust in researchers from various public and private organizations if a secure website is implemented.ConclusionProviding information through a transparency mechanism, particularly via a secure website, appears acceptable, valued, and potentially effective for increasing trust in the use of health data for research. Our findings support the feasibility of a web-based transparency portal and suggest tailoring communication to demographic, trust and awareness profiles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28232-0.

Background

Secondary use of health data collected during patient care has an established potential to increase the speed of scientific discovery and implement personalized health care [1–3]. From the patient’s perspectives, there are many concurrent conversations around data sharing and its secondary use soliciting different ethical values such as privacy, autonomy, reciprocity, altruism, etc. [4–8]. While some data sharers emphasize the importance of data altruism as a contribution to the common good, citizens in our recent survey prioritized autonomy when asked : “Is it more important that each person may easily decide the use or not of their medical data in research OR that the people’s medical data be easily accessible in research?” [9]. This importance given to autonomy over common good occurs in a context where numerous studies have shown the importance of building and maintaining public trust [10, 11]. Given significant events of security breaches and data misuse, and a potential deficit in trust, more attention might be given to the risks associated with data sharing rather than the risks of not using the data to improve knowledge and care [12–18].

Given this natural focus on autonomy and an expectation of having some control over one’s data, the right path to sustained social acceptability over heath data reuse has yet to be defined. We posit that knowledge and understanding will be essential factors to support trust (and informed consent where applicable), and respect citizen expectations regarding transparency. This double objective is important as illustrated in a previous survey in which citizens were more inclined to allow secondary use of their data when transparency is there, even when consent is not possible [9].

In Quebec, as in many Western jurisdictions, there is no formal, automated mechanism to inform individuals about the use of their own health data for research purposes. The primary objective of this study was to assess whether providing access to information, through a transparency mechanism, influences trust in the use of health data for research purposes. The secondary objectives focused on how to operationalize this transparency, specifically by examining whether (a) implementing an information-sharing tool should be prioritized within the next five years at the national level, and (b) whether a secure website would serve as an effective method of providing information for the population. An additional exploratory objective was to assess whether such website would increase trust in researchers and for which subgroups of the population.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a cross-sectional telephone survey among French- and English-speaking adults in Quebec selected via stratified random sampling based on age. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and exploratory multivariate logistic regressions were performed. Sociodemographic characteristics collected included age, level of education and health care utilization in the past year.

This survey was conducted as part of a mixed methods study whose aim is to identify more broadly the information to be communicated to the public about research carried out using health data, and the methods for communicating this information.

Participant recruitment and selection

Survey administration was managed by a specialized survey firm [19]. Participants were selected randomly after confirming a valid telephone number, using a two-step process: (1) A non-proportional stratified random sampling of households in three strata (Montreal, Quebec City, and the rest of the province of Quebec), (2) a random selection of an adult household member based on age.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this survey was determined before data collection based on the following main survey question: “Ways could be put in place to inform you about how your health data is used for research. If you had access to information, would it increase your trust in the use of your health data for research?”. A sample size of 365 participants provided sufficient precision to detect a statistically significant difference from p0 = 50% using a 95% Wald-type confidence interval for a sample proportion, with a margin of error of approximately 5.1%.

Survey questionnaire

The survey content was developed in an iterative process by our research team in close collaboration with a research patient-partner (D.B.) and an organization accompanying citizens with lower literacy levels. Questions identified in the survey were based on findings from two scoping review articles previously conducted by our research team on transparency’s impact and public expectations of transparency in the context of health data secondary use [20, 21].

Initially designed in French, the questionnaire was translated in English and validated by an English-speaking plain language expert. The detailed questionnaire is provided in Supplementary Material 1. In the questionnaire and throughout the manuscript, each survey question is labeled as [QX], where X represents a keyword describing the question. Sociodemographic questions are labeled as [SQX].

The survey was initially pre-tested with 10 participants. A team member (R.D.) reviewed recorded interviews to ensure clarity and provide feedback to interviewers. Experienced interviewers then conducted the computer-assisted survey, which was administered to members of the public over a 3-week period between October 25 and November 15, 2023.

Predictors

The choice of predictors for the analyses was made a priori (before accessing the raw data) by the research team based on expert inputs and prior literature [20, 21]. The predictors are derived from survey questions spanning three main dimensions, as detailed below. This section describes these survey questions and how the predictors were generated from them. Variables derived from survey questions are referred to as [VarX] to differentiate them from the raw survey questions denoted [QX], which can be found in Supplementary Material 1.

Respondents’ knowledge of the use of health data for research in Quebec

The predictor [VarKnow] is based on two survey questions [Qknow1; Qknow2] about respondents’ knowledge of the use of health data for research in Quebec. These questions were asked at the beginning of the survey, before giving respondents more information about the current regulatory model for data access. This methodological choice was made to avoid influencing the measure of baseline knowledge and to capture their actual knowledge on the matter. Following these two questions, the survey included a paragraph informing respondents that currently, in Quebec, health data may be used by researchers without their knowledge or consent, so that each respondent would answer all other survey questions on equal footing.

The three categories generated for the predictor [VarKnow] are:

Category 1 (No at [Qknow1]): Individuals who don’t think that their health data is being used for research (reference category);Category 2 (Yes at [Qknow1] and No at [Qknow2]): Individuals who think that their health data is being used for research, but not without their knowledge;Category 3 (Yes at [Qknow1] and Yes at [Qknow2]): Individuals who think that their health data is being used for research, and without their knowledge (current situation in Quebec).

Categories 1 and 2 reflect respondents who held incorrect initial knowledge. In Quebec, observational health studies are permitted to access health data (often deidentified, but not always) without having to inform the individuals concerned, which corresponds to category 3. In practice, two indicator variables were created to integrate this predictor into the analyses. Whenever a respondent preferred not to answer either [Qknow1] or [Qknow2], both values of the indicator variables were set as missing values.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.5.0 (R Core team, released on 2025-04-11) [22].

Descriptive analyses

Descriptive analyses (proportions, percentages and count tables) were applied to report respondents’ answers to questions used for predictors derivation and for predictors themselves, as well as for the following additional sociodemographic characteristics: gender, language most often spoken at home, minority groups (First Nations and visible minorities), regular internet access and previous or current participation in health research studies.

Tests on proportions

To assess the primary and secondary objectives, two-sided chi-squared tests for proportions were performed at a nominal significance level of 5% (no adjustment for multiplicity was undertaken). Corresponding confidence intervals (CI) were reported to see whether they include, or not, the baseline proportion of 50%.

Exploratory regression analyses

Firstly, exploratory multivariate analyses were conducted using logistic regression models to examine associations between derived predictors and the primary and secondary study outcomes. Secondly, to examine the situation where there would be access to a website providing information regarding the use of health data, the same analyses were conducted to assess the association between the predictors and increased trust in researchers from different types of organizations, i.e. exploratory outcomes. Predictors included in the regression models were identified a priori by the research team, as described in methods section Predictors. Details on the models are presented in Supplementary Material 2. The statistical significance of each predictor variable was assessed at a nominal two-sided significance level of 5%. Corresponding CI of odds ratios (OR) are presented through forest plots.

In an effort to avoid introducing bias, multiple imputations by chained equations were used before creating the regression models to account for missing data, assuming data were missing at random [23]. Study outcomes [Qinfo-trust, Qinfo-priority, Qinfo-website, Qinctrust-uni, Qinctrust-gov, Qinctrust-health, Qinctrust-privQc, Qinctrust-privOut] as well as derived predictors [VarKnow, VarAge, VarEdu, VarHcu, VarTrust-Pub, VarTrust-Priv] were all included in the imputation model and were also imputed through logistic regressions.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to, (a) compare the results obtained from the multiple‑imputation datasets with those from a complete‑case analysis, and (b) assess whether the trust‑related variables acted as mediators between the other predictors and the outcomes. Additional details are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Discussion

Principal contributions

Our study offers important insights into public perceptions of transparency in the secondary use of health data for research. This study reaffirms that the role of secondary use of health data has not yet percolated to the general population. Prioritizing the development of a web-based patient portal to provide information about health data use would increase trust and meet citizen expectations regarding transparency. Although our sample was slightly younger and more literate than the general population, respondents showed comparable internet access, supporting the feasibility and relevance of a web‑based transparency portal.

Trust in public‑sector researchers remained high despite limited awareness of current data uses, while trust in private‑sector researchers was noticeably lower, reflecting concerns documented in prior literature. Our study also showed that transparency can meaningfully strengthen trust, particularly among younger adults, highlighting the need for tailored communication strategies that account for sociodemographic differences.

Selected exploratory analyses support a positive impact on trust even towards researchers from private organizations. Because there was an association between awareness of secondary use of health data and trust, providing more knowledge to a greater number of citizens represents an opportunity to create a virtuous cycle.

Respondents’ awareness of the use of health data for research and trust towards researchers

More than half of respondents were unaware that their health data may be used for research purposes, confirming findings from previous studies [26–28]. Despite this lack of awareness, we observed high levels of trust in researchers from public organizations. In contrast, trust in researchers from private organizations was relatively low, a trend consistently reported in the literature. Public organizations generally benefit from greater public trust due to the collective benefits they provide and the non-commercial nature of their research activities. Conversely, private organizations tend to generate significant mistrust, often due to concerns about profit-driven motives, loss of control over the data, and a perceived lack of confidentiality and transparency [28–30].

Our results also reflect public perceptions of academic researchers and the attributes associated with trustworthiness [31], as well as the key role of social positioning in shaping relationships of trust [32]. When health data is used without individuals’ knowledge or consent, trustworthiness shifts from individuals to broader organizations and partnerships, entities whose trust cannot be taken for granted [33]. This reinforces the representativity of respondents regarding these trust and transparency questions.

Transparency: impact on trust

Three-quarters of respondents indicated that their trust in research using their health data would increase if they were provided with information about such use. Interestingly, trust in public or private organizations did not emerge as a significant predictor of this increased trust. However, respondents’ age did. Individuals aged 18 to 44 were more likely to report increased trust if they were provided with information compared with those aged 45 or older. Other studies have also shown that variations in trust depend on sociodemographic characteristics, and that certain age groups, particularly younger individuals, are more likely to increase their trust when transparency efforts are implemented, whereas older individuals tend to show a less pronounced effect [28, 29, 34]. Some studies have further reported that older individuals express more privacy-related concerns than younger individuals [28].

This is an important aspect to consider for the eventual communication campaigns that would surround the launch of a web-based portal where communication strategies might differ based on the targeted age groups.

Transparency mechanisms: priority for implementation

Our study also revealed that over 90% of respondents believe it is important to prioritize the implementation of provincial-wide transparency mechanisms in the coming years to better inform the population about how their health data is used for research purposes. Interestingly, awareness of current research uses of health data emerged as a predictor of the perceived importance of implementing such information mechanisms. This result aligns with previous findings in the literature: when individuals are aware that their health data may be used for research, they tend to express a stronger desire to receive clear and accessible information about these secondary uses, particularly regarding who accesses their data, the intended benefits, potential risks, and the safety measures in place [35, 36].

Transparency mechanisms: a secure website as an acceptable approach

Over 75% of respondents identified the website as an effective way to be informed about the use of their health data for research, noting that most had internet access.

As mentioned earlier, our respondents underscored the importance of having access to information about how their health data is used for research. Once initial strategies are implemented, it will be essential to assess whether they effectively reach citizens with higher information needs and whether they succeed in increasing awareness, understanding, and trust. It will also be important not to assume that transparency will necessarily have positive impacts. Studies indicate that finding the right balance is essential to avoid overwhelming citizens, overusing public resources or inadvertently undermining trust [20].

Our survey also showed that such a website would positively influence trust in researchers from different types of public and private organizations. However, the individuals whose trust would be most impacted by the implementation of this website are those who are already aware of how health data is currently accessed for research in Quebec and those who already have high levels of trust in researchers from public organizations. The website would also have a positive impact on trust in researchers from private organizations among people who already trust this type of organization.

Representativity

Overall, our study sample is comparable to the broader Quebec population in terms of sociodemographic characteristics. However, we observed that our respondents had higher education levels and tended to be slightly younger than the general population. At the same time, internet access was very similar to that of the broader population, which supports the relevance and potential usefulness of web‑based transparency mechanisms such as an online portal.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the sample size in this survey is small and cannot be considered representative of Quebec’s population. However, the goal of this survey was not to achieve representativeness, but rather identifying avenues for exploration in the focus groups planned as part of our research program.

In comparison with previous studies, the response rate for this telephone survey was lower (9.4%) [9]. Polling firms are increasingly favoring web platforms due to feasibility concerns. We opted for a telephone survey, despite its significant higher cost, in order to reach individuals with reading difficulties or lower numerical literacy as pooling exclusively individuals already familiar enough with the web to be part of pooling panels would have been introducing biases and the literature is unclear on the impact of web surveys for certain types of questions remains unclear [37–40].

We used factorial analyses to guide our choice of scenario to reduce dimensionality. Alternative analysis tools could have been used to achieve similar results, such as polychoric principal component analysis or multiple correspondence analysis.

For our regression analyses, we used multiple imputations to address missing values, based on the assumption that the data was missing at random, noting that one of the survey questions used as predictor had a percentage of missing information above 12%. While it is possible that the missing values were not missing at random, this assumption is inherently untestable and should be acknowledged as a limitation. Complete-case analyses presented in Supplementary Material 2 led to results similar to those obtained with multiple imputations.

Conclusion

Our results reveal strong support for the short-term implementation of transparency mechanisms. A secure website was perceived as an acceptable method for providing information about the use of health data for research, and our findings suggest that it could enhance trust across different types of research organizations, especially among individuals who are already somewhat informed or trusting. Taken together, these findings underscore both the public demand and practical viability of developing a secure online transparency portal, while also emphasizing the value of initially targeting specific individual profiles in implementation efforts.
