Multi‐Omics Analysis Reveals Differentially Expressed Proteins and Metabolites in Malignant Pleural Effusion
Authors: Senlin Xue, Jinhong Huang, Zhicheng Zhang, Haixia Ma, Ying Liu, Xiufeng Zhou, Zhenming Sun, Zhenhua You, Tingwang Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMalignant pleural effusion (MPE) is a common manifestation in advanced malignancies. However, its differentiation from benign pleural effusions, such as parapneumonic effusion (PPE) and tuberculous pleural effusion (TPE), remains challenging. This study aimed to profile differentially expressed proteins and metabolites between MPE, PPE, and TPE.MethodsPleural effusion samples from patients with MPE, PPE, and TPE were collected and subjected to proteomic and metabolomic profiling. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) and differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) were identified, followed by functional enrichment analyzes. Integrated proteome–metabolome analysis was performed to construct protein–metabolite interaction networks and reveal key pathways associated with malignant and benign pleural effusions.ResultsProteomic and metabolomic profiles differed significantly between MPE and non‐MPE. Arginine‐proline and glutamate metabolism were commonly enriched at both protein and metabolite levels in MPE versus PPE and TPE. At the protein level, Isocitrate Dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1) was significantly upregulated in MPE compared with both PPE and TPE. At the metabolite level, sarcosine and l‐glutamine were markedly elevated, while creatine was significantly decreased in MPE. Integrated analysis further indicated that these four molecules play important roles in distinguishing MPE from PPE and TPE.ConclusionCombined proteomic and metabolomic analyzes revealed the critical involvement of IDH1 in glutamate metabolism and arginine and proline metabolic pathways during MPE metabolic reprogramming. Identified molecules, including IDH1, l‐glutamine, creatine, and sarcosine, may serve as potential diagnostic biomarkers for differentiating malignant from benign pleural effusions.

Introduction

Pleural effusion (PE), characterized by the abnormal buildup of fluid in the pleural cavity, is a common clinical sign, with about 3000 cases of pleural disease per million people worldwide. It can be broadly divided into benign and malignant types based on the underlying cause [1, 2, 3]. Malignant pleural effusion (MPE), caused by tumor growth, is a frequent and severe complication of advanced malignancies. Despite its substantial clinical significance, there is no standardized therapeutic strategy for MPE [4]. Patients with MPE have a poor prognosis, with a median survival of only 4–7 months, and often experience recurrent effusions that lead to severe shortness of breath and reduced quality of life [5, 6]. In contrast, benign pleural effusion (BPE) can result from various nonmalignant conditions, such as severe pneumonia, tuberculosis, or congestive heart failure [7]. The management of pleural effusion primarily aims to relieve symptoms and treat the underlying cause. Treatment options vary depending on the cause, severity, and clinical condition [8]. Since PE impairs respiratory function and decreases patients' quality of life, early and accurate distinction between BPE and MPE is crucial for guiding treatment decisions and improving outcomes [2, 9].

Thoracentesis with cytological analysis of the pleural fluid remains the primary laboratory test for pleural effusion. While this method exhibits high specificity, the diagnostic sensitivity is often limited to around 40%, and MPE can only be confirmed when malignant cells are detected [10]. For patients with a negative cytology result but a strong clinical suspicion of MPE, pleural biopsy is recommended to improve diagnostic accuracy. However, this procedure is invasive and is associated with potential complications, including bleeding, infection, and pneumothorax [11]. Although imaging modalities such as chest X‐ray, CT, PET‐CT, and ultrasound can be used to assess effusion volume and anatomical distribution, their sensitivity and specificity in differentiating between benign and malignant effusions remain suboptimal [12]. In addition, although several tumor markers have been explored for diagnostic purposes, their specificity is inadequate to meet clinical demands [13]. Therefore, reliable and minimally invasive biomarkers are urgently needed to distinguish BPE from MPE accurately.

Metabolomics systematically profiles small‐molecule metabolites in biological samples. This approach helps establish links between metabolites and phenotypes and characterize the overall metabolic state of biological fluids, thereby facilitating the discovery of disease‐related biomarkers [14, 15, 16]. Increasing evidence highlights its critical role in tumor biomarker discovery, cancer classification, and the differentiation between benign and malignant tumors [17, 18, 19]. In parallel, proteomics, the large‐scale study of protein composition and expression in cells, tissues, and organisms [20], has arisen as a valuable tool for diagnosing diseases and characterizing tumors [21]. By revealing differences in protein expression among tumor subtypes, proteomics provides new opportunities to distinguish between benign and malignant ovarian tumors, and to elucidate tumor heterogeneity in salivary gland neoplasms [21, 22]. Despite these advances, the single‐omics approach only provides a partial view of the complex molecular mechanisms driving disease. Studies demonstrate that integrated multiomics strategies substantially enhance predictive accuracy [23]. Integrating metabolomics and proteomics offers a promising approach to characterize BPE and MPE, and to systematically understand their underlying biochemical and pathophysiological processes.

In this study, we applied untargeted metabolomics combined with data‐independent acquisition (DIA) proteomics to characterize BPE and MPE. We systematically analyzed differentially expressed proteins and metabolites, along with their associated biological functions and networks, to identify potential targets for improving diagnostic strategies.

Methods

Study Design

To investigate the proteomic and metabolomic profiles of benign and malignant pleural effusions, samples were collected from patients in the Department of Respiratory Medicine. The diagnostic confirmation for MPE relied on pleural fluid cytology, thoracoscopy, and pleural biopsy. For this study, both parapneumonic and tuberculous pleural effusions were categorized as BPE. BPE was identified based on clinical characteristics and response to treatment. The diagnosis of parapneumonic effusion (PPE) was made using different methods. These included imaging findings, microbiological testing, pleural biopsy, and clinical response to antibiotic therapy similar to what has been recommended [24]. Tuberculous pleural effusion (TPE) was confirmed based on pleural biopsy, Ziehl–Neelsen staining, Mycobacterium tuberculosis culture, and response to anti‐tuberculosis therapy.

This study included eight patients with MPE in the experimental group, seven patients with PPE, and eight with TPE in the control group. Baseline clinical data were collected from all participants. These baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. The Ethics Committee of Affiliated Changshu Hospital of Nantong University approved this study (Approval No. 2025‐03‐005‐H01). Additionally, we procured written informed consent from every participant or their legal guardians.

Specimen Collection

Pleural effusion samples were collected at the time of patient enrollment, before the initiation of in‐hospital treatment. Samples were placed in anticoagulant‐free tubes and centrifuged at 1800×g (10 min) to obtain the supernatant, which was promptly kept at −80°C for subsequent multiomics analysis.

Proteomic Detection and Analysis

Proteomic profiling was performed using DIA‐based proteomics. All experiments were conducted by Nanjing Paisenon Biotechnology Co. Ltd. Briefly, PE samples from MPE, PPE, and TPE were lysed using SDT buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.6) for total protein extraction, followed by protein quantification using the BCA assay. Protein quality assessment and spectral library construction were subsequently performed. Samples were digested using the filter‐aided sample preparation (FASP) method with trypsin. The peptide segments were fractionated by high pH reversed‐phase peptide separation kit and desalted using C18 cartridges prior to subsequent analysis.

Both data‐dependent acquisition (DDA) and DIA mass spectrometry analyzes were performed using an Evosep One nano‐LC system coupled with a timsTOF mass spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). DDA mode was used for spectral library generation, whereas DIA mode was applied for protein quantification. Mass spectrometry was operated in positive ion mode with an MS/MS scan range of 100–1700 m/z and an accumulation time of 100 ms per window. DIA data were acquired using the DIA‐PASEF mode and processed using Spectronaut software (Spectronaut 14.4.200727.47784) for protein identification and quantification. Protein identification was performed with the UniProt database, and all identified proteins were filtered with a q value < 0.01.

Untargeted Metabolomics From Pleural Effusion by LC–MS and Analysis

Untargeted metabolomic profiling was conducted to characterize the metabolic composition of pleural effusion samples. The experimental procedures were performed by Nanjing Paisenon Biotechnology Co. Ltd. In brief, the pleural effusion samples were thawed on ice and prepared sequentially according to the MPE, PPE, and TPE. The metabolites were extracted using a solution of acetone, methanol, and acetonitrile (1:1:1, v/v/v), evaporated under nitrogen, and reconstituted in methanol (50%) before analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 A, 1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) maintained at 40°C. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile), with the following gradient: 0–1 min, 5% B; 1–7 min, 5%–95% B; 7–8 min, 95% B; 8.1–12 min, 5% B. The flow rate was set at 0.4 mL/min with an injection volume of 2 μL. Mass spectrometry analysis was performed on a Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 120 equipped with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) ion source in both positive and negative ion modes. Full scan spectra were acquired over an m/z range of 100–1000 at a resolution of 60 000. DDA was applied, selecting the top 4 precursor ions for higher‐energy collisional dissociation (HCD) fragmentation at 30% collision energy, with a dynamic exclusion of 8 s. Raw data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for peak detection, alignment, and gap filling. Metabolite identification was achieved by matching against multiple databases, and data normalization was performed based on total peak area to reduce systematic variation.

Clinical Data

Baseline clinical and pleural fluid characteristics of patients were presented in Table 1, including total protein, adenosine deaminase (ADA), white blood cell (WBC) count, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), glucose, and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), which were measured using pleural effusion samples on the Beckman AU5831 and ADVIA 2400 platforms. Clinicopathological information was additionally collected from the participants' medical records.

Proteomic and Metabolomic Bioinformatics

All bioinformatics analyzes were conducted in R (≥ 4.1.0). Data were normalized, batch‐corrected, and subjected to quality control. Differentially expressed Proteins(DEPs) were identified using the limma R package (p < 0.05, a 1.5‐fold increase or decrease). Our bioinformatic investigation encompassed Gene Ontology (GO) classification (covering biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis, and Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) network construction. PPI networks were constructed using the STRING database (https://string‐db.org/). The network was generated using the “full STRING network” option with evidence‐based edges, and the minimum required interaction score was set to medium confidence (0.400), according to the default STRING settings. Core nodes within the PPI network were identified primarily based on node degree, with proteins showing higher connectivity considered hub proteins. Differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) were identified using DESeq2 (p < 0.05, |log2FC| ≥ 1), followed by KEGG. The integrative analysis of proteomics and metabolomics was based on overlapping or closely related KEGG‐enriched pathways. Proteins and metabolites were simultaneously mapped onto KEGG pathway maps to visualize enzyme‐substrate relationships and construct a joint protein and metabolite network. This comprehensive approach allowed identification of key pathways perturbed at both the protein and metabolite levels.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyzes for clinical characteristics were performed using SPSS Statistics software. Clinical data are expressed as the median (interquartile range, IQR) or as counts (percentages). Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using ANOVA. A p‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Patient Data

Table 1 details the clinical profiles of the three patient groups. Significant differences were observed in sex distribution, age, and pleural fluid biochemical parameters among the groups. The proportion of male patients was higher in the PPE group [6 (85.7%)] than in the TPE [1 (12.5%)] and MPE [2 (25.0%)] groups (p = 0.01). Patients with TPE were younger [32.0 (31.0, 70.0) years] than those with PPE [67.0 (44.0, 76.0) years] or MPE [75.0 (72.0, 80.0) years] (p = 0.02). Pleural fluid WBC counts were significantly elevated in TPE [2700.0 (1988.0, 4000.0) × 106/mL] compared with PPE and MPE (p = 0.02). The TPE group exhibited significantly higher proportions of mononuclear cells (MNCs), whereas the PPE group showed higher proportions of polymorphonuclear cells (PMNs) (p < 0.01). LDH and total protein levels were also significantly different among the groups (p = 0.03 for both). ADA levels were markedly increased in TPE [50.0 (44.0, 60.0) U/L] relative to PPE and MPE (p = 0.01). Pleural fluid glucose concentrations showed intergroup variability (p = 0.03). Notably, CEA levels were strikingly elevated in the MPE group [803.75 (162.6, 1000.0) ng/mL], whereas they remained low in PPE and TPE (p < 0.01).

Proteomic Profiling and Functional Enrichment Analysis of MPE Versus PPE

Proteomic profiling revealed distinct DEPs between MPE and PPE. Compared with PPE, a total of 301 DEPs were identified, of which 160 were upregulated and 141 were downregulated in MPE (Figure 1A). GO analysis indicated that these proteins were predominantly involved in immune and inflammatory processes. Specifically, they were significantly enriched in biological processes including acute‐phase response (GO:0006953), defense response to Gram‐positive bacterium (GO:0050830), immune response (GO:0006955), defense response to Gram‐negative bacterium (GO:0050829), defense response to bacterium (GO:0042742), complement activation, classical pathway (GO:0006958), and symbiont entry into host cell (GO:0046718) (Figure 1B). These findings suggest that differences in the host's acute immune and inflammatory responses play a pivotal role in distinguishing MPE from PPE. KEGG analysis further revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to the biosynthesis of amino acids (Figure 1C). To explore the potential interactions among these differentially expressed proteins in the biosynthesis of the amino acid‐related pathway, a PPI network was constructed (Figure 1D), highlighting several hub proteins (e.g., IDH1, GOT2, ALDOB, MAT2B, ENO2, PFKP) that may be critical mediators in the pathophysiological differences between MPE and PPE.

Proteomic Analysis of MPE Versus TPE

Additionally, proteomic analysis revealed distinct differences between MPE and TPE. Compared with TPE, 351 proteins were differentially expressed, including 217 upregulated and 134 downregulated in MPE (Figure 2A). GO analysis indicated that these proteins were mainly associated with innate immune and antibacterial responses. The enriched pathways included innate immune response (GO:0045087), defense response to Gram‐negative bacterium (GO:0050829), defense response to bacterium (GO:0042742), cell adhesion (GO:0007155), acute‐phase response (GO:0006953), defense response to Gram‐positive bacterium (GO:0050830), antimicrobial humoral immune response (GO:0061844), and cell–cell adhesion (GO:0098609) (Figure 2B). These findings suggest that alterations in innate immunity, specific antibacterial defenses, and intercellular interactions may play key roles in differentiating MPE from TPE. KEGG pathway analysis further identified enrichment in glutathione metabolism (Figure 2C). A PPI network was constructed for proteins involved in this glutathione metabolism‐related pathway, highlighting several hub proteins (e.g., GGCT, GSTP1, IDH1, LANCL1, IDH2, LAP3) potentially critical for the metabolic differences between MPE and TPE (Figure 2D).

Metabolomic Analysis of MPE Versus PPE and TPE

Multivariate statistical analysis demonstrated clear separation between MPE and PPE groups in the orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS‐DA) model (Figure 3A). Compared with PPE, a total of 72 differential metabolites were detected in MPE. Among these, 38 were upregulated and 34 were downregulated (Figure 3B). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed significant involvement of multiple pathways, particularly amino acid metabolism–related pathways such as alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, as well as glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism (Figure 3C), suggesting that alterations in amino acid metabolism and glutathione biosynthesis may contribute to the metabolic differences between MPE and PPE.

Integrated Proteomic and Metabolomic Analysis of MPE Versus PPE and TPE

An integrated proteomic and metabolomic analysis of MPE compared with PPE and TPE was performed to investigate their connections. Based on differentially expressed proteins and metabolites, KEGG pathway enrichment, functional annotation, and PPI network analysis, we identified overlapping pathways between MPE versus PPE and MPE versus TPE. Notably, hsa00330:Arginine and proline metabolism emerged as a shared metabolic pathway, with critical metabolites (e.g., l‐Glutamine, sarcosine) and the regulatory protein IDH1 serving as central nodes. These alterations converged on the biosynthesis of amino acids, which is further connected to Arginine and proline metabolism and glutamate metabolism through l‐glutamine flux. Correlation analysis further demonstrated that pleural fluid CEA was positively correlated with IDH1 (r = 0.67, p < 0.01), suggesting a potential association between conventional tumor biomarkers and the metabolic reprogramming‐related key proteins identified in this study (Figure S1). Collectively, our findings highlight the potential diagnostic role of IDH1 in distinguishing MPE from non‐MPE. Discussion

PE is classified as benign or malignant depending on whether malignant tumor cells are detected. BPE often results from PPE, TPE, heart failure, or pulmonary embolism, while MPE is mainly linked to lung or breast cancer [25]. Each type of pleural effusion reflects the characteristics of the underlying disease, providing important diagnostic information and guiding therapeutic strategies. Omic Technologies is accelerating the discovery of new drug targets and markers [26]. In this research, we profiled the metabolome and proteome of BPE and MPE samples to identify characteristic DAMs and DEPs. Furthermore, we explored the key pathways associated with these DAMs and DEPs, which may ultimately offer novel insights into the differential diagnosis of MPE and non‐MPE.

Proteomics is the systematic analysis involving all the proteins expressed by cells, tissues, or organisms under specific conditions. Comparing protein expression in cancerous tissues or body fluids with that in normal controls, it enables the identification of novel protein biomarkers linked to tumor initiation and progression [27, 28, 29]. In our proteomic analysis, proteins differentially expressed between MPE and PPE were primarily enriched in immunity and inflammation‐related pathways. Meanwhile, the DEPs between MPE and TPE were predominantly enriched in pathways related to the innate immune response. These findings suggest that alterations in immunity may play a pivotal role in the differential diagnosis of MPE and BPE. Consistently, a previous study identified 38 upregulated and 29 downregulated proteins in PPE, with enhanced glucose and amino acid metabolism. Meanwhile, protein polymerization was attenuated in complicated PPE [30]. Another proteomic study compared 20 lung adenocarcinoma‐associated MPE with 30 BPE. GO analysis revealed that extracellular proteins were the most significant cellular component, while KEGG analysis highlighted the involvement of the cell adhesion molecule pathway [31]. Taken together, the differential enrichment of the immune profile shifts revealed by proteomic analysis delineates the distinct protein signatures of various types of pleural effusions.

Metabolomics aims to comprehensively analyze all low molecular weight metabolites (< 1500 Da) within a given system at a specific time point, thereby directly reflecting the physiological and pathological state [32]. In the present study, we observed significant common alterations in metabolic pathways across different types of PE, including MPE, PPE, and TPE. Specifically, differential metabolites between MPE and PPE were predominantly enriched in pathways associated with amino acid metabolism. These findings suggest that alterations in amino acid metabolism, particularly concerning arginine and glutamate, may be crucial for distinguishing between MPE and PPE. On the other hand, for the MPE and TPE, the differential metabolites were mainly enriched in hsa00330: Arginine and proline metabolism, and hsa00380: Tryptophan metabolism. This suggests that the rapid proliferation of malignant tumor cells increases the demand for amino acids, leading to differences in their metabolic patterns compared to TPE. Among them, the metabolism of arginine and glutamate may be an important indicator. Previous studies have revealed significant disruptions in pyruvate metabolism, glycolysis, and lysine degradation when comparing metabolite profiles of MPE with those of nonmalignant effusions [33]. In another study of 58 pleural effusion samples, 26 differential metabolites were identified among malignant, exudative, and tuberculous effusions, primarily involved in amino acid, carbohydrate, and lipid metabolism. Notably, methionine and acetic acid levels were significantly elevated in MPE [32]. Collectively, these metabolic alterations reflect disease‐specific metabolic reprogramming and may provide signatures for the differential diagnosis of pleural effusions.

In the joint analysis and exploration of proteomics and metabolomics of MPE, PPE, and TPE, the key role of amino acid metabolic pathways, especially arginine biosynthesis and glutathione metabolism, was emphasized. Previous comparative multi‐omics analysis of MPE and PPE further emphasized the centrality of amino acid biosynthesis and arginine metabolism [34]. One key enzyme in amino acid metabolism is isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1). IDH1 is highly expressed in metastatic breast cancer and glioblastoma (GBM). In addition, inhibition of mutant IDH1 reduces the production of the oncometabolite 2‐hydroxyglutarate (2‐HG), thereby suppressing tumor growth and invasion and prolonging progression‐free survival [35, 36, 37]. Glutamic‐oxaloacetic transaminase 2 (GOT2) mediates the interconversion of aspartate and glutamate, thereby sustaining glycolysis and promoting rapid tumor cell growth [38], which also plays a central role in glutamine metabolism reprogramming [39]. Aldolase B (ALDOB), enolase 2 (ENO2), and phosphofructokinase platelet type (PFKP) are core glycolytic enzymes, consistent with the Warburg effect, whereby tumor cells preferentially rely on glycolysis rather than oxidative phosphorylation for energy production, even under aerobic conditions [40]. The enrichment of l‐glutamine in the arginine biosynthesis pathway suggests that tumor cells upregulate glutamine metabolism to supply precursors for arginine synthesis, supporting increased protein synthesis and proliferation [41]. Together, these findings indicate that glutamate‐driven arginine and proline metabolism or arginine biosynthesis may represent a critical determinant for distinguishing MPE from PPE.

When the MPE and TPE were compared, it was found that DEPs were predominantly enriched in the glutathione metabolism pathway. Glutathione, a key intracellular antioxidant that maintains redox homeostasis, detoxification, and immune regulation, is upregulated in tumor cells under oxidative stress to counteract Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) damage and promote proliferation and therapeutic resistance [42]. Gamma‐glutamyl cyclotransferase (GGCT) is involved in glutathione catabolism, and its dysregulation contributes to tumor progression by modulating the cell cycle, proliferation, migration, and epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) [43, 44]. Glutathione S‐transferase P1 (GSTP1) primarily participates in the detoxification of intracellular toxic compounds, including various chemotherapeutic agents. Elevated GSTP1 expression is often associated with chemotherapy resistance [40]. In addition to amino acid biosynthesis, IDH1 and IDH2 generate NADPH, which is essential for glutathione reductase [45]. The enrichment of IDH1 and IDH2 in glutathione metabolism implies that enhanced NADPH production may help maintain reduced glutathione, thereby facilitating tumor adaptation to oxidative stress and contributing to disease progression. This mechanism may represent a key differentiating point among MPE, PPE, and TPE, reflecting how tumor cells reprogram metabolic pathways, particularly amino acid metabolism, to support rapid proliferation and survival [46, 47]. Previous studies have found that significant alterations in arginine and proline metabolism between MPE and TPE suggest that tumor cells may promote proliferation by upregulating arginase, driving arginine breakdown into urea and ornithine [48]. Taken together, our integrated proteomic and metabolomic analyzes indicate that alterations in glutamate‐associated arginine metabolism, potentially involving proteins such as IDH1, distinguish MPE from BPE and may provide insights into potential diagnostic and therapeutic targets.

Proteomic analysis revealed significant enrichment of immune and inflammation‐related pathways between MPE and BPE. Meanwhile, metabolomic analysis showed enrichment of arginine and proline metabolism as well as glutathione metabolism pathways. These findings suggest a potential interaction between metabolic reprogramming and immune regulation within the MPE microenvironment. On the one hand, some tumor cells undergo glutamine metabolic reprogramming to support energy production and the biosynthesis of other amino acids, thereby sustaining rapid proliferation and redox homeostasis [49, 50]. On the other hand, this altered glutamine metabolism may directly impair the function of immune cells, including CD8+ T cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages [51]. In addition, some tumors enhance arginine uptake through upregulation of arginine transporters. Elevated intracellular arginine may function as a “second messenger” like molecule to regulate downstream metabolic gene expression, thereby promoting global metabolic reprogramming involving glucose, amino acids, nucleotides, and fatty acids to support tumor growth and proliferation. Meanwhile, excessive arginine consumption within the tumor microenvironment may contribute to CD8+ T cell dysfunction [52]. Collectively, these findings suggest that tumor cells may promote their own growth and survival through glutamate metabolism and arginine and proline metabolic reprogramming, while simultaneously suppressing immune and inflammatory responses within the pleural microenvironment, thereby creating a feedback loop that further facilitates tumor progression.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small, and the complexity and heterogeneity of PE, affected by individual variability, comorbidities, and prior treatments, may introduce confounding effects. Second, this study primarily focused on exudative pleural effusions, including MPE, PPE, and TPE, without inclusion of normal pleural fluid or transudative effusions. Although this design reflects a clinically relevant diagnostic scenario, the absence of these comparison groups may restrict a more comprehensive evaluation. In addition, the present study was designed as an exploratory, discovery phase investigation, and the identified proteomic and metabolomic markers still require validation in large‐scale, independent, and multicenter cohorts before clinical application.

Conclusions

In summary, our integrated proteomic and metabolomic analyzes revealed distinct molecular signatures between MPE and BPE. The consistent enrichment of arginine and proline metabolism and glutamate metabolism, along with the upregulation of IDH1, L‐glutamine, and sarcosine, and the downregulation of creatine suggests a pivotal role of metabolic reprogramming in malignant pleural effusion. These findings provide promising candidates for future diagnostic biomarker development and therapeutic intervention.
