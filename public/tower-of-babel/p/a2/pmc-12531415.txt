Fair allocation of telemedical counseling services in early detection of Alzheimer's disease: Empirically informed ethical considerations
Authors: Felix Roth, Silke Schicktanz, Julia Perry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONCounseling for early Alzheimer's disease (AD) detection is essential, especially regarding emerging blood‐based biomarkers. The increasing need for counseling requires innovative approaches and simultaneously consideration of ethical issues.ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONSTelemedicine is discussed as a means of providing more accessible and fairer health care. Nevertheless, barriers to accessing telemedicine are important to consider, such as required technical hardware and ethical criteria regarding interpersonal counseling, including individual adaptation of information to ensure self‐determined decision‐making. Regarding resource allocation, issues must be considered before telemedicine is implemented.THEORETICAL REFLECTIONFair counseling structures require the discussion of resource allocation. To avoid justifying telemedical counseling based solely on cost‐effectiveness and overlooking further ethical demands, we propose a ranked approach. To strengthening self‐determined decisions, we argue that equal care structures can be built on these prerequisite aspects and enable realization of fair resource allocation as a last step.Highlights Blood‐based biomarkers in Alzheimer's disease (AD) facilitate easier access to risk assessment and early detection. Currently, insufficient pre‐diagnostic counseling structures exist which support informed decision‐making. Telemedicine may be a meaningful approach for innovative counseling services. However, unresolved ethical and legal issues regarding telemedical counseling services for the early detection of AD must first be addressed.Telemedical counseling in the early detection of AD is yet rarely discussed in the literature. Therefore, we combine insights from ethical analysis of telemedical counseling with the ethical issues of fair and empowering counseling in the early detection of AD separately. In a second step, we discuss the use of telemedical counseling for the early detection of AD inductively to highlight ethically relevant aspects and present our considerations in light of the principles of autonomy, non‐maleficence, beneficence, and justice.To provide ethical guidance for possible future implementation without overprioritizing one approach to counseling for the early detection of AD, considerations regarding fair resource allocation are required. We argue that three major ethical topics should be considered in the future: Strengthening individuals’ autonomy, equal care structures, and fair resource allocation. Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, early detection, ethics, fair resource allocation, telemedicine

EXPENDING POSSIBILITIES THROUGH TELEMEDICAL COUNSELING STRUCTURES IN EARLY ALZHEIMER'S DISEASE DETECTION

Today, the assessment of biomarkers is part of the diagnostic process for Alzheimer's disease (AD). In addition to a symptomatic assessment using psychological tests (e.g., cognitive impairment tests) and physiological tests (e.g., structural brain imaging), biomarkers determined from cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or positron emission tomography (PET) scans are used. 1 Biomarkers highlight the reconceptualization of AD as a continuous, degenerative disease and shift the focus from symptomatic dementia detection, to the importance of early detection based on biological assessment. 2 While biomarker testing using CSF or amyloid PET scans is limited due to availability and cost issues, some expect that using blood‐based biomarkers as a cheap and noninvasive method, for example, as “entry point” into more detailed assessment is likely to be implemented in clinical practice soon. 1 , 3 , 4 Currently, the first blood‐based biomarker tests for the early detection of AD have been FDA approved and the implementation in other countries is also likely. 5

This new kind of early detection, which is able to determine high risk for developing symptoms has been subject of intense ethical and practical debate, especially regarding how counseling and communication of related information should be offered when such tests are implemented. 6 However, the introduction of disease‐modifying therapies such as lecanemab and clinical trials in preclinical individuals will likely increase the interest in early detection. 7 Therefore, pre‐diagnostic counseling is also becoming increasingly important as an ethically required aspect to make informed decisions. 8 To meet the requirements for counseling in the context of early AD detection, counseling should include essential information about AD in general, the early detection process, and the reliability of risk assessment results thus providing individuals with derived steps of action. 8 Available recommendations for early AD counseling address necessary aspects such as weighing of risks and benefits and often address settings only available in specialized memory clinics. 9 As in many other countries, specialized memory clinics in Germany are not widespread leading to varying accessibility depending on the region and to long waiting times due to a growing number of patients. To address these issues, telemedical services have been proposed as a meaningful approach and are being discussed for counseling services in the early detection of AD. 9 , 10 , 11 Telemedicine is an umbrella term for medical activities that are offered across space and/or time with the help of telecommunication systems. 12 It has the potential to improve patient care and integrate underserved areas. 13 However, telemedical counseling for dementia care has not yet been widely adopted. Furthermore, issues remain unsolved regarding how it differs from interpersonal counseling and whether this difference can harm physician‐patient‐communication. Therefore, we raise the following questions:

1. What aspects should be considered for ethical feasible telemedical counseling for early AD detection?

2. What elements should be considered to achieve fair resource allocation in the implementation of telemedical approaches to enable best‐practice counseling in the early detection of AD?

To preliminarily answer these questions, we develop a conceptual ethical perspective, that also considers empirical insights from relevant stakeholders, especially patients and caregivers. This perspective addresses the conditions under which telemedicine can or should be introduced to support counseling services for the early detection of AD and has the potential to influence its further development. 14

To inform our ethical‐theoretical reflection, we reviewed relevant literature to identify ethical aspects. To ensure the relevance of our ethical‐theoretical reflection from a practical perspective involving stakeholders, we analyzed evaluative interviews with users of a telemedical counseling service for the early detection of AD within a pilot project to learn about users’ expectations, and from users’ experiences.

Ethical background

For our ethical analysis, we refer to Beauchamp and Childress's 15 four principles approach. This approach of middle range principles focuses on autonomy, non‐maleficence, beneficence, and justice. These four principles serve as a justifiable, pragmatic, and transparent way of reflecting medical applications in action in many fields of medicine. 6 Since the analysis along these principles allows us to detect major conflicts (e.g., between autonomy and justice) we use it as heuristic approach here. Based on this analysis, a future step will be to develop a concrete ethical framework for practical application. Here, we focus on a detailed analysis of ethical issues that must be addressed before telemedical counseling in early detection of AD can be implemented. A special focus should be on fairness regarding the allocation of resources as argued in this article. To apply Beauchamp and Childress's broad four principles approach to the context of fair resource allocation, we argue for the integration of four additional principles discussed for resource allocation in telecare for the elderly by Nordgren 16 to guide our ethical reflections: cost‐effectiveness, needs and solidarity, self‐responsibility, and human dignity. We understand the principles of cost‐effectiveness and needs and solidarity as an extension of the principle of justice. The principle of self‐responsibility is considered a limitation within the principle of autonomy and the principle of human dignity is discussed as a further extension of the principle of autonomy. Our approach is not considered definitive and must be specified and interpreted in detail for different healthcare context.

CURRENT DEBATE ON TELEMEDICAL COUNSELING FOR THE EARLY DETECTION OF AD

Based on a literature review (see Supporting Information ‐ Methods A: Literature Review) we identified 34 publications of which only two concretely address telemedical approaches to counseling for the early detection of AD. Perry and colleagues 11 examined the insufficiently addressed needs of individuals as well as their expectations of a personalized communication approach in Germany. Schwegler and colleagues 17 discuss relevant aspects of predementia counseling and risk prediction, only briefly mentioning digital media (e.g., the Internet) as a source of knowledge. To gain a more detailed understanding of ethical issues, we employed an inductive approach and reviewed existing literature on telemedical counseling (2.1) and counseling for the early detection of AD (2.2) separately to discuss them jointly in a second stage.

Ethical issues discussed for telemedical counseling services

Telemedicine has been discussed as a means of providing easier access to healthcare for a broader population, including overcoming existing infrastructural limitations in underserved regions. 18 , 19 , 20 It may also reduce the number of appointments at medical facilities if individuals can receive counseling at home, 11 reduces travel time to the next specialist, 21 , 22 and allows for flexibility in timing and location of counseling for professionals and patients alike. 21 , 22 , 23 These changes might reduce costs as part of a rationalization process without rationing services.

Regarding supply structures, concerns about equal access to telemedicine have been raised. For example, implementing telemedicine requires technical hardware and expertise from both, professionals, and patients. 23 , 24 This can lead to access barriers, especially for marginalized groups or minorities, who generally have more limited access to telemedical services. 20 , 22 , 25 Furthermore, older adults, who are generally considered to have less digital literacy, could be excluded from digital counseling. Therefore, when implementing telemedical counseling, equal access to the target group should be ensured, for example, by technical skill development or the choice of technical hardware. 21 , 26

Like interpersonal counseling, telemedical counseling aims to address individual information needs and, ideally, achieve empowerment. 18 , 21 , 27 , 28 Empowerment via counseling is seen as a process to support individuals’ autonomous decisions for obtaining risk information. Ensuring access to information about how knowledge is limited (e.g., as risk information) and the comprehension of risk information are essential parts to strengthen self‐determined decisions. 28 Telemedicine can substantially impair one's ability to understand information due to technical issues and disruptions during consultations, which can result in information loss. 22 , 23 , 29 If the understanding is limited to a crucial point, misunderstandings can occur, which are a challenge for self‐determined decisions. 23

Hence, regarding non‐maleficence, professionals have been found to be responsible for ensuring the quality of telemedical services and minimizing potential ethical issues. 21 , 27 , 30 Therefore, training for professionals is also required. 19 Guidelines have been proposed to ensure quality and standardized telemedical counseling. 23 These guidelines should address data security to ensure the protection of personal data according to data protection laws. 19 , 27 Without data protection, trust in telemedical counseling is at risk. 15 For example, insurance companies may be interested to know whether individuals have received counseling on early AD detection. Commercial counseling services could sell this personal information. Data protection concerns underscore the need to establish a sound relationship of trust between (telemedical) professionals and help‐seeking individuals. 23 , 31 However, building trust through virtual communication seems more difficult than through interpersonal contact 23 and can lead to depersonalization which even can result in an increased risk of psychological harm to the patient. 21 , 22 , 23 , 27

State of the art in ethics of counseling for early detection of AD

Supporting informed decision‐making when blood‐based biomarkers increase the availability of early detection of AD has been identified as a special challenge, adding to the existing challenge of informing patients about the implications of early AD detection (e.g., due to uncertainty of the final test results). 32 , 33 Overall, balancing the right to know and the right not to know requires various ethical considerations for counseling and providing professional support for autonomous decision‐making. 7 , 34 Patients must be informed of their right to know or not to know, the implications for their well‐being and future decisions. 9 , 32 Therefore, patients’ informed consent for early detection should be based on all relevant information, considering the advantages and disadvantages according to their own moral beliefs. 6 , 8 , 32 , 33 , 35 , 36 , 37 Pre‐diagnostic counseling is a demanding process that should involve general and practical information about the possibility of life planning, the drafting of living wills to maintain autonomy long‐term, 15 , 38 , 39 , 40 the involvement of proxies at an asymptomatic stage to avoid legal challenges, 32 possible disease‐modifying therapies, 3 , 41 and non‐medical benefits such as family support. 6 In particular, the distinction between risk prediction using blood‐based biomarkers and an actual diagnosis must be addressed in counseling. 34 , 35 , 40 , 42 However, also the comprehension of the complex information is an ethical requirement, which demands the use of appropriate language and the explanation of the complex terminology by professionals. 9 , 32 , 35 , 40 Pre‐diagnostic counseling is important as it is beneficial for post‐diagnostic communication of test and risk results, leading to greater comprehension levels. 17 , 35

Dementia is often considered a “human catastrophe,” 8 the fear of losing control and one's personality dominates society's image of dementia. As a result, individuals are often affected by such fears and worries. Such fears have been considered a source of psychosocial harm. Therefore, current recommendations follow positions of non‐maleficence, leading to the assessment that asymptomatic individuals should not be tested. 32 , 43 The lack of curative treatments can lead to discrimination against individuals with dementia, especially when the condition is detected early and the individual is still employed. Discrimination has been linked to psychological consequences such as depression and suicidal thoughts. 32 , 38 , 39 , 44 However, counseling tends to reduce or prevent negative consequences 11 and a sound relationship of trust between the counselor and the help‐seeking individual is required. 9 , 31

Another possible hurdle for self‐determined decisions and non‐maleficence are pre‐existing cognitive impairments which can result in a limited understanding of the complex information about the early detection of AD or impaired decision‐making capacity. 32 , 34 , 36 , 37 Also differences in cultural perspectives can reduce substantial understanding. 35 Misunderstanding or misconception of risk results can lead to psychosocial harm, when individuals think they have received a full diagnosis. 44 , 45

Further, ethical demands regarding justice and fair counseling structures must be considered by providing high and equal quality of counseling through training for professionals and standardization. 9 , 35 , 36 , 39

EMPIRICAL INSIGHTS FROM A PILOT STUDY TO INFORM ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

To understand more practically what patients and healthcare professionals require for telemedical counseling, we explore the perspectives of telemedicine users in our ethical considerations. For this, we refer to a qualitative study that offered telemedical counseling for the early detection of AD. 11 Here we briefly present examples taken from this study to substantiate ethical‐practical considerations (see Supporting Information ‐ Methods B: Qualitative Content Analysis, 46 , 47 ).a

The interviews revealed that participants wish for much more information regarding the early detection of AD. While wishing for interpersonal counseling (e.g., general practitioners) they pointed out that rural areas in particular, face challenges of accessible counseling. One 86‐year‐old woman expressed the moral view: “I think that's actually the responsibility of the health system.” We interpret this as need to discuss alternative counseling approaches such as telemedicine for fair resource allocation.

The participants, especially from the countryside also described specific barriers to accessible interpersonal counseling. For example, traveling to the doctor's office and inadequate rural counseling structures were identified as barriers. These barriers pose a challenge to promote self‐determined decisions. As another woman, 84 years old, said: “If you have to go to [the big city] every time, that will […] be very difficult. […] I still drive, but of course that's not so great anymore. […] That's the bad thing about living in the countryside, you can't [access health care easily ].”

If telemedical counseling for the early detection of AD is discussed, ethical demands for equal access to counseling structures become important. Regarding the use of telemedicine, the participants described low‐threshold counseling and the resulting easy accessibility as beneficial. A 59‐year‐old woman commented: “I say super low‐threshold offer, right. I don't have to go to my GP first and explain my worries and get a referral or see someone else […].” However, participants also pointed out barriers to the accessibility of telemedical counseling. First, its existence must be publicized for the target group. Furthermore, counseling was only available during working hours, which limited access especially for individuals who are employed. These factors must be considered when allocating resources for telemedicine.

Some participants expressed a lack of trust and skepticism toward telemedical services in general: for example, if the service was unavailable during business hours, they had concerns about the communication medium used, or it was beyond their individual technical competence. The counselor's competence was especially questioned because the counselor was unknown and could not be seen over the telephone. Such mistrust in telemedical counseling must be taken seriously as a long‐term challenge for the acceptance of such services.

Nevertheless, our qualitative data also revealed an advantage of telemedicine regarding access to counseling. Some participants stressed that anonymous counseling via telemedicine is beneficial, as it avoids public discussion of dementia and prevents potential social discrimination or stigmatization. A 59‐year‐old, woman commented: “[…] So if I were to stand there under this sign – memory or dementia counseling – that […] doesn't necessarily appeal to me, I'm kind of afraid […] and that's why I find this telephone counseling so attractive.”

ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS REGARDING TELEMEDICAL COUNSELING FOR THE EARLY DETECTION OF AD

Introduction

To formulate conceptual conditions, we used the principles of Beauchamp and Childress 15 and Nordgren 16 (see “1.1 Ethical Background”) to discuss the findings from literature and insights derived from the empirical study above. We argue that the main purpose of telemedical counseling for the early detection of AD should be to strengthen the autonomy of the concerned individual and to address challenges in establishing equal and fair counseling structures. Nevertheless, ethical demands regarding beneficence and non‐maleficence are subsequent steps to be considered. We understand these two principles as the foundation for, in general, providing early detection of AD with blood‐based biomarkers (e.g., diagnostic with potential psychological harm) and the necessary pre‐diagnostic counseling (e.g., physicians’ obligation to promote well‐being). We argue that these aspects are prerequisites on which the discussion about fair resource allocation for telemedical counseling builds upon. Therefore, when discussing counseling for the early detection of AD, we advocate that beneficence should be understood as a foundation for the principle of autonomy. Additionally, we advocate that non‐maleficence should be understood as a limitation for the principle of justice regarding the ethical feasibility of telemedical counseling in this context. Requirements for ethical and fair telemedical counseling for AD

In the following, fair resource allocation is discussed in conflicts between the principle of autonomy (human dignity and self‐responsibility) and the principle of justice (cost‐effectiveness and needs and solidarity), focusing on whether telemedicine should be used for counseling in the early detection of AD or not:

Due to an aging society and rising healthcare costs, it is important that alternative counseling services are cost‐effective. 16 Low‐threshold counseling can help to support individuals in making decisions regarding life planning and whether an early detection meets their individual preferences. Future disease‐modifying therapies, lifestyle changes, and the use of telemedicine are all expected to lower healthcare costs (see Table 1, 4.1.A.). 3 , 41 , 48 However, cost‐effectiveness arguments lead to a conflict with the concept of needs and solidarity which requires that the sickest (individuals with AD) are prioritized in resource allocation. 16 Nevertheless, we argue that individuals with AD will benefit from the accessibility of telemedicine. To ensure high accessibility of telemedical counseling, it is important to consider country‐specific health care systems and socio‐demographic situation and adapt our findings accordingly (see Table 1, 4.B.). For example, it is necessary to identify marginalized groups in each country to identify which measures are needed to promote equal access. We argue that not all communication media used for telemedical counseling will meet the principle of needs and solidarity. If cost‐effectiveness is unduly prioritized in the context of counseling, it is necessary to consider the point at which telemedicine becomes too impersonal and is no longer justifiable or in accordance with individual needs. This could, in the future, occur with the use of artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots 49 which must be viewed with caution and should not be considered for telemedical counseling for the target group at this time. Regarding self‐responsibility it is important that individuals can decide whether they prefer conventional or telemedical counseling. 16 We argue that telemedical counseling should close gaps in care but not replace conventional counseling. Concerns about the respective technology can pose barriers for telemedical counseling (see Table 1, 4.2.B.). It is therefore important that healthcare professionals and the target group participate early in the development process of innovative forms of counseling to minimize the risk of distorted trust. 16 This is precisely the starting point of our study which aims at incorporating the perspectives of affected people by means of evaluation. 14 , 16 For respecting human dignity individual needs must be considered. 16 To avoid the argument of over‐prioritized cost‐effectiveness for a large‐scale introduction of telemedical counseling, we ranked our considerations with a lexical order with each field building upon the preceding one: strengthening autonomy is needed as a foundation and includes ethical demands of the principle of autonomy. Equal counseling structures include ethical demands of the principle of justice and building upon the prerequisite aspects, thus enabling fair resource allocation only to be achieved if individual needs and ethical considerations are respected. Conclusion

Future research should consider the integration of dementia‐friendly technologies, 50 as this can help to better address target‐group specific needs in telemedical counseling. Finally, the issue of scare resources in this context and an aging society will make alternative counseling services inevitable (see Table 1, 1.A.). To ensure respect for autonomy and the right to know consented guidelines regarding the clinical implementation of blood‐based biomarkers for the early detection of AD are needed (see Table 1, 4.A.). Making early detection more accessible for a wider population group, necessary counseling as a key aspect of fair resource allocation is often overlooked. Following current recommendations for counseling in early stages of AD, the mandatory provision of counseling services should be secured, including innovative forms of counseling such as telemedical approaches. 7 , 33 This requires a reorientation of healthcare systems worldwide with a focus on ensuring accessible counseling. We stress the importance of adapting our ethical considerations to country‐specific circumstances and to establish them in guidelines and recommendations. At this point before blood‐based biomarkers are widely implemented in clinical contexts, our considerations have a high chance of influencing the development process of telemedical counseling services, 14 making a telemedical approach to counseling for the early detection of AD feasible and enabling the realization of fair resource allocation.
