Association Between Triglyceride Glucose Index and Chronic Kidney Disease in Normal‐Weight Chinese Adults With Hypertension
Authors: Chao Yu, Wei Zhou, Xinlei Zhou, Lingjuan Zhu, Tao Wang, Huihui Bao, Xiaoshu Cheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aimed to examine the association between the triglyceride‐glucose (TyG) index and chronic kidney disease (CKD) in normotensive adults with hypertension and further investigate potential effect modifiers of this association. A total of 7975 normoweight hypertensive participants were enrolled from the Chinese H‐type hypertension registry (CHHRS) cohort. The TyG index was calculated using the formula: ln (fasting triglyceride [mg/dL] × fasting plasma glucose [mg/dL])/2. CKD was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 of body surface area. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed a 50% increased risk of CKD (OR: 1.50, 95% CI: 1.26–1.79) for each unit increase in the TyG index. A linear dose‐response relationship between the TyG index and CKD risk was observed using restricted cubic spline analysis. Compared to the first quartile of the TyG index, the fourth quartile showed a significantly higher risk of CKD (OR: 1.88; 95% CI: 1.41–2.50). Subgroup analysis identified a stronger association between the TyG index and CKD risk in males and individuals with a history of alcohol consumption (all p values for interaction < 0.05). In conclusions, the TyG index was significantly associated with an increased risk of CKD in normoweight adults with hypertension, particularly in males and those with a history of alcohol consumption.Keywords: chronic kidney disease, hypertension, normal weight, triglyceride glucose index

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) poses a growing burden on public health. Projections indicate it will be the fifth leading cause of death globally by 2040 [1]. The insidious onset of CKD often leads to its neglect in the early stages, highlighting the need for heightened awareness [2]. Hypertension, a frequent complication of CKD, also significantly increases the risk of accelerated kidney decline [3, 4, 5]. Furthermore, the co‐occurrence of hypertension and CKD substantially elevates the risk of adverse cardiovascular events, the leading cause of mortality in CKD patients [4]. Despite aggressive standard therapies, the number of patients with end‐stage renal disease requiring renal replacement therapy continues to rise [6, 7]. Accordingly, identifying and addressing additional modifiable risk factors is crucial to optimizing primary prevention strategies for CKD in hypertensive individuals. In recent years, insulin resistance (IR) has emerged as a major area of interest in CKD research, with some scholars proposing it as a potential predictor of CKD risk [8, 9, 10].

Previous studies have established that obesity, as defined by body mass index (BMI), is an independent risk factor for CKD [11]. However, BMI has limitations; it cannot differentiate between adipose tissue and lean muscle mass [12]. Consequently, some individuals with normal weight but high body fat percentage may be misclassified as non‐obese. These individuals with normal‐weight obesity are demonstrably at greater risk for metabolic dysfunction compared to their healthy counterparts and, thus, have an increased susceptibility to CKD [13, 14]. Notably, estimates suggest that nearly 20% of individuals with normal weight may be classified as metabolically obese [15, 16]. This subgroup represents a significant population that warrants further investigation. Furthermore, the current BMI classifications were established primarily using data from Caucasian populations. Although the baseline BMI for the Chinese population tends to be lower, recent research suggests that the risk of metabolic complications associated with elevated BMI may be even greater in this population compared to Caucasians [17]. This highlights the importance of reevaluating CKD risk assessment in normal‐weight individuals, particularly within the Chinese population.

Indeed, IR, characterized by decreased sensitivity of the human body to the hypoglycemic effect of insulin, plays an important role in glycolipid metabolism [18]. Hyperinsulinemic euglycemic clamping is well‐established as the “gold standard” for assessing IR [19]. However, its high cost and complexity limit its use in large epidemiological studies. Consequently, researchers have increasingly turned to the triglyceride‐glucose (TyG) index as a readily available, reliable, and simple alternative that correlates well with hyperinsulinemic‐euglycemic clamping [20, 21, 22]. Previous studies have reported a significant association between the TyG index and CKD [23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Additionally, our prior research identified heterogeneity in the association between the TyG index and CKD across different BMI strata (BMI < 25: OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.29–1.77; BMI ≥ 25: OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 0.97–1.55; p for interaction = 0.115). This finding suggests a potential predominance of the association within the normal‐weight population (BMI < 25) compared to individuals with overweight or obesity (BMI ≥ 25). Notably, the association between the TyG index and CKD, specifically in the normal‐weight population, remains unexplored in the current literature.

In light of these observations, the present study leveraged data from the Chinese H‐type hypertension registry (CHHRS) to examine the association between the TyG index and CKD within the Chinese population with normal weight and hypertension. Furthermore, we sought to explore potential factors that might modify this association.

Methods

Study Population

Data for this analysis were obtained from the CHHRS (ChiCTR1800017274), a real‐world, multicenter observational study conducted in Wuyuan, China. Details regarding inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as the data collection protocol for the CHHRS study, can be found in the published article [28]. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Commission of Anhui Medical University's Institute of Biomedicine (no. CH1059) [29]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

A total of 7975 participants were included in the analysis. Details regarding the exclusion process specific to this analysis are presented in Figure 1.

Flow chart of the study participants. BMI indicates body mass index.

Data Collection

Baseline data were collected from participants through standardized clinical examinations and detailed questionnaires. For smoking and alcohol consumption history, a binary questionnaire (“yes” or “no”) was employed. Educational attainment was categorized into three levels: illiterate, primary school, and secondary school or higher. Physical activity levels were classified as light, moderate, or vigorous based on individual assessments. Medication use was determined according to information on current medication bottles. Diabetes mellitus was defined as the current use of hypoglycemic agents, a self‐reported history of diabetes, or a fasting blood glucose level ≥ 7.0 mmol/L. Following a 10‐min seated rest, pulse and blood pressure were measured three times using electronic sphygmomanometers (Omron; Dalian, China). The mean of the three measurements was used for analysis. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (meters) squared. Normal weight was defined as a BMI ≥ 18.5 kg/m2 and < 25 kg/m2 [30].

Laboratory Examination

Following an overnight fast exceeding 8 h, venous blood samples were collected from participants and transported within 24 h to the central laboratory (National Clinical Research Center for Kidney Disease, Guangzhou, China) for processing and analysis. Low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), serum homocysteine (Hcy), serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and serum γ‐glutamyltransferase (GGT) were measured using a Beckman Coulter automated clinical analyzer. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD‐EPI) equation [31].

Definition of the TyG Index and CKD

The TyG index was calculated using the formula: ln (TG [mg/dL] × FPG [mg/dL]/2) [27]. CKD was defined as an eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 of body surface area, as described by the Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative [30].

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of participants were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequency (%) for categorical variables. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Chi‐square test, as appropriate, was used to compare baseline characteristics across quartiles of the TyG index. To assess the independent association of the TyG index with CKD risk, multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) after adjusting for covariates. Covariates were selected based on clinical significance, a potential confounding effect of at least 10%, and statistical significance in univariate analysis. A generalized additive model (GAM) with a penalized spline method was employed to evaluate the dose‐response relationship between the TyG index and CKD risk. Additionally, stratified analyses and interaction testing were performed to explore potential effect modifications on the association between the TyG index and CKD risk.

We used the statistical software R (https://www.r‐project.org/) and Empower (R) (www.empowerstats.com; X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA) for all data analysis and form production. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used.

Association Between TyG Index and Risk of CKD

Multiple logistic regression analyses were performed (Table 2) to investigate the association between the TyG index and the risk of CKD, which demonstrated a significant positive association. Specifically, for every unit increase in the TyG index, the odds of developing CKD increased by 50% (OR: 1.50, 95% CI: 1.26, 1.79). Furthermore, when the TyG index was categorized into quartiles, participants in the highest quartile had a significantly increased risk of CKD compared to those in the lowest quartile (OR: 1.88, 95% CI: 1.41–2.50). These findings were further supported by the data presented in Figure 2, which visually depicted the positive correlation between the TyG index and CKD risk.

Subgroup Analysis

Subgroup analyses were performed to identify potential factors that might modify the association between the TyG index and the risk of CKD (Figure 3). The results showed a stronger association between the TyG index and CKD in individuals with a history of alcohol consumption (OR: 2.03, 95% CI: 1.50, 2.74) compared to those without a drinking history (OR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.03, 1.58; p for interaction = 0.005), suggesting that drinking history may act as a moderator in this relationship. Additionally, the effect of the TyG index on CKD risk appeared to be more pronounced in males compared to females. However, stratification by other factors, such as diabetes and age, did not significantly alter the overall association.

Discussion

Herein, we provide hitherto undocumented evidence of an association between the TyG index and an increased risk of CKD in normotensive adults with hypertension. Furthermore, this association was more pronounced in males and individuals with a history of alcohol consumption.

Studies across various weight groups support a positive association between the TyG index and CKD risk. Analyzing data from multiple cohorts in Japan, Takuro et al. observed a gender difference with a 32% increased CKD risk in males and 50% in females with higher TyG index [23]. Similarly, Yoshida et al. found a significant association between the TyG index and declining renal function (HR = 2.25, 95% CI:1.40–3.60) in a large Japanese population [24]. This association extends to the general population, with studies like the Northern Shanghai Study showing a 1.67‐fold increased risk of CKD for individuals with elevated TyG index [25]. Furthermore, a longitudinal study in Iran demonstrated an increasing trend of CKD risk in both men and women with rising TyG levels [26]. Our own prior research also confirms this positive association between the TyG index and CKD risk, even within a hypertensive population [27]. Given this finding, we additionally performed a fitted smoothing curve analysis (presented in Figure S1) to substantiate that the association between TyG and CKD might be limited to BMI‐defined non‐obese populations. Notably, prior research has not examined the association between the TyG index and the risk of CKD in normal‐weight individuals. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between the TyG index and the risk of CKD, specifically in the normal‐weight hypertensive population. We found a positive association between the TyG index and the risk of CKD in this group. This suggests that the TyG index might be a valuable tool for identifying normal‐weight hypertensive individuals at high risk for CKD.

The mechanism underlying the association between the TyG index and CKD risk remains unclear. However, several potential explanations have been proposed. One possibility is that the adverse effects of IR on the kidney play a role. Studies suggest that abnormalities in the adipocytokine‐induced insulin signaling pathway may be involved. This pathway dysfunction could lead to reduced NO‐mediated vasodilation, increased vasoreactivity, and angiogenesis, ultimately promoting renal function decline [32, 33, 34]. Podocyte IR is another potential mechanism. Recent research demonstrates that podocytes in mice lacking the insulin receptor undergo apoptosis, followed by effacement of the podocyte foot process and thickening of the glomerular basement membrane, ultimately resulting in kidney damage [35]. Additionally, abnormal salt sensitivity, oxidative stress, and inflammatory response may also contribute to the detrimental effects of IR on kidney function [36, 37, 38].

In subgroup analyses, the association between the TYG index and risk of CKD was stronger in those with a history of alcohol consumption compared to non‐drinkers. In this respect, it has been reported that alcohol exposure could potentiate IR‐induced kidney injury via β‐arrestin 2/Akt/GSK3β‐mediated signaling in the kidney [39]. Besides, it is highly conceivable that long‐term drinking could cause abnormal expression of the alcohol dehydrogenase gene in the body, which in turn aggravates insulin receptor damage, inhibits insulin signaling, decreases insulin sensitivity, and aggravates the occurrence of IR, thereby promoting the occurrence of CKD [37, 40]. Additionally, an interaction between sex and TyG index on CKD was also found in this study. The OR of the TyG index with an elevated risk of CKD was more pronounced in males compared with females. This might be because males had a higher prevalence of diabetes and early abnormal glucose metabolism than females, and insulin sensitivity has previously been reported to be stronger in females [41, 42]. Therefore, males were associated with a higher risk of IR than females.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, due to its cross‐sectional design, causality could not be inferred. Prospective studies are required to confirm the observed associations. Secondly, although we adjusted for several important covariates in the model, residual confounding by unmeasured variables is still possible. Thirdly, the generalizability of our conclusions may be limited as the study population consisted solely of Chinese individuals with hypertension. Further studies in diverse populations are needed to verify our findings.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a significant association between the TyG index and CKD risk in normotensive adults with normal weight. Notably, this association was particularly pronounced in males and individuals with a history of alcohol consumption. These findings highlight the vulnerability of even normal‐weight individuals to CKD and emphasize the importance of focusing on IR control and alcohol abstinence, especially among males, as primary prevention strategies for CKD.
