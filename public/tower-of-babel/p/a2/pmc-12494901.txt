Association between triglyceride-glucose index and myocardial injury in patients with heat stroke: an observational, retrospective study
Authors: Guo Tang, Ying Yang, Tianshan Zhang, Tao Cheng, Hongguang Gao, Wei Wei, Rong Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeat stroke (HS) can lead to myocardial injury (MI), a critical factor affecting patient prognosis. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, a surrogate marker for insulin resistance, has been associated with MI in patients with ischemic stroke and diabetes. However, its relationship with MI in HS patients remains unclear. This study aimed to explore the correlation between the TyG index and MI in HS patients. Clinical data from HS patients admitted to the emergency department of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, between July 1, 2022, and September 30, 2023, were retrospectively analyzed. Patients were divided into MI and non-MI groups based on the presence of MI. MI was defined as cardiac troponin ≥ 1.5 ng/mL. Multivariate logistic regression evaluated the association between the TyG index at admission and MI. A restricted cubic spline (modeled with four knots) was used to assess the dose-response relationship between the TyG index and MI. The study included 169 HS patients (mean age: 55 years; 85 [50.3%] male), of whom 63 (32.7%) developed MI. After adjusting for confounders, multivariate logistic regression revealed a nonlinear association between the TyG index and MI risk. Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a non-linear relationship between the TyG index and MI risk, with a decreasing trend observed for TyG values ≤ 8.897, followed by a marked increase in risk for values ≥ 8.966. Female patients and those in specific age groups (18–44 and 75–89 years) exhibited higher risk of MI. This study identifies a U-shaped relationship between the TyG index and MI in HS patients, highlighting the TyG index’s potential indicator value and underscoring the importance of gender and age in risk assessment.Keywords: Heat stroke, Myocardial injury, Triglyceride-glucose index, Restricted cubic splineSubject terms: Environmental impact, Cardiology, Diseases, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Heat stroke (HS) is a life-threatening condition characterized by a core body temperature exceeding 40 °C due to thermoregulatory failure in hot environments or during exertion, triggering systemic inflammation and central nervous system dysfunction1–3. Cardiac involvement is common in HS4–6, manifesting as myocardial injury (MI), arrhythmias, and cardiac dysfunction7, which significantly contributes to multiple organ dysfunction and increased mortality8. Early identification of MI risk factors is therefore critical for improving prognosis. Insulin resistance, a key driver of metabolic dysfunction and cardiovascular disease9,10, can be assessed using the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index11. While an elevated TyG index is associated with cardiovascular events12,13, its relationship with MI specifically in HS patients remains unexplored. This study aimed to investigate the association between the TyG index and MI in HS patients, with a focus on characterizing its potential nonlinear relationship.

Materials and methods

Study population

Patients diagnosed with HS at the emergency department of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, between July 1, 2022, and September 30, 2023, were enrolled. The diagnosis of HS based primarily on1: recent exposure to hot weather (in the classic form) or physical exertion (in the exertional form). Core temperature > 40 °C, dry hot skin, and central nervous system abnormalities (e.g., delirium, convulsions, or coma). Exclusion criteria include: (1) age < 18 years; (2) comorbidities causing MI (e.g., acute coronary syndrome, myocarditis, aortic dissection, severe infection, acute poisoning); (3) incomplete medical records.

Study parameters

Demographics, medical history, HS type, vital signs, and laboratory data (blood tests, liver/kidney function, coagulation profile, cardiac biomarkers) were extracted from electronic medical records. The TyG index was calculated as ln[fasting triglycerides (mg/dL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)/2]14.

Based on the worst in-hospital indicators, patients were categorized into MI and non-MI groups. MI was defined as cardiac troponin ≥ 1.5 ng/mL15,16. Acute kidney injury followed KDIGO criteria17: ≥26.5 µmol/L increase in serum creatinine within 48 h or ≥ 1.5× baseline Scr within 7 days. Acute liver injury was defined as Alanine aminotransferase ≥ 5× upper limit of normal (ULN), Alkaline phosphatase ≥ 2× ULN, or ALT ≥ 3× ULN with total bilirubin ≥ 2× ULN18.

Statistical analysis

R 4.4.1 was used for analysis. Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± SD (independent t-test), while non-normal data used median [interquartile range] (Mann-Whitney U test). Categorical variables were compared via χ² or Fisher’s exact test. Multivariable logistic regression evaluated TyG’s association with MI in HS subtypes—Model 1 (unadjusted) and Model 2 (adjusted for age/sex). Restricted cubic spline (four knots) assessed dose-response relationships. Two-tailed P < 0.05 was statistically significant.

Ethics

The experimental protocol was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (No. 2022 − 1478) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The study strictly adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (No. 2022 − 1478) due to the retrospective nature of this research.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 169 patients (mean age 55 [67,74] years; 50.3% male), 113 (66.9%) had classic heat stroke (CHS), and 56 (33.1%) exertional heat stroke (EHS). CHS patients were older (71.0 vs. 56.5, P < 0.001) and presented with higher initial body temperatures (39.5 vs. 38.0 °C, P < 0.001) than EHS cases (Table 1).

Myocardial injury in heat stroke

MI occurred in 63 patients (32.7%). Compared to the non-MI group, the MI group had lower initial systolic (114 mmHg vs. 125.5 mmHg, P = 0.014) and diastolic blood pressure (65 mmHg vs. 74 mmHg, P = 0.023), lower Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores (5 vs. 8, P < 0.001), but higher heart rates (125 bpm vs. 104 bpm, P < 0.001), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores (8 vs. 5, P < 0.001), incidences of liver injury (38.1% vs. 18.9%, P = 0.01), kidney injury (92.1% vs. 59.4%, P < 0.001), and in-hospital mortality (44.4% vs. 28.3%, P = 0.0049). Age (66.0 years vs. 67.5 years, P = 0.666), sex (35 males vs. 50 males, P = 0.371), body temperature (39.1 °C vs. 39.2 °C, P = 0.576), and TyG index (8.98 vs. 8.85, P = 0.341) showed no significant differences in groups(Table 2).

Logistic regression analysis of TyG index and myocardial injury in heat stroke patients

Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed an independent association between TyG index and MI risk in HS patients, with a nonlinear relationship (Model 1: OR: 0.907, 95% CI: 0.605–1.358, P = 0.0193, P for nonlinear = 0.005; Model 2: OR: 0.886, 95% CI: 0.583–1.346, P = 0.0175, P for nonlinear = 0.0045) (Table 3).

Dose-response relationship between TyG index and myocardial injury in heat stroke patients

Restricted cubic spline curves (fitted with four knots) were used to evaluate the dose-response relationship. Figure 1 demonstrates a complex relationship between the TyG index, demographic factors, and MI risk. In unadjusted analyses, MI risk decreased when the TyG index was ≤ 8.275 but increased when it was ≥ 8.938. After adjusting for sex and age, the risk thresholds shifted slightly, with a decrease observed at TyG ≤ 8.897 and an increase at TyG ≥ 8.966. Additionally, female patients exhibited a significantly higher risk of MI compared to males, while the highest risk was identified in patients aged 18–44 and 75–89 years.

Analysis of non-myocardial injury and myocardial injury groups across heat stroke subtypes

The relationship between TyG index and MI was further examined in clinical subtypes. Table 4 compares baseline characteristics of MI and non-MI groups in CHS patients. The MI group had lower systolic blood pressure (120 vs. 133, P = 0.014), diastolic blood pressure (65 vs. 73, P = 0.041), and GCS scores (5 vs. 8, P < 0.001) at admission, but higher heart rate (126 vs. 107, P < 0.001), respiratory rate (25 vs. 22, P = 0.036), SOFA scores (8 vs. 5, P < 0.001), and in-hospital rates of liver injury (37% vs. 17.9%, P = 0.04) and renal injury (93.5% vs. 53.7%, P < 0.001). Logistic regression confirmed a nonlinear association between TyG index and MI in CHS (Model 1: OR: 0.791, 95% CI: 0.264–2.372, P = 0.0754, P for nonlinear = 0.0375; Model 2: OR: 0.928, 95% CI: 0.285–3.020, P = 0.0734, P for nonlinear = 0.0362) (Table 5). Restricted cubic spline analysis showed decreased risk with TyG ≤ 7.799 and increased risk with TyG ≥ 8.863 (Fig. 2A). After adjustment, risk decreased with TyG ≤ 7.711 and increased at 8.992 ≤ TyG ≤ 8.994 (Fig. 2B). Females and patients aged 18–44 years had the highest MI risk in CHS (Fig. 2C and D).

Discussion

As a novel marker for evaluating insulin resistance, TyG index has been demonstrated by multiple studies to correlate closely with cardiovascular events19–21. This study is the first to explore the relationship between the TyG index and MI risk in HS patients, revealing a unique U-shaped association. Considering the high incidence of MI in HS patients and its impact on prognosis, this study provides a simple, accessible, and potentially valuable indicator for early identification and risk assessment of MI in HS.

The TyG index, calculated from fasting triglycerides and fasting blood glucose, reflects glucose and lipid metabolism—key processes in maintaining cardiomyocyte energy supply and function22. Elevated TyG levels typically reflect severe insulin resistance linked to metabolic disturbances, inflammation, and oxidative stress—all critical risk factors for cardiovascular diseases19–21. Under HS conditions, upregulated glycolytic enzymes disrupt normal metabolic pathways, causing an imbalance between glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation. This leads to excessive lactate production and subsequent MI22. Animal studies suggest HS may further exacerbate glucose-lipid dysregulation by inhibiting the AMPK signaling pathway. As a central regulator of energy metabolism, AMPK activation promotes glucose metabolism while suppressing fatty acid metabolism, maintaining metabolic balance and protecting cardiomyocytes from ischemia-reperfusion injury23. However, AMPK suppression during HS aggravates metabolic dysregulation, potentially elevating the TyG index, which creates a vicious cycle of worsening insulin resistance and impaired cardiomyocyte energy supply24. Targeting the AMPK pathway to modulate glucose-lipid metabolism may thus represent a potential therapeutic strategy for HS-induced MI.

This study identified a U-shaped relationship between the TyG index and MI risk in HS patients. Prior research has similarly reported U-shaped associations between TyG and all-cause/cardiovascular mortality in chronic kidney disease, osteoarthritis, diabetic retinopathy, and depression25–29. High TyG levels correlate with many established cardiovascular risk factors, such as insulin resistance, metabolic disorders, dyslipidemia, inflammation, and oxidative stress25,26. On the other hand, low TyG may be related to malnutrition or frailty, particularly in elderly populations, which also could increase cardiovascular risk27–29. Our study also found significantly higher MI risk in female patients, particularly those aged 18–44 and 75–89 years. Potential explanations include physiological and hormonal differences affecting thermoregulation, higher body fat impairing heat dissipation, and age-related vulnerabilities. For example, younger patients often face more risks from extreme physical exertion, while older patients more suffer from diminished heat dissipation capacity.

Subgroup analyses revealed distinct TyG index and MI relationships between EHS and CHS subtypes. A U-shaped association persisted in CHS—typically affecting elderly patients with preexisting comorbidities and impaired heat dissipation—but not in EHS, which predominantly occurs in younger adults due to excessive heat production during intense activity. The stronger correlation in CHS may reflect higher baseline insulin resistance levels, making TyG index a more relevant marker in this population.

This study has some limitations. First, as a retrospective single-center study, it could not fully account for other factors influencing TyG index measurements, such as dietary, lifestyle, or genetic factors. Second, while MI involves multidimensional indicators, this study focused solely on troponin levels without comprehensively assessing other cardiac structural, electrophysiological or functional markers. Additionally, the analysis could not completely disentangle the effects of preexisting comorbidities such as diabetes. Finally, comparison with homeostasis model assessment was infeasible due to absent insulin data. Despite these constraints, this work provides initial evidence supporting TyG index as a potential risk assessment tool for HS-induced MI and lays groundwork for future research.

Conclusion

This study found a U-shaped relationship between the TyG index and the risk of MI in HS patients, demonstrating its potential indicator. For patients with a TyG index ≤ 8.897, the risk of MI decreased as the TyG index increased, whereas for those with a TyG index ≥ 8.966, the risk rose as the index increased. Additionally, female patients and those aged 18–44 or 75–89 years had a higher risk of MI. These findings suggest the TyG index’s utility as a potential risk marker, but prospective multicenter validation is needed to address residual confounding and confirm causality.
