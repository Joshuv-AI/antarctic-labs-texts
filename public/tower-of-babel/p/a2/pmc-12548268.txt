Feasibility and usefulness of in-hospital and at-home salivary sampling in healthy dogs and trilostane-treated dogs with Cushing’s syndrome: a prospective observational study
Authors: Solène M. Meunier, Clarissa D. Voegel, Elisabeth Neubert, Livia A. Harburger, Barbara Riond, Regina Hofmann-Lehmann, Felicitas S. Boretti, Nadja S. Sieber-Ruckstuhl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMonitoring Cushing’s syndrome (CS) in dogs remains a significant challenge and currently relies on clinical signs, biochemistry results and hormone tests, such as prepill or post-ACTH cortisol. Salivary cortisol testing at home, effective in humans, could benefit dogs by minimizing in-hospital fear impacts on cortisol measurements. The objectives of this study were to (1) assess the feasibility of saliva sampling in client-owned dogs and (2) compare at-home and in-hospital salivary cortisol concentrations for monitoring trilostane treatment. Ten healthy controls and 38 trilostane-treated dogs with CS were included in this prospective observational clinical trial. Saliva was collected using a ginger-dipped Salimetrics® swab held in the dogs’ mouths for 30 seconds. Saliva was collected from controls at the hospital and at home. For trilostane-treated dogs, saliva was collected in the hospital before blood sampling for prepill serum cortisol and at home by owners. Salivary cortisol concentrations were measured using liquid chromatography high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS), and serum cortisol by LC-HRMS and chemiluminescence immunoassay. Dogs with CS were classified as well-controlled if clinical signs had resolved.ResultsIn-hospital and at-home saliva sampling was successful in all 10 controls, but only in 18/38 (47%) and 7/18 (39%) dogs with CS, respectively. Salivary cortisol was significantly lower at home in CS dogs but not in controls. Among the 7 CS dogs sampled at both locations, the uncontrolled dog had the highest at-home cortisol level. Four of 6 well-controlled dogs had prepill serum cortisol above the target range, with at-home salivary cortisol undetectable in 3 and lower than the uncontrolled dog in one.ConclusionSaliva sampling remains challenging in CS dogs. This method needs further refinement before stress-free, at-home salivary cortisol evaluation surpasses current monitoring techniques, such as prepill serum cortisol measurement. Background

Monitoring trilostane treatment in dogs with naturally occurring Cushing’s syndrome (CS) remains a significant challenge and currently relies on clinical signs, biochemistry results and hormone tests, such as prepill or post-ACTH cortisol concentration [1, 2]. The need for regular time-consuming re-evaluations, along with some dogs’ fearful reactions to blood sampling, can reduce owners’ willingness to pursue life-long treatment [3]. Additionally, stress induced by veterinary visits can lead to increased cortisol concentrations, complicating the assessment of treatment efficacy [1]. Nevertheless, relying solely on clinical signs can be misleading, as owner’s observations may lack accuracy. A non-invasive test, using sample material that can be easily collected by the owner at home, would be a distinct advantage.

In human medicine, determination of late-night salivary cortisol is an excellent method for postoperative monitoring of surgical failure or recurrence [4, 5], as well as for medical treatment monitoring [6]. Salivary cortisol is in equilibrium with the biologically active free cortisol in the blood and is not affected by the rate of saliva production [7]. A rise in blood cortisol results in an increase in salivary cortisol within five minutes [8]. While saliva collection is a straightforward, minimally invasive procedure in human adults, challenges arise in infants [9, 10] and dogs [11, 12].

Dogs with newly diagnosed CS have significantly higher salivary cortisol concentrations than healthy dogs [12]. In the first clinical trial using salivary cortisol to diagnose CS in dogs, enough saliva was retrieved in only 50% of dogs with CS and 27% of healthy controls [12]. Therefore, before salivary cortisol could be used to diagnose or monitor CS in dogs, a reliable collection method had to be established.

Sampling with a ginger powder-embedded cotton swab and measuring salivary cortisol concentrations using liquid chromatography high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) has yielded promising results in a preliminary experimental study involving healthy dogs [13]. LC-HRMS is an advanced analytical technique that combines liquid chromatography for separating compounds with mass spectrometry for their precise identification and quantification. Due to its high sensitivity and specificity, LC-HRMS is particularly valuable for the quantification of molecules in various biological matrices [14]. The key advantage of the developed method is that LC-HRMS requires only a small sample volume, while ginger stimulates salivary production, allowing sufficient saliva to be collected within only 30 seconds [13]. The measurement of salivary cortisol concentrations in 36 healthy dogs before and after a 10-minute veterinary visit by either chemiluminescence immunoassay or LC-MS has recently been shown to provide comparable results [15]. Nonetheless, LC-MS has not yet been evaluated in dogs with CS in a clinical setting.

Therefore, the objectives of this study were to evaluate the feasibility of saliva sampling in healthy controls and client-owned dogs with CS, and to compare at-home and in-hospital salivary cortisol concentrations for monitoring trilostane treatment.

Results

Study population

Healthy controls

Ten clinically healthy staff-owned dogs, consisting of 5 females (3 spayed) and 5 males (1 neutered), were included. They ranged in age from 4 to 11 years (median, 6 years) and weighed 3.8–39 kg (median, 20.9 kg). Three dogs (30%) weighed less than 15 kg and the remaining seven (70%) were heavier. Following breeds were represented (number of dogs per breed): Grand Gascon Saintongeois (1), Jack Russel Terrier (1), Collie (1), Vizsla (1), Bolognese (1), Border Collie (1), Nova Scotia Duck Tolling Retriever (1), Australian Shepherd (1), and mixed breed (2). Individual signalment data are presented in Table 1 of the supplementary materials.

Dogs with CS

Thirty-eight client-owned dogs with confirmed CS were included.

The cohort comprised 19 females (16 spayed) and 19 males (13 neutered), ranging in age from 7.5 to 16 years (median,11 years) and weighing between 1.6 and 34.8 kg (median,8.2 kg). Only seven dogs (18%) were heavier than 15 kg. Dogs with CS were significantly lighter than healthy controls (P =.003). Various breeds (number of dogs per breed) were represented: Chihuahua (6), mixed breed (4), French Bulldog (2), German Spitz (2), Jack Russel Terrier (2), Lhasa Apso (2), Beagle (1), Beauceron (1), Bolognese (1), Border Terrier (1), Boston Terrier (1), Cavalier King Charles Spaniel (1), Dachshund (1), Doberman Pinscher (1), English Cocker Spaniel (1), Labradoodle (1), Maltese (1), Miniature Poodle (1), Parson Russell Terrier (1), Pomeranian (1), Portuguese Podengo (1), Schnauzer (1), Tibetan Terrier (1), Toy Poodle (1), Welsh Terrier (1), Yorkshire Terrier (1). Individual signalments are detailed in Table 2 of the supplementary materials.

PDH was diagnosed in 36 dogs according to the current ALIVE criteria defined by the European Society of Veterinary Endocrinology (28 at our hospital and 8 at the referring veterinary practice).

PDH was highly suspected in the remaining two dogs. The first was a 10-year-old spayed female mixed-breed dog (14.5 kg) with increased endogenous ACTH concentration (13 pmol/l), a normal-sized right adrenal gland (caudal pole 4.3 mm), and a left-sided adrenal mass (11 × 15 mm) on abdominal ultrasound.

The second was a 10-year-old intact male Teckel with bilaterally enlarged adrenals (left caudal pole 9.6 mm, right caudal pole 9.7 mm) and a small nodule (4 × 4.6 mm) at the caudal pole of the left adrenal gland.

Feasibility of saliva collection

Sufficient saliva volume was collected from all 10 control dogs at both locations. Median (range) handling, excitement and chewing movements scores at home were 2 (1–3), 2 (1–4), and 3 (1–5), respectively. For the individual sampling time points, the scores were: 2 (1–3), 2 (1–3) and 3 (1–5), respectively, at 8 a.m.; 1 (1–3), 2 (1–3) and 3 (1–5), respectively, at 4 p.m.; and 2 (1–2), 2 (1–4) and 2 (1–4), respectively at 8 p.m.

For trilostane-treated dogs with CS, in-hospital saliva sampling was successful in 18 out of 38 dogs (47%). However, only 7 of these 18 dogs (39%) provided sufficient saliva during at-home collection, and all 7 owners reported difficulties retrieving the samples. Median (range) handling, excitement and chewing movements scores were 2 (1–4), 3 (1–4), and 3 (1–5), respectively. For the individual sampling time points, the scores were: 2.5 (2–3), 3.5 (2–4) and 3 (1–5), respectively, at 8 a.m.; 2 (2–2), 3 (2–4) and 3 (3–5), respectively, at 4 p.m.; and 1.5 (1–4), 2 (1–4) and 4 (2–4), respectively at 8 p.m.

Challenges leading to sampling failure included defensive behaviors such as head shaking, attempts to bite, pawing at the hand holding the Salimetrics® cotton swab, excessive freezing, reluctance to chew the swab, or excessive chewing that damaged the swab, rendering it unusable.

In-hospital serum and salivary cortisol concentrations for monitoring trilostane treatment

In-hospital serum and salivary samples were available from 10 controls and 18 dogs with CS, respectively. For dogs with CS, two serum samples and two saliva samples, taken one hour apart, were available for all 18 dogs. There was a significant correlation between serum cortisol concentrations measured by LC-HRMS and competitive immunoassay for dogs with CS at both time points (r =.5, P =.04 and r =.8, P 0.001, respectively; data not shown). As no significant differences were found between the two time points for either the serum or the saliva samples (P =.96 and P =.1, respectively; data not shown), the samples from each dog were pooled (serum pooled separately from saliva) and subsequently compared with the corresponding serum and saliva samples from healthy controls. In-hospital serum and salivary cortisol did not differ significantly between groups (P =.14 and P >.99, respectively) (Fig. 1A and B).

Performance of at-home salivary cortisol concentrations for monitoring trilostane treatment

The evaluation of at-home monitoring performance in dogs with CS during trilostane treatment was carried out using samples from 7 dogs.

Six dogs were clinically well-controlled and 1 uncontrolled. The six clinically well-controlled dogs had a median clinical score of 45.5 (minimum: 43; maximum: 55). The uncontrolled dog had a clinical score of 57.

In 4 of the 6 well-controlled dogs, in-hospital prepill serum cortisol values were above the target range; values were within the target range for the remaining 2 dogs. The uncontrolled dog had in-hospital prepill serum cortisol values above the target range. Individual values can be found in Table 3 in supplementary materials.

The uncontrolled dog had the highest at-home salivary cortisol concentration. Both well-controlled dogs with serum prepill cortisol concentration within target had salivary cortisol concentrations at home under the detection limit but one had its in-hospital value above the uncontrolled dog. For 3 of the 4 well-controlled dogs with in-hospital prepill serum cortisol values above the target range, at-home salivary cortisol concentrations were below the detection limit, and in the fourth, they were lower than those of the uncontrolled dog (Fig. 5; Table 3 in supplementary materials).

Discussion

With this study, we aimed to evaluate the feasibility of saliva sampling in healthy dogs and in dogs with naturally occurring CS, and to evaluate the determination of salivary cortisol concentrations as a reliable and less invasive alternative for monitoring trilostane treatment in dogs with CS.

Saliva sampling was successful in all healthy control dogs, consistent with the 100% success rate reported in a previous experimental study [13]. Promising results were achieved in healthy Beagle dogs using a ginger powder-embedded cotton swab held in the dog’s mouth for 30 seconds, followed by measurement of salivary cortisol concentrations via LC-HRMS [13]. This experimental study addressed several previously reported challenges in collecting adequate saliva volume: thinner, longer cotton swabs that could be held in the dog’s mouth by hand, the use of ginger powder to stimulate salivation and reduce sampling time, and the implementation of a laboratory method for the cortisol measurement requiring smaller sample volumes [13].

In this study, saliva collection was considerably more challenging in client-owned, trilostane-treated dogs with CS than in healthy control dogs. In-hospital sampling was successful in only 47% of attempts in the former group, compared with 100% in the latter. At-home saliva collection by owners was even less successful, with sufficient saliva retrieved in only 18% of cases. The samples collected during the day in these dogs were pooled to ensure sufficient material for measurement by LC-HRMS. Switching to a laboratory method that requires significantly smaller saliva volumes compared to the immunoassays used in previous studies on infants [9, 10] and dogs [11, 12] did not fully resolve the challenges of collecting sufficient saliva volumes.

One reason may be that the significantly smaller and lighter dogs with CS have difficulty chewing adequately on the cotton swab, which helps stimulate saliva production [13]. Additionally, salivary secretion is under neural control, and sympathetic nerve stimulation (e.g., from stress) can lead to vasoconstriction and reduced salivary flow [16]. Stress and excitement, noticeable both in the hospital and at home, likely contributed to the inadequate saliva production in the trilostane-treated dogs with CS. In contrast, staff-owned controls were accustomed to the hospital environment and were sampled at home by experienced professionals (veterinary nurses or veterinarians), improving the likelihood of successful sampling.

Although current challenges in obtaining adequate saliva samples limit the practical applicability of this approach as an alternative to serum prepill cortisol measurements in trilostane-treated dogs with CS, improving the sampling method could ultimately facilitate broader use of at-home monitoring. With the use of another collection material or more palatable dips, the technique may still prove useful in future. Cotton swabs have been shown to be very absorbent, preventing saliva from being adequately released [17, 18]. Replacing swabs with sponges has resolved this issue in recent studies evaluating other biomarkers in canine saliva [19, 20]. In addition, it may be beneficial to provide more thorough and repeated training to owners on how to collect salivary samples, possibly over several follow-up visits. In this study, we demonstrated the sampling technique to the owners and provided them with written instructions. However, this was done only once, and more consistent, hands-on teaching could potentially lead to better results.

In the samples obtained, no significant difference was found between controls and dogs with CS for serum and salivary cortisol concentrations, either in the hospital or at home. This likely reflects the good clinical control achieved in most dogs with CS.

As previously shown, serum cortisol concentrations did not significantly differ between the two in-hospital time points for dogs with CS at the group level [1]. The same observation was made in the current study for salivary cortisol concentrations.

In-hospital serum cortisol concentrations in dogs with CS correlated significantly when measured by either chemiluminescence assay or LC-HRMS, as previously described in healthy dogs [13, 15].

Healthy dogs exhibited no daily variation in salivary cortisol concentrations, consistent with veterinary literature [11, 21, 22] and in contrast to the diurnal patterns observed in healthy humans [23, 24].

Most dogs with CS had at-home salivary cortisol concentrations below the assay’s detection limit. These dogs had no historical, clinical, or clinicopathologic findings compatible with iatrogenic hypoadrenocorticims, and their in-hospital cortisol values were within or above the target range, indicating adequate cortisol secretory capacity. Thus, low at-home salivary cortisol concentrations alone do not appear to indicate overtreatment. This suggests that identifying dogs with iatrogenic chronic hypocortisolism may be challenging when relying solely on at-home monitoring. The absence of significant difference in salivary cortisol concentrations between at-home and in-hospital collection for control dogs is probably due to their habituation to hospital setting, notably reducing their stress upon presentation. This contrasts with the dogs with CS, in which in-hospital salivary cortisol concentrations were higher compared to at-home values, as it is the case for human patients [25]. This is also reflected in the inappropriately high serum prepill cortisol values in well-controlled dogs, findings that highlight the limitations of in-hospital sampling. Stress- and fear-related increases in cortisol levels could lead to misinterpretation of actual control of the CS and unnecessary adjustments of trilostane dosages [1]. After an acute stressful event, serum cortisol level rises after less than 10 min [26], followed by an increase in salivary cortisol within minutes [8]. Therefore, it seems unlikely that the saliva sampling prior to blood sampling may have caused cortisol increments.

Provided the saliva sampling method can be further improved, at-home sampling by the owner in a non-stressful environment could mitigate this issue and in addition improve both the dog’s and the owner’s compliance to regular rechecks. The original aim to establish reference or target values for samples collected at home could not be followed because of the considerable number of unsuccessful attempts. If salivary samples were to be used as a monitoring tool at home in the future, appropriate target ranges would need to be established for this new collection setting [25].

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size, which resulted from the limited feasibility of the sampling technique in our clinical setting, and the inclusion of only dogs with a clinical score indicative of good control of CS. Further, although LC-HRMS analysis requires only minimal sample volumes and provides high accuracy, its still limited routine availability in clinical practice may hinder the widespread application of salivary cortisol measurements for CS monitoring. Finally, the study focused solely on cortisol concentration, even though LC-HRMS could facilitate parallel measurement of other related biomarkers. Without evaluating additional steroids or cortisol precursors, the full potential of this diagnostic approach remains unexplored.

Conclusion

Saliva sampling at home by owners remains challenging in dogs with CS. Further refinement is necessary before this method can outperform current monitoring techniques, such as prepill serum cortisol measurements. However, the elevated in-hospital salivary cortisol levels and the inappropriately high prepill serum cortisol values in well-controlled dogs highlight the limitations of in-hospital sampling, suggesting that home-based methods may offer a more accurate reflection of the dog’s true cortisol status. Salivary cortisol measured at home was frequently below the detection limit, which may hinder the identification of overdosed dogs unless a more sensitive method is developed.

Materials and methods

Animals

All dogs were privately owned. Signed informed consent was obtained from all owners prior to inclusion in the study. The study design and protocols were approved by the Cantonal Veterinary Office of Zurich (permission number ZH076/19 and ZH153/22). All applicable national, and/or institutional guidelines for the care and use of animals were followed.

Healthy controls

The dogs were considered healthy based on their medical history, inconspicuous physical examination results, and normal hematology and serum biochemistry.

Dogs with CS

Dogs were included in the CS group if following criteria were fulfilled: (1) previous confirmation of CS, (2) twice daily treatment with trilostane (Vetoryl, MSD Animal Health GmbH, Lucerne, Switzerland), (3) presentation for a re-check and (4) owner’s permission for saliva sampling.

Diagnosis of CS had initially been made based on a positive low-dose dexamethasone test (LDDS test) or positive ACTH stimulation test along with supportive history and compatible clinical signs (e.g. polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia, panting, skin problems, weakness and abdominal enlargement).

Pituitary-dependent hypercortisolism (PDH) was diagnosed by proving ACTH-dependence originating from the pituitary through at least one clear differentiation test result, according to the current ALIVE criteria defined by the European Society of Veterinary Endocrinology. The differentiation tests used in the present study (alone or in combination) included the following: measurement of the ACTH concentration, pattern of a LDDST, ultrasound examination of the adrenal gland and computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging of the brain.

Experimental design

The prospective observational clinical trial was conducted between 2020 and 2023 at the Clinic for Small Animal Internal Medicine of the University of Zurich. Healthy staff-owned dogs (controls) were included from 2020 to 2022, and client-owned trilostane-treated dogs with CS from 2020 to 2023, respectively.

Each dog with CS undergoing trilostane treatment was assessed using a standardized owner questionnaire routinely used at our hospital and a clinical examination. Owners were asked to assess drinking behavior (scale of 1 to 10; 1: absent, 5: normal, 10: extremely much), urination (scale of 1 to 10; 1: absent, 5: normal, 10: extremely much), appetite (scale of 1 to 10; 1: absent, 5: normal, 10: extremely much), general wellbeing (scale of 1 to 10; 1: severely reduced, 5: normal, 10: extremely excited), fitness (scale of 1 to 10; 1: severely reduced, 5: normal, 10: extreme excitement), sleeping behavior (scale of 1 to 10; 1: not present, 5: normal, 10: extremely much), presence of panting (scale of 1 to 10; 1: never, 5: normal, 10: extremely much), weight loss or gain (scale of 1 to 10; 1: extreme weight loss, 5: stable weight, 10: extreme weight gain), abdominal circumference (scale of 1 to 10; 1: extreme loss of abdominal circumference, 5: stable, 10: extreme gain of abdominal circumference), skin (yes: 1/no: 0) and fur (yes: 1/no: 0) changes, and behavioral changes (yes: 1/no: 0). A total score was calculated (from 9 to 93; perfectly well-controlled dogs receiving a score of 45). The dogs were then classified by the attending clinician as either well-controlled or uncontrolled based on the presence of typical clinical signs for CS (e.g. polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia, panting, weakness) as previously described [1].

Salimetrics® cotton swabs were dipped 1 cm into a tube containing commercially available ginger powder (TRS Asia’s finest foods, Ginger Powder, TRS Wholesale Co, Middlesex, England), as previously described [13].

For controls, blood drawing was performed using a 20 mL syringe from a jugular vein: 10 mL were collected in tubes with no anticoagulant (Sarstedt AG & Co, Nümbrecht, Germany) and 2 mL on ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid plasma (Greiner Bio-One, Gremsmünster, Austria), respectively, to perform full hematology and serum biochemistry aiming at confirming good health status. Immediately before, a veterinarian collected a salivary sample using a plain Salimetrics® cotton swab coated with powdered ginger. The swab was held in the dog’s mouth for 30 s, allowing the dog to chew on it to stimulate saliva production and enhance absorption by the cotton.

For dogs with CS, blood drawing was performed using a 3 mL syringe from a jugular or cephalic vein and 2 mL were collected in tubes with no anticoagulant (Sarstedt AG & Co, Nümbrecht, Germany). Blood was drawn twice at one-hour interval immediately before the morning trilostane dose (prepill cortisol values 0 and 1) to perform serum biochemistry and hormone measurements as part of the routine control. Saliva samples were collected immediately prior to each blood draw using the same method as for controls.

Dog owners of both groups were instructed to collect saliva samples three times in a single day (approximately at 8 a.m., 4 p.m. and 8 p.m.). They were asked to collect saliva at least 5 days after the hospital visit, to ensure that potential in-hospital stress would not have any negative impact on cortisol measurement.

Handling, excitement, and chewing movements were scored at each sampling time by the owners on a 5-point evaluation scale, with 1-very easy to 5-very difficult, 1-very low to 5-extremely high and 1-barely to 5-all the 30 s, respectively.

Analytical procedures

For controls, each of the 3 at-home saliva samples were first analyzed separately, and then a pooled sample was submitted again to hormone measurement. For dogs with CS, the 3 at-home saliva samples were pooled prior to hormone measurement to ensure sufficient material for analysis, after confirming no significant daily variation in the controls.

Blood samples were processed immediately for hematology. After separation of plasma or serum by centrifugation at 1500 x g for 10 min, serum biochemistry was directly performed. The remaining plasma and serum samples were stored at − 80 °C until further batch-wise hormone measurements. The salivary cotton swabs were centrifuged at 1862 x g for 20 min to recover saliva, which was stored at − 80 °C for later batch-wise hormone analysis.

Cortisol in saliva and serum was measured by LC-HRMS in an ISO 17,025 accredited laboratory as described previously [13, 14]. The minimum required sample volume was 50 µL. Saliva samples were thawed and centrifuged at 5500 x g for 15 min and diluted with 500 µL water. To the serum samples, 250 µL of zinc sulfate (0.1 mol/L; Sigma-Aldrich, Buchs, Switzerland) and 500 µL of cold methanol (− 20 °C; Sigma-Aldrich, Buchs, Switzerland) were added for protein precipitation and steroid extraction, samples were vortexed and centrifuged for 5 min at 8000 g. Both serum and saliva samples were then purified using solid phase extraction on an OasisPrime HLB 96-Well Plate (Waters, UK). LC-HRMS measurements (20 µL injection volume) were carried out by coupling of a Vanquish UHPLC to a QExactive Orbitrap Plus (both Thermo Fisher Scientific, Reinach, Switzerland). Separation was achieved using gradient elution over 12 min using water and methanol both supplemented with 0.1% formic acid (all Sigma-Aldrich, Buchs, Switzerland) as mobile phases. Data analysis was performed using TraceFinder 4.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Reinach, Switzerland). Hormone concentrations were calculated in nmol/L.

In dogs with CS, serum cortisol concentrations were assessed immediately by competitive immunoassay (DPC Immulite® 2000, Siemens Schweiz AG, Zurich, Switzerland) performed according to manufacturer’s instruction, at a commercial laboratory. The accuracy of the methodologies was assessed by continuous yearly participation in an external. quality assurance program, Veterinary Endocrinology External Quality Assessment Scheme of the European Society of Veterinary Endocrinology. The required sample volume was 300 µL. As reported by the manufacturer, the sensitivity of the assay was 5.5 nmol/L, and the intra-assay coefficients of variation were 10% and 6% at cortisol concentrations of 74.4 and 521.5 nmol/L, respectively. The target range for the prepill serum cortisol values was set at 28–140 nmol/L for CS dogs undergoing trilostane treatment. The results were immediately available for the treating clinician to guide therapeutic decision-making, as described previously [1].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using commercially available software (GraphPad Prism10, Graph Pad Software, San Diego, CA, USA; SPSS Statistics Version 25.0, IBM SPSS Statistics, Armonk, New York). Values were tested for normality by the d’Agostino and Pearson omnibus normality test. As most of the data were not normally distributed, ranges and median values are reported. Differences between locations were evaluated by the Wilcoxon matched-pairs sign rank test, differences between controls and dogs with CS by Mann-Whitney test, and differences over time for controls by Friedman test. Spearman correlation was used to assess the strength of the association between serum cortisol measured by LC-HRMS and competitive immunoassay in dogs with CS. Significance was defined as a P-value < 0.05.
