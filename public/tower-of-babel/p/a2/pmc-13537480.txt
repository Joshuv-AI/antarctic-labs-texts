Representation in robotic surgery randomised controlled trials: a systematic review
Authors: Sara Varadharajulu, Robin Ji, Sanket Dhruva, John Neuhaus, Ken Cohen, Rita Redberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRobotic surgery has been widely adopted across surgical specialties since its introduction in the 1980s, making it important to assess the representation of patients from varying demographic backgrounds in clinical trials. The objective of this study was to evaluate the demographic representativeness of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) on robotic surgery.Research Design and MethodsThis systematic review was conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines. PubMed, Embase and Web of Science databases were searched for English-language RCTs conducted between January 2000 and December 2022. Only RCTs that enrolled participants of both sexes and compared robotic surgery with a non-robotic comparator were included. Primary outcomes included the reporting of participants’ demographic information, specifically sex, age, race and ethnicity, the inclusion of subgroup analyses based on these demographics and the distribution of demographics within each study.ResultsOut of 75 RCTs, 71 (95%) trials reported participant sex, 73 (97%) RCTs reported age and four (5%) RCTs reported race or ethnicity. Six (8%) RCTs provided sex-specific results, three (4%) RCTs provided age-specific outcomes and no RCTs reported outcomes based on race or ethnicity. The average percentage of female patients per RCT was 47.1% and the average age was 59.7 (SD=9) years. Of the RCTs that reported race or ethnicity, 59.5% of the population was Caucasian, 25.0% was Hispanic, 13.9% was African American and 1.6% was Asian/Pacific Islander/other.ConclusionsThere was underreporting of demographic data, especially race and ethnicity of participants, a lack of demographic-specific study outcomes and underrepresentation of women and non-white participants across RCTs. This study highlights the need for greater efforts to improve diversity in robotic surgery clinical trials.

Introduction

Robotic surgery is an innovative approach whose use has increased dramatically, from 1.8% to 15.1%, across various surgical specialties between 2012 and 2018.1 2 Given this rapid growth, it is important to understand how well patients from diverse clinical, demographic and socioeconomic backgrounds are represented in the evidence base for this technology. Historically, there has been a lack of representation of women, older adults and racial and ethnic minorities in surgical medical device trials.3 Moreover, these clinical trials may not report the composition of study populations or incorporate subgroup analyses.35

Risks and benefits of surgical procedures often vary among demographic groups, so it is critical to enrol a diverse study population and analyse demographic-specific outcomes to understand a surgical technology’s effectiveness and safety.610 Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are the gold standard for evaluating the safety and efficacy of medical interventions and are most often relied on to guide clinical decision-making.11 12

We examined the reporting of demographic information—specifically sex, age, race and ethnicity—of trial participants and demographic specific-outcomes in RCTs comparing robotic surgery with a conventional procedure modality to understand the validity and generalisability of the evidence behind this technology. To assess whether the patient populations in RCTs represent the typical demographics of patients undergoing these procedures in real-world settings, we also reviewed observational studies of the same procedures performed without robotic assistance and analysed the distribution of key demographic variables across these studies. Age is a biological characteristic that is strongly associated with higher prevalence of disease and decreased responsiveness to treatments, in particular, surgical outcomes.1315 Biological sex is also associated with differences in manifestations of disease, treatment outcomes and complications.3 13 Race is a social construct based on social perceptions that has no biological basis; ethnicity is a characteristic influenced by geographic, cultural and sociopolitical forces.16

This systematic review aims to (1) assess the reporting of demographic information and demographic-specific outcomes in robotic surgery RCTs and (2) compare the demographic composition of RCT participants with real-world patient populations. Diversity in clinical trial participants can strengthen results and help reduce sex-based, age-based and racial and ethnic inequities in clinical care.10 15 17 18

Methods

Study protocol and registration

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) transparent reporting system for systematic reviews.19 The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO international prospective register of systematic reviews on 12 March 2023 (CRD42023396493).20

Search strategy

PubMed, Embase and Web of Science were searched for English-language articles from January 2000 to December 2022. The search strategy was adapted for each database’s indexing system: Medical Subject Headings terms in PubMed, Emtree controlled vocabulary in Embase and keyword-based searches in Web of Science. Core search terms across databases included “robotic surgery”, “robot-assisted surgery”, “robotic AND laparoscopic surgery” and “robotic AND open surgery” (see online supplemental appendix 1 for the full search strategy). Additional records were identified through reference list reviews of relevant systematic reviews. Articles from the literature search were exported to Zotero (V.6.0.37; Corporation for Digital Scholarship, VA, USA). Two authors (SV and RJ) assessed titles, abstracts and full-text articles for eligibility.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criterion was any full-length report about an RCT comparing robotic surgery with a conventional procedure modality (eg, laparoscopic) in a study population of both sexes. Exclusion criteria included studies that were not full reports (abstracts, trial protocols, oral presentations and comments on papers) and studies that included non-human subjects or cadavers. In addition, studies regarding skills development, teaching/training, learning curves and curriculum development with robotics were excluded.

If there were multiple publications associated with an RCT (eg, interim analyses, 6-month follow-ups), only the publication with the largest number of patients was included in the analysis in order to accurately capture the demographics of as many trial patients as possible.

Data extraction

The following variables were extracted from each study: publication year, country, type of procedure, robotic device name and manufacturer, number of patients, average age of patients, age-specific outcomes, number of female patients, proportion of female patients, sex-specific outcomes, number and proportion of patients across racial categories and number and proportion of patients across ethnic categories. Any missing data were noted. One author (SV) extracted data and another author (RJ) double checked and verified when data were missing.

Search Strategy and Data Extraction for Articles Reviewed for Demographic Distributions of Participants in Non-robotic Observational Studies, ‘Typical Populations’

We searched PubMed for observational studies analysing data published between 2000 and 2020 on non-robotic surgery methods to understand the ‘typical populations’ that receive these procedures. The following search terms were used: “procedure name” and “database” or “registry” or “population”. The observational study filter was applied. Out of the relevant search results, the articles with the largest patient populations were chosen to ensure adequate demographic representation of patients. Articles that were published in languages other than English were excluded. Articles that focused on specific demographic groups such as older adults were not chosen for analysis. If an article included patients undergoing multiple surgical procedures, only the demographics of the patients undergoing the procedure in question were extracted.

The following variables were extracted: number of patients who had undergone the surgery in question, average age of patients who underwent the procedure (median or mean was extracted depending on the article), the proportion of female patients in the study population and the race or ethnicity of study participants.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the reporting of participants’ demographic information, specifically sex, age, race and ethnicity, the inclusion of demographic-specific subgroup analysis and study-specific distributions of sex, age and race and ethnicity. It is important to note that research publications frequently do not make any distinction between sex and gender; accordingly, for the purposes of this study, all sex and gender reporting were grouped together as ‘sex’.21 22 In order to understand if the patient populations in robotic surgery RCTs reflect the ‘typical populations’ of patients receiving these procedures in more real-world settings, we also identified observational studies involving the same surgeries without the use of robots and analysed distributions of the same demographic variables in these studies. We used non-robotic observational studies for comparison in order to best represent ‘typical populations’ because previous research has found disparities in access to robotic surgery.23 For example, to assess whether patient populations in robotic hip arthroplasty studies reflect typical demographics, we compared them to a population in a retrospective analysis of patients undergoing non-robotic hip arthroplasty using National Joint Registry data.

A secondary outcome was representation of sex, age and race and ethnicity in robotic surgery RCTs that were conducted in the USA specifically. The US population is heterogeneous, and it is well-established that there are significant differences in health outcomes between demographic groups.24 25 Another secondary outcome was the temporal trend in demographic distribution, focusing on more recent RCTs (2020–2022), as prior studies have shown improved demographic reporting and representation in recent years.26

Statistical analysis

We examined the reporting and distribution of demographic variables—sex, age, race and ethnicity—across robotic surgery RCTs using univariate and bivariate analyses. We calculated the proportion of RCTs that reported sex, age, race and ethnicity and the weighted distributions of these variables across RCTs. Weights were assigned to data from each RCT depending on the number of participants in that specific RCT. We also calculated the proportion of RCTs that reported differences in primary endpoint by demographic variable.

The weighted mean ages of patients, the weighted proportion of female patients and weighted proportion of patients from different racial and ethnic groups in robotic surgery RCTs were then compared with demographic distributions of these variables in observational studies involving similar procedures. Two-sample z-tests for equality of proportions and Welch two sample t-tests were used to identify significant differences between study design types (p<0.05).

The subgroup analyses examined demographic variables in RCTs conducted in the USA and RCTs conducted in recent years (2020–2022). Sex, age, race and ethnicity in RCTs were explored in these subgroup analyses. Between-group differences were examined using t-tests and χ2 tests, and associations between variables were examined using Pearson’s correlation and linear regression. R Studio (V.2022.12.0-353) and Excel (V.16.77.1) were used for statistical analysis.

Quality assessment

The Cochrane Collaboration tool for assessing risk of bias in RCTs was used to assess bias.27 We adapted the tool to focus on three domains most pertinent to our research question: (1) selection bias due to inadequate randomisation procedures, which could create demographic imbalances between treatment groups; (2) attrition bias due to incomplete outcome data, which could disproportionately affect certain demographic subgroups and (3) reporting bias due to selective outcome reporting, which could obscure demographic-specific outcomes. Domains related to blinding were not assessed as they are less relevant to evaluating demographic reporting practices. Two authors (SV and RJ) independently evaluated each domain as having high risk of bias, unclear risk of bias or low risk of bias. Disagreements in bias assessments were discussed until consensus was achieved.

Patient and public involvement

No patient or public involvement in this study.

Results

A total of 4627 studies were identified through the initial literature search (see figure 1 for PRISMA diagram). After duplicate studies and studies written in languages other than English were removed, 4172 were screened by abstracts and an additional 3688 were removed because they did not meet inclusion criteria. The remaining 484 full-text articles were reviewed, and 97 articles were included in the systematic review. 22 articles that involved the same RCT population but had fewer participants were excluded, leaving 75 RCTs including 8654 patients in the analysis (see online supplemental appendix 2 for study descriptions).

Non-robotic observational studies for ‘Typical Populations’

47 non-robotic observational studies involving 2,700,459 patients were identified; the average number of patients per study was 57,456.6 (SD=8.7) (see onlinesupplemental appendices 34 for study descriptions and citations). Patient demographics from these observational studies were used to understand if robotic surgery RCT enrollment was similar to or significantly different from the demographic distribution of patients that typically receive these surgeries.

Patient sex

71 (95%) out of 75 RCTs provided a breakdown of study population by sex, and the weighted percentage of female patients per RCT was 47.1%. Six (8%) RCTs provided sex-specific results on primary endpoint(s); all these RCTs were conducted between 2017 and 2022. Three (50%) of these RCTs found significant differences in primary outcome by sex (see table 2 for RCTs with sex-specific analyses). One RCT showed benefit of robotic versus laparoscopic surgery in female patients, one RCT showed benefit of robotic versus laparoscopic surgery in male patients and one RCT found increased odds of conversion to laparotomy in male patients independent of surgical modality.

The weighted percentage of female patients across observational studies was 60.5%, which is significantly higher than the 47.1% across robotic surgery RCTs (p<0.001; 95% CI 0.12 to 0.14). For 12 (44%) of 27 procedure types, the proportion of female patients included in robotic surgery RCT populations was lower than the proportion of female patients in observational studies; for the remaining procedure types, the proportion of female patients in RCTs was equal to or greater than those reported in observational studies.

Patient age

Of the 73 out of 75 (97%) RCTs which provided age, the weighted mean age of patients across all RCTs was 59.7 (SD=9.0) years. Three out of 73 (4%) RCTs reported age-specific outcomes. One (33%) RCT found significant advantage to using robotic over laparoscopic surgery for patients at or over 60 years of age; no such benefit was found in patients under 60 years of age (see table 2 for age-specific analyses in RCTs).

The mean age in observational studies was 59.6 (SD=8.7) years, which is similar to the mean age (59.7 years) in comparable robotic surgery RCTs (p=0.52; 95% CI −0.250.13). For 22 out of 27 procedure types (82%), the mean ages of robotic surgery RCT participants were lower than the mean ages reported in observational studies; for the remaining procedure types, the mean age of patients in RCTs was equal to or greater than those reported in observational studies.

Patient race and ethnicity

Race and ethnicity data were reported by four RCTs (5%) including 561 patients. Two RCTs reported ‘race/ethnicity’, one reported ‘race’ and two reported ‘ethnicity’. Of the RCTs that reported race or ethnicity, three were in the USA and one was in both the USA and Greece. Across RCTs, 59.5% of the population was Caucasian or white, 25.0% was Hispanic, 13.9% was African American or black and 1.6% was Asian, Pacific Islander or other.

Due to limited race and ethnicity data reported in RCTs and observational studies, comparison of racial and ethnic categories between robotic surgery RCTs and observational studies could not be performed.

Subgroup analysis of US-RCTs

Eight out of the 75 (11%) RCTs were conducted in the USA only; these included 862 patients with a mean 107.75 (SD=84.8) patients per RCT. Procedure types included three cystectomies, two incisional/ventral hernia repairs, one inguinal hernia repair, one catheter ablation for atrial flutter and one Roux-en-Y gastric bypass. All RCTs reported sex and age of patients. The weighted average percentage of female patients across US RCTs was 31.4%, which was significantly greater than the 26.3% female patients across observational studies involving the same procedures (p<0.001; 95% CI 0.02 to 0.08). No US-RCTs provided sex-specific results. The weighted average age across all US RCTs was 61.2 (SD=8.2) years, which is similar to the 61.3 (SD=3.8) years across observational studies assessing the same procedures (p=0.61; 95% CI −0.40 to 0.67). No RCTs presented age-specific outcomes. Three (38%) US RCTs included race and/or ethnicity breakdown. Across US RCTs, 60.9% of patients were Caucasian/white, 23.1% were Hispanic, 14.6% were African American/black and 1.4% were Asian, Pacific Islander or other. Two (67%) of three US RCTs had Caucasian/white patients accounting 82.0% and 84.2% of the study populations.

Recent RCTs (2020–2022)

In years 2020–2022, 33 RCTs were conducted. All recent RCTs reported sex and age of participants, and three of 33 RCTs (9%) reported race or ethnicity. Three (9%) of 33 studies presented sex-specific and age-specific analyses, which is similar to the proportion of older studies that included these analyses (3 of 42, 9%) (p=1; 95% CI −0.13 to 0.16). The proportion of female patients across RCTs conducted between 2020 and 2022 was 52.1% (2403 of 4612), which is significantly higher than the 39.1% (1580 of 4042) across RCTs before 2020 (p<0.001; 95% CI 0.11 to 0.15). The average age across recent RCTs was 59.2 (SD=9.9) years, which is biologically similar to the average age of 60.3 (SD=7.9) years before 2020 (p<0.001, 95% CI −1.25 to −0.49). The recent RCTs that reported race or ethnicity were the same as the US RCTs that reported race or ethnicity (see subgroup analysis of US RCTs).

Overall, there was low or unclear risk of bias across the three evaluated domains in RCTs included in the analysis, with no studies rated as high risk of bias for any domain (table 3). This may reflect the quality of trials that progressed to publication, though it should be noted that other important bias domains (performance bias and detection bias) were not assessed in this review.

Discussion

In this review of robotic surgery RCTs conducted between 2000 and 2022, there was under-reporting of demographic data and under-representation of women and non-white participants. Most RCTs reported patient sex and age, but only around 5% (n=4) of RCTs reported race and/or ethnicity of patients. Across all RCTs, the average proportion of female patients was significantly lower than the average proportion of female patients undergoing these procedures in large observational studies. Caucasian/white patients made up close to 60% patients in robotic surgery RCTs, which is unrepresentative of the demographic makeup of the global population.28 Less than 10% (n=6) of RCTs provided demographic-specific results by sex, age and race and/or ethnicity.

There was suggestion of sex-specific and age-specific differences in response to treatment, even in the limited number of RCTs providing these data. The few studies conducting demographic-specific analyses revealed that robotic surgery benefits differed by sex and age, varying depending on the procedure. These divergent results underscore the necessity of routine demographic-stratified analyses to determine which patient subgroups benefit most from robotic approaches. The importance of adequate sex, age, racial and ethnic diversity is well-established, with prior research showing significant differences in short and long-term surgical outcomes depending on sex,2932 age33 and race or ethnicity.3437 Despite professional society and governmental guidelines recommending or mandating reporting of demographic data to highlight health inequities across different populations, robotic surgery RCTs failed to adequately do so.3840

Across US RCTs, there was a lack of adequate representation across racial and ethnic categories. Two of three US RCTs that reported race or ethnicity had over 80% of participants identified as Caucasian/white, compared with about 60% of the US population that identifies as white.41 This highlights a lack of diversity in US clinical trials which is consistent with previous research.42 43 Additionally, despite the fact that 6.2% of the US population identified as Asian, Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander (ANHPI) in 2022, <1.5% of patients in US robotic surgery RCTs were identified as ANHPI.44

In recent years (2020–2022), there has been some improvement in consistency of reporting demographic data. All recent RCTs reported sex and age of enrolled patients. However, race or ethnicity reporting remained persistently low at 9% (n=3) in recent studies versus 2% (n=1) in older studies, and the proportion conducting demographic-specific analyses remained unchanged at 9% in both periods. This suggests that despite literature such as the Sex and Gender Equity Reporting guidelines (2016) and the Journal of the American Medical Association’s guidance on reporting of race and ethnicity (2021), implementation in robotic surgery trials has lagged guideline development. Progress in sex representation has been notable, with female patient enrollment increasing significantly in recent RCTs. However, where race and ethnicity data are reported, under-enrollment of non-Caucasian/white patients still persists.

Addressing these disparities will require systematic policy changes. In 2024, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) released draft guidance for industry on ‘Diversity Action Plans to Improve Enrollment of Participants from Underrepresented Populations in Clinical Studies’.45 Unfortunately, this draft guidance has not been finalized and its future is uncertain. Diversity in clinical trials would be greatly improved if regulators such as the FDA required representative patient enrolment in both drug and device trials as condition of approval.46 47 Other strategies to improve diversity include requiring representative enrolment by sponsors, standardising demographic reporting through institutions such as the National Institutes of Health and having peer-reviewed publications mandate that authors explain any under-enrolment of demographic groups.48 Such changes would help ensure that findings from robotic surgery RCTs can meaningfully inform clinical practice across all patient populations.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, only English-language articles were included in the review, which limits generalisability. Second, exclusion of single-sex RCTs decreased the number of eligible studies for inclusion; the impact of this exclusion on the distribution of age, race and ethnicity reporting is difficult to determine, as we did not extract demographic data from these single-sex studies. Third, due to under-reporting and misreporting of race and ethnicity data in RCTs, racial and ethnic categories were grouped together. Additionally, race and ethnicity data collection is not permissible in some European countries; given that a number of included studies were conducted in these regions, this regulatory restriction likely contributed to the limited reporting of these demographics. Fourth, the number of US RCTs available for the subgroup analysis was limited, which reduced the strength of this analysis. Lastly, the availability and size of large observational studies varied by procedure type, which likely led to differences in accuracy when estimating demographics of typical populations that receive the procedure.

Conclusions

This systematic review underscores the presence of significant disparities in trial recruitment and reporting of demographic data within robotic surgery RCTs, although improvements in sex representativeness in recent years are promising. As the adoption of robotic surgery continues to grow across surgical specialties, it will be imperative that RCTs study robotic devices in populations that reflect their intended use.
