Simulation-enhanced interprofessional education in surgery – a scoping review
Authors: Sean Stevens, Oscar Morice, Deborah Colville, Vijayaragavan Muralidharan, John Paige, Debra Nestel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSimulation-enhanced interprofessional education (Sim-IPE) has been successfully used in various health professions and shown to improve communication and teamwork. Surgery, where preventable serious adverse outcomes are often attributed to issues with communication and teamwork, represents a compelling context for Sim-IPE. However, implementing Sim-IPE in surgery requires investment in faculty development, equipment and facilities while incurring the cost of reduced staff availability and theatre productivity during simulation activity. We conducted a scoping review to characterise how Sim-IPE in surgery is practised and researched.MethodsThis scoping review followed the methodological framework described by Arksey and O’Malley. Reporting followed the PRISMA-ScR checklist. Electronic databases (Medline, EMBASE, Web of Science, PsycINFO and ERIC) were searched for records pertaining to Sim-IPE in surgery published after 1st January 2000 and in English.ResultsThe final analysis included 81 articles. Simulation scenarios showed a rich variety of combinations of professions, disciplines, training levels and settings. While most scenarios used manikins (N = 54, 75%), other modalities included simulated participants, cadavers, task trainers, computers, laparoscopic simulators, role play and animal models with many scenarios using multiple modalities (N = 20, 27.8%). Of studies reporting on evaluation outcome, almost half (N = 32, 47.1%) were limited to participants’ views of their learning experience and satisfaction with the program. Few studies reported using educational theory to inform Sim-IPE design and even fewer used theoretical frameworks in the research design. Facilitators for implementing Sim-IPE in surgery included a skilled faculty, institutional support and evidence of benefit. Barriers included lack of protected training time, restricted access to facilities and unreliable ongoing funding.DiscussionStudies described a rich variety of Sim-IPE scenarios, including multiple professions, disciplines, training levels, simulation modalities and settings, facilitating training in a broad range of skills reflective of the complexity of modern surgical care. While barriers such as time, funding, and cultural resistance to simulation are commonly reported in the simulation-based education literature, these issues may be amplified in surgery due to surgical service pressures and traditional hierarchies (e.g. surgeon as “captain of the ship”). Overcoming these barriers will likely require structural (policy, funding, scheduling) and cultural (leadership, team identity) change.Keywords: Simulation-enhanced interprofessional education (Sim-IPE), Simulation-based education (SBE), Simulation-based training (SBT), Interprofessional education (IPE), Health professions education (HPE), surgery

Introduction

Simulation-based education (SBE) is widely practised within health professions education (HPE) [1]. A fundamental benefit of SBE is shifting clinicians’ focus from service provision to training, creating an environment conducive to education. Simultaneously, SBE facilitates experiential learning without patient risk [2]. Given the potential for high complexity, patient risk and practitioner stress, surgery appears particularly well suited to SBE approaches [3]. Predominantly, SBE has been used in surgery to improve medical knowledge and technical skills [3]. More recently, the use of SBE in surgery has expanded to train other core competencies of surgical practice such as communication, decision-making, teamwork and leadership (e.g. the Non-technical Skills for Surgeons (NOTSS) training system) [4–6]. While the term “non-technical skills” is widespread in the literature, the negative framing may be perceived to imply inferiority to technical skills [7]. As this is not the intent [8], this review adopts the term “professional skills” instead [9].

Interprofessional education (IPE) occurs when two or more health professions learn with, from, and about one another to improve collaborative, patient-centred practice and quality of care [10–13]. IPE has been shown to improve interprofessional collaboration and teamwork [14], reduced negative professional stereotypes [15] and enhanced patient care [16]. In recent decades, advocacy by prominent bodies such as the United States’ National Academy of Medicine and the World Health Organisation has helped drive the integration of IPE into HPE curricula [17–19]. Concurrently, the surgical community has emphasised the importance of professional skills as essential competencies in optimising patient-centred care [20–22]. Consequently, IPE has been increasingly implemented in surgical education and training programs and with favourable results [23–25].

Simulation has emerged as an effective method for conducting IPE (Simulation-enhanced Interprofessional Education or Sim-IPE) [12, 26, 27]. Sim-IPE has successfully been used in various health professions including nursing, medicine, physical therapy and pharmacy to improve professional skills [26–29]. Critique of Sim-IPE practice includes a need to incorporate IPE theoretical concepts into simulation design and strengthen evaluation methods [30].

Surgery, where serious adverse outcomes are often linked to issues with teamwork and communication [31–33], represents a compelling context for Sim-IPE. Evidence shows training in professional skills is associated with improved clinical outcomes in surgery [34]. However, implementing Sim-IPE in surgery may be onerous, requiring investment in faculty development, equipment and facilities while incurring the cost of reduced staff availability and theatre productivity during simulation activities [35]. This scoping review aims to map how Sim-IPE is currently practised, studied and reported within surgery and to identify gaps and opportunities for future practice and research.

Methods

A protocol for this scoping review has been published [36]. We conducted this scoping review in accordance with the methodological framework described by Arksey and O’Malley (2005) and progressed by Levac (2010) [37, 38]. Reporting followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist and was further informed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis and other best practice guidelines [39–41]. Protocol deviations are identified and justified below.

Eligibility criteria

Population

We included records where the study population comprised healthcare professionals – either students, trainees or fully qualified. Eligible records must have included surgeons, surgical trainees or medical students learning surgical topics.

Concept

We included records that described both SBE and IPE as applied to the field of surgery. We defined SBE as a learning technique whereby a real-life task is performed in a modified learning environment [42]. We broadened the definition of ‘IPE’ to include individuals of the same profession (e.g. medical practitioners) who belonged to different specialties (e.g. surgery and anaesthesia). Surgery was defined as the medical specialty primarily responsible for the management of surgical conditions. Records pertaining to obstetrics and gynaecology were excluded unless another surgical specialty was also involved. This was to constrain the scope of this review to be conceptually coherent given obstetrics and gynaecology represents a large and distinct area of surgical and simulation-based educational practice with its own extensive interprofessional literature. We also excluded records where surgery was not central to the learning objectives, team composition or simulated task, including scenarios where surgical involvement was peripheral or incidental to a predominantly non-surgical scenario.

Context

We included records on Sim-IPE in surgery published after January 1, 2000, as preliminary searches did not identify suitable records prior to this time. Eligibility was limited to hospital settings (e.g. emergency department, operating theatre or surgical ward) where interprofessional team-based care involving surgery is most concentrated.

Evidence type

We included non-interventional articles (e.g. editorials, commentaries, perspective articles, review articles and book chapters) when they contributed substantively to understanding Sim-IPE in surgery beyond simple background description, for example through discussion of program design, implementation, evaluation or unique insights into the topic. We intended to include non-English language records but this proved impractical and such studies were excluded along with conference abstracts.

Information sources

We searched five electronic databases for records pertaining to Sim-IPE in surgery: Medline, EMBASE, Web of Science, PsycINFO and ERIC.

Search strategy

Our search strategy has been published (Table 1) [36]. We planned additional simulation journal and grey literature searches but the database search yielded so many records that we deemed further searches impractical and with likely marginal benefit.

Selection process

We imported all records into Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia, www.covidence.org) for reference management and screening. Following exclusion of duplicates, two researchers (SS and OM) independently screened all records by title and abstract for eligibility, then reviewed full texts of included records. Differences were reconciled by consensus with input from a senior researcher (DN).

Data charting

OM, DN, DC and SS charted the data using Covidence with SS reviewing all records. In addition to charting scenario and participant characteristics, we collected selected research-process and reporting variables including ethics approval, funding and conflict-of-interest reporting to help characterise how Sim-IPE in surgery is being studied and reported as an emerging field of scholarship. The data items charted are shown in Appendix 1.

Data analysis and presentation

To synthesise and present our findings we analysed quantitative data (e.g. record and scenario characteristics) with simple descriptive numerical summaries and qualitative data using thematic analysis in an iterative process.

Knowledge user consultation and statement of positionality

We followed the ACTIVE framework for “stakeholder” involvement [43] but replaced the word “stakeholder” with “knowledge user” given the sensitivity of the term. Knowledge users were recruited by invitation and often held multiple roles including general surgeons (SS, VM, JP), an ophthalmic surgeon (DC), a surgical trainee (OM), simulation practitioners (DN, SS, JP) and surgical education researchers (SS, DC, JP, DN). They contributed to shaping the review’s scope and approach, interpreting the findings and reviewing drafts of the manuscript. The initial clinical knowledge user group did not include a non-surgeon clinician, so interprofessional clinical input was later broadened through review of the draft manuscript by additional knowledge users including a nurse simulation educator and emergency department physician.

Results

Selection and characteristics

The search identified 1145 unique records (Fig. 1) of which 81 articles were included for analysis (Appendix 2). The characteristics of the selected articles are presented in Table 2. Annual publication numbers increased from 2000 to 2023 (Fig. 2). The earliest article was from 2007 and more than half (N = 42, 51.9%) were published between 2019 and 2023. The 81 articles originated from 11 countries; 90% of articles were from the Anglosphere with 58% from the USA. Ethical review was not applicable for 14 articles (i.e. reviews, book chapters, commentaries and simulation protocols). Among the 67 interventional studies, 49 (72.3%) reported institutional review board approval or exemption, while 18 (26.8%) did not report ethics review. Only 36 articles (44.4%) reported funding. Of those, 30 (83.3%) received academic funding only, 1 (2.8%) industry funding only and 5 (13.9%) both. Sixteen articles (19.8%) received no funding and 29 (35.8%) did not report funding status. Conflicts of interest were declared in 16 articles (19.8%, typically authors affiliations with simulation companies). No conflicts were declared in 37 articles (45.7%) and 28 (34.6%) did not report on conflict of interest. Regarding study design, 43 articles (53.1%) were non-randomised experimental studies. Sixteen (19.8%) were simulation scenarios without evaluation. Less common study designs included reviews (N = 7, 8.6%), randomised controlled trials (N = 4, 4.9%), qualitative (N = 4, 4.9%) and mixed methods studies (N = 3, 3.7%).

Proportional representation of disciplines in Sim-IPE in surgery scenarios

Sim-IPE in surgery scenario characteristics

The characteristics of the 72 articles that described specific simulation scenarios were analysed. Nine articles were excluded because of the general nature of their discussion of Sim-IPE in surgery. All simulation scenarios included doctors and the vast majority (N = 62, 86.1%) involved nurses. Just over half (38 scenarios, 52.8%) involved the combination of doctors, nurses and other workers. “Other workers” included a wide variety of professions and roles including physiotherapists, social work, pharmacists, paramedics, radiographers, theatre technicians, perfusionists, physician assistants, health care managers and administrative staff, blood bank, pastoral care and patient transport. The next most common combination was doctors and nurses (N = 24, 33.3%). A small proportion of articles involved doctors only (N = 9, 12.5%).

General surgery was the most commonly represented surgical specialty (N = 46, 63.9%), followed by cardiothoracic (N = 6, 8.3%), paediatric (N = 3, 4.2%), otolaryngology (N = 3, 4.2%), vascular (N = 2, 2.8%), plastic (N = 2, 2.8%) and neurosurgery (N = 1, 1.4%), with surgical specialty not being specified in 11 (15.3%) studies. After surgery, the most represented disciplines were from critical care; anaesthesia (N = 44, 61.1%), emergency medicine (N = 19, 26.4%) and intensive care (N = 9, 12.5%) (Fig. 2). Amongst the critical care disciplines, representation was by doctors, nurses or both doctors and nurses. Medicine (N = 5, 6.9%), obstetrics (N = 3, 4.2%) and radiology (N = 2, 2.8%) were the other disciplines represented and all by doctors.

Participants were from all training levels: students (N = 16, 22.2%), trainees (N = 50, 69.4%) and fully qualified professionals (N = 53, 73.6%). All possible combinations of these levels were seen; the most common was a mix of students, trainees and fully qualified professionals (N = 27, 37.5%), followed by fully qualified only (N = 16, 22.2%) (Table 3).

Most studies reported simulation scenarios that were ex situ (i.e. occurring in a simulated clinical space such as within a simulation centre) (N = 48, 66.7%), with 21 (29.2%) being in situ (i.e. occurring within a real clinical space) and three (4.2%) including both (Fig. 3). Scenarios were set in various environments, most commonly the emergency department (N = 27, 37.5%) and operating theatre (N = 48, 66.7%), with fewer in the intensive care unit (N = 6, 8.3%) and surgical ward (N = 4, 5.6%). Eleven studies (15.2%) used multiple settings.

Manikins were the most commonly used simulation modality (54 scenarios, 75%). Other modalities included simulated participants (N = 11, 15.3%), cadavers (N = 7, 10%), task trainers (N = 6, 8.3%), computers (N = 5, 6.9%), laparoscopic simulators (N = 4, 5.6%), role play (N = 3, 4.2%) and animal models (N = 2, 2.8%). Twenty simulation scenarios (27.8%) used multiple modalities.

Most scenarios were conducted at a single institution (N = 53, 73.6%). Eight (11.6%) involved two sites and eight (11.6%) involved three or more sites. Three articles did not report the number of sites involved.

Participant numbers per study ranged from 5 to 329 (median 48; interquartile range 76). Ten articles did not report the number of participants involved.

Analysis, reported effects, educational theory and theoretical frameworks

No articles focused solely on individual (micro-level) outcomes. Most (N = 69, 92.07%) examined team (meso-level) outcomes while six (8.0%) studied hospital (macro-level) outcomes.

Studies reporting evaluation outcomes (N = 68) were categorised by the Modified Kirkpatrick Model [44] (see Appendix 3) as shown in Fig. 4. Almost half the studies (N = 32, 47.1%) were limited to participants’ views of their learning experience and satisfaction with the program (Level 1). Other levels were less common; modification of participants’ attitudes (N = 5, 7.4%, Level 2 A); participants’ acquisition of knowledge or skills (N = 14, 20.6%, Level 2B); participants’ behaviour in the workplace (N = 12, 17.6%, Level 3); changes in organisational practice (N = 1, 1.5%, Level 4 A); and, benefits to patients (N = 4, 5.9%, Level 4B).

Most interventional studies reported positive effects from Sim-IPE. These were commonly described as improvement in knowledge, skills, team performance or communication behaviours such as “speaking up” and “expressing disagreement” [45, 46]. Occasionally, studies reported “negative” findings such as reduced confidence in leading trauma resuscitations after Sim-IPE [47]. Other notable findings included learning benefits for observers [48], use of Sim-IPE to shape professional attitudes among students [49], incremental gains across repeat scenarios [50] and application of Sim-IPE to just-in-time implementation of new clinical processes [51].

Educational theory was seldom discussed: 69 (85.2%) articles did not mention educational theory. Of the 12 (14.8%) that did, the most discussed was Ericsson’s theory of deliberate practice (N = 6, 7.4%), Kolb’s theory of experiential learning (N = 5, 6.2%) and Salas’ theory of effective teamwork (N = 2, 2.5%). Vygotsky’s theory of social constructivism and complex adaptive systems theory were each mentioned in a single article (1.2%). Three (3.7%) articles discussed more than one educational theory.

Similarly, the vast majority (92.6%) of articles did not mention a recognised theoretical framework. Of the six (7.4%) that did [52–57], only one (1.2%) article [52] explicitly used a theoretical framework (Rogers’ Diffusion of Innovation theory) to anchor their research’s design and analysis.

Facilitators, barriers and major results

Numerous facilitators for implementing Sim-IPE in surgery were identified. Faculty with experience and skill in SBE were difficult to develop but highly valuable in designing and delivering Sim-IPE. Studies suggested that representation from participating professions and disciplines among faculty supported scenario design, participant buy-in and debriefing quality [58, 59]. Institutional support was crucial for facilitating funding, accessing clinical spaces, quarantining training time, and driving program organisation [57, 60]. Access to well-resourced facilities, appropriate equipment and clinical spaces was commonly reported as supporting scenario authenticity and delivery [4, 61, 62]. In addition, scenarios perceived as relevant to all participating groups appeared to enhance acceptability and learner buy-in [45, 60]. Finally, robust evidence of benefit, endorsement by national or international bodies and clear guidelines for developing Sim-IPE activities were seen as imperative for successful implementation [45].

In contrast, the most frequently cited barrier to implementing Sim-IPE in surgery was lack of quarantined time for training within regular working hours, with surgeons and surgical trainees particularly affected. However, there was concern that catering specifically to one profession or discipline was contrary to the ethos of team-based training. Another commonly reported barrier was developing faculty with the capability to both design and deliver Sim-IPE programs. Numerous studies reported the termination of successful Sim-IPE programs due to lack of funding once initial research grants ended. Cultural factors, such as prevailing attitudes toward both SBE and IPE, influenced the success of such programs, with some institutions reporting persistent scepticism towards IPE. Lastly, although recognised as beneficial, in situ simulation was particularly challenging to implement due to impacts on clinical service provision and revenue generation and this was especially so when conducted in the operating theatre.

Discussion

Using a robust scoping review methodology, we mapped a rapidly expanding but uneven literature on Sim-IPE in surgery. The field is characterised by a rich diversity of scenarios, learner mixes, modalities and settings but is limited by inconsistent reporting, weak theoretical alignment and a focus on short-term, learner-level outcomes rather than organisational or patient outcomes.

A recurrent issue was variability in reporting of ethical approvals, funding sources and conflicts of interest. Given the emerging nature of Sim-IPE in surgery research, variability in standards of reporting is unsurprising and parallels early trends in SBE research that prompted the publication of reporting guidelines [63]. Notably, while these guidelines specifically recommend disclosure of funding sources and conflicts of interest, they omit guidance on ethical approval.

Research within HPE has been criticised for variability in ethical conduct and reporting [64]. Yet, numerous ethical concerns are known to exist within HPE research (e.g. participant/researcher power imbalances, preserving confidentiality and ensuring psychological safety) [64–66]. These concerns have led to dedicated guidelines on ethical practice in HPE research [67]. Despite these resources, uncertainty seemingly persists regarding when ethical review is required and in the differentiation between quality improvement (QI) and research. Interventional studies submitted to peer-reviewed journals for publication clearly fall under “research” rather than “QI” [68, 69]. Consistently applying established reporting checklists, clearly documenting ethical approvals and distinguishing QI from research will likely strengthen Sim-IPE in surgery’s credibility as the field matures.

Cook et al. (2008) categorised medical education research as descriptive (“what was done?”), justification (“did it work?”) and clarification (“why or how did it work?”) [70]. The literature is dominated by descriptive and justification studies that generally show SBE is effective [71]. This review shows that trend to be present in Sim-IPE in surgery. We did not find any clarification studies, which the SBE research community has been challenged to produce [1]. Such clarification studies require moving beyond research paradigms typical of the physical sciences and utilising educational theory and theoretical frameworks, all of which were rare in the studies included in this review [72]. There is a lack of studies that apply educational theories or frameworks to guide both design and evaluation. Without such theoretical grounding, it is unclear which constructs are being targeted, how change is expected to occur and whether results are generalisable across settings.

Outcomes were typically evaluated at Kirkpatrick level 1 (participant response) and 2 (acquisition of new knowledge, attitude or skills). This aligns with previous critiques of the SBE literature that too few studies evaluate changes in clinical practice and organisational or patient outcomes [71]. However, the studies [51, 52, 57, 73, 74] that did evaluate outcomes at Kirkpatrick level 4 show that with robust methodologies, longer follow up times and greater resourcing, such work is feasible. Similarly, a minority of studies showed that multi-site studies are also feasible despite the coordination challenges. Furthermore, multi-site Sim-IPE offers potential efficiency gains (e.g. reduced faculty time designing scenarios). Most importantly, multi-site implementation improves the robustness and generalisability of the findings. Weller (2020) exemplifies what is achievable with sustained high-level support and funding. New Zealand’s nation-wide trauma team Sim-IPE program (NetworkZ project), demonstrated improved system-level outcomes across multiple institutions [57].

The generally positive outcomes reported by interventional studies were unsurprising given the broader SBE literature and the likelihood of publication bias favouring positive findings. More interesting were the less intuitive findings. Apparent “negative” outcomes, such as reduced confidence after training, may represent productive recalibration of learners’ self-assessment rather than educational failure. Another notable finding was the benefit to observers. This aligns with social learning theory [75] and suggests simulation programs consider explicitly designing for observer engagement and not solely active participation [76]. Doing so provides the additional benefit of increased throughput of learners through the simulation program. Similarly, the use of Sim-IPE with students may offer an opportunity to shape collaborative attitudes early, before negative professional stereotypes become established. Finally, the incremental gains reported across repeated scenarios highlight an important unanswered question regarding the optimal “dose” of Sim-IPE [56].

Although rigorous evidence of its impact is limited, Sim-IPE is widely implemented in surgical training and many scenarios are highly complex. In situ simulation, despite being operationally demanding, is widely practiced [77, 78]. While in situ and ex situ simulation appear to offer similar learning benefits at the individual and team levels, in situ simulation may provide additional organisational learning, such as the identification of latent safety threats [77]. Additionally, while manikins have long been used in SBE [79], this review found that Sim-IPE in surgery scenarios often employed multiple simulation modalities allowing training in a broader range of skills and to a higher degree of quality. For example, Fiorentino (2021) conducted trauma resuscitation scenarios with manikin and augmented the simulation by having participants practise communication skills (breaking bad news to a family member) with simulated participants [80]. Finally, Sim-IPE’s flexibility is a major strength. Studies show Sim-IPE to provide educational solutions to novel challenges within surgical practice. Prominent examples include rapidly training staff in new protocols related to COVID-19 [51] and in facilitating the safe rollout of robotic surgery programs [81–83].

Studies described a rich variety of Sim-IPE scenarios – especially in emergency department and operating theatre settings and involving surgeons and critical care. Yet, gaps in practice were evident. Settings such as the intensive care unit and surgical ward were rarely represented and clinics, multidisciplinary meetings and the maternity ward not at all. Certain disciplines, notably radiology, were under-involved despite their importance in time-critical multidisciplinary decision-making. Obstetric contexts were sparsely represented within the reviewed literature, although this should be interpreted in the context of our exclusion of obstetrics and gynaecology as a primary specialty domain.

Our finding that skilled, multi-disciplinary faculty and institutional support are key facilitators for the implementation of effective and sustained Sim-IPE in surgery programs aligns with findings from the broader SBE literature [84, 85]. Sim-IPE is more than a “plug-and-play” educational novelty; it is resource-intensive and relies on organisational investment. This highlights the need for deliberate faculty development programs and strong institutional buy-in when commencing or scaling Sim-IPE in surgery. Barriers such as unprotected training time, unreliable funding, and cultural resistance to simulation as an educational modality are commonly reported in SBE [86, 87]. These issues may be amplified in surgery due to surgical service pressures (e.g. clinical utilisation of operating theatres) and traditional hierarchies (e.g. surgeon as “captain of the ship” rather than “member of the crew”) [88–90]. Overcoming these barriers will likely require both structural (policy, funding, scheduling) and cultural (leadership, team identity) change.

Many simulation scenarios were centred on the team performance of an action (e.g. trauma resuscitation in the emergency department or an operation in operating theatre). Typically, there was a clear expectation of how the action should be performed. Thus, the capacity for team decision-making was minimally challenged. However, in clinical practice, complex scenarios arise where there is uncertainty regarding the best course of action. Such scenarios increase the likelihood for interprofessional conflict, which is linked to poorer patient outcomes [91]. While such complex decision-making scenarios may occur in the emergency department, the operating theatre, intensive care unit, surgical ward or in multidisciplinary meetings, they could be simulated in easily accessible non-clinical spaces and with minimal equipment. We consider such simulations – focused on team communication and shared decision-making under uncertainty – to be underexplored in surgical Sim-IPE and a worthwhile area for future development.

While focused on surgery, the approach used in this review may be helpful to educators and researchers in other clinical domains as an example of how Sim-IPE can be characterised across settings, participants, modalities, implementation factors and evaluation methods.

Limitations of this review include restriction to English-language publications and the decision not to search grey literature. Given the heterogeneity of terminology in this field, some relevant studies describing teamwork or communication without explicit interprofessional descriptors may not have been captured by our search strategy. Further, we recognise practice may be advancing ahead of published research in this emerging field (the “theory-practice gap” [92]), so we have missed innovative Sim-IPE practices not yet reported in the literature. Finally, we note a transparency point: the published protocol focused on mapping Sim-IPE in surgery practice and research. During analysis a secondary objective to identify gaps and opportunities was added. This objective guided our thematic synthesis and recommendations reported here.

Conclusion

This scoping review showed Sim-IPE in surgery is a new and rapidly expanding field. Studies described a rich variety of Sim-IPE scenarios, including multiple professions, disciplines, training levels, simulation modalities and settings, facilitating training in a broad range of skills reflective of the complexity of modern surgical care. The field needs more rigorous reporting, theory-driven design and implementation and higher-level evaluation to demonstrate organisational and patient benefits. Addressing the identified gaps – namely in multidisciplinary decision-making and under-represented settings – should be a priority for future practice and research.

Appendix 3

Modified Kirkpatrick level

Level 1: learners’ reactionThese outcomes relate to participants’ views of their learning experience and satisfaction with the program.Level 2a: modification of attitudes/perceptionsOutcomes here relate to changes in reciprocal attitudes or perceptions between participant groups, towards patients/clients and their condition, circumstances, care and treatment.Level 2b: acquisition of knowledge/skillsFor knowledge, this relates to the acquisition of concepts, procedures and principles of interprofessional collaboration. For skills, this relates to the acquisition of thinking/problem-solving, psychomotor and social skills linked to collaboration.Level 3: Change in behaviourThis level covers behavioural change transferred from the learning environment to the workplace prompted by modifications in attitudes or perceptions, or the application of newly acquired knowledge/skills in practice.Level 4a: Change in organisational practiceThis relates to wider changes in the organisation/delivery of care, attributable to an education program.Level 4b: Benefits to patients/clientsThis final level covers any improvements in the health and well-being of patients/clients as a direct result of an education program.
