A bibliometric analysis of systematic reviews and meta-analyses on diabetes mellitus: global research trends from scopus database (1988–2024)
Authors: Alaleh Dalvand, Hossein Aazami, Hassan Akbarniakhaky, Sajad Parvar, Reza Habibi, Ghazal Mohammadi, Ali Faryabi, Hojat Dehghanbanadaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetes mellitus, a global epidemic, necessitates robust evidence synthesis to guide clinical practice. This study analyzes systematic reviews and meta-analyses in diabetes research to identify trends, gaps, and key insights for advancing diabetes management.MethodsWe analyzed 10,714 systematic reviews and meta-analyses on diabetes mellitus, published between 1988 and 2024, sourced from Scopus. Publication trends, collaboration networks, and research clusters were examined using VOSviewer and Scimago Graphica.ResultsResearch output surged post-2011, with China leading overall (3,241 publications, 30.25%), followed by the United States (1,915, 17.87%) and the United Kingdom (1,560, 14.56%). Twelve clinical clusters emerged, including therapeutics (e.g., metformin, dual-agonist therapies), cardiovascular outcomes, lifestyle factors, mental health, maternal health, and bone health. High-impact studies underscored vascular complications, mental health links, and gestational diabetes risks, shaping practice. Recent trends emphasize dual-agonist therapies like Tirzepatide, digital health, and weight management, while gastric bypass surgery, bone mineral density, and maternal health reflect enduring influence. Research shifted from early methodological approaches to later comorbidity-driven studies, yet low- and middle-income country (LMIC) contributions remain limited.ConclusionThis study illuminates a dynamic evidence base for diabetes mellitus, spanning decades of impactful clinical research and emerging innovations. However, disparities in LMIC representation and insufficient long-term evaluations of novel therapies limit its global applicability. Future research must prioritize LMIC populations and sustained efficacy studies to strengthen diabetes management strategies worldwide.

Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) poses a formidable global health challenge, exerting immense pressure on healthcare systems worldwide [1, 2]. The escalating prevalence of DM has fueled a dramatic rise in research output, with systematic reviews and meta-analyses emerging as essential tools to synthesize this expansive literature and guide evidence-based practice [3, 4].

These study designs are particularly vital for diabetes mellitus, where diverse interventions and conflicting findings necessitate rigorous evidence integration. Bibliometric analysis, a quantitative approach to mapping scientific literature, offers a powerful lens to uncover trends, collaborations, and gaps within this domain [5]. While previous bibliometric studies have examined the broader diabetes research landscape or focused on specific subtypes, the evidence synthesis literature has remained largely uncharted despite its critical role in shaping clinical guidelines and informing policy decisions [3, 6].

This study employs bibliometric mapping to dissect the landscape of DM systematic reviews and meta-analyses, analyzing citation patterns, institutional networks, landmark contributions, and thematic evolution. By charting the field’s knowledge structure and identifying underexplored areas, our work provides a novel, comprehensive perspective on diabetes evidence synthesis.

Method

Our study was conducted using Scopus, a comprehensive academic database, on February 14, 2025. This database was chosen for its extensive journal coverage, advanced citation tracking capabilities, and inclusion of diverse academic outputs. The search strategy targeted systematic reviews and meta-analyses by searching for these terms and their variants in the title field, combined with diabetes mellitus-related terms in both title and abstract fields using Boolean operators. The complete Scopus search query was: (TITLE(“meta analysis”) OR TITLE(“meta-analysis”) OR TITLE(“meta analyses”) OR TITLE(“systematic review”)) AND (TITLE-ABS(“Diabetes Mellitus”) OR TITLE-ABS(“Type 1 Diabetes”) OR TITLE-ABS(“Type 2 Diabetes”) OR TITLE-ABS(“Non-Insulin-Dependent Diabetes Mellitus”) OR TITLE-ABS(“Non Insulin-Dependent Diabetes Mellitus”) OR TITLE-ABS(“Non-Insulin Dependent Diabetes Mellitus”) OR TITLE-ABS(“Non Insulin Dependent Diabetes Mellitus”) OR TITLE-ABS(“Noninsulin-Dependent Diabetes Mellitus”) OR TITLE-ABS(“Noninsulin Dependent Diabetes Mellitus”)) AND PUBYEAR < 2025. The search encompassed all publications from the database’s inception in 1988 through the end of 2024, focusing exclusively on English-language articles. We excluded non-English publications and other document types (e.g., retracted publications) to maintain consistency and quality in our analysis. The comprehensive bibliometric dataset, containing publication details (titles, abstracts, years), author information, affiliations, citations, keywords, and geographic data, was extracted in CSV format. Microsoft Excel was then employed for preliminary analysis to quantify publication trends, and institutional and country-level contributions.

To quantify publication trends, the Annual Growth Rate (AGR) was calculated for each year using the following formula:

AGR = [(Publications in Current Year − Publications in Previous Year) / Publications in Previous Year] × 100.

To further analyze trends, publication counts were tracked over time. Based on substantial shifts in annual publication volume and notable changes in trend slopes, three distinct periods were identified. Key transition points were observed at 2000 to 2001 (increase from 4 to 16 publications) and 2010 to 2011 (increase from 129 to 172 publications), marking the boundaries of these periods. These divisions reflect notable changes in publication volumes, with each period showing a distinct scale of research output. While growth rates varied considerably within each period, the overall trajectory and mean annual publications differed markedly across the three phases. For each identified period, several indicators were assessed, including the cumulative publication count, mean annual publication output, and mean annual growth rate (AGR). The resulting periods were characterized as “Emergence” (1988–2000), “Development” (2001–2010), and “Rapid Expansion” (2011–2024), reflecting the evolving nature of systematic reviews and meta-analyses in DM research over time.

Data visualization employed Scimago Graphica for line, bar, and geographic distribution charts, and visualizing scientific landscapes (VOSviewer) (version 1.6.20) for network analysis of author keywords, countries, and authors. In the VOSviewer analyses, we implemented specific occurrence thresholds for various network analyses: a minimum threshold of 20 occurrences was applied for co-authorship networks and country collaborations, while a minimum threshold of 10 occurrences was used for author keyword analysis. These thresholds were carefully determined through systematic testing to achieve an optimal balance between comprehensive representation and analytical clarity. This threshold selection was made following careful consideration to provide a comprehensive yet manageable representation of the data and achieve an optimal balance between inclusivity and clarity in our visualizations and analyses across different aspects of the study.

Using these parameters, we generated network visualizations that elucidated various bibliometric relationships, including international country collaborations, author co-authorship networks, and keyword co-occurrences. The spatial arrangement of elements in these networks reflected their interconnectedness, with proximal items signifying stronger relationships. Element significance was denoted by node and label size, while inter-node connections represented relationship strength. A clustering algorithm categorized related items, assigning distinct colors to different clusters based on their network associations. Period-specific keyword trends were analyzed by segmenting the dataset into the three predefined periods, with frequencies calculated in VOSviewer.

Based on the trend analysis, we identified three distinct periods in the field’s development (Table 2). The emergence period (E, 1988–2000) was characterized by sporadic publications, with a total of 22 articles published in 13 years. Despite the high mean AGR (69.59%), the absolute number of publications remained low, indicating a nascent field. The development period (D, 2001–2010) represented a significant shift, with publications increasing from 16 in 2001 to 129 in 2010. This period demonstrated steady growth in research output, maintaining a high mean AGR (57.52%) and an average of 52.50 publications per year. The rapid expansion period (RE, 2011–2024) demonstrated exponential growth in the field. The mean annual publications increased dramatically to 725.92, nearly 14 times higher than in P2. Although the mean AGR decreased to 19.36%, the absolute number of publications continued to rise substantially.

Highly cited studies

The top-cited systematic reviews and meta-analyses on DM demonstrate significant trends in research focus and impact. The majority of highly cited papers (24 out of 30) were published during the development period (2001–2010), with 6 papers from the Rapid Expansion Period (2011–2024), and none from the Emergence period (1988–2000). The most cited papers examined crucial aspects of diabetes: Sarwar et al. (3,875 citations) investigated the association between diabetes and vascular disease risk; Anderson et al. (3,218 citations) focused on comorbid depression in diabetes; and Bellamy et al. (2,672 citations) studied the risk of type 2 diabetes (T2DM) after gestational diabetes. Among journals, 7 of the top 30 cited papers appeared in Diabetes Care, followed by The Lancet (6 papers) and JAMA (4 papers). The research categories most represented were Interventions (11 papers), Associated Conditions (7 papers), and Risk Factors (7 papers). Additional categories included Epidemiology, Interventions (Nutritional) and Risk Factors, Associated Conditions and Epidemiology, Diagnosis and Screening, and Associated Conditions and Risk Factors. Discussion

Our analysis of 10,714 systematic reviews and meta-analyses on DM identifies three distinct phases - Emergence (1988–2000), Development (2001–2010), and Rapid Expansion (2011–2024) - reflecting DM’s rising burden and evidence synthesis growth. The Emergence period’s low output (mean 2.75 articles/year) indicates early constraints in tools and data. The Development period’s rise (mean 52.50 articles/year) aligns with milestones like the United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS) which likely boosted focus [8]. The Rapid Expansion phase (mean 725.92 articles/year) corresponds to a global epidemic [9], demonstrating the field scalability.

China’s dominance in DM publications (30.25%) corresponds to its high diabetes prevalence and research focus [10], surpassing the United States and United Kingdom, both key players in global health research. However, China’s quantitative leadership in systematic reviews and meta-analyses warrants deeper examination within the broader context of diabetes research ecosystems across different countries. While we specifically analyzed evidence synthesis publications, the underlying primary research landscapes provide crucial insights into factors driving systematic review production and quality.

Comparative analysis demonstrates significant differences in research characteristics between China and developed countries. Chinese diabetes research exhibits a distinctive methodological profile, emphasizing laboratory-focused investigations with greater concentration on basic science and pharmacological studies compared to patient-centered clinical research approaches in countries like the USA [11]. These patterns reflect broader diabetes research rather than specifically systematic reviews. Nevertheless, they provide essential context for understanding research environments that inform evidence synthesis efforts.

China’s laboratory-centric approach may partially explain certain characteristics observed in systematic review analysis. Countries with stronger clinical research foundations, particularly those emphasizing patient-centered studies and robust clinical trial infrastructures, may be better positioned to produce high-impact systematic reviews that inform clinical practice guidelines and policy decisions. This laboratory-centric approach may create limitations in the pool of clinically applicable evidence available for systematic reviews to synthesize, potentially affecting the direct clinical relevance of evidence synthesis outputs despite their numerical abundance.

Moreover, research quality patterns across different regions demonstrate substantial disparities in evidence synthesis capacity. Analysis of diabetes research from South Asian countries shows that approximately 50% of publications appear in lower quartile journals, contrasting sharply with developed countries where 77% of research is published in top-tier venues [12]. While this analysis encompasses broader diabetes research rather than systematic reviews specifically, it suggests that primary research quality foundations significantly influence a region’s capacity to contribute to high-quality evidence synthesis.

China’s substantial output in systematic reviews and meta-analyses, therefore, represents more than simple numerical superiority-it reflects a research infrastructure capable of supporting large-scale evidence synthesis efforts. However, transforming this evidence synthesis capacity into clinically meaningful outcomes requires careful consideration of the primary research characteristics that inform these reviews. The interplay between basic research focus and evidence synthesis output suggests significant opportunities for China to leverage its substantial research infrastructure toward more patient-centered systematic reviews that directly address clinical practice gaps and population health challenges.

Tehran University of Medical Sciences’ leadership position indicates Iran’s rising role, driven by regional prevalence [13] and supported by its prolific output in diabetes synthesis [14]. This institutional dominance reflects Iran’s strategic focus on diabetes research infrastructure, with Tehran University of Medical Sciences producing 1,470 diabetes articles between 2010 and 2019, ranking first among Middle Eastern institutions. Iran’s research capacity is further evidenced by housing 40% (8 institutions) of the top 20 Middle Eastern diabetes research institutions, indicating concentrated but effective research ecosystem development [15]. However, Iran’s institutional leadership in systematic reviews likely stems from Iran’s emphasis on evidence synthesis as a cost-effective research strategy, particularly given resource constraints that may limit large-scale primary research initiatives. The concentration of expertise at Tehran University of Medical Sciences represents a hub-and-spoke model for regional diabetes research capacity building, though researchers must evaluate the sustainability of the centralized approach for broader national research development remains to be evaluated.

Systematic review efforts cluster in East Asia, North America, Western Europe, Brazil, and Iran, while low-income regions’ minimal input demonstrates disparities in generating synthesized diabetes evidence [16]. Evidence clearly illustrates this pattern through authorship data showing that only 58.7% of global health publications about low-income countries include any low- and middle-income country (LMIC)-affiliated authors, with just 36.8% and 29.1% having LMIC first or last authors respectively [17]. The observed underrepresentation of LMIC contributions reflects deeper structural challenges in global research capacity and resource distribution. Research funding for diabetes studies remains heavily concentrated in high-income countries, with significant disparities in financial support reaching LMIC research institutions [18]. This funding inequality creates a fundamental mismatch between where diabetes burden is highest and where research resources are concentrated, resulting in limited context-specific data and understanding of diabetes management in resource-constrained settings [19].

Beyond funding limitations, LMICs face substantial infrastructural challenges that hamper research capacity building. Many countries lack comprehensive health information systems necessary for conducting large-scale evidence synthesis, demonstrating significant gaps in diabetes data collection and management capabilities [20]. Limited access to trained epidemiologists and biostatisticians essential for conducting high-quality research compounds these capacity constraints.

The concentration of research output in high-income countries perpetuates a cycle where global health needs remain unmet by research efforts. Diseases affecting predominantly LMIC populations receiving disproportionately less research attention [21]. For LMICs, the challenge of maximizing limited research budgets to achieve meaningful societal impact further constrains their ability to contribute to the global evidence base [22]. The relationship between scientific knowledge production and economic development creates a reinforcing pattern where countries with greater economic resources are better positioned to generate research output, while those with the highest disease burdens often lack resources to conduct comprehensive evidence synthesis [23]. The systematic exclusion has practical implications, as diabetes management models synthesized from high-income country research may inadequately address the unique challenges of low-resource settings. These challenges include medication accessibility constraints, health system capacity limitations, and context-specific cultural factors affecting care delivery [24].

The four collaborative clusters among 64 countries, with a total link strength of 17,086, highlight strong T2DM research networks. The United States and the United Kingdom lead, leveraging extensive partnerships [25], while Australia and China reflect Asia-Pacific and trans-continental ties [26].

Analysis of highly cited diabetes research articles identifies key themes that have significantly shaped researchers’ understanding and management of the disease. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses, representing high-quality evidence synthesis, have been particularly influential and widely cited in the field. Studies on associated conditions, particularly cardiovascular complications [27–29], have consistently demonstrated diabetes’s substantial impact on cardiovascular risk. Exploration of risk factors [30–33] has emphasized the importance of lifestyle factors and gestational diabetes in disease development. Research on interventions, both pharmacological [34, 35] and non-pharmacological [36, 37], has provided crucial evidence for treatment efficacy and emphasized patient empowerment in diabetes care. Epidemiological studies [38, 39] have offered valuable insights into global prevalence patterns, while genetic research [40] has opened avenues for understanding individual susceptibility. These highly cited studies, predominantly systematic reviews and meta-analyses, have not only advanced scientific understanding but also profoundly influenced clinical practice, shaping guidelines for diagnosis, treatment, and prevention. The studies underscore the multifaceted nature of diabetes and the need for comprehensive, multidisciplinary approaches in both research and patient care.

We identified distinct research domains in diabetes through cluster analysis, with the first cluster focusing on therapeutic interventions. The cluster emphasized medication efficacy studies, particularly examining SGLT2 inhibitors and Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) for T2DM management. Recent systematic reviews have shown that both drug classes significantly reduce cardiovascular and renal outcomes in patients with T2DM [41, 42], with SGLT2 inhibitors demonstrating a favorable risk-benefit ratio despite potential adverse events [41].

The second and third clusters investigated metabolic health and lifestyle interventions. Research in the clusters demonstrated strong associations between visceral fat mass and T2DM risk, while metabolic phenotypes significantly influence diabetes risk regardless of BMI [43, 44]. Meta-analyses have demonstrated that physical activity interventions can improve glycemic control in both T1DM and T2DM, emphasizing the importance of lifestyle modifications in diabetes management [45].

Clusters four through seven explored various aspects of diabetes. Studies within the clusters showed that COVID-19 significantly impacts patients with diabetic nephropathy, increasing the risk of kidney injury and disease progression [46, 47]. In the area of gestational diabetes, pre-pregnancy lifestyle interventions and modified dietary approaches demonstrated potential in reducing risk and improving outcomes [48, 49].

Research in the clusters emphasized the higher prevalence of depression in individuals with both T1DM and T2DM compared to those without diabetes, underscoring the need for mental health screening and management [50, 51].

The eighth through twelfth clusters provided significant insights into various aspects of diabetes complications and treatments. Studies in the clusters demonstrated that diabetes substantially increases the risk of cardiovascular complications, including coronary heart disease and stroke, with women potentially at higher risk than men [52, 53].

Researchers also explored genetic factors, with systematic reviews identifying several polymorphisms associated with diabetic neuropathy and nephropathy [54, 55].

In terms of treatment approaches, meta-analyses suggested that Traditional Chinese Medicine may have potential as an adjuvant therapy for diabetes and its complications, though more rigorous studies are needed [56–58].

Bariatric surgery showed promise in improving glycemic control and achieving diabetes remission in both T2DM and T1DM diabetes patients [36, 59, 60]. However, the relationship between diabetes and bone health remains unclear, with conflicting evidence regarding the risk of fractures in diabetic patients [61, 62]. The findings collectively underscore the complex nature of diabetes and its complications, emphasizing the need for comprehensive management strategies and further research in the areas.

Analysis of publication timing and keyword trends identified emerging research focuses in diabetes management, particularly on innovative treatments, digital health interventions, and effective weight management strategies. Tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP-1RA, has shown promising results in glycemic control and weight loss for T2DM patients, generating significant interest in its potential to improve outcomes [63–65]. Recent comparative meta-analyses demonstrate that while Semaglutide shows superior efficacy over Liraglutide and Dulaglutide in reducing HbA1c levels, Tirzepatide exhibits even greater effectiveness, achieving significantly better HbA1c reduction compared to Semaglutide [66]. However, beyond efficacy, cost-effectiveness analysis demonstrates favorable incremental ratios of $54,699-$70,147 per QALY gained versus semaglutide, well below standard thresholds [67]. Tirzepatide 15 mg achieved cost savings of $1,409 versus placebo while gaining 0.69 QALYs [68], and provides superior value at $985 per 1% body weight reduction versus $1,845 for semaglutide [69]. However, high upfront costs ($17,527 for 72-week treatment) create accessibility barriers despite long-term savings, raising equity concerns for patients without comprehensive coverage [70].

Simultaneously, the rapid adoption of digital health technologies, accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, has led to increased research on digital interventions for diabetes self-management and remote monitoring [7, 71–74]. The technologies offer scalable and accessible solutions for personalized diabetes care. The evolution of digital health interventions in diabetes management represents a paradigm shift from traditional care models toward patient-centered, technology-enabled approaches. We found that digital health technologies encompass diverse modalities including mobile-based applications, web-based support programs, teleconsultation platforms, and telemonitoring systems, each addressing specific aspects of diabetes self-management. Digital health technologies offer scalable and accessible solutions for personalized diabetes care, particularly benefiting elderly patients who can receive consultations at home, thereby reducing travel costs and improving treatment adherence [75].

Critical examination of digital health effectiveness demonstrates complex outcomes that extend beyond simple technological implementation. Meta-analytical evidence shows that digital behavior change interventions can significantly increase physical activity steps and reduce sedentary behavior breaks in adults with diabetes. Effectiveness correlates strongly with intervention complexity and duration. Interventions incorporating more than 10 behavior change techniques and lasting moderate to long durations show superior outcomes in improving light physical activity and moderate-to-vigorous physical activity [76].

However, the implementation of digital health initiatives faces significant psychosocial barriers that current research inadequately addresses. Systematic qualitative analysis reveals five critical themes affecting usability: unmet emotional needs, challenges in enhancing self-management, desire for comprehensive education, user experience complexities, and the risk of biomedical marker reductionism. The findings suggest that successful digital health interventions must transcend mere technological sophistication to address the holistic psychosocial needs of individuals with type 2 diabetes [77].

Digital health interventions demonstrate measurable improvements in key diabetes management parameters through their clinical efficacy. Umbrella review evidence indicates that approximately 53% of studies show significant HbA1c reduction through electronic health interventions, with text messaging reminders proving particularly effective among various intervention modalities [78]. Mobile health applications show clinically significant effectiveness in reducing glycated hemoglobin levels by 0.39% compared to usual care groups, alongside improvements in treatment adherence and self-management behaviors [79].

Recent research has refined researchers’ understanding of weight management in T2DM. While weight loss can significantly improve glycemic control and even lead to diabetes remission [80], the most effective approaches vary. Very low energy diets and meal replacements show promise for short-term results [80], while new pharmacological interventions like semaglutide offer potential for substantial weight loss [81]. However, long-term sustainability remains a challenge, underscoring the need for personalized, long-term weight management strategies in diabetes care [80]. The integration of digital health technologies with traditional diabetes care models requires careful consideration of implementation frameworks and sustainability factors. Future digital health interventions should prioritize the development of standardized protocols and guidelines that address both technological functionality and patient-centered care principles [75]. Comprehensive evaluation of cost-effectiveness, long-term patient outcomes, and healthcare provider perspectives remains essential for widespread adoption and healthcare system integration [78].

High citation rates for “Gastric Bypass”, “Bone Mineral Density”, and “Maternal Health” demonstrate the significant impact and ongoing relevance of these topics in diabetes research. The citation patterns suggest that in these areas have been particularly influential, potentially driving important advances in understanding the complex relationships between diabetes, surgical interventions, bone health, and maternal-fetal medicine.

Based on the trends observed in the bibliometric analysis, several specific areas for future research in diabetes emerge. Future studies could focus on the long-term efficacy of newer therapies like Tirzepatide, particularly in diverse populations, and further investigate personalized medicine approaches to identify predictive markers for treatment response. The optimizing digital health interventions requires rigorous investigation of multi-component approaches that combine behavioral change techniques with personalized feedback mechanisms, while addressing the identified psychosocial barriers to technology adoption [77]. Research priorities should include developing evidence-based frameworks for digital intervention design, establishing standardized outcome measures for digital health effectiveness, and conducting comprehensive cost-effectiveness analyses to inform healthcare policy decisions [82]. Researchers might explore sustainable strategies for diabetes remission, considering various demographic factors, and investigate integrated care models that address diabetes and the common comorbidities simultaneously. Evidence synthesis from low- and middle-income countries, particularly in Africa and South Asia, requires expansion. Future research should prioritize more systematic reviews and meta-analyses focusing on local data from LMIC regions to address region-specific risk factors, dietary patterns, and healthcare access disparities in diabetes management and prevention. The analysis suggests that long-term safety, adherence, and real-world effectiveness of new therapies are areas that could benefit from further research. Future meta-analyses integrating observational data and extended trial follow-ups might provide valuable insights in these areas. Studies addressing disparities in diabetes care and outcomes among different socioeconomic and ethnic groups, as well as research on the role of environmental factors in diabetes risk and progression, could provide valuable insights. Targeted meta-analyses are needed to evaluate the efficacy of Traditional Chinese Medicine in diabetes management. The specific direction of future research should be guided by ongoing developments in the field and emerging evidence gaps identified through continued systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

Study limitations

Several important limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, our study’s focus on English-language publications may limit the representation of certain regional research contributions, particularly from non-English speaking countries where local language publications might provide additional insights into diabetes management approaches and outcomes. We acknowledge that this exclusion may underrepresent valuable contributions from regions where research is primarily published in local languages (e.g., Chinese, Spanish, Portuguese). This linguistic bias is a recognized challenge in bibliometric studies and reflects broader patterns in international scientific communication, where English serves as the dominant language for disseminating high-level evidence syntheses. Future research incorporating multilingual databases or region-specific repositories could provide complementary insights into diabetes research patterns in non-English speaking regions. Additionally, our citation-based analyses are subject to inherent limitations including temporal lag effects, where recent publications have less time to accumulate citations, which may influence citation patterns and research visibility across different time periods and geographic regions. Furthermore, our reliance on a single database (Scopus), while methodologically consistent and comprehensive, may not capture the complete global literature on diabetes systematic reviews and meta-analyses, as different databases may include slightly different subsets of publications, and multi-database approaches could potentially provide additional insights into the research landscape. However, it is important to note that Scopus provides broader journal coverage compared to Web of Science [83]. Our temporal keyword analysis compared absolute frequencies across periods with substantially different publication volumes without normalization, which may affect the interpretation of keyword evolution trends and research focus shifts over time. Our temporal keyword analysis compared absolute frequencies across periods with substantially different publication volumes without normalization, which may introduce bias when interpreting keyword evolution trends and research focus shifts over time. Direct comparisons between early periods and recent periods can be particularly misleading for trend interpretation, as apparent increases in keyword frequency may reflect overall growth in publication volume rather than genuine shifts in research priorities. Additionally, our analysis did not employ sophisticated attribution methods for multi-country and multi-institutional publications, as such approaches present significant technical challenges and many standard bibliometric tools are not designed for these nuanced counting methods, which may affect the precision of country- and institution-level contribution assessments.

Conclusion

This bibliometric analysis of 10,714 systematic reviews and meta-analyses on DM reveals three distinct phases of research development from 1988 to 2024: Emergence (1988–2000), Development (2001–2010), and Rapid Expansion (2011–2024), with rapid growth since 2011. China emerged as the leading contributor (30.25%), followed by the United States and the United Kingdom, signaling shifting global research dynamics. Our cluster analysis identified 12 diverse research domains, ranging from therapeutic interventions to lifestyle modifications, reflecting the complexity of DM management. Highly cited studies predominantly focused on cardiovascular complications, risk factors, and treatment efficacy, shaping clinical practice. The analysis also highlighted emerging trends in diabetes research, such as digital health interventions and novel pharmacological treatments like Tirzepatide. Four distinct collaborative clusters among 64 countries demonstrate strong international research networks, with the United States and United Kingdom leading extensive partnerships. However, the study revealed significant disparities in research output from low- and middle-income countries and limited long-term data on newer therapies. These findings underscore the need for enhanced global research collaboration to address geographic disparities in evidence synthesis capacity, particularly supporting research infrastructure development in underrepresented regions. Future research priorities should focus on long-term safety and real-world effectiveness of emerging therapies, optimization of digital health interventions through multi-component behavioral approaches, and development of personalized medicine strategies for diverse populations. Additionally, there is a critical need for sustainable diabetes remission strategies and integrated care models that simultaneously address common comorbidities to address the evolving global challenges of diabetes mellitus.
