Assessment of CMIP6 models and multi-model averaging for temperature and precipitation over Iran
Authors: Narges Azad, Azadeh Ahmadi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, the performances of 40 Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 are evaluated against observational data at synoptic stations in Iran using various evaluation criteria. The results reveal diverse model accuracy across different climate conditions and criteria, emphasizing particularly notable disparities in the nonstationarity R criterion compared to others. Although according to the ranking of the raw and bias-corrected outputs of CMIP6 GCMs for Iran, the NorESM2-MM, AWI-ESM-1-1-LR, and MPI-ESM1-2-LR models are consistently among the top six ranked models for precipitation in both raw and corrected outputs. For temperature, MPI-ESM1-2-LR, TaiESM1, INM-CM4-8, and IITM-ESM are consistently among the top six models for both the raw and bias-corrected outputs of CMIP6 GCMs. The Bias correction methods, including quantile mapping and linear scaling, integrated with Bayesian model averaging, were applied. While quantile mapping demonstrates superior performance and less disparity than linear scaling, it proves ineffective for correcting biases at stations with bias nonstationarity over time. The RMSE for monthly precipitation ranges from almost 0 to 200 mm, with a large RMSE value related to the high precipitation stations, and the monthly temperature exhibits a range of 0 to 4 °C. The use of a multi-model ensemble improves accuracy compared to individual models, resulting in a reduction in the differences between the minimum and maximum RMSE values from 178.6 to 91.0. Additionally, the range for mean absolute error decreases from 126.9 to 93.3, and the difference in the correlation coefficient narrows from 0.9 to 0.42. Averaging models after bias correction prevents significant fluctuations while maintaining higher accuracy, in contrast to the second method, which involves bias-correcting models after averaging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74789-4.Subject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

The growing global concern over climate change underscores the imperative need for a comprehensive understanding of anticipated changes to effectively address their impacts. Governments, as demonstrated by initiatives like the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), actively compile and publish assessment reports grounded in global research. The recent release of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) emphasizes the crucial role of Global Climate Models (GCMs) in elucidating future climate patterns. However, despite their significance, considerable uncertainty remains in predicting and assessing climate change impacts on environmental and hydrological systems1–3.

The selection of suitable GCMs plays a pivotal role in enhancing the reliability of climate projections. While some studies advocate for excluding poorly performing models to improve accuracy4, others argue for incorporating a broad spectrum of models to encompass the range of potential climate scenarios5. The availability of multi-model GCMs datasets facilitates the enhancement of climate projections by offering a diverse range of climate scenarios for analysis.

In the context of aggregating outputs from multi models, Bayesian Model Averaging (BMA) has gained attention for its capability to analyze uncertainties and combine multi models. BMA assigns weights to individual forecasts based on probabilistic criteria, offering a more reliable representation of overall prediction uncertainty compared to primary ensembles6. Furthermore, the inherent chaotic nature of the climate system and the differences in GCM responses contribute to random or unsystematic model errors, posing significant challenges to climate projection accuracy7,8. These errors manifest as biases in historical simulations and impact the Reliability of future climate projections, addressing the biases inherent in GCM outputs is essential for aligning model projections with observed data. Bias correction methods in climate science have been extensively studied, with various approaches, including univariate and multivariate methods, evaluated for their effectiveness in improving climate model outputs. While some studies highlight the benefits of advanced bias correction techniques in capturing climate change signals and enhancing model performance, others emphasize the importance of traditional methods and their continued use in many climate studies9,10. Although multivariate bias-adjusting methods have been developed in recent years to better address biases, their more complex calculations make them more susceptible to bias nonstationarity. Consequently, univariate bias-adjusting methods, which are computationally less complex and do not account for potentially changing correlations, appear to be more robust10. Additionally, many climate studies continue to rely on these conventional methods11,12.

Given the numerous GCMs and various bias correction methods available, there is no single definitive approach in climate model studies. Studies have shown that climate change can yield varied consequences across different regions, and Iran, being an expansive nation with diverse climatic conditions, is no exception. The results are also sensitive to the selection of GCMs and the processing methods used. Additionally, these analyses are often computationally expensive. Therefore, model selection, application of bias correction methods, and aggregation of outputs from multiple models are standard steps in climate model studies. Evaluating the performance of these GCMs, along with the effectiveness of bias correction and averaging methods in specific case studies, motivates this study and can provide readers with essential information valuable for their own research, especially on Iran.

Extensive research has evaluated climate models across various regions, including Australia13, Europe14, Africa15,16, the Americas17, Southeast Asia18, East Asia19–21, India22,23, and the Middle East24,25. For the specific region of Iran, Zamani and Berndtsson26 ranked and evaluated 20 CMIP5 models over three river basins. Zamani et al.27 compared 6 GCMs of CMIP6 and CMIP5 models in Iran, finding CMIP6’s superior performance. Yazdandoost et al.28 assessed the performance of precipitation estimates from historical runs of the CMIP6. Using a set of deterministic evaluation metrics, they compared 12 GCMs to observation data from the Global Precipitation Climatology Centre (GPCC) at a common 1° spatial resolutions for the period 1950–2014. Results inform what types of models are suitable for different climate zones. Zareian et al.29 evaluate the effectiveness of 10 CMIP6 climate models in simulating regional precipitation and temperature patterns using reanalysis data from the ECMWF database (ERA5). By analyzing model outputs, the research identifies how well these models capture climatic variations specific to Iran and its neighboring areas. Zabihi and Ahmadi30 assessed the performance of CMIP6 models for precipitation and temperature using a global dataset (UDEL) over Iran as observational data. Additionally, Yousefi et al.31 investigated future precipitation and meteorological droughts across the country, considering the performance of 41 GCMs.

Despite the progress made with CMIP6 models, several gaps remain. A thorough analysis across diverse regions and time periods, particularly in Iran, is still imperative. The varied consequences of climate change underscore the necessity for greater precision in model selection and analysis. Recent studies have often examined limited climate models or from CMIP526–29, one variable31 and relied on limited deterministic criteria27,30,31. Previous research has focused on evaluating climate models to identify the best ones using various evaluation criteria (ECs). This paper expands on the previous research by utilizing a large number of CMIP6 GCMs and employing a wide range of ECs, each assessing specific aspects of the models. Additionally, it evaluates the effectiveness of bias correction methods across different regions using recorded observational data. The study illustrates the efficiency and challenges of bias correction methods during historical periods, divided into calibration and verification phases. Moreover, the Bayesian averaging method is employed to aggregate the results of the climate models, with an examination of its strengths and weaknesses. In other words, this study not only evaluates a large-number of climate models, but also provides valuable insights for readers and researchers by depicting the evaluation results of bias correction and averaging methods.

In this study, the existing gaps were addressed through the evaluation of sixth-generation climate models, with a specific focus on disparities among different regions in Iran. The objective of this research is to assess the simulated temperature and precipitation of 40 CMIP6 GCMs across historical periods. Initially, the accuracy of the CMIP6 models is assessed using various ECs from the main categories of deterministic, dichotomous, and probabilistic approaches, as well as trend and nonstationarity criteria, to identify models suitable for the study regions. Bias correction methods are then implemented to enhance the raw outputs of the climate models. Following bias correction, the outcomes are compiled using the Bayesian weighted averaging technique. In alternative cases, uncorrected climate models are combined via weighted averaging, and bias correction is subsequently applied. In order to improve the effectiveness and accuracy of ensemble climate models, various combinations of superior models based on different ECs are tested. This research involves discussing the effectiveness of bias correction methods and the selection of appropriate climate models. Additionally, the consequences of utilizing weighted averaging methods for climate models were investigated.

Case study ad data

Iran, situated in Western Asia, showcases significant geographical diversity that profoundly influences its climate. Spanning from the Caspian Sea in the north to the Persian Gulf in the south, with the Oman Sea nearby, the country experiences a wide range of climatic conditions, encompassing arid deserts and more temperate zones. The northern coastal regions benefit from a milder, humid climate and higher precipitation, thanks to the Alborz mountain range acting as a moisture conduit from the Caspian Sea. Conversely, the central and southern regions face hot and arid weather patterns due to the formidable barrier presented by the Zagros Mountains, resulting in decreased precipitation and high summer temperatures. The intricacies of Iran’s climatic variations underscore the need for a systematic and scientific approach to assess and address the challenges posed by climate change. The results of this study can extend to critical areas such as water resource management, sustainable agricultural practices, and the development of resilient infrastructure.

In the study, simulations of precipitation (Pr) and minimum and maximum temperatures (Tmax and Tmin) were utilized from thirteen climate models, as detailed in Table 1. These simulations, derived from GCMs, are obtained from CMIP6 (https://esgf-node.llnl.gov/search/cmip6/). Additionally, observational data, including daily precipitation and minimum and maximum temperatures, are sourced from the Iran Meteorological Organization. The data spans from 1961 to 2014 for 33 stations and from 1985 to 2014 for 64 stations, representing the longest available periods for all synoptic stations. Results and discussion

CMIP6-GCM model analysis

An assessment of a climate model’s accuracy is necessary due to the performance variability across models within a single climatic region and across different climatic zones. Neglecting this evaluation can lead to significant errors in data interpretation and the dissemination of inappropriate findings. To address the issue of climate model selection and the utilization of information from multiple models, a comparative analysis of 40 climate models was conducted across 64 synoptic stations for the period from 1985 to 2014. The results were aggregated for the whole of Iran and its six main watersheds. The watershed-specific results are presented in the supplementary material.

Various ECs were employed to assess the performance of the models in comparison to observational data. These criteria include deterministic, dichotomous, and probabilistic measures, as well as trend and nonstationarity analysis metrics. Deterministic criteria such as Mean Bias (MB), Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and the Correlation Coefficient (CC) measure the differences and relationships between observed and simulated values, offering insights into the model’s predictive capability. Dichotomous criteria, including the Frequency Bias Index (FBI) and Peirce Skill Score (PSS), evaluate the categorical predictions of the model by comparing the frequency of forecasted events with observed events. Probabilistic criteria like the Linear Error in Probability Space (LEPS) score, Brier Score (BS), and Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS) assess the probabilistic forecasts, quantifying the accuracy and reliability of predicted probabilities. Trend analysis tools such as the Standardized Mann-Kendall Test (S_MK) and Sen’s Slope (SS) are used to evaluate trends and changes in the time series data. Additionally, the R criterion evaluates the bias nonstationarity in simulated time series, providing insights into the model’s ability to capture long-term climate patterns. The ECs were applied to each station to assess the performance of the models with respect to the observed data. The results showed that the rankings of the models varied across different ECs and also differed across various stations according to specific criteria. Therefore, to summarize, the average results across various stations were calculated to determine the overall rank for each model based on specific criteria. Figures 2 and 3 depict the rankings of raw GCMs based on criteria related to precipitation and temperature, respectively. Each row of the diagram corresponds to a specific criterion, while each column corresponds to a CMIP6 GCM. The names of the criteria and models are positioned in their respective rows and columns. Additionally, the ranking of each model is represented by a color bar: darker colors indicate superior models, whereas brighter colors represent lower-ranked models.

Based on the results shown in Figs. 6 and 7, the range of ECs after bias correction is expected to improve and reduce the extent of variation. In many ECs, the bias correction method successfully improves the amount and reduces the range of variation, demonstrating the effectiveness and applicability of the QM bias correction method. However, for PSS and R criteria for both temperature and precipitation, as well as LEPS for temperature, the opposite effect is observed: the range R increases and PSS decreases after bias correction. A decrease in the value of the R criterion and an increase in PSS will indicate the improvement of the modified model and the proper performance of the bias correction method.

PSS measures forecasting skill by comparing the number of correct predictions to incorrect ones, while the R criterion indicates bias nonstationarity, reflecting the variation in bias over time. The limited performance of top-ranked models and the restricted applicability of the bias correction method based on these ECs need further discussion. The inherent variability in the data, which the bias correction method might not sufficiently address, could be one reason for this discrepancy. Additionally, the QM method may fail to fully capture the nonstationary nature of the bias, leading to suboptimal adjustments. The specific characteristics of the models and datasets used might also impact the effectiveness of the bias correction. Furthermore, variations in regional climate patterns and the temporal resolution of the data can have an impact on the correction method’s performance. Contrary to the simplified assumption of constant bias that underpins bias correction methods like QM, nonstationarity in bias exists in climate model outputs. Consequently, the bias correction method may be inadequate under such nonstationary conditions, resulting in poor performance and improper corrections. Thus, while the QM method shows potential, its limitations in addressing these complex factors require further investigation and refinement. The next section uses the QM bias correction method over a longer period of time (1961–2014) and shows the results for different stations to give a more in-depth look at how well it works and how the raw and bias-corrected outputs of GCMs change.

Bias correction

The outputs of 40 climate models for 33-gauge stations were bias-corrected using two methods: linear scaling (LS) and QM. The effectiveness of these bias correction methods was assessed during the historical period, employing observational data.

Bias correction methods comparison

During the observational data period from 1961 to 2014, both QM and LS methods were employed in climate model simulations covering a 54-year historical span. The assessment of each model’s performance at each station, in comparison to the corresponding observational data, involved the consideration of criteria such as RMSE, MAE, and CC. In Fig. 8, the variations in criteria values resulting from the application of these two bias correction methods are visually illustrated. Temperature and precipitation are represented in columns ‘a’ and ‘b,’ respectively. Each model is uniquely identified by a distinct color in the figures.

Changes in RMSE, MAE, and CC of the corrected outputs using QM and LS.

In each figure, 33 pairs of data are represented, symbolizing two corresponding datasets for each of the 33 stations. On the left side of each data pair, criterion values based on the QM method are depicted, while on the right side, values based on LS are presented. Essentially, the RMSE, MAE, and CC values for each corrected model at each station, using the QM method, are linked to the corresponding values using the LS method. The LS method demonstrates a higher level of result dispersion compared to the QM approach. Specifically, LS outputs exhibit a notable degree of divergence; this contrasts with the bias correction results attained through the QM method, which effectively alleviates uncertainties associated with model selection. Overall, the LS method caused an impairment in the performance of lower-accuracy models while marginally enhancing the performance of higher-precision models.

The inherent nature of the LS correction method, which relies on simulated data with an additive value (temperature) or multiplying factor (precipitation), makes it highly sensitive to simulation performance. This analysis focuses on a historical period, where the results may be more reliable than for future projections. Future projections are expected to contain more errors. However, as illustrated in Fig. 4, the QM results are more reliable since the QM correction is based on variable distribution probability, providing more dependable outcomes. Although the LS method is less sensitive to the selection of General Circulation Models (GCMs), with results being closer to each other, the reliability of LS outcomes is significantly lower if a low-performance model is chosen for a case study. This is not an issue for the QM method. Moreover, selecting the top-performing model based on historical data does not necessarily ensure more reliable future projections due to the nonstationarity of climate variables. Thus, the improvement in LS method performance relative to QM does not guarantee similar results for other periods. In summary, based on the findings, QM outputs are more reliable and applicable than LS for future projections.

After determining that the performance of QM method in general better than the LS method, additional analysis is performed. To conduct a more comprehensive evaluation of bias correction methods’ performance, the 54-year recorded data for temperature and precipitation at 33 stations were divided into two 27-year periods. The first period, spanning from 1961 to 1987, is designated as the historical calibration period, while the second period, ranging from 1988 to 2014, serves as the test period for validation. What is of paramount importance is the effectiveness of the bias correction method, especially in instances where the time series of raw simulations exhibits a constant bias from observational data. In these cases, the corrected outputs, depicted in green and red in Figs. 9 and 10, respectively, closely approximate the observational time series and exhibit a comparable trend. These circumstances underscore the high proficiency of the QM method in mitigating biases within climate models. However, challenges arise in situations where the bias between observational and simulated time series is inconsistent, leading to divergent trends. In such cases, the performance of the bias correction method falls short, potentially resulting in corrected simulations that deviate from the actual values, either surpassing or falling below.

After examining all stations, a notable observation emerged: in the coastal stations of the Caspian Sea, which experience higher annual precipitation on average compared to other locations, the output of climate model simulations exhibits a relatively significant bias in relation to observational precipitation data. However, since their bias remains relatively constant and stationary in relation to observations, the QM method has demonstrated satisfactory performance in correcting these biases. Therefore, it can be argued that, more than the bias of climate models, the bias trend, whether stationary or nonstationary, becomes crucial. The results of this research focus on examining the simulation capabilities of climate models and the performance of correction methods. They indicate that the appropriate selection of climate models for the specific study region and the suitable bias correction method are crucial, depending on diverse research objectives. This significance has been highlighted in previous studies and emphasized in various aspects of the investigation and conclusion10,32,33. But selecting an appropriate model is challenging because it depends on various factors, and the characteristics exhibited by each model differ according to various criteria and the geographical location under consideration. This issue is particularly challenging in Iran, given its diverse climatic regions.

Multi model ensemble

GCMs are inherently uncertain, with no single model consistently outperforming others34,35. The selection of the optimal model can vary depending on climate conditions, variable types, and the specific objectives of the study. To improve the effectiveness and accuracy of ensemble climate models, this study applied Bayesian Model Averaging (BMA) at each monitoring station. Various combinations of top models, selected based on different evaluation criteria (ECs), were tested at each station. The models whose aggregated results yielded the highest accuracy were selected, though these could differ across stations. In other words, the most accurate models were initially identified using model evaluation criteria, and weights were then assigned to these top-performing models using a Bayesian approach through BMA.

Bayesian model averaging

BMA was used to determine individual model weights through two distinct procedures. Initially, Bayesian weighted averaging was applied to raw climate model outputs at each station point, referred to as BMA (Raw). The bias correction method using QM was then applied to produce time series data at each station, named QM (BMA (Raw)). In the second procedure, uncorrected outputs from individual climate models underwent initial bias correction using the QM method. Subsequently, a Bayesian weighted averaging technique aggregated outputs from the models, collectively referred to as BMA (QM (Raw)). In Figs. 11 and 12, the observed changes in the time series data for precipitation and temperature are illustrated, respectively. The utilization of a multi-model ensemble of bias-corrected models mitigates extreme values (fluctuations or variability) in the time series. This is done by taking the weighted average of the models. In contrast, QM (BMA (Raw)) demonstrates significant variations in the time series due to the bias-corrected approach applied after the weighted average of models, thus replicating the variability observed in the data.

It should be noted that the evaluations are based on the accuracy of the aggregated model compared to observations using deterministic criteria. Thus, the closer the simulation average is to the observed average, the better the evaluation results. However, multi model averaging approaches can also have limitations. Specifically, depending on the objectives of the study, model aggregation might not always be appropriate. For example: When it is crucial to understand the full range of potential outcomes, individual models can provide valuable insights into extreme scenarios or variability that might be masked by the ensemble mean. Individual models may excel in particular conditions or regions, and their unique strengths might be diluted when averaged with other models. In some decision-making scenarios, detailed insights from individual models are preferable, especially if those models have particular strengths or if specific conditions are better represented by individual simulations. These considerations highlight that while ensemble methods can enhance overall accuracy, they may not always be the best choice for every assessment.

At the end the key findings of this research are summarized as follows:

The performance of climate model simulations varies across different locations. Bias correction tends to enhance agreement when uncorrected model outputs align with observational data trends. However, if raw model outputs diverge from observational trends, bias correction will not effectively align results with reality. When comparing the results of two bias correction methods, it is observed that models with higher accuracy are more effectively improved by LS, and less enhancement is provided for models with lower accuracy. On the other hand, when compared to LS, the QM method reduces response variations by bringing the corrected results of various models closer to one another. The results of the multi-model ensemble, BMA, reveal that the MME performs better when compared to individual corrected models. The reduction of error metrics and the enhancement of correlation coefficients are attributed to the BMA method. As the BMA method moderates both minimum and maximum values, it is advisable to consider appropriate ensemble methods for studies focusing on daily scales, especially for extreme events.

The studies aiming to showcase climate models with superior simulation performance, either as optimal models for the specific area under investigation or as inputs for impact models, reveal, according to the results presented in this paper, that when considering the overall ranking (RM), the selected top models are positioned lower based on the R nonstationarity criterion. In other words, while the ECs during the calibration period may indicate good performance, the models may also exhibit temporal nonstationarity, thereby influencing the projection results. Hence, in evaluating the overall rank, the application of equal weights to various criteria becomes a subject of discussion and investigation. Given the debatable nature of judging and adjusting climate models in the presence of non-stationary bias trends, prioritizing the R criterion might not only fail to aid in selecting better models but could also potentially assign greater significance to models with moderate or low performance in predicting future outcomes.

On the other hand, when comparing the results of selecting a specific number of top models for a multi-model ensemble, it becomes evident that averaging a large number of models generally outperforms the selection of a limited number of top models, even if the differences are small. Since this research employs a Bayesian averaging method, it suggests that aggregating a larger number of models yields more accurate results. However, multi-model averaging approaches also come with limitations. Depending on the study’s objectives, model aggregation may not always be the most appropriate strategy. For instance, when it is essential to capture the full spectrum of possible outcomes, individual models can offer valuable insights into extreme scenarios or variability that might be obscured by the ensemble mean. Some models may perform exceptionally well under specific conditions or in certain regions, and their unique strengths could be diluted when averaged with other models. Moreover, if a different aggregation or averaging method is used, such as in studies where machine learning techniques are employed to combine data from various climate models, it may be necessary to carefully consider the number and types of models incorporated into the ensemble. This ensures that the ensemble is tailored to the study’s specific needs and objectives.

Methodology

In the opening section of this research, the evaluation of the CMIP6 GCMs is conducted using various deterministic, dichotomous, and probabilistic criteria. These criteria include Root Mean Square Error as proposed by Legates and McCabe36, Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency according to Nash and Sutcliffe37, Pearson Correlation Coefficient following Wilks’ methodology38, S_ManKendall as introduced by Hamed and Rao39, Sen’s Slope based on Sen’s work40, Mean Bias according to Willmott and Matsuura’s approach41, Mean Absolute Error38, and R-criterial as defined by Van de Velde et al.10. Additionally, the Frequency Bias Index and Peirce Skill Score are employed to evaluate the skill of dichotomous forecasts. Probabilistic criteria such as the Brier Score, Continuous Ranked Probability Score, and Linear Error in Probability Space Score, as described by Wilks38, have also been incorporated to enhance the robustness of the evaluation framework. These additions allow for a more nuanced assessment of model performance, particularly in terms of forecasting accuracy and probability distributions. The selection of these criteria is grounded in their widely recognized significance in climate modeling assessments, providing a comprehensive and robust framework for evaluating model performance across various aspects, including accuracy, reliability, and the analysis of nonstationarity. In this study, a comprehensive Rating Metrics3,42 is employed to determine the overall rankings of GCMs. The overall rankings for each time series are based on these diverse criteria, ensuring a thorough evaluation of the models. Details of this method are provided in Appendix 1, listed in Table 3.

Subsequently, bias correction methods are applied to the downscaled outputs of the climate models through two methods: linear scaling43 and quantile mapping44. Bayesian Model Averaging6 is then employed to calculate the weighted average of selected bias-corrected climate model outputs (the methods are explained in detail in Appendix 2). BMA is a statistical inference approach used to obtain probabilistic predictions from ensemble simulations of multiple models based on Bayesian theory. Simultaneously, the raw outputs are averaged using the same technique and then subjected to bias correction once again. Appendix 2

Bias correction method

In this study, three bias correction methods are employed, with detailed explanations provided in the subsequent sections. The linear scaling and quantile mapping methods are widely recognized and commonly used in climate studies. Notably, the linear scaling bias correction method aimed to align model outputs with observed data, contributing to a more accurate representation of real-world conditions. The quantile mapping method played a crucial role in mitigating biases by ensuring that the statistical distribution of modeled variables closely mirrored that of observed data. The last method, namely Quantile Delta Mapping, represents one of the recent advancements in bias correction models, considering the trends in climate simulation. Together, these methods constitute a comprehensive approach to refining climate models, providing valuable insights and improving the robustness of our understanding of complex climate systems.

Linear scaling

The Linear Scaling approach43 is one of the most commonly used downscaling methods, frequently employed to reduce discrepancies between Global Climate Model (GCM) outputs and observational data. While it shares mathematical similarities with the Delta method, its conceptual approach differs. This method operates by correcting monthly values based on the difference between current observed and simulated values. After extracting monthly temperature and precipitation for overlapping periods of observational and model data, the long-term temperature differences and percentage changes in precipitation for that period are calculated. These estimates are then applied to historical basin data (for temperature) and multiplied (for precipitation). In this approach, it is assumed that the correction coefficients and additives remain unchanged even for future conditions.
