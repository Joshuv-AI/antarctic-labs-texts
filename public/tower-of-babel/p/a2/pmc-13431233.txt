Evaluating Cardiotoxicity and Factors Associated with In-Hospital Mortality Among Emergency Department Patients with Substance Use Disorder: An 11-year retrospective study
Authors: Darpanarayan Hazra, Nadia Ahmed Said Alqusaimi, Alyaqeen Ahmed Abdullah Al Breiki, Raghad Saleh Mohammed Al Akhzami, Aya Hasan Hadi AalHamad, Suad Abdullah Al Abri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjective:Substance use disorder (SUD)-related emergencies are increasing globally, with cardiotoxicity contributing substantially to morbidity and mortality. This study evaluated cardiotoxicity patterns and factors associated with in-hospital mortality among adults with SUD presenting to the emergency department (ED).Methods:This retrospective observational study was conducted from January 2014 to December 2024 at Sultan Qaboos University Hospital, University Medical City, Muscat, Oman. Adults aged ≥18 years with SUD and positive urine drug screening (UDS) were included if they had elevated troponin (>14 ng/L), electrocardiographic abnormalities or echocardiographic structural dysfunction. Clinical, laboratory, imaging, management and outcome data were analysed.Results:A total of 4,400 ED medical records were reviewed; 478 patients had SUD and 78 (16.3%) met the inclusion criteria (mean age = 40.8 ± 12.3 years, 92.3% male). Opiate-class positivity on UDS was the most frequent finding (62.8%), followed by methamphetamine (29.5%). Sinus tachycardia (41.0%) and ST-T changes (15.4%) were the most common electrocardiographic abnormalities. Elevated troponin was present in 87.2%. Overall, in-hospital mortality was 15.4%. Hypokinesia (odds ratio [OR] = 5.12, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.08–24.22; P = 0.040) and left ventricular impairment (OR = 6.14, 95% CI: 0.95–39.88; P = 0.057) showed the strongest mortality associations.Conclusion:Among ED patients with SUD, positive UDS and cardiotoxicity, structural cardiac dysfunction was associated with higher in-hospital mortality. These findings support early cardiovascular assessment, including electrocardiography, troponin testing and selective echocardiography, in high-risk patients with substance-related toxicity.

1. Introduction

Substance use disorder (SUD)-related emergencies have become an escalating global health concern, with cardiotoxicity representing one of the most life-threatening manifestations.[12] Globally, substance use contributes substantially to premature mortality, with opioids accounting for a large proportion of direct drug-related deaths.[34] In Western settings, opioids, amphetamine-type stimulants and polysubstance exposures are increasingly implicated in acute cardiovascular presentations, while in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, rising drug use has created a parallel burden on emergency services.[5]

Opioids and stimulants can produce cardiovascular injury through several interrelated mechanisms, including hypoxia, catecholamine excess, coronary vasospasm, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, calcium dysregulation, inflammation and direct myocyte injury; experimental and animal studies support these pathways and their contribution to arrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, and ventricular dysfunction.[345] Opioid exposure may also contribute to sudden cardiac death through respiratory depression leading to hypoxaemia, as well as opioid-related QT interval prolongation and other arrhythmogenic effects.[678] These mechanisms provide biological plausibility for the biochemical, electrocardiographic and echocardiographic abnormalities observed in patients with substance-related toxicity. In recent years, the MENA region has witnessed a dramatic rise in illicit drug consumption, transitioning from a transit hub to an active consumer market for opioids, amphetamines and novel psychoactive substances.[67] Laboratory-based surveillance from Kuwait demonstrated that amphetamines, benzodiazepines, cannabis and heroin were the most frequently detected drugs, with amphetamine and heroin combinations strongly associated with drug-related deaths.[8] In Algeria, Tahar et al. reported that signs of cardiac decompensation doubled the risk of intensive care unit admission among recreational drug users, highlighting the burden of cardiovascular compromise in regional emergency departments (EDs).[9] A distinct trend in the Middle East is the resurgence of fenethylline, a synthetic amphetamine derivative, now a dominant stimulant in Syria, Saudi Arabia and Gulf states.[101112] At the same time, the growing prevalence of potent synthetic opioids has heightened concern regarding severe overdose, cardiorespiratory collapse and cardiovascular complications, further underscoring the relevance of drug-related cardiotoxicity in emergency care.[101112] Together, these reports suggest that stimulant- and opioid-related cardiovascular toxicity is emerging as a broader emergency care problem in the region.

Despite growing recognition of drug-related cardiac injury, the regional literature remains limited in several important respects. First, most published reports from the MENA region focus on specific drugs, poisoning cohorts or isolated cardiovascular outcomes rather than the broader spectrum of cardiotoxicity encountered in routine emergency practice.[10111213] Second, few studies integrate toxicology findings with cardiac biomarkers, electrocardiographic abnormalities and echocardiographic features within the same clinical cohort. Third, data from Oman remain scarce despite the country's location within a region experiencing evolving opioid and stimulant use patterns. This limits understanding of which ED patients with SUD are at greatest risk of adverse in-hospital outcomes. Echocardiography may be particularly relevant in this context because structural abnormalities such as left ventricular systolic impairment and regional wall motion abnormalities may reflect more advanced myocardial injury than isolated biomarker elevation alone.[131415] However, the prognostic significance of these findings in drug-related cardiotoxicity remains insufficiently characterised in regional ED populations. In a recent study from Egypt, a validated nomogram incorporating hypoxia, ST–T changes, QT prolongation and shock index accurately predicted acute cardiovascular events in poisoning patients, underscoring the role of bedside cardiac assessment in early risk stratification.[15] Nevertheless, whether similar clinical, biochemical, electrocardiographic and echocardiographic patterns are associated with mortality among ED patients with SUD in Oman has not been well described.

To address this gap, this study aimed to investigate cardiotoxicity patterns and factors associated with in-hospital mortality among adult ED patients with SUD, positive urine drug screening (UDS) and evidence of cardiotoxicity. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Secondary objectives were to describe the clinical, biochemical, electrocardiographic and echocardiographic manifestations of cardiotoxicity in this cohort. Although electrocardiographic and echocardiographic findings were central to the clinical characterisation of the cohort, they were prespecified as secondary objectives because mortality remained the principal outcome of interest. The authors hypothesised that structural cardiac abnormalities would be associated with higher in-hospital mortality.

2. Methods

This single centre, 11-year retrospective observational study was conducted at the ED of Sultan Qaboos University Hospital, University Medical City, Muscat, Oman, between January 2014 and December 2024. The hospital functions as a regional referral centre for toxicology, emergency medicine and critical care, with an average annual emergency attendance exceeding 80,000 visits. Only ED presentations were included in this study. Patient medical records were reviewed using the hospital electronic medical record and laboratory information systems.

All adult patients aged 18 years and above who presented to the ED during the study period with a positive UDS for one or more illicit or psychoactive substances and a documented history of SUD were screened for eligibility. Patients were included if they had at least one objective feature of cardiotoxicity, defined as elevated troponin (>14 ng/L), electrocardiographic abnormalities or echocardiographic evidence of structural cardiac dysfunction; electrocardiographic abnormalities were therefore part of the inclusion criteria. Patients were excluded if they had a negative UDS, or if they had a positive drug screening or SUD history without objective evidence of cardiotoxicity. Duplicate visits were excluded and each patient was included only once. Patients declared dead on-arrival following a suspected drug overdose and records with missing or incomplete data were also excluded. Patients with pre-existing cardiac disease were not excluded, as the study was intended to reflect the real-world ED population presenting with SUD and suspected cardiotoxicity; however, these comorbidities were recorded and were considered potential confounders in the interpretation of cardiac abnormalities.

Cardiotoxicity was defined as the presence of at least one objective cardiac abnormality, including elevated troponin (>14 ng/L), electrocardiographic evidence of arrhythmia or ST-T segment abnormalities, or echocardiographic evidence of left ventricular dysfunction or regional hypokinesia. Clinical symptoms and chest radiographic findings were recorded as supportive features but were not used in isolation to define cardiotoxicity. As pre-presentation baseline ECGs, troponin values and echocardiographic data were not uniformly available, cardiotoxicity was defined operationally on the basis of abnormalities documented during the index ED presentation rather than confirmed deviation from each patient's baseline. Isolated sinus tachycardia may be non-specific and was therefore interpreted cautiously when assessing cardiotoxicity.

Data were extracted using a standardised data abstraction form and included demographic characteristics, pre-existing comorbidities, presenting complaints, vital signs, laboratory parameters, electrocardiography findings, radiographic and echocardiographic results, interventions and outcomes. Echocardiography was performed in a subset of patients (n = 29) based on clinical indication rather than a standardised study protocol. Data extraction was performed by trained physician reviewers using a standardised abstraction form. Extracted data were cross-checked for accuracy; discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

The UDS was performed on random urine samples collected at presentation to the ED and included testing for 6-acetylmorphine (<10 ng/mL), amphetamines (<500 ng/mL), barbiturates (<200 ng/mL), benzodiazepines (<200 ng/mL), cocaine (<150 ng/mL), tetrahydrocannabinol (THC, cannabis; <20 ng/mL), methamphetamines (<1000 ng/mL), methylenedioxymethamphetamine (ecstasy/MDMA; <300 ng/mL), methadone (<150 ng/mL), morphine and other opiates (<300 ng/mL), phencyclidine (PCP) (<25 ng/mL), tricyclic antidepressants (<1000 ng/mL), ethyl alcohol (<100 mg/dL) and tramadol (<200 ng/mL). Results were reported qualitatively as positive or negative according to the laboratory cut-off values for each analyte. As UDS is a screening rather than confirmatory toxicological approach, the assay is subject to false-positive and false-negative results related to cross-reactivity, variable detection windows and assay-specific sensitivity and specificity. Confirmatory testing by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry or liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry was not performed. Therefore, UDS findings were interpreted in conjunction with the clinical presentation and documented substance use history whenever available. Creatinine concentration (>2.0 mmol/L) was measured in each specimen to verify urine sample validity.

Data analysis was performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software, Version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR) according to distribution, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Bivariate comparisons were made using the Chi-square or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, and the Student's t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. To identify predictors of in-hospital mortality, bivariate Firth-corrected logistic regression was performed because of the limited number of mortality events. As all included patients had evidence of cardiotoxicity by design, the regression analysis was not intended to identify predictors of cardiotoxicity; rather, bivariate Firth-corrected logistic regression was performed as an exploratory analysis of factors associated with in-hospital mortality within this selected cardiotoxicity cohort, given the limited number of mortality events. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to evaluate time to in-hospital mortality. Given the small sample size, limited number of deaths and selective use of echocardiography, these analyses should be interpreted as exploratory.

3. Results

A total of 4,400 ED medical records were reviewed over the 11-year study period, of which 478 patients had documented SUD and 78 (16.3%) met the inclusion criteria for cardiotoxicity. The mean age of the cohort was 40.8 ± 12.3 years, and most patients were male (92.3%) [Table 1]. Altered mental status was the most frequent presenting complaint (59.0%), followed by chest pain (19.2%) and shortness of breath (14.1%); fever and nausea/vomiting were each reported in 14.1% of patients. The mean heart rate was 93.97 ± 24.85 bpm and oxygen saturation was below 94% in 44.9% of patients. Vital signs demonstrated systemic toxicity. Metabolic and cardiac comorbidities were the most common underlying illnesses [Table 2].

UDS revealed a predominance of opioids and stimulants. Opiate-class positivity on UDS was identified in 62.8% of cases, followed by methamphetamines (29.5%), benzodiazepines (21.8%) and amphetamines (15.4%). Tricyclic antidepressants, THC, methadone, ecstasy and tramadol were detected less frequently. Polysubstance use occurred in over one-third of cases, indicating complex exposure patterns within the cohort. Over half of the patients reported intravenous (55.2%) or oral drug administration (51.3%) and half reported concurrent alcohol use (50.0%) [Table 3].

In the entire cohort, left ventricular impairment was identified in 4 patients (5.1%) and regional wall hypokinesia in 7 (9.1%) [Table 4].

4. Discussion

This 11-year retrospective study provides one of the few ED-based analyses from the Middle East examining the spectrum of cardiotoxicity and its association with in-hospital mortality among patients with positive UDS and SUD. The findings highlight that opioids and stimulants, particularly opiate-class positivity on UDS and methamphetamines, were the predominant substances identified in patients with cardiotoxicity, while echocardiographic abnormalities were associated with in-hospital mortality in this cohort. These observations align with international data but also reflect evolving substance use trends within the MENA region. Most patients in the current cohort were male, which is consistent with reports showing that males account for a substantial proportion of substance-related ED presentations. This pattern may reflect differences in substance use behaviour, risk-taking, delayed care-seeking and exposure to social and occupational stressors, and highlights the importance of recognising gender-related patterns when assessing high-risk SUD presentations in the ED.

Globally, substance use continues to contribute to a rising burden of acute cardiovascular events.[5] The widespread availability of synthetic opioids and amphetamine-type stimulants has transformed the profile of drug-related emergencies, with increasing reports of mixed exposures and delayed cardiovascular complications.[161718] Novel synthetic opioids, often exceeding the potency of morphine and heroin, have emerged as important contributors to overdose deaths and cardiac arrest through mechanisms of profound respiratory depression, hypoxia and direct myocardial toxicity.[192021] Similarly, concurrent opioid and methamphetamine use has been associated with prolonged intoxication and poorer clinical outcomes.[22] Within the MENA region, recent toxicological surveillance also supports a growing burden of opioid and stimulant misuse. Laboratory-based analyses from Kuwait demonstrated that amphetamines, benzodiazepines and heroin were among the most frequently detected substances, with methamphetamine and heroin combinations commonly identified in drug-related deaths.[8] Comparable findings from Saudi Arabia indicate a growing prevalence of fenethylline and methamphetamine use, often in combination with opioids and benzodiazepines, resulting in increasing emergency presentations.[23] These regional trends support the relevance of the current findings, in which opioids and stimulants predominated among patients with cardiotoxicity.

The cardiac manifestations observed in the current cohort, ranging from elevated troponin and electrocardiographic abnormalities to left ventricular dysfunction, are biologically plausible in the context of drug-induced myocardial injury. Stimulants such as amphetamines and methamphetamines increase catecholamine release and sympathetic activation, promoting oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, calcium dysregulation, coronary vasospasm and inflammatory signalling, all of which may contribute to myocardial injury and ventricular dysfunction.[5161822] Opioids may contribute through respiratory depression, hypoxaemia, hypotension and secondary ischaemic injury. Endothelial dysfunction, microvascular ischaemia and apoptosis may further amplify myocardial damage in both exposure groups. These mechanisms may help explain the spectrum of biochemical, electrocardiographic and echocardiographic abnormalities observed in this cohort. Troponin elevation was common in the current study and likely reflects myocardial stress or injury related to hypoxia, vasospasm, arrhythmia, hypotension or direct toxic myocardial effects. Creatine kinase elevation was also frequent, although it is less specific for myocardial injury and may reflect concurrent skeletal muscle injury or rhabdomyolysis in substance-related presentations. In this context, troponin may provide greater clinical specificity than non-cardiac muscle enzymes when evaluating suspected drug-related cardiotoxicity in the ED.

In the current study, echocardiographic abnormalities, particularly left ventricular impairment and regional hypokinesia, were associated with in-hospital mortality. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Echocardiography was performed in only a subset of patients and appears to have been obtained on clinical grounds rather than as part of a standardised protocol, which introduces potential verification bias and confounding by severity. Patients who were more clinically unwell may have been more likely to undergo echocardiography, and this may have influenced the observed association with mortality. In addition, prior echocardiographic data were not consistently available, so some structural abnormalities, including left ventricular dysfunction, may have been pre-existing rather than newly attributable to the index substance exposure. Although this observation is consistent with broader literature showing that impaired ventricular function is associated with worse outcomes across a range of cardiac and critical care settings, the current study's findings should be regarded as exploratory rather than definitive evidence of prognostic value in drug-related cardiotoxicity.[2425]

The overall in-hospital mortality rate in this study was 15.4%, which is broadly comparable to published mortality estimates in severe drug-related cardiovascular presentations.[26] This underscores the clinical severity of the included cohort. However, the current study population was highly selected, including only ED patients with SUD, positive UDS and objective evidence of cardiotoxicity. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as representing the prevalence of cardiotoxicity among all patients with SUD or all UDS-positive presentations. Because all included patients had evidence of cardiotoxicity by design, the bivariate Firth-corrected logistic regression was not intended to identify predictors of cardiotoxicity. Rather, it served as an exploratory analysis of factors associated with in-hospital mortality within this selected cardiotoxicity cohort. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces the possibility of missing data, incomplete documentation and unmeasured confounding factors. Second, the inclusion strategy selected patients who already had cardiotoxicity, which limits inference regarding predictors of cardiotoxicity itself and precludes estimation of cardiotoxicity risk in the broader SUD population. Third, baseline electrocardiograms, troponin values and echocardiographic data prior to presentation were not uniformly available; therefore, some abnormalities may not have represented new changes attributable to the index substance exposure. This is particularly relevant in patients with pre-existing cardiac or other systemic comorbidities, in whom troponin elevation, electrocardiographic abnormalities or echocardiographic dysfunction may have reflected chronic disease, physiological stress or mixed aetiologies rather than substance exposure alone. Fourth, UDS was based on qualitative screening without routine confirmatory testing, so false-positive and false-negative results remain possible. Fifth, polysubstance use was common, limiting attribution of specific cardiac findings to individual drugs. Sixth, echocardiography was performed in a minority of patients and was not protocol-driven, limiting generalisability of the structural findings. Finally, the small number of deaths limited statistical power and precluded robust multivariable modelling; therefore, the reported mortality associations should be interpreted as exploratory.

Despite these limitations, this study contributes clinically relevant data from an underreported regional setting. By integrating UDS results with laboratory, electrocardiographic and echocardiographic findings, it provides a broader characterisation of substance-related cardiotoxicity in emergency care than is currently available from most regional reports. From a clinical perspective, these findings support early cardiovascular assessment, including electrocardiography, troponin testing and selective echocardiography, in high-risk patients presenting with suspected opioid- or stimulant-related toxicity. Detection of structural cardiac abnormalities may help identify patients who require closer monitoring, higher-acuity admission or earlier cardiology involvement. Prospective multicentre studies are needed to validate these findings, clarify the prognostic role of echocardiography and determine whether structured cardiac assessment can improve risk stratification in this population.

5. Conclusion

This 11-year retrospective study provides insight into cardiotoxicity among emergency department patients with substance use disorder and positive UDS. Opiate-class positivity and stimulant use were the most frequently detected substances, and a substantial proportion of patients demonstrated biochemical and electrocardiographic evidence of cardiac injury. Echocardiographic abnormalities, particularly left ventricular impairment and regional hypokinesia, were associated with higher in-hospital mortality, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the selective use of echocardiography and the potential for pre-existing cardiac disease. These findings support early cardiovascular assessment, including electrocardiography, troponin testing and selective echocardiography, in high-risk patients presenting with substance-related toxicity. Identification of structural cardiac abnormalities may help guide risk stratification, monitoring intensity and early cardiology involvement in the emergency department. Further prospective multicentre studies are needed to validate these findings and to better define the prognostic role of echocardiography in substance-related cardiotoxicity.
