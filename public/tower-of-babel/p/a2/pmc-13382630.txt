Structural insights on the ligand selectivity of the mouse trace amine-associated receptor TAAR7e
Authors: Yingjian Liu, Junhong He, Jiahui Sun, Chen Zhang, Hanyi Zhuang, Jiaqi Song, Peipei Shi, Weihong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTrace amine-associated receptors (TAARs), a G-protein-coupled receptor subclass detecting volatile amines, are co-expressed with odorant receptors in the main olfactory epithelium. While many behaviorally relevant amines are known for olfactory TAARs, the molecular basis of their selectivity for amines of varying lengths and primary/tertiary amines remains unclear. Using mTAAR7e as a model, we combined cAMP accumulation luciferase assays and computational simulations to investigate this issue. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and structure-activity relationship analysis established a clear preference for medium-chain, tertiary amines. Optimal steric complementarity within the binding pocket dictates the preference for medium-length alkyl chains like DMHA and DMOA. Site-directed mutagenesis further confirmed the essential role of Asp1273.32 in anchoring the amine group. Together, these findings offer valuable insights into TAAR ligand selectivity and may advance understanding of TAAR-mediated olfactory and systemic aminergic signaling.Keywords: GPCR, G-protein-coupled receptor, SAR, structure-activity relationship, TAAR, trace amine-associated receptor

Introduction

Trace amine-associated receptors (TAARs) are a group of G-protein-coupled receptors that are responsive to trace and volatile amines.1 With a total of 15 functional TAAR genes in the mouse genome and 6 in the human genome, the olfactory TAARs (TAAR subfamilies 2–9) are expressed alongside olfactory receptors (ORs) in the mammalian olfactory epithelium.1,2,3 In parallel to the deorphanization effort of ORs, past studies have revealed ligands for a number of TAARs. As the name suggests, the ligands of TAARs are aminergic; yet diverse structures are seen within the TAAR ligand repertoire.3,4,5 Ranging from primary to tertiary, aliphatic to aromatic, and monoamines to polyamines, the biological functions of most of these volatile ligands are olfactory cues relevant to various aspects of animal behaviors.

Previous studies have examined the structural features constituting the ligand selectivity of TAARs. Positively charged amine groups require either an aspartic acid or a glutamic acid for ligand coordination. Li et al. suggested a possible structural basis for the evolution of the selectivity for the number of amines in TAAR ligands through the study of the diamine cadaverine receptor TAAR13c. The involvement of two remote transmembrane aspartic acids, Asp3.32 and Asp5.42, forming a di-cation recognition motif in the receptor, is required for diamine response, whereas the presence of either Asp3.32 or Asp5.42 confers response to primary or tertiary monoamines.6 A similar diamine binding pocket with Asp3.32 and Asp5.43 is seen in the mouse and human TAAR6s and TAAR8s but whether these receptors are responsive to diamines is not known.6,7 One exception to the rule above is TAAR9, which only has Asp3.32 and can recognize ligands with one, two, and three amine groups,5 pointing to the possibility of the existence of yet another cation recognition site.

Early phylogenetic and functional classifications based on cAMP assays broadly divided mammalian TAARs into primary amine-sensing clades (TAAR1-4) and tertiary amine-sensing clades (TAAR5-9).4,8 However, recent high-resolution structural and functional evidence from Guo et al. has unraveled a far more nuanced ligand recognition landscape with unexpected mechanistic characteristics.9 For instance, canonical tertiary amine-sensing receptors including mTAAR5 and mTAAR9 exhibit broader ligand permissiveness than previously thought, as they can successfully accommodate not only traditional tertiary amines but also polyamines and diamines through a dual-binding pocket architecture. Notably, beyond the conserved orthosteric binding pocket that mediates the recognition of tertiary amines, these receptors possess a secondary hydrophobic pocket composed of the F/Y/T3.37, Y/F6.51, and V/I7.39 residue motif; this auxiliary pocket engages in extensive hydrophobic interactions to bind linear primary polyamines, yet this specific binding event does not elicit downstream G protein coupling and subsequent signaling activation. Nevertheless, despite these advances in elucidating the flexible ligand recognition mechanism of TAAR5 and TAAR9, the molecular basis for precise amino acid substitution preferences and polyamine chain-length selectivity observed in specific lineage-expanded TAAR members remains unresolved and represents a key gap in our understanding of TAAR family functional diversification.

While TAAR7 is a pseudogene in the human genome, the mouse TAAR7s underwent lineage-specific expansion to contain five members sharing high-sequence identities yet exhibiting different expression locales and diverse response profiles against various tertiary amines. Specifically, mTAAR7e recognizes a variety of amine odorants, including primary, secondary, and tertiary amines, to generate distinct physiological responses. Various screening efforts revealed that its agonists include 5-methoxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine, a urinary tryptophan metabolite; N,N-dimethyl-2-phenylethylamine; N,N-dimethylbutylamine (DMBA); N,N-dimethyloctylamine (DMOA); 2-methyl-1-pyrroline; heptylamine; and octylamine (OA), among others.4,5 Both TAAR7e and TAAR7f are expressed exclusively in the dorsal portion of the olfactory epithelium10 and the amino acid sequence identity between the two receptors is 93% (Figure S1). Ferrero et al. showed that each of the two receptors preferentially binds to certain ligands as a result of two adjacent amino acid side chains in the ligand-binding pocket in TM3. Changing the amino acids at 1323.37 and 1333.38 of TAAR7e to those of TAAR7f resulted in a response profile similar to that of TAAR7f and vice versa. Homology modeling (HM) revealed that the bulky side chain of tyrosine 1323.37 in TAAR7f hinders interactions with large ligands.4 This shows that the broadened receptive range of TAARs over the course of evolution is a result of gene duplication followed by mutations in ligand-binding pockets.

Recently, several cryo-EM structures of TAARs were resolved, including mTAAR9,9 mTAAR7f,11 and TAAR1.12,13,14 The structures of both chemosensory TAARs, mTAAR9 and mTAAR7f, were determined against the tertiary amine ligand, dimethylcyclohexylamine (DMCHA). Although the pose of the ligand was different between the two structures, it was observed that the receptors bind to the tertiary amine through a charge-charge interaction with the carboxylate group of the aspartic acid residue Asp1173.32 in mTAAR99 and Asp1273.32 in mTAAR7f.11 The same aspartic acid was also implicated in the binding of mTAAR9 to spermidine and β-phenylethylamine and in the binding of mTAAR1 and hTAAR1 to its ligands.12,13,14 These static snapshots have been extensively augmented by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to elucidate the dynamic mechanisms of recognition. For instance, computational analyses of mTAAR9 validated the dynamic stability of the essential salt bridge formed by the highly conserved Asp3.32, operating in concert with a surrounding aromatic cage to anchor primary amines.9 Similarly, MD studies on TAAR1 revealed profound pocket plasticity, demonstrating how the binding site dynamically remodels to accommodate chemically diverse ligands ranging from endogenous trace amines to bulkier synthetic compounds.13 Furthermore, structure-guided simulations of mTAAR7f highlighted the critical contribution of extracellular loop flexibility and specific hydrophobic subpockets in shaping odorant specificity.11 These advancements underscore the importance of dynamic conformational states in TAAR signaling.

Here, we elucidate the structure-activity relationship (SAR) of mTAAR7e by combining homology modeling, molecular docking, and functional assays with a congeneric series of amine ligands (Figure 1). Using the closely related mTAAR7f structure as a template, we constructed a homology model to probe the mTAAR7e binding pocket and uncover the structural basis governing its selectivity for tertiary versus primary amines and its preference for specific alkyl chain lengths. This integrated approach not only clarifies the mechanistic underpinnings of ligand discrimination in mTAAR7e but also establishes a computational framework for predicting amine recognition across TAARs. Studying TAAR7-ligand interactions could reveal insights into chemical communication pathways relevant to health and disease.4,11

Results and discussion

Overall architecture of mTAAR7e and the orthosteric binding site

A homology model of mTAAR7e was generated using the published mTAAR7f structure as a template.11 Given the high-sequence identity and template quality, most residues adopted sterically favorable conformations, as validated by Ramachandran plot analysis (Figure S2). Manual inspection of the remaining residues revealed they were primarily localized in loop regions, which are unlikely to affect the orthosteric binding site (OBS) within the helix bundle. Further validation was achieved by superposing the mTAAR7e and mTAAR7f structures in complex with DMCHA. The high degree of similarity at the binding site, with an root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of 0.56 Å for the key residues (Figure S3), demonstrates that the model accurately captures the conformation of the OBS. The molecular docking workflow was validated across multiple TAAR proteins of known structure (Figure S4).

To better understand the binding mechanism of mTAAR7e, we conducted a sequence conservation analysis of the OBS residues (within 3.9 Å of each ligand) in the mTAAR7e-ligand complex structure obtained from molecular docking, along with TAARs and OR51E2 structures resolved by cryo-EM experiments. This analysis confirmed the conservation of the established DWY motif (Asp1273.32, Trp2866.48, and Tyr3167.42) and identified a functionally distinct residue substitution at position 5.43 in TAARs (Figure 2A). This phenomenon was also validated by extensive sequence alignment of mTAARs (Figures 2D and 2E). Notably, ligand-binding residues in OR51E2 overlap with TAARs only at positions 5.43 and 3.33, which are hypothesized to play roles in receptor activation pathways (Figure 2A). OR51E2 exhibits limited structural similarity and minimal sequence conservation compared to TAARs,15 reflecting fundamental mechanistic differences in odorant recognition between human ORs and mouse TAARs.

Building on these sequence insights, a comparative analysis of the mTAAR7e model with experimentally resolved TAAR structures was performed to validate the model’s reliability (Figure 2B). The observed high structural conservation in the transmembrane (TM) region, with an average RMSD of less than 1 Å (Figure 2C), confirms that the model accurately reflects the expected TAAR fold, thereby providing a robust foundation for subsequent OBS conformation analysis. In DWY motif, Asp1273.32 occupies the same position on TM3, and the carboxylic acid side chain is located near the nitrogen atom of the ligand, while the positions of Trp2866.48 and Tyr3167.42 in mTAAR7e, mTAAR7f, and mTAAR9 are also highly similar. In contrast, mTAAR1 and hTAAR1 exhibited rotation of Trp2866.48 toward TM7 due to steric hindrance from isoleucine at position 3.40.11 This structural divergence in a key motif suggests that distinct ligand recognition may underpin the functional differences between the mTAAR7/9 subfamily and the mTAAR1/hTAAR1 clade.

This initial structural and sequence comparison offers a preliminary view of the mTAAR7e binding site, setting the stage for subsequent computational and experimental validation of the proposed residue functionalities.

Docking and MD simulations reveal the binding interactions between mTAAR7e and DMCHA

Having established the structural reliability of the model, we next investigated the detailed binding interactions using DMCHA as a representative ligand (Figure 3A). We found that the binding pocket consists of 11 residues: four hydrophobic (Val1283.33, Cys1313.36, Val3127.38, and Ala3157.41), four aromatic (Trp2866.48, Tyr2896.51, Phe2906.52, and Tyr3167.42), and three polar (Asp1273.32, Ser1323.37, and Asn2175.43). Key stabilizing interactions include a hydrogen bond with Asp1273.32 and a cation-π interaction between the protonated tertiary amine of DMCHA and the aromatic ring of Tyr2896.51 (Figure 3D). Cation-π interactions have also been shown to stabilize other amine-sensing systems, such as cholinergic and adrenergic receptors.16,17 Three rounds of 300-ns MD simulations on the complex system (Table 1; Table S1) showed a strong polar interaction between the charges on Asp1273.32 and DMCHA’s tertiary amine, with the relative distance stabilizing at 2–4 Å (Figure 3B), while the distance between the cyclohexane ring centers of DMCHA and Trp2866.48 stabilized at 4–6 Å, highlighting the stability of ligand-protein binding (Figure 3C). Additionally, the contact frequency heatmap was analyzed, revealing high interaction frequencies between the odorant atoms and residues Asp1273.32, Cys1313.36, Trp2866.48, and Tyr2896.51 within the OBS (Figure 3E). Beyond the established hydrogen bonding, van der Waals contacts were consistently observed, demonstrating complementary non-covalent stabilization mechanisms. The low RMSF values of key residues in the binding pocket were also demonstrated, confirming our conclusions (Figure 3F).

To validate these computational predictions, we performed mutagenesis experiments on key binding pocket residues. In most instances, introducing mutations at key residues within the OBS significantly reduced mTAAR functional activity, as demonstrated by a rightward shift of the response curves (Figure 4). As the key interaction of our predicted binding mode, the mutation of D127A3.32, W286A6.48, and Y289A6.51 significantly reduced the G-protein-coupling ability, underscoring the essential role in odorant recognition of mTAAR7e. In the vicinity of Asp1273.32, Tyr3167.42 bridges with Asp1273.32 to stabilize the potential hydrogen-bond network, and a near-complete abolishment of response was observed for mutant Y316A7.42. Additionally, varying degrees of reduction in activity were also observed in other amino acid mutations within the OBS, including V128A3.33, C131A3.36, S132A3.37, N217A5.43, and F290A6.52. Specifically, the polar interaction mutant N217A5.43 and the aromatic interaction mutant F290A6.52 show more pronounced effects, while the hydrophobic interaction mutant C131A3.36 exhibits a weaker impact. These integrated computational and mutagenesis findings provide robust validation of the predicted binding mode and underscore the essential role of these key residues in DMCHA recognition and binding stability.

Activation of mTAAR7e

The activation of class A GPCRs upon recognition of their respective ligands involves a series of structural changes, among which the most prominent is the outward shift of the intracellular C-terminal of TM6, enabling coupling of G protein and the subsequent activation of signaling pathways.18,19 Two indicators are employed to quantify this outward displacement of TM6: TM6 distance and TM6 angle.18,20,21,22,23 TM6 distance refers to the distance between the Cα atoms of residues 2.46 and 6.37, specifically Leu89 and Leu275, respectively, while TM6 angle represents the angle formed by the Cα atoms of residues 6.34, 6.47, and 2.41—namely Ala272, Ser285, and Phe84—with residue 6.47 (Ser285) serving as the apex of the angle. Statistical analysis of class A GPCR structures in different activation states reveals distinct distributions of TM6 distance and TM6 angle (Figure 5B). We compared three structural models: the AlphaFold3-predicted apo state,24 the HM structure (agonist-bound), and the centroid structure from the most populated cluster of the 300-ns MD trajectory (Figure 5A). Compared to the AF3 model, both the HM agonist-bound structure and the MD-derived representative structure showed a consistent outward shift of TM6, a conformational rearrangement consistent with early stages of class A GPCR activation. The stability of this TM6 outward displacement was further supported by its maintenance throughout the equilibrated MD trajectory, as quantified by the TM6 distance and angle measurements (Figure 5B).

A comparison of mTAAR7e with active/inactive hβ2AR structures suggests that the conformation of its key activation residues is more consistent with an active state (Figure 5C).25 These structural observations support the hypothesis that mTAAR7e may undergo an activation process similar to other class A GPCRs, involving conformational rearrangements in conserved motifs such as DRY and NPxxY that couple ligand binding to TM6 movement (Figure 5D).11 As illustrated schematically in Figure 5D, this proposed pathway comprises sequential transitions: from ligand recognition at the orthosteric pocket, to rotameric adjustments within conserved motifs (e.g., DRY and NPxxY), and ultimately to the outward tilt of TM6.26 Such a cascade of conformational changes is consistent with the general activation paradigm of class A GPCRs and aligns with recent findings on the closely related mTAAR7f receptor.11 While further validation through extended simulations and experimental conformational studies will be required to definitively establish this mechanism, the current model provides a plausible framework to guide future investigations into TAAR activation and may improve our understanding of TAAR receptor function.

Specificity of mTAAR7e to alkyl chain length

With a comprehensive understanding of the activation mechanisms established, we are poised to address the fundamental question of mTAAR7e′s ligand selectivity. We selected a series of primary and tertiary amines with varying aliphatic chain lengths and structural features for experimental validation to investigate the structural basis of mTAAR7e ligand selectivity (Figure 1). A previous report has shown that DMBA may be a ligand of mTAAR7e.5 By performing a luciferase assay on heterologously expressed mTAAR7e, we confirmed this result with a full dose-response curve (Figure 6A; Table 1). Expanding from this, we systematically profiled the receptor’s response to a congeneric series of tertiary amines. This revealed a clear structure-activity relationship dependent on chain length: potency peaked with DMHA (6 carbons), followed by DMOA (8 carbons), DMDA (10 carbons), DMCHA (cyclohexane), and DMBA (4 carbons), while the shortest analogues, TMA (1 carbon) and DMEA (2 carbons), elicited no measurable response (Figure 6A; Table 1). This demonstrates a clear optimal chain length for receptor activation.

The structural basis of the chain length selectivity of mTAAR7e was further investigated using molecular docking of key ligands (Figure S5). We characterized the binding pockets of mTAAR7e in complexes with DMBA, DMCHA, DMHA, DMOA, and DMDA (Figure 7). Comparative analysis revealed that the optimal steric complementarity was achieved with DMHA, whose alkyl chain length best matched the volume and geometry of the binding pocket. In contrast, both shorter (e.g., DMBA) and longer (e.g., DMDA) ligands resulted in suboptimal packing, with either empty cavity space or steric clashes. This precise size-matching requirement provides a structural explanation for mTAAR7e′s selectivity toward specific carbon chain lengths, reinforcing the functional data on receptor activation.

Previous studies have demonstrated that mTAAR7e and mTAAR7f exhibit distinct preferences for ligand chain length.4 To analyze this phenomenon, we compared their conserved modular structures with functional responses. Previous SAR studies on the experimental mTAAR7f indicated that odorant binding is highly dependent on the shape complementarity between the ligand and OBS, which is strongly affected by the CYC motif (Cys1313.36, Tyr1323.37, and Cys1333.38).4 In the CYC motif, two cysteines form the disulfide bond to stabilize the shape of binding pocket, while the tyrosine residue separates the OBS into two distinct cavities. Unlike mTAAR7f, wild-type mTAAR7e harbors a CSS motif in place of CYC, eliminating the disulfide bond at positions 3.36/3.38 and the cavity partition. This yields a larger, more flexible pocket that enhances responsiveness to the medium-chain ligand DMOA (Figure 8). Conversely, mutating CSS to CYC restores sensitivity to DMCHA, which has a shorter fatty acid chain (Figure 8). The same result was also found in DMBA. DMBA, which has a shorter chain length than DMOA, also showed increased luciferase activity to the doubly mutated mTAAR7e (Figure S6). Consistently, introducing the CSS motif into mTAAR7f produced the opposite effects (Figure S7): the response to the shorter-chain DMCHA was weakened, whereas the logEC50 values for longer-chain ligands DMOA and DMDA were reduced (indicating enhanced potency), further supporting the role of the motif in dictating chain length selectivity. This finding suggests that the differences between the CYC and CSS motifs, through shaping the topological structure and stability of the binding pocket, are the main molecular driving force behind the distinct selective activation of amine-type ligands of different sizes by mTAAR7e and mTAAR7f.

Specificity of mTAAR7e to amine class

Having analyzed the structural basis for mTAAR7e’s selectivity toward carbon chain length, we next sought to investigate its discrimination between tertiary and primary amines. Previous evolutionary analyses provide background for this selectivity for amine compounds: mTAAR subfamilies diverged under distinct selective pressures, with mTAAR5-9 (responsive to tertiary amines like DMOA/DMBA) undergoing gene duplication and positive selection, while mTAAR1-4 (responsive to primary amines like OA) remained more evolutionarily conserved.8 Our cAMP signaling assays revealed a striking contrast between tertiary and primary amines (Figure 6B). The corresponding primary amines BA (four carbons), HA (six carbons), and OA (eight carbons) exhibited only minimal and severely attenuated efficacy compared to their tertiary amine counterparts, highlighting an indispensable role for the N,N-dimethyl substitution. This critical distinction was further solidified by testing cyclohexane-containing analogues: the tertiary amine DMCHA acted as a clear agonist, whereas its primary amine counterpart, CHA, failed to activate the receptor (Figures 6A and 6B). In summary, our experimental results demonstrate that the ligand-binding pocket of mTAAR7e exhibits a strong preference for the tertiary amine motif.

To elucidate the structural basis of this amine class discrimination, we selected three representative ligands—the high-potency agonist DMOA, the intermediate-potency DMBA, and the low-efficacy primary amine OA—for more rigorous computational analyses through MD simulations (Table 1; Figure S8; Table S1). MMPBSA free energy calculations from MD simulations highlighted residues with significant contributions (|ΔΔG| > 1 kcal/mol) (Figures 9A–9C), where Asp1273.32 consistently emerged as the primary contributor across all ligands, underscoring its critical role in binding. DMOA and DMBA exhibited similar energy contribution patterns, whereas the absence of the dimethyl group in OA led to distinct interactions.

Decomposition of free energy differences between DMOA and OA complexes (Figure 9D) identified Trp2866.48 and Tyr3167.42 as key divergent residues, driven primarily by electrostatic (ΔEEEL) and van der Waals (ΔEVDW) interactions, respectively, with residue Trp2866.48 coinciding with a previously identified structural divergence site between the mTAAR7/9 and mTAAR1/hTAAR1 subfamilies (Figure 2C). These two residues create a hydrophobic and aromatic microenvironment within the mTAAR7e binding pocket. This facilitates π–π and cation–π interactions, which stabilize bulky tertiary amines. Furthermore, it participates in a hydrogen-bonding network with the conserved residues, which maintains the structural integrity and electrostatic balance of the binding site. Tertiary amines can adopt an orientation that preserves aromatic interactions while maintaining the Asp1273.32–Tyr3167.42 hydrogen bond network, thereby promoting strong, selective binding. In contrast, primary amines, due to their smaller size and distinct charge distribution, are unable to engage in effective aromatic interactions and often disrupt the hydrogen-bond network, resulting in impaired receptor activation.

Moreover, hydrophobic comparisons of mTAAR1 and mTAAR7e revealed significant residue variations within the OBS, notably at positions 5.43, 6.51, 6.52, and 7.38—among which position 5.43 showed the most dramatic shift (Figure 9E), transitioning from a hydrophobic residue (Ala/Leu) in primary amine-sensing mTAARs to hydrophilic residue (Asp/Asn/Ser) in mTAAR7e and most tertiary amine-sensing mTAARs (except mTAAR9) (Figure 9F). Given the critical role of Asp1273.32 in ligand recognition, we propose a plausible structural hypothesis: the hydrophilic residue at position 5.43 in mTAAR7e may create a local electrostatic environment that favors tertiary amines, whereas the hydrophobic residue in primary amine-sensing receptors may permit primary amine access (Figures 9G and 9H). The hydrophobicity and charge distribution of the DMOA binding pocket are consistent with this hypothesis (Figures 9I and 9J).

Based on the structural and functional analyses described above, we further validated the proposed binding modes by performing structural modeling and molecular docking of DMCHA with all the aforementioned mutants, as well as several additional ones (Table S2). The computational binding scores for these mutant models showed a strong correlation with the experimental dose-response data, thereby providing independent support for our proposed ligand-receptor interaction mechanism. Collectively, by employing a combined experimental and computational approach, we propose a mechanistic hypothesis for mTAAR7e’s dual selectivity: optimal steric complementarity within the binding pocket dictates the preference for medium-length alkyl chains, while distinct residue compositions and electrostatic landscapes at the top of the pocket may account for the pronounced discrimination against primary amines.

Limitations of the study

This study employs a combined computational and experimental approach to investigate the selective recognition mechanism of the mTAAR7e receptor for ligand carbon chain length and primary/tertiary amine groups. While our findings establish a foundational model, certain limitations also point to future research directions. Our analysis primarily relies on a homology model constructed from the mTAAR7f structure. Although the model was rigorously validated, a direct experimental structure of mTAAR7e would help clarify finer conformational details, particularly in dynamic regions such as extracellular loops. Similarly, while molecular dynamics simulations provide insights into binding and activation dynamics, extended simulation times or advanced sampling methods would comprehensively capture the entire activation process. Our MD simulations focused exclusively on ligand-bound complexes; the absence of apo-state simulations limits our ability to fully decouple intrinsic receptor fluctuations from purely ligand-induced dynamic effects. Moreover, these simulations were conducted in aqueous solution without an explicit lipid bilayer. Future membrane-embedded simulations will be important in capturing the regulatory effects of lipid composition.

At the experimental level, extending mutagenesis studies to a broader group of residues would deepen our understanding of selectivity. Extending the chemical scope of tested ligands to include secondary amines and other diverse scaffolds would also further illuminate the receptor’s recognition landscape. Furthermore, our structure-activity relationship analysis is based on cAMP assays, which measure overall receptor function. The absence of direct binding measurements limits our ability to definitively dissect whether observed differences in potency arise from altered binding affinity, altered activation efficacy, or both. Future studies incorporating direct binding assays and BRET-based assays will be important to fully deconvolute these contributions. In conclusion, this study lays a groundwork for understanding mTAAR7e function. The models and hypotheses proposed here open the door to subsequent investigations aimed at unraveling the receptor’s mechanistic details and exploring potential strategies for its modulation.

Experimental model and study participant details

HEK-293 cells (human embryonic kidney cells, female origin) were obtained from Shanghai Fuheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (FH0242). Cells were cultured in CD293 medium (Gibco, 11913019) supplemented with 4 mM L-glutamine at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. Method details

Homology modeling and docking

HM structure of mTAAR7e was conducted using Swiss Model27 based on a high-resolution cryo-EM structure of mTAAR7f (PDB ID: 8PM2, 2.3 Å resolution) and the quality of models were measured by Ramachandran plot,38 which showed that over 95% of residues were found in favored regions, indicating reliable backbone geometry.

All docking calculations were carried out by AutoDock Vina.28 ADFR protocol in AutoDock Vina was implemented for the preprocess of receptors. During preprocessing, protein structural problems such as missing excitation structures and steric clashes were identified and corrected. Hydrogen atoms were added to the protein consistent with physiological pH (7.4), and the protonation states of titratable residues were assigned using the PROPKA module. The receptor structure was further energy-minimized with the OPLS_2005 force field until the gradient convergence threshold reached 0.05 kcal/mol·Å, ensuring a relaxed conformation suitable for docking.39 The grid box utilized in docking was determined by the atomic center of the ligand in the mTAAR7f. The box size was set to 10 Å, which is sufficient to encompass the orthosteric binding site and the residues adjacent to the transmembrane cavity. The ligand conformations were optimized and generated using the Python package Meeko before docking and scoring by autogrid4.40 All docking simulations were conducted with an exhaustiveness value of 32 to ensure adequate sampling of ligand orientations. The docking results were ranked according to the predicted binding affinity (ΔG, kcal/mol), and the top-ranked poses were visually inspected in PyMOL32 and Discovery Studio Visualizer to confirm consistency with the known GPCR binding cavity and to identify key interaction residues. The grid and scoring functions used in Vina were based on the AutoGrid4 algorithm.

Molecular dynamic simulation

All MD simulations were conducted using the GROMACS package (version 2020)29 with the ff14SB force field,41 starting from docking-derived complex structures. The protonation states of titratable residues at physiological pH (7.4) were assigned using PROPKA 3.0.37 Initially, the complexes were prepared using the pdb4amber tool in AMBERTOOLS 202330 to ensure proper protonation states and residue naming, and subsequently solvated in a cubic box of TIP3P water molecules. Counterions were added as needed to neutralize the system. Following system setup, the topology was generated, and heavy atoms were subjected to a three-step energy minimization with position restraints of 5, 3, and 0 kcal/(mol·Å2) to relax steric clashes and remove unfavorable contacts. The minimized system was then gradually heated from 0 K to 300 K over 1 ns in the NVT ensemble, employing a Nosé-Hoover thermostat42 to control temperature while maintaining structural integrity through harmonic restraints. After heating, the system underwent 10 ns of equilibration in the NPT ensemble, during which position restraints were reduced to 3 kcal/(mol·Å2) to allow the system to relax further, while pressure and temperature were maintained at 1 atm and 300 K, respectively. Finally, the last frame of equilibration was used as the starting point for three independent 300-ns production runs, which were performed in the NPT ensemble without any position restraints. Each production trajectory was initialized with a unique random seed to ensure independent sampling. Snapshots were recorded every 10 ps for downstream analyses, providing a comprehensive sampling of the conformational space and ensuring statistical reliability of subsequent calculations. During the production phase, system stability and local residue flexibility were rigorously evaluated through RMSD and RMSF analyses (Figure S8).

Ligand-receptor interactions

Contact frequencies were calculated using the Python package named “get contacts” (https://getcontacts.github.io/), and the following contacts between specific atoms of ligands and residues of receptors were counted: hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic and van der Waals interactions.

RMSD

The backbone atoms of receptors were used to calculate the Root-Mean-Square Deviation (RMSD) by gmx rms (GROMACS package 2019)29 function to determine whether simulations were stable.

RMSF

The Root-Mean-Square Fluctuation (RMSF) analysis was performed on the Cα atoms of the receptor using the MDanalysis43 Python package. The transmembrane backbones and extracellular loops were labeled on RMSF plot as follows: 48–68 (TM1), 84–104 (TM2), 122–143 (TM3), 167–187 (TM4), 213–233 (TM5), 275–295 (TM6), 310–333 (TM7), 188–212 (ECL2), 296–309 (ECL3).

The binding free energies of ligand-receptor complexes were estimated using the Molecular Mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann Surface Area (MMPBSA)44 method implemented in MMPBSA.py from AMBERTOOLS 2023.30 For each system, snapshots from the last 50% of the production trajectory (150–300 ns) were extracted at intervals of 10 ps for analysis. Distance of residue

To calculate the distance between two residues, specific atoms within each residue were selected and then the gmx distance tool was utilized to calculate these measurements for all frames in the simulations. The reference distance in the initial conformation of the complex system was determined using the measurement tool in PyMOL.32

Molecular dynamics conformational clustering

To identify representative and stable conformations during the MD simulations, trajectory clustering was performed based on the backbone RMSD. The equilibrated portion of each trajectory was extracted and subjected to clustering analysis using the GROMACS gmx cluster module. The gromos algorithm was employed with an RMSD cutoff of 0.2 nm to group structurally similar frames. The centroid structure of the most populated cluster was selected as the representative stable conformation for subsequent analyses, including binding mode visualization and energy decomposition. This approach ensures that the selected structure reflects the dominant, energetically favorable state of the system during equilibrium.

GPCR activation metrics

To characterize conformational changes of TM6 in the GPCR, two complementary metrics were employed. The TM6 distance is defined as the distance between the Cα atoms of residues 2.46 and 6.37. The TM6 angle represents the angle formed by the Cα atoms of residues 6.34, 6.47, and 2.41, with residue 6.47 serving as the apex. These metrics provide a quantitative measure of TM6 outward movement, which is closely associated with GPCR activation.18,23

Luciferase assay

Luciferase assay was performed using Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) as previously described.45 HEK293T-derived cells were plated on 96-well plates (Corning Inc., Kennebunk, ME, USA). For each 96-well plate, we transfected 1 μg of CRE-Luciferase, 0.5 μg of pRL-SV40, 0.5 μg of TAAR, 0.5 μg of RTP1S, and 0.25 μg of M3R using the transfection reagent Lipofectamine2000 (Invitrogen, Life Technologies Corp., Carlsbad, CA, USA). Twenty-four hours post transfection, we removed the transfection media and rinsed the cells with 50 μL PBS buffer (pH 7.4) per well. Then we immediately replaced the PBS buffer with 25 μL TAAR ligands diluted in CD293 medium (Gibco Brand, ThermoFisher Biochemical Products Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Four hours post stimulation, Firefly luciferase (FL) and Renilla luciferase (RL) activities were measured following manufacturer’s protocols, using a Synergy H1 plate reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). We calculated normalized luciferase activity using the formula: (FL/RLX ─ FL/RLXC)/(FL/RLmax ─ FL/RLmaxC), where FL/RLX is the response of the wild-type or a mutant TAAR to a test sample; FL/RLXC is the basal value of the same receptor to a blank control; FL/RLmax is the maximum response of the wild-type or a mutant TAAR to the same or another ligand, and FL/RLmaxC is the basal value of the receptor with the maximum response to a blank control.
