Site-Specific Hydrogen Deuterium Exchange Difference Mass Spectrometry Measurements for Ligand Binding
Authors: Joseph Anacleto, Suzanne Ackloo, Cheryl Arrowsmith, Esther Wolf, Yves Leblanc, Cristina Lento, Derek J. Wilson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Conventional bottom-up HDX-MS experiments are highly suitable for use in drug development; however, a major limitation of this approach is that it generally provides only peptide-level structural resolution. Site specific (i.e., single amino acid-resolved) HDX-MS measurements have been achieved using ECD/ETD, but the low efficiency of these fragmentation techniques, combined with poor ion transmission associated with ‘detuning’ the instrument to fully prevent deuterium scrambling, results in sensitivity losses that make ligand binding measurements impractical in a ‘real-world’ (e.g., drug development) context. Here we apply a recently developed method for zero scrambling, high efficiency ECD in the challenging context of ligand binding differential HDX experiments, demonstrating a wealth of additional information that can be acquired when HDX-MS analyses are conducted at the amino acid level.

Introduction

The conventional bottom-up HDX-MS experiment has quickly become embedded in industrial drug development workflows. For industry, this approach offers several advantages over classical structurally-resolved techniques, particularly in the areas of throughput, inexpensiveness, sensitivity and adaptability to a wide range of systems. The disadvantage of conventional HDX-MS compared to classical structural biology is structural resolution, which is typically around 5 residues.

Several experimental and computational methods have been introduced to maximize HDX-MS resolution, with occasional reports of site-specific (i.e., single amino acid) measurements being achieved. , However, none of these approaches have been widely adopted, possibly because they rely on extensive abstraction from the data, unusually high peptide redundancy, or because they are impractical under ‘real-world’ conditions. Single amino acid resolution has also been directly achieved by fragmenting HDX-labelled peptides (or intact proteins) using ECD or ETD; however, this approach also has not been widely adopted, owing most likely to the very substantial signal loss associated with low fragmentation efficiency and poor transmission under the necessary ‘zero deuterium scrambling’ instrument settings. Site-specific HDX-MS in the context of ligand binding has been reported twice to the best of our knowledge, first by Griffin and co-workers in 2011 and then in 2017 by Williams and co-workers. However, these experiments also were never repeated, and more recent work has suggested that signal loss remains a major impediment for ETD- and ECD-based methods.

In previous work, we introduced a method for (comparably) high efficiency ECD coupled to zero signal loss, zero scrambling ion transmission achieved on a ZenoToF 7600 instrument. This advance sets the stage for routine site-specific ligand-binding characterization under ‘real-world’ conditions and on a chromatographic time scale. In this note, we carry out such an experiment using binding of a small molecule DS0413 () to WDR5, a well characterized, highly druggable domain with many known ligands.

Methods

Materials

WDR5 protein and the small molecule ligand DS0413 were provided by the University of Toronto Structural Genomics Consortium (Toronto, ON). Protein stocks (WDR5 at approximately 20 μM or WDR5 20 μM + ligand 200 μM) were prepared in 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2% DMSO (v/v). Deuterium oxide (D2O, 99.99%), ammonium acetate (≥98%) and formic acid (≥98%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Pepsin (porcine gastric mucosa, Sigma-Aldrich) was cross-linked in-house onto NHS-activated agarose (Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

HDX Labeling Experiments

Peptide-level HDX measurements were carried out as described previously (coverage map provided in ). Amino-acid specific HDX labelling measurements were conducted using a previously described continuous flow injection for the time resolved HDX-MS apparatus (CFI-TRESI-HDX). Briefly, a capillary-based sub-second to minutes mixing device was connected to PerkinElmer Series 200 pumps that deliver the running solvents, 250 mM ammonium acetate as the injected protein carrier (5 μL/min), H2O as the injected D2O carrier (15 μL/min) and 3% formic acid/40 mM ammonium formate as the pre-cooled (∼15 C) pH 2.4 quench solution (80 μL/min). PerkinElmer Series 200 autosamplers injected the protein (3 to 5 μL) and D2O into the carrier flows, leading to the TRESI device, which was connected to an in-house constructed pepsin digestion column (130 mm of PEEK tubing with an ID of 0.030′′ and a 2 μm pore-size frit). Directly after the TRESI quench mixer, digested protein samples were loaded onto a cooled C18 trap column (ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 VanGuard Precolumn, Waters, 2.1 mm × 5 mm, Milford, MA) for 3 min and eluted using a 2.5 min gradient (2% to 35% acetonitrile with 0.23% formic acid at 50 μL/min, ACQUITY UPLC M-Class System, Waters) directly to the MS. Peptide chromatographic peaks were typically 0.2 to 0.25 min wide at baseline ().

Instrument Setup and Data Analysis

The ZenoTOF 7600 was operated using low-energy, high efficiency EAD tuned for negligible deuterium scrambling and no loss in performance, as previously described. Specific WDR5 peptides targeted for EAD analyses were selected based on previously acquired peptide-level HDX-MS data. Data was acquired using a SWATH experiment template with targeted peptide Q1 mass ranges that would cover 0 to 100% deuterium labeling of the peptide of interest (typically 6 to 8 amu wide). One to four peptide Q1 mass ranges were included in each acquisition, limited by the instrument duty cycle and the SWATH acquisition template. Data preprocessing was performed using PeakView Software (Sciex, AB Sciex Pte. Ltd, Canada) including spectral averaging and calculating average masses based on intensity weighted centroid masses, which were used for subsequent data analysis in MS Excel. The deuterium content of EAD product ions was calculated as the difference between the corresponding ions of the labeled and unlabeled sample. An example of a difference HDX calculation is provided in the SI (xlsx file).

Results and Discussion

To demonstrate site-specific, differential HDX in a ligand binding context, we used WDR5, a highly druggable, WD40 repeat protein with the classic β-propeller structure. What makes this system attractive for us as a model is that we have previously characterized it extensively by conventional HDX-MS, allowing us to compare a variety of signal types (i.e., direct binding, allosteric decrease in deuterium uptake, allosteric increase in deuterium uptake, etc.) between peptide-level and site-specific resolution data. At present, we are only able to examine select peptides, due primarily to a lack of automated data processing and analysis software for this type of measurement.

At the peptide level, HDX-MS confirms a known binding site for DS0413 (and many other ligands) through a strong uptake decrease in two overlapping peptides, corresponding to 250–263 and 257–263, (Figure A, region 1). The site-specific data confirms this measurement exactly but provides the additional, possibly rather important detail that this signal is heavily dominated by a single backbone amide belonging to C261 (Figure B). The importance of this residue for ligand binding is well established in numerous crystal structures.

The peptide-level WDR5 difference profile contains several regions that undergo allosteric changes in conformational dynamics upon complexation with DS0413. To explore the extent of agreement between our peptide and site-specific data, we focus first on a high redundancy region at the C-terminus, corresponding to a.a. 318–329, that exhibits a strong decrease in deuterium uptake (Figure A, region 2). Here, the site-specific data indicate that this allosteric effect strongly impacts the K325 backbone amide, with significant contributions from N323 and T326. The shorter redundant peptides in this region are not ideal candidates for ECD fragmentation (many are 2+ with overlapping fragment signals); however, the signal for 319–329 was sufficient to acquire a profile indicating agreement between redundant peptides ().

Interestingly, the site-specific measurements in this region exhibit substantially reduced error compared to peptide-level measurements. This may be due in part to the fact that peptide and site-specific HDX measurements are fundamentally different. For instance, in the peptide-level experiment, deuterium uptake is measured by estimating the magnitude of the perturbation in the peptide isotopic distribution caused by deuteration, at best by modeling the distribution or (more often) at worst by estimating the distribution centroid. In the site-specific experiment, uptake is determined by measuring the mass difference associated with the removal of a single amino acid from the peptide that is either labeled or unlabeled. This results in two inherent advantages for accuracy in site specific measurements: (i) Mass difference measurements are almost always more accurate than direct mass measurements (especially on ToF instruments) and (ii) the fractional mass increase associated with deuteration is always higher for a single amino acid than for a peptide.

Another important note arising from the region 2 site-specific profile is that the strong negative signals at the primarily impacted amino acids are accompanied by a statistically significant positive signal at IKL (individual signals for these amino acids cannot be adequately resolved in the EAD data). In peptide-level data, any such opposing sense signals cancel in the observed signal, which is one factor that can, in principle, allow for the detection of features in site specific data that are not observed in peptide-level data.

Finally, we examined the singular case where an allosteric increase in dynamics was observed upon DS0413 binding, corresponding to amino acids 220–231 (Figure A, region 3). Here the site-specific data show that the most affected backbone amide is F222, with positive signals at Y228 and I229. The particular profile in this region (and, in fact, the particular profiles in all site-specific measurements) highlights that it may be possible to distinguish the binding modes of different ligands engaging at a singular binding site and to reveal the unique way in which each ligand binding at the same site induces allostery.

Conclusions

While we have demonstrated the potential power of site specific HDX to rapidly deliver information-rich characterizations of ligand binding in ‘real-world’ experiments, much work remains to be done to enable widespread adoption. In particular, commercial HDX-MS software must be updated to manage ECD/ETD data, and new data acquisition modes must be developed to enable automated ‘moving target’ parent peak selection. Both of these advancements are currently underway. At the same time, we are undertaking a thorough exploration of site-specific HDX-MS in drug candidate library binding that includes a comparison of multiple ligands, time-courses and the detection of features that are unobserved in peptide-level HDX-MS.
