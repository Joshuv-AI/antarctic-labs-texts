Influence of hydrogen on the cohesion and charge density distribution in bcc iron matrix by DFT calculations
Authors: Yuanshuang Liu, Feng Qiu, Dingrong Qu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrogen-induced delayed fracture is a critical failure mode in low-alloy steels, particularly impacting equipment reliability in petrochemical, hydrogen energy, and related fields, with a lack of effective early warning technologies. Using first-principles calculations based on density functional theory (DFT), this study investigates body-centered cubic (bcc) iron matrix models with hydrogen concentrations of 0, 4.17%, and 6.25% to explore hydrogen’s influence on cohesion. DFT results show hydrogen doping causes severe lattice distortion and plastic deformation along the [001] direction, reducing matrix cohesion and inducing fracture trend. From the simulation results, it was found that a higher concentration of hydrogen atoms does not necessarily reduce the cohesion of the matrix, but rather the increase in local charge density caused by hydrogen atoms is the intrinsic driving factor that induces a decrease in cohesion in the bcc iron matrix. Notably, higher hydrogen concentrations do not necessarily reduce cohesion. Instead, the increased local charge density from hydrogen is the critical driver for cohesion decrease. This work reveals the electronic mechanism of hydrogen-induced cohesion reduction, providing a theoretical basis for developing hydrogen-induced fracture warning technologies.Keywords: bcc Fe matrix, Hydrogen, DFT, Cohesion, Charge densitySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Hydrogen induced delayed fracture is a common form of failure in steel materials. Hydrogen embrittlement (HE) is caused by the combined effect of hydrogen and stress, resulting in a decrease in the toughness and plasticity of steel materials1. Low-alloy high-strength steels, such as 2.25Cr-1Mo steel, are widely used in key components including hydrogenation reactor shells, hydrogen service high-pressure pipelines in refineries, and valve flanges of hydrogen pipelines. In the fields of petrochemicals and petroleum and natural gas, HE has always been an important factor affecting the reliability of equipment in service. The Clarke gas field in France experienced a malignant accident of pipeline rupture due to the occurrence of HE without warning during the mining process. In atmospheric, oil well, oil refining, or high-pressure hydrogenation environments, molecules such as H2 and H2S first adsorb and dissociate on the metal surface into hydrogen atoms, making it easier to enter the metal matrix and then transport and diffuse in the lattice2. Ultimately leading to the initiation and propagation of cracks3–6. In addition, with the development of hydrogen production and storage technologies related to hydrogen energy, the safe service performance of equipment used for hydrogen production and storage will also become a key area for continuous research and breakthroughs. In engineering, due to the lack of HE theory and the hidden and sudden nature of HE behavior, there is still a lack of effective technologies to early warn of hydrogen induced material failure and fracture.

Despite the continuous development of research on HE mechanisms, each mechanism has certain limitations in explaining HE phenomena7, and contradictory conclusions may even arise between different HE mechanisms. The currently highly recognized HE mechanisms include hydrogen-enhanced decohesion (HEDE)8,9, hydrogen-enhanced localized plasticity (HELP)10,11, adsorption-induced dislocation emission (AIDE)12, and hydrogen-enhanced strain-induced vacancy (HESIV)13. HE is a multiscale problem where hydrogen diffusion is influenced by mechanisms across scales, and the interplay between different scales is far from straightforward. HE in body-centered cubic (bcc) iron and its alloys has long been a critical issue in structural materials, driving extensive investigations into the atomic-scale mechanisms underlying hydrogen trapping, diffusion, and its impact on mechanical properties. With the development of science and technology and the improvement of computer performance, it is common to use numerical simulation methods to study the structure and properties of materials14,15. Density functional theory (DFT) has emerged as a powerful tool to unravel these phenomena, with numerous studies focusing on hydrogen’s interaction with lattice defects and its influence on material cohesion16–21. Hydrogen trapping at defects such as vacancies, dislocations, and grain boundaries (GBs) significantly affects its distribution and mobility[18]. Most of DFT studies have focused on the GBs and defects in hydrogen environments to investigate the mechanism of hydrogen atoms’ influence on these specific sites22–26. In actual high concentration hydrogen environments, the influence of hydrogen atoms on the interior of the bulk iron matrix is also crucial. Many recent DFT studies have shown that quantum effects cannot be ignored in the interaction and influence mechanism between hydrogen and the matrix. Hydrogen diffusion in bcc iron is governed by both classical thermally activated processes and quantum effects17. At high temperatures, phonon contributions play a critical role in modifying diffusion barriers, improving agreement between theoretical predictions and experimental data17. In addition to the influence of phonons on hydrogen diffusion, the contribution of electrons in quantum effects cannot be ignored, which is an important factor affecting the bonding strength between matrix atoms. Based on DFT calculations, starting from the interaction law between atomic nuclei and electrons, the structural properties and other information of materials can be obtained by solving the Schrödinger equation of the system, which can better predict the electronic properties of matrix in hydrogen environment27,28. Researches have shown that hydrogen can reduce the cohesion of iron matrix18,20, but the atomic scale mechanism by which hydrogen regulates the cohesion of bcc iron, especially the charge distribution of the matrix under hydrogen action and the bonding strength between atoms, is still not fully understood. The focus of this work is a detailed analysis of the correlation between charge density redistribution and cohesion.

This study focuses on revealing the mechanism of charge density-induced changes in matrix cohesion in bcc Fe matrix gaps after capturing hydrogen atoms. Specifically, two bcc Fe matrix models with different hydrogen atom concentrations were constructed, and DFT calculations were used to quantify the cohesive forces of the block models at different hydrogen concentrations. The changes in bond lengths between H atoms and Fe atoms inside the matrix, as well as the differences in charge density distribution around hydrogen atoms, were compared at different hydrogen concentrations. From the simulation results, it was found that a higher concentration of hydrogen atoms does not necessarily reduce the cohesion of the matrix, but rather the increase in local charge density caused by hydrogen atoms is the intrinsic driving factor that induces a decrease in cohesion in the bcc Fe matrix. This study proposes a physical mechanism that reveals the reason for the decrease in cohesion of metal substrates caused by hydrogen atoms at the microelectronic scale and lays the foundation for predicting the mechanical properties of metal materials caused by hydrogen embrittlement.

Method

Figure 1 shows the primitive cell and supercell models of bcc Fe matrix. Under the condition of a certain number of hydrogen atoms, the hydrogen concentration in the matrix can be controlled by controlling the size of the iron lattice. Construct supercells of 1 × 1 × 1, 2 × 2 × 3 (HFe24), and 1 × 2 × 2 (HFe16) in sequence, with corresponding hydrogen doping concentrations of 0, 4.17%, and 6.25%, respectively. Two main reasons for selecting these two hydrogen concentrations from higher hydrogen concentration levels. On the one hand, the actual HE failure is not dominated by the macroscopic average hydrogen concentration, but rather driven by local hydrogen enrichment29. However, the concentration of local hydrogen enrichment cannot be directly verified experimentally. In previous studies, the supercell size of the bcc model used in DFT calculations is similar to that of the supercell model in this work30,31. The core objective of selecting a relatively high hydrogen concentration in this work is to amplify the intrinsic interaction signal between hydrogen and the bcc iron matrix under extreme high hydrogen concentration conditions, thereby clearly capturing the influence law of hydrogen on the cohesive energy. On the other hand, the high hydrogen concentrations with atomic ratios of 4.17% and 6.25% selected in this study do not exceed the physical accommodation limit of the bcc iron lattice. For a bcc iron primitive cell, the total number of octahedral and tetrahedral interstitial sites is approximately twice the number of iron atoms, so the theoretical maximum hydrogen solubility can reach 50% atomic ratio. The atomic ratios of 4.17% and 6.25% selected in this study correspond to interstitial site occupancies of 2.08% and 3.125%, respectively, which are far lower than the theoretical value of 50% for hydrogen occupancy at interstitial sites. Therefore, it is reasonable to use this hydrogen concentration for conducting DFT calculations under hydrogen enrichment conditions.

Fe atom models under different doping concentrations of H atoms, where green represents H atoms and purple represents Fe atoms. (a) Fe cells. (b) HFe16 model, hydrogen concentration 6.25%. (c) HFe24 model, hydrogen concentration 4.17%.

For the HFe16 model, the hydrogen atom occupies the interstitial positions of the unit cell (0.5a, 0.5b, c). For the HFe24 model, the hydrogen atoms occupy the interstitial positions of the unit cell (a, b, 1.5c). Among them, a, b, and c are the lattice constants of the unit cell. The study employed the CASTEP module in Materials Studio software to investigate the structural relaxation of bcc Fe matrix primitive cell and supercells with different hydrogen doping concentrations. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) and Perdew Burke Ernzerhof (PBE) functional are used to describe the exchange correlation potential. Firstly, optimize three bcc Fe matrix models. In the process of structural relaxation optimization, in order to ensure calculation speed and accuracy, the cut-off energy of the plane wave used is taken as 550 eV, the convergence standard of the interatomic interaction force is 1 × 10− 6eV/Å, the maximum internal stress is 1 × 10− 3GPa, the maximum atomic displacement is 2 × 10− 3/Å, and the energy convergence standard is 1 × 10− 6eV/atom. The k points of Fe, HFe24, and HFe16 are selected as 19 × 19 × 19, 9 × 9 × 7, and 11 × 11 × 7, respectively. The valence state electrons of Fe involved in the calculation are 3d64s2, and the H atom is 1s1. After optimization, electronic property calculations are carried out, and the cut-off energy and k-point settings during this process are consistent with the optimization process.

Effect of hydrogen doping on bcc Fe matrix cohesion

Furthermore, for matrix models with different hydrogen concentrations, calculate the total energy of the system after each step of deformation displacement along the [001] direction. Specifically, a deformation displacement with a step size of 0.2 Å is applied along the c-axis direction, i.e. the [001] direction. During the displacement application process, the angles α, β, and γ between the coordinate axes remain unchanged. The energy calculation results are shown in Fig. 2a. It was found that in the absence of hydrogen doping, the energy of the matrix system shows a linear continuous change with the increase of deformation displacement, and there is no sudden decrease in energy inflection point. This indicates that under continuous stress, the occurrence of fracture is a continuous gradual process and does not undergo sudden changes. In the case of hydrogen doping, the energy of the matrix decreases overall with deformation displacement, but an energy inflection point appears under local stress, indicating that the matrix will undergo sudden fracture under hydrogen action.

Moreover, from the perspective of bond length variation, hydrogen doping causes significant changes in the lattice parameters of the matrix, thereby altering the atomic bond length and consequently altering the interatomic forces. This study calculated the bond length between hydrogen atoms and their surrounding matrix atoms at different hydrogen concentrations, as shown in Fig. 3. When hydrogen atoms enter the center position of the octahedral gap in the matrix, hydrogen forms bonds with four surrounding iron atoms at low hydrogen concentrations, and the bond length of H-Fe atoms is greater than 1.83 Å. At high hydrogen concentrations, hydrogen forms bonds with six surrounding iron atoms, and their bond lengths are smaller than those at low hydrogen concentrations, thereby increasing the bonding force between hydrogen and iron atoms and enhancing the cohesion of the matrix.

Analysis of space charge distribution

Furthermore, the charge density distribution of the matrix around hydrogen doped with different hydrogen concentrations was calculated, and the results are shown in Fig. 4, where red represents the decrease in lost electrons and blue represents the increase in obtained electrons. It can be clearly seen that under a constant charge density of hydrogen atoms, when low hydrogen concentration is doped, the iron atoms around hydrogen lose fewer electrons than when high hydrogen concentration is doped, which makes it difficult for H to bond with the iron atoms around the matrix doped with low hydrogen concentration. For high hydrogen concentration doping conditions, due to the large increase in electron gain and loss of hydrogen and iron atoms, as well as the enhanced electronegativity between different atoms, interactions are more likely to occur, thereby increasing the cohesion of the matrix. For the model with a hydrogen atom concentration of 4.17%, the local increase in electron density in the gaps occupied by hydrogen significantly enhances Pauli repulsion, leading to lattice expansion and weakened metal cohesion. When hydrogen atoms are trapped in the gaps of polyhedra, hydrogen nuclei located in the gaps attract electron clouds, resulting in an increase in local charge density. According to the Pauli exclusion principle, the lattice will expand due to a significant increase in repulsion. At a hydrogen doping concentration of 6.25%, the charge density between hydrogen atoms and iron atoms decreases, partially restoring the cohesion of the matrix. Therefore, the charge density is the key factor affecting the cohesion of the bcc Fe matrix in the presence of hydrogen.

Conclusion

This study investigates the mechanism by which charge density changes induce cohesion variations in bcc Fe matrix interstitials after hydrogen capture. Two bcc Fe matrix models with different hydrogen concentrations were constructed, and DFT calculations were used to quantify their cohesive forces. Changes in H-Fe bond lengths and differences in charge density distribution around hydrogen atoms at varying concentrations were analyzed. Findings reveal that higher hydrogen concentrations do not necessarily reduce matrix cohesion; instead, the increased local charge density caused by hydrogen is the intrinsic driver for cohesion decrease in bcc Fe matrix. This work uncovers the microelectronic-scale physical mechanism underlying hydrogen-induced cohesion reduction in metal substrates, while noting that hydrogen doping causes severe lattice distortion and plastic deformation along the [001] direction. It provides a theoretical basis for predicting hydrogen embrittlement-induced mechanical properties and developing hydrogen-induced fracture warning technologies.
