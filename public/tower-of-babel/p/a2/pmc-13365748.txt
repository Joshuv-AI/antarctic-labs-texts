fAI-BRO: a multimodal AI decision-support system to address diagnostic delay in fibromyalgia syndrome
Authors: Vincenzo Venerito, Giuseppe Lopalco, Maria Morrone, Italia Agresta, Antonella Celano, Sergio del Vescovo, Thomas Hügle, Florenzo Iannone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the diagnostic accuracy and patient acceptability of fAI-BRO (Fibromyalgia AI-Based Rheumatology Observer), a multimodal artificial intelligence (AI) system integrating video-based visual descriptor analysis and psycholinguistic transcript evaluation, for distinguishing fibromyalgia syndrome (FMS) from other rheumatic and musculoskeletal diseases (RMDs).MethodsIn this cross-sectional proof-of-concept study, 100 patients at the University of Bari (50 FMS per ACR (American College of Rheumatology) 2016 criteria, 50 RMD controls including inflammatory arthritis, osteoarthritis and other conditions) underwent standardised interviews in Italian. Audio was processed with OpenAI Whisper in translate mode. Magistral-Small (Mistral AI), deployed locally in zero-shot configuration without FMS-specific fine-tuning or in-context examples, analysed four video frames using OpenCV-derived image-level descriptors (30% weight) and psycholinguistic transcript features (70% weight). Classification thresholds were optimised post hoc via Youden’s J index.ResultsAmong 100 patients, fAI-BRO achieved 91% accuracy (94% sensitivity (95% CI 83.5% to 98.7%), 88% specificity (95% CI 75.7% to 95.5%), area under the curve 0.96) for FMS detection on a 0–100 composite score. Youden’s J identified the empirically optimal cut-off at 47, rounded down to 45 to favour screening sensitivity. All inflammatory arthritis cases (rheumatoid arthritis (RA), psoriatic arthritis (PsA), spondyloarthritis (SpA), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE); n=36) and mechanical conditions (OA, impingement; n=8) were correctly classified as RMD. All six false positives for FMS occurred in dermatomyositis (n=3) and polymyalgia rheumatica (n=3)—a biologically plausible overlap involving muscle tissue rather than synovial pathology, presented here as a hypothesis-generating observation rather than incidental noise. Perturbation analysis confirmed widespread pain descriptors as the dominant classification drivers. Binary classification was robust to the English translation effect. Patient comfort was high (mean 4.5/5). Mean processing time was 324 s.ConclusionfAI-BRO demonstrates promising diagnostic accuracy for FMS screening as a human-in-the-loop decision-support tool, with perfect inflammatory arthritis classification and high patient acceptability. Post hoc optimised thresholds require external validation before clinical deployment.

Introduction

Fibromyalgia syndrome (FMS) affects 2%–4% of the global population and remains diagnostically challenging due to subjective symptom presentation, overlap with inflammatory arthritis and other rheumatic and musculoskeletal diseases (RMDs), and diagnostic delays averaging 5–7 years.15 The ACR 2016 criteria rely on patient-reported outcomes that are time-intensive and inherently subjective,5 creating a clinical need for objective screening tools.

Artificial intelligence (AI) offers new approaches. Computer vision can detect pain-related facial microexpressions,6 7 while natural language processing can identify linguistic patterns characteristic of FMS narratives, including catastrophising, affective pain descriptors and cognitive markers.810 Our group previously demonstrated that large language model (LLM)-driven sentiment analysis can distinguish FMS from other chronic pain conditions with 87% accuracy using transcript analysis alone,11 validating the hypothesis that patients with FMS exhibit specific linguistic patterns detectable by AI. A multimodal extension is clinically motivated: pain communication is not exclusively verbal—non-verbal distress signals (facial tension, gaze patterns, postural cues) carry diagnostic information that pure transcript analysis cannot capture, and patients with alexithymia or limited health literacy may under-report verbally what their visual presentation conveys.6 7 While validated facial expression recognition systems exist,6 7 our approach uses structured image-level descriptors as an exploratory video complement to the transcript-driven core.

Building on this, we developed fAI-BRO (Fibromyalgia AI-Based Rheumatology Observer), integrating video-based visual descriptor analysis and psycholinguistic transcript evaluation. This study aimed to validate diagnostic accuracy against a diverse RMD cohort, assess patient acceptability and provide perturbation-based explainability.

Patients and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional proof-of-concept study was conducted at the Rheumatology Unit, University of Bari Aldo Moro, Bari, Italy, between November 2025 and January 2026. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was not prospectively registered given its proof-of-concept design; the full study protocol is available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Consecutive patients attending the rheumatology outpatient clinic were screened for eligibility. Eligible FMS patients were diagnosed according to ACR 2016 criteria—selected as the most current revision incorporating both widespread pain index and symptom severity scales5—confirmed by a board-certified rheumatologist blinded to fAI-BRO results, with symptom duration of at least 3 months. The RMD control group included a diverse spectrum of conditions with active disease at the time of assessment (untreated or flared for inflammatory conditions; symptomatic for mechanical conditions; online supplemental table S1): inflammatory arthritis (rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, spondyloarthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus), inflammatory myopathy (dermatomyositis (DM), polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR)) and mechanical conditions (knee osteoarthritis, subacromial impingement). Exclusion criteria were inability to provide informed consent, severe cognitive impairment, technical issues preventing adequate video recording (minimum 3 min duration with visible face), insufficient transcript length (<50 words after Whisper processing), and comorbid FMS diagnosis in the RMD group. No participants were excluded for data-quality reasons after enrolment.

Assessment protocol

All participants underwent standardised semistructured interviews (5–10 min) recorded using a Logitech StreamCam (1080 p, 30 fps) covering pain location, duration, quality, severity, associated symptoms, functional impact and emotional well-being. The assessing rheumatologist established the reference standard diagnosis independently, without access to fAI-BRO outputs; conversely, the fully automated AI system had no access to clinical diagnoses. Following analysis, patients completed a comfort questionnaire (5-point Likert Scale) and a binary visual assessment validation question.

fAI-BRO multimodal analysis

fAI-BRO integrates two analytical modalities using Magistral-Small (Mistral AI) deployed locally via LM Studio (V.0.40) on Apple Silicon (MacBook Pro M4 Max, 128 GB unified memory, macOS). The system operates in a strict zero-shot configuration—no fine-tuning, no in-context examples and no exposure to the study population—to maximise generalisability and ensure that performance reflects the model’s pretrained capability rather than data set specific tuning. All processing was performed locally without external application programming interface calls, ensuring patient data privacy under GDPR. The intended end user is a healthcare professional (rheumatologist or trained nurse) operating the desktop application; no AI expertise is required. The detailed scoring algorithm, prompt engineering specifications and technical architecture are provided in online supplemental appendix A. For video-based visual descriptor analysis (30% weight), four evenly distributed frames were extracted from each patient video and processed using OpenCV-based metric extraction. Three quantitative features were computed per frame: brightness (mean grayscale value), contrast (SD) and edge density (Canny edge detection as a visual complexity proxy). These metrics were formatted as structured text descriptions and submitted to Magistral-Small for interpretation, yielding structured visual descriptors including image-derived distress indicators, pain-suggestive visual patterns and overall visual distress scoring (0–10). It is important to acknowledge that, in this configuration, Magistral-Small does not ‘see’ the image: the LLM receives a textual summary of OpenCV-derived numerical features (brightness, contrast, edge density) and infers pain-relevant descriptors from these indirect, image-level proxies rather than from the visual content itself. This is a known limitation of text-only multimodal pipelines and motivates future migration to native vision-language architectures or validated facial action coding systems (eg, OpenFace, FACS). The 30% video weight reflects the exploratory nature of this component: OpenCV-derived features are image-level descriptors rather than validated facial expression measures, and no prior studies have validated this approach for FMS differentiation.11

For psycholinguistic transcript analysis (70% weight), patient interviews were conducted in Italian and translated to English using OpenAI Whisper in translate mode. This was a deliberate methodological choice rather than a workaround: large open-weight LLMs—including Magistral-Small—are predominantly pretrained on English corpora, and controlled bilingual testing in our cohort confirmed superior marker detection in translated English (composite score 78 vs 57; see Translation effect). Operating in English also supports cross-site standardisation and global scalability of the pipeline, since it decouples downstream model performance from the language of source consultation. The transcript scoring domains—catastrophising, sensory and affective pain lexicon, helplessness, cognitive dysfunction markers, and fibromyalgia narrative probability—were selected based on linguistic features that emerged as discriminative for patients with FMS in our prior work,11 where these features were consistently more prevalent in FMS than in other RMD narratives. These domains were integrated into a structured analytical prompt with explicit negation handling (online supplemental appendix A). The 70/30 weighting was chosen to be evidence-proportional: transcript analysis has a peer-reviewed validation precedent for FMS11 and a substantial psycholinguistic literature base,810 whereas image-level visual descriptors are unvalidated for this purpose. Equal weighting (50/50) would overcredit the exploratory modality, whereas a transcript-only design would discard a complementary signal; the 70/30 split was calibrated against this asymmetry and confirmed empirically (table 1).

The two modalities yield a composite score on a continuous 0–100 scale (video 0–30 points, transcript 0–70 points). The binary classification threshold was determined post hoc using Youden’s J index on this study cohort. Youden’s J identified 47 as the empirically optimal cut-off; we rounded this down to 45 to bias the screening tool towards sensitivity, consistent with the ruling-in objective of an early stage diagnostic aid. Patients scoring ≥45 were classified as FMS-positive for all diagnostic performance calculations. We emphasise that this cut-off was derived on the same cohort used for evaluation and therefore represents an internally optimised snapshot rather than an externally generalisable threshold; prospective re-derivation in independent populations is required before any clinical deployment.

Sensitivity analyses compared the primary 70/30 weighting against transcript-only (100/0) and equal-weight (50/50) configurations, with thresholds re-optimised via Youden’s J for each scheme. Perturbation-based explainability analysis used model-agnostic leave-one-out approaches: sequential sentence removal for transcripts and sequential frame removal for video (online supplemental appendix B). Bilingual comparison (English vs original Italian) was also performed.

Statistical analysis

Diagnostic performance was assessed by sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy and F1-score. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis with area under the curve (AUC) and 95% CIs was performed. Score distributions were compared using independent samples t-tests. Categorical variables were analysed using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed value of p<0.05. No formal sample size calculation was performed a priori; n=100 was selected as pragmatically achievable for a proof-of-concept study and provides 80% power to detect a sensitivity difference of ≥15% from a null of 75% (one-sided exact binomial test). No missing data occurred: all 100 enrolled patients completed both the interview and fAI-BRO analysis, and all produced valid composite scores. Analyses were performed using Python V.3.8 (SciPy, NumPy).

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 100 patients completed the study. Patients with FMS (n=50) were significantly younger than RMD controls (n=50) (46±8 years vs 53±14 years, p<0.01) and more likely to be female (84% vs 54%, p<0.001), consistent with epidemiological data (table 2). These imbalances reflect the real world demographics of FMS but introduce a residual concern that age-correlated or sex-correlated narrative styles could partially confound classification; this is revisited in the Discussion section.

Sensitivity analysis of modality weights

Transcript-only analysis (100/0) achieved 88% accuracy (90% sensitivity, 86% specificity, AUC 0.93), with seven false positives and five false negatives. Compared with the primary 70/30 scheme, transcript-only missed two additional patients with FMS with atypical linguistic profiles but detectable video-derived visual signals, and misclassified one additional patient with PMR. The 70/30 multimodal approach thus provided a modest but consistent improvement over transcript alone, consistent with the prior transcript-only validation (87% accuracy).11 Under equal 50/50 weighting, performance declined further to 83% accuracy (AUC 0.89), confirming that overweighting the video component degrades classification (table 1).

Explainability analysis

Perturbation analysis on a representative patient with FMS confirmed widespread pain descriptors and sensory lexicon as the dominant classification drivers. Removal of the sentence containing diffuse pain language caused a 45-point transcript score drop (58 to 13), while removal of sleep disturbance content caused only a 1-point drop. The same pattern held in Italian (28-point vs 2-point drops), confirming that classification is driven by pain descriptors rather than functional impairment.12 The video component (20 of 30 points) showed consistent distress-suggestive patterns across all four frames, with frame-level ablation producing 0-point deltas, indicating redundant visual signal across frames (figure 6).

Translation effect

All 40 high-scoring (≥70) FMS-positive patients were re-analysed using their original Italian transcripts. All remained FMS-positive (≥45), confirming robustness of the binary classification. However, 9 (22.5%) dropped into the 45–69 intermediate range. A controlled bilingual comparison showed a 21-point score difference (English 78 vs Italian 57), driven by reduced catastrophising detection (‘high’ vs ‘medium’) and lower narrative probability (85% vs 70%) in Italian. These findings reflect the English-optimised prompt design and Magistral-Small’s likely stronger English comprehension, validating the use of Whisper translate mode.

Patient acceptability and processing time

Patient comfort was high, with a mean Likert Scale Score of 4.5±0.7 (88% scoring 4 or above). Visual assessment agreement was 78%, with no significant difference between FMS (76%) and RMD (80%) groups (p=0.63). Mean processing time was 324±45 s (range 220–450 s). While longer than questionnaire-based screening instruments, this processing time remains comparable to a standard clinical encounter and yields richer multimodal data than self-report measures alone.

Discussion

fAI-BRO achieves 91% diagnostic accuracy, with 94% sensitivity comparing favourably to our prior transcript-only approach (87% accuracy).11 The 100% accuracy for inflammatory arthritis (n=36) and mechanical conditions (n=8) is clinically significant: these conditions share pain symptoms with FMS but exhibit distinct linguistic patterns—localised, articular descriptors versus diffuse, body-wide pain language with higher catastrophising.9 10 12 The perturbation analysis confirmed this mechanism.

Fibromyalgia does not currently have established biomarkers, and diagnosis relies entirely on clinical assessment and patient-reported outcomes.1 5 fAI-BRO’s composite score, derived from objective multimodal analysis, could function as a digital biomarker in two clinical contexts. First, as a differential diagnostic digital biomarker, the system’s ability to distinguish FM from inflammatory arthritis (100% accuracy) and its quantitative scoring may help reduce the diagnostic delay of 5–7 years currently experienced by patients with FM.3 Second, as a monitoring biomarker, longitudinal tracking of fAI-BRO scores could provide standardised, objective measurement of FM symptom trajectories over time—particularly relevant for patients with secondary fibromyalgia coexisting with inflammatory RMDs, where disentangling disease activity from FM-related symptoms is a persistent clinical challenge. While the present cross-sectional design cannot validate monitoring applications, the continuous 0–100 score scale was designed to be sensitive to within-patient changes, and longitudinal validation is planned.

The systematic misclassification of DM and PMR—six of six cases—is the most consequential finding of this study, and we have reframed it as a hypothesis-generating observation rather than an isolated limitation. The pattern is biologically coherent: every misclassified case involved a muscle-tissue condition (DM, PMR), whereas every synovial-tissue inflammatory condition (RA, PsA, SpA, SLE) was correctly distinguished. This dissociation suggests that pain narratives map onto tissue compartment more than onto the inflammatory/non-inflammatory axis—patients describe diffuse, deep, body-wide muscular pain in remarkably similar language whether the underlying biology is central nociplastic (FMS) or peripheral myopathic/myalgic (DM/PMR). To our knowledge this is a novel observation that warrants prospective testing in larger myopathy cohorts. Two safety implications follow. First, rare inflammatory myopathies are, by definition, under-represented in the general medical corpora on which open-weight LLMs are trained, so the model is plausibly out of distribution when it encounters them and lacks the linguistic priors needed to detect the subtle red flags that distinguish myositis from FMS. Second, a clinician shown a high fAI-BRO Score may feel falsely reassured of an FMS diagnosis and bypass the laboratory workup (creatine kinase, aldolase, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, myositis-specific antibodies) that distinguishes treatable inflammatory myopathy from non-inflammatory chronic pain—a delayed myositis diagnosis can be catastrophic. We therefore frame fAI-BRO explicitly as a human-in-the-loop screening adjunct: it must be deployed alongside, and never instead of, the standard inflammatory and immunological workup, particularly in patients presenting with proximal weakness, raised muscle enzymes or constitutional features. Future iterations will need to incorporate objective biological inputs—at minimum acute-phase reactants and muscle enzymes—and hierarchical classification logic that gates an FMS call on prior exclusion of inflammatory myopathy. The sensitivity analysis confirmed that transcript analysis carries the strongest discriminative signal,811 with transcript-only achieving 88% accuracy (AUC 0.93)—consistent with our prior transcript-only validation.11 The 70/30 multimodal scheme provided a modest improvement (91%, AUC 0.96) by capturing the complementary signal from the image-level descriptors, while the overweighting video (50/50) degraded performance (83%, AUC 0.89), supporting the evidence-proportional rationale of the chosen weighting.

As a hypothesis-generating observation, all false positives and all translation-downgraded cases clustered in the 45–69 score range, while no errors occurred among patients scoring ≥70. Although not formally validated as a classification boundary, the 45–69 range may function as a zone of diagnostic uncertainty, analogous to indeterminate zones in serological testing. Patients scoring in this range may benefit from additional workup, while scores ≥70 may confer sufficient confidence for FMS-directed management. Validation of this two-tiered structure is a priority for future work and will require prospective re-derivation of both boundaries in an independent population. The bilingual analysis further showed that translation to English is preferable to analysing the original Italian when using English-optimised models; future work should explore language-matched prompt engineering.

This study has several limitations. It is a single-centre proof-of-concept study with n=100; subgroup power is limited, particularly for DM and PMR (n=3 each). The 50:50 case-control design enriches for FMS and does not reflect the true FMS prevalence expected in a general rheumatology clinic; PPV and NPV will therefore differ in real world settings, and prospective validation with consecutive unselected patients is needed. Diagnostic thresholds were optimised post hoc on this cohort using Youden’s J and represent an internally optimised snapshot rather than a generalisable cut-off; external validation in independent populations is mandatory before any claim of a fixed operating point. The age and sex differences between FMS and RMD groups, although epidemiologically expected, may introduce latent bias by allowing the model to exploit age-correlated or sex-correlated narrative styles in addition to genuine FMS markers; subgroup analyses in larger cohorts will be needed to disentangle these effects. The video analysis relies on image-level descriptors (brightness, contrast, edge density) interpreted by an LLM that does not directly observe the visual content; these metrics may be confounded by lighting, camera settings, skin tone or patient characteristics unrelated to pain, and future iterations should evaluate validated facial action coding systems (eg, OpenFace, FACS) or native vision-language models. Although perturbation analysis identifies classification drivers, the LLM’s internal reasoning remains partially opaque. Regarding fairness, the predominantly female FMS cohort (84%) reflects known epidemiology but limits assessment of sex-specific performance; formal evaluation across demographic subgroups is planned for the validation study. Finally, fAI-BRO cannot, in its current form, distinguish FMS from DM or PMR, and clinicians using the tool must continue to perform the inflammatory and myopathic workup that this limitation makes essential.

Importantly, fAI-BRO is not intended to replace clinical judgement. FMS diagnosis requires integration of history, examination, laboratory exclusion and longitudinal assessment—none fully captured by a single interview analysis. fAI-BRO is conceived as a human-in-the-loop decision-support tool providing standardised quantification of FMS-associated linguistic and visual patterns, particularly where access to experienced rheumatologists is limited. Its output should inform, not supplant, clinical reasoning, and must always be interpreted alongside physical findings and laboratory data. The DM/PMR misclassification underscores that no AI system operating on interview data alone can substitute for comprehensive clinical assessment, and that for rare and potentially serious conditions the human clinician remains the indispensable safeguard against AI false reassurance.

In conclusion, fAI-BRO—used consistently as an integrated decision-support entity rather than a collection of subcomponents—demonstrates promising diagnostic performance for distinguishing FMS from inflammatory arthritis and mechanical conditions, with high patient acceptability, clinically feasible processing time and transparent perturbation-based explainability. The weighted ensemble approach, with transcript analysis as the dominant modality, captures FMS-specific linguistic markers, and the local zero-shot deployment supports both data-privacy compliance and cross-site generalisability. As a screening aid embedded within, not replacing, the clinical workflow, fAI-BRO may help reduce the diagnostic delay of 5–7 years currently experienced by patients with FMS and improve standardisation of FMS assessment. External validation studies addressing DM/PMR differentiation, multilingual optimisation and integration into rheumatology practice are needed before clinical adoption.
