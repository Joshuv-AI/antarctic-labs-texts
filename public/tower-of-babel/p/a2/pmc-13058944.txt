Voltage-gated sodium channel 1.8 contributes to hyperalgesia induced by the coexistence of anxiety and remifentanil exposure
Authors: Jinxia Cai, Linyao Chen, Yanan Wang, Jianwen Ye, Zixuan Xu, Linglin Gao, Zijun Zhou, Jiehao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRemifentanil-induced hyperalgesia (RIH) is directly mediated by altered mitochondrial dynamics. Moreover, anxiety is proven to worsen hyperalgesia. Voltage-gated sodium channel 1.8 (NaV1.8) is involved in both nociceptive initiation and anxiety. However, its role in modulating RIH, particularly in the coexistence of anxiety and RIH (CARIH), remains unexplored.MethodsA total of 148 Sprague–Dawley male rats were used to explore whether silencing NaV1.8 activation could alleviate CARIH. The behavioral effects were assessed using the open field, novelty suppressed feeding, and paw withdrawal tests. The mechanisms of antisense oligodeoxynucleotides (AS-ODN) targeting NaV1.8 (AS-NaV1.8) were investigated through western blot, RT-qPCR, electron microscopy, mitochondrial superoxide imaging, glutathione (GSH/GSSG) ratio, and malondialdehyde measurement.ResultsNaV1.8 was upregulated at the mRNA level in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) (P < 0.001) after CARIH. Meanwhile, spinal NR2B and Drp1 began to increase at 4 hours (P = 0.021) and 24 hours (P < 0.001), respectively. AS-NaV1.8 decreased spinal NR2B and Drp1 levels (P < 0.01), while increasing Mfn2 and OPA1 expression (P < 0.01). The MitoSox-positive cells in Group AS (AS-NaV1.8 injected into rats with CARIH) were fewer than those in Group AR (CARIH group) (P = 0.013) and Group MM (mismatch-ODN of NaV1.8 injected into rats with CARIH) (P = 0.018). Abnormal fission of spinal mitochondria was reduced in Group AS compared to Group AR (P = 0.023) and Group MM (P = 0.018).ConclusionsAnxiety exacerbates RIH, while NaV1.8 knockdown in DRG effectively attenuates CARIH by suppressing abnormal mitochondrial dynamics. Oral NaV1.8 inhibitors show promise in preclinical trials, suggesting NaV1.8 targeting as a novel approach to mitigate CARIH.

INTRODUCTION

Remifentanil, a mu-opioid receptor agonist, is commonly used for intraoperative analgesia and ICU sedation. However, it can initiate postoperative hyperalgesia. Studies have shown that N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor activation plays a role in remifentanil-induced hyperalgesia (RIH) [1].

Anxiety, a major stress-induced psychiatric disorder, is prevalent during the perioperative period and considerably affects both postoperative behavior and neurological function. Clinical evidence suggests that preoperative anxiety can intensify postoperative pain [2]. An animal model such as chronic restraint stress was found to promote spinal activation, induce anxiety-like behaviors, and worsen the severity of hyperalgesia in rats [3]. Moreover, it leads to excessive corticosterone release and downregulation of glucocorticoid receptors [4]. This model closely mimics the anxiety commonly observed in humans following preoperative stress exposure [5], making it a widely used tool for investigating potential therapeutic targets for stress-induced anxiety. While most studies on neuropathic pain and anxiety have been conducted separately, both anxiety and RIH are known to independently contribute to postoperative hyperalgesia. However, few studies have explored their combined effects. Anxiety is not merely an additive factor in postoperative RIH; without proper management, postoperative hyperalgesia can progress to chronic neuropathic pain. Therefore, it is vital to achieve a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying postoperative RIH and comorbid anxiety.

Dysfunction of mitochondrial dynamics plays a key role in hyperalgesia. Imbalance between mitochondrial fission and fusion leads to mitochondrial fragmentation and excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. This ROS outburst in turn contributes to synaptic plasticity changes, enhancing pain signal transmission and exacerbating hyperalgesia. Inhibition of abnormal mitochondrial fission can reduce spinal mitochondrial superoxide levels, alleviating neuropathic pain [6]. Both anxiety and RIH are associated with redox imbalance [7,8], suggesting a potential interaction between these two factors.

Increased expression of voltage-gated sodium channel 1.8 (NaV1.8) enhances sodium current activity in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG), promoting ROS production and hyperalgesia [9,10]. Persistent peripheral stimulation and NaV1.8 hyperactivation in the DRG facilitate central sensitization [11]. Although NaV1.8 is predominantly expressed peripherally and initiates peripheral sensitization [10], it also interfaces with central pain mechanisms and amplifies pain intensity through NMDA/NR2B receptor activation in the spinal cord. NaV1.8 knockdown has been shown to mitigate NMDA-induced mechanical hypernociception [12]. However, the role of NaV1.8 in modulating RIH, particularly in conditions where anxiety and remifentanil exposure coexist (the coexistence of anxiety and RIH [CARIH]), remains unexplored.

This study aimed to investigate whether targeting NaV1.8 function could provide a therapeutic strategy for managing CARIH. To test this hypothesis, a rat model of CARIH was established to evaluate NaV1.8 expression and mitochondrial function. Subsequently, the authors assessed whether loss-of-function variants of NaV1.8 could suppress mitochondrial fission and alleviate CARIH.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Animals

This study employed a total of 148 Sprague–Dawley (SD) male rats aged 6–8 weeks at the time of the experiment. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care Committee of Wenzhou Medical University (No. WYDW2019-0549). All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and to reduce the number of animals used. The SD rats (weighing 210–250 g) had an acclimation period for at least 7 days with food and water freely available. All animals were maintained at controlled temperature (23°C ± 0.5°C) and humidity (55% ± 10%) with 12-hours light: 12-hours dark cycle. Behavioral tests were performed between 8:00 and 20:00. Animals were deeply anesthetized with sevoflurane before euthanasia.

2. Establishment of the rat model with CARIH

1) Anxiety model induced by restraint

To prepare for chronic restraint induced anxiety, the rats were subjected to a restrained state for 21 days (Fig. 1A, B). A transparent plastic tube (height: 6 cm; inner diameter: 5.5 cm; length: 22 cm) was used to restrict movement for 6 hours (from 9:00 to 15:00) with free breathing every day [13]. The rats in the control group were not restricted.

2) Remifentanil-induced hyperalgesia

Rats were anesthetized with sevoflurane (induction, 3.0%; maintenance, 1.5%) by a nose mask. The spontaneous breathing and eyelid reflex of the rats were monitored to ensure that the depth of anesthesia was appropriate. Remifentanil hydrochloride (Ultiva, batch number: 30A03131; Ren Fu Co.) was infused at a rate of 1.2 μg/kg/min for 60 minutes through caudal vein [14]. Rats in the control group were infused with normal saline.

3) CARIH model

The restraint induced anxiety model was prepared for 21 days and was followed by remifentanil exposure for 60 minutes (Fig. 1A–C).

3. Intrathecal catheter placement for test drugs administration

After disinfecting the skin with iodine, a sterilized PE-10 tube (ID 0.28, OD 0.61; Smiths Medical) was inserted between the L3-4 vertebral space under sevoflurane anesthesia. All the rats were injected subcutaneously with 40,000 UI penicillin to prevent infection after surgery. During the study, rats did not develop infection-related adverse events or death. Then 20 μL of 2% lidocaine was administered to confirm the correct intrathecal positioning of the catheter, which was assessed 3 days later. Only rats with lower limb paralysis after the lidocaine test were included in further experiments.

4. AS-NaV1.8

An antisense oligodeoxynucleotide (AS-ODN) sequence of the rat NaV1.8 (AS-NaV1.8: 5’-TCCTCTGTGCTTGGTTCTGGCCT-3’) and a corresponding mismatch oligodeoxynucleotide (MM-ODN) sequence (MM-NaV1.8: 5’ -TCCTTCGTGCTGTGTTCGTGCCT-3’) were synthesized as phosphodiester ODNs (Shenggong Company) [15]. The ODNs were dissolved in nuclease-free ultrapure water to a final concentration of 9 μg/μL. Intrathecal injections of ODNs or saline were made in a volume of 5 μL followed by 10 μL saline to flush the catheter. Either antisense or mismatched ODN were administered twice daily for up to 5 days.

5. Behavioral test

Paw withdrawal mechanical threshold (PWMT) was assessed using a model 2390 electronic von Frey anesthesiometer (IITC INC; Life Science instrument). Through the bottom of the mesh (1 × 1 cm), a straight filament was manually applied vertically to the right plantar surface with increasing force. The maximal force was recorded when the rat paw was withdrawn or licked. A maximum cut-off value of 65 g was used to prevent tissue damage (Fig. 1D).

Paw withdrawal thermal latency (PWTL) was assessed using infrared heating equipment (Model 336, IITC INC; Life Science instrument) under a glass (3 mm thick). The PWTL stimulated by infrared heat was defined as the interval from the beginning of the stimulation to the lifting of the paw on the left hind plantar surface. A maximum cut-off time of 25 seconds was applied to prevent thermal damage (Fig. 1E).

The PWMT and PWTL tests were repeated three times at an interval of 5 minutes and the average values were obtained.

The open field test (OFT) was performed using a square apparatus (100 × 100 × 40 cm3). The area inside the apparatus was divided into 16 equal grids, with the center four grids representing the central area and the other 12 grids representing the surrounding regions. The test was performed in a room with dim lighting (a red light bulb, 8 lux) without noise. Each rat was placed in the center of a square apparatus and allowed to move freely for 5 minutes. The trajectory was recorded using a camera, and data were obtained using EthoVision XT Version 17 software (Noldus Information Technology) (Fig. 1F).

After 24 hours of food deprivation, the rats were placed in one corner of an unfamiliar square apparatus, the same as that used in the OFT. The novelty suppressed feeding test (NSFT) was defined as the latency of a rat to bite a high-sucrose, high-fat pellet placed in the center of the square apparatus [16]. A maximum cut-off value of 10 minutes was applied (Fig. 1G).

After each test, the apparatus was carefully cleaned with 75% ethanol. Since animals tested in the OFT cannot be tested in the NSFT (unwanted habituation to a novel environment), the authors randomly distributed the rats for either the OFT or NSFT.

The behavioral tests were carried out in a blinded manner.

6. Western blot and RT-qPCR

After behavioral tests, the L4-6 segments of the spinal cord were quickly removed and frozen at –80°C for further detection of protein (NR2B, Drp1, Mfn 2 and OPA1), while bilateral L4-6 DRGs were used to measure the mRNA expression of NaV1.8.

Samples of the L4-6 segments were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Sigma-Aldrich Co.). The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was collected as the total protein. Protein samples were separated via sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (10%) gel and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-NR2B (1:1000, 4212; CST), anti-Drp1 (1:1000, ab184247; Abcam), anti-Mitofusin 2 (1:2000, ab124773; Abcam), anti-OPA1 (1:1000, DF8587; Affinity). Meanwhile, GAPDH (1: 1000, 5174; CST) was used as an endogenous control. The membrane was washed with tris-buffered saline with tween buffer and further incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:10000, BL003A; Biosharp Co.). The intensity of each band was assayed using the Chemi-Doc XRS imaging system and quantified using ImageJ software (Syngene). The results are expressed as the percentage of GAPDH immunoreactivity.

The mRNA expression of NaV1.8 was assessed using reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Total RNAs of the L4-6 segments or bilateral L4-6 DRG were extracted using Trizol reagents (Invitrogen), and then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a Reverse Transcription Reagent kit (TaKaRa). RT-qPCR was performed in a 10-µL mixture containing cDNA, primers, and 2 × TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus; TaKaRa). PCR amplification was performed using specific primers for NaV1.8 (forward: 5’-CGACGATGGTGAATAACAAG-3’; reverse: 5’-GAAAACGACGAAGTACAGGT-3’), and GAPDH (forward: 5’-GACATGCCGCCTGGAGAAAC-3’; reverse: 5’-AGCCCAGGATGCCCTTTAGT-3’). The thermal cycling conditions comprised 30 seconds polymerase activation at 95°C, 40 cycles of 5 seconds at 95°C for denaturation and 34 seconds at 60°C for annealing and extension. The reaction was terminated by heating at 95°C for 15 seconds, 60°C for 60 seconds, and 95°C for 15 seconds. Each sample was run in duplicates, and data were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. All gene expression changes were expressed as fold changes compared with the control, and were normalized to the expression of GAPDH.

7. GSH/GSSG and MDA

The L4-6 samples were harvested after behavioral testing. The supernatant of the homogenate was collected after centrifugation according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A lipid peroxidation malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kit (S0131S; Beyotime Biotechnology) was used to determine the MDA levels. Estimation of glutathione/oxidized glutathione (GSH/GSSG) levels were estimated using GSH and GSSG assay kit (S0053; Beyotime Biotechnology).

8. Mitochondrial superoxide imaging

MitoSox Red, a mitochondrial superoxide indicator (Invitrogen), was dissolved in dimethylsulfoxide to a final concentration of 33 μM [17,18]. Approximately 70 minutes after MitoSox (30 μL) was injected intrathecally, rats were perfused intracardially with 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer. Then, the L4-6 segments were removed, post-fixed in the same solution overnight, and cryoprotected with 30% sucrose in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) for the next 2 days. The 35-μm sections were examined under a fluorescent microscope with a rhodamine filter. The number of MitoSox-positive cellular profiles with distinctive nuclei was determined from the images [17].

9. Electron microscopy

Under sevoflurane anesthesia, the rats were perfused with normal saline via the right ventricle. The spinal L4-6 segments were removed and sliced into 1 mm3 sections on ice and then quickly fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for over 2 hours at 4°C. After fixation, each spinal cord tissue sample was washed thrice with 0.1 M PBS. Thereafter, the samples were dehydrated using a graded acetone series, followed by 1% osmic acid treatment for 2 hours and embedding in a porous template. Subsequently, the samples were sliced into ultrathin sections and placed on a copper grid. The number of abnormal mitochondria was counted via transmission electron microscopy, as evidenced by moderate or severe swelling, vacuolization, irregular morphology, and broken or disrupted cristae (reduced mitochondrial cristae) [19]. TEM images were acquired at a final magnification of 20,000x from randomly selected areas. For each rat, mitochondrial numbers were counted in 5 randomly selected fields. The median number from each field was used for analysis. The quantitative analysis and evaluation of mitochondria was carried out in a blinded manner.

10. Experiment protocol

1) Phase 1: the effect of anxiety on RIH

Overall, 48 rats were randomly divided into four groups using randomization codes generated by Excel (n = 12 per group): Group C (control group), Group A (anxiety group), Group R (RIH group), and Group AR (CARIH group).

The PWMT and PWTL tests were performed at baseline (–21 days) and at 4, 8, 24, and 48 hours after remifentanil infusion. The OFT and NSFT were performed 46 hours after remifentanil infusion. After the behavioral tests, the L4-6 segments and DRG were excised for further experiments. Sixteen rats (n = 4 per group) were euthanized for western blot analysis and RT-qPCR, 16 rats (n = 4 per group) for MDA measurement, and 16 rats (n = 4 per group) for GSH/GSSG measurement. The timeline and flow chart of the experimental design is provided in Fig. 1A.

To further detect the time-dependence of NR2B expression and mitochondrial dynamics after the establishment of the CARIH model, 20 rats were randomly divided into five groups: Group 0 hours, Group 4 hours, Group 8 hours, Group 24 hours, and Group 48 hours (0, 4, 8, 24, and 48 hours after CARIH model establishment). The L4-6 segments were obtained at different time points.

2) Phase 2: effect of NaV1.8 on the rats with CARIH

The authors investigated whether loss-of-function variants in NaV1.8 could modulate the nociceptive effect in the rats with CARIH. Eighty adult rats were randomly allocated to four groups using randomization codes (n = 20 in each group): Group C, Group AR (CARIH model), Group MM (MM-NaV1.8 injected into rats with CARIH), and Group AS (AS-NaV1.8 injected into rats with CARIH). PWMT and PWTL assessments were conducted at baseline (–21 days) and at 4, 8, 24, and 48 hours after remifentanil exposure. The OFT and NSFT were performed at 46 hours after remifentanil infusion. After the behavioral tests, the L4-6 segments and DRG were excised for further experiments. Sixteen rats (n = 4 per group) were euthanized for western blotting and RT-qPCR, 16 rats (n = 4 per group) for mitochondrial superoxide imaging, 16 rats (n = 4 per group) for MDA measurements, 16 rats (n = 4 per group) for GSH/GSSG measurements, and 16 rats (n = 4 per group) for electron microscopy analysis. The timeline and flowchart of the experimental design is provided in Fig. 2A.

11. Statistical analysis

Sample sizes were determined based on prior studies that used the same RIH model for investigation [20]. Although statistical power was not calculated beforehand, the observed significant differences in key outcomes in the current experiments indicate that the sample sizes were adequate to detect meaningful effects. Summary data were presented as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise noted. The authors tested the data for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and performed non-parametric (or parametric) statistical tests if normality was (or was not) rejected. Hyperalgesia-related behavioral tests among the groups at different time points were compared using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (mixed model). To standardize the differences among different groups, they calculated the differences of the behavioral indices between each experimental group and the control group and converted the differences into Z-scores (standard scores) [7]. After the conversion, Pearson correlation analysis was used to measure the correlation of difference in PWMT, PWTL and anxiety levels. Statistical analyses, including anxiety-related behavioral tests and molecular experiments were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed with Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc.), R package (V4.4.0) and Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26 (IBM Co.).

RESULTS

1. Effect of anxiety on RIH

1) Behavioral test

Fig. 1D and E display the PWMT and PWTL at different time points. Compared with the control group, the other three groups significantly exhibited mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia (PWMT: interaction F [12, 176] = 102.0, P < 0.0001; time factor: F [3.338, 146.9] = 480.2, P < 0.0001; group factor: F [3, 44] = 1,236, P < 0.0001; PWTL: interaction F [12, 176] = 31.28, P < 0.0001; time factor: F [3.528, 155.2] = 136.6, P < 0.0001; group factor: F [3, 44] = 384.9, P < 0.0001; two way ANOVA [mixed model], n = 12). Compared with anxiety or remifentanil exposure alone, the coexistence of anxiety and remifentanil exposure potentiated a more pronounced nociceptive effect, with more severe mechanical hyperalgesia at different time points (P < 0.01).

In the OFT results (Fig. 1F), anxiety in Group A and Group AR led to a significant reduction in both central area entries (P < 0.01, n = 12) and time (P < 0.01, n = 12), compared with the other two non-anxiety groups.

In the NSFT (Fig. 1G), latency to feed in Groups AR and Group A was significantly longer than in Groups C and Group R (P < 0.001, n = 12). There was no significant difference between Group C and Group R (P = 0.954, n = 6).

Pearson correlation analysis indicated that neither PWMT (r = –0.05, P = 0.830, 95% CI: –0.51 to 0.42) nor PWTL (r = 0.04, P = 0.880, 95% CI: –0.44 to 0.50) exhibited a statistically significant linear correlation with anxiety after Z-score standardization. However, the nociceptive effects in Group AR were significantly heightened compared to Group R alone. Specifically, post-hoc analyses revealed that the slope between anxiety and PWMT (slope = –1.888, P < 0.001, 95% CI: –2.31 to –1.47); anxiety and PWTL (slope = –1.470, P = 0.008, 95% CI: –2.44 to -0.50) was significant. These findings indicated that anxiety exacerbated RIH.

2) RT-qPCR tests

RT-qPCR results showed that NaV1.8 mRNAs in the spinal cord of the L4-6 segments did not differ significantly among the four groups. However, NaV1.8 mRNAs in the DRG of the L4-6 segments was significantly upregulated in Group AR compared with that in Group A (P < 0.001, 95% CI: –0.75 to –0.43; n = 4) and Group R (P = 0.031, 95% CI: –0.33 to –0.01; n = 4) (Fig. 1H).

3) The spinal expression of proteins and measurement of MDA and GSH/GSSG ratio

Compared with Group A and Group R, Group AR exhibited a significant decrease in the expression of OPA1 proteins (Group AR vs. Group A, P < 0.001, 95% CI: 0.31 to 0.59; Group AR vs. Group R, P = 0.003, 95% CI: 0.08 to 0.36; one way ANOVA, n = 4). There was noticeable upregulation of Drp1 expression (P = 0.003, 95% CI: –0.36 to –0.08) and NR2B expression (P = 0.005, 95% CI: –1.23 to –0.22) in Group AR compared to those in Group R (Fig. 1I–M).

Group AR exhibited a significant elevation in MDA content compared to the other three groups (P < 0.001, n = 4) and a decrease in the GSH/GSSG ratio (P < 0.001, n = 4). (Fig. 1N, O).

2. CARIH induced time-dependent expression of NR2B and mitochondrial dynamic proteins in spinal cord

As shown in Fig. 3, Western blotting analysis revealed the time-dependent upregulation of NR2B and Drp1 expression, accompanied by the downregulation of Mfn2 and OPA1 expression after CARIH.

3. AS-NaV1.8 prevented CARIH by modulating mitochondrial dynamic and ROS production

1) Behavioral test

Compared with Group AR and Group MM, Group AS significantly reduced thermal and mechanical hyperalgesia (PWMT: interaction F [12, 304] = 238.1, P < 0.0001; time factor: F [3.454, 262.5] = 822.2, P < 0.0001; group factor: F [3, 76] = 2,805, P < 0.0001. PWTL: interaction F [12, 304] = 32.58, P < 0.0001; time factor: F [3.731, 283.5] = 192.5, P < 0.0001; group factor: F [3, 76] = 292.4, P < 0.0001; two way ANOVA [mixed model], n = 20) (Fig. 2B, C).

In the OFT (Fig. 2D), AS-NaV1.8 did not significantly increase central area entries or time compared to Group AR (entries: P = 0.066, 95% CI: –6.35 to 0.15; time: P = 0.142, 95% CI: –22.90 to 2.29; n = 10) or Group MM (entries: P = 0.095, 95% CI: –6.15 to 0.35; time: P = 0.160, 95% CI: –22.61 to 2.59; n = 10).

In the NSFT, the latency to feed in Group AS was significantly shorter than that in Group AR (P = 0.019, 95% CI: 22.37 to 318.00; n = 10) and Group MM (P = 0.043, 95% CI: 3.67 to 299.30; n = 10) (Fig. 2E).

2) RT-qPCR tests

AS-NaV1.8 led to a dramatic decrease in NaV1.8 mRNA in the DRG, compared with Group MM (P < 0.001, 95% CI: 0.57 to 0.86; n = 4) and group AR (P < 0.001, 95% CI: 0.61 to 0.90; n = 4) (Fig. 4A).

3) The spinal expression of proteins

Additionally, western blotting revealed the downregulation of Drp1 (P = 0.005, 95% CI: 0.08 to 0.41) and NR2B (P < 0.001, 95% CI: 0.21 to 0.75), as well as upregulation of OPA1 (P = 0.004, 95% CI: –0.45 to –0.09) and Mfn2 (P = 0.004, 95% CI: –0.28 to –0.06) expression after AS-NaV1.8 administration (Fig. 4B–F).

4) The change of ROS production in the spinal dorsal horn

Group AS showed decreased MDA content and an increased GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 4G, H). As shown in Fig. 4I and J, the number of MitoSox-positive cells in Group AR (b) was significantly higher than that in Group C (a) (P = 0.009, 95% CI: –8.31 to –1.19; n = 4), indicating increased ROS production after the anxiety and remifentanil exposure. The MitoSox-positive cell count in Group AS (d) was significantly lower than those in Group AR (b) (P = 0.013, 95% CI: 0.94 to 8.06; n = 4) and Group MM (c) (P = 0.018, 95% CI: 0.69 to 7.81; n = 4).

5) The change of mitochondria in spinal dorsal horn

In the L4-6 spinal dorsal horn, the number of mitochondria with abnormal fission in Group AR (b) was significantly higher than that in Group C (a) (P = 0.005, 95% CI: –12.26 to –2.24; n = 4; Fig. 4K, L). After intrathecal injection of MM-NaV1.8 and AS-NaV1.8, the number of mitochondria in Group AS (d) was significantly fewer than that in Group AR (b) (P = 0.023, 95% CI: 0.74 to 10.76; n = 4) and Group MM (c) (P = 0.018, 95% CI: 0.99 to 11.01; n = 4). No significant difference was noted between Group MM and Group AR (P = 0.998, n = 4).

DISCUSSION

The principal findings of this study revealed that neither mechanical nor thermal hyperalgesia showed a linear correlation with anxiety levels. Nevertheless, preoperative anxiety was found to exacerbate the severity of RIH, characterized by enhanced NaV1.8 mRNA expression, increased mitochondrial fission, and a significant rise in ROS. Notably, NaV1.8 knockdown significantly reduced hypernociception in rats with CARIH.

Preoperative anxiety and postoperative pain are major clinical concerns. While the combined effects of these factors have been studied [3], the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Both RIH and anxiety-related hyperalgesia are associated with factors like NMDA receptor activation, neuroinflammation, and glial activation, all contributing to central sensitization [21,22]. However, the peripheral mechanisms of CARIH, particularly the role of NaV1.8 in the DRG, may connect peripheral and central pain sensitization.

NaV1.8 plays a critical role in regulating the excitability of nociceptive sensory neurons [23,24]. Recent studies found that functional upregulation of NaV1.8 can heighten pain sensitivity [25]. Although NaV1.8 is mainly expressed in the DRG [10,23], peripheral NaV1.8 activation facilitates high-frequency, persistent firing in nociceptors, ensuring reliable transmission of pain signals to the spinal cord, thereby initiating and maintaining central sensitization [11]. Moreover, activation of peripheral NaV1.8 also receives central feedback mechanisms via dorsal root reflexes [26]. Glutamate, released spinally by primary sensory neurons, acts retrogradely on presynaptic NMDA receptors, facilitating the induction and maintenance of peripheral hypernociception [12]. This process establishes a vicious cycle that amplifies pain through reciprocal peripheral-central interactions. Even after the resolution of peripheral inputs, this induced state of hyperalgesia can perpetuate amplified central pain processing [12,27]. This may account for the observation that hyperalgesia induced by the short-acting remifentanil can persist for several days.

Evidence indicates that NaV1.8 binds directly to superoxide dismutase 2, preventing its deacetylation and mitochondrial localization [24]. The consequent ROS accumulation and mitochondrial Ca2+ overload are key contributors to the progression of neuropathic pain. ROS surges are implicated in various pathophysiological conditions and can be mitigated by ROS scavengers [18]. Anxiety, which triggers ROS production, may exacerbate pathological pain and hyperalgesia via mitochondrial pathways [7]. Mitochondrial dynamic disorders, leading to mitochondrial fragmentation and ROS outbursts, are critical to the development of neuropathic pain [28]. Drp1 promotes mitochondrial fission, whereas OPA1 and Mfn2 are involved in mitochondrial fusion [29]. In pain models, Drp1 has been found to be upregulated, alongside the downregulation of Mfn1 and OPA1 [30]. These findings suggest that mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS imbalance are key drivers of pain and provide a mechanistic link to NaV1.8.

The involvement of NaV1.8 in pain regulation underscores its potential as a therapeutic target for pain modulation. Abnormal NaV1.8 activity has been shown to cause mitochondrial alterations [31] in sensory neurons under oxidative stress [32]. Moreover, mitochondrial ROS scavengers markedly reduce NaV1.8-induced upregulation of pro-inflammatory factors, suggesting that NaV1.8 promotes inflammation via ROS-mediated signaling [9]. Oxidative outbursts and morphological alterations in mitochondria following H2O2 exposure were prevented by NaV inhibitors, supporting the idea that NaV1.8 inhibition plays a protective role in maintaining mitochondrial integrity [31]. Meanwhile, NaV1.8 inhibition has been targeted to alleviate hyperalgesia. Animal studies have shown that selective NaV1.8 blockers have antinociceptive effects on neuropathic pain [33]. The present findings provide direct electron microscopic evidence that NaV1.8 knockdown ameliorates CARIH-induced abnormal mitochondrial fission, demonstrating that NaV1.8 inhibition helps preserve mitochondrial integrity, thus regulating pain.

Although Pearson correlation analysis showed no significant linear relationship between anxiety and hyperalgesia, this does not imply the absence of an association. In animal models, moderate to high anxiety levels were not invariably accompanied by strong hyperalgesia. Z-score standardization revealed that anxiety scores in Group R spanned both extremes of the X-axis, suggesting that RIH may still provoke anxiety. This variability could be attributed to the inherent resilience of rodents to anxiety and depression, which may mask a more subtle relationship [34]. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm this relationship.

The difference between animals treated with remifentanil alone and those exposed to both remifentanil and chronic stress is relatively small. This additive effect of combining anxiety and remifentanil exposure may become more pronounced with an extended observation window. The authors’ observations were limited to the first 48 hours after model establishment. In future experiments, the observational period can be extended, which may reveal a more pronounced effect of anxiety in exacerbating RIH. Additionally, this study focused primarily on the mitochondrial pathway, while NaV1.8 may also exert effects through other mechanisms, such as the ERK5/CREB pathway or ectopic discharge routes [25]. Although NaV1.8 knockdown reduced mitochondrial fission and ROS production, the exact mechanism by which NaV1.8 regulates mitochondrial oxidative stress warrants further investigation. Furthermore, the intrathecal NaV1.8 administration in this animal study limits its direct clinical application. Fortunately, there is growing progress in the clinical translation of NaV1.8-targeted therapies. Oral selective NaV1.8 inhibitors, already applied in preclinical trials, have shown potential to provide effective and safe relief for postoperative pain without the concerns of addiction [35]. This suggests that NaV1.8-targeted therapies could become viable treatments for pain relief in human patients in the future.

In summary, the findings of this study indicate that anxiety exacerbates RIH. Targeting NaV1.8 as a therapeutic strategy could provide the dual benefit of alleviating hyperalgesia and stabilizing mitochondrial function. NaV1.8 knockdown may potentially alter mitochondrial dynamics, thereby alleviating CARIH. This study highlights AS-NaV1.8 as a promising avenue for improving postoperative hyperalgesia caused by the coexistence of opioid exposure and anxiety.
