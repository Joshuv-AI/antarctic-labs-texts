Spine surgery in Denmark 2009–2024: A 15-year overview from the DaneSpine registry
Authors: Andreas K. Andresen, Karen Højmark, Mikkel Mylius Rasmussen, Michael Nielsen, Charlotte Sommer Meyer, Kresten Wendell Rickers, Mikkel Ø. Andersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe DaneSpine registry is Denmark's national clinical quality and research database for spine surgery, established in 2009 under the Danish Spine Surgical Society (DRKS). It currently holds more than 115,000 cases and represents the largest patient-reported outcome (PRO)-based surgical registry in the country.Research questionTo describe surgical trends and outcomes in degenerative spine surgery in Denmark using aggregated data from the DaneSpine registry's annual reports (2010–2024).MethodsAggregated descriptive data from DaneSpine annual reports were reviewed. Key variables included patient demographics, diagnostic categories, procedure counts, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), and temporal trends. PROMs were collected preoperatively and at 1, 2, 5, and 10 years postoperatively.ResultsFrom 2009 to 2024, approximately 115,000 surgical cases were registered across 16 participating clinics (9 public and 7 private). The most common diagnoses were lumbar spinal stenosis (38%) and lumbar disc herniation (32%). There was a statistically significant and meaningful mean improvement in EQ-5D, Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) pain scores at 1-year follow-up. Satisfaction with treatment outcome was reported in more than 75% of patients. Over time, registry completeness and PROM compliance remained high, with gradual improvements in outcome measures across several diagnostic groups.Discussion and conclusionOver its 15-year history, DaneSpine has emerged as a cornerstone of spinal surgical quality assessment in Denmark. It provides reliable outcome benchmarks, supports clinical decision-making, and offers a model for integrating PROMs into surgical registries. Continued development of the registry will enable even more precise patient selection and support value-based spine care.

Introduction

The assessment of surgical outcomes is essential to maintain and improve quality of care in modern healthcare systems. However, generating high-quality evidence in surgery presents inherent methodological challenges. Unlike pharmacological interventions, surgical procedures often make blinding of patients and surgeons infeasible, thereby limiting the applicability of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and increasing the risk of performance and detection bias (McCulloch et al., 2009; Ergina et al., 2009; Cook, 2009).

To address these limitations, national and international clinical registries have emerged as critical tools for outcome monitoring, quality assurance, and research (Stromqvist et al., 2009; Melloh et al., 2008). In particular, the integration of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) into registry-based data collection has provided a more complete view of treatment efficacy. PROMs offer unique insight into symptom relief, functional recovery, and quality of life—domains often underrepresented in traditional clinical endpoints (Black, 2013; Deshpande et al., 2011). Embedding PROM – data into routine clinical registries allows for longitudinal outcome tracking and institutional benchmarking, strengthening the foundation for real-world evidence in spine surgery.

Today major spine surgery registries are active worldwide, including Swespine (Sweden), NORspine (Norway), FinSpine (Finland), the British Spine Registry (UK), and Spine Tango (EUROSPINE), each contributing valuable data for international comparisons and research collaborations (Andersen et al., 2019; Lonne et al., 2019; Lagerback et al., 2019; Abbott et al., 2025).

In the evidence hierarchy, observational studies are traditionally considered inferior to RCTs. However, registry-based studies occupy an important middle layer —providing large-scale, real-world data that complements and, in some cases, rivals the validity of randomized trials (Benson and Hartz, 2000). A Cochrane review published in 2023 further supported this position, concluding that effect estimates from observational studies were either similar to or only marginally different from those reported in RCTs (Toews et al., 2024).

These findings emphasize the increasing scientific legitimacy of observational registry data and its value in reflecting actual patient outcomes in surgical practice.

In Denmark, the DaneSpine registry was launched in 2009 to systematically capture outcomes of spinal surgical interventions across both public and private sectors. Initially as a 1:1 implementation of Swespine (originally in Swedish and hosted in Sweden), the registry was handed over as a functional, structured platform for national use. Since then, and under the ownership of the Danish Spine Surgical Society (DRKS), DaneSpine has become the country's largest PRO-based registry, covering more than 80% of spine surgeries performed nationally (Simony et al., 2014).

With more than 15 years of consistent national data collection, DaneSpine now offers a unique opportunity to explore trends in surgical indications, techniques, and outcomes, based on real-world data. This study presents a comprehensive analysis of aggregated data from the registry's annual reports (2012–2024) on the largest cervical and lumbar degenerative surgically treated patient cohorts, highlighting developments in clinical practice, patient-reported outcomes, and the central role of structured data collection in enhancing spine care.

Materials and methods

DaneSpine is a national registry incorporating simultaneous clinical and PROM data from patients undergoing spine surgery. It is clinician-owned and receives no direct public funding. All participating clinics pay a modest annual fee based on their surgical volume, covering database hosting. Registry data is maintained by a national secretariat at the Spine Research Unit, Spine Centre of Southern Denmark.

Study design

This is a retrospective, descriptive study based on aggregated, anonymized data from DaneSpine annual reports (2012–2024) on degenerative spinal diseases, as the proportion of other pathologies represents less than 5% of the total number of patients in the registry. Reports are publicly available through DRKS (Andersen et al., 2024). Average incidence rates were calculated based on population data from Statistics Denmark (Statistics Denmark, 2026).

Variables collected

The DaneSpine registry systematically collects patient-reported and surgeon-reported data at multiple time points.

Preoperative data are reported directly by patients, while intraoperative details are provided by the operating surgeons. Postoperative follow-up includes patient-reported outcomes at 1, 2, 5, and 10 years after surgery.

Patient-reported data include basic demographic information, including duration of leg- and backpain, duration of sick leave and comorbidities as well as the following validated outcome measures: Visual Analogue Scale (Williamson and Hoggart, 2005) (VAS) for back and leg pain (or neck and arm pain), EuroQol-5 Dimensions-3 Levels (EQ-5D) (EuroQol, 1990; Sorensen et al., 2009), Short Form-36 (SF-36) (Bjorner et al., 1998), Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) (Lauridsen et al., 2006; Fairbank et al., 1980) and the Neck Disability Index (NDI) (Lauridsen et al., 2017) and Scoliosis Research Society-22 questionnaire (SRS-22) (Simony et al., 2016).

Surgeon-reported data include: Primary surgical diagnosis, surgical procedure performed, implant types and specifications, intra- and postoperative complications, Tokuhashi score (Tokuhashi et al., 1990) (for spinal metastases) and Lenke classification (Lenke et al., 2001) (for adolescent idiopathic scoliosis).

Between 2009 and 2024, a total of 15,385 cervical spine surgeries were registered in DaneSpine. This represents approximately 15% of all spine surgeries recorded in the DaneSpine registry in Denmark. This most common diagnosis most often due to cervical disc herniation with radiculopathy (37%) or cervical foraminal stenosis with radiculopathy (42%).

Over the years, DaneSpine data have served as the foundation or a key component in data collection for 58 peer-reviewed scientific publications.

Additionally, since 2012, an annual report has been published, and in the past two years, a brief public version of the report has also been released. All reports are available at: https://drks.ortopaedi.dk/aarsrapporter/(in Danish).

Discussion

This 15-year review of DaneSpine registry data offers valuable insights into the outcomes of spine surgery for common degenerative conditions in Denmark. The analysis underscores the sustained benefits of national, clinician-led registries in monitoring surgical quality and enabling evidence-based improvements in care. Across diagnoses, patients report significant and clinically meaningful improvements in pain, function, and quality of life following surgery.

Despite inherent heterogeneity in patient profiles and diagnoses, outcome trends remained stable—or even improved—since implementation of the database. The consistent integration of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), high data completeness, and clinical ownership have made DaneSpine a robust platform for national quality assurance and research.

Spine registries compared to DaneSpine

Other registries have consistently collected patient reported data on spine surgeries and related patient reported outcomes. Since the 1990'es, data has been collected in SweSpine, which has achieved extensive coverage of spine surgical activity with a national coverage of ∼85% and national follow-up of 70%. SweSpine is owned and driven by the Swedish government. SweSpine's long duration and collection of patient reported outcomes measures including ODI, VAS and EQ-5D, provide a reference standard for Scandinavia and international comparison.

NORSpine was established in 2007, inspired in large part by the Swedish experience. It is government-funded and includes all providers of spine surgery in Norway, representing a population-based clinical quality registry with structured data collection on surgical and patient outcome variables at baseline and follow-up (3 and 12 months). Data completeness is more than 80%, with follow-up of more than 80% after 3- and 12 months. NORSpine does not include longer term follow-up as in SweSpine and DaneSpine.

FinSpine was launched in 2016 and is supported by the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare. The goal is to monitor surgical activities and nationwide outcomes, including long-term effectiveness or surgical treatment. Since its launch in 2016 is has obtained growing nationwide coverage and is used in 83% of public hospitals with growing response rates on patient reported outcomes which include 3 months, 1-, 2-, 5- and 10 years follow up (Marjamaa et al., 2023).

While the SPINE tango has collected data on spine surgeries and patient reported outcomes over a 22 year period, and with 30 participating countries, the 2024 yearly report shows that data has been collected on 168,583 surgeries, where outcome data is available on between ¼ and 1/3 of patients, depending on patient diagnosis. Data form many participating countries can be useful to investigate trends of types of surgeries and complications, however a significant selection bias may be induced when looking at patient reported outcomes.

Each registry serves its national or international community by capturing PROMs and clinical data systematically. The data is collected to monitor quality, inform practices and support research. While the designs differ in scope and structure, the databases collectively represent a movement towards evidence-based spinal care driven by patient reported outcomes.

For all registries, data coverage and data completeness is a major challenge. In DaneSpine, the completeness of registered cases is ∼80% and follow-up rates differ substantially between hospitals. In 2016, a dropout analysis performed on DaneSpine data from one hospital showed that non-responders were younger, more often male and smokers than responders, but no differences were found in satisfaction or pain between responders and non-responders (Hojmark et al., 2016).

Degenerative spondylolisthesis: shifts in surgical practice

DaneSpine data has shown a decline in number of patients undergoing surgery for degenerative spondylolisthesis. This reduction—down from a peak of approximately 500 surgeries annually—likely reflects growing international skepticism toward routine fusion procedures in all patients with degenerative spondylolisthesis (Austevoll et al., 2021; Forsth et al., 2016).

Reflections on symptom duration and surgery

Patients undergoing lumbar disc herniation surgery typically present with severe baseline disability, as indicated by high scores in pain and functional limitation measures. Nonetheless, postoperative outcomes are generally excellent at the group level. Significant and clinically relevant improvements are observed across all PROMs. However, it is important to emphasize that while leg symptoms improve most reliably after surgery, complete relief from back and leg pain cannot be guaranteed. Data from registries like DaneSpine enable appropriate counseling preoperatively, particularly regarding the more variable resolution of axial back pain.

The timing of surgery in lumbar disc herniation remains a matter of clinical debate, while most patients with a disc herniation can be handled with non-operative care (Lilly et al., 2021), a subgroup of patients require surgery to obtain a satisfactory clinical result (Peul et al., 2007; Bailey et al., 2020). Apart from the well-established correlation between symptom duration and reduced overall patient-reported treatment outcomes, timing of surgery seems to be particularly critical for patients with severe disc herniation symptoms who are on sick leave. A study based on data from the DaneSpine registry revealed that only 25% of patients returned to the workforce if their sick leave had exceeded three months prior to disk herniation surgery, while patients with shorter duration of symptoms were more likely to return to the workforce (Andersen et al., 2017).

All patients included in the registry had undergone conservative management of variable duration, some extending over months to years. Based on the Data from the DaneSpine registry it seems reasonable to offer surgery to patients with symptoms not responsive to conservative care as data suggests meaningful clinical improvement in this patient group (Bailey et al., 2020).

While most data on the subject of symptom duration is created on patients treated for lumbar spine conditions, data from the DaneSpine registry suggests similar effect for patients with cervical radiculopathy. As these patients have gone through the same conservative care regimen as the lumbar patients and patients report good outcomes across multiple outcome measures it seems safe to advocate surgical intervention in patients with symptoms not responsive to conservative care as data in this group also suggests meaningful clinical improvement.

Between 2009 and 2024, preoperative data were registered for 1782 patients undergoing surgery for cervical myelopathy. These patients experienced clear and clinically relevant improvement across all measured outcomes, particularly in terms of neck and arm pain relief. However, the symptom profile and surgical objectives in myelopathy differ significantly from those with pure radiculopathy.

In patients with cervical myelopathy, the primary goal of surgery is to halt or slow neurological deterioration rather than to fully resolve symptoms. Indeed, preserving neurological status quo—particularly in patients with early neurological deficits -can be considered a successful outcome, given the often irreversible nature of spinal cord injury. Preoperative symptom severity was lower in the myelopathy group than in the radiculopathy group, reflecting a lower threshold for surgical indication in myelopathy cases. Whereas improvements in VAS arm and neck were modest in the Myelopathy group, the improvements in quality of life, measured by the EQ-5D, were similar to the improvements seen in patients with isolated radiculopathy.

Research impact and knowledge translation

DaneSpine has not only functioned as a national quality-monitoring tool but also as a valuable research infrastructure. To date, 58 peer-reviewed publications based on DaneSpine data have been published. This number is expected to increase in the future as more data will be gathered in smaller patient groups such as spinal cancer surgery, deformity surgery, trauma surgery and infectious surgery.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged, most attributed to the inherent nature of database data. Firstly, the lack of data completeness and missing data may institute bias as the reason for non-response is unknown. Secondly, the use of aggregated registry data limits the ability to conduct detailed individual-level statistical analyses, such as multivariable modeling or subgroup stratification beyond predefined categories. Furthermore, while patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) compliance is generally high, it varies across participating centers, which may introduce bias or reduce the generalizability of certain findings.

Conclusion

DaneSpine serves as a model for how clinician-led national registries can support both continuous clinical quality monitoring and clinical research. With more than 115,000 procedures recorded to date, it stands as one of the world's largest and most comprehensive spinal surgical registries. The consistent integration of PROMs, and a strong foundation of clinician ownership have positioned DaneSpine as a central pillar of spine care evaluation in Denmark. As the registry moves into its next phase, its ongoing development will be critical for advancing patient-centered, evidence-based spine surgery—both nationally and as a reference for international efforts in registry-based spine care research.
