Laparoscopic endoscopic cooperative surgery versus laparoscopic segmental resection for the treatment of endoscopically unresectable colorectal polyps: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Sufen Fang, Kaiming Wu, Ruisheng Cai, Xueyan Hu, Wenqing Wang, Qiuyue Liu, Yulong He, Xuefu Zhou, Dongjie Yang, Tengfei Hao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLaparoscopic segmental resection (LSR) is a common treatment modality for endoscopically unresectable colorectal polyps. Laparoscopic endoscopic cooperative surgery (LECS) has emerged as a promising alternative, yet current evidence of its efficacy remains limited.ObjectivesThis meta-analysis aims to compare the therapeutic outcomes of LECS versus LSR for endoscopically unresectable colorectal polyps and to provide robust evidence for clinical practice.MethodsThis meta-analysis was prospectively registered on the PROSPERO website. We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CNKI, Wanfang Data, VIP, and CBM for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies from database inception to May 16, 2025. Data analysis was conducted using Review Manager 5.4 and Stata 18 software. Certainty of evidence was assessed using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE).ResultsA total of 13 studies involving 715 patients were included in this study. Meta-analysis results showed that the LECS group had a significantly shorter length of hospital stay (MD: −2.89 days, 95% CI: −3.43 to −2.34, 13 studies, 715 patients), shorter operation time (MD: −41.62 min, 95% CI: −56.96 to −26.28, 12 studies, 593 patients), fewer postoperative overall complications (OR: 0.29, 95% CI: 0.17 to 0.49, 9 studies, 513 patients), and lower estimated blood loss (MD: − 26.12 mL, 95% CI: −37.07 to −15.17, 8 studies, 435 patients) when compared to the LSR group.ConclusionsLECS demonstrates superior short-term outcomes compared to LSR and should be considered a preeminent alternative for endoscopically unresectable colorectal polyps. However, long-term efficacy remains to be confirmed by well-designed, high-quality trials.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00464-025-12181-7.Keywords: LECS, LSR, Colorectal polyps, Therapeutic outcomes

The incidence of colorectal polyps rises with age. In the USA, the prevalence of colorectal polyps is 30% among individuals over the age of 50 [1]. In China, the highest prevalence is observed in the 60–74 age group, reaching 41.22% [2]. Most colorectal cancers (CRCs) originate from adenomatous polyps [3], and the progression from adenoma to invasive CRC typically takes several years [4]. Timely detection and removal of colorectal polyps can significantly reduce the incidence of colorectal cancer [5]. Advances in endoscopic techniques, such as endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR), endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), and endoscopic full-thickness resection (EFTR), have made endoscopic resection the primary method for removing colorectal polyps. However, 10–15% of colorectal polyps remain difficult to resect endoscopically [6, 7].

Laparoscopic resection is a common treatment strategy for endoscopically unresectable colorectal polyps, often involving segmental colectomy and other procedures [8]. Laparoscopic segmental resection (LSR) is associated with significant surgical risks and complications [9]. Recently, laparoscopic endoscopic cooperative surgery (LECS) has emerged as a potential alternative for managing endoscopically unresectable colorectal polyps [10]. LECS aims to avoid colectomy and offers advantages in organ preservation [11]. However, the therapeutic efficacy of LECS is predominantly supported by small sample studies or observational research [12, 13], with individual studies having insufficient levels of evidence.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to systematically evaluate and compare the therapeutic outcomes of LECS versus LSR for endoscopically unresectable colorectal polyps. Primary outcomes included length of hospital stay (LOS), operation time, postoperative overall complications, and estimated blood loss (EBL). The certainty of evidence for these outcomes was assessed using the GRADE framework [14], thereby providing evidence to guide clinical practice.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) in May 2025, with the registration number CRD420251050935. The protocol is available at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251050935.

Data sources and search strategy

To identify relevant studies, we conducted comprehensive searches in the following databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Chinese Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP), Wanfang Data, and Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (CBM). The search period covered from the inception of the databases to May 16, 2025.

The search entries for “LECS” included: “combined endoscopic and laparoscopic surgery” OR “combined endo-laparoscopic surgery” OR “CELS” OR “colonoscopic-assisted laparoscopic” OR “combined endoscopic-laparoscopic surgery” OR “laparoendoscopic” OR “laparoscopic-assisted colonoscopic” OR “laparoscopic-endoscopic cooperative surgery” OR “LECS” OR “laparoscopic endoscopic cooperative surgery” OR “laparoendoscopic rendezvous” OR “laparoscopic-assisted polypectomy” OR “endoscopic-assisted laparoscopic wedge resection” OR “endoscopic-assisted laparoscopic resection”. The search entries for “colorectal polyps” included: “colorectal tumor” OR “colorectal polyp” OR “colonic polyps” OR “colonic neoplasms” OR “colorectal neoplasms” OR “colonic polyp” OR “colon polyps” OR “rectal polyps” OR “colorectal polyps” OR “colorectal neoplasm” OR “colorectal tumors”. We combined MeSH and non-MeSH terms searching to ensure comprehensive coverage of relevant studies.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Our meta-analysis included all types of comparative studies, encompassing case–control studies, cohort studies, controlled clinical trials, randomized clinical trials (RCTs), and non-randomized controlled trials that compared LECS with LSR. Inclusion criteria: (1) patients diagnosed with endoscopically unresectable colorectal polyps; (2) individuals aged 18 years or older; (3) English or Chinese literature; (4) at least one of the outcomes must be reported.

These were the exclusion criteria: (1) case reports, case series, expert opinions, review articles; (2) children (age < 18 years); (3) animal experimental studies; (4) studies that do not clearly describe LECS comparison with LSR; (5) duplicate studies or the same institution studies, only the latest or most complete study is included; (6) non-English and non-Chinese literature.

Outcome and definitions

The primary outcomes of the present meta-analysis were LOS, operation time, postoperative overall complications, and EBL. Secondary outcomes encompassed time to pass flatus, time to resume diet, postoperative C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, and readmission rate.

The LOS was defined as the number of days from admission to discharge. The operation time was defined as the duration from the initial skin incision to the final skin closure. The postoperative overall complications were defined as any deviation from the normal postoperative course occurring within 90 days after surgery. Postoperative CRP levels were defined as the highest CRP value measured within the first five postoperative days. The readmission rate was defined as any hospital readmission related to the previous surgery within 30 days of discharge.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers (Reviewer Sufen Fang and Reviewer Kaiming Wu) using a standardized extraction form. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (Reviewer Tengfei Hao). Data extracted included: first author, study design, publication year, sample size, study country, outcomes.

Given the heterogeneity in reporting formats across studies, some original data required standardization for meta-analysis. The following conversions were applied: If the literature reports the median, minimum value, maximum value, and sample size, we converted them to means and standard deviation (SD) using the formulas suggested by Luo et al. [15] and Wan et al. [16]. If the literature reports the sample size, mean, and p-value, we use the formula recommended by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions to calculate the SD [17]. All steps of the data conversion process were documented thoroughly, including the formulas and methods used, to ensure the reproducibility of our analysis. For studies with missing data, we contacted authors via email to request the missing information. If the data could not be obtained, those studies were excluded from relevant analyses to prevent bias.

Quality assessment

The quality of the included studies was rigorously assessed to ensure the reliability and validity of the findings. For RCTs, the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool was utilized, which comprehensively evaluates key domains such as random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential biases. For observational studies, the Newcastle–Ottawa scale (NOS) was applied to assess the quality [18]. The NOS evaluates studies based on selection, comparability, and exposure or outcome. According to the NOS scoring criteria, studies with a total score of 7 or more are considered high quality, those with a score of 4–6 are of medium quality, and those with a score of < 4 are low quality.

Statistical analysis

In this meta-analysis, we employed Review Manager version 5.4 software to synthesize and evaluate the data from the included studies. Heterogeneity among the studies was assessed using the I2 statistic. Heterogeneity was interpreted according to the I2 statistic, with values of 25, 50, and 75% serving as thresholds for low, moderate, and high inconsistency, respectively [19]. For dichotomous variables, we calculated the odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In continuous variables, the mean difference (MD), along with 95% CIs, was used. A random-effects model was applied if significant heterogeneity was detected (I2 > 50%); otherwise, a fixed-effects model was adopted. To evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimates and to identify possible drivers of heterogeneity, we carried out sensitivity analyses using Stata 18 software. In addition, subgroup analyses were stratified according to study design. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, as prespecified for any meta-analysis with ten or more studies, in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews [20] of Interventions and the recommendations of Sterne et al. [21]. The certainty of the evidence was evaluated using the GRADE framework.

Results

Literature identification and selection

We initially retrieved 4962 records from various databases: PubMed (1095 records), Embase (661 records), Cochrane Library (29 records), Web of Science (343 records), CNKI (267 records), WangFang (1330 records), VIP database (323 records), CBM database (914 records). After excluding 1417 duplicate items, 228 case reports, and 57 meta-analyses, 3260 records remained. We further eliminated 1486 records that were irrelevant to the disease, leaving 1774 records. After carefully examining their titles, abstracts, and full texts, we included 13 references [12, 13, 22–32]. The detailed procedure and results are presented in Fig. 1.

Literature characteristics and quality assessment

This systematic review and meta-analysis included 13 studies involving 715 patients from North America, Asia, and Europe. The LECS group comprised 301 (42%) patients, while the LSR group consisted of 414 (58%) patients. The studies included ten retrospective or cohort studies and three RCTs. Six studies were conducted in the USA, three in China, two in Netherlands and one in Spain and Japan. The detailed literature characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Meta-analysis results

LOS

LOS was reported by all studies included in our meta-analysis: 301 in the LECS group and 414 in the LSR group [12, 13, 22–32]. The pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in LOS in the LECS group compared to the LSR group (MD: − 2.89 days, 95% CI: − 3.43 to − 2.34, P < 0.00001). The analysis exhibited high statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 73%, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3).

Forest plot comparing the length of hospital stay between the LECS and LSR groups

Operation time

Operation time was reported by twelve studies included in our meta-analysis [12, 13, 22–29, 31, 32]. The pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in operation time in the LECS group compared to the LSR group (MD: − 41.62 min, 95% CI: − 56.96 to − 26.28, P < 0.00001). The I2 value was 93% (high heterogeneity), with a P-value < 0.00001, which was statistically significant (Fig. 4).

Forest plot comparing the operation time between the LECS and LSR groups

Postoperative overall complications

Postoperative overall complications were reported by nine studies included in our meta-analysis: 198 in the LECS group and 315 in the LSR group [12, 13, 24, 26, 27, 29–32]. The overall complication rates were estimated at 10.1% for LECS and 28.3% for LSR. The pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in postoperative complications in the LECS group compared to the LSR group (OR: 0.29, 95% CI: 0.17–0.49, P < 0.00001). The analysis exhibited no statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.62) (Fig. 5).

Forest plot comparing the postoperative overall complications between the LECS and LSR groups

EBL

EBL was reported by eight studies included in our meta-analysis: 193 in the LECS group and 242 in the LSR group [12, 22, 23, 25, 27, 29, 31, 32]. The pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in EBL in the LECS group compared to the LSR group (MD: − 26.12 ml, 95% CI: − 37.07 to − 15.17, P < 0.00001). The analysis exhibited high statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 95%, P < 0.00001) (Fig. 6).

Forest plot comparing the estimated blood loss between the LECS and LSR groups

Time to pass flatus

Time to pass flatus was reported by four studies included in our meta-analysis: 129 in the LECS group and 125 in the LSR group [23, 25, 27, 29]. The pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant short in time to pass flatus in the LECS group compared to the LSR group (MD: − 1.26 days, 95% CI: − 2.20 to − 0.33, P = 0.008). The analysis exhibited high statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 96%, P < 0.00001) (Fig. 7).

Forest plot comparing the time to pass flatus between the LECS and LSR groups

Time to resume diet

Time to resume diet was reported by two studies included in our meta-analysis: 32 in the LECS group and 85 in the LSR group [29, 32]. The pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant short time to resume diet in the LECS group compared to the LSR group (MD: − 2.28 days, 95% CI: − 3.27 to − 1.29, P < 0.00001). The analysis exhibited no statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.87) (Fig. 8).

Forest plot comparing the time to resume diet between the LECS and LSR groups

Postoperative CRP levels

Postoperative CRP levels were reported by two studies included in our meta-analysis: 38 in the LECS group and 91 in the LSR group [24, 32]. The pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant low in postoperative CRP levels in the LECS group compared to the LSR group (MD: − 27.35 mg/l, 95% CI: − 33.32 to − 21.38, P < 0.00001). The analysis exhibited no statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.96) (Fig. 9).

Forest plot comparing the postoperative CRP levels between the LECS and LSR groups

Readmission rate

The readmission rate was reported by three studies included in our meta-analysis: 72 in the LECS group and 60 in the LSR group [12, 26, 29]. The readmission rate was estimated at 2.8% for LECS and 3.3% for LSR. The pooled analysis demonstrated no significant difference in readmission rate in the LECS group compared to the LSR group (OR: 0.8, 95% CI: 0.1–6.54, P = 0.83). The analysis exhibited low statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 12%, P = 0.29) (Fig. 10).

Forest plot comparing the readmission rate between the LECS and LSR groups

Subgroup analysis

Given the substantial heterogeneity in LOS, operation time, EBL, and time to pass flatus, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by study design (RCTs vs. non-RCTs). The findings are presented in Figs. 3, 4, 6, and 7. However, subgroup analyses did not identify the sources of heterogeneity.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness and heterogeneity of the meta-analysis results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis. The findings demonstrated that the primary outcomes remained consistent regardless of the exclusion of any individual study (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4), confirming the reliability and stability of our results.

Publication bias and certainty of evidence

We used funnel plots to examine publication bias. Visual inspection revealed no substantial evidence of publication bias (Supplementary Figs. 5, 6). We used the GRADE system to evaluate the certainty of evidence for the four primary outcomes. LOS was rated as high-quality evidence owing to its significant efficacy and highly consistent results. Both postoperative overall complications and operative time were assessed as moderate-quality evidence (see Table 2; Supplementary Fig. 7).

Discussion

Previous researchers have published similar studies. The Kolosov study [33], originating from Russia, included 17 articles through a literature search and conducted a meta-analysis. However, the Kolosov study included a substantial proportion of single-arm retrospective case series lacking control groups [34–37] and relied solely on a single-database (PubMed) search. These limitations are likely to increase the risk of selection and reporting bias.

The Laure study [38], from the Netherlands, included nine articles through a literature search and conducted a review of colonoscopic-assisted laparoscopic wedge resection (CAL-WR) for colonic neoplasms. However, the Laure study merely provided subjective descriptions of the literature and failed to perform a quantitative meta-analysis. The authors emphasize that CAL-WR is a promising technique, yet underline the need for further clinical investigations.

As the inaugural systematic review of laparoendoscopic resections for colorectal neoplasia, the Arezzo study centered on the reoperation rate following combined procedures [39], an endpoint distinct from ours. Consequently, the two reviews are complementary rather than overlapping.

Our meta-analysis results demonstrated that the LECS group had a significantly lower LOS, operation time, postoperative overall complications, EBL, postoperative CRP levels, and shorter time to pass flatus and time to resume diet when compared to the LSR group. However, no significant difference was observed in the readmission rate between the two groups.

Beck and colleagues performed LECS on three patients with colon polyps and first described the technique in 1993, proposing it as a potential alternative to traditional colectomy procedures [40]. LECS combines the strengths of laparoscopy and endoscopy, enabling more limited resection and better preservation of organ function.

Given these advantages, LECS was associated with shorter operative time, lower EBL, and significantly reduced postoperative CRP levels compared with LSR, further confirming the reduced surgical trauma and stress in the LECS group. Additionally, the shorter time to pass flatus in the LECS group allowed for an earlier resumption of diet and a shorter hospital stay. These findings are consistent with a previous study [33].

A total of nine studies documented postoperative overall complications, indicating that LECS did not result in a higher complication rate. The LECS group had a complication rate of 10.1% (20 out of 198 patients), compared to 28.3% (89 out of 315 patients) in the LSR group. The most common types of complications reported in the LECS group were ileus, urinary tract infection, and urinary retention. The most common complications in the LSR group were ileus and surgical site infection. Kolosov’s study reported a comparable overall morbidity rate of 10.3% after LECS, yet observed no significant reduction relative to LSR, a finding that conflicts with our results. This discrepancy is probably attributable to the poor methodological quality of the studies pooled in Kolosov’s study and the marked baseline imbalance between the groups [33].

Although sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our findings, substantial heterogeneity was observed for several outcomes. Subgroup analyses undertaken to explore its sources yielded no definitive explanation. This is likely attributable to the limited number of included studies. Moreover, other potential contributors to heterogeneity, such as variations in LECS protocols, inconsistent definitions of endoscopically unresectable colorectal polyps, and surgeon learning curve, could not be incorporated into the subgroup analyses, thereby precluding definitive identification of their origin.

Eight studies [12, 13, 24, 28–32] reported postoperative pathology after LECS (n = 129), revealing malignant polyps in 15 lesions (11.6%). After excluding the study by Suzuki et al. [32], the remaining seven studies recorded six patients who required additional oncologic resection, yielding a reoperation rate of 5.3%. These results mirror the systematic review by Arezzo et al., which reported an adenocarcinoma incidence of 10.5% and a 7.9% rate of subsequent oncologic surgery. Although the reoperation rate is not negligible, it is clinically acceptable because more than 90% of patients avoid more extensive procedures. Additionally, LECS is associated with lower procedural costs than LSR [26].

Five studies reported polyp recurrence after LECS. Suzuki et al. [32] observed no recurrences but gave no follow-up duration. Among the remaining four (median/mean follow-up 9.9–39 months), two studies [24, 30] detected no recurrences; one study [29] recorded one recurrence (n = 10); and another [13] recorded two (n = 5). Sparse and heterogeneous data preclude reliable synthesis. One study included 209 polyps from 160 patients and recorded no recurrences during a median follow-up of 65 months [41].

A limitation of our study is that most of the included studies were retrospective, with only three being clinical randomized controlled trials. Another limitation is the substantial heterogeneity observed for several outcomes. Additionally, restricting the search to English and Chinese language studies may also have introduced bias. These limitations may have potentially influenced the study outcomes. Therefore, there is a need for more rigorous, standardized, multicenter, randomized controlled trials in future research.

Conclusion

LECS demonstrates superior short-term outcomes compared to LSR and should be considered a preferred alternative for endoscopically unresectable colorectal polyps, given its lower postoperative complication rates and faster postoperative recovery. Our findings provide evidence-based support for clinical decision-making. However, the long-term efficacy of LECS awaits confirmation by well-designed, high-quality studies.
