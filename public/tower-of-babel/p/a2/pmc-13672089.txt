Inhaling high THC cannabis acutely increases inflammation but not insulin sensitivity: a quasi-randomized trial
Authors: Angela D Bryan, Carillon J Skrzynski, Jinqiu Yang, Kyle A Chrystal, L Cinnamon Bidwell, Kent E Hutchison, Leigh Perreault
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCannabis is linked with increased appetite, raising concern about obesity-related disease and type 2 diabetes. Yet data suggest metabolic benefits of cannabis through inflammatory processes and insulin sensitivity. This was a quasi-randomly assigned experimental study comparing acute effects of a CBD-dominant versus THC-dominant versus CBD + THC cannabis flower product. Data were collected from community participants through a mobile pharmacology laboratory and a clinical research center. Participants smoked their assigned pre-rolled cannabis flower cigarette ad libitum. Primary outcomes were IL-10, chemokine MCP-1, a composite of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, IL-12, IFNG, and IL-4), and insulin sensitivity (Matsuda index). Outcomes were predicted by peak blood levels of THC and CBD immediately after cannabis use. Hypotheses were that inflammation and insulin sensitivity would vary as a function of CBD and THC exposure, and that inflammatory changes would be associated with insulin sensitivity. Participants (n = 108) were 25 to 45 (Mage = 29.7, sd = 5.4), 56.5% Female, and 88.0% white. Mixed effect models indicated a time X peak THC blood level interaction (B = 0.003, SE = 0.001, p = 0.037). As THC blood levels increased, inflammation increased more after acute use. There was a THC X CBD X time interaction on IL-10 (B=-0.001, SE = 0.000, p < 0.001). Higher than average peak blood THC was related to increasing IL-10 over time while lower than average peak THC was related to decreasing IL-10 over time but only at average or lower peak CBD levels. Cannabis use decreased MCP-1 acutely (effect for time: B=-48.757, SE = 15.061, p = 0.002), while there was no effect on the Matsuda Index. Acute cannabis use is associated with increases in both pro- and anti-inflammatory circulating cytokines, but not with insulin sensitivity. Introduction

State-level legalization of cannabis is expanding1 and the rescheduling of cannabis from a Schedule I to a Schedule III drug is in process2. In concert, cannabis use is increasing and daily use is more common than daily use of alcohol in the United States3. Increases in cannabis use raise questions concerning public health impacts. Cannabis is composed of hundreds of cannabinoids, terpenes, and flavonoids, of which Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD) are the two most abundant cannabinoids4. Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the principal psychoactive constituent of cannabis, produces the intoxicating effects (“high”) associated with cannabis use and stimulates appetite primarily through activation of cannabinoid receptor type 1 (CB1). The endocannabinoid system, particularly CB1 receptors and their endogenous ligands, plays a central role in regulating energy homeostasis in mammals5. Cannabidiol (CBD) is the second major cannabinoid, is non-intoxicating and has relatively low affinity for CB1 and CB2 receptors, but can modulate endocannabinoid signaling through multiple mechanisms, including negative allosteric modulation of CB16,7. Because THC and CBD differ in their pharmacological actions, they may exert distinct, or even opposing effects on physiological, cognitive, and metabolic processes relevant to public health7–14. This divergence is particularly evident in studies of metabolism, where cannabis presents a paradox; one which may partially be explained by the differing actions of THC and CBD.

THC is linked with increased appetite15 and acute over-eating16, leading to concerns about increases in obesity-related disease including type 2 diabetes17. Yet epidemiological and some preclinical data suggest metabolic benefits of cannabis including lower body mass index (BMI)18–20, obesity prevalence20, type 2 diabetes prevalence, fasting insulin, and insulin resistance21,22.

The complex interplay between THC, CBD, and the major cannabinoid receptors CB1 and CB2, has made the search for mechanisms to clarify the metabolic effects of cannabis challenging23. One proposed mechanism is modulation of inflammation. Cannabinoids have peripheral and central effects on immune system function and inflammation24. Although both THC and CBD exert inhibitory effects on the production of inflammatory cytokines (including TNFα, IL-2, IL-6, IL-12, IL-1b24) their activities involve distinct intracellular pathways that also involve non-CB1 or CB2 mechanisms25. CBD may also interact with or alter the effects of THC15, suggesting that THC and CBD may synergistically produce different anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects26. Furthermore, CBD may alter the pharmacological effects of THC15,

The association between inflammation and insulin resistance is well recognized27–29, hence decreasing inflammation – including through cannabis use - may decrease insulin resistance and risk for type 2 diabetes30. Confirmation of this hypothesis has been elusive31 but prior studies are limited by small sample sizes32–34, focusing on only one cannabinoid32,33, and/or studying an already insulin resistant population35.

The goal of the current study was to examine the acute effects of cannabinoids found in legal market cannabis flower products on inflammatory markers and insulin sensitivity among healthy individuals who are not regular users of cannabis. Participants were quasi-randomly assigned36 to a one-time use of a cannabis flower product that was either THC dominant, CBD dominant, or contained both THC and CBD. Our global hypothesis was that the effects of cannabis use on inflammation and insulin sensitivity would vary as a function CBD and THC exposure, and that inflammatory changes would be associated with insulin sensitivity.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from the Boulder/Denver, CO USA area. Data collection occurred between November 2019 and September 2024. Participants were eligible if they were age 21–40 (due to effects of age on inflammation and insulin resistance37,38 had no weight fluctuations above > 12 pounds in the last 6 months, and had smoked/vaped cannabis at least once with no negative effects (e.g., paranoia, hallucinations) but had not used cannabis in the last 3 months. Exclusion criteria included currently using anti-inflammatory, immunosuppressive, or glucose lowering medications, current or past history of psychotic or bipolar disorder; acute illness (e.g., influenza) or chronic disease (e.g., HIV, diabetes); daily tobacco use, heavy drinking (i.e., indicated by scores over 8 on the Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Task39, or illicit drug use; fasting blood glucose outside of 55–126 mg/dL at baseline; donating blood in the past 8 weeks or intention to donate in next 8 weeks; a carbohydrate restricted diet; or pregnancy, nursing, or trying to become pregnant.

Procedures

The study was approved by the University of Colorado Boulder Institutional Review Board (Protocol #19 − 005) and was pre-registered at ClinicalTrials.Gov (NCT04114903, https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04114903). Participants completed a baseline appointment on campus followed by a second appointment that began in our mobile pharmacology laboratory40 which was driven to the participant’s residence and then, following cannabis use in the participants’ residence, transported the participant to our laboratory on campus for the remainder of the appointment. Prior to both appointments, participants were asked to not use cannabis or alcohol for 24 h and not to consume food or beverage besides water for 12 h. At baseline, participants completed informed consent and a urine toxicology screen and pregnancy test. An indwelling IV catheter was placed for blood samples to quantify inflammatory biomarkers, fasting glucose levels, plasma cannabinoids, and as the first assessment for the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT, see Measures). The OGTT lasted 120 min during which participants completed self-report measures. Participants were then quasi-randomly assigned to purchase one of three cannabis flower products from a collaborating dispensary 1 [The dispensary that we work with performed cannabinoid and terpene potency testing on each batch produced and set aside a specific lot of the strains corresponding to each of the three groups to ensure individuals used the product corresponding to the group they agreed to be a part of.] that was THC-dominant (23% THC, 0% CBD), THC + CBD (10% THC, 8% CBD), or CBD-dominant (20% CBD, 1% THC) but not to use it until the mobile laboratory appointment. Products were selected to match those available on the legal market35. Quasi-randomization refers to the use of random assigned to condition where participants can accept or decline this assignment36, and was utilized given federal and IRB regulations precluding true random assignment. Participants rolled a die, were told which group they were assigned to, and could then accept that assignment or choose another. Most (83%; N = 95) accepted their assignment, and there were no differences between acceptors versus those who changed group36. Researchers were blind to condition. Participants were compensated $50 and scheduled for the mobile laboratory appointment one week later. They were instructed to maintain the same diet and physical activity before their mobile laboratory appointment as before the baseline as diet and physical activity can influence insulin sensitivity41–43.

The mobile lab arrived at the participant’s home for the second appointment and pre-use blood was drawn via IV catheter for the quantification of cannabinoids, inflammatory biomarkers, and fasting glucose. Participants were asked to record the time that they began using their product ad libitum in their home and then to return to the mobile laboratory when they were finished. Blood was drawn immediately after they returned to the mobile laboratory. Our target was for blood to be drawn approximately 20 min after cannabis use began. In practice, the elapsed time between the recorded start of cannabis use and the post-use blood draw averaged 12.35 min (SD = 6.43; range = 2–30 min). This variability reflects the naturalistic design of the study while remaining well within the window during which cannabinoids are readily detectable following inhalation. Pharmacokinetic studies have demonstrated that cannabinoids are detectable in plasma within seconds after inhalation, with peak plasma concentrations typically occurring within 3–10 minutes44,45. The participant was then driven in the mobile laboratory to our clinical laboratory space with the reclining infusion chair. Two hours post-use, participants completed a third blood draw as well as the OGTT. Participants were compensated $150 and a rideshare was called to return them home. Measures

Demographics. Self-reported demographic information (e.g., age, race) was collected.

Alcohol Use. Alcohol use was measured via an online 30-day Timeline Followback (O-TLFB46. Number of days of alcohol use and number of drinks per day were multiplied to create a quantity/frequency variable.

Sleep. Sleep quality was measured with the item “During the past month, how would you rate your sleeping quality overall?” from the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index47.

Body Mass Index. BMI was calculated using height and weight measured at baseline.

Alcohol use, sleep, and BMI were included as covariates in all analyses due to their known associations with inflammation48–50 and insulin resistance50–52.

24-Hour Energy Balance. Energy balance was calculated by subtracting the prior 24-hour energy expenditure in kcal (from the Stanford 7-day Physical Activity Recall53 from the prior 24-hour energy intake in kcal (from the Nutrition Data System for Research54–56. There were no significant changes in physical activity, dietary intake, or overall energy balance between the baseline and acute sessions.

Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT). Blood samples for glucose and insulin were collected before and 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after participants consumed a standardized glucose (75 g) beverage.

Cannabinoids, inflammatory biomarkers, and insulin sensitivity. A panel of pro-inflammatory biomarkers (including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-12, IFNG), anti-inflammatory biomarker IL-10, and chemokine (MCP-1) concentrations were measured using the Quanterix Simoa™ CorPlex™ Cytokine Panel (Item 85–0329) and the Quanterix Chemokine Panel (Item 88–5002), respectively, and assayed via ELISA plate. All samples and standards were run in duplicate according to standard procedures35.

For cannabinoid processing, samples were centrifuged at 1000xg for 10 min to separate erythrocytes from plasma. Final materials were transferred to a fresh microcentrifuge tube and stored at −80 °C until analysis using validated high-performance liquid chromatography/mass-spectroscopy (HPLC-MS/MS) (API550034)57.

Insulin sensitivity was assayed using plasma glucose and insulin concentration levels across OGTT timepoints. Glucose and insulin assays were conducted using standard procedures35. Insulin sensitivity58 was computed using the Matsuda Index.

Power and statistical analyses

Sample size was selected to permit analysis at two-tailed alpha of 0.05 and power level of 0.95. Power analysis followed Cohen59 and was conducted in G*Power60. To test the effect of user group on changes in outcomes and assuming a moderate effect size of f=0.40 we required 100 participants. All analyses were conducted in R61,62 using mixed-effects models. For each model, time (i.e., pre-use and immediate post-use for all models, with 2 h post-use for inflammation and chemokine variables versus 4 h post use for the Matsuda Index; see Supplementary Fig. 1), peak blood levels of THC and CBD (e.g., from immediate post-use), and all interactions (e.g., time x THC, time x CBD, THC x CBD, time x THC x CBD) were entered as predictors. Models were estimated using the nlme package in R63. Peak blood levels were centered prior to computation of interaction terms. All models included random intercepts and slopes per person and used maximum likelihood estimation to account for missingness (see Supplemental Fig. 2 for CONSORT). Where models resulted in significant interactions, directional effects were probed using the emmeans function64 and graphed using ggplot265.

Pro-inflammatory cytokine composite change over time based on low (1 SD below mean), average (mean), and high (1 SD above mean) peak blood THC levels including simple effects of time by peak THC blood level.

Changes over time (i.e., baseline, pre-use, immediate post-use, and 2 h post-use) in blood cannabinoids and their metabolites (e.g., 7-OH-CBD and 7-COOH-CBD as well as 11-OH-THC and THC-COOH, respectively) were compared across conditions to ensure protocol adherence. Blood levels below the limit of quantification were recoded as zero, and all values above or below 3 SDs of the mean were Winsorized. The Matsuda Index was natural log transformed66. Six individuals were removed from analyses for blood levels indicating cannabis use prior to the mobile lab visit; final n = 108.

There were differences over time across groups in blood THC and metabolite levels (time x group interaction F(6,281) = 5.33, p < 0.001). Post hoc analyses showed no THC at baseline or pre-use for any groups, but immediately (approximately 20 min) after use, levels increased for both the THC and THC + CBD groups and then decreased at 2 h post-use with no changes in the CBD group (see Fig. 1A). Similarly, there was a time x group interaction for CBD and metabolite levels (F(6,257) = 30.22, p < 0.001) with post-hoc tests demonstrating no CBD at baseline or pre-use for any group but immediate post-use increases for the CBD group and then decreases at 2 h post-use and no changes for the THC or THC + CBD groups (see Fig. 1B). Findings verify that participants used the product type to which they were assigned. That said, there was substantial heterogeneity in actual cannabinoid levels within group; for example, multiple participants in the THC + CBD group actually had higher THC and metabolite concentrations than those in the THC group. Thus, we utilized blood concentration of cannabinoids in analyses as it reflected a more exact assessment of cannabinoid exposure and a more rigorous test of the effect of cannabinoid exposure on the biomarkers we assessed.

Primary analyses

Effects of cannabis on panel of pro-inflammatory cytokines

Mixed effect models examining the effects of peak blood cannabinoid levels on the inflammation composite variable controlling for BMI, sleep, and alcohol use, indicated a main effect of BMI (B = 0.127, Standard Error (SE) = 0.055, p = 0.025) where higher BMIs were associated with more inflammation. There was also a main effect of time (B = 0.205, SE = 0.037, p < 0.001), qualified by an interaction between time and peak THC blood levels (B = 0.003, SE = 0.001, p = 0.037) indicating that as THC increased, inflammation increased more over time (see Fig. 2). All other main effects and interactions, including those involving CBD, were non- significant (ps > 0.24).

Effects of cannabis on anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10

Mixed effect models examining peak blood cannabinoid levels on IL-10 controlling for BMI, sleep, and alcohol use, indicated a main effect of peak THC blood levels (B=−0.023, SE = 0.003, p < 0.001) as well as an interaction between peak THC blood levels and peak CBD blood levels (B = 0.001, SE = 0.000, p = 0.003). There was also an interaction between peak THC blood levels and time (B = 0.017, SE = 0.003, p < 0.001), and these effects were qualified by a three-way interaction between peak THC blood levels, time, and peak CBD blood levels (B=−0.001, SE = 0.000, p < 0.001). Post hoc tests revealed that higher than average peak THC was related to increasing IL-10 over time while lower than average peak THC was related to decreasing IL-10 over time but only at average or lower peak CBD levels. At high CBD levels, IL-10 levels did not change over time regardless of peak THC levels (see Fig. 3).

Effects of cannabis on MCP-1

Mixed effect models examining peak blood cannabinoid levels on MCP-1 controlling for BMI, sleep, and alcohol use, indicated only a main effect of time (B=−48.757, SE = 15.061, p = 0.002) such that MCP-1 decreased for all groups over time from Mpre−use=1067.2 (SD = 788.5) to Mpost−use= 971.8 (SD = 800.8).

Effects of cannabis on insulin sensitivity

Mixed effect models indicated only a main effect of BMI (B=−0.069, SE = 0.013, p < 0.001). Higher BMI was associated with lower Matsuda Index. There was no change in the Matsuda Index after acute use of cannabis (M = 2.5, SD = 0.6) compared to baseline (M = 2.4, SD = 0.6).

Discussion

The main hypothesis was that the effects of cannabis use on inflammation and insulin sensitivity would vary as a function of CBD and THC exposure, and that inflammatory changes would be associated with insulin sensitivity. Findings showed increases in pro-inflammatory cytokines after consumption of cannabis containing high levels of THC. The anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 also increased after use of higher amounts of THC, but only when CBD levels were low or average. At high levels of CBD the effect of THC on IL-10 appears blunted. Chemokine MCP-1 showed decreases after acute use of cannabis regardless of cannabinoid concentration. There was no effect of acute use of cannabis on insulin sensitivity as measured by the Matsuda Index, nor were there consistent relationships between insulin sensitivity and circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines or chemokine MCP-1. There was a significant positive association between IL-10 and the Matsuda Index, suggesting some association between circulating inflammatory biomarkers and insulin sensitivity, though there is conflicting evidence about the existence and direction of this association67,68. On balance, the data indicate that effects on insulin sensitivity, either directly or via effects on pro-inflammatory cytokines, do not seem a likely explanation for the epidemiological link of cannabis to decreased risk for type 2 diabetes, replicating published evidence31.

Acute versus chronic inflammation

Prior work35,69 has shown that adults who use cannabis regularly have lower levels of circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines. In contrast, these same cytokines increased in response to acute cannabis use in the current study. The pattern of effects of cannabis on MCP-1 was also reversed in acute versus chronic use35. Finally, anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was increased in response to acute cannabis use (so long as CBD levels were not high) but showed no difference chronically in those using cannabis regularly35. Taken together, these findings are reminiscent of the effects of exercise. Exercise is well-known to be acutely inflammatory70,71, but over time consistent exercise results in lower levels of circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6, CRP, and TNF-α70–72. Molecular work has shown that these transient exercise-related increases in inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6) trigger a counteracting release of anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1ra) that over time/repeated bouts of exercise creates a “circulating anti-inflammatory environment”72. Similar contrasting effects of cannabis use on acute versus chronic inflammation suggest the intriguing possibility that the mechanisms of effects of cannabis on circulating inflammatory biomarkers may be similar to those shown for exercise.

Acute effects of inhaled smoke

A potential alternative explanation of our effects is that inhaling smoke (of any kind) causes an inflammatory response. There is ample evidence that chronic cigarette use is associated with elevated circulating inflammatory cytokines and related biomarkers73,74. There is comparatively less evidence of acute effects of smoking on inflammation, and almost none on individuals who are not already chronic smokers, but the few studies that have been done show some acute inflammatory effects75,76. Thus, part of the explanation for our findings may be exposure to cannabis smoke. That said, MCP-1 has been shown to increase (rather than decrease as our findings showed) after cigarette smoking in habitual smokers with no increases in IL-677. Clearly additional research is needed with non-combustible forms of cannabis (i.e., edibles) to disentangle effects of cannabinoids versus inhaling smoke.

Obesity, cannabis, and lower risk for type 2 diabetes

If insulin function is not the pathway through which cannabis use influences risk for type 2 diabetes, it is possible that its effects on adiposity may be a more likely mediator. Persistent cannabis use is associated with lower likelihood of obesity and, longitudinally, a lower likelihood of transitioning to obesity and less weight gained over three years78. The combination of lower chronic systemic inflammation with or as the result of lower weight may better explain the relationship of cannabis to lower type 2 diabetes risk. Future studies should be conducted with samples who are less active and with higher BMI to better understand these mechanisms.

Limitations

Findings should be considered in light of study limitations. The sample included both smokers and vapers, who may have different inflammatory profiles or responses; future trials should recruit separate samples to test these effects. While our design maximizes external validity, the use of quasi-random instead of true random assignment as well as the heterogeneity in ad libitum dosing are limitations for causal inference.

Conclusions

Acute cannabis use is associated with increases in both pro- and anti-inflammatory circulating cytokines, but not with changes in insulin sensitivity. The complex relationship between cannabis use and systemic inflammation seems to depend on whether the use is acute or chronic. Additional research on the possible mediating mechanisms involved in the connection between cannabis use and type 2 diabetes will require variability in both the types of products and populations studied.
