Development and Validation of a Machine Learning Model for Early Detection of Untreated Infection
Authors: Kevin G. Buell, Kyle A. Carey, Nicole Dussault, William F. Parker, Jay Dumanian, Sivasubramanium V. Bhavani, Emily R. Gilbert, Christopher J. Winslow, Nirav S. Shah, Majid Afshar, Dana P. Edelson, Matthew M. Churpek
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Early diagnostic uncertainty for infection causes delays in antibiotic administration in infected patients and unnecessary antibiotic administration in noninfected patients.OBJECTIVE:To develop a machine learning model for the early detection of untreated infection (eDENTIFI), with the presence of infection determined by clinician chart review.DERIVATION COHORT:Three thousand three hundred fifty-seven adult patients hospitalized between 2006 and 2018 at two health systems in Illinois, United States.VALIDATION COHORT:We validated in 1632 patients in a third Illinois health system using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).PREDICTION MODEL:Using a longitudinal discrete-time format, we trained a gradient boosted machine model to predict untreated infection in the next 6 hours using routinely available patient demographics, vital signs, and laboratory results.RESULTS:eDENTIFI had an AUC of 0.80 (95% CI, 0.79–0.81) in the validation cohort and outperformed the systemic inflammatory response syndrome criteria with an AUC of 0.64 (95% CI, 0.64–0.65; p < 0.001). The most important features were body mass index, age, temperature, and heart rate. Using a threshold with a 47.6% sensitivity, eDENTIFI detected infection a median 2.0 hours (interquartile range, 0.9–5.2 hr) before antimicrobial administration, with a negative predictive value of 93.6%. Antibiotic administration guided by eDENTIFI could have decreased unnecessary IV antibiotic administration in noninfected patients by 10.8% absolute or 46.4% relative percentage points compared with clinicians.CONCLUSION:eDENTIFI could both decrease the time to antimicrobial administration in infected patients and unnecessary antibiotic administration in noninfected patients. Further prospective validation is needed.

KEY POINTS

Question: Does the use of definitions of infection determined by clinicians performing manual chart review facilitate the development of a machine learning model that detects infection before antimicrobial administration?

Findings: The model detected infection a median 2 hours before antimicrobial administration and outperformed the systemic inflammatory response syndrome criteria. It identified patients at low-risk of infection and could have approximately halved unnecessary antibiotic administration in noninfected patients.

Meaning: Model implementation may facilitate early clinician assessment of the likelihood for infectious vs. noninfectious causes of acute illness, enable earlier antimicrobial administration, and reduce unnecessary antibiotic administration.

The mortality of sepsis, a life-threatening organ dysfunction characterized by a dysregulated host response to infection, increases with every hour of delayed antibiotic administration (1–3). However, nondiscriminate use of antibiotics is unsustainable due to rising antimicrobial resistance (4). As infectious and noninfectious disease processes present similarly, the potential benefit of early antibiotics in infected patients must be weighed against the potential for harm in noninfected patients (5). The uncertainty around this trade-off currently delays early antibiotic administration in over 50% of infected patients (1, 2, 6, 7) and harms 20% of noninfected patients via antibiotic-associated drug events and antibiotic resistance (8–10). Therefore, there is an urgent need to facilitate early clinician assessment of the likelihood for infectious vs. noninfectious causes of acute illness (11).

Development of clinical decision support systems (CDSSs) for the early identification of infection has been limited by challenges in identifying infected patients using the electronic healthcare record (EHR). The quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) and systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) criteria perform poorly when repurposed for this task (12–16). Compared with definitions of infection determined by clinicians performing manual chart review, EHR definitions have variable accuracy, time of infection onset, and associated patient outcomes (17–19). Therefore, in this multicenter study at six hospitals from three health systems, we developed and externally validated a machine learning model for the early detection of untreated infection (eDENTIFI) using definitions of infection determined by clinicians performing manual chart review.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient Population

The study used data from a previously published multicenter retrospective electronic chart review performed by clinicians of adult patients hospitalized between 2006 and 2018 at the University of Chicago Medical Center, NorthShore University HealthSystem, and Loyola University Medical Center in Illinois, United States (17). In brief, if patients met one or more eligibility screening criteria for potential infection of: 1) at least one antibiotic order, 2) a blood culture order, and/or 3) Angus billing codes (20) for infection and death, they were eligible for chart review. Clinician reviewers used the entire medical record to clinically assess the likelihood of infection and determine the time of infection onset. The likelihood of infection was rated as “proven” (clinical concern with positive culture), “probable” (clinical concern without positive culture), “possible” (equal clinical concern for infectious and noninfectious cause), “unlikely” (temporary clinical concern disproven by a noninfectious cause), or “not infected” (no clinical concern) (for full instructions provided to clinician chart reviewers, see Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418). Inter-observer reviewer assessed by weighted kappa was high and assessed at each medical center (17). A proportional number of patients that did not meet any eligibility criteria were randomly selected and assumed to not be infected.

Data Collection and Descriptive Statistics

The study (“Sepsis early prediction and subphenotype illumination study”) was approved by the University of Wisconsin Institutional Review Board (IRB 2019-1124) on October 29, 2019 by waiver of informed consent due to minimal risk of harm and general impracticality. The study procedures followed the ethical standards of the responsible institutional committee on human experimentation and the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. Patient demographics, vital signs, laboratory results, medication administration, and nurse documentation were obtained from each hospital’s EHR and compared between infected and noninfected patients using Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Pearson chi-square test. A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Outcome

Infection was defined by a “proven,” “probable,” or “possible” likelihood of infection as determined by clinician manual chart review. Infection was categorized as “present at admission” if infection occurred before or within the first 24 hours from admission, and the infection onset was set as the time of first vital signs. Conversely, infection was categorized as “not present at admission” if infection was absent during the first 24 hours, and infection onset was determined by clinician manual chart review. The duration of untreated infection was defined as the time difference between first vital signs and antimicrobial administration for infection present at admission and the time difference between infection onset determined by manual chart review and antimicrobial administration for infection not present at admission. Each observation time with new data for one of the model predictor variables was labeled with an outcome of whether infection occurred within the next 6 hours from that time point. A positive infection outcome remained positive at each time point until patients were censored due to antimicrobial administration or after 48 hours of untreated infection (given that some infections will never receive antimicrobial therapy) (eFig. 1, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418). Noninfected patients who received an antimicrobial were temporarily censored for 48 hours to train the model on patient data where infection was temporarily suspected but later excluded. A 48-hour window was chosen to account for the intermittent dosing of antimicrobials in patients with end-stage renal disease. An antimicrobial included any nonprophylactic antibiotic, antiviral or antifungal and excluded anti-retroviral medications for HIV, antibiotics given prophylactically 6 hours before or after surgery, and prophylactic oral acyclovir, valacyclovir, fluconazole, and rifampin. Patients with chronic infections that were not active, new, worsening, or the cause of the patient’s hospitalization were not considered infected during the chart review. The analysis only included first episodes of infection.

Model Development

Model derivation used data from the University of Chicago and NorthShore University HealthSystem and external validation used data from Loyola University Medical Center. Derivation data was structured using a discrete-time analysis framework to facilitate fitting a binary classifier onto a longitudinal dataset (21–23). This method separates time into discrete, nonoverlapping intervals where newer data and predictions continuously replace older data and predictions at the end of every time interval.

eDENTIFI is a Gradient Boosting Machine algorithm, a tree-based nonparametric model that builds a collection of decision trees where each tree attempts to improve the predictions from the previous tree with regularization to reduce overfitting (24). Five-fold cross validation was used for hyperparameter tuning to maximize the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) in the derivation cohort. The model was developed in accordance with the transparent reporting of a multivariable prediction model for individual prognosis or diagnosis recommendations (eTable 1, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418).

Predictor variables were age, body mass index, hospital location, vital signs (temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, peripheral oxygen saturation, and alert verbal pain unresponsive scale), laboratory results (components of complete blood count, complete metabolic panel, coagulation panel, blood gas, and lactate), Braden score for pressure ulcer, urine output, and presence of foley catheter. The maximal value, minimal value, mean, slope of change, and sd over the last 24 hours for vital signs and the change from the most recent laboratory value were included, as predictor variable trends have been shown to improve model performance (25). eTable 2 (http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418) describes all predictor variables. Nonphysiologic vital signs and laboratory results were replaced as missing (eTable 3, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418). No predictor imputation was performed for missing data as the model inherently learns from the frequency of available data. To align with clinical workflow, all predictor variables for eDENTIFI were made available to the model at the time they were released in the EHR.

Model Validation

Predicted probabilities were calculated at each time step in the continuous data (without discrete-time formatting) of the validation cohort. Model discrimination between infected and noninfected patients in the next 6 hours was assessed by calculating AUC and 95% CIs using the method by DeLong et al (26). It was compared with the SIRS criteria using the total number of criteria met because the SIRS criteria have been shown to outperform qSOFA in identifying infection (14, 27). Model calibration was accessed using a logistic calibration curve comparing the predicted risk and observed rate of infection. Variable importance was calculated using the mean decrease in impurity and illustrated by plotting the relative importance of predictor variables on a normalized scale between 0 and 100. Partial dependence plots illustrated the relationship between the continuous variable and predicted risk of untreated infection.

Model Performance

As nonantimicrobial interventions, such as IV fluids, can temporarily normalize infected patients’ vital signs and fluctuate model predictions, implementation of eDENTIFI as a CDSS would continuously update patients’ predicted probabilities but only display the maximal prior probability. Therefore, model performance was calculated at different threshold cutoffs in patients’ maximal predicted probability of untreated infection in the next 6 hours. Once patients surpass the threshold, the CDSS would alert for infection and prompt physician bedside assessment. Therefore, the duration of untreated infection identified was calculated as the time between infected patient’s predicted probability surpassing the threshold cutoff and antimicrobial administration. The observed infection and IV antimicrobial administration rates were compared across ten equally sized groups of patients created from patients’ maximal predicted probability of untreated infection. The proportion of noninfected patients that received one or more IV antibiotic was compared with the number of noninfected patients whose maximal predicted probability of infection surpassed the threshold cutoff (e.g., false positive alarm for infection in a CDSS). To select a clinically useful threshold, an efficiency curve compared model sensitivity against the proportion of all patients whose maximal probability crossed varying threshold cutoffs.

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analysis

Model discrimination was assessed across different types of infections, hospital locations, and pathogens by recalculating the AUC for patients belonging to each subgroup chosen a priori. A time-dependent ROC curve was constructed by discretizing the validation cohort into nonoverlapping time intervals and recalculating the AUC using patient’s maximal predicted probability of untreated infection within each time interval (28). Two sensitivity analyses were performed defining patients with infection as: 1) “proven” or “probable” and 2) “proven” likelihood of infection by chart review. A third sensitivity analysis used time-varying predictors only. A fourth sensitivity analysis evaluated model performance over time. All analyses were performed using R, Version 3.6.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Patient Population

The final cohort contained 4989 patient encounters (eFig. 2, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418). Patients in the derivation (n = 3357 patient encounters) and validation (n = 1632 patient encounters) cohort differed in their age (62 vs. 60 yr; p < 0.001) and prevalence of chronic pulmonary disease, heart failure, renal failure, and liver disease (p < 0.05 for all comparisons; eTable 4, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418). Patient demographics are presented by health system in eTable 5 (http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418).

Infection occurred in 1317 of 4989 (26.4%) patient encounters. Infected patients were older and had higher prevalence of chronic comorbidities compared with noninfected patients (p < 0.05 for all comparisons; Table 1). Before antimicrobial administration, patients with untreated infection exhibited more deranged vital signs and laboratory results, with the exception of lactate, compared with noninfected patients (p < 0.01 for all comparisons; Table 1). Maximal lactate levels were comparable between the two groups (2.3 vs. 2.1 mmol/L; p = 0.52). Infected patients had higher mortality than patients without infection (5.8% vs. 1.1%, p < 0.001). The median duration of untreated infection was 3.6 hours (interquartile range [IQR], 1.4–9.2 hr) (Table 2; and eFig. 3, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418).

Model Validation

eDENTIFI had an AUC of 0.80 (95% CI, 0.79–0.81) for predicting untreated infection within the next 6 hours in the validation cohort. The hyperparameters for the final model are shown in eTable 6 (http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418). eDENTIFI outperformed the SIRS criteria with an AUC of 0.64 (95% CI, 0.64–0.65) (p < 0.001) (eFig. 4, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418). As shown in the logistic calibration curve, the model underestimated the risk of infection at lower predicted probabilities of infection and overestimated it at higher predicted probabilities (eFig. 5, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418).

The most important predictor variables were body mass index, age, mean temperature in the last 24 hours, temperature, and heart rate (Fig. 1). Patients at the extremes of body mass index and age and patients with higher values of temperature and heart rate had a greater predicted risk of untreated infection in the next 6 hours (eFig. 6, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418).

Variable importance plot for early detection of untreated infection (eDENTIFI). This figure displays the ten most important predictor variables for eDENTIFI. Variable importance is calculated using the mean decrease in impurity and displayed on an adjusted scale of 0–100.

Model Performance

Model performance of eDENTIFI (Table 3) and the SIRS criteria (eTable 7, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418) varied across threshold cutoffs in patients’ maximal probability of untreated infection in the next 6 hours. At a threshold with a 47.6% sensitivity, eDENTIFI detected infection a median 2.0 hours (IQR, 0.9–5.2 hr) before antimicrobial administration and flagged 20.5% of all patients as infected with a negative predicted value of 93.6% and positive predictive value of 17.8%. A total of 289 (23.3%) noninfected patients with a maximal predicted risk of infection under the threshold received an IV antibiotic. In comparison, 155 (12.5%) noninfected patients surpassed the threshold (e.g., false infection alert), representing a potential 10.8% absolute difference or 46.4% relative risk reduction for antibiotic administration in noninfected patients guided by eDENTIFI compared with clinicians (eTable 8, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418). Patients in eDENTIFI’s lowest risk decile had an infection rate of 1.8% but 40.2% received an IV antibiotic, with a median 2.5 doses (IQR, 2–3.25 doses) given to noninfected patients (Fig. 2). At a given sensitivity, fewer patients crossed the alert threshold for eDENTIFI than the SIRS criteria, resulting in fewer false positive alarms for eDENTIFI (eFig. 7, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418).

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analysis

eDENTIFI had an AUC of 0.87 (95% CI, 0.86–0.88) for predicting infection present at admission, which represented 89.2% of all infections, compared with an AUC of 0.64 (95% CI, 0.62–0.66) for infections not present at admission, which represented 10.8% of all infections. Model performance was comparable between the emergency department, hospital ward, and ICU and between patients with Gram-negative, Gram-positive, viral, and drug-resistant infections (Table 2). A time-dependent ROC curve demonstrated stable model performance in the first 96 hours of patients’ hospital stay (eFig. 8, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418). In two sensitivity analyses, model performance with infection defined as: 1) “proven” or “probable” and 2) “proven” likelihood of infection by clinician chart review was comparable to the primary analysis (eTable 9, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B418). In a third sensitivity analysis, model performance using only time-varying predictors was unchanged to the primary analysis. In the final sensitivity analysis, model performance slightly increased over time with an AUC of 0.78 (95% CI, 0.76–0.80) between 2006 and 2010, AUC of 0.80 (95% CI, 0.79–0.81) between 2010 and 2014, and AUC of 0.81 (95% CI, 0.80–0.82) between 2014 and 2018.

DISCUSSION

In this multicenter study at six hospitals in three health systems, we used definitions of infection determined by clinician manual chart review to develop and validate a machine learning model—eDENTIFI—that continuously detects untreated infection using routinely collected EHR data. eDENTIFI detected infection a median 2 hours before antimicrobial administration, identified patients at low-risk of infection, and could have approximately halved unnecessary antibiotic administration in noninfected patients. Model implementation in real-time may therefore result in earlier antimicrobial administration in infected patients and reduce unnecessary antibiotic administration in noninfected patients.

The Surviving Sepsis Campaign recommends rapid assessment of the likelihood of infectious vs. noninfectious causes of acute illness for patients to prevent the indiscriminate use of antimicrobials (11). Multiple models predict the complications of critical illness (e.g., end-organ damage, sepsis, or septic shock) in potentially infected patients (3, 29–32), but few models discriminate between infected and noninfected patients. The Infection Prediction Score was the first to model the risk of infection over twenty years ago at a single center using the 1988 Centers for Disease Control definitions of infection (33). Although accurate for critically ill adults, model performance decreased to an AUC of 0.51 in hospital wards (34). The Infections score, a simple scoring system to predict bacterial infections in emergency departments of low- or middle-income countries, has not yet been externally validated (35). Finally, Feng et al (36) used a machine learning approach to predict nosocomial infections across three healthcare systems. International Classification of Diseases, 9th Edition codes and microbiology culture test orders were used to define infection and their best performing model had an AUC of 0.88 one hour before clinical suspicion of infection. Given the aforementioned limitations and the lack of a validated model currently used in clinical practice, the performance of eDENTIFI was compared with the SIRS criteria. Although the SIRS criteria was not originally designed to identify infection, it is sometimes used clinically for this purpose and has been shown to outperform qSOFA in identifying infection (14, 27).

In contrast to these prior studies utilizing administrative billing data or clinician orders from the EHR, which either lack timing data or are imprecise, definitions of infection determined by clinician manual chart review likely represents the closest approximation to the ground truth infection status and the initial time of when the first signs/symptoms presented (17, 37, 38). This is important because different definitions of infection will affect model performance and clinical utility (19, 39). The low lactate levels in untreated infected patients support that our definition captures the onset of infection sufficiently early that it preceded tissue hypoperfusion.

We designed eDENTIFI for implementation as a hospital-wide CDSS, which increased the difficulty of the modeling task in several ways. First, our model continuously predicts the risk of all types of infection (drug-resistant and nondrug-resistant bacterial infections requiring antibiotics, fungal infections requiring antifungals, and viral infections requiring antivirals) across all areas of the hospitals using routinely available clinical data. Predicting one type of infection, one site of infection, in one hospital location, or including biomarkers not routinely used in clinical practice would have each likely increased model performance. However, as approximately 90% of all infection was present at admission and AUC was 0.87 for these patients, the CDSS may be most impactful for patients with community-acquired infection. Second, eDENTIFI predicts infection at the earliest possible time point (first vital signs) for patients with infection present at admission and 6 hours before infection onset for patients with infection not present at admission. Models with a prediction window closer to sepsis will outperform models predicting early infection (30). Third, eDENTIFI is limited to predictor data at the time they are made available in the EHR and data after antimicrobial administration is censored. This construct likely decreases model performance as was observed in an analysis of the Epic Sepsis Model, where model performance decreased from an AUC of 0.87 to 0.47 when limited to predictions that preceded the treatment of sepsis (40). However, these modeling choices all increase the clinical utility of eDENTIFI as a potential CDSS because they facilitate clinicians’ evaluation of the likelihood for infection before antimicrobial administration. Finally, we chose to use the antimicrobial administration time as the target of our model because we have previously found that delays from order time to delivery is associated with increased mortality (1). By continuing to alert on patients who have not yet received antimicrobials, our model could be used across the entire clinical workflow from recognition of infection to administration.

Our study has several limitations. First, although eDENTIFI was developed at three health systems, the study population was limited to hospitals in Illinois (United States), which may limit external generalizability. Similarly, eDENTIFI was developed using data from hospitalized patients, and further validation is required for patients in the emergency department who do not require hospitalization. Second, machine learning algorithms include complex interactions between multiple predictors, which can be challenging to interpret. However, we provided a complete description of predictor variables, variable importance plot, and partial dependence plots to increase model transparency and interpretation. Third, lack of a true gold standard for infection may potentially reduce the accuracy in our definition (5). In patients with infection not present at admission, variability in the reviewers’ determination of infection onset could have reduced model performance in this subgroup. However, high inter-observer reviewer agreement was observed during manual chart review, and we performed sensitivity analyses altering the certainty in the likelihood of infection by clinician chart review to define infection (17). Finally, the risk threshold at which eDENTIFI classifies patients as infected or noninfected will likely vary between hospital sites with different antibiotic stewardship priorities, availability of rapid diagnostic testing, and rates of infection. Further validation is likely needed at each hospital site before implementation.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, we developed a prediction model that accurately discriminates between patients with early untreated infection and noninfected patients in the emergency department, hospital wards, and ICU. Future real-time implementation of eDENTIFI could facilitate earlier antimicrobial administration in infected patients and reduce unnecessary antibiotic administration in noninfected patients.
