Quantifying interest in and sentiment of online media about greenhouse gas emissions from cattle production in the United States
Authors: Michael L Smith, Jinho Jung, Nicole Olynk Widmar, Danielle J Ufer, Maria Berikou, Jayson Lusk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe public is paying increased attention to the environmental impacts associated with cattle production systems in the US. Thus, cattle producers are increasingly exploring ways to communicate their efforts to reduce those environmental and climate impacts. This study uses online and social media data from 2018 to 2022 to examine public interest in and sentiment about beef and dairy cattle production when discussed within the context of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. Variation in mentions and net sentiment over the reporting period is quantified and analyzed. Over the period of data collection, which was interrupted by the COVID-19 pandemic, we find that consumers became increasingly aware of cattle production’s role in GHG emissions and the relationship between emissions and climate change. We also find that consumers have become increasingly interested in emissions mitigation strategies and sustainable practices employed in the production of beef and dairy products.

INTRODUCTION

The US beef and dairy cattle industries provide a variety of economic and social benefits, including food products that are nutrient dense food products and of high export value. The combined US cattle herds consist of an estimated 98.8 million head of cattle, of which roughly 9.5 million are dairy cows (USDA ERS, 2022). The total value of US beef, veal, and dairy exports in 2022 exceeded $20 billion. Despite the tremendous value of the US cattle industry, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from agricultural production contribute to emergent climate change. According to the EPA, U.S. agriculture emitted 594.7 MMT CO2 Eq. in 2020, accounting for 10% of US total GHG emissions, which include carbon dioxide, nitrous oxides, and methane (USDA ERS, 2020; EPA, 2022). While determining exact emissions profiles from livestock operations can be difficult, beef and dairy cattle operations are especially high emitters (Stanley et al., 2018), and agricultural GHG emissions are now among the public’s top worries (Blenda and Valente, 2009).

Increasingly, consumers are taking an interest in the cultivation methods and ethics of livestock production and report concern about how their food is produced in terms of environmental sustainability (Van Loo et al., 2014; Kuchler et al., 2017). Haller et al. (2019) reported that more than half of consumers (57%) are willing to change their eating habits to help reduce negative environmental impact. Agricultural GHG, in particular, has drawn consumer attention, with developing market opportunities for products labeled with reduced GHG emissions and evidence of consumer willingness to purchase and pay a premium for foods with carbon-related labels in international markets (Shuai et al., 2014; Zhao et al., 2018; Canavari and Coderoni, 2020). While consumers are aware of the term “product carbon footprint,” many retain a substantial misunderstanding of its meaning (Hartikainen et al., 2014), and very few recognize its relationship to GHG emissions or climate change (Rondoni and Grasso, 2021). Though some evidence is emerging to suggest negative consumer attitudes towards and perceptions of beef and dairy production’s climate impacts, it remains unclear what the true landscape of US consumer sentiment looks like.

Before 2018, news media in the US and UK paid little attention to communicating animal agriculture’s role in the creation of GHG emissions (Kristiansen et al., 2021). As the digital economy has grown, however, online and social media (such as blogs and online social networks) have become a more common intersection for consumers, producers, and researchers to discuss the carbon footprint of livestock production. Internet users spend more and more time on social networking and online information exchange, generating a digital footprint through their interactions (Arakerimath and Gupta, 2015; McDermot, 2018). According to Pew Research Center, 69% of Americans reported use of Facebook and 23% reported use of X (Twitter) (Auxier and Anderson, 2021). The widespread use of these digital communication tools creates a unique opportunity to study and understand public opinion. This can be done in a two-fold strategy. First, through online and social media listening, which enables researchers to collect and measure conversations occurring in online media. Second, through sentiment analysis, which is a technique using natural language processing to unlock insights related to the emotion behind posts in online media (Quid, 2024b). This analysis seeks to quantify online and social media interest by measuring the volume and sentiment of online media regarding beef and dairy cattle in the context of carbon footprint, broadly defined. Specifically, we observe online and social media mentions and net sentiment specific to dairy and beef cattle, within the context of carbon footprints and GHG emissions.

This paper explores the volume and sentiment of online media discussion on the greenhouse gas emissions from cattle production in the US in both the beef and dairy industry contexts. We seek to understand the volume and topics of discussion and how the COVID-19 pandemic impacted discussion volume. Further, we aim to explore how public opinion of beef and dairy production evolves over time by analyzing online media post rhetoric. Further, by identifying the geographic origin of posts (where possible) we examine how these conversations and opinions differ in regions with significant beef or dairy production versus regions without. Geographic origins were determined by the Quid platform using the metadata collected by Twitter/X (X Developer Platform, 2025). We hypothesize that there will be noticeable differences in discussion volume and online media sentiment between these regional groups.

ONLINE AND SOCIAL MEDIA DATA ON FOOD AND AGRICULTURE MARKETS

Online and social media has become a part of many people’s daily lives (Auxier and Anderson, 2021). Many researchers and businesses have recognized both the increased consumption of information and creation of media content by Internet users (Felt, 2016). Over the past decade, social media has emerged as one of the most important data sources for quantitative communication research (Sugimoto et al., 2017). Social media platforms generate large amounts of content by users who may have competing points of view (Gunster, 2022). These competing networks play host to varying discourses and topical narratives, such as those pertaining to climate change or another subject (Fine and Love-Nichols, 2023). Discussions and portrayals of some activities such as livestock production can lead to polarization of the public on controversial subjects (Olausson, 2018). But social media is not purely an echo chamber; as discourses and counter-discourses interact, discussion and interaction can facilitate an exchange of ideas (Fine and Love-Nichols, 2023). Nevertheless, online and social media can be a powerful tool for influencing public opinion and consumer behavior. Sogari et al. (2017) observed the power of social media in raising wine industry sustainability awareness and impacting consumer purchasing behavior. Consumer social media use has also been found to be associated with higher levels of concern for food quality and environmental issues (Simeone and Russo, 2017).

Among the different social media platforms, Twitter, now called X, has long enjoyed wide user acceptance, quick increases in conversation volume, and interest from sentiment analysis researchers. Consumer data can be gathered from various social media platforms and forums, thus it is helpful to examine blog comments, forum threads, and review websites. Social media has been useful to analyze how people utilize social media to understand social, cultural, and environmental challenges (Jung et al., 2021; Widmar et al., 2021; Ellman et al., 2023). Markets for food, a common topic of study, have also been successfully examined using online media (Mahoney et al., 2020; Ortez et al., 2023). One common goal of social media analysis is to examine public sentiment, which often extends to determining what circumstances can cause people to change their opinions, identifying key influencers, and observing how opinions change over time (Maynard et al., 2017).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Online and social media listening was used to understand online conversations while amassing data from multiple social media platforms, including Twitter, news discussion boards, blogs, and forums. The collected online data is from the United States between 2018 and 2022 using English-speaking posts. Quid was utilized to collect and analyze online and social media data (Quid, 2025). Quid works by searching for posts and mentions of “included terms” and omitting posts or media containing “excluded terms,” both of which are defined by researchers. Once online and social media posts are collected through researcher parameterized searches, Quid offers a sentiment analysis capability based on Natural Language Processing (NLP) technology (Quid, 2024a).

Data was collected on the second week of August 2022 by year, beginning July 29, 2018, and ending July 31, 2022, with samples of mention counts and net sentiment observed on a weekly basis. A total of 15 “inclusionary terms” were included for initial searching, namely carbon, #carbon, sustainability, #sustainability, climate change, #climatechange, greenhouse gas, methane, #methane, emissions, #emissions, sustainable, #sustainable, GHG, and #GHG. A post constitutes a “mention” when it contains a sentence that is retrieved by Quid. that Mentions may include reposted, or otherwise shared content.. Collected posts were then searched for specific terms referencing dairy or beef cattle or related products, and then further subdivided into geographic categories referred to as inclusive and exclusive zones.

Species sub-searches included both dairy and beef. Searches for each of these cattle production systems and associated products occurred separately to allow researchers to discern media volume and sentiment about dairy and beef cattle separately. The dairy sub-search included an additional list of inclusive terms for searches, specifically dairy cow, #dairycow, dairy, #dairy, milk, #milk, butter, #butter, yogurt, #yogurt, cream, #cream, ice cream, #icecream, cheese, #cheese, cream cheese, #creamcheese, mozzarella, #mozzarella, cheddar cheese, #cheddar. The beef sub-search included the terms beef, #beef, cattle, #cattle, feedlot, ribeye, steak, #steak, tenderloin, filet mignon, sirloin, London broil, brisket, ground round, ground chuck, ground sirloin, and ground beef. Each of these sub-searches was accomplished independently, layering the sub-search terms under the primary 15 inclusionary search terms, which included references to carbon, climate change, and emissions.

The beef and dairy industries each have respective geographic zones in which production is common, as well as areas where production is uncommon. Information about the location of where beef versus dairy production takes place was used to build inclusive and exclusive geographic zones. That is, geographic inclusive and exclusive zones were determined according to whether the specific production system (meaning beef or dairy) occurs prominently in the region where the online data originates. Organizing the data into categories of inclusive and exclusive allowed us to better understand variance and relations in the sentiment and number of mentions between areas where the production occurs and areas where it does not. Maps of the distribution of cattle across the U.S. were compiled using USDA NASS county-level census data from the 2017 Agricultural Census (Figures 1 and 2). Inventory values for beef cows were used for a map of beef cattle production in the U.S. (Figure 1), and inventory values for milk cows were used for a dairy cattle map (Figure 2). County-level maps were then used to identify major production regions and low production regions. These are classified as “inclusive” zones where major production occurs, and “exclusive” zones where relatively little production takes place, if any.

Zones inclusive of beef production included the following states: California, Oregon, Washington, Idaho, Nevada, Utah, Montana, Wyoming, Colorado, New Mexico, Texas, North Dakota, South Dakota, Nebraska, Kansas, Oklahoma, Missouri, Florida, Arkansas, Kentucky, Tennessee, Virginia, and Alabama. States not mentioned in a species inclusive zone are categorized into the species exclusive zone. Zones inclusive of dairy production include the following states: California, Washington, Arizona, Idaho, Kansas, New Mexico, Wisconsin, New York, Pennsylvania, Minnesota, and Colorado. X/Twitter, our primary source of social media posts, collects user geo-data, which is made available to developers, including Quid (X Developer Platform, 2025). Note that posts originating on other platforms may not be able to receive geographic classification, and as such, may be excluded from our sub-search for results by inclusive and exclusive zones.

Sentiment analysis allows for online activity to be characterized as generally positive, negative, or neutral, relative to the topic of interest (Quid, 2023). Sentiment analysis has proven useful to investigate public reactions to major food safety events like the baby formula recalls and shortages in 2022 (Jung et al. 2023) and to examine the relationship between online media volume and sentiment about dairy products/markets and futures prices for Class III milk (Ortez et al. 2023), which is milk used to make hard cheese products (see Federal Milk Marketing Order Program: An Overview of the System (2019) for explanation). Online data is also heavily utilized for sentiment analysis in the travel, tourism, and food service industries (Markopoulos et al., 2015; Widmar et al., 2020b; Trivedi and Singh, 2021; Farzadnia and Raeesi Vanani, 2022; Lai et al., 2023).

Sentiment analysis was performed using Quid’s natural language processing (NLP) capacities. Search results were analyzed by the NLP and assigned a sentiment (positive, negative, or neutral). A ratio of positive posts to negative posts, is calculated weekly which yields a net sentiment metric ranging between −100% (completely negative posts) to 100% (completely positive posts) (Jung et al., 2022; Quid, 2024b). Neutral posts are not included in the calculation of net sentiment, but are used in determining terms identifiable as sentiment drivers.

When discussing climate change and the environmental performance of goods and services, rhetoric can be diverse. Social media has proven fruitful to study rhetoric in climate change discourse (Adams and Gynnild, 2013; Bloomfied and Tillery, 2018). When discussing climate, some writers or content creators may include apocalyptic or doomsday rhetoric (Foust and Murphy, 2009; McQueen, 2016). Others may engage in discourse that denies the role of human activities in climate change, and they may even deny the trends of climate change altogether (Dryzek and Lo, 2015). Still, others may dismiss the impacts (realized or potential) of climate change, or be dismissive to the potential of mitigation strategies (Adams and Gynnild, 2013; Bloomfied and Tillery, 2018). Mitigation strategies are often discussed with emissions accounting estimates to provide context to the value of the mitigation technique (Shao and He, 2022). In content generated by news platforms, the impacts of climate change are sometimes touted in terms of risk and dangers to our security (Sonnett, 2022). Other studies of online chatter related to agriculture have found extensive discussion of recipes and ingredients and found extensive discussion of sustainable practices (Fisk, et al., 2024). Research from the USDA suggests that 98% of farms in the US are family-owned or operated (Burns and MacDonald, 2018). This analysis investigates the sample collected to find evidence of these rhetorical devices and discourses. A full glossary of key terms for this analysis (including rhetorical drivers) is included in Appendix A.

Before 2020, the broader conversation about the intersection of carbon and cattle production may be characterized as “doom and gloom” or in other words, rhetoric that evokes feelings of despair, often employing apocalyptic imagery. However, that dialogue gives way to a more positive and “action-oriented” rhetoric, wherein commenters focus on genuine mitigation strategies in 2020 and 2021. During the most acute impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic in the US, specifically March 2020 through summer 2020, there was a notable positive shift in the net sentiment of the search results collected for cattle in the context of carbon. Average annual net sentiment jumped from 13 in 2018 and 18 in 2019 to 34 in 2020 and 25 in 2021 for beef. Dairy search net sentiment was higher overall, starting out at 34 in 2018 and then peaking in 2020 at 58.

When comparing simple averages of weekly mentions and net sentiment over time, a parabolic relationship was observed in the mentions (with respect to years), and in net sentiment, an inverse parabolic relationship. In other words, the number of mentions was highest in 2018 and 2021 (the first and last years), as shown in Figure 3. This contrasts with the general descriptions of net sentiment, which is highest (or most positive) in 2020.

Table 2 displays the correlations between the inclusive and exclusive geographical sub-searches for beef cattle. Note that for the mentions data we observe strong correlation coefficients. This indicates homogeneity in the amount of online media on beef cattle between zones where beef cattle production occurs (inclusive areas) and zones where it does not occur (exclusive areas). Sentiment in inclusive regions does not exhibit a strong correlation to exclusive regions (0.32 in the case of all cattle, and 0.3 in the case of cattle on feed). Worth noting, however, that each of these correlation coefficients (however strong or weak) is positive. We do not observe any instances where discussion of one beef cattle variety (or discussions occurring in one area) has a negative relationship with their opposite case (inclusive or exclusive zone). This observation of highly correlated mention counts, but weakly correlated sentiments, suggests that Americans engage in similar levels of discussions of beef cattle regardless of geography, but they do not necessarily feel similarly about them in the context of beef cattle environmental performance.

That we would observe homogeneity in discussion levels but heterogeneity in sentiment of the related discussions between sub-groups of beef and dairy, and between exclusive and inclusive zones, might stem from a few factors. First, farmers in the US have been found to generally believe in the existence of climate change, but to harbor skepticism of anthropogenic causes (Gramig et al., 2013; Chatrchyan et al., 2017). This skepticism may extend to their appraisal of their own role in producing emissions. Studies that analyze belief in climate change by geography in the US have observed variations along political and demographic lines, including beliefs correlating with population density (counties with high population show higher levels of belief) and correlating with the presence of high-emitting industries (Howe, et al., 2015). With these findings in mind, it is not surprising that we observe geographic heterogeneity in sentiment levels.

The beef mentions data exhibits a parabolic trend, with the lowest weekly average mentions occurring in 2020. Examining sentiment, we see a weak inverse parabolic relationship in the weekly sentiment averages, with a maximum in 2020. Beef mentions saw a few major spikes (Figure 4), first in September 2019, although much of the web activity around this time is related to a feud between scientists, where an expert panel concluded Dr. Peter Ridd sought to discredit studies of bleaching in the Great Coral Reefs (Schaffelke et al., 2018). At the same time in 2019, the Iowa Steak Fry was held, a ritual in presidential primaries in the US election (Jones, 2020). This campaign event included significant discussion of climate change and climate policy. Later, we see spikes in February May and 2021 that seem to be related to news articles related to Bill Gates suggesting rich nations switch to synthetic beef (Temple, 2021) and a story in the Daily Mail which (falsely) speculated President Biden’s climate plan would limit Americans to one hamburger a month (Lynch and Pierrehumbert, 2019). Lastly, we see a spike in July 2022, which seems to be driven, in part, by a climate action plan that the Netherlands had recently unveiled, which targets emissions of nitrogen oxides from agriculture.

In the negative drivers, much of the discussion relates to emissions, along with mentions of climate and weather-related events. Concerns over climate and security (including in the context of weather events) were prevalent in all 4 yr, and the relative interest in this topic was largely driven by other conversations coming and going in popularity. This is consistent with findings of Bloomfield and Tillery (2018) and Adams and Gynnild (2013) who find climate impacts to be important topics of discussion regarding climate change. For example, 2019 was a bad year in weather and climate. As wildfires raged on multiple continents, sentiment became more negative, and people became increasingly concerned with conversations regarding topics including “destroy the planet.” This is similar to the findings from Sonnet (2022) who found that the risks and dangers of climate change are a threat to our own security. These findings might suggest a larger “buy-in” to climate science (or the consequences of a changing climate) among the public, where much of the negative sentiment stems from actual news, as opposed to dismissal of the problem and/or conspiracy theories. Dismissal and conspiracy rhetoric was, however, still persistent in the data, consistent with findings from Dryzek and Lo (2015). In 2019, this rhetoric ranked as the second most common driver of negative sentiment, and every year, the word “hoax” was in the top 25 terms, suggesting a consistent dismissal of science. To highlight this, the term “hoax” was included in our analysis as a subgroup of dismissive to highlight its prevalence in the mentions. “Heat-trapping gases” entered conversations as a driver of sentiment in both dairy and beef in 2021.

In search results about dairy in the context of carbon footprint, similar trends to those found in beef were identified in top sentiment drivers. Table 6 summarizes the major drivers into top areas of conversation by year, as well as overall. Before 2020, we saw positive sentiment driven by interest in farming practices (such as “feed efficiency”) and terms related to pride and tradition (including “family farming”). Mitigation strategies, being a frequent topic of discussion, is consistent with the findings of Shao and He (2022) and an important finding for industry and policy maker stakeholders. Family farming was a top theme in the positive sentiment for dairy and not found in beef. This suggests a strong family and pride-centered rhetoric in dairy production markets. Each year also included frequent mentions of industry and market conditions in addition to discussion of emissions. Like beef, in 2021, discussion of “heat-trapping gases” entered the conversation but was not a top mention before this time period.

Among the major drivers of negative sentiment is frequent discussion of emissions. Mentions of market and economic conditions were common as well; we saw less dismissive rhetoric and mention of “hoax,” but these were still prevalent. Unlike beef, the use of the term “hoax” declined, with observations falling 66% between 2018 to 2021. Admittedly some noise is seen in the search results in this context, with numerous references to essential oils and popular culture. In the dairy search results, looking at the categories driving negative sentiment, we saw less discussion of climate security concerns, in favor of more discussion of economics and industry within the context of GHG emissions. There was also far less “doom and gloom” or despair-centered rhetoric in the sentiment drivers for dairy in aggregate, when compared to beef in aggregate. It is important to note that the “Dismissive” category groups instances where people have dismissed scientific research, such as questioning its legitimacy. Examples of this include characterization terms like “irrelevant to atmospheric warming,” “silly,” or “bullsh*t.” Across both species sub searches, the word “hoax” was a consistent negative driver, while its use does not curtail in beef, its use did attenuate in the dairy categories in later years.

CONCLUSION

The agricultural industry has much to gain from studying online media data, as these sources can help stakeholders better understand how the public views and engages with their products (Fisk et al., 2024). Examining consumer interest in and sentiment towards cattle production in the context of GHG emissions and carbon footprints from 2018 to 2022 yielded insights that are likely to reflect a larger social discourse. When examining mentions counts and net sentiments in regions inclusive to beef or dairy production against regions exclusive to these industries, we notice similar trends in discussion volumes but different trends in net sentiment. This indicates a divide between public opinion on these industries in the US, with respect to their greenhouse gas emissions.

The findings presented here are significant to industry stakeholders and policy makers alike who have an interest in monitoring trends in public sentiment. Observing social and online news media data on the subject of the environmental performance of beef cattle and dairy cow production enables us to observe the evolution of debate. Stakeholders in the private and public sectors should note that various rhetorical devices are common in the data we observe. Over the course of data collection, we observe increasing interest in mitigation strategies. These findings may be useful to producers and marketers of agricultural commodities in beef and dairy to combine advertisements of mitigation strategies employed on the farm with more traditional elements of promotion, including use of recipes and discussion of the family-centered nature of their agricultural production. Policymakers should also take note of the increasing interest in effective mitigation strategies among consumers, who are increasingly displaying thoughtful engagement in discussions of climate change and its consequences.

At the beginning of the period, consumers show increasing awareness of the environmental impacts of their beef and dairy products. The data collected also shows that the COVID-19 pandemic shutdowns were a significant event for online and social media sentiments. Before the lockdowns, fears about extreme weather and lack of climate action competed for attention online with conspiracy theories and doomsday rhetoric. As the pandemic emerged and the US economy began its lockdowns, much of the negative sentiment dissipated, which could be a sign of a migration of public interest. Alternatively, this dissipation of negative sentiments could reflect changing media coverage priorities and consumer purchasing patterns. After the lockdowns expire, the negative sentiment returns, but the discussion topics shift; we see focused and practical discussion of high-footprint activities and mitigation strategies. The trajectory of conversations, in favor of detailed mitigation strategies, seems to reflect an increasingly action-oriented discussion. Yet, there were differentiating features prominent in the topics of and/or types of conversations had, mentioning dairy versus beef in this context of carbon and emissions, broadly defined. Social media networks affect consumers interaction and ability to receive information intended for them (Olbrich and Holsing, 2011). Communication theorists have posited that consumers do not passively take in information; instead, they will condition it according to their preconceived notions and experiences (Olausson, 2018). Social media platforms may host competing discourses resulting from robust networks that frame and maintain controversial subjects from specific points of view (Gunster, 2022). These networks are leveraged to push engagement with media preferred by the networks and in doing so, they can curate news feeds to cultivate and reaffirm ideological stances (Gunster, 2022). For example, vegetarians tend to consume more personalized news feeds (via social media) than non-vegetarians, who are more likely to obtain news information from traditional forms of media (Kley et al., 2022). As social media users increasingly become comfortable with curated feeds, they may be susceptible to misinformation and disinformation that conforms to their beliefs (Taddicken and Wolff, 2023).

While consumers and producers frequently interact on social media and discuss products, not all communications strategies work the same. It is possible that differences in topics of conversation differ between beef and dairy systems in the context of carbon footprint due to different networks being active on different topics, meaning that the individuals invested in conversations about dairy may differ from those invested in conversations about beef. Further, there was some evidence of differing search results within versus outside of production regions. Further analyses may seek to investigate why certain topics are associated with different production systems. The findings presented are exclusive to the time period of study; additional studies exploring different time periods and geographical scopes may reveal localized perceptions and nuances and/or changing viewpoints over time.
