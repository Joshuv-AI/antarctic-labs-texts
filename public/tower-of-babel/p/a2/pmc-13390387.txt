Comparison of ocular biometric parameters between nanophthalmos and primary angle closure disease: a cross-sectional comparative study
Authors: Sharmila Rajendrababu, Tos TJM Berendschot, Vijayalakshmi A. Senthilkumar, Udhayakumar Maghalakshmi, Mythri Krishnamurthy Rao, Balagiri Sundar, Carroll Andre Bertha Webers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare the ocular biometric parameters between eyes with nanophthalmos and primary angle closure disease (PACD).MethodsThis was a cross-sectional, comparative hospital-based study. A total of 144 subjects (144 eyes) including 58 nanophthalmos patients and 86 primary angle closure disease (26 primary angle closure and 60 primary angle closure glaucoma) patients were included in the study. The parameters studied were central corneal thickness (CCT), anterior chamber depth (ACD), anterior chamber angle (ACA), angle opening distance (AOD500 &AOD750), lens vault (LV), lens thickness (LT), axial length (AL), lens axial factor (LAF), relative lens position (RLP), and retinochoroido-scleral (RCS) thickness. All measurements were obtained prior to any laser or surgical intervention.ResultsThe mean ± SD age was comparable without any statistically significant difference across all the groups (57.3 ± 11 years in the nanopthalmos group,56.7 ± 9.3 years in the PAC group,56.1 ± 9.2 in the PACG group).The median IOP was highest in PAC eyes (17mmHg) followed by PACG eyes (16mmHg) and nanophthalmic eyes (14mmHg) (p = 0.003). A significant difference was found in ACA among the three groups (p = 0.003), PAC and PACG eyes exhibited wider angles (median 26◦ for each) whereas nanophthalmic eyes showed narrower angle (median 20.5◦). The median AL was highest in PACG eyes, intermediate in PAC eyes and shortest in nanophthalmic eyes (p < 0.001). Similarly, median ACD was shallower in nanophthalmic eyes compared with PAC and PACG eyes(p = 0.0001). RCS thickness was significantly greater in nanophthalmos (~ 2.0 mm) compared to PAC and PACG (~ 1.5 mm), highlighting its diagnostic value. No difference was found in LT(p = 0.879) or CCT(p = 0.434) between the groups. Nanophthalmic eyes had higher LV, greater RCS thickness, higher LAF and normal RLP, compared to PAC and PACG.ConclusionNanophthalmos represents a more anatomically crowded and complex entity compared to PACD, characterized by critical biometric alterations that predispose to severe angle closure. Comprehensive biometric evaluation is essential for early diagnosis, risk stratification, and individualized management planning in these eyes.

Background

Nanophthalmos is a relatively rare, bilateral condition characterized by small ocular volume, typically associated with marked hyperopia and shallow anterior chamber [1]. The axial length is typically lower than 20.5 mm, associated with increase in retinochoroido-scleral thickness (RCS) > 1.7 mm [2, 3]. The globe has a small equatorial diameter and small corneal diameter, however the lens is normal. A disproportion between the lens and the ocular volume (Lens eye volume ratio/LEVR) in nanophthalmos contributes to its iris convexity and anterior chamber shallowing. Unlike normal adult eyes, where the lens occupies about 4% of the ocular volume, in nanophthalmos the lens occupies about 10 to 30% [4]. Angle closure is a common association in nanophthalmos and can occur primarily as a result of relative pupillary block due to critically shallow anterior chamber and increased lens thickness or secondary to choroidal effusions and forward rotation of ciliary body [5–8]. The management differs depending on the underlying mechanism.

In contrast, primary angle closure disease (PACD) is a spectrum varying from primary angle closure suspect (PACS) to primary angle closure glaucoma (PACG) based on international society of geographical and epidemiological ophthalmology (ISGEO) classification [9, 10]. However, there is often an overlap in the diagnosis of both these entities with many cases of subtle borderline nanophthalmos being labelled as PACD. Direct head-to-head comparative studies evaluating detailed biometric and anterior segment parameters using AS-OCT between nanophthalmos and the full spectrum of primary angle closure disease (PAC and PACG) are scarce, with most existing literature focusing on either condition in isolation or limited to glaucomatous stages [4]. Although the anterior segment features in eyes with nanophthalmos are similar to eyes with PACD, it often presents as a more severe form of PACG [4, 11].

Angle closure in nanophthalmic eyes can be secondary to spontaneous choroidal thickening and effusions [7]. The choroidal changes can lead to forward rotation of the ciliary body, leading to pushing of the peripheral iris forward and exacerbating the relative pupillary block. The abnormally thickened sclera in nanophthalmic eyes often predispose to uveal effusions and secondary angle closure because of poor trans scleral permeability to proteins and causes vortex vein compression [8, 12]. Nanophthalmic eyes have complex ocular anatomy, predisposing to several vision threatening complications like uveal effusion and exudative retinal detachment with any surgical intervention [13, 14]. Malignant glaucoma is a common complication that is found to occur both in PACD and nanophthalmos particularly after an intraocular surgery [4, 14]. This study aims to provide a comprehensive, head-to-head biometric comparison between nanophthalmos and PACD to identify objective parameters that aid in their differentiation.

Methodology

Study design and participants

This is a cross sectional, comparative study conducted at a tertiary eye care centre in India. The study was approved by the Aravind eye Hospital Instituitional Ethics Committee (IRB no-RES2023065CLI) and adhered to the tenets of the declaration of Helsinki. All the participants provided informed written consent for participation in the study.

The study population included patients diagnosed with nanophthalmos and also patients with primary angle closure and primary angle closure glaucoma. Nanophthalmos was defined based on short axial length (< 20.5 mm) in combination with increased RCS thickness (> 1.7 mm), in accordance with established diagnostic criteria [2, 3]. PACD classified as primary angle closure suspect (PACS), Primary angle closure (PAC) and Primary angle closure glaucoma (PACG) based on international society of geographical and epidemiological ophthalmology (ISGEO) classification [9, 10]. Patients with more than 180 degrees of iridotrabecular contact (ITC) with normal IOP and normal fundus were diagnosed as PACS. The presence of normal or raised IOP with or without peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS) in PACS were diagnosed as PAC. If patients had visual field changes and glaucomatous disc changes, then they were included in the PACG category.

We included patients diagnosed with nanophthalmos between August 2023 to October 2024 with age more than 18 years, all with clear ocular media to facilitate ASOCT and fundus examination. The control group consisted of age matched patients with PACD (PAC and PACG), included in the same period. All participants received oral and written information about the study, and participants were enrolled only after they provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included patients with media opacities, drug induced secondary angle closure disease and patients with poor images on ASOCT or ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM), those who had undergone previous YAG peripheral iridotomy, cataract and /or glaucoma surgery, ocular anomalies (microphthalmos, microcornea, coloboma, aniridia), PACS and those who presented with acute angle closure disease.

Study parameters

All patients received a comprehensive ocular examination including best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) with a Snellen chart, IOP through Goldmann applanation tonometer, anterior segment examination with slit lamp biomicroscopy, angle assessment through gonioscopy using Zeiss 4 mirror gonio prism and fundus examination with a 90D lens. The central corneal thickness (CCT), axial length (AL), anterior chamber depth (ACD), lens thickness (LT) and IOL power were measured by noncontact partial coherence interferometer (IOL Master, Carl Zeiss, Germany) and RCS thickness was measured by B scan (4 Sight Accutome by Keeler plus, USA). All examinations were performed by two operators using a standardized protocol. The probe was placed gently over the closed eyelid with coupling gel, ensuring minimal pressure to avoid globe indentation. A transverse axial scan was obtained through the posterior pole, with particular attention to include the macular region. RCS thickness was defined as the distance from the inner retinal surface to the outer scleral boundary. Measurements were taken at the posterior pole in the region of maximal thickness, typically subfoveal or parafoveal, using inbuilt electronic calipers. Care was taken to obtain well-focused images with clearly demarcated retinal, choroidal, and scleral interfaces. Each measurement was repeated at least twice, and the average value was used for analysis. The operators performing the B-scan were masked to the clinical diagnosis to minimize measurement bias.

All patients underwent ASOCT measurements (Heidelberg Spectralis, Heidelberg engineering, Heidelberg, Germany), the details of which have been described in many previous publications [11]. Anterior segment measurements using ASOCT were performed under the same room conditions by a single clinician who was masked in terms of groups. To reduce accommodation, the target refraction was set to the patient’s distance refraction. Patients were instructed to sit in front of the device and place their chin on a chin rest. After the real-time image was optimally aligned, the scan was performed. For proper alignment, the optically produced corneal reflex became visible as a vertical white line along the center of the cornea.

The following parameters were assessed:

Anterior Chamber Angle (ACA) measured in degrees of arc. The ACA is the angle between the corneal endothelium and the anterior iris surface.Angle Opening Distance (AOD), the distance from the corneal endothelium to the anterior iris surface, perpendicular to a line drawn at 500 μm (AOD 500) and 750 μm (AOD 750) from the scleral spur in the nasal and temporal angles.Lens Vault (LV), the perpendicular distance between the line joining the two scleral spurs and the anterior pole of the lens.

The lens axial factor (LAF) was calculated as the lens thickness/axial length and relative lens position (RLP) was calculated as lens position/axial length. The lens position was defined as the sum of ACD and one-half LT, that is, (ACD + 1/2 LT). Details regarding the number of antiglaucoma medications and details regarding visual fields measured with Humphrey perimetry (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA) were documented.

For all patients we followed the same order of investigations starting with A Scan, followed by ASOCT and B scan.

Sample size calculation

A 5-degree difference in anterior chamber angle was assumed for sample size estimation, based on prior studies evaluating variability in angle measurements using UBM and AS-OCT [15, 16] In order to determine a mean difference of 5 degrees difference in the anterior chamber angle between the nanophthalmos and PACD eyes, we calculated a sample size of 128 (64 per group), assuming a common standard deviation of 10 with a power of 80% and 5% level of significance. During sample size estimation PAC and PACG were combined as a single group. However, in subsequent analysis, these were stratified into PAC, PACG and nanophthalmos groups to enable more granular comparisons. For consistency across groups, the worse eye was included for analysis, in nanophthalmos, the eye with shorter axial length was selected; in PACD, the eye with higher IOP and a narrower ACA was chosen, or the eye with shorter axial length if both were comparable.

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics were presented for demographics and clinical parameters. Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check the normality of the data. One way ANOVA and post-hoc Bonferroni test was used for normally distributed data, and Kruskal Wallis test and post-hoc Dunn’s test with Holm correction to control the family-wise error was used for skewed data to compare the demographics, A-scan and AS-OCT parameters respectively. Effect sizes were estimated using eta squared (η²) for parametric and epsilon squared (↋2) for non-parametric comparisons. Chi Square test and Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the categorical outcomes between the groups. Multivariable associations for AL, ACD, and ACA were assessed using ordinary least square regression (OLS), heteroscedasticity was evaluated by Cook–Weisberg test and residual variances were found to be constant. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with all values < 5 indicating no significant multicollinearity. Only clinically relevant predictors with sufficient numbers in each category were included in the multivariable model. All statistical tests were two-sided and a p-value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. All the analysis was done by using STATA v17.

Results

Demographic parameters

A STROBE-compliant flow diagram summarizing the number of participants screened, excluded (with reasons), and included in the final analysis is shown in Fig. 1. In total, 58 nanophthalmic eyes, 26 PAC eyes and 60 PACG eyes were included for the study (Table 1). Mean ± SD age was similar across all the groups (57.3 ± 11 years in nanopthalmos group,56.7 ± 9.3 years in PAC group,56.1 ± 9.2 in PACG group). The proportion of females was highest in the Nanophthalmos group (87.9%) compared to PAC and PACG group (p < 0.001).

Baseline clinical parameters

Best-corrected-visual-acuity was significantly worse in the nanophthalmic eyes compared to PAC and PACG eyes (p < 0.001). The median IOP was higher in the PAC eyes (17mmHg) followed by PACG eyes (16 mmHg) and nanophthalmic eyes (14 mmHg) (p < 0.001). Eyes with PACG required more than one AGM compared to PAC and nanophthalmic eyes. Notably, lower cup-disc-ratio (CDR) was seen in 93% of the nanophthalmic eyes and 92.3% in eyes with PAC (p < 0.001). Compared to PAC and PACG eyes, nanophthalmic eyes showed greater RCS thickness (p < 0.001).

A scan parameters

Median AL was higher in the PACG eyes (22.6 mm) followed by PAC eyes (22.1 mm) and was lower for nanophthalmic eyes (19.9 mm) (p < 0.001). Similarly, Median ACD was found to be lower for the nanophthalmic eyes (2.6 mm) compared to PAC (2.7 mm) and PACG eyes (2.8 mm) [Fig. 2]. No difference was found in lens thickness (LT) and central corneal thickness (CCT) between the groups, (p = 0.879, p = 0.434) [Table 2]. Compared to PAC and PACG eyes, nanophthalmic eyes showed higher LAF (0.24) and normal RLP (0.254). Post-hoc Dunn test showed no difference between PAC and PACG eyes for these parameters.

In multivariable analysis, PAC (β = 2.57, 95% CI: 1.68 to 3.45) and PACG (β = 3.07, 95% CI: 2.22 to 3.93) had significantly longer axial length than nanophthalmic eyes. Increasing age was also positively associated with axial length (β = 0.03, 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.06), while RCS thickness was associated with shorter axial length (β=-1.53, 95% CI: -2.74 to -0.32). Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive comparative analysis of anterior segment and global ocular biometric parameters between nanophthalmos and PACD. Our findings demonstrate that nanophthalmic eyes exhibit significantly greater anatomical crowding, characterized by shorter axial length, increased lens vault, and thicker retinochoroidoscleral complex. However, no significant differences were observed in certain parameters like LT and CCT, suggesting that these parameters may not contribute substantially to the anatomical distinction between the two groups.

In our study, the axial length in the nanophthalmos subgroup ranged from 14.7 mm to 20.4 mm, representing a marked deviation (greater than 2 standard deviations) from the normal axial length range of 22–24 mm. It was also significantly shorter than that observed in the PACG subgroup. Previously, Day et al. reported that an axial length below 20.5 mm was associated with a fourfold increased risk of complications [17]. In a retrospective study by Yosar et al. involving 71 nanophthalmic eyes, 25.4% (18 eyes) experienced intraoperative or postoperative complications, including iris prolapse, Descemet membrane detachment, corneal edema, and cystoid macular edema [18]. This is consistent with previous reports indicating that short axial length (< 20 mm) increases the risk of complications, particularly aqueous misdirection (0.4–6%) following cataract surgery [19].

Nanophthalmos is a rare developmental condition with an estimated prevalence ranging from 0.00009% − 0.017% [18]. Angle closure glaucoma in these eyes is often a common association. Management of angle closure in nanophthalmic eyes is particularly challenging, and is often associated with poorer outcomes and suboptimal IOP control [3, 4]. Furthermore, cataract surgery in these short eyes has been reported to carry a higher risk of intraoperative and postoperative complications than the normal population [7, 18, 20, 21]. The most probable explanation for these unfavourable outcomes lies in the complex interplay of certain critical ocular biometric parameters including anterior chamber angle configuration, lens position, lens thickness, axial length and RCS thickness.

We observed increased RCS thickness of 1.7 mm to 2.6 mm in nanophthalmic eyes compared to 1.2–1.5 mm in angle closure glaucoma patients. In a prior study by our group, we similarly noted a mean RCS thickness of 1.99 (0.25) mm in nanophthalmos [20]. Previous studies have demonstrated increased RCS thickness (more than 1.7 mm) in these short eyes, attributed to abnormal collagen deposits and disorganised collagen arrangement within all scleral layers, predisposing them to choroidal complications [8, 12–14, 18]. Given these anatomic predispositions, prophylactic measures such as laser YAG PI, preoperative intravenous mannitol with prophylactic sclerostomy should be considered whenever necessary in nanophthalmic eyes, to minimize the risk of intraoperative and postoperative complications [23, 24].

In our study, both nanophthalmos and PACG eyes had shallow ACD, however the reduction was more pronounced in nanophthalmos group (1.2 to 3 mm). Yang et al., similarly reported mean ACD values ranging from 1.38 to 2.30 mm in nanophthalmic patients [21]. However, it is important to note that these differences were not statistically significant after adjusting for confounding factors in multivariable analysis, suggesting that they may be influenced by demographic and clinical variables.

Nanophthalmic eyes in our study showed larger LV compared to PACG eyes suggesting a more crowded anterior segment. Additionally, angle opening distance parameters AOD500 & AOD750 were markedly reduced in nanophthalmos than angle closure glaucoma eyes, further supporting the presence of extreme anterior chamber crowding in nanophthalmos. Increased lens vault may exacerbate pupillary block by increasing lens–iris contact, thereby impeding aqueous flow from the posterior to anterior chamber. Further, forward displacement of the peripheral iris contributes to angle narrowing. Monisha et al., demonstrated that increased LV enhances the contact area between lens-iris surface, thereby promoting a narrow or even closed anterior chamber angle [24]. In a retrospective comparison of nanophthalmic eyes (32 eyes) with chronic angle closure glaucoma eyes (36 eyes), the authors reported higher risk of aqueous misdirection in nanophthalmos. This susceptibility is plausibly explained by the constellation of critical biometric parameters, including narrower anterior segment, forward movement of larger lens, more anteriorly rotated ciliary body and small ciliary process-ciliary process distance [4].

The lens axial factor defined as the ratio of lens thickness to axial length (LT/AL), reflects the relationship between iris-lens diaphragm and cornea, and serves as a reliable indicator of angle configuration. George et al., reported a mean LAF of 0.192 in normal adults and 0.199 in individuals with occludable angles in South India [25]. Furthermore, Agarkar et al., identified a higher LAF (> 0.239) as being significantly associated with an increased risk of angle closure [26]. We observed higher LAF (0.250) in nanophthalmos patients as compared to angle closure glaucoma patients (0.210 − 0.204). This finding indicates that nanophthalmic eyes are at a greater risk of progressive angle narrowing, underscoring the potential benefit of early lens extraction. Nevertheless, the increased risk of surgery-related complications in these short eyes should be carefully considered before planning any surgical intervention.

Relative lens position (RLP) denotes the proximity of lens to cornea. In emmetropic eyes, RLP values typically range from 0.23 to 0.25 whereas values between 0.20 and 0.21 have been documented across the spectrum of PACD [27–29]. In a previous study, Li et al., demonstrated that PACG eyes with higher AL exhibited an anteriorly positioned lens with associated zonular laxity, contributing to progressive angle narrowing [30]. Interestingly, in our study, the nanophthalmos group demonstrated a relatively normal RLP (0.258) compared with the PAC and PACG subgroups (0.223–0.225).This peculiar finding can be explained by the disproportionately short axial length in nanophthalmos, which reduces the denominator of the RLP formula and thus gives a deceptively higher value, despite clinically evident crowded anterior segment. Despite RLP within normal limits, the lens lies more anteriorly within the shortened globe, contributing to increased angle crowding in nanophthalmos. Consequently, RLP as a single assessment in isolation may underestimate the degree of angle crowding in nanophthalmic eyes. Therefore, other critical biometric parameters, such as ACD, AOD, ACA, LV, and AL should be collectively assessed before planning any surgical intervention in these patients if RLP reveals a normal value [31, 32].

The major limitation of this study is its cross-sectional, hospital-based design at a tertiary eye care centre in India, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population. Being a single-center study, selection bias may be present, and phenotypic heterogeneity within groups was not explored. Another limitation is the smaller sample size in the PAC sub-group may have limited the ability to detect subgroup-specific differences, increasing the possibility of type II error. Therefore, non-significant findings in this subgroup should be interpreted with caution. A potential limitation is the inclusion of only the worse eye (defined by shorter axial length), which may introduce selection bias, as disease severity in PACD is multifactorial and not solely determined by axial length. Further, intra-observer and inter-observer reproducibility for AS-OCT and B scan measurements were not assessed, which may limit the reliability of these measurements.

AS-OCT provides static measurements under light conditions and does not capture dynamic changes in angle configuration. Additionally, the lack of UBM limits detailed evaluation of iris configuration and ciliary body parameters, which are important in understanding the mechanisms of angle closure. However, all ocular biometric parameters were assessed before any laser or surgical intervention eliminating postoperative or procedural bias. Parameters such as axial length, lens vault, and RCS thickness may aid in differentiating nanophthalmos from PACD in clinical practice and may help guide surgical planning and risk stratification. Based on our findings, the coexistence of short axial length and increased RCS thickness (> 1.7 mm) should raise strong suspicion for nanophthalmos over typical PACD and warrants cautious surgical planning. Longitudinal follow-up would further clarify the predictive value of these biometric parameters on surgical outcomes and its relationship between these parameters and clinical outcomes.

Our study echoes the findings of previous studies demonstrating significant differences in ocular biometry between PACD and nanophthalmos. However, the nanophthalmos group is a more complex entity with multiple interplay of risk factors (including angle parameters, AL, lens parameters and RCS thickness) contributing to increased risk of angle closure than the PACD group. A detailed clinical evaluation with comprehensive biometry and imaging are essential in all patients presenting with narrow angles.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the nanophthalmos group exhibited a significantly more crowded anterior segment compared to PACD group. Among the various biometric indices, nanophthalmic eyes showed critically shallow ACD, increased LV, higher LAF, and greater RCS thickness, indicating a more severe anatomical configuration. Laser Nd: YAG peripheral iridotomy though is the primary treatment to relieve the pupillary block component in these eyes, one should be aware of other secondary causes of angle closure like increased LV and ciliary body effusion. In eyes demonstrating short AL in combination with increased RCS thickness, clinicians should consider nanophthalmos as a distinct entity and adopt tailored surgical strategies to mitigate potential intraoperative and postoperative complications. Hence, a comprehensive evaluation of these biometric risk factors is essential for early identification, management and prevention of complications.
