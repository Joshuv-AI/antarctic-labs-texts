Tailoring the compressive properties of 3D printed composites using bioinspired micro/macrostructure designs
Authors: Lizhi Guan, Weixiang Peng, Hao Yuan, Hongyu Zhou, Hortense Le Ferrand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDelicate hierarchical designs in natural composites are known to significantly enhance mechanical properties while maintaining light weight. By contrast, achieving complex structures with such properties in man-made composites remains a challenge. Recent advances combining 3D printing and shear forces have enabled the efficient and cost-effective fabrication of microstructured hierarchical structures. Inspired by the Bouligand structure in the mantis shrimp, we 3D printed glass microfiber-reinforced composites in porous Bouligand structures with lattice designs to yield lightweight, strong, and energy-absorbing composites. The bulk Bouligand composites exhibited the maximum compressive stress and energy absorption values of 117 MPa and 19 MJ/m3 at a pitch angle θ = 40°. Conversely, the lattice Bouligand composites with 25 and 50% porosity demonstrated superior compressive performance at θ = 90°, outperforming those at θ = 40°. Our investigation revealed the critical role of micro/macrostructure designs in tuning the compressive strength, modulus, and energy absorption. Fracture mechanisms in bulk composites such as crack twisting and crack bridging, were identified as key contributors to the enhanced compressive behavior. While in the Bouligand lattice structure, the crack propagated straight along the radial direction of the strut, and subsequently, crack bridging was generated near the nodes where glass microfibers were pulled out and broken. Numerical simulations further showed the local stress distributions within the lattice under compression, providing additional insights into their mechanical performance. These findings provide a promising design for high compressive strength and energy dissipation in lightweight composites for aerospace, architecture, or defense applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20341-x.Keywords: 3D printing, Bioinspired composites, Compressive properties, Bouligand structureSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Hierarchical structures in natural composites, such as bone, wood, or Euplectella sponges, maximize their mechanical properties while being lightweight1–3. Another interesting biomaterial is the dactyl club of mantis shrimps that performs like a hammer. The dactyl club delivers one of nature’s fastest and most powerful strikes. It can reach high speeds of 14–23 m·s− 1, angular speeds of 670–990 rad·s− 1, and accelerations of 65–104 km·s− 2 in 2.7 ms without shattering4. The impact at the contact point generates forces reaching 400–1500 N and peak cavitation forces of 504 N, crushing the prey’s hard shells through repetitive impacts, where these shells are recognized as a benchmark of super-tough and strong biocomposites5,6. Recent studies have deciphered that the performance of the dactyl club is correlated to its unique hierarchical structure including (i) an impact surface with herringbone architecture composed of densely packed (about 88 vol%) ~ 65 nm bicontinuous nanoparticles of hydroxyapatite integrated within an organic matrix, (ii) a helicoidal arrangement of mineralized alpha-chitin fibers, also called Bouligand structure, and (iii) a striated region, which consists of circumferentially oriented fibers7,8. Among the three regions of the dactyl clubs, the Bouligand structure (ii) has been identified as primarily dissipating stresses and resisting fracture propagation9. The combination of alpha-chitin nanofibers and amorphous minerals provides a periodic modulus mismatch leading to crack deflection and twisting8. Additionally, this structure exhibits a shear wave filtering effect during dynamic impact10. The Bouligand structure is also found in other creatures, such as in the exoskeleton of crabs, the cuticle of the lobster Homarus americanus, the scales of Arapaima gigas, and the exocuticle of beetles11–17. Therefore, the investigations into their microstructure features have provided critical insights into the design of reinforced engineering composites6–8. However, introducing the microstructure design into a porous macrostructure to enhance its mechanical performance has been rarely studied yet.

Various strategies for synthetic composites with Bouligand structures have demonstrated remarkable enhancements in fracture toughness and impact resistance9,18,19. One such strategy involves manually laying up continuous fiber-reinforced composites with controlled angles between each layer20–22. The approach effectively limits the development of transverse cracks resulting in improved energy absorption and penetration resistance compared to conventional unidirectional and cross-ply composites. A combination of computational and experimental approaches has been used to investigate the high damage resistance of the Bouligand structure in continuous fiber composites. These results reveal that crack twisting, driven by the fiber architecture, is the dominant fracture mechanism at low pitch angles, while crack delamination prevails at high pitch angles23. Bouligand composites can also be produced by brush-induced assembly of short micro/nanofibers on a heated substrate24,25. These composites exhibit superior tensile strength, energy absorption, fracture toughness, and fatigue durability, primarily due to a synergetic toughening mechanism via crack twisting and fiber bridging. Moreover, adjusting the pH, aspect ratio, and concentration values of chitin whiskers in a solution triggered their self-assembly into a Bouligand structure26. However, these approaches are generally limited to simple geometries and conventional micro/macrostructure control, which strictly limits their applications.

By contrast, 3D printing or additive manufacturing enables the fabrication of complex shapes from digital models. The approach allows the use of various materials, such as plastics, liquids, or powder, which are deposited, joined, or solidified layer by layer under computer control. The multi-material 3D printing of the soft and stiff materials enables tuning the pitch angles, fiber lengths, initial crack orientations, and twist angle distribution in a composite with a Bouligand structure27. Notably, energy dissipation in Bouligand structures is insensitive to initial crack orientations and is optimized at critical pitch angles. The hybrid toughening mechanisms of crack twisting and crack bridging modes are balanced by tuning the fiber lengths and pitch angles to maximize the fracture energy. Using shear-induced force, 3D printing enables the creation of Bouligand structures reinforced with discontinuous fibers and the fabrication of complex macro-scale shapes. The resulting composites exhibit enhanced toughness under Izod impact, driven by mixed fracture modes including crack deflection, twisting, bridging, and microcracking18,28. Fracture surface roughness in Bouligand composites increases exponentially with pitch angle (correlation R2 = 0.99), achieving maximum energy absorption and surface roughness at critical pitch angles18. While the mechanical benefits of Bouligand structures—such as improved fracture toughness, impact resistance, and mechanical strength—have been well-documented, there is limited research on the compressive resistance of bulk Bouligand composites and lattice structures. Inspired by natural hierarchical cellular materials2,29, lattice structures with isotropic materials achieve lightweightness and mechanical properties that are derived from their topological design30–33. Thus, developing a lightweight Bouligand structure with adequately stiff and strong properties and understanding the reinforced mechanism and fracture behavior are quite interesting, which is rarely found elsewhere.

Inspired by the Bouligand structure in the mantis shrimp’s dactyl club, we develop lightweight composites with high-resistance axial compression by fabricating bulk and lattice Bouligand structures through direct ink writing (DIW). Specifically, we utilize a printable ink composed of discontinuous glass microfibers and epoxy resin, and containing carbon black and multiwall carbon nanotubes as rheological modifiers. Using shear-induced alignment at the nozzle, we 3D print various structural designs, including unidirectional structures (α = 0° and 90°), Bouligand structures with pitch angles θ = 20°, 40°, and 90°, and lattice Bouligand structures with relative densities of 25%, 50%, and 100% at pitch angles θ = 40°and 90°. Subsequently, the quasi-static compressive properties are evaluated. Numerical simulations are employed to analyze local stress distributions and quantify the fraction of the nodes within the lattice structure under compression. The role of micro/macrostructure designs in the lattice Bouligand structure is revealed in the fracture behavior. Understanding the fracture mechanism of the Bouligand structure under compression contributes to advancing high-strength and tough engineering composites. The remarkable mechanical performance of the lightweight composites demonstrates significant potential applications in aerospace, architecture, and defense.

Materials and methods

Materials

Carbon black was obtained from PRINTEX® XE 2-B, and multiwalled CNTs (MWCNTs) were obtained from Graphistrength® Arkena Innovative Chemistry. Discontinuous E-glass fibers (milled glass microfibers) were sourced from Japan’s Nippon Electric Glass Co. The lengths and diameters of the microfibers ranged from 20 to 300 μm and 7–14 μm, respectively, with average values of 50 μm and 10 μm. Commercial epoxy resin and epoxy hardener (Casting Resin MS1000) were purchased from WEICON Southeast Asia Pte Ltd.

Methods

Ink preparation

MWCNTs (0.15 g) and carbon black (0.03 g) were dispersed into 7.98 g of epoxy resin using a Thinky Mixer (THINKY ARE-250, Japan) for 5 min at 2000 rpm. Subsequently, 7.98 g of glass microfibers was added to the above mixture and mixed for 5 min at 1000 rpm using an overhead mixer (Base Hei-TORQUE VALUE 400, Heidolph Instruments GmbH & Co. KG, Germany). Subsequently, 1.6 g of epoxy hardener was introduced and mixed using the same overhead mixer for 5 min at 500 rpm at a cold temperature in an ice bath. The slow mixing speed and the use of an ice bath reduced the heat generated during the mixing, slowing the epoxy reaction and consolidation of the mixture, which was the ink utilized for DIW.

DIW of the lattice bouligand composites

The 3D printer (3D Potter Micro 8, 3D Potter Inc., USA) consisted of a vertical nozzle with a mechanical piston to extrude the ink through the nozzle. The 3D models were created using the computer-aided design software Rhinoceros 7. IdeaMaker was used to turn the printing path and slice the designs into layers for printing. Unidirectional microfibers orientation at angles α = 0°, and 90° within all the layers and Bouligand structures with θ = 20°, 40°, and 90° were designed. To realize these Bouligand structures, the 3D printer first deposited a layer with aligned glass fibers and then twisted the printing path by the pitch angle θ for each layer. The same process was applied to Bouligand lattice structures with relative densities of 25%, 50%, and 100%, and θ = 40° and 90°. The following printing parameters were used: a diameter nozzle of 0.8 mm, printing speed of 8 mm/s, layer thickness of 0.4 mm, and flow rate of 50%. At least five specimens of each design were printed for each test. The length (L), width (W), and height (H) values of the printed specimens after polishing were 10 mm, 10 mm, and 10 mm, respectively. For these dimensions, 25 layers in total were deposited on top of each other.

Finite element (FE) analysis was conducted using Abaqus 2020. In this study, the filament cross-section size is set at 5 × 5 mm, the layer thickness at 5 mm, the number of layers at 20, and the pitch angles at 40° and 90°, respectively. All compression tests on the Bouligand structure were simulated using the finite element software ABAQUS/Explicit. In the model, the upper and lower surfaces of the Bouligand structure were in contact with rigid plates, with loading applied via a downward displacement of the upper rigid plate. General contact with a friction coefficient penalty of 0.1 was implemented. Material properties were defined using a VUMAT subroutine written in Fortran. Results and discussions

In previous work, Bouligand composites containing 35 wt% discontinuous glass microfibers were successfully printed using DIW18. To further improve the mechanical properties of the composites, the glass microfiber content in the printable ink was increased to 45 wt%. Increasing the fiber concentration makes the extruded ink much more robust, enabling the filament to print overhang structures. In Fig. 1a and Supplementary Information (SI) Fig.S1, the viscosity and yield stress of the prepared ink are 1 × 104 Pa·s and 3 × 103 Pa after mixing with the hardener, showing non-Newtonian fluid behavior. After half an hour, the viscosity of the ink gradually increased to 1 × 105 Pa·s and the yield stress to 1 × 105 Pa, due to the chemical crosslinking reaction between hardener and epoxy resin. When the shear stress is beyond the yield stress, the ink presents shear thinning flow behavior due to the fiber orientation and the friction of the fiber-epoxy interface and epoxy-epoxy chains36,37. After the ink is mixed, it is immediately transferred into the 3D printing syringe before the epoxy matrix is cured. During DIW, the glass microfibers transitioned from a random orientation behind the nozzle tip (Fig. 1b(i)) to an aligned orientation along the walls of the nozzle at the tip (Fig. 1b(ii)). After extrusion and deposition, the microfibers maintained their orientation, which was further fixed by the increase in viscosity due to the curing of the resin (Fig. 1b(iii)). This orientation of the glass microfibers in the composite after 3D printing was verified by optical microscopy and the utilization of the hue-saturation-brightness (HSB) color-coded map, revealing the angles of the oriented microfibers (Fig. 1c). Furthermore, the distribution orientation of glass microfibers was quantified by the ImageJ/Fiji plugin OrientationJ (Fig. 1d). The obtained glass microfibers exhibit homogenous dispersion and highly aligned degree distribution due to the shear-induced force during 3D printing. Carbon black and carbon nanotubes cannot be observed in a high-resolution SEM image, indicating that they are dispersed homogenously in the resin (see SI Fig.S2). Inspired by the Bouligand structure in mantis shrimp, DIW 3D printing enables the creation of bulk Bouligand structures with helicoidal orientations of the glass microfibers in epoxy (Fig. 1e). Interestingly, more complex macrostructures, such as lattice Bouligand structures, can also be designed to explore lightweight structures with high mechanical reinforcement (Fig. 1f). Unidirectional and Bouligand glass microfiber composites were first designed and fabricated by DIW (Figs. 2, 3). To observe the crack propagation in the microstructure under uniaxial compressive loading, unidirectional microfiber composites with horizontal (xy plane, α = 0°) and vertical (yz plane, α = 90°) directions were successfully 3D printed, polished, and submitted to compression test. As shown in Fig. 2a, the compressive stress and modulus of the unidirectional composite with α = 90° present higher values compared to the composites with α = 0° and the control composites without glass microfibers. Compared to the control, the composites with α = 0° present slightly higher compressive stress but lower modulus. The control has slightly higher stress in the elastic region than the composites with α = 0° (see arrows in Fig. 2b before yield stress). This is possibly due to the high loading (45 wt%) of the glass microfibers inducing lower epoxy cross-linking density. Afterward, the compressive stress of the composites with α = 0° increased slightly due to the stress transfer to the glass microfibers. The compressive stress (118 MPa) and modulus (2 GPa) of the glass microfiber composites with α = 90° are superior to those with α = 0° (74 MPa and 1.36 GPa). In the former case, where the strong and stiff glass microfibers are parallel to the loading direction, the stress distribution is more effective, leading to higher strength and modulus. In contrast, when the glass microfibers are perpendicular to the loading direction, they do not contribute significantly to bearing compressive loads. Instead, the load is transferred through the weaker polymeric matrix, which typically has lower strength and stiffness. However, energy absorption of the composites with α = 0° (9942 kJ/m3) is nearly two times that of those with α = 90° (4942 kJ/m3) (Fig. 2c). The cracks in the composites with α = 90° propagate straight along the loading direction (Fig. 2d). The interfacial debonding between the oriented glass microfibers and epoxy matrix induces short crack tortuosity along the loading direction. Notably, crack propagation in composites with α = 0° is more complex. In Fig. 2e, the crack circumvents and twists around the glass microfibers resulting in interfacial debonding and crack deflection and contributing to energy dissipation38. The increase in crack tortuosity and fracture surface of the composites with α = 0° remarkably enhances energy absorption. This phenomenon, along with crack deflection, resulted in these composites exhibiting higher energy absorption levels than the composites with α = 90°. Therefore, tailoring the orientation of the glass microfibers in the composites significantly enhances the compressive properties.

Previous work showed that the impact energy absorption and flexural properties of bulk Bouligand composites containing glass microfibers initially increased and then decreased as the θ increased from 10° to 90° with the maximum value at θ = 40°18. Aiming to explore the quasi-static compressive performance of these composites, Bouligand composites with varied pitch angles θ = 20°, 40°, and 90° based on the first layer at α = 0° were 3D printed. Increased compressive strength, modulus, and energy absorption were recorded for all pitch angles compared to the unidirectional composites (α = 0°). Interestingly, the Bouligand composites with θ = 40° yielded maximum compressive strength of 117 MPa and energy absorption of 19 MJ/m3 but minimal modulus of 1.95 GPa (Fig. 3), which is close to the unidirectional composites with α = 90°. The Bouligand composites with θ = 90° exhibited high compressive stress and energy absorption but low modulus compared to those with θ = 40° (Fig. 3a, b). As described previously, tuning the pitch angle of the microfibers could tune the crack propagation in Bouligand composites, such as crack twisting and crack bridging18,27. Therefore, the compressive strain in the glass microfiber Bouligand composites with θ = 40° amplifies the crack tortuosity (Fig. 3c), resulting in a large fracture surface and energy absorption. This crack mechanism is recorded by electron microscopy after failure in Fig. 3d. Under loading, the crack in the xz plane twists around the glass microfibers when the crack direction is parallel to the microfiber orientation. Once the crack approaches the front domain within the microfibers turning 40°, crack bridging (crack penetration, blue arrows) occurs, leading to glass microfibers being pulled out and broken (red arrows). This phenomenon is observed during the impact test18. Tuning the θ in Bouligand composites therefore effectively improves compressive properties.

The remarkable compressive properties of the bulk Bouligand composites provide opportunities to explore more lightweight structures with high resistance against compression. To this aim, we 3D printed lattice Bouligand structure with θ = 40° and 90° varying the relative density (ρ/ρ0) at 0.25, 0.5, and 1. Quasi-statistic compression test was performed on these lattice Bouligand composites (Fig. 4). Interestingly, the results presented in Fig. 4a-c illustrate that the compressive performances (stress, modulus, and energy absorption) of the lattice composites with θ = 90° for the ρ/ρ0 at 0.25 and 0.5 are superior to those with θ = 40°(Fig. 4b-d), which is unlike those for the bulk Bouligand composites. From the printing path generated by the slicer software, it is clear that the nodes or intersection points between two adjacent layers in the lattice structure with θ = 40° are misaligned in the vertical direction (see SI Fig.S3). In contrast, the nodes in the lattice structure with θ = 90° are aligned in a vertical strand. The increased number of nodes in the vertical strand results in higher stress, which is certified experimentally and theoretically39. Therefore, the mechanical performance of the lattice structure with θ = 40° drops rapidly with decreasing relative density. Notably, the fracture behavior in the lattice Bouligand composites is unlike the bulk composites mentioned above. As reported by Ashby, the architecture can deform by either bending or stretching the cell walls40. Under loading, the width of the sample expanded along the x-axis because of the tension generated as shown in Fig. 4a. Under tension, the crack propagates straightly along the radial direction of the strut at the macroscale (Fig. 4e, f). Closely observing the fractured microstructure, crack deflection and twisting occurred along the crack direction. While the crack propagated perpendicularly to the strut with θ = 40°, crack bridging occurred. With increased compressive strain, the higher driving tension force also resulted in the crack bridging being generated near the nodes where the glass microfibers were pulled out and broke (Fig. 4g).

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed lightweight and high-compression resistant lattice Bouligand composites using direct ink writing. Inspired by the hierarchical Bouligand structures in nature, our approach demonstrated the ability to tune the compressive properties of these composites through careful design of micro- and macrostructures. The experimental results revealed that both bulk and lattice Bouligand composites exhibited enhanced compressive strength and energy absorption. Fracture mechanisms in bulk composites such as crack twisting and crack bridging, were identified as key contributors to enhanced compressive behavior. While in the Bouligand lattice structure, the crack propagates straightly along the radial direction of the strut, and subsequently crack bridging was generated near the nodes where glass microfibers were pulled out and broken. Notably, the lattice Bouligand composites with a pitch angle of θ = 90° showed superior performance at lower relative densities, emphasizing the influence of nodal alignment on mechanical properties. Numerical simulations further validated the stress distribution and deformation behavior, providing insights into optimizing the lattice designs for high-performance lattice Bouligand composites. These findings offer a promising pathway for engineering composites with exceptional energy absorption and lightweight characteristics, ideal for applications in aerospace, building construction, and defense. Future work may explore further optimization of macrostructure in the lattice Bouligand composites and extend the scope of applications to include dynamic impact conditions.
