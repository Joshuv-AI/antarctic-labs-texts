Reconstructing the evolutionary history of herbaceous crops through trait‐based ecology
Authors: Alicia Gómez‐Fernández
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractReconstructing the evolution of crop plants is fundamental to understanding their origins, ecological adaptations, and impacts on ecosystem processes. However, our understanding of crop evolution stems largely from archaeology and genetics, with less focus on a trait‐based ecological approach. Crop‐specific studies have shown that phenotypic traits changed substantially during domestication and modern breeding. Yet, global comparative analyses across multiple species and traits remain scarce. Moreover, we largely ignore which plant traits distinguish wild species that were domesticated (progenitors) from those that were not, and how their ecological profiles differ. Here, I propose a conceptual model that integrates crops, their wild progenitors, and other non‐domesticated herbaceous species into Grime's CSR theory and the integrated framework of plant form and function. This model provides insights into the evolutionary trajectories of domesticated plants along economics, root‐microbial collaboration, and size spectra, shedding light on the ecological strategies of the wild progenitors of crops. After a comprehensive review, I emphasize that crops and their wild progenitors share similar resource‐use traits, as progenitors were already highly acquisitive species. Conversely, main trait differences between domesticated and progenitor plants occur along size and collaboration axes, driven primarily by selection of large‐seeded genotypes and intensive agricultural practices, respectively. I propose that crops deviate from the disturbance‐adapted strategies of their wild progenitors toward more competitive ones, including links to different stages of evolution under cultivation. Finally, I outline implications for future breeding programs and the origins of agriculture, and recommend research directions to further advance our understanding of crop evolution.Keywords: crop wild progenitors, domestication syndrome, ecological strategies, evolution under cultivation, functional traits, Grime's CSR triangle, phenotypic space, plant‐microbe interactions, seed size, trade‐offs

Only a tiny fraction of the potential riches of the plant kingdom has ever been domesticated. It is estimated that there are ~354,000 flowering plants worldwide, compared to ~1000 crop species (Purugganan, 2022; Qian et al., 2022). These plants display a variety of growth forms and life cycles, with annual herbs being the most representative, covering the largest share of the world's cropland and food consumption (Pimentel et al., 2012). They also harbor great diversity in terms of their phylogenetic, geographic, and historical origins, their agricultural relevance, and the ways they are used for agricultural purposes (as food, fiber, or fuel; Milla and Osborne, 2021a). Despite their diverse evolutionary and geographic origins and their different domestication histories, crops generally display similar domestication‐related traits, due to the existence of common forms of selection pressure during evolution under cultivation. These include similar abiotic conditions, agricultural management practices, and human cultural preferences (Vavilov, 1922). Here, I review the evolutionary patterns of different traits in multiple crops and infer their ecological profile from a functional trait‐based approach. I focus on annual herbaceous crops because of their global predominance, extensive available literature, and economic importance.

Domesticated plants can be recognized on the basis of morphological, physiological, and phenological modifications relative to their wild counterparts (crop wild progenitors), which often manifest in a highly convergent manner (i.e., the domestication syndrome; Hammer, 1984). In the archaeobotanical record, classical traits of convergence include non‐shattering seeds, larger seed sizes, and the loss of germination inhibition (Fuller and Allaby, 2009). Similarly, domesticated plants tend to exhibit high yields, synchronous phenology, upright and compact growth habits, reduction in physical and chemical defenses, and enlargement of harvestable organs (Harlan et al., 1973; Gepts, 2004; Meyer and Purugganan, 2013). However, the transition between wild plant forms and domesticated species was not abrupt and involved a series of coevolutionary processes that continue to this day (Gepts, 2001).

Archaeobotany and genetics have provided deep insights into these evolutionary processes (Meyer and Purugganan, 2013; Larson et al., 2014). Fuller et al. (2023) provide evidence that the pace of this evolution was considerably slower than previously supposed; different types of selection pressure acted in at least four chronologically separate stages, each affecting different phenotypic traits (Fuller and Allaby, 2009). In herbaceous crops, the first stage involved early selection pressure driven by pre‐domestication sowing and tilling activities; during this period, certain desirable plants with specific phenotypes were selected for cultivation by conscious or unconscious human choice‐making (Zohary, 2004). In a second stage, natural and artificial selection pressure led to morphological changes as wild species adapted to the human‐driven selective environments and food needs (initial domestication). Following domestication (third stage), many crops spread beyond their centers of origin and diversified into a wide range of locally adapted varieties, or landraces, influencing patterns of genetic diversity (crop diversification; Meyer and Purugganan, 2013; Wang et al., 2017). Finally, the Green Revolution, which occurred between the 1940s and 1970s, led to the development of high‐yielding varieties through breeding for crop improvement and adaptation to intensive agriculture. This stage boosted global food production but also led to long‐term environmental and socioeconomic challenges (Evenson and Gollin, 2003; Pingali, 2012).

There is an important distinction between changes associated with initial domestication and those resulting from subsequent crop diversification and improvement (Yamasaki et al., 2005; Burke et al., 2007; Abbo et al., 2012, 2014; Meyer and Purugganan, 2013). Initial domestication led to changes between wild ancestor populations and the first domesticated plants managed by early farmers. Because many domestication traits are undetectable in the archaeobotanical record, and others are scarce or constrained to very few locations (Abbo et al., 2014), the closest extant wild relatives of crops (so‐called wild progenitors) can be used as proxies for the original ancestral populations. By contrast, landraces are domesticated genotypes that have not been intensively bred in recent centuries and so serve as the best proxy for the first domesticates. Thus, the effects of domestication are usually studied by comparing landraces with wild progenitors. Finally, changes during modern breeding can be assessed by comparing landraces and improved cultivars, the latter being the last improved domesticated plants resulting from Green Revolution breeding programs.

In addition to the influential study of archaeology and genetics, trait‐based ecology has become increasingly important for understanding the evolution of plant domestication (Garnier and Navas, 2012; Milla et al., 2015; Martin and Isaac, 2018). A trait is a measurable (morphological, phenological, physiological, behavioral, or cultural) characteristic of an organism that is assessed at the individual or other organizational level (Dawson et al., 2021). Trait‐based approaches have led to discoveries about the influence of domestication traits on yield, and how these effects extend to community‐ or ecosystem‐level functions, such as increased competitive ability (Milla et al., 2014), augmented litter decomposability (García‐Palacios et al., 2013), reduced mycorrhizal responsiveness (Martín‐Robles et al., 2018), diminished herbivore resistance (Chen et al., 2015; Whitehead et al., 2016), decreased plant trait plasticity to abiotic stresses (Matesanz and Milla, 2018), and reduced biodiversity‐driven benefits (Chacón‐Labella et al., 2019) in herbaceous crops compared to their wild progenitors. However, while considerable effort has been dedicated to understanding the evolutionary trends in individual traits and their effects on ecological processes, there have been fewer comparative studies that integrate multiple trait dimensions while assessing temporal shifts in trait spectra throughout evolution under cultivation.

Grime's CSR theory is one of the most relevant and pioneering works in the field of ecological strategies that facilitates the integration of trade‐offs between traits (Grime, 1974, 1977). This theory, originally developed for herbaceous plants, assigns three primary strategies: competitive (C), stress‐tolerant (S), and ruderal (R), based on the overall adaptations of species to two environmental factors, resource availability and disturbance. Competitors are adapted to highly productive, low‐disturbed habitats and typically grow fast and reach large sizes to outcompete neighbors. Stress tolerators grow slowly and invest in defense and protection to survive in resource‐poor, climatically harsh habitats. By contrast, ruderals thrive in highly productive but frequently or intensely disturbed environments, allocating a large proportion of resources to reproduction and having short life cycles to ensure reproduction before the next disturbance (Grime et al., 1997; Grime and Pierce, 2012). Given their close dependence on environmental conditions, these ecological strategies serve as meaningful indicators of the adaptability of species to past, present, and future environments.

The ordination of species by functional traits has identified three main axes of global plant trait variation: (1) plant resource economics, (2) plant and organ size, and (3) belowground collaboration (Wright et al., 2004; Díaz et al., 2016; Weigelt et al., 2021). The resource economics axis is captured by traits that confer rapid resource acquisition in productive habitats, and efficient conservation of resources in unproductive ones (Reich, 2014). The size axis runs from short species, which tend to have small seeds and leaves, to tall species, with larger seeds and leaves and deeper root systems (Niklas, 2004). The root collaboration axis relates to associations with mycorrhizal fungi and ranges from the possession of thick, highly mycorrhizal roots to species with long, thin roots that are less reliant on belowground interactions for resource uptake (Bergmann et al., 2020). These three independent, orthogonal axes are key determinants of plant ecological strategies according to the CSR theory (Pierce and Cerabolini, 2018). The resource economics and root collaboration axes are associated with the gradient between ruderals and stress tolerators (the R–S side of Grime's triangle), while the plant and organ size axis corresponds to the height of the triangle and reflects traits related to competitive ability, dispersal, and longevity (Moles, 2018).

Here, I propose a conceptual model for the functional characterization of herbaceous crops, based on CSR theory and the global spectrum of plant trait variation. The model aims to provide a better understanding of the evolutionary trajectory of domesticated plants along resource economics, belowground collaboration, and plant and organ size spectra, and to shed light on the ecological strategies of crop wild progenitors. Understanding these evolutionary dynamics and ecological profiles can (1) provide valuable information on the phenotypic origins of agriculture; (2) improve our understanding of ecological adaptations and the phenotypic basis of yield‐limiting factors; (3) shed light on which adaptive traits of crop wild progenitors could be used in trait introgression breeding; and (4) identify new species candidates for neo‐domestication, with broad implications for food security, agricultural sustainability, and adaptation to climate change.

THE CSR MODEL FOR THE EVOLUTION OF CROP PLANTS

Evolution under cultivation involved a series of coevolutionary processes resulting from a mutualistic ecological interaction in which humans (the domesticators) controlled and managed the fitness and environment of a selected plant species (the domesticate) to obtain resources and/or services (Purugganan, 2022). As plants became increasingly dependent on the domesticator, artificial selection for desirable plants and traits, along with the relaxation of natural selection pressures compared to the wild environment, led to a decrease in genetic variation and, ultimately, a reduction in the range of phenotypic trait values (Krug et al., 2023; Figure 1A). However, natural selection continued to operate within agroecosystems and played a crucial role in the formation of locally adapted landraces. This process, together with conscious and unconscious human selection, favored genotypes best adapted to local abiotic and biotic conditions, and to human needs (Ruiz Corral et al., 2008).

My conceptual model predicts that the sequential stages of early selection, domestication, diversification, and improvement have left distinct signatures on the phenotypic trait spaces. The main “bottlenecks” occurred at two stages: (1) early selection, during which only a small fraction of the regional flora was selected by the first proto‐farmers; and (2) initial domestication, which caused the most notable constrictions in trait variation, including marked changes at the seed level (non‐shattering seeds, increased seed size, higher yield, and loss of seed dormancy). Later, the introduction and adaptation of crops to new environments led to distinct phenotypes within crop species, expanding trait ranges (Ross‐Ibarra et al., 2007; Figure 1A). Such crop diversification was achieved through processes such as local adaptation and hybridization. However, during crop improvement, the prioritization of yield and the reliance on a few input‐intensive crops and varieties led to a further loss of phenotypic diversity (Krug et al., 2023).

The phenotypic trait spaces of each stage of evolution under cultivation can be visually represented as triangles overlaid on both the Grime's triangle and the major axes of plant trait variation: economics, collaboration, and size (Figure 1B). This conceptual model provides a functional framework to position crops and their wild progenitors in the context of global phenotypic diversity. While the Grime's triangle represents the three main ecological strategies (competitiveness, stress tolerance, and ruderalism), the whole‐plant phenotypic space, defined by the three orthogonal axes, forms a tetrahedral structure in which each point represents a unique combination of functional traits (Figure 1C). Changes in traits during evolution under cultivation along any of these axes can affect plant functions and, consequently, their ecological strategies. For example, increased seed mass can scale up to larger leaves and other plant organs, enhancing competitiveness while limiting dispersal, and thereby promoting a shift toward C‐selected strategies (see further discussion in the section “From seeds to crop yields”). Path analysis supports this directional link from seed to total plant size in crops (Milla and Matesanz, 2017; Gómez‐Fernández and Milla, 2022).

I propose that evolution under cultivation began mainly with ruderal and competitive species, and then, through the cascading effects of selection for larger, thinner‐coated seeds, led to more competitive profiles during initial domestication and diversification of crops, whereas modern breeding favored plants with less competitive profiles and less ability to collaborate belowground. As part of the model, I also argue that the trait values of crops and their wild progenitors tend to remain within the boundaries of phenotypic variation found across other wild herbaceous plants, without expanding beyond the phenotypic space set by wild species, and thus that artificial selection has not altered the underlying multidimensional trait coordination. Rather than focusing on comparisons among closely related taxa, which are better suited for the study of evolutionary divergence, my model adopts a broad functional ecological approach. The aim is to assess whether domesticated plants and their wild progenitors, across multiple lineages, occupy distinct regions within the trait space defined by wild herbaceous species. In the following sections, I develop the model further with empirical examples from the literature.

THE ECOLOGICAL STRATEGIES OF CROP WILD PROGENITORS

The conceptual model suggests that evolution under cultivation (i.e., early phases of plant domestication and subsequent improvement) was largely shaped by early selection of wild plants with specific ecological profiles: primarily R‐ and C‐selected species (Cunniff et al., 2014). Comparative studies show that crop wild progenitors tend to have faster resource‐acquisitive traits than other wild herbaceous species that were never domesticated. At the leaf level, wild progenitors exhibit higher rates of photosynthesis, stomatal conductance, leaf nitrogen content, and softer leaves, and lower water‐use efficiency, than other wild annual herbs (Gómez‐Fernández et al., 2024). At the root level, wild progenitors have thicker, weaker, and less dense roots than other wild herbs (Martín‐Robles et al., 2018). Early germination, larger seedlings, and rapid growth rates have also been reported, although reproductive allocation and phenology (traits that distinguish ruderal from competitive plants) show diverse results (Cunniff et al., 2014; Preece et al., 2015, 2018, 2021). My proposed model places crop wild progenitors at the fast and large end of the plant resource economics and size axes, which, together with the studies cited above, supports the idea that wild progenitors tend to be either competitors or ruderals.

Different theories support the ruderal and competitive profile of crop wild progenitors. According to the “dump heap hypothesis” (Engelbrecht, 1916), early human selection favored traits that were advantageous in the disturbed, resource‐rich habitats surrounding human settlements, where activities such as woodland clearance and waste accumulation improved soil nutrient content (Byrd, 2005). Ruderal plants, characterized by rapid growth and high seed production, may have been more likely adopted for cultivation due to their ability to thrive in such anthropogenic conditions (Hawkes, 1969; Harris, 1972). Alternatively, the competitive strategy may have been associated with deliberate human selection for plants with vigorous profiles and larger organs. Indeed, crop wild progenitors tend to have heavier seeds than other annual herbaceous species that were never domesticated (Cunniff et al., 2014; Figure 2A). This trait may have favored their early adoption for cultivation, given its association with higher yields, greater competitiveness in anthropogenic environments, and/or correlation with other traits such as loss of seed dormancy (Milla et al., 2024). Finally, the acquisitive strategies might also be linked to human preferences for palatability and nutrient content. Wild species belonging to genera that include grain crops tend to show lower levels of secondary compounds than wild species from genera without crops, even after accounting for phylogenetic relatedness (Garibaldi et al., 2021, citing M. M. Strelin, INIBIOMA–CONICET, Argentina, personal communication). Furthermore, taste and nutritional value were likely the main determinants in the selection of wild chickpea by early farmers (Kerem et al., 2007). To test these hypotheses, further experimental evidence is needed that directly compares the organ size, chemical and physical defenses, and nutritional quality of crop wild progenitors with those of wild relatives or other wild species found near early human settlements.

However, according to the model, the stress‐tolerant strategy is not absent among crop wild progenitors (Figure 1B). Some wild legume and cereal progenitors are found in environmentally challenging habitats such as stony soils, shaded steep slopes, or high‐altitude steppes, suggesting that species from extreme environments were also targeted for domestication (Zohary and Hopf, 1988; Willcox, 2005). In addition, populations of several crop wild progenitors are distributed across diverse edaphoclimatic conditions, including arid climates and nutrient‐poor soils (Fernández‐Alonso et al., 2023 [preprint]). Notably, among the seven Near Eastern founder grain crops (Zohary and Hopf, 1988; Lev‐Yadun et al., 2000), only wild einkorn (Triticum monococcum L.), bitter vetch (Vicia ervilia (L.) Willd.), and wild barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) occur as weeds in disturbed environments, while the remaining taxa are restricted to minimally disturbed and often stressful habitats (Abbo et al., 2005). This pattern suggests that stress‐tolerant strategies may have been more widespread in certain regions or historical periods. By contrast, many New World progenitors, such as wild amaranth (Amaranth spp.), wild chenopod (Chenopodium spp.), and wild sunflower (Helianthus annuus L.), have been described as ruderal species (Sauer, 1967; Rieseberg and Seiler, 1990). These differences highlight the need to consider geographic origin, crop antiquity, and phylogeny when interpreting the ecological strategies of crop wild progenitors (Milla and Osborne, 2021b).

EVOLUTION OF CROP TRAITS ALONG THE MAJOR AXES OF PLANT FUNCTIONAL VARIATION

The evolution of traits in herbaceous crops during and after domestication can also be explained by my conceptual model. Domestication and improvement have altered crop traits along the three main functional axes: resource use, root collaboration, and plant size. Here, I explore which axes changed most notably and how these changes shaped crop phenotypes.

Effects of domestication and diversification

A. Resource‐use axis

Domestication and diversification did not consistently change resource‐use traits (Figure 1B; Evans, 1993). For example, no significant changes in leaf ecophysiological traits have been observed after crop domestication (Gómez‐Fernández et al., 2024). Growth rate and its components evolved idiosyncratically during domestication and diversification, with trends varying in magnitude and direction both within and across crops (Preece et al., 2017; Simpson et al., 2017; Gómez‐Fernández et al., 2022). The response of leaf C, N, and P concentrations, and their ratios, to domestication varied according to the geographic origin of each crop (Delgado‐Baquerizo et al., 2016). Other traits related to the economics axis, such as root tissue investment, remained unchanged during domestication in different crops (Martín‐Robles et al., 2018). In addition, crops exhibited less variability in growth rates and leaf gas exchange rates than their wild counterparts, possibly due to the decrease in phenotypic diversity and the already high resource‐acquisition capacity of crop progenitors, both of which limited the scope for improvement on the economics axis (Gómez‐Fernández et al., 2024).

B. Root collaboration axis

A widespread, yet poorly understood, trend was shifts along the collaboration axis (symbiotic association between roots and soil fungi or other soil microbes; Gutierrez and Grillo, 2022). This applies, for example, to root exudates (i.e., primary and secondary metabolites released by plant roots and also via dead tissues and cells) that are important for the recruitment of soil microorganisms in the rhizosphere (Sasse et al., 2018). Fast‐growing plants (with acquisitive trait profiles) tend to have a faster exudation rate and a higher content of labile exudates than slow‐growing plants (Kaštovská et al., 2015; Guyonnet et al., 2018). Similarly, domesticated plants release different compositions of root exudates than their wild progenitors, which can, in turn, lead to differences in rhizosphere microbial diversity (Pérez‐Jaramillo et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2021; Deng et al., 2024). For example, the rhizosphere microbiome of wild progenitors was more diverse and harbored a higher abundance of Bacteroidetes (i.e., a bacterial phylum known for its ability to degrade complex biopolymers) than that of domesticates, regardless of crop species (Pérez‐Jaramillo et al., 2018). The exudation of more labile and less chemically diverse compounds in domesticated plants may have led to lower microbial diversity in the rhizosphere and favored the dominance of copiotrophic bacterial taxa (i.e., fast‐growing, low carbon use efficiency) over oligotrophic taxa (i.e., slow‐growing, high carbon use efficiency; Martínez‐Romero et al., 2020). However, studies on landraces and their wild progenitors remain scarce, and the variation in exudation across root traits and its relationships with microbial communities has not yet been explored (Isaac et al., 2021).

The effects of plant domestication on plant‐microbe interactions may also vary with nutrient availability and fertilization practices (Preece and Peñuelas, 2020). For example, high levels of fertilization reduced the benefits of microbial associations in domesticated plants by decreasing their responsiveness to symbiotic microbes. By contrast, wild progenitors maintained beneficial mycorrhizal symbioses regardless of soil fertility, suggesting that domestication altered plant‐microbe interactions, especially under high‐nutrient conditions (Martín‐Robles et al., 2018). In addition, increases in nutrient inputs can shift soil microbial composition and diversity, as well as the functional profiles of these communities in agroecosystems (Leff et al., 2015; Dai et al., 2020). However, we still lack information on how domestication may have decoupled plant–microbe interactions from other plant functional traits through changes in soil fertility.

C. Plant size axis

Domestication generally promoted increases in plant size and height (Figure 1B); for example, previous studies have found that landraces are generally larger than their wild progenitors (Milla et al., 2014; Milla and Matesanz, 2017; Martín‐Robles et al., 2018; Gómez‐Fernández and Milla, 2022). In most species, larger plant size is associated with higher individual reproductive output (Aarssen and Jordan, 2001) and higher competitive ability in resource‐rich habitats such as agricultural lands (Grime, 1974, 1979), which could explain their general increase during domestication.

Plant size also correlates with other size‐related traits, including leaf area and seed mass (Moles and Leishman, 2008). Domestication consistently selected for larger leaves and heavier seeds. Seed enlargement is one of the classic traits associated with domestication (Meyer and Purugganan, 2013) and has been observed across a wide range of crops, from cereals and legumes harvested for seeds to vegetables harvested for edible leaves, stems, or roots (Kluyver et al., 2017; Gómez‐Fernández and Milla, 2022). There are a variety of hypotheses about the selection pressures underlying seed enlargement in herbaceous crops during domestication, including unconscious, conscious, and natural selection. For example, large seeds may have been selected for their contribution to higher yields, competitive advantage, greater tolerance to deeper burial in cultivated fields, or correlation with other traits such as loss of seed dormancy or higher ploidy (Otto and Whitton, 2000; Dempewolf et al., 2012; Kluyver et al., 2013; Wu et al., 2019; Garibaldi et al., 2021; Preece et al., 2021). In addition, the larger seed size during initial domestication may have resulted from insularity effects (i.e., reduced gene flow and small effective population sizes) and the influence of genetic drift (Spengler, 2022). Although all these selection pressures may have led to heavier seeds, I propose that the correlation between heavy seeds, larger leaves, and taller plants (see the next section) could have conferred a competitive and individual yield advantage that was favored during early selection and domestication.

Effects of modern breeding

Crop improvement also led to inconsistent changes in resource‐use traits among species (Figure 1B; Evans, 1993). Trends in growth‐related traits remained variable across crops and showed a smaller range of variation than that observed during domestication (Gómez‐Fernández et al., 2022). Modern breeding further reduced plant reliance on microbial associations (Figure 1B), although the evidence for this is based on only a few crops (Pérez‐Jaramillo et al., 2017). This trend likely reflects the widespread use of fertilization practices, which reduce plant dependence on beneficial microbes and dampen the responsiveness of domesticated plants to these symbiotic interactions (Preece and Peñuelas, 2020).

Modern breeding did not continue the trend toward larger plant size (Figure 1B; Roucou et al., 2017; Gómez‐Fernández and Milla, 2022). Rather, semi‐dwarf varieties were favored in some cereals to reduce competition and improve yields (Jennings and de Jesus, 1968; Weiner and Freckleton, 2010). This shift in selection criteria, aimed at improved communal performance, explains the stagnation or even reduction in plant size in modern varieties (Anten and Vermeulen, 2016). While the initial and late stages of domestication led to seed enlargement, crop improvement did not significantly alter seed size, as shown by both interspecific and intraspecific studies (Gómez‐Fernández and Milla, 2022; Figure 2B).

FROM SEEDS TO CROP YIELDS

As predicted by the model, many of the competition‐related traits arise from the cascading effects of seed mass to higher levels of plant organization (Figure 1A). Ecological theory is based on the assumption that there are correlations and constraints between traits, meaning that they rarely evolve in isolation (Garnier et al., 2016). For instance, traits at higher levels of organization (whole‐plant level) result from the multiplicative effects of many underlying traits due to allometric constraints and phenotypic integration (Violle et al., 2007). Recent research has shown that phenotypic changes caused by the selection of large seeds during initial domestication positively impacted mature plant size and yield, via cascading effects throughout ontogeny (Gómez‐Fernández and Milla, 2022). Early selection and domestication selected for larger‐seeded plants, with an initial advantage in seedling vigor and organ size. This initial size advantage potentially led to larger leaves, stouter stems, and longer, heavier roots, and thus to larger mature plants overall (Niklas, 2004). These findings suggest that ontogenetic or organ‐to‐organism allometry might be a relevant driver of plant size evolution under domestication.

However, the scaling exponent of growth–plant size relationships in crops is similar to that in wild species and has not changed during evolution under cultivation (Westgeest et al., 2024). This suggests that resource‐use traits did not drive the increases in plant size through domestication. By contrast, seed mass channeled its initial effects on seedling size throughout ontogeny, ultimately contributing to mature size. Therefore, the cascading effects brought about by seed enlargement have been a driving force behind the increase in crop size and competitiveness. However, it is essential that we also understand the role of traits associated with the root collaborative axis in channeling the increase in plant size and yield, which has been much less explored to date.

Finally, the proposed model shows that the phenotypic spaces of crops generally remain within the boundaries observed in wild plants. In other words, crops do not overcome the constraints and trade‐offs that shape trait correlations and limit phenotypic diversity in wild species. This is consistent with the results of studies suggesting that artificial selection has limited potential to push the phenotypes of herbaceous crops beyond those observed in the wild (Donovan et al., 2014; Rotundo and Cipriotti, 2017; Milla et al., 2018; Garibaldi et al., 2021) or to alter trait‐trait correlation patterns (Gómez‐Fernández and Milla, 2022). However, exceptions can be found in some C4 crops, such as maize (Zea mays L.), which have undergone substantial phenotypic and genetic modifications, including changes in correlations among traits (Yang et al., 2019). Therefore, although herbaceous crops do not usually exhibit trait values outside the range observed in wild herbs, evolutionary innovations such as C4 physiology have occasionally allowed artificial selection to expand the phenotypic space beyond wild boundaries (Gómez‐Fernández and Milla, 2022). Overall, crop wild progenitors lie at the acquisitive end of the trait space defined by wild species, whereas crops have moved along the size and collaboration axes during domestication and improvement, generally remaining within the bounds of the wild trait space.

FUTURE RESEARCH LINES

The proposed model emphasizes the importance of integrating plant strategies, phenotypic spaces, and trait dimensions to better characterize the functional profile of crops and their wild progenitors, and to understand crop evolution from an ecological perspective. The study of crop plants has been hampered by the limited number of studies comparing crops and their wild progenitors with other species that were never domesticated (Romero et al., 2025). In addition, although there is plenty of data on individual traits, these data often remain unintegrated and disconnected from other traits and the local environmental context (Díaz, 2025). Numerous questions in trait‐based ecology, population genetics, agronomy, and evolutionary biology arise from this integrative approach. For example, we could use this approach to address the following questions: (1) How does the phenotypic and geographic distribution of crop wild progenitors differ from the general pattern observed in other taxa, particularly after phylogenetically corrected comparisons, and how did the environmental conditions to which the wild progenitors were exposed influence the subsequent evolution of ecological strategies? (2) Is this conceptual model applicable to variation at the intraspecific and/or local scales, and, if so, can comparisons among populations or varieties within species help identify genotypes with traits that confer higher resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses, or more collaborative root traits that may be targeted in breeding programs? (3) How have domestication effects on plant microbiome recruitment impacted plant size, agricultural yields, and, ultimately, ecological strategies? (4) Has the cascade of trait relationships on the size axis influenced the lowest organizational levels (genome, cell, and tissue sizes)? (5) To what extent does this model help identify other wild plant taxa that overlap in trait values with crop wild progenitors and thus are potentially suitable for neo‐domestication?

However, an important limitation of this model is that it relies on existing crops and their wild progenitors, which may not fully represent the diversity of plants domesticated in early agriculture. Many species that were once cultivated have been lost over time, leaving little or no trace in the archaeological record. Examples include Silphium and hulled cereals that were later replaced by free‐threshing forms (Zohary and Hopf, 1988; Parejko, 2003). This survivorship bias may constrain the phenotypic space for early cultivated and domesticated species. Nevertheless, for historically documented lost crops, functional trait analysis could help determine why they were abandoned, whether due to environmental changes, cultural preferences, or the introduction of more suitable alternatives. This raises further questions: (1) Were the lost crops functionally redundant with the species that were ultimately adopted in agriculture, or did they occupy distinct phenotypic niches? (2) Were crop replacements primarily driven by trait advantages, environmental changes, or shifting cultural preferences?

Although the model proposed here focuses on herbaceous plants—particularly annuals—for which most functional data are currently available, future research should expand its scope to perennial plants, both woody (tree and shrub) and herbaceous (root and tuber) crops. These groups include many key species in agricultural history, notably some of the earliest known domesticated plants such as taro, figs, and olives (Kislev et al., 2006; Ahmed et al., 2020), as well as root crops such as cassava, yam, and potato, which formed the basis of major agricultural societies in the Old and New Worlds (Zohary and Hopf, 1988). While many morphological changes during domestication parallel those observed in annual crops (e.g., larger fruit and seed size, indehiscent and less toxic fruits, fewer defensive structures), important differences exist in mating systems, reproduction mode (including clonal propagation and resprouting capacity), phenology, and biomass allocation patterns (Miller and Gross, 2011). In general, evolutionary rates are slower in perennial crops, with weaker genetic bottlenecks and higher rates of interspecific and intraspecific hybridization. Selection in these species may have favored increased investment in reproductive or storage organs, rather than altering overall plant size or resource economics (Garnier et al., 2025). Incorporating these perennial groups would provide a more comprehensive understanding of domestication syndromes and the evolution of ecological strategies in crops. However, to date, the historical and data bias toward annual herbaceous plants has limited comparative analyses.

Overall, the model proposed here aims to guide future research on the functional characterization of crops and their wild progenitors, highlighting the significance of trait‐based ecology and comparative methods. A better understanding of crop evolution may open new avenues for identifying wild taxa or varieties with adaptive traits that are valuable in the face of global change, and novel crops that could be critical for future agricultural sustainability and food security. Ultimately, integrating this model with archaeological, genetic, and cultural data could shed light on the origins of agriculture and the mechanisms underlying crop replacement and loss, and support efforts to reevaluate lost crops for future agriculture.
