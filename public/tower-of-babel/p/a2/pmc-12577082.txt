Evaluating the quality and reliability of cervical cancer related videos on YouTube, Bilibili, and Tiktok: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Shiyuan Qi, Qi Chen, Jinghong Liang, Xiaohui Yang, Xiaomao Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer continues to pose a significant global health burden for women, especially in low-resource settings. Although HPV vaccination and screening programs are available, public awareness remains limited. Social media platforms have become major sources of health information; however, the quality of content varies considerably. This study assesses the quality, reliability, and dissemination patterns of cervical cancer–related videos on YouTube, Bilibili, and TikTok, with a focus on how uploader characteristics influence information accuracy.MethodsOn February 21, 2025, we retrieved the top 100 videos using the keyword “cervical cancer” on YouTube, and its Chinese equivalent “宫颈癌” on Bilibili and TikTok. Two independent reviewers evaluated video quality using the Global Quality Score (GQS), Video Information and Quality Index (VIQI), modified DISCERN (mDISCERN), and Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool (PEMAT). Inter-rater agreement was assessed, and statistical analysis was performed using non-parametric tests and Spearman correlation.ResultsA total of 84 YouTube videos, 82 Bilibili videos, and 91 TikTok videos were included. TikTok videos showed significantly higher user engagement than those on other platforms (p<0.001), but scored significantly lower in GQS, VIQI, mDISCERN, and PEMAT evaluations (p<0.001). Videos uploaded by professionals consistently received higher quality scores than those from non-professionals. (p<0.001) Although TikTok had higher uploader activity, its content was largely based on personal experiences, lacking scientific rigor and practical guidance. The video quality on TikTok and Bilibili is negatively correlated with their interactivity.ConclusionsThe three platforms show distinct differences in how cervical cancer–related health information is disseminated. TikTok demonstrates superior dissemination and engagement performance, whereas YouTube provides higher content quality and credibility. These findings underscore the importance of leveraging each platform’s strengths to promote evidence-based health communication. Introduction

Cervical cancer remains the fourth most prevalent malignancy among women globally. In 2022, an estimated 150,000 new cases and 56,000 related deaths were reported in China, posing a major public health burden, particularly in underserved areas [1–4]. Nonetheless, public knowledge of cervical cancer and awareness of the human papillomavirus (HPV) remain limited [5–7]. Providing the public with accurate and reliable information about cervical cancer is therefore of paramount importance.

With the expansion of internet access, increasing numbers of individuals are turning to online sources for health information [8, 9]. Compared to text, videos offer vivid and concise communication, enhancing engagement and promoting proactive health behavior [10, 11]. However, the spread of inaccurate or misleading health information on social media may lead to inappropriate medical decisions [12–14]. As such, effective screening and quality control of online health content is essential to ensure the dissemination of trustworthy medical knowledge.

YouTube, as the world’s largest video-sharing platform, has a broad global user base [15]. In China, Bilibili has become especially popular among younger users due to its accessibility and diverse content [16]. TikTok, a short-video platform launched in 2017, has rapidly gained widespread adoption both domestically and internationally [17, 18]. In recent years, numerous studies have examined the quality, accuracy, and dissemination patterns of health-related videos on social media. On YouTube, researchers have evaluated videos on various medical topics such as breast cancer [13], laryngeal cancer [10], gastric cancer [19], oral cancer [20], gastrointestinal bleeding [21], and postoperative urinary incontinence [22], revealing wide variability in content quality. On TikTok (Douyin) and Bilibili, studies have investigated a range of health issues, including esophageal cancer [23], liver cancer [16], and non-small cell lung cancer [24], as well as non-malignant conditions like migraine [25], gastroesophageal reflux disease [26], orthopedic disorders [27, 28], and systemic lupus erythematosus [29]. Several studies have also examined female reproductive system conditions, including uterine fibroids [30], premature ovarian insufficiency [31], and vulvar cancer [32]. However, research on cervical cancer–related videos has primarily focused on short video platforms in China (e.g., Tik Tok and Kwai) [33], with limited cross-platform comparisons among YouTube, Bilibili, and TikTok. In particular, the roles of YouTube and Bilibili in digital health communication have yet to be systematically explored.

Therefore, this study aims to systematically compare the content characteristics, quality, and reliability of cervical cancer-related videos on YouTube, Bilibili, and TikTok. We further examine the impact of uploader type (professional vs. non-professional) on information credibility and explore the relationship between video quality and user engagement. By identifying platform-specific differences in health communication, this study seeks to inform future strategies for improving the standardization and effectiveness of cervical cancer education and public health messaging on social media.

Method

Data retrieval and inclusioncriteria

This cross-sectional study involved a keyword-based search conducted on February 21, 2025. The term “宫颈癌” was used on the TikTok (version 33.1.0) and Bilibili (version 8.32.0) mobile apps, while “cervical cancer” was used on the desktop version of YouTube. To minimize bias caused by personalized recommendations, we registered new accounts on each platform. Videos were included based on the following criteria: search date as specified, default sorting without filters, and exclusion of sponsored or ad-labeled videos. The initial selection yielded 300 videos (100 per platform).

Exclusion criteria

Duplicate or similar content

If two videos originated from the same source, the version from a verified account was retained; otherwise, the earlier upload was selected.

Irrelevant content

Videos were excluded if they did not address any of the following topics: anatomy, etiology or prevention, pathology, epidemiology, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, or prognosis.

Recent uploads

Videos uploaded within one week prior to the search date were excluded, as view and engagement metrics during the initial posting period may fluctuate and not accurately reflect audience interaction.

Data collection

Video characteristics included duration (in seconds), upload date, days since upload (as of the search date), and topic. Engagement metrics (views, likes, comments, saves, shares) were standardized over 30 days. Note: TikTok does not display view counts, and YouTube omits save and share counts.

Uploader information included ID, follower count, and classification as either professional or non-professional. The professional category included verified health professionals, licensed medical institutions, certified science communicators, and commercial medical organizations. The non-professional category included general users without verified credentials (e.g., laypersons sharing health-related content), patients or survivors recounting personal experiences, unverified individuals who self-identified as medical or scientific experts, and unverified media or commercial accounts lacking formal accreditation.

Video quality assessment

From February 28 and March 15, 2025, two authors (SY.Q and QC) independently evaluated the quality of all included videos. In cases of disagreement, a third reviewer served as an adjudicator to determine the final score. Four validated tools were used to evaluate video quality. The scoring criteria for each tool (GQS, VIQI, mDISCERN, and PEMAT) are detailed in Supplementary Tables S4–S6.

Global quality score (GQS)

A 5-point scale used to evaluate the overall quality of videos, ranging from poor (1) to excellent (5) [10].

Video information and quality index (VIQI)

This tool assesses the informativeness and quality of video content across four domains: VIQI 1: Flow of Information. VIQI 2: Accuracy. VIQI 3: Production Quality. VIQI 4: Precision. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating better performance and higher content quality [34].

modified DISCERN (mDISCERN)

Consists of five items assessing credibility. Each item is scored as 1 (yes) or 0 (no), with higher total scores indicating greater reliability [35].

Patient education materials assessment tool (PEMAT)

Evaluates both understandability (21 items) and actionability (4 items) of educational materials. Each item is scored as “Agree = 1,” “Disagree = 0,” or “Not Applicable (NA).” Scores for PEMAT-Understandability (PEMAT-U) and PEMAT-Actionability (PEMAT-A) were calculated as: (Total score/Total possible score) × 100,with higher percentages indicating better performance [36].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of continuous variables. As all variables deviated from normality, non-parametric tests were used throughout. Group comparisons used the Kruskal–Wallis or Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, or the Fisher–Freeman–Halton test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median, range, and interquartile range [M (P25, P75)].

GQS and mDISCERN scores were treated as ordinal variables, and inter-rater agreement was evaluated using the weighted Cohen’s Kappa coefficient. Agreement was interpreted as follows: κ > 0.80 = excellent; 0.60–0.80 = substantial; 0.40–0.60 = moderate; and κ < 0.40 = fair to poor.

VIQI and PEMAT scores were treated as continuous variables, and inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). According to the 95% confidence interval of the ICC estimate, < 0.50 = poor, 0.50–0.75 = moderate, 0.75–0.90 = good, >0.90 = excellent [37].

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (r) was used to explore associations between video engagement metrics and quality scores. A positive r indicated a positive correlation, while a negative r indicated an inverse correlation. Correlation strength was interpreted as:|r| ≤ 0.2 = no correlation; 0.2 < |r| ≤ 0.4 = weak correlation; 0.4 < |r| ≤ 0.6 = moderate correlation; 0.6 < |r| ≤ 0.8 = strong correlation; |r| >0.8 = very strong correlation. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Figures were generated using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2.

As shown in Table 1, video durations differed significantly across the three platforms (p<0.001). TikTok videos were the shortest, followed by YouTube, and Bilibili. Upload times also varied (p<0.001), but there was no significant difference between YouTube and Bilibili (p = 0.707).

In terms of user engagement metrics, TikTok showed significantly higher 30-day engagement across likes, comments, saves, and shares (p<0.001). Only Bilibili and TikTok featured save and share functions, and TikTok again showed significantly higher values for both indicators (p<0.001).

Uploader characteristics

The number of unique uploaders across the three platforms was comparable, with 67 on YouTube, 64 on Bilibili, and 75 on TikTok. However, there were significant differences in follower counts among platforms. YouTube uploaders had the highest median follower count at 165,000, followed by TikTok uploaders at 13,000, and Bilibili uploaders with the lowest at 3, 429(p<0.001).

Uploader type distributions also differed significantly (Fig. 2a). On YouTube, most videos were uploaded by medical institutions (47.76%), absent on TikTok and rare on Bilibili (1.56%). Bilibili was dominated by general users (61.19%), while TikTok featured mainly patients or survivors (56.00%). Neither general users nor patient-uploaders were present on YouTube.

Video categorization

Thematic distribution also varied (Fig. 2b and Table S1). Videos on YouTube and Bilibili primarily focused on etiology and prevention, diagnostic procedures, and symptom recognition. In contrast, TikTok videos were predominantly centered on prognosis and treatment, particularly patient-shared content related to postoperative recovery and personal experiences. Epidemiology-related content was virtually absent on TikTok, and the proportion of etiology/prevention-themed videos was also significantly lower compared to the other two platforms.

Video quality assessment

We conducted a consistency evaluation of the ratings given by two raters. The weighted Kappa coefficient for GQS was 0.714, and for the total mDISCERN score was 0.737, indicating good agreement. When the score difference exceeded two points, videos were re-evaluated to improve consistency. ICC for the total VIQI score was 0.89 (95% CI: 0.86–0.91). For the PEMAT tool, the ICCs for the Understandability and Actionability subscales were 0.82 (95% CI: 0.76–0.85) and 0.70 (95% CI: 0.62–0.77), respectively. Arbitration was applied to PEMAT-A items with confidence intervals below 0.75, to balance scoring reliability with efficiency and feasibility.

As shown in Table S2 and Fig. 2c, median GQS scores were 4 for YouTube and Bilibili, and 1 for TikTok (p < 0.0001). For VIQI-sum, the median scores were 14 on YouTube and 16 on Bilibili, both markedly higher than TikTok’s median score of 4 (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2d). Across all four VIQI subdomains (VIQI-1 to VIQI-4), TikTok consistently scored the lowest (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2h).

In terms of mDISCERN scores, the median total score was 3 for both YouTube and Bilibili, compared to 0 for TikTok (p<0.001) (Fig. 2e). For the first three mDISCERN items, over 80% of YouTube and Bilibili videos met the criteria, compared to only 26–45% on TikTok. All three platforms performed poorly on item 4 (provision of further information). For item 5, “Does the video address areas of uncertainty?”, compliance rates were 47.6% for YouTube, 53.7% for Bilibili, and only 1.1% for TikTok (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2i).

PEMAT-U scores were highest for YouTube (84.5), followed by Bilibili (73.0) and TikTok (57.0) (p<0.001) (Fig. 2f). For PEMAT-A, YouTube and Bilibili had similar median scores (33.0), significantly higher than TikTok (0) (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2g).

Comparison of video quality between professional and non-professional uploaders

As shown in Fig. 3 and Table S3, videos uploaded by professionals scored significantly higher on GQS, VIQI, and mDISCERN (p < 0.0001). This trend extended to individual VIQI subdomains and the first three mDISCERN items. No significant differences were found for items 4 and 5 of mDISCERN. Similarly, videos from professionals received significantly higher PEMAT scores for both understandability and actionability (p < 0.0001 for both).

Discussion

This study systematically compared the content characteristics of cervical cancer–related videos on three major platforms: TikTok, Bilibili, and YouTube. Significant differences were observed in video duration, uploader profiles, content quality, and user engagement. TikTok videos were the shortest but demonstrated the highest levels of user interaction. However, most TikTok videos were uploaded by patients or general users, focusing on personal experiences related to diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis. These often lacked professional certification or structured guidance. In contrast, videos on YouTube and Bilibili were more frequently produced by medical institutions or science communicators, with content centered on professional topics such as etiology, prevention, and screening. These differences directly influenced the type and quality of health information available to the public.

Differences in quality scores were particularly striking. On all three primary assessment tools—GQS, VIQI, and mDISCERN—YouTube and Bilibili consistently outperformed TikTok. For instance, the median GQS score for TikTok videos was only 1 (“poor”), compared to 4 (“good”) for both YouTube and Bilibili. This three-point difference was not only statistically significant but also clinically and educationally meaningful, indicating that TikTok videos are generally less comprehensive for patient education. Similar trends were observed in VIQI and mDISCERN scores, especially in critical items such as “provision of further information,” where TikTok showed lower scores. These results indicate gaps in reliability and scientific rigor and raise concerns about the potential for information incompleteness—particularly among users with limited health literacy [38]. Most notably, the median PEMAT-A score for TikTok was nearly zero, indicating that despite its wide reach, the platform contains few videos with actionable guidance. In the context of social media becoming a dominant channel for health information, the prevalence of this less comprehensive content on high-traffic platforms raises substantial public health concerns. This issue is particularly critical for conditions such as cervical cancer, where early detection and scientific intervention are essential, and the reliability and usability of information can directly influence patient behaviors and outcomes.

Further analysis revealed that high-quality videos did not achieve a proportional advantage in dissemination. On TikTok and Bilibili, there were significant negative correlations between video quality scores (e.g., GQS and mDISCERN) and engagement metrics such as comment and like counts. This disconnect between popularity and quality indicates that although short-video platforms are effective in dissemination, they fall short in delivering credible and actionable health education. This discrepancy may be attributed to the platform’s recommendation algorithms and user behavior preferences. Prior studies have demonstrated that social media algorithms primarily rely on users’ revealed preferences—such as clicks, likes, and shares—to optimize content exposure, thereby amplifying emotional, brief, and anecdotal content rather than prioritizing scientific rigor and completeness [39, 40]. Additionally, many users prefer health-related videos that are intuitive, easy to understand, and emotionally resonant rather than those that are information-dense or require prior medical knowledge [10, 16, 41–43]. TikTok’s short-video format naturally aligns with these viewer preferences. Consequently, under the dual influence of algorithmic logic and user bias, high-quality videos often underperform in terms of reach, illustrating that content which spreads widely is not necessarily high-quality or trustworthy. Notably, on TikTok, PEMAT-U scores were positively correlated with save and share counts, suggesting that content that is clear and easy to comprehend may enhance user retention and the likelihood of information sharing.

Our findings also indicate that the professional background of the uploader plays a critical role in video quality. Videos uploaded by medical institutions, licensed healthcare professionals, or certified science communicators significantly outperformed those posted by non-professional users across multiple evaluation dimensions. These results are consistent with previous research indicating that professional content creators are more capable of delivering accurate, well-structured, and educationally valuable content [16, 44–46]. However, professional contributors remain underrepresented on user-generated content–dominated platforms such as TikTok. In contrast, YouTube features a higher proportion of content from medical institutions, resulting in greater visibility and engagement for professionally produced videos.

To improve the accessibility and reliability of health information on social media, coordinated efforts are required at multiple levels. First, more professionals should be encouraged to participate in the creation of popular science content and convey authoritative information through new media formats such as short videos. Second, health content should prioritize clarity, memorability, and actionable guidance, rather than relying on abstract or overly technical language. Third, platforms should establish more comprehensive health content review mechanisms and optimize recommendation algorithms to improve the visibility and coverage of professional content while ensuring dissemination efficiency. Through the joint efforts of uploaders, content forms, and platform mechanisms, the development of health communication on social media can be achieved.

This study has several limitations. Although two independent raters and a third adjudicator reviewed the videos, subjective bias may persist. The assessment tools used capture specific aspects of quality but cannot fully reflect the complexity of health communication. Recent work has introduced graphical methods that integrate multiple metrics, offering potential value for future research [22]. In addition, video quality was assessed from an expert perspective only, without incorporating audience feedback or comprehension. Second, multiple pairwise comparisons were performed without correction, potentially inflating Type I error. Third, certain data points (such as view counts on TikTok) were unavailable due to platform restrictions, limiting the comparability of user engagement metrics. Moreover, TikTok videos were significantly shorter than those on YouTube and Bilibili, which may have introduced variation in content depth and presentation style. Video retrieval was also limited to a single time point (February 21, 2025), so dynamic updates may have led to the omission of relevant content. Lastly, while both Chinese and English videos were included, the majority of content was designed for Chinese audiences. Differences in information-seeking behavior and platform usage across cultural and regional contexts may have affected the generalizability of findings. Future research should consider a more linguistically diverse sample to enhance global applicability.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the quality of cervical cancer-related content across three major video platforms. YouTube and Bilibili offered more reliable and professionally structured content, whereas TikTok, despite its broader reach, featured more personal narratives . The professional background of uploaders was closely associated with video quality, yet such content did not necessarily achieve higher engagement. These findings suggest that improving the effectiveness of health communication on social media requires not only stronger quality oversight but also broader collaboration among professionals, content creators, and platform administrators. Such efforts may contribute to more effective information dissemination and better public health education.
