Geometry quantification for growth assessment of abdominal aortic aneurysms under surveillance
Authors: Juan C Restrepo, Pratik Mitra, Haehwan Park, Seungik Baek, Victor De Oliveira, Satish C Muluk, Mark K Eskandari, Vikram S Kashyap, Ender A Finol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbdominal Aortic Aneurysms (AAAs) are localized expansions of the abdominal aorta with complex growth patterns, which offer challenges in accurately characterizing aneurysm growth and morphological evolution using traditional maximum diameter assessments. Alternative geometric indices and advanced modeling techniques have emerged to address the limitations inherent to diameter-based metrics. The objective of the present work is to elucidate AAA growth dynamics by comparing linear and exponential growth models and examining their correlations with changes in a geometry-driven rupture proxy index (GDRPI), emphasizing additional geometric indices beyond . In a cohort of 40 patients, exponential models exhibited strong correlations with multiple geometric indices, with aneurysm volume (V) and thrombus-related indices demonstrating high predictive relevance. Thrombus-related indices strongly correlated with the maximum value of GDRPI, highlighting their potential utility as supplementary surveillance markers. Growth trajectories showed substantial inter-patient variability; aneurysms with mean volumes exceeding 75 predominantly followed linear growth, while smaller aneurysms with mean volumes of 50 displayed ambiguous growth patterns. Mixed effects techniques significantly improved growth prediction accuracy (mean for linear growth models and for exponential growth models). Although linear models slightly outperformed exponential models, both effectively described patient-specific AAA progression when used with mixed effects techniques. Integrating thrombus-related indices and mixed effects growth modeling offers a promising pathway for enhanced geometric characterization of AAAs under surveillance, potentially improving longitudinal growth assessment and individual monitoring strategies.Keywords: Abdominal Aortic Aneurysms, Growth, Geometry-conditioned wall stress, Geometry-driven rupture proxy index, Mixed effects modelsSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

AAAs are localized dilatations of the abdominal aorta that represent a silent, but potentially devastating, vascular condition, often unnoticed until they reach a critical stage of development. They are typically found between the renal arteries and the aorto-iliac bifurcation and gradually weaken the vessel wall. AAAs are commonly diagnosed in people between 55 and 85 years old, with a higher incidence in Caucasian men older than 60 and with a history of smoking1. The risk of having an AAA increases with age and familial history. Smoking is a significant risk factor2; current smokers are more than seven times more likely to develop an AAA compared to non-smokers. Furthermore, each year of smoking increases the relative risk of having an AAA by 3.

The clinical management of AAAs relies on the measurement of the maximum transverse diameter using advanced imaging modalities such as computed tomography angiography (CTA), ECG-MRI, and ultrasound. The Society for Vascular Surgery suggests rescreening after 10 years of aortic diameters between 2.5 cm and 3 cm. Surgical intervention is recommended when the aneurysm exceeds 5.5 cm in men and between 5 and 5.4 cm in women4. When rapid growth is observed, defined by a growth rate greater than 1 cm/year, immediate referral to a vascular surgeon is recommended5. Between surveillance periods, a small diameter does not require immediate surgical intervention, but continued monitoring is required to characterize aneurysm growth and morphological evolution over time. During these periods, the maximum diameter4,6,7 is measured over time to assess growth. However, this may not always be a reliable way to characterize aneurysm expansion, as it does not take into account changes in the shape of the aneurysm over time8,9. Martufi et al.10 and Siika et al.7 observed that not every AAA experiences its fastest diameter growth at the maximum diameter location. In addition, growth assessed by aneurysm volume has been proposed as a more sensitive descriptor of aneurysm enlargement7,11,12.

Fundamentally, AAA rupture occurs when wall stress exceeds the strength of the wall13. Finite element analysis (FEA) is the gold standard for wall stress estimation, for which predefined material properties are required, usually derived from postmortem studies. These properties could differ on an individual basis and be spatially distributed throughout the AAA wall and intraluminal thrombus (ILT). However, such material characterization is unknown in vivo because the standard of care lacks a clinical imaging modality that can be used to quantify the aforementioned properties. Critically, tissue remodeling and changes in wall composition evolve throughout disease progression, introducing temporal variability in wall strength that cannot be characterized from standard CTA imaging alone.14 As an alternative to traditional FEA, multiple methods have been proposed to estimate AAA wall stress, thus overcoming the need for patient-specific material properties15–18. For example, a nonlinear elastic membrane analysis (NEMA) was postulated19,20 and validated21 by Thirugnanasambandam et al. for estimating wall stress in AAAs by solving the statical determinacy in the AAA wall18,20.

Geometric measures are also used for the characterization of aneurysm growth and morphological progression complementary to those based on maximum diameter () and volume (V). Hirose et al.22 claim to be the first to study AAA growth and found growth curves that exhibit exponential trends. In contrast, Kurvers et al.23 reported discontinuous growth or ’staccato’, characterized by alternating periods of growth and inactivity. Martufi et al.10 introduced the exponential model formulation based on diameter measurements. Akkoyun et al.24,25 demonstrated an improved prediction of future maximum diameter using an exponential growth model based on maximum circumscribed spherical diameters, suggesting that aneurysm growth may occur in sudden and unpredictable bursts10,22,24,25. Conversely, Olson et al.26 and Siika et al.7 concluded that diameter follows a linear growth and volume tends to increase rapidly7 and correlates with baseline volume, while diameter-based growth does not correlate with baseline diameter11. This discrepancy in growth models underscores the complexity of predicting aneurysm evolution, as maximum diameter measurements may not always reflect regions of rapid expansion, and volume measurements have been proposed as potentially more sensitive indices of aneurysm growth11,12.

The present work seeks to bridge the gap between traditional diameter-based assessments and emerging geometric assessment methodologies. Using a surveillance population group, our objective is to quantify AAA growth using linear and exponential models to explore the interrelationships between size and geometry. In addition, we examine the association between geometric evolution and a geometry-driven rupture proxy index with the aim of identifying geometric indices that most strongly influence the rupture proxy redistribution. These potentially useful relationships provide insight into aneurysm morphological evolution and support for improved geometric characterization of AAA growth.

Methods

Study cohort

We retrospectively acquired 140 contrast-enhanced CT scan data sets from 40 AAA patients (10 females, 30 males) with non-ruptured AAAs who underwent at least two and up to a maximum of seven follow-ups during the surveillance program (mean = 3.5, SD = 1.47). The age and sex of the patients were also obtained from the medical records, while the research coordinators deidentified the records before sharing the data with the authors. Subjects were scanned at intervals ranging from 1.5 to 56 months, with a median follow-up period of 6.1 months (IQR: 6–14 months). Data were collected from pre-existing databases at Allegheny General Hospital (Pittsburgh, PA), Northwestern Memorial Hospital (Chicago, IL) and Seoul National University Hospital (Seoul, Korea) between 2001 and 2014. The Institutional Review Boards of Allegheny General Hospital, Northwestern Memorial Hospital, and Seoul National University Hospital reviewed and approved the protocol for data collection request, while waiving the need to obtain informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study. All data processing was performed according to the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Geometry-driven rupture proxy estimation

We performed image segmentation using an in-house MATLAB-based tool (AAAVasc v1.0.3, The University of Texas at San Antonio, San Antonio, TX; MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA) that resulted in three different contours: lumen, inner wall, and outer wall. Specific methodological details about the segmentation protocol are described elsewhere27. The segmented images were converted to binary masks that were imported into a meshing tool28 known as AAAMesh (v0.168, The University of Texas at San Antonio, San Antonio, TX), to generate surface meshes of the outer wall of each AAA. These meshes were refined using explicit remeshing conducted in MeshLab29 (Visual Computing Lab, ISTI-CNR, Italy) to decrease edge size according to a mesh sensitivity analysis for NEMA19. The patient-specific wall thickness, obtained from AAAVasc, was mapped to each node on the surface mesh.

The force-based analysis NEMA was used to estimate a geometry-conditioned wall stress field by solving the statical determinacy for the deformed configuration of the AAA wall. This elastostatic equilibrium problem models the AAA wall as a thin surface, neglecting bending stresses and stress gradients across the wall. AAA models were simulated with fixed proximal and distal ends under a standardized mean arterial pressure (MAP) of 93.33 mmHg because patient-specific cuff pressures were not available for all clinical records. The wall strength was calculated following the formulation proposed by Vande Geest et al.13, assuming no familial history for any medical records that omitted such information. Under these assumptions, the resulting stress estimates are not interpreted as patient-specific wall stresses. Instead, they represent geometry-conditioned stress distributions that reflect the influence of aneurysm shape, curvature, wall thickness, and thrombus distribution under identical intraluminal pressure conditions. Finally, a Geometry-Driven Rupture Proxy Index (GDRPI) was calculated as the ratio of the first principal geometry-conditioned wall stress to wall strength, while the maximum Geometry-Driven Rupture Proxy Index was calculated as the peak value of GDRPI in the AAA sac for each patient at each imaging follow-up.

Geometric quantification and growth assessment

As part of investigating the behavior of patient-specific geometries in AAAs under surveillance, a post-segmentation geometric characterization was performed for each aneurysm sac. In total, 53 geometric indices were calculated for each AAA at each follow-up: 15 one-dimensional size, 10 two-dimensional shape, 4 three-dimensional size, 2 three-dimensional shape, and 4 second-order curvature–based indices, in addition to 13 wall thickness–based and ILT-based indices. In addition to these models, fixed and mixed effect32 techniques can be used to study global and subject-specific variations. Growth modeling using fixed effects only incorporates population-level information, while mixed effects also consider random behavior, capturing individual-level variations. In this context, the fixed effects models represent the population-level regression trend, estimating the average intercept and growth rate for all patients. Mixed effects models extend this trend by incorporating subject-specific random intercepts and slopes, thereby modeling individual deviations from the population-level trend. Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis carried out in this study is organized into two distinct components. First, we investigated the association between and geometric indices to identify those that exhibit the strongest correlations within longitudinal surveillance; some of these geometric indices serve as a response Y for the models in Table 1. This analysis was designed to examine how different geometric indices relate to the rupture proxy redistribution under standardized assumptions. We also quantified the interrelationships that govern the growth of these geometric indices and assessed their normality and skewness using the Shapiro–Wilk33 and skewness tests, respectively. We measured associations using Spearman’s34 rank correlation coefficient for all available scans to identify the monotonic relationship between pairs of variables. Following Akkoyun et al.24, we interpreted the correlation coefficients in the intervals of 1.00–0.90, 0.90–0.75, 0.75–0.50, 0.50–0.25, and 0.25–0.00 as very high, high, moderate, weak and without association, respectively. We used a threshold of to highlight at least a moderate association for growth trajectory estimation. In addition, was measured for five different groups according to two criteria, sex and size, resulting in four subgroups (male, female, small, and large AAA) and the complete sample. Small AAAs are defined by < 5.5 cm (at the last imaging follow-up) and large AAAs otherwise.

Second, we evaluated the growth trajectories of the most highly correlated geometric indices using the fixed- and mixed effects techniques described in Table 1. These models were fitted in MATLAB with the indices expressed in two ways: absolute measures (e.g., the raw diameters (mm) or volumes () measured from each CT follow-up) and relative measures, which were obtained by normalizing every index at each follow-up to the first CT exam so that each relative baseline is equal to 1. Comparison of the goodness-of-fit with the coefficient of determination () allowed us to discern whether normalization improved model performance. A deeper assessment was performed for and V, examining how each model parameter behaved under absolute versus relative scaling. Lastly, we validated the models with quantile regression35 using relative measures for and V to characterize dispersion, and studied the time evolution of and V with mixed effects using the package R (nlme)36. Statistical significance for all analyses was set at .

Finally, we used leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) to assess the out-of-sample performance of two growth models (Linear ME and Exponential ME) for the absolute and relative measures of and V. The goal was to the effectiveness of each model in reproducing the growth distribution via quantile regression. The steps involved in LOOCV are: (1) Data splitting: in each round, the training set included all patients except one; the test set comprised only the held-out patient. (2) Iteration: the process was repeated until every patient had served once as the test set (i.e., we left out patients, not individual follow-ups). (3) Model fitting: in each round, both growth models were fit on the training set. (4) Prediction: for the held-out patient, we predicted the 5th, 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, 90th, and 95th conditional quantiles () at the last observation, using the first observation (baseline) as input. (5) Scoring: for each , we computed the pinball (check) loss on the held-out data (primary metric) and the coverage (fraction of held-out outcomes below the predicted -quantile). A well-calibrated model yields coverage near (within sampling variability) and low pinball loss.

Discussion

Clinical management guidelines for AAAs, primarily based on the maximum transverse diameter (), have previously been under scrutiny37–39. To address the limitation of such guidelines, Akkoyun et al.25 and Ristl et al.40 introduced linear and exponential regression models for and extended these models to V. Recent findings by Siika et al.7 indicate that AAA growth appears to be orderly and continuous when measured by and V. Olson et al.26 and Siika et al.7 postulated that AAA growth patterns follow a linear behavior, in contrast to the work of Akkoyun et al.25 and Hirose et al.22, who demonstrated an exponential growth pattern. Given this discrepancy in AAA growth patterns in the literature, the present work aims to establish a relation between linear and exponential growth patterns at the global and local scales. To this end, we examined associations between geometric growth descriptors and a geometry-driven rupture proxy index (GDRPI) computed under standardized assumptions to identify geometric indices that most strongly influence rupture proxy redistribution. We analyzed AAA growth and GDRPI in a cohort of 40 patients under surveillance for whom we identified 12 geometric indices highly and moderately correlated with and growth. We further studied their variation over time and established mathematical relationships using FE and ME techniques and quantile regression analysis.

Initially, we quantified the correlation of the 12 most significant geometric indices to identify the measure with the strongest correlation with . Our findings indicate that the four highest correlated indices are thrombus-related measures, suggesting that ILT-related geometry is strongly associated with geometry-driven rupture proxy redistribution calculated for standard mean arterial pressure. We may infer that there is a need for potential monitoring of ILT formation when such information is available during surveillance follow-ups. Of these measures, represents the maximum ILT thickness, which occurs in a highly localized area, while represents the average ILT thickness throughout the AAA sac. Similarly, (the index with the highest correlation with ) represents the volume of ILT within the sac. The ratio of ILT volume to sac volume, , was also highly correlated with . The fifth most significant index was the maximum distance between the lumen and outer wall centroids, , which provides an indirect assessment of the preferential growth of ILT towards one side of the sac (typically along the anterior wall). This pattern of ILT formation could lead to a greater wall stretch that makes the wall thinner on the anterior side, affecting its structural integrity and stability, thus increasing the risk of rupture41. The exact role of thrombus-related measures in AAA evolution remains an active area of investigation, and the present results should be interpreted as geometric associations rather than patient-specific rupture risk or wall failure predictions, as the pathobiomechanical role of ILT remains controversial7,42,43.

The growth rate was calculated using linear and exponential models with absolute measures of the 12 aforementioned geometric indices to assess possible differences between these indices to characterize AAA growth. We evaluated the correlation of these indices with the model type and observed higher correlations with the exponential growth model compared to the linear one. Our findings show that volumetric growth is highly correlated with growth assessed with maximum diameter, which is in agreement with25. We also observed that was correlated with and surface area . Similarly, was correlated with . Of the 12 geometric indices, only three were highly correlated, with S having the highest overall correlation. The work of Tzirakis et al.44 supports the notion that the average surface growth rate of an AAA is strongly correlated with volume growth rather than maximum diameter growth, which is consistent with our findings. However, the study also found that the surface growth of AAAs is not uniform and the best correlations were obtained between the maximum diameter and volume-based growth rates. When there are localized points of growth, volumetric growth could be a better measure of the growth rate as it encompasses the entire AAA sac.10,30

The present work is subject to several important limitations. The human subjects population involved a relatively small sample size in which the majority of patients were male and details on pre-existing comorbidities were unavailable. Furthermore, the number of CTA screenings varied among patients. For patients with only two screenings, it is important to acknowledge that the growth rate could be linear or non-linear, while the latter is not possible to prove without additional imaging data. Conducting prospective surveillance studies with multiple follow-ups at equally spaced intervals could address this limitation, which would lead to a better understanding of growth curves and improve characterization of future AAA growth trajectories. Limitations of the NEMA-based calculation of GDRPI was the absence of patient-specific blood pressure and unavailability of consistent patient-specific familial history, leading to the assumption of a standard MAP and no family-history indicator in the absence of such data. For two patients of the same sex and identical AAAs, the one with a positive family history would have a globally weaker wall by approximately 12 N/ compared to a patient without a family history.13 For the assumptions in this work, the absence of family history of AAA in the clinical records would result in slightly different GDRPI distributions and potentially lower values. The retrospectively acquired CTA exams were not gated and therefore their correspondence to a precise point in the cardiac cycle is unknown. This resulted in reconstructed AAA geometries that may not necessarily correspond to the standard MAP. The lack of patient-specific blood pressure translates into geometry-conditioned wall stress and GDRPI spatial patterns that are indeed not patient specific. Therefore, the reported values of GDRPI should be interpreted as geometry-driven proxies under standardized assumptions rather than patient-specific biomechanical states. Aneurysm wall strength and failure properties may evolve over time due to biologically driven tissue remodeling processes that are not captured by standardized assumptions or population-averaged strength models, and therefore patient-specific wall weakening cannot be inferred from geometry alone.14 Consequently, the correlations described in Figure 3 (a) effectively represent associations between changes in geometry and changes in the geometry-driven rupture proxy. Furthermore, the geometry-conditioned wall stress distributions estimated by NEMA are based on the absence of bending stresses and stress gradients through the AAA wall. Although wall stress could have been estimated with FEA, the latter is also subject to the assumption of population-averaged tissue properties for the wall and ILT. Future work in AAAs under surveillance should focus on reexamining the present findings using a larger and more diverse population sample, while incorporating comprehensive clinical data and patient demographics. A larger data set would improve the generalizability and reliability of the results herein. Similarly, future analyses could explore the use of artificial intelligence tools, where the independent contribution of each geometric index highly correlated with can be assessed to further enhance the interpretability and performance of geometry-driven growth models.

Conclusion

For AAAs under surveillance, there is a strong correlation between changes in thrombus-related geometric indices and changes in the geometry-driven rupture proxy index (GDRPI), particularly compared to changes in size-related indices such as and V. These findings indicate that thrombus-related geometric indices are strongly associated with geometry-driven rupture proxy redistribution calculated for a standardized mean arterial pressure. Thus, the change in thrombus-related indices may offer a more comprehensive understanding of the evolution of aneurysm morphology over time, beyond traditional size-based descriptors. There is significant patient-specific variability in the growth of AAAs under surveillance, which can be represented by incorporating mixed effects techniques with growth rate models. Normalizing geometric indices with respect to a baseline value is not sufficient for growth rate models to adequately represent this individual variability. Mixed effects formulations using linear or exponential growth models provide a robust framework for characterizing subject-specific geometric growth trajectories, while maintaining population-level interpretability.
