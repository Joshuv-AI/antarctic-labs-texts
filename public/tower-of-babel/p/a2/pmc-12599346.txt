Awareness, uptake, and barriers to vaccination against respiratory tract infections in patients with diabetes mellitus: A multi-center study in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Abdulelah M. Aldhahir, Mohammed M. Alyami, Ahmed H. Alasimi, Amirah M. Alqahtani, Roaa E. Mousa, Shahad A. Alqahtani, Lubna M. Alhalbub, Ozuf A. Mohammed, Saleh M. Alosaimi, Ali S. AlQahtani, Abdullah A. Alqarni, Jaber S. Alqahtani, Hussin Albargi, Maryam M. Almulhem, Rayan A. Siraj, Hassan Alwafi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetes mellitus (DM) predisposes individuals to respiratory tract infections (RTIs), making vaccination an essential preventive measure; however, evidence regarding vaccination awareness, uptake, and barriers among DM patients in Saudi Arabia is limited. This study aimed to assess the awareness, uptake, and barriers related to influenza, pneumococcal, and COVID-19 vaccinations among adults with DM in Saudi Arabia. A cross-sectional survey was conducted between December 2023 and March 2025 among 1,556 adults with type 1 and type 2 DM attending endocrinology clinics across three sites. A structured questionnaire evaluated awareness, uptake, and barriers, and data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and chi-square tests. Uptake rates were 60.8% for influenza, 88.7% for COVID-19, and 27.1% for pneumococcal vaccines, with pneumococcal vaccination lower in type 1 than type 2 DM (23.2% vs. 28.5%; p = .002) and COVID-19 vaccination higher in type 2 DM (90.1% vs. 84.6%; p = .001). Influenza infection was more common in type 2 DM (58.6% vs. 52.4%; p = .030), while pneumonia was less frequent in type 1 DM (10.2% vs. 16.3%; p = .003). Overall, 82.6% recognized the purpose of vaccination and 70.4% agreed that DM patients should be vaccinated, though misconceptions persisted, especially regarding pneumococcal vaccine efficacy. Healthcare providers were the main information source for influenza (45.6%) and pneumococcal vaccines (23.6%), while social media predominated for COVID-19 (49.1%). Despite reasonable awareness, vaccine uptake remains suboptimal, highlighting the need for targeted education and stronger healthcare provider engagement.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a major global public health concern, affecting an estimated 537 million adults worldwide in 2024 and projected to reach 853 million by 2050.1 Individuals with DM are particularly vulnerable to respiratory infections such as influenza, pneumonia, and COVID-19, leading to increased morbidity, hospitalization, and mortality compared with non-diabetic populations.2–5 Evidence indicates that diabetic patients are more likely to experience severe disease, intensive care admission, and complications that further impair glycemic control, ultimately worsening their overall prognosis.6,7

Vaccination against influenza and pneumococcus is particularly impactful in individuals with diabetes, significantly reducing morbidity, mortality, and lowering hospitalization rates, although uptake remains variable.8,9 International guidelines strongly recommend routine immunization against seasonal influenza, pneumococcal disease, and COVID-19 for diabetic patients.10–12 In Saudi Arabia, these vaccines are provided free of charge through public health programs, eliminating out-of-pocket costs for recipients. The national COVID-19 vaccination campaign, launched on December 17, 2020, offered vaccines to all citizens and residents via the Sehaty and Tawakkalna platforms, with extensive public health endorsement.13,14 Similarly, annual influenza and pneumococcal vaccinations are recommended for high-risk groups, including individuals with chronic conditions such as diabetes, and both are incorporated into the national immunization schedule.15 The recommended regimen for adults typically includes one dose of the 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13), followed by a 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23) at least one year later.16

Despite these recommendations, vaccination uptake in individuals with DM remains suboptimal worldwide. For instance, a US national study found that among adults aged 19–64 years with DM, only 38.7% had received an influenza vaccine and only 13.6% had received the pneumococcal vaccine, despite their eligibility as an at-risk population.17 This gap is not limited to Western countries. In fact, barriers such as limited awareness, fear of side effects, vaccine misinformation, and a lack of healthcare provider recommendations continue to prevent widespread vaccine acceptance and coverage in this vulnerable population globally.18,19

In Saudi Arabia, the prevalence of DM is among the highest in the world, affecting approximately 18.3% of the adult population.20 It is particularly crucial to address preventive health gaps, such as vaccination. Several recent studies conducted in Saudi Arabia have highlighted low vaccination rates among patients with DM. For example, a multicenter study involving 13 hospitals found that while 85.9% of patients with DM had received at least one dose of the COVID-19 vaccine, only 34.8% had received the influenza vaccine, and uptake of the pneumococcal vaccine was almost negligible.21 In addition, they determined key barriers to vaccination, including a lack of knowledge, insufficient physician recommendations, and concerns about vaccine safety. Another cross-sectional study conducted in Saudi Arabia revealed that 34.7% of patients with DM had received the COVID-19 vaccine. However, there was hesitancy driven by concerns over rapid development, limited trials, and genetic risks.22 Further study conducted in Taif showed that only 43.5% of patients diagnosed with DM received influenza vaccinations. The study proved that age, educational level, and belief in vaccine efficacy significantly influenced the uptake.23

Although these findings offer valuable insights, most studies in Saudi Arabia have focused on single vaccines or have been limited to specific regions. There is a lack of representative data assessing awareness, uptake, and barriers related to respiratory disease vaccines among adults with diabetes. Identifying vaccine-related gaps among adults with diabetes is key to improving preventive care and reducing the impact of respiratory infections in this high-risk group of patients. Such data will help inform policy and practice and enhance preventive care delivery for DM populations in Saudi Arabia. Therefore, the aim of this study is to assess the awareness, uptake, and barriers related to vaccination against respiratory diseases, specifically influenza, pneumococcal disease, and COVID-19, in adults with DM in southern and western regions of Saudi Arabia.

Materials and methods

Study design and study population

A cross-sectional survey was administered to diabetic patients attending endocrinology clinics across three sites in Saudi Arabia. The study was conducted between December 20, 2023 and March 18, 2025.

Sampling strategy and data collection

Patients were recruited from three endocrinology clinic sites: (1) the Endocrine and Diabetes Center at Aseer Central Hospital, a major public tertiary hospital located in Abha serving patients across the southern region of Saudi Arabia; (2) the Internal Medicine Clinic at Saudi German Hospital in Khamis Mushayt, a large private hospital serving both insured and self-paying patients from surrounding areas; and (3) the Internal Medicine Clinic at King Abdulaziz University Hospital in Jeddah, a public teaching hospital affiliated with King Abdulaziz University, serving diverse populations from the western region. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older with a confirmed diagnosis of DM. Patients were excluded if they declined to participate, were younger than 18 years, or were pregnant. Prior to data collection, both verbal and written informed consent were obtained from all participants. The study was introduced to patients by trained staff, after which trained students guided participants through the survey step by step, clarifying questions and ensuring accurate recording of responses.

Study instrument

The questionnaire used in this study was adapted from a previously published instrument assessing awareness of vaccinations among diabetic patients.24 The survey was modified to focus on vaccinations against respiratory viruses relevant to the target population.

The questionnaire consisted of six main sections totaling 30 questions: Demographic and Clinical Characteristics (11 questions):This section collected information on participants’ diabetes diagnosis status, gender, age, marital status, education level, region of residence, smoking status, type and duration of diabetes, last HbA1c result, and diabetes treatment modalities.Pneumococcal Vaccine (5 questions):Questions assessed participants’ vaccination status, knowledge about the pneumococcal vaccine dosing, sources of information, and perceptions regarding vaccine effectiveness and safety.Influenza Vaccine (5 questions):This section included questions on influenza vaccination history, awareness of annual vaccine administration, sources of information, and opinions about vaccine efficacy.COVID-19 Vaccine (4 questions):Participants were asked about their COVID-19 vaccination status, knowledge about the vaccine, information sources, and attitudes toward the vaccine.Past Infection History, Vaccine Awareness, and Acceptance (5 questions):This section explored participants’ history of respiratory infections by asking whether they had previously experienced pneumonia, influenza, or COVID-19. It also assessed their knowledge and perceptions regarding vaccination, including understanding of vaccine purpose, belief in the necessity of vaccination for diabetic patients, preferences for specific vaccines, and potential reasons for vaccine hesitancy.

The estimated time to complete the questionnaire was approximately seven minutes. The survey was originally developed in English and translated into Arabic using the forward – backward translation method by bilingual experts to ensure cultural relevance and linguistic accuracy. The questionnaire underwent expert review and pilot testing to confirm its validity, comprehensibility, and suitability for the study population.

Sample size

According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), the estimated number of adults living with diabetes in Saudi Arabia in 2024 is 5,344,600.25 Based on this population and using a 99% confidence level and a 4% margin of error, the required sample size was calculated to be 1,037 participants.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was granted by the Research Ethics Committee at King Abdulaziz University prior to commencement (Reference No. FMRS-EC2024-019; approved on November 20, 2023).

Statistical analysis

Data from the questionnaires were entered into Microsoft Excel and subsequently imported into SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for analysis. Descriptive statistics summarized the dataset, with categorical variables expressed as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables reported as means ± standard deviations. Differences between type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus groups were assessed using the chi-square test. Statistical significance was set at a p-value less than 0.05.

Results

Demographic data of the study participants

A total of 2,003 patients from three diabetic care centers were approached and screened between December 20, 2023 and March 18, 2025. Of these, 447 (22%) were excluded as ineligible, leaving 1,556 patients with diabetes for analysis, 410 (26.3%) with type 1 and 1,146 (73.7%) with type 2. Patients with type 2 diabetes were significantly older than those with type 1 (45 ± 15 vs. 33 ± 13 years; p < .001). More than half of the cohort were male (815, 52.4%) and married (881, 56.6%). Regarding education, 519 (33.4%) held a bachelor’s degree, followed by 395 (25.4%) with a high school education, with no significant difference between the two diabetes groups (p = .09). Most participants were from the southern region (924, 59.4%), and more than two-thirds were nonsmokers (1,068, 68.6%), with significant differences between groups (p = .01). The duration of diabetes was comparable between groups (type 1: 8 ± 8 years vs. type 2: 9 ± 8 years; p = .256), as were mean serum HbA1c levels (type 1: 7.80 ± 2.03 vs. type 2: 7.79 ± 1.95; p = .170). The most common management strategy reported was dietary modification (827, 53.1%), followed closely by oral medications (823, 52.9%). Vaccines uptake and disease history for diabetes patients

In terms of vaccination, 782 participants (50.3%) had not received the pneumococcal vaccine, with a significantly higher proportion among those with type 1 diabetes compared to type 2 (237 [57.8%] vs. 545 [47.6%]; p = .002). Influenza vaccination was reported by 946 participants (60.8%), with a slightly higher rate in type 2 diabetes (697 [60.8%]) compared to type 1 (249 [60.7%]; p = .049). COVID-19 vaccination coverage was high overall (1,380 [88.7%]), but significantly higher in type 2 than type 1 diabetes (1,033 [90.1%] vs. 347 [84.6%]; p = .001).

Regarding past infection history, 887 participants (57.0%) reported previous influenza infection, which was more common in type 2 than type 1 diabetes (672 [58.6%] vs. 215 [52.4%]; p = .030). Most participants (1,327 [85.3%]) had never experienced pneumonia, with this proportion significantly higher in type 1 than type 2 diabetes (368 [89.8%] vs. 959 [83.7%]; p = .003). Additionally, 1,095 participants (70.4%), including 277 (67.6%) with type 1 DM and 818 (71.4%) with type 2 DM, believed that individuals with diabetes should receive the recommended vaccines. Regarding pneumococcal vaccine, a total of 1,017 participants (65.4%) were unaware that the pneumococcal vaccine is a single-dose vaccine, and 1,110 (71.3%) did not know whether it is effective. Only 487 participants (31.3%) reported having information about the pneumococcal vaccine, with healthcare providers identified as the most common source (367, 23.6%) (Table 3).

In contrast, 1,122 participants (72.1%) knew that the influenza vaccine should be administered annually, and 969 (62.3%) reported having information about it, with healthcare providers again being the primary source. However, 640 participants (41.1%) were unsure about its effectiveness.

Regarding the COVID-19 vaccine, 1,193 participants (76.7%) reported having information about it, most commonly obtained through social media (764, 49.1%). More than half of participants perceived the vaccine as effective in reducing COVID-19 infection, 465 (29.9%) rated it as “very effective” and 332 (21.3%) as “partially effective” (Table 3).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of vaccination awareness, attitudes, and practices among diabetic patients, highlighting critical gaps in knowledge and potential barriers to effective disease management, specifically, among patients with type 1 and type 2 diabetes in Saudi Arabia. In addition, it addresses a significant gap in the literature by analyzing multiple vaccines in southern and western regions diabetic cohort. While previous Saudi studies focused on a single vaccine or a specific demographic, this research integrates data on influenza, COVID-19, and pneumococcal vaccines across a broader patient population.21,23 Overall, our findings revealed that although broad awareness of vaccines is considerable, detailed knowledge of immunization schedules and confidence in vaccine effectiveness, particularly for the pneumococcal vaccine, remain suboptimal.

In this study, vaccine uptake among patients with diabetes varied considerably by vaccine type, with COVID-19 coverage highest at 88.7%, followed by influenza at 60.8% and pneumococcal vaccination at 27.1%. These findings align with national and international reports indicating that influenza and pneumococcal vaccines consistently show lower uptake compared with newer vaccines like COVID-19.17,18,21,23,26 The high COVID-19 vaccination rate likely reflects the strong public health campaigns and urgency of the pandemic, whereas influenza vaccine coverage, though higher than previously reported rates of 34–44% in Saudi studies,21,23 remains below recommended targets. Pneumococcal vaccination continues to lag behind despite long-standing availability and inclusion in adult immunization schedules. This pattern of suboptimal influenza and pneumococcal vaccine uptake is also evident globally, for instance, U.S. data show only 38.7% of diabetic adults receive annual influenza vaccination and 13.6% receive pneumococcal vaccination, while a Turkish study reported rates of 36.6% and 34.9%, respectively.17,26 Collectively, these findings underscore a persistent global challenge in achieving optimal adult immunization rates among individuals with diabetes, particularly for routine vaccines that have been available for decades.

Moreover, our results showed that patients with type 2 DM were more likely to have received vaccinations for COVID-19 (90.1% vs 84.6%) and pneumococcal vaccine (28.5% vs 23.2%), while influenza vaccination rates were nearly identical between type 2 and type 1 DM patients (60.8% vs. 60.7%). This trend is consistent with other studies that showed lower uptake of routine adult vaccines among diabetic patients.21,27 This may be attributed to increased healthcare use among type 2 patients, as more frequent interactions with healthcare providers could facilitate increased awareness and administration of recommended immunization.21,27 More importantly, patients with type 2 DM remain more susceptible to infections than those with type 1 DM, even with relatively higher influenza vaccination rates, due to several physiological and clinical factors. Chronic hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation in type 2 DM impair immune cell function and cytokine responses, weakening infection defense mechanisms.28,29 Additionally, the older age and higher prevalence of comorbidities among type 2 DM patients further compromise immunity and diminish vaccine efficacy.8,30 Consequently, despite vaccination reducing disease severity, metabolic dysregulation and immune dysfunction in type 2 DM prevent full protection, leading to a persistently higher infection burden.31

However, awareness of vaccines was notably high, with 82.6% of respondents affirming general awareness of its preventive role and 70.4% agreed that diabetic patients should receive all recommended vaccinations. Nevertheless, vaccine-specific knowledge varied substantially across different vaccine types. For instance, 72.1% of respondents identified the flu vaccine as an annual requirement, while 62.3% reported having some knowledge about it; nonetheless, 41.1% expressed uncertainty regarding its efficacy. On the other hand, knowledge about pneumococcal vaccination was markedly limited, with nearly two-thirds of the cohort reporting a lack of information about it. Additionally, Awareness of COVID-19 vaccination was highest, with 76.7% reporting information and more than half confidence in its effectiveness. These findings align with other studies highlighting increased awareness and uptake of COVID-19 vaccines compared to routine immunizations, possibly influenced by widespread public health campaigns and the preserved urgency of the pandemic.24,32–34

Vaccine hesitancy factors also became evident in our study. Among those participants who had not been vaccinated, a significant proportion held negative perceptions about vaccines: approximately 11% believed vaccines were “dangerous,” and about 10% felt vaccines were not effective. These misconceptions mirror well-documented barriers in other diabetic populations, where fear of side effects and doubts about efficacy were among the most common reasons for refusing influenza vaccination.35,36 In the Saudi context, such concerns may have been amplified by the COVID-19 “infodemic,” the rapid spread of misinformation during the pandemic, and by the lack of tailored vaccine education.37 Although public confidence in COVID-19 vaccines has improved over time due to sustained public health campaigns, a residual skepticism toward vaccines in general persists.22,38 Therefore, enhancing vaccination rates among patients with diabetes requires a multifaceted approach combining clinician-led advocacy, patient education, and digital reminders.31,39 Personalized recommendations and provider training to address hesitancy improve acceptance, while repeated, tailored reminders increase completion.40,41 Integrating these strategies into routine diabetes care can sustain higher and more consistent vaccination coverage.42

Notably, our results illuminated the powerful influence of information sources in shaping individuals’ perceptions of vaccines. The most common source of influenza vaccine information for our sample was healthcare providers (45.6% of participants), while social media was the primary source of information regarding COVID-19 vaccines (49.1% of participants). This divergence shows that when patients receive information from trusted medical professionals, their likelihood of vaccine acceptance significantly increases.43 On the other hand, an overreliance on less credible sources, such as social media, can foster mistrust and contribute to vaccine hesitancy.44 Social media emerged as a powerful force during the COVID-19 pandemic, simultaneously spreading accurate information and misinformation, which led to varied public responses.45 These results suggest that public health authorities should ensure a significant and active presence on social media to circulate accurate and fact-based vaccine information, in addition to empowering healthcare providers in their trusted roles to offer consistent recommendations.

Strengths and limitations

This study is among the first large, multi-center investigations to examine vaccine literacy, perceptions, and uptake for three major respiratory vaccines among individuals with type 1 and type 2 diabetes in Saudi Arabia. The diverse sample and concurrent assessment of multiple vaccines enhance generalizability and allow identification of vaccine-specific gaps. Face-to-face interviews likely improved response accuracy by reducing misunderstandings and miscommunications. However, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and self-reported vaccination status may be affected by recall or social desirability bias. Future studies should adopt longitudinal designs and assess provider- and system-level interventions to improve vaccination coverage in diabetic populations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that while vaccine awareness is widely accepted among Saudi adults with diabetes, critical information regarding individual vaccine schedules and confidence in the effectiveness of vaccinations remains suboptimal. Despite high levels of COVID-19 vaccine coverage, the administration rates of routine adult immunizations, particularly for influenza and pneumococcal diseases, continue to present a public health challenge. These findings suggest multifaceted solutions beyond general knowledge, such as improving patient education, ensuring healthcare providers actively discuss and prescribe vaccines. By overcoming these various challenges, health officials and clinicians can address barriers to achieving respiratory vaccine coverage, preventable complications, and, most importantly, improve health outcomes for those with diabetes.
