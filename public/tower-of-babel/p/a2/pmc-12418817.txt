Serial viral passage and reverse genetics protocol for identifying the antiviral drug targets of EV-D68 antivirals
Authors: Haozhou Tan, Kan Li, Jun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryIdentifying antiviral targets and mechanisms is crucial for drug discovery. Here, we present a protocol to select EV-D68 variants resistant to Jun6504 by combining serial passage and reverse genetics. We describe steps for viral serial passage, sequencing, and viral genome mutagenesis. We then detail procedures for producing and characterizing recombinant viruses. This protocol provides a robust strategy to identify targets from phenotypic screening hits.Subject areas: Microbiology, Molecular Biology, Chemistry

Before you begin

Overview

Enteroviruses are single-stranded, positive-sense RNA viruses that encompass several species, including Enterovirus A, B, C, and D.1,2 Among them, Enterovirus D68 (EV-D68) has attracted particular attention due to outbreaks linked to respiratory illness, especially in children.3,4 In severe cases, EV-D68 infection can result in serious neurological complications. One of the most concerning outcomes is acute flaccid myelitis (AFM), a polio-like condition characterized by sudden muscle weakness and paralysis.5,6 AFM involves inflammation of the spinal cord, particularly the gray matter, and can lead to long-term disability or death.7 Transmission occurs through direct contact with respiratory secretions, feces, or contaminated surfaces.8 Despite its clinical significance, no specific antiviral agents have been approved for EV-D68,1 underscoring the urgent need for ongoing surveillance and the development of safe, target-specific small-molecule therapeutics to combat this virus.

Most reported EV-D68 antivirals have been identified through phenotypic screening; however, the limited understanding of their mechanism of action hinders their advancement.1 Deconvoluting the antiviral target is essential for prioritizing hits for further development. Serial viral passage, coupled with reverse genetics, offers a powerful approach to overcoming this challenge, especially for targets with undefined or poorly understood functions. Specifically, the virus is serially passaged under gradually increasing concentrations of the antiviral compound. Sub-inhibitory drug pressure in each passage allows viral replication while enriching spontaneous mutations, enabled by the virus’s lack of proofreading activity. Mutations that enhance viral survival under drug pressure become dominant within the population. These mutations are then introduced into recombinant viruses, individually or in combination, using reverse genetics to confirm their role in resistance, thereby validating the antiviral target. This broadly applicable method can be adapted to study the mechanism of action of a wide range of infectious agents. We have successfully used this approach to validate several EV-D68 antivirals developed by our group.9,10,11,12,13,14,15

The 2C protein is a prominent antiviral drug target due to its high sequence conservation across enteroviruses and its essential role in the viral life cycle.16 It exhibits ATPase and helicase activities critical for viral genome replication and morphogenesis.17,18 Additionally, 2C interacts with host proteins to assemble the replication complex necessary for efficient viral replication.16 Several structurally diverse 2C inhibitors have been identified, including pirlindole, formoterol, fluoxetine, quinoline derivatives, and pyrazolopyridine analogs.1 These inhibitors were all discovered through phenotypic antiviral screening, with their target identity confirmed via resistance selection. Here, we use Jun6504—an EV-D68 2C inhibitor developed and optimized by our group9—as a representative example to demonstrate the detailed procedures and key considerations for deconvoluting drug targets using serial viral passage and reverse genetics.

Innovation

The key innovation of this protocol is the establishment of a standardized and integrated workflow that combines serial viral passage with reverse genetics. This pipeline enables comprehensive characterization of antiviral compounds by simultaneously providing target validation, resistance profiling, and mechanistic insights. Through drug-driven selection of resistant variants, sequencing, and functional validation using recombinant viruses, the protocol not only identifies the molecular target but also pinpoints resistance mutations and confirms their phenotypic impact. This unified strategy offers a powerful and broadly applicable tool for advancing hits from phenotypic screens into well-characterized antiviral leads.

Timing: 1–2 months

Schematic diagram of identifying antiviral drug targets by serial viral passage and reverse genetics

(A) Serial viral passage experiment. EV-D68 virus is cultured in RD cells under gradually increasing concentrations of the antiviral compound to promote the emergence and enrichment of drug-resistant mutant viruses.

(B) Viral genome sequencing and cell-based drug antiviral assays. Drug-resistant viruses obtained from serial passage experiments are analyzed using antiviral assays to confirm resistance and subjected to genome sequencing to identify the associated mutations.

(C) Reverse genetics. Recombinant mutant viruses are generated by introducing specific mutations into the viral genome through site-directed mutagenesis, followed by transfection of the modified genome into host cells.

(D) Characterization of recombinant viruses. Recombinant viruses are analyzed by whole-genome sequencing, and their drug sensitivity is evaluated using cell-based antiviral assays.

Step-by-step method details

After identifying mutations in the serial passage experiment, recombinant mutant and WT viruses are produced to confirm which mutation confers drug resistance (Figure 1C). Here, we utilize the plasmid-based reverse genetic system to produce recombinant viruses. EV-D68 US/MO/14-18947 WT is cloned in a pHH21 vector.19 Upon transfection of the plasmid into the RD cell, the viral genome is translated and replicated using cellular machinery. The progeny viruses can proceed to the next replication cycle. To generate the mutant recombinant virus, PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis is performed on the wild-type plasmid. The mutant plasmid is then transfected into the RD cells to generate recombinant viruses.

For detailed cloning of the viral genome into the pHH21 plasmid, please refer to Pan et al.19

Expected outcomes

In the last passage of the serial passage experiment, the drug-treated virus should have an EC50 that is more than 20-fold higher than that of the DMSO-treated virus. This indicates that, after multiple cycles of incubation with increasing drug selection pressure, the mutation that allows the virus to be resistant to the drug accumulates and becomes dominant in the sample. In the example experiment of this protocol, the drug sensitivity of P7 DMSO-treated virus had an EC50 of 0.24 μM, whereas the drug sensitivity of P7 drug-treated virus reached an EC50 of 23.84 μM. (Figures 2B and 2C) This ∼100-fold EC50 shift indicated that the drug-treated virus sample had already breached the genetic barrier and was resistant to the testing compound. Therefore, the virus was ready for sequencing to identify the mutations.

In viral genome sequencing, multiple mutations could occur in certain viral proteins. By comparing the sequencing results of the DMSO-treated virus in the last passage, the mutations that occur in both the drug-treated and DMSO-treated groups can be ruled out as random mutations or cell culture-adapted mutations. The mutations that occurred uniquely in the drug-treated virus of the last passage are the possible drug-resistant mutants. In the example experiment of Jun6504 against EV-D68, no mutations were identified in the DMSO-treated sample, whereas 2C-I112V, 2C-D183V, and 2C-D323G were identified in the viral 2C protein region in the Jun6504-treated sample (Figure 2B, last row). These mutations were likely to contribute to drug resistance, and the 2C protein was likely to be the drug target of Jun6504 in the EV-D68 virus.

To validate the drug-resistant mutants, single and multiple mutant recombinant viruses should be produced by reverse genetics approaches. By determining the EC50 assay of recombinant single mutants versus recombinant WT, mutations that confer resistance to the drug can be identified. Single mutant recombinant viruses r-2C-112V, r-2C-D183V, and r-2C-D323G were produced by reverse genetics (step-by-step method details, step 1-24). By testing the Jun6504 sensitivity in plaque assay (Figure 3A) (step-by-step method details, step 25–26), all single mutant recombinant viruses showed different levels of resistance compared to the WT recombinant virus (Figure 3B). This indicated that all three mutants contributed to drug resistance, and the 2C protein was therefore confirmed as the drug target of Jun6504.

Limitations

Serial passage experiments combined with reverse genetics are powerful and widely used methodologies in antiviral research for characterizing drug targets and resistance. However, this approach has several limitations. First, it is time-intensive—completing serial passaging and validating resistance can take several months. While effective for identifying drug targets, this method alone cannot fully elucidate all molecular interactions between the drug and its target. Because drug-resistant mutations arise randomly during passaging, different experimental replicates or cell lines may yield distinct resistance profiles. As a result, obtaining a comprehensive understanding of drug-target interactions and resistance mechanisms requires substantial additional effort and complementary approaches.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Cell detachment.

Potential solutions

Cell detachment during the plaque assay may result from inadequate swirling during the infection step (before you begin step 16). To ensure the 0.5 mL infection solution consistently covers the cell monolayer, gently swirl the plate every 10 min throughout the adsorption period.

Contamination is another potential cause of cell detachment. In such cases, discard all culture media, overlay, and viral diluent. Prepare fresh reagents under sterile conditions in a biosafety cabinet. Restart the assay using a new vial of RD cells from frozen stock. After two subculture passages, the cells should be ready for use in plaque assays.

Problem 2

No CPE during serial passage.

Potential solutions

CPE should reach at least 90% by day 6 post-infection (Before-you-begin, step 11). If sufficient CPE is not observed by this time, repeat the previous passage, either by reducing the drug concentration by half or by increasing the multiplicity of infection (MOI) tenfold.

Problem 3

No CPE after viral genome transfection.

Potential solutions

The titer of the recombinant virus may be low following transfection (see step-by-step method details, step 15). To increase viral yield, collect the culture supernatant on day 5 post-transfection and amplify the virus in fresh cell culture.

When transfecting mutant recombinant viruses, note that specific mutations may be lethal or significantly impair viral fitness, potentially preventing recovery of the virus.

Always include a wild-type (WT) virus transfection as a positive control and a PBS-only transfection as a negative control. If the positive control does not produce CPE, the transfection was unsuccessful. In such cases, optimize transfection conditions by ensuring cells are approximately 70% confluent at the time of transfection and using RD cells that have undergone fewer than 35 subculture passages.

Problem 4

Low quality in viral sequencing.

Potential solutions

Commercial Sanger sequencing typically yields high-quality reads for approximately 800 base pairs downstream of the primer site. To cover a larger target region, design additional primers as needed. For improved sequence quality, repeat viral RNA extraction and reverse transcription following the manufacturer’s protocols to ensure higher RNA purity. If Sanger sequencing results show excessive overlapping peaks, this suggests that drug-resistant variants have not yet become dominant, indicating insufficient serial passaging. In such cases, continue serial passage for at least two additional cycles using drug concentrations below the 50% cytotoxic concentration (CC50).

Problem 5

Recombinant viruses show no resistance.

Potential solutions

During serial passage, random mutations can arise throughout the viral genome, not all of which are related to the drug target. Some mutations may enhance viral fitness rather than confer true drug resistance. To accurately identify resistance-associated mutations, it is recommended to sequence the entire viral genome (before you begin, step 31). Additionally, it is important to ensure that enough passages are performed to allow resistant mutants to accumulate and dominate the viral population. A practical indicator of successful enrichment is a greater than 20-fold increase in EC50 in the drug-treated group compared to the DMSO control in the final passage.
