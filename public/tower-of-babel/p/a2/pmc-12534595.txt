Hepatitis B virus infection and associated risk factors among mothers attending public health facilities in Bahir Dar, Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Mekuanint Geta, Endalew Yizengaw, Kefale Ejigu, Asrat Hailu, Yimtubezinash Woldeamanuel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobally, the burden of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a serious health problem for women that could be transmitted to their newborn infants. Screening and treating at an early stage help prevent the spread of this infection and its complications. Therefore, the study aimed to assess the seroprevalence and associated risk factors of HBV infection among mothers attending public health facilities in Bahir Dar, Northwest Ethiopia. A cross-sectional study was conducted in a total of 325 mothers attending Bahir Dar city public health facilities from December 2023 to June 2024. Data on sociodemographic characteristics and possible risk factors were collected using a structured questionnaire, and serum samples were screened for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) using sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA). Data were entered and analyzed using SPSS version 23 software. Logistic regression was used to check the association between variables with an odds ratio (OR) at 95% confidence intervals (CI). P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The overall seroprevalence of HBV infection was 3.4% (95% CI; 1.0–5.0). The history of sexually transmitted diseases (STDs) (AOR = 7.60; 95% CI: 1.84–31.34, p = 0.005), history of contact with chronic hepatitis B virus-infected (CHB) family members (AOR = 20.86; 95% CI: 2.48-175.03, p = 0.005), and female circumcision (AOR = 13.76; 95% CI: 1.63–116.80, p = 0.016) were significantly associated risk factors with HBV infection. Generally, an intermediate seroprevalence of HBV infection was reported in this study. This showed that HBV infection is still a critical public health concern among mothers in the study area. History of contact with CHB family members, history of STDs, and female circumcision were significantly associated risk factors with HBV infection. These findings call for enhanced public health strategies to effectively reduce HBV infection in mothers.Keywords: Ethiopia, Hepatitis B virus, HBsAg, Mothers, SeroprevalenceSubject terms: Immunology, Microbiology, Diseases, Health care, Risk factors

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a significant public health problem worldwide, especially in developing countries1. Several factors increase the risk of contracting HBV, including unprotected sexual contact with an infected person, sharing needles, sharing personal items (e.g., toothbrushes, razors), direct contact with the blood of an infected individual, and exposure during childbirth2. The global burden of HBV infection is particularly concerning for mothers, as transmission primarily occurs through unprotected sexual activity, including the risk factors mentioned above1.

In pregnancy, HBV infection has been associated with several harmful outcomes, including premature delivery, stillbirth, neonatal death, low birth weight, and congenital infections3,4. Early diagnosis and treatment of women who tested positive for HBV have proven effective in reducing stillbirth and neonatal death, as well as the risk of congenital infections4.

Hepatitis B virus infection poses a significant risk of both acute and chronic liver disease5. Chronic HBV infection (CHB) is associated with increased morbidity and mortality, primarily due to a high risk of death from cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)6. Globally, the world health organization (WHO) estimated that 254 million people were living with CHB, with approximately 1.1 million deaths in 20221. Among patients with CHB, 15–40% develop cirrhosis, liver failure, or HCC throughout their lifetimes7. CHB remains a significant public health concern in sub-Saharan Africa, where access to adequate diagnostic and treatment facilities is very limited8. This region has the second-highest prevalence of HBV infection in the world, with an estimated 80 million people living with CHB9 and 87,890 HBV-associated deaths occurring each year10. It was estimated that over five million people are living with CHB in Ethiopia11.

Chronic hepatitis B is also a significant problem in Ethiopia, where the prevalence among the general population was 6%12, with variations across different regions. Among pregnant women, the prevalence was reported as 5.44%, causing serious pregnancy-related problems13. This reflects the inadequacies of maternal health services in preventing the chronicity of the infection and its associated complications, such as cirrhosis and HCC14. Effective identification and linkage to care for individuals infected with HBV largely rely on access to affordable diagnostic tools, including tests for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and molecular assays for HBV deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) quantification, which are essential at the primary care level14. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the seroprevalence of HBV infection and factors associated with it among mothers in Bahir Dar, Northwest Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design, area, and period

A health facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Bahir Dar, located in northwest Ethiopia, from December 2023 to June 2024. The city is located 585 km from Addis Ababa and has a total population of 412,361. According to the city health office report, the population includes 218,551 females, of whom 148,450 are women of childbearing age (15–49 years). The healthcare services available in the city’s public health sector include two specialized hospitals, one primary hospital, ten health centers, and thirty-nine health posts. For this study, three public health facilities (Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Addis Alem Hospital, and Abbay Health Center) were participated.

Populations

The source population consisted of all mothers attending the selected health facilities for various reasons. The study population included all mothers who met the inclusion criteria during the data collection period. The inclusion criteria were mothers attending pediatric clinics to seek medical treatment for their children at the selected health facilities and giving informed consent to participate in the study. This approach facilitated access to both maternal and child health information and ensured the availability of relevant clinical records.

Sample size and sampling procedures

The sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula, based on a 5% expected margin of error, a 95% confidence interval (CI), and a 36% prevalence of hepatitis B core antibody (anti-HBc)15. After considering a 5% non-response rate, the total sample size for this study was set at 372 participants. Three health facilities were selected from the 13 maternal and child health service providers in Bahir Dar. The number of facilities selected was determined based on logistical feasibility, including available resources, data collection capacity, and expected client flow to ensure a representative sample of maternal service utilization across the city. However, due to the lack of a complete list of all eligible attendees during the study period, convenience sampling technique was employed to recruit mothers as they attended services at the selected facilities.

Study variables

The dependent variable was HBV infection. While the independent variables included sociodemographic factors (age, residence, education, marital status, and occupation), reproductive health history (history of abortion, number of pregnancies, and pregnancy complications), medical/surgical history (hospitalization, surgery, history of unsafe tooth extraction, blood transfusion, and sexually transmitted diseases (STDs) history), behavioral factors (multiple sexual partners, household contact with HBV, and sharing personal items), and cultural practices (tribal scarification, ear piercing, tattooing, and circumcision/genital mutilation).

Data collection and laboratory methods

Data collection was performed through face-to-face interviews using structured questionnaires to gather information on basic demographic characteristics and potential risk factors. The questionnaire included sections on sociodemographic information, reproductive Health, medical history, surgical history, behavioral factors, and cultural practices. Additionally, 5 ml of venous blood was drawn from each participant using Vacutainer tubes. The samples were allowed to clot and were centrifuged at 3000 rpm. Serum samples were screened for HBV infection using AiDTMHBsAg sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Wanti Biological Pharmacy Enterprise Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The laboratory analyses were performed following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Data analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using the Statistical Products and Service Solutions (IBM SPSS Inc., Armonk, New York, USA) version 23 software. Descriptive statistics, such as frequencies and percentages, were computed, and the findings were presented in tables. Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the association between dependent and independent variables, with odds ratios calculated at a 95% CI. A p-value of < 0.05 in multivariable analysis was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 325 mothers were recruited in this study. The median age of the participants with an interquartile range was 30 (27–35) years. The maximum and minimum ages were 56 and 21 years, respectively. The great majority (88.3%) and 53.5% were currently married and housewives, respectively. The majority of women (52.6%) were in secondary school orhigherin their education, and 28% of women had no formal education. The great majority (80%) were urban dwellers (Table 1).

HBV infection

Out of 325 women screened for HBV infection, 11 (3.4%; 95% CI: 1.0–5.0) tested positive for HBsAg. Among the participants, one (5.6%) and seven (4.0%) women in the age groups of above 40 years and 21–30 years tested positive for HBsAg, respectively. Additionally, two (5.3%) widowed or divorced women and nine (3.1%) married women were HBsAg positive. Furthermore, 6 (3.5%) women who attended secondary school or higher were HBsAg positive. By occupation, four (4.8%) unemployed women tested positive for HBsAg, along with 10 (3.9%) multigravida women who tested positive for HBsAg. Regarding their living situation, nine (3.5%) of urban dwellers’ mothers tested positive for HBsAg. Notably, 10 (7.0%) of circumcised women were also positive for HBsAg. In terms of reproductive history, nine (3.9%) women who had no history of abortion were HBsAg positive, and six (28.6%) women who had sexual partners with a history of STDs were found to be HBsAg positive (Table 2).

Gynecological and risky cultural behavior-related characteristics

About 79% of the women were multigravida, and 95.4% of them had < 5 parity. All women had no history of liver disease or ectopic pregnancy. One mother had a history of intrauterine death. Of the respondents, 29 (8.9%) had a history of sharing sharp materials, and 75 (23.1%) had more than one sexual partner. One hundred twenty-three (37.8%) women had a history of hospitalization. Fifty-eight (17.8%) had a history of surgery in their life, and 50 (15.4%) had a history of catheterization. Regarding risky practices, 93 (28.6%) women had experienced abortion (80, 10, and three of them had one, two, and three times, respectively). All women had pierced ears, 48 (14.8%) had a history of tooth extraction, and 135 (41.5%) women had a history of body tattooing. Forty (12.5%) women had a history of STDs (30, six, and four of them had gonorrhea, syphilis, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), respectively). Twelve (3.7%) women had a history of stillbirth (of whom ten had one and two had two times). Thirty-seven (11.4%) of women had a history of jaundice (Table 3).

Risk factors for HBV infection

In the bivariate analysis, potential factors associated with HBV infection were identified. These variables were subsequently included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis to control for confounding factors. The results showed that most variables remained statistically significant. Specifically, mothers with a history of living with CHB were found to be twenty times more likely to test positive for HBsAg compared to those with no such history (AOR = 20.9, 95% CI = 2.5–175.0, p = 0.005). Additionally, mothers with a history of circumcision were 13.8 times more likely to test positive for HBsAg than those without a history of circumcision (AOR = 13.8, 95% CI = 1.6-116.8, p = 0.016). Moreover, mothers with a history of STDs were 7.6 times more likely to be positive for HBsAg compared to those with no such history (AOR = 7.6, 95% CI = 1.8–31.3, p = 0.005) (Table 4).

Discussion

Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) is the primary marker for determining the seroprevalence and endemicity of active HBV infections in a specific geographical area16. A seroprevalence range of 2% to 7% is classified as intermediate endemic17. In the present study, we found a 3.4% seroprevalence of HBsAg among mothers, indicating that the seroprevalence of HBV infection is intermediate. This finding aligned with national prevalence rates in Ethiopia, which were reported as 7.4% in 201618 and 6% in 201912. Additionally, a prevalence of 5.4% was estimated among pregnant women in Ethiopia in 202413. Consequently, HBV infection is a significant health problem that is endemic in Ethiopia, particularly affecting mothers in the country. However, our findings reflected a lower proportion compared to the above studies conducted in Ethiopia, which might be due to variations in sample size and methodologies, possibly due to the comprehensive meta-analysis of various prior studies conducted throughout different regions of the country.

The seroprevalence of HBV infection observed in the current study was consistent with findings from various studies conducted among pregnant women in different regions in Ethiopia, ranged from 2.3%19 in Gondar to 4.99%20 in Ambo. Specifically, the seroprevalence of HBsAg reported in these studies showed that 4.7% in Bahir Dar21, 3.8%15 and 4.6%22 in Gondar, 2.3%−4.5%13,23–25 in Addis Ababa, and 4.99% in Ambo20. Moreover, the result was comparable to studies conducted in other African countries, where the seroprevalence of HBsAg positivity was reported as 3.2% in Eritrea26, 2.9%27 and 2.1%28 in Uganda, and 3% in Tanzania29. Globally, comparable seroprevalence rates have been documented in other regions, including 3.1% in Shenyang, China30, 3.8%31 and 1.6%32 in India, 3.7% in Pakistan33, and 2.1% in Turkey34.

Our finding was consistent with those from studies conducted in Ethiopia, as well as other African and Far Eastern countries. This might confirm that these regions are classified as intermediate to high endemic for HBV infection. This might also result from a lack of awareness about HBV transmission and shared social factors influencing transmission rates. Additionally, many countries in the Far East still experience an intermediate prevalence of HBV infection due to historical prevalence and limited access to healthcare in certain areas.

However, HBV infection in the current study was lower than that reported in other previous studies in different regions of Ethiopia. Specifically, previous studies showed HBV infection seroprevalence of 6.2% in Addis Ababa35, 7.3% in Gondar36, 22.2% in Debre-Tabor37, 8.3% in Debre Markos38, 6% in Guhala39, 11.6% in Tigray40, 7.9% in Gambella41, 10.9%42 in Southern Ethiopia, 6.9% in Eastern Ethiopia43, 8.5% in Jijiga44, and 5.7% in Borena Zone45. Additionally, the seroprevalence of HBV infection in the current study was found to be lower than in several other studies conducted in other African countries. For example, the seroprevalence rates were 8.5% in South Sudan46, 9.3% in Kenya47, 9.2% in Gambia48, 22.3% in Nigeria49, 10.6% in Togo50, 8.6% in South Africa51, 6.0% in Ghana52, and 18.8% in Sierra Leone53. Moreover, the result of this study was also lower than in studies conducted in some countries: 15.7% in Yemen54, 5.44% in Leos55, and 9.51% in China56. The variation might be attributed to several factors: differences in the study periods, sample sizes, population demographics, sociocultural practices, socioeconomic conditions, and behavioral factors; challenges in disease awareness; the methodologies employed for screening and infection prevention; as well as the degree of HBV endemicity which often correlates with the predominant mode of transmission.

The seroprevalence of HBV infection among women in this study was higher than that reported in studies conducted in Japan, which reported a prevalence of 0.8% in 200057 and 0.5% in 201758. Additionally, a study in Spain in 2020 found a seroprevalence of 0.42%59, and a study in Iraq in 2019 reported a rate of 0.54%60. The high seroprevalence of HBV infection in this study, which was recognized as having intermediate endemicity in the country, clearly accounts for the high rates of HBV infection. Specific risky behaviors and cultural practices in our study settings significantly contribute to the potential for infection, while low-endemic countries elsewhere benefited from effective public health policies that have significantly lowered infection.

In this study, sociodemographic factors such as residence, marital status, occupation, and educational background of mothers, along with gynecological characteristics like gravidity and parity, were not significantly associated with the risk of acquiring HBV infection. However, the history of STDs, female circumcision, and contact with CHB family members were significantly linked to HBsAg positivity. The study population of the current study was relatively homogeneous concerning these sociodemographic factors, which might obscure differences in HBV prevalence. It also might be due to unsafe medical practices or cultural practices, which are more directly linked to HBV transmission than sociodemographic characteristics.

In this study, mothers who had a history of contact with CHB family members were found to be twenty times more likely to be infected with HBV compared to mothers without such a history. This finding aligns with other studies conducted in various regions of Ethiopia, including Gondar, Debre Markos, Amhara region hospitals, Addis Ababa, and West Hararge, as well as in Uganda and a meta-analysis of African women13,15,22,24,27, and38. The increased risk may be attributed to the sharing of personal and Household items contaminated with HBV, specifically toothbrushes, razors, nail clippers, or sharp materials, which could facilitate the transmission of the virus from infected individuals to other family members. A meta-analysis conducted in Iran and India indicated that the rate of interfamilial transmission of HBV infection through close contact could be as high as 44% and more than half of the participants in Oman had interfamilial history of HBV infection61,62.

Additionally, in this study, mothers who had been circumcised were found to be 13.8 times more likely to be infected with HBV compared to those who had not been circumcised. Previous studies conducted in Ethiopia and Nigeria have demonstrated a significant association between female circumcision and HBsAg positivity15,63. The high prevalence of HBsAg among circumcised women might be due to the use of unsterilized sharp materials among multiple individuals. This could be due to a lack of understanding of the transmission routes of HBV, insufficient caution when sharing sharp materials, adherence to traditional practices, or engaging in unsafe sexual practices. As this study indicated, circumcision is among the main associated risk factors for the transmission of HBV infection. The Ethiopian Ministry of Health should strengthen health education and mobilize communities to eliminate female circumcision and HBV infection.

Moreover, in the current study, it was found that mothers with a history of STDs were 7.6 times more likely to be infected with HBV compared to those without such a history. This might be due to the increased vulnerability of women to risky sexual behaviors, such as early marriage, having multiple sexual partners, and engaging in unprotected sexual practices. Various studies have indicated that women with multiple sexual partners are at an increased risk of STDs, including HBV infection24,64. Having a history of STDs, particularly those that increase the likelihood of ulcerative lesions, can increase the odds of HBV transmission during sexual contact.

This study had some limitations, including the use of convenience sampling might limit the generalizability of the findings, sexual history, history of STDs, and female circumcision were based on self-reported data, which involved sensitive topics and might be subjected to underreporting or misreporting. Moreover, this study did not collect information on the HBsAg status of the participants’ mothers, which prevented the assessment of the potential role of vertical transmission as a risk factor. Furthermore, the potential for false-negative results, especially during the window period, in cases of low viral load and occult HBV infection (OBI). Furthermore, due to a lack of laboratory setup and financial constraints, we didn’t perform the HBV DNA test.

Public health significance

The 3.4% seroprevalence of HBV infection in this study among mothers in Bahir Dar reflected a significant community burden, emphasizing the need for targeted public health intervention. Identified factors such as history of STDs, contact with chronically infected family members, and female circumcision highlighted specific socio-cultural and behavioral factors that are still prevalent in the region. To reduce HBV transmission, interventions should include strengthening routine antenatal HBV screening, improved vaccine coverage (especially the birth dose), targeted interventions, such as screening household contacts of HBV-positive mothers, public education on safe sexual behavior and harmful traditional practices, and household contact tracing with vaccination. These measures are vital for reducing HBV-related illness and supporting Ethiopia’s hepatitis elimination targets. Future research should adopt community-based sampling, longitudinal designs, and broaden population to enhance understanding of HBV epidemiology, vertical transmission, and the effectiveness of interventions in the sociocultural context of Bahir Dar.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the current study revealed an intermediate seroprevalence of HBV infection among mothers in Bahir Dar city, indicating that HBV remains a significant public health concern in the area. This prevalence highlighted the need for targeted screening and preventive measures to address the identified risk factors, which include contact with CHB family members, female circumcision, and a history of STDs.
