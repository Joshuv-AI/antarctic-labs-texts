Impact of Microporous Layer Composition on the Water Content in the Membrane Electrode Assembly of Polymer Electrolyte Fuel Cells
Authors: Kinanti Aliyah, Anne Berger, Timon Lazaridis, Christian Appel, Christian Prehal, Andreas Menzel, Manuel Guizar-Sicairos, Glen J. Smales, Brian R. Pauw, Hubert A. Gasteiger, Lorenz Gubler, Felix N. Büchi, Jens Eller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polymer electrolyte fuel cells are paramount for future emission-free mobility. One of the vital challenges for prospering commercialization of PEFCs is water management in the cells. A microporous layer (MPL) is typically positioned between the gas diffusion layer (GDL) and the catalyst layer (CL) to boost the cell performance and facilitate water management. In this study, three different MPLs were investigated, namely, carbon black-based MPLs with different PTFE binder contents (20 wt % PTFE as a base case and 40 wt % PTFE), as well as perforated MPLs with micron-sized pores coated on the same GDL-Substrate (Freudenberg H14). The water content in the membrane, catalyst layers (CL), and MPLs was explored using operando scanning small- and wide-angle X-ray scattering (S/WAXS). At T cell = 80 °C, relative humidity = 100%, p abs = 3 bar, the MPL with larger pores exhibits slightly higher performance than the base case, while a considerably lower cathode CL and MPL saturation levels were observed for the MPL. In contrast, the MPL with higher amounts of PTFE binder shows lower performance than the base case, with considerably higher cathode CL and MPL saturation levels. 3D representations of how the pores could be filled in the cathode CLs were obtained from operando SAXS profiles using representative structure modeling for the different materials and operating conditions.

Introduction

Hydrogen is a promising energy carrier to combat CO2 emissions of the transportation sector. , When combined with oxygen in a polymer electrolyte fuel cell (PEFC), hydrogen produces electricity with only heat and water as byproducts. Water accumulating on the reactants’ path harms cell performance. This can particularly inhibit transport of oxygen to the active sites of the cathode catalyst layers (CL), where the electrochemical oxygen reduction reaction occurs. Hence, it is vital to mitigate excessive water accumulation. ,

The gas diffusion layers (GDLs) aim is to distribute the reactant gases from the flow fields to the catalyst layers, transporting water in the opposing direction, conducting electrons and reaction heat, and offering mechanical support of the membrane electrode assembly (MEA). GDLs commonly consist of a GDL-Substrate (GDL-S) and a microporous layer (MPL). The GDL-S is based on either a nonwoven or a woven carbon fiber material and is commonly hydrophobized to result in 5–30 wt % PTFE polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE). An MPL is commonly employed at the interface of CL and GDL-S to reduce the accumulation of water in the GDL, as well as to prevent GDL fibers from piercing through CL and membrane, along with reducing the electrical contact resistance. − MPLs typically comprise carbon or graphite particles bound by a hydrophobic binder (PTFE) in different amounts. , Inoue et al. found that the MPL relative diffusion coefficient can be described by MPL porosity and an exponent of four-power law dependency. They furthermore studied the dependency of electric conductivity as a function of the carbon conductive MPL additive. Schweiss et al. observed a linear decrease of thermal and electrical conductivity as a function of MPL thickness and GDL substrate penetration. Andisheh-Tadbir et al. and Sadeghifar et al. could consistently model the lower thermal conductivity of MPLs at different compression rates.

Without an MPL, water accumulates, especially at high humidity conditions, at the interface between the cathode CL and the GDL, thereby blocking the oxygen diffusion to the cathode CL. − With the help of an MPL, water accumulation at this critical interface can be prevented. Furthermore, the water saturation level in the GDL is also reduced. , The physical properties of the MPL, such as porosity, pore size distribution, and hydrophobicity, can steer the water removal at wet operating conditions (high relative humidity (RH), low temperature, high pressure, and high current density). − The properties mentioned above can be altered by varying the PTFE content of the MPL. There are two adverse trends when adding progressively more PTFE: (i) the hydrophobicity increases with a larger amount of PTFE, which enhances the MPL’s ability to remove liquid water, and (ii) the reduction of porosity and pore size, which reduces the pore space for liquid water removal. Park et al. found an optimum of 20 wt % of PTFE, while a higher concentration of 40 wt % of PTFE has led to a decreased water removal capability.

Liquid water is preferentially transported through MPL cracks and larger pores, ,,, which are thought to arise from fabrication defects or intentional design, such as pore formers, ,, to help water transport. The emergence of a preferential water pathway is due to the typically submicrometer hydrophobic pores in the MPL that need considerable capillary pressure to be overcome. Thus, adding micrometer-sized features facilitates water removal in the liquid form. Larger pores may also help to maintain higher gas phase connectivity due to constriction effects during partial liquid saturation, where preferentially large pores of hydrophilic MPLs are filled first. Graded MPL porosity eventually combined with hydrophilic domains has been shown to improve PEFC performance.

X-ray imaging methods have been developed to resolve the larger micron-sized pores found in the MPL, and investigate their role in water transport. However, there are only a few techniques that allow tracking the water saturation in the small pores of the MPL (<1 μm) or in the catalyst layer (≪1 μm) operando. As reported recently, the quantification of water in the MPL pores via X-ray tomography, even when pores are smaller than the resolution, is possible. However, in an operating fuel cell, this analysis is difficult to realize for the MPL domain near the CL. Small-angle neutron or X-ray scattering has been previously used to track the ionomer hydration state in in situ measurements. Additionally, Lee et al. have demonstrated the water content in the catalyst pores using small-angle neutron scattering.

This study focuses on quantifying the water content in the anode and cathode CL as well as in the cathode MPL (CMPL) using the high spatial resolution capabilities of synchrotron-based small- and wide-angle X-ray scattering (S/WAXS). In our earlier work, we developed a dedicated operando cell (see Figure ) to conduct operando S/WAXS experiments with the ability to distinguish the PEFC layers in the scanned field of view (namely, membrane, CL, and MPL). Using this cell design, the deconvolution of hydration and saturation differences on the nanoscale is possible. Specifically, this study addresses the effect of MPL material variation on the water content of the MEA by utilizing the operando S/WAXS setup. Two alterations in the MPL design are investigated and compared to a base case of a nanoporous MPL: (1) the introduction of larger micron-sized pores through a pore-former and (2) the incorporation of a higher content of PTFE binder. These alterations would either facilitate (i.e., promote higher performance) or exacerbate (i.e., impair performance) liquid water removal through the MPL, respectively. The resulting liquid water saturation for the catalyst layer, the MPL, and membrane hydration will be quantified. Ultimately, 3D structure modeling aims at the visualization of the water distribution in the cathode catalyst layer.

Materials and Methods

MPL Fabrication

Three different types of MPLs were prepared for this work, as described previously. The base MPL, termed Li100, contains acetylene black Li100 (Denka) carbon particles with a specific surface area of 68 m2/g and an average particle size of 35 nm. A PTFE dispersion was used (TF 5135GZ, 58 wt % PTFE from 3M Dyneon) for binding and hydrophobization, targeting a final PTFE content of 20 wt % in the MPL. The slurry was prepared by mixing the carbon black with deionized water (Milli-Q, 18 MΩ cm), Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich), and methyl cellulose (Sigma-Aldrich) in an ARV-310 planetary mixer (Thinky). The PTFE dispersion was added in a subsequent mixing step. For the MPL with an increased PTFE content of 40 wt %, referred to as Li100 + PTFE, twice the amount of PTFE dispersion compared to the base MPL was added to the MPL slurry.

For the MPL incorporating micrometer-sized pores, termed Li100 + Pore, a pore former was added to the base MPL slurry. Monodisperse poly­(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) particles with an average diameter of 30 μm (Soken MX-3000) were added in an additional last mixing step. The amount of PMMA particles was estimated to reach a volume fraction of 20% for large MPL pores. For further details about the slurry preparation, see Simon et al.

Slurries were coated on Freudenberg H14 GDL substrates (hydrophobically treated by the manufacturer) using a doctor blade and a stencil. After the wet slurry was applied, the GDLs were dried immediately in an oven at 80 °C for 30 min. In a final preparation step, the coated GDLs were subjected to a multistep heat treatment procedure under air, with a maximum temperature of 380 °C. This treatment removes the slurry additives (methyl cellulose and Triton X-100) and sinters the PTFE. The MPLs were prepared to yield a dry film thickness of 50 μm in order to obtain a sufficiently thick GDL-free MPL domain that could be scanned during the S/WAXS measurements.

Membrane Electrode Assembly Preparation

A 15 μm-thick (according to the manufacturer’s datasheet) PTFE-reinforced PFSA-based GORE-SELECT membrane is employed to minimize membrane swelling-related data analysis issues. In addition, the waviness of the MEA between flow field channels and lands was minimized by using a narrow flow field channel of 0.3 mm width only. Achieving sufficient X-ray transmission requires a narrow (1 mm) active area dimension (width) in the beam direction. MPL-coated Freudenberg H14 GDL substrates (Freudenberg, Germany) with a 150 μm initial thickness and 15% target compression were used as anode/cathode GDL. Kapton gaskets with different thicknesses were used to achieve such compression values (75 μm at the cathode and 50 μm at the anode). The catalyst layers were prepared by mixing catalyst powders (Pt/KB: Tanaka Kikinzoku Kogyo, KetjenBlack, 20% wt. Pt) with 1-propanol and ionomer dispersion, and the resulting catalyst ink was subsequently coated onto PTFE decals. The dried catalyst layers (≈7–10 μm thick) with a Pt-loading of 0.1 mgPt/cm2 were cut to the active area’s dimensions of 1 mm × 25 mm and transferred to both sides of the membrane by hot pressing at 155 °C, 3.5 min. Later, also the gaskets and the GDLs (also 1 mm × 25 mm) were hot-pressed (at 110 °C, 3 min) to the CCM for easier MEA mounting into the SAXS cell.

MEA Preconditioning and Electrochemical Characterization

Upon assembly of the MEAs into the operando cell at the beamline, the MEAs were preconditioned using an oxygen starvation-based protocol at 80 °C, 1.7 bar abs., and 100% relative humidity. The protocol contained the following steps: (1) recording of a polarization curve from open circuit voltage (OCV) to 300 mV using constant voltage operation with 50 mV steps and 5 s hold, (2) hold at 300 mV for 60 s, (3) change to a current density of 0.28 A/cm2 and hold for 5 s, (4) stopping of the cathode gas flow for 10 s while holding the current density of 0.28 A/cm2, (5) turning on the cathode gas flow while still holding the current density, and (6) repeating the process until the current at 300 mV stabilizes (see Figure S1 for a representative preconditioning protocol taken from an MEA with a Li100 + Pore MPL at the cathode). Constant voltage operation was used to avoid radiation damage effects while probing an area of MEA, which may become inactive afterward, while the other part of MEA overcompensates.

After preconditioning, beginning of life (BoL) H2/Air polarization curves were taken at 80 °C, 1.7 bar abs., and 100% RH from OCV to 2.2 A/cm2 in constant current mode with 0.2 A/cm2 steps and 30 s hold, with an additional constant voltage point at 300 mV with a hold time of 30 s. The cell was then dried by flowing N2 (200 mL/min) with a RH of 33% on the anode and cathode for at least 40 min before the scanning of the S/WAXS measurements. The high frequency resistance of the cell was measured with a Tsuraga milliohm-meter at 1 kHz.

S/WAXS Data Acquisition (Synchrotron-Source)

S/WAXS measurements were conducted at the cSAXS beamline of the Center for Photon Science at Paul Scherrer Institut, Switzerland. A beam with a size comparable to (or less than) the CL thickness in the membrane through-plane direction is needed to probe only the thin CL. The spot size (FWHM) on the sample, as measured by scanning a sharp edge, was ≈7 μm vertically and ≈28 μm horizontally. An evacuated flight tube was placed between the sample and detector to reduce air scattering. SAXS profiles were recorded with a PSI-developed detector, Pilatus2M, with a sample–detector distance of 2.1 m. At the direct beam position, a 1.5 mm beamstop was placed. The direct beam/transmission signal was indirectly recorded using a detector (Oxford Danfysik CyberStar) by recording the X-ray scattering of the beam stop. A WAXS detector (Pilatus 300k, Dectris) was in the flight tube close to the sample. An X-ray energy of 11.2 keV was selected by using a Si(111) double-crystal monochromator. The beam was focused on the sample by combining a horizontally focusing monochromator crystal and a vertically focusing mirror.

A scanning pattern of 5 lines each with 201 vertical (membrane through-plane direction) measurement points 0.6 μm apart during a continuous sample displacement was employed to capture the different layers of the MEA. A horizontal step size of 28 μm (gas flow channel direction) was used to capture any possible inhomogeneity of the layers in the in-plane direction. The exposure time per scattering pattern was 0.1 s, without a beam shutter closing between the vertical measurement positions.

Before the S/WAXS scans, the cells were equilibrated under H2/Air at 750 mV for 15 min (100% RH and 1.7 bar abs. pressure level, 80 °C). After an OCV hold for 60 s, the cell voltage was set to 650 mV, and scanning S/WAXS profiles were acquired during the first 10 min of the voltage change (referred to as “current jumps”). After another 10 min equilibration, further scanning S/WAXS measurements were acquired (referred to “steady state”). Similarly, current jump and steady state S/WAXS data were collected after changing to 300 mV. The whole protocol, consisting of the sequence equilibration at 750 mVOCV hold −650 mV (data acquisition)300 mV (data acquisition), was repeated at 3 bar abs. pressure, with a drying operation (N2/N2, RH 33%) for at least 30 min in between (see Figure S2 for the electrochemistry time series data during S/WAXS acquisition).

For every MEA location where operando S/WAXS data were collected, dry references (line scans with nitrogen feed gas at a RH of 33% (dew point of 55 °C) both at the cathode and anode) and wet references (line scans with nitrogen feed gas at a RH of 100% both at the cathode and anode for at least 30 min) were acquired as well to enable the quantification of saturation and hydration during cell operation.

Data reduction of 2D SAXS patterns was performed with cSAXS data processing scripts. The noise from the indirectly recorded, direct beam intensity was higher than the theoretical self-absorption from water in the pores (<10% in fully filled case). Hence, transmission normalization was not carried out. Instead, we incorporated precise data acquisition runs between operando data and dry references. For operando data sets with beam flux fluctuation, a calibration of the flux was carried out by normalizing the PTFE peak in the flow field area with the PTFE peak of the dry reference.

Synchrotron S/WAXS Data Analysis

The catalyst layer saturation level was calculated by a three-phase invariant calculation. The invariant calculation was only applied to data falling in the q-range between 0.05 < q < 5 nm–1, after the Pt contribution was subtracted from the data as described in ref . The Pt contribution was derived by fitting the Pt “bump” in the dry and wet data with log-normal distributed polydisperse spheres. The catalyst layer porosity as an input to the three-phase invariant calculation from (transmission-uncorrected, arbitrary unit) SAXS operando-dry data pair is calculated by assuming the packing density of the carbon to be 28 μm/(mgC/cm2 geo). In other words, a loading of 1 mg of carbon per cm2 of electrode area will result in 28 μm dry catalyst layer thickness. The ionomer-to-carbon mass ratio, set by the catalyst ink composition, is 0.65, and the Pt loading from the catalyst powder used is 0.1 mgPt/cm2. Densities of carbon (skeletal), Pt, and ionomer used to calculate the solid volume fraction are 2 g/cm3, 21.45 g/cm3, and 2 g/cm3, respectively. Based on the mass ratio with the components’ densities (and considering the carbon packing density), the volume fraction of carbon, Pt, and ionomer in the cathode catalyst layer were calculated to be 17.9%, 0.4%, and 11.6%, respectively, making the solid porosity of catalyst layer used for invariant calculation 70% (round down from 70.1%). After subtracting the Pt contribution from CL data, the relevant scattering length density (SLD) becomes the SLD of the carbon + ionomer solid phase. The SLD at 11.2 keV of the carbon + ionomer solid phase (obtained from the volume-averaged ionomer and carbon SLDs) for the invariant calculation is 17.6 × 1010 cm–2. The porosity of a catalyst layer could also be obtained by using SAXS data in absolute units calibrated with glassy carbon, although not done in this study.

The saturation level of the microporous layer was calculated by a three-phase invariant calculation as described previously. For the microporous layer, the porosity inputs for three-phase invariant calculation for the derivation of liquid water saturation levels are 82% for the Li100 MPL, 83% for the MPL with pore former Li100 + Pore, and 80% for MPL with the higher PTFE content Li100 + PTFE [all derived from mercury intrusion porosimetry; see eqs S(1) and S(2); see Figure S3 for pore size distributions and apparent contact angle in Figure S4]. The MPL scattering length density (SLD) at 11.2 keV is assumed to be 17.8 × 1010 cm–2.

The separation of hydrophilic and hydrophobic domains in the ionomer of the membrane was judged by the d-spacing of the ionomer diffraction peak. The d-spacing was obtained by first fitting the ionomer peak region with a Porod background (I ∼ Aq –4 + C, A is the slope, and C is the background) plus a Gaussian peak. Finally, the d-spacing was then calculated by taking the reciprocal value (2π/q) of the Gaussian peak maximum.

Laboratory-Source S/WAXS Data Acquisition and Processing

The lab-based SAXS/WAXS measurements were conducted using the MOUSE (Methodology Optimization for Ultrafine Structure Exploration) protocols. X-rays were generated from microfocus X-ray tubes, followed by multilayer optics to parallelize and monochromatize the X-ray beams to wavelengths of Cu Kα (λ = 0.154 nm). Scattered radiation was detected on an in-vacuum Eiger 1M detector (Dectris, Switzerland), which was placed at multiple distances between 138 and 2507 mm from the sample. The resulting data have been processed using the DAWN software package in a standardized, complete 2D correction pipeline with uncertainty propagation. ,

Representative Structure Modeling

Dry Structure Modeling

A 3D representation of the porous carbon + ionomer of the CL is generated using a 5 classes Intersected Boolean model (IBM), similar as that reported in ref . Since the density of ionomer and carbon is similar (2 g/cm3 and 1.98 g/cm3), we assume the solid structure consists of carbon and ionomer in an undifferentiated manner (referred to as carbon + ionomer) to simplify the modeling effort. For each class of IBM, there are two parametersthe radius of carbon spheres and its volume fraction. Based on the two parameters, one simulation box of 512 voxels and 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 nm3 each voxel is populated by the radius of the carbon + ionomer sphere and the volume fraction, labeled as 1 for solid and 0 for pore. Hence, there are 5 simulation boxes in total, for 5 classes of IBM. The final structure of carbon + ionomer solid is constructed from the intersection of the 5 simulation boxes. An intersection of the five simulation boxes means that only when a voxel is labeled 1 in all the simulation boxes; the final structure will adopt the solid phase labeled with a scattering length density (SLD) of carbon and ionomer of 17.6 × 1010 cm–2 in the resulting catalyst layer volume. The porosity of the resulting carbon + ionomer structure must follow the porosity value of the CL, which adds another constraint to the available parameter space.

Subsequent addition of Pt nanoparticles of the final carbon + ionomer is done with a consideration of ionomer thickness parameter, defined as how deep the Pt is buried inside the carbon + ionomer structure, in addition to Pt size (in this study, to mimic real life size distribution, Pt radii of 2, 3, and 4 nm are used) and volume fraction. To simulate burying of the Pt, the accessible loading region (another simulation box) for Pt nanoparticles is defined from erosion of the carbon + ionomer solid, followed by a morphological operation of binary dilation using a ball structural element with Pt nanoparticles’ size. The placed Pt nanoparticles in the loading region simulation box are labeled with the SLD of Pt (126 × 1010 cm–2).

In summary, the Pt + carbon + ionomer structure is parametrized by one radius of sphere and its corresponding volume fraction, applied to each Boolean class (in this case, five Boolean classes), three Pt particle sizes and their corresponding volume fraction, and ionomer thickness. The final Pt + carbon + ionomer structure is formed by simply adding the simulation box of carbon + ionomer, resulting in a structure of 5 IBM classes with the simulation box populated with only Pt. Care needs to be taken that the added values in the two simulation boxes do not exceed the SLD of Pt. If there exist values that exceed the Pt SLD because of the addition of carbon + ionomer SLD and Pt SLD at the same voxel, the Pt SLD is used. The 3D representation of Pt + carbon + ionomer is converted to a SAXS profile using IMG2SAS and compared to the dry ex situ SAXS data of the catalyst layer. 3D dry structure generation is iterated until achieving a good experimental-model fit, usually in 300 iterations, by residual error minimization using an evolution strategy optimizer. The dry structure generation pipeline is summarized in Figure S5.

Liquid Water Structure Modeling

After the dry representation that fits the ex situ dry SAXS data of the CL has been obtained, liquid water structure modeling is the next step to interpret changes in operando data compared to the dry state. In our previous work, we have detailed how to simulate three different wetting scenarios and how changes in the water morphology affect the shape/q-dependency of SAXS data. The three different wetting scenarios investigated in previous work are water filling the smaller pores first, water filling the larger pores first, and the formation of water covering the solid structure as a thin film. In this work, we extend our previous method, increasing the degree of freedom of which pore sizes are filled, to better fit the operando SAXS data.

The new liquid structure generator uses a combination of a local thickness map (already used earlier) to categorize pores according to size ranges and a distance map (new feature) to assign different pore saturation levels. This distance map describes the distance to the pore walls and is used to mimic water film formation. Depending on the designated water fraction of a selected pore, more or less water is added from the pore wall toward the center of the pore. Both maps are created from the initial dry structure before water structure generation starts. Pore sizes are divided into 10 bins, with the size range and water fraction for each used as input parameters for the fitting process. Water thin film formation on surfaces is modeled and included by using only the distance map. Dry structure pore voxels that meet the specified pore size and distance map criteria set by the input parameters are converted into water voxels and assigned a water SLD of 9.447 × 1010 cm–2.

In summary, the 3D representation of the combined solid–liquid-void structures is parametrized by, in addition to the dry structure mentioned in the last section, the pore size range that is filled with water within a certain distance from the solid. The 3D representation of the combined solid–liquid-void CL structures is converted to a 1D SAXS intensity profile using IMG2SAS and compared to the operando SAXS data of the cathode catalyst layer. The 3D liquid structure generation is repeated until a good model fit to the experimental data is achieved by minimizing the residual error of the relative intensity ((intexp,operandointexp,dry÷intimg2sas,operandointimg2sas,dry)−1)2 using an evolution strategy optimizer, usually within 600 iterations. Both dry structure modeling and liquid water structure generation were calculated on a workstation with an AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 3995WX CPU and NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU cards. X-ray Tomography Data Acquisition

X-ray computed tomography (CT) scans of the MEAs used in the scanning SAXS experiments were performed using a Nanotom-m Lab-CT scanner (GE Sensing and Inspection Technologies GmbH) at PSI. The SAXS cell MEAs were imaged while placed in a CT-compatible dummy flow field by using an acceleration voltage of 80 kV and a tube current of 230 μA. For each scan, 1800 projections were recorded during the 360° sample rotation. Each projection consists of two averaged images with an exposure time of 0.5 s each. A 33-fold geometric magnification was used, which results in a final voxel size of 3 μm for the high resolution XTM elucidating the different components of the MEA (see Figure S6). A 10-fold geometric magnification was used to capture the whole 25 mm × 1 mm active area within the field of view. The resulting final voxel size of 10 μm was employed to determine the active area by labeling the images and subsequent determination of the overlap between anode and CMPL and CL (see Figure S7).

Results and Discussion

Electrochemical Performance Comparison

Beginning of life (BoL) polarization curves of the three different cell configurations with three different CMPLs operated in the operando SAXS cell for two conditions (standard: 80 °C, RH 100%, 1.7 bar abs.; highly humid: 80 °C, RH 100%, 3 bar abs.) are shown in Figure a,b. The OCV at both conditions is about 930 mV for all cells. Operando scanning SAXS was measured at two voltages of 650 and 300 mV, as indicated by the star symbols in Figure , to study water accumulations at low and high current densities, respectively.

Polarization curves of the three cathode MPL materials investigated: Li100 as the base case, Li100 + Pore, and Li100 + PTFE, (a) at standard conditions and (b) at high pressure condition. Both at RH 100% and 80 °C. The small symbols connected by lines depict short hold polarization curves, while the stars depict the steady state conditions during the operando SAXS measurements.

At standard conditions at low current density (>650 mV), the BoL performance after preconditioning of the three cells is similar during the short hold polarization curves shown by the small symbols in Figure a. During the SAXS measurements at a steady state voltage of 650 mV, we observed a slight difference in the current ranging from 0.69 to 0.94 A/cm2 between the three cells (marked by the star symbols). The performance trend is Li100 + PTFE > Li100 + Pore > Li100­(base). We assume these differences to be within measurement accuracy in the operando cell. Regarding the BoL performance at high current densities (<400 mV), the base case Li100 (blue) outperforms the pore former Li100 + Pore (red) and the high-content PTFE Li100 + PTFE (purple) at a high current density (<400 mV, ranging from 2.25 to 2.52 A/cm2) consistently during short hold time (small symbols) and steady state conditions (star symbols), with up to 0.25 A/cm2 difference in current density. The better performance at high current density of the base case Li100 compared to the Li100 + PTFE case is consistent with the literature, where 40% PTFE resulted in lower high-current-density performance. The better performance of the base case Li100 compared to the pore former Li100 + Pore case at these moderate conditions was observed previously by Simon et al.

In humid conditions at 3 bar and lower current density (650 mV), the performance of the three cells is again similar (see Figure b). The effect of the increased pressure on the performance is also clear, as the averaged current density of the short hold polarization curves increased from 0.7 A/cm2 at 1.7 bar abs. to 1.1 A/cm2 at 3 bar abs.

Although at high current densities, the polarization curves show that Li100 + Pore slightly outperforms the base case Li100 at 300 mV (small red versus blue symbols in Figure b), this difference is negligible when scanning at steady state conditions (blue and red stars). Simon et al. have previously observed a ≈23% increase in the current density at 300 mV under humid conditions (T cell = 80 °C, RH = 100%, p abs = 3 bar) when using Li100 + Pore materials in a cell with 5 cm2 active area. Such a high difference in performance was not observed in the SAXS-compatible cell. The saturation levels from the two different materials, however, can be probed only using the SAXS-compatible cell; they are still a good indicator to see the variation of oxygen transport.

Out of the three samples, Li100 + PTFE performs clearly worse, both during short hold polarization curve measurement and when scanning in the steady state at 300 mV (purple small symbols and stars in Figure b). Our result agrees with a previous study by Park et al., which has confirmed that there exists a Pareto-optimal content of PTFE in the MPL. The base case Li100 in our study has a PTFE content of 20% wt. and is regarded as closer to the optimal PTFE content. Li100 + PTFE is expected to perform worse, especially at highly humid conditions, as increasing the PTFE content is hypothesized to impede water transport from CL to GDL.

S/WAXS Intensity Profiles of the Different MEA Materials

The through-plane field-of-view of the scanning S/WAXS for our cell/setup is limited by the Kapton gasket thickness, as the flow field material will contribute significantly to the scattering signal. Anode MPL, anode CL, membrane, cathode CL, and CMPL are located inside the field of view (see Figure S7 for labeled through-plane (TP) X-ray tomography cross-section of the MEA using X-ray CT compatible dummy flow fields). Each of the components available in the field-of-view has distinguishable S/WAXS intensity features. Ex situ measurements of the individual MEA components oriented perpendicular to the beam (see Figure a–c) allow for comparison with their features seen in the operando SAXS cell (Figure d–f).

(a–c) The different components (membrane, cathode CL, and MPLs) in the PEFC showing distinguishable features in SAXS profiles, while the inset highlights the WAXS profiles, with through-plane configuration taken ex situ at ambient temperature and pressure, and (d–f) with in-plane configuration taken with the operando SAXS cell under N2/N2, 80 °C, RH 33%.

The membrane’s ex situ profile (Figure a) shows an ionomer peak at q ≈ 2 nm–1, a broad PTFE amorphous phase at q ≈ 10 nm–1, and a PTFE crystalline phase at q ≈ 12.8 nm–1. In the operando cell, the membrane can contribute from two distinct locations during S/WAXS scanning (Figure d): the membrane in the active area of the cell and the membrane under the gaskets, partially exposed to the environment outside the cell (as can be seen in X-ray tomography scans in Figure S6). The ionomer peak at q ≈ 2 nm–1 shows up much weaker in the membrane in the active area of the operando SAXS cell because of the low RH (33%) and high temperature (80 °C) employed compared to the ex situ data. In the WAXS data, there is a carbon (002) peak at ≈ 15.6 nm–1 coming from the Kapton gasket, in addition to the broad PTFE amorphous phase at q ≈ 10 nm–1, and the PTFE crystalline phase at q ≈ 12.8 nm–1 (see Figure c). The Li100 ex situ profile taken with a lab-source shows a broadened peak at (q ≈ 10–15 nm–1) that is not observed in the synchrotron results and may be attributed to a residual Kapton signal outside our area of interest in their qualitative comparison.

The membrane PTFE crystalline peak accurately locates the core of the membrane, in both through-plane and in-plane orientation. Since the membrane is slightly bent between the gaskets and the active area, the membrane’s PTFE peak appears twice in the through-plane line scan: once for the membrane in the active area and once for the membrane between the Kapton gaskets (see Figures S8 and S9). Precise alignment of the PTFE peaks using error minimization between the dry references and operando scans is done to account for and correct for membrane swelling and movement, thanks to a scanning step size of 0.6 μm (see Figure S8). This ensures the reliable comparison between dry and operando states (see Figure S9 top).

A prominent feature of the catalyst layer is the Pt bump at q ≈ 1 nm–1, in both ex situ and operando configuration (see Figure b,e, respectively). The discontinuity between the SAXS regime and the WAXS regime, prominent in Figure b, arises because different detectors are used for the SAXS q-range and the WAXS q-range. Combining data from these two detectors can cause discontinuity, particularly when their overlap is limited, as observed in our study. Additionally, there are two smaller curvatures/bumps in the SAXS profiles of the catalyst layer centered at q ≈ 0.07 nm–1 (real space dimension d of 2π/q ≈ 90 nm), and at q ≈ 0.3 nm–1 or (d ≈ 21 nm). Possibly, the two smaller bumps come from the same origin, which is the primary carbon particles that have broad-range polydispersity (see SEM in Figure S11). In the WAXS regime, the ex situ profile exhibits a broad peak at q ≈ 10 nm–1, indicating the amorphous phase of PTFE in the ionomer of the catalyst layer. The broad amorphous PTFE shoulder at q ≈ 10 nm–1, a less intense PTFE crystalline peak than that in the membrane at q ≈ 12.8 nm–1, and the carbon (002) peak at q ≈ 15.6 nm–1 are more distinctly observed in the operando SAXS cell. The quality difference between the ex situ and operando cell WAXS signal is most likely an effect of the material arrangement in the operando cell, where the bent membrane and the Kapton gasket (carbon peak) are also in the beam path and contribute to the scattering signal of the CL. The Pt bump presence is crucial to locate the catalyst layers with confidence in the scanning S/WAXS (Figure S8 bottom), as absorbance may not enable the CL to be precisely located in the case of low loaded Pt/C electrodes.

In contrast to the CL, the MPL possesses a relatively featureless SAXS profile with a power law decay observed in both the ex situ and operando configurations (Figure c,f, respectively). The feature at q ≈ 4 nm–1 for ex situ measured MPLs may be attributed to the strong peak of Kapton from the samples’ substrates that are not well corrected by the background subtraction. The power laws are similar for all MPLs, and the intensity differences observed in Li100 (base) may come from a slight difference in thickness. The minimal differences between ex situ-measured MPL samples are not observed in the operando cell under in-plane configuration. This may be due to the more significant thickness in beam direction (1 mm, width of MPL) of the in-cell samples compared to the ex situ samples (thickness approximately ≈60 μm), such that inhomogeneities are averaged out in in-cell samples. The most notable difference, consistent for ex situ and in-cell samples between the three investigated MPLs, is the higher intensity of the amorphous and crystalline peaks of PTFE in the profile of the MPL with a higher PTFE binder content Li100 + PTFE (Figures c,f and S10). Again, the contributions from the Kapton gasket in the in-cell data are present in the operando WAXS data at 15 nm–1.

Cathode Catalyst Layer Saturation

The S/WAXS intensity profiles of the nanoporous catalyst layers are further investigated for differences between cell operating conditions at steady state (see Figure for the cathode catalyst layer (CCL), and Figure S12 for the anode catalyst layer (ACL); in this section, we will focus on the CCL). There is a notable intensity decrease from the dry reference [under N2/N2 flow, at 33%RH, see S/WAXS Data Acquisition (synchrotron-source) section] to the cell operating conditions, as shown for the MEA with the base MPL Li100 in Figure a in the q-range of 0.05 < q < 2 nm–1 (130 > d > 3 nm). The intensity decrease can be attributed to water accumulation due to the smaller electron density difference between the CL-solid and air compared to the CL-solid and water. The change in scattering intensity over multiple orders of magnitude can be better visualized when the operando intensities of the CLs are normalized by the corresponding dry intensity.

(a) Scattering intensity profiles of the cathode CL of the MEA with the base MPL Li100 at the four steady state polarization conditions. The relative intensity profiles are normalized by the dry reference conditions to better visualize the changes in scattering intensities for MEAs with (b) the Li100 MPL (base), (c) the Li100 + Pore MPL, and (d) the Li100 + PTFE MPL. The CCL water saturation levels (S CCL) derived from Invariant calculations are indicated in a similar color in each panel for each of the four operating conditions. The averaged curves are from the center of the TP position of the CCL. The saturation values represent the liquid water saturation at the conditions according to the color code.

At 1.7 bar abs., similar changes to the intensity profiles compared to the dry state can be seen for all MEAs (see Figure b–d). The saturation levels are similar at the 650 mV condition, though slightly lower for the MEA with the Li100 + Pore MPL (50.0 ± 0.2­(standard error of mean)%), and slightly higher for the Li100 + PTFE MPL MEA (56.0 ± 0.8%) compared to the base MPL Li100 MEA (53.0 ± 0.1%). The q-range with the maximum intensity decrease (at about 0.3 to 0.4 nm–1) shifts consistently to lower q values for all MEAs when going from low current density (650 mV) to high current density (300 mV), even though the invariant-based CCL saturation levels increase only slightly by 1–3%. For all three MEAs, a plateau-like behavior in the relative intensity changes at q values below 0.2 nm–1 is observed, as there is no change in scattering intensity at these low scattering angles. In our earlier pore filling modeling work, it was observed that filling CL pores from small to large pores should result in an increase of scattering intensity at low q (≤0.5 nm–1 in the case of the PEFC catalyst layer), accompanying the intensity decrease at higher q (≥0.5 nm–1). Since no increase is observed, only a minor decrease at 1.7 bar, it seems likely that the fraction of larger pores filled with liquid water at these conditions is small.

Changing the pressure from 1.7 to 3 bar abs. results in a more pronounced scattering intensity reduction toward 50% and even lower for the Li100 + PTFE MEA in the range of 0.3 to 0.4 nm–1. The scattering intensity decreases toward 75% for the base Li100 MEA and Li100 + PTFE MEA at the smallest examined scattering angles. Consequently, also pores of up to 100 nm appear to be significantly filled with liquid water, and a considerable increase (>10%) of the CCL saturation was found for all MEAs. Probably, this effect is due to a reduced water vapor diffusivity at higher cell pressure. The CCL saturation increases to 74% for the MEAs with the base Li100 MPL and the Li100 + PTFE MPL at 650 mV. For the MEA with the perforated MPL, the scattering at low q changes in a not so pronounced manner at these conditions, and the CCL saturation increases only to 68%, and larger pores will also fill less. There is a slight increase in CCL saturation levels at high current density to 77% for the base case, since the decrease in the scattering intensity is only very limited at lower q values. The CCL saturation increases clearly for the Li100 + PTFE MEA at high current density to 85%. In contrast, the CCL saturation of the Li100 + Pore MEA slightly decreases at a high current density by 5%, which seems to stem from changes in larger pores as scattering intensity changes only below 0.2 nm–1. In summary, the CCL saturation levels follow the order S CCL, Li100 + Pore < S CCL, Li100 < S CCL, Li100 + PTFE for all the investigated steady state operating conditions.

At 1 < q < 2 nm–1, or 6 > d > 3 nm, there is a Gaussian-peak-like relative intensity increase for all the conditions and MPL materials. This may be attributed to the occurrence of hydration in the ionomer of the catalyst layer. In the q-range of 2 < q < 6 nm–1, or 2 > d > 1 nm, the relative intensity decrease is negligible for the MEAs with the Li100 + Pore MPL, visible for the base Li100 MPL, and slightly stronger for the Li100 + PTFE MPL. The change in this q-range may be attributed to wetting in the internal/primary porosity in the carbon. The relative intensity increase at 20 < q < 30 nm–1, i.e., in the WAXS region, can be attributed to the water scattering peak. , The assigned water peak seems to roughly follow the saturation level calculated by the invariant calculation. The water peak for Li100 + PTFE is already high at 1.7 bar abs., whereas for Li100 and Li100 + Pore, it is only high at 3 bar abs.

The saturation level of the cathode catalyst layer varies between 50 and 56% for the driest conditions (650 mV, 1.7 bar abs.) and 77–85% for the wettest conditions (300 mV, 3 bar abs.). Considering that the Pt in the CL is hydrophilic, high saturation values up to 85% can be expected. Little experimental imaging data are available to compare our reported values. In a recent study, Lee et al. have used small-angle neutron scattering to assess the liquid water in the CL. They found a saturation of 20–30% in the CCL, however, at significantly drier conditions (80%RH, 30 °C, 150 mA/cm2) than those examined in our study. When turning to simulation data, Pournemat et al. have found 40 and 70% depending on the local RH conditions (channel or rib) for slightly wetter conditions (70 °C, 100%RH, 1 A/cm2) using a steady-state Monte Carlo approach. We expect that at our wettest condition, more liquid water is formed than that for the data previously mentioned in the literature.

Multiple experimental studies have shown that CL water uptake occurs via (i) ionomer absorption at low RH, (ii) surface adsorption in the smallest pores, and (iii) capillary condensation in mesopores (∼<20 nm) at intermediate RH. The exact RH thresholds and amount of water absorbed are greatly affected by the carbon PSD and ionomer loading. − High-resolution cryo-STEM tomography indicates that some pore volume may be geometrically inaccessible to ionomer and reactants, causing an overestimation of the water volume that is actually electrochemically accessible. Weber et al. (2023) developed a recent model that combines these three physical uptake mechanisms and effectively reproduces the general relationship between RH and ECSA. In this study, saturation levels of the catalyst layer for various MPLS were studied at potentials of 650 and 300 mV, under pressures of 1.7 and 3 bar, respectively, expanding upon previous saturation studies by examining pressure effects. Although ionomer adsorption and thin water films (less than 1 nm) are considered important for oxygen transport, our SAXS study suggests that differences in electron density from these phenomena do not produce significant changes in the SAXS data. Thus, we focus on water filling the pores in the 3–100 nm range.

Cathode Microporous Layer Saturation

The intensity decrease compared to the dry references is clearly smaller in the CMPL and difficult to see with the logarithmic intensity scaling (Figure a). The CMPL saturation values are found to be dictated by operating pressure rather than by current density, such as the CCL saturation values (see Figure b–d). While current density variations at constant pressure result only in 1 to 3% CMPL saturation change, the cathode MPL saturations increase by up to 12% when the cathode pressure is increased to 3 bar abs., though not as significantly as was observed for the CCL saturation (see Figure b–d).

The relative intensity profiles are normalized by dry reference conditions to better visualize the changes in scattering intensities for cell configurations with (b) the Li100 (base) MPL, (c) the Li100 + Pore MPL, and (d) the Li100 + PTFE MPL. CMPL water saturation levels (S CMPL) derived from Invariant calculations are indicated in a similar color in each panel for each of the four operating conditions. The averaged curves are from the center of the through-plane position of the CMPL. The saturation values represent the liquid water saturation at the conditions according to the color code.

The dependency of the CMPL saturation on MPL type is more pronounced than in the CCL but shows a similar trend of S CMPL, Li100 + Pore < S CMPL, Li100 < S CMPL, Li100 + PTFE at every investigated operating condition. Already at 1.7 bar abs., the saturation in the Li100 + PTFE MPL is 7 to 10% higher compared to the base MPL (S CMPL, Li100 = 15%). The perforated MPL, on the other hand, has a 7 to 8% lower CMPL saturation within the pores of the detection range: for SAXS with q ranges down to 0.05 nm–1, only structural features of up to about 100 nm can be examined. Additional methods like subvoxel analysis of micro-CT data need to be involved to provide complete saturation values for MPLs with micrometer-sized pores. The saturation differences increase at 3 bar to about 10% lower values for the perforated MPL, and 10% higher values for the higher PTFE loaded MPL, compared to the base Li100 MPL with about 22% saturation.

There is a small decrease in CMPL at lower cell voltage in a few conditions, namely, for the base Li100 MPL at 3 bar (−1%), for the Li100 + PTFE MPL at 1.7 bar (−3%), and for the Li100 + Pore MPL at 3 bar (−3%). It remains unclear if this is an effect caused by the operating conditions and materials, the spatial variation of the material properties (MPL and CL) in the cell, or the measurement reliability. Given the randomness of the effect, a combination of the last two explanations is more likely.

At 1 < q < 2 nm–1, or 6 > d > 3 nm, there is again the Gaussian-peak-like relative intensity increase for all the conditions and materials. Because in MPLs there ought to be no contribution of the ionomer peak, like in the membrane/CL, the signal may come from increasing ordered structures in the PTFE binder of the MPLs due to the water accumulation within the q-range. Since the binder is made of PTFE, we expect to see a difference between the different levels of PTFE percentage. Indeed, the Gaussian-peak-like relative intensity increase is narrower and more Gaussian-like in the Li100 + PTFE sample (Figure d), compared to the others (Figure b,c).

It needs to be noted that SAXS was recorded only, representing a maximum pore diameter of 100 nm. A large fraction of the pores within the three types of MPL have a pore size larger than the 100 nm threshold, and the pores of the pore former are not represented in the SAXS data as their diameter is in the μm-range (see Figure S3 and ref ). However, we believe that the detectable pore range still gives meaningful results as it includes the characteristic (peak) pore diameter of the Li100 MPL and includes the pore diameters, which are crucial to allow a percolating network, as shown by Bozzetti et al.

The saturations extracted for the different MPL configurations lie in a reasonable range. Chen et al. have analyzed the water saturation for the Li100 (base) case via X-ray tomographic imaging and have reported that the saturation remained below 25%, which is consistent with the 15–21% reported in this study. Nagai et al. have used X-ray tomographic imaging to compare a uniform MPL with an MPL with a pore former. They reported a lower saturation in the GDL-S when applying the MPL with the pore former. In our work, we report a lower saturation of the MPL with pore former Li100 + Pore compared with the base case as well. It seems reasonable that a lower saturation in the GDL-S (as reported by Nagai et al.) is in accordance with a lower MPL saturation. Additionally, we are only able to report the saturation for the measurable pore diameter range of <100 nm, meaning that the water in the large pores is not included in the saturation values reported in Figure b–d. Mohseninia et al. have studied the water content spatially resolved over the components of the fuel cell for CMPL with different PTFE contents via neutron radiography. They reported a higher water thickness (averaged over CCM + GDL area) for their cell with a 40 wt % PTFE containing MPL compared to the cell with a 20 wt % containing MPL. Similarly, we observe a higher water saturation locally in the CMPL and in the CCL compared with the base case.

Through-Plane Water Distribution

Averaging over the different cell locations by scanning multiple in-plane locations, through-plane line scans (see Figures S13–S18) were implemented to obtain through-plane water distribution profiles from ACL to CMPL. The different line scans were quite homogeneous for each condition over the scanned in-plane domains of 150 μm (see Figures S13–S18). The gradient of saturation levels across material thickness in CLs and MPL is lower the farther away from the membrane. The highest CCL saturation levels are observed at the membrane-CL interface and decrease toward the CL-MPL interface. Saturation levels of the cathode CL and MPL generally increase with current density at the same pressure level, and increase more significantly with higher pressure levels (see Figures a–c and S19, for averaged saturation levels versus current density relations).

When comparing the MEAs with the different MPL materials at each of the four operating conditions, already at 650 mV at 1.7 bar abs., the CMPL saturation levels diverge with a trend S CMPL, Li100 + PTFE > S CMPL, Li100 > S CMPL, Li100 + Pore(Figure a–c). Increasing the pressure to 3 bar abs. at 650 mV, the CMPL saturation increases by 5–10% for each MPL configuration. Showing the same trend, the CCL saturation levels of the three MEAs increase by roughly ≈15–20% for each of the three MPL when the pressure is increased from 1.7 bar abs. to 3 bar abs. There are no considerable CCL saturation differences among the cells with the three MPL materials at 650 mV.

The pressure effect is also observed in the CMPL, ACL, and membrane saturation levels, albeit it is less significant (≈5–10%). ACL saturation levels generally follow the trend of the CCL saturation levels, but to a smaller degree. The membrane hydration states seem to be a bit higher with a higher current density at similar pressure levels. The hydration state of the membrane at 650 mV at 1.7 bar abs. for the base MPL may be an outlier based on the 0.4 nm difference in d-spacing between the pressure levels, compared to the 0.2 nm difference for other materials.

There exist different slopes in saturation levels in the through-plane direction of the CCL among the cells with the different MPL materials: Li100 + PTFE’s CCL is less steep compared to the other two (Figure a–c). The slope of the MPL saturation levels across thickness is rather similar for all materials. A larger TP range for most of the CMPL domains is shown in the 2D maps in Figures S13–S18. The slope of the ACL saturation levels shows a different trend than the CCL: Li100 + PTFE exhibits the highest slope, and Li100 + Pore is flatter. The most apparent gradient from the cathode side to the anode side also appears in the membrane hydration state of the Li100 + PTFE MEA, whereas the others are less steep or U-shaped. The U-shaped or sudden increase in membrane hydration only at the CL-membrane interfaces may also be caused by artifacts such as Pt from the CL, clouding the d-spacing analysis. Hence, only apparent gradients also occurring throughout the middle of the membrane thickness can be trusted, as observed in the Li100 + PTFE MEA. This might signal a stronger back-diffusion of water through the membrane toward the anode because of the severe water accumulation in the Li100 + PTFE MEA.

Out of the four operating conditions, the differences between the materials are most striking at 300 mV and 3 bar abs. (see Figure d). Here, it is clear that all the porous components’ saturation levels, S, follow the trend S porous layers, Li100 + PTFE > S porous layers, Li100 > S porous layers, Li100 + Pore, although the membrane saturation values follow a different trend of S membrane, Li100 > S membrane, Li100 + PTFE > S membrane, Li100 + Pore. Zenyuk et al. and Sabharwal and Secanell predicted CL saturation levels as high as 80–90%, similar to those reported in this work, though at different operating conditions, temperatures, and materials. Precise comparison with PEFC modeling results will likely not be possible based on the existing literature, but only with a dedicated fuel cell modeling study for the materials and operating conditions of this study, which is beyond the scope of this work. With a presumably higher capillary entry pressure at higher CMPL PTFE levels, the increased ACL saturation observed in this work agrees with model predictions. The readers may refer to Kosakian et al. and Weber et al. for an overview of PEFC models and modeling challenges, respectively.

Modeled Representation of Water in the Cathode Catalyst Layer

Visualization of the full complexity of solid and liquid water in the cathode catalyst layer is highly sought after and not available with current imaging methods. Representative structure modeling of SAXS data can be used to obtain visualizations of how the morphology of the solid structure and the water contained within the CCL may look and unprecedently bridge scales, if carefully applied and validated against different methods. Although there remains a considerable distance to achieving this vision, we wish to share insights into its current status, challenges, and progress opportunities. To do so, we first fitted the experimental SAXS profile from ex situ data of the catalyst layer, as explained in the Materials and Methods section, to achieve a 3D representation of a dry catalyst layer (see Figure S5). The obtained structural properties and fitting parameters are given in Figure S20. Next, model fits were performed on the operando data, using the local thickness map and the distance map of the pore space as water structure parametrization tools (see Figure for the scheme). The combined strategy follows our previous work elucidating three different scenarios in water-filling modes and how it affects q-dependency in SAXS profiles.

The obtained fits for the data and cumulative histograms of pores filled that correspond to the fits are summarized in Figure . Slices of the corresponding statistically representative 3D water structures are presented in Figure . The SAXS model fits were able to reproduce the major trends in relative intensity changes, although with some artifacts such as a periodicity in the relative intensities caused by too regular structures of the water clusters (see Figure a,c,e). At the lowest saturation levels of all materials investigated, the smaller pores (mainly pores within carbon aggregates, most fractions of pore size <20 nm in radius) are filled first, and only a comparatively minor fraction of larger dimensioned water clusters accumulates in pores between 50 and 80 nm (see Figure b,d,f for cumulative water saturation plots and Figure for 2D representations). This trend would match the fact that capillary condensation is limited to small pores, eventually first near the hydrophilic Pt sites inside the carbon particles. Even though the overall trend for the pore-size-dependent water filling is evident, note that the exact water saturation level in the larger pore fractions must be taken with care. As the larger water cluster dimension is affecting predominantly the fits at smaller q values, a slight mismatch between the experimental data and model can significantly alter the model’s pore distribution in the larger pore size. For example, the fitted cumulative water saturation for all materials at 300 mV and 1.7 bar abs. is consistently lower than at 650 mV and 1.7 bar abs. Conversely, in the experimental data, this is not the case.

The dots in b,d,f are the applied bin centers of the pore size bins when parametrizing the water distribution.

A 2D slice of the 3D water structures corresponding to the best fits of the operando SAXS data of the CCL in combination with the various CMPLs shown in Figure (color-coded to Figure , carbon in black, Pt in red, water in blue, void in white).

With a higher current density at the same pressure level, there is no clear tendency for a specific pore size to be more filled. Increasing the pressure to 3 bar abs. significantly increases the saturation level. In very humid conditions, 300 mV and 3 bar abs., the pores larger than 20 nm in radius remain filled with liquid water. The CCL of Li100 + Pore is less saturated and has a higher fraction of larger pores still empty compared to the base Li100 MPL. The CCL of Li100 + PTFE with the highest saturation level is almost but not completely flooded, with some large pores remaining unfilled, as also shown by the cumulative histogram (see Figure f). Beyond 50 nm in pore radius, the cumulative water saturation plateaus and does not follow the cumulative pore volume. This is also depicted in the cross-section of the model in Figure , bottom row, where at the top left corner, the large pores are not filled. This again may be attributed to a slight low-q mismatch of the fit to the experimental data.

Consequently, to better fit the saturation increase in pore sizes larger than 100 nm (primarily corresponding to pore sizes between carbon aggregates), an improved model would be beneficial; however, it is out of scope and is left for future work. One possible way to improve the model is fitting with larger 3D structures (larger than what is used currently; 512 voxels, each 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 nm3), or by hierarchical structure fitting (e.g., one simulation box of 512, at varying voxel size for each decade in q with overlap q-region). For the mid-q range of the experimental SAXS data in the study, the current simulation box parameter is a good starting point and is computationally feasible to fit all of the data in varying operational conditions. To avoid the thin water film bridges in the fitted water structures, a change from local thickness-based pore size classification to watershed-based pore labeling could be helpful.

Conclusion

Operando scanning S/WAXS was used to examine the water levels of the different MEA components of a PEFC, including the catalyst layer, the microporous layer, and the membrane, under realistic operating conditions at 80 °C cell temperature. Two contrasting material design parameters applied to a baseline cathode MPL (Li100), namely, either the introduction of micrometer-sized pores to the cathode MPL by using a pore former during its preparation (Li100 + Pore), or an increase of the PTFE binder content of the cathode MPL (Li100 + PTFE) were investigated. The operando scanning S/WAXS allowed the differentiation between the ≈10 μm-thick anode and cathode catalyst layers and the ≈15 μm thick membrane in-between with a through-plane scanning step size of 0.6 μm. The fine S/WAXS scanning step size was beneficial to compensate for the CL and MPL misalignment due to membrane swelling by precise differentiation between the dry reference and operando states.

Saturation levels S of the anode and cathode CL, as well as the cathode MPL follow the trend S Li100 + Pore < S Li100 < S Li100 + PTFE at all investigated operating conditions (650 mV or 300 mV at 1.7 bar abs. or 3 bar abs.), with the most significant difference observed at the low voltage (high current density) and high pressure level. No significant difference between low and high current densities at 1.7 bar was found, indicating that the water saturation levels already plateau at the low current densities at this pressure. A significant increase in the water saturation levels was observed when increasing the pressure to 3 bar abs. Additionally, at 3 bar abs., an increasing current density resulted in minor water saturation level increases. Consistently, an increase in membrane hydration has been observed during cell operation at higher pressure levels, based on the membrane’s nanodomain spacing.

The results show that the incorporation of larger pores in the cathode MPL decreases the water saturation level of the cathode porous layers (both CL and the MPL). Though the saturation was found to be lower when using pore formers, its performance benefit reported from large cell tests was only at 3 bar operation at 300 mV present in the SAXS cell. Additionally, due to the decrease in porosity and increase in hydrophobicity by a higher MPL PTFE content, the saturation level of both CL and MPL at the cathode increases due to impeded gas and liquid phase water transport. More specifically, fitting the SAXS data by representative cathode CL structure modeling indicates a preferential filling of smaller pores and only a minor filling of larger pores at 1.7 bar. At 3 bar, the fraction of the filled large pores of the cathode catalyst layers increases.
