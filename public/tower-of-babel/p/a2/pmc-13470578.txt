Bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary tuberculosis and associated factors among adult household contacts of patients with smear-positive pulmonary tuberculosis in Kaffa Zone public hospitals, Southwest Ethiopia
Authors: Daniel Mulat Eshetu, Mikias Getahun Molla, Zebene Ambaw, Aynekulu Baylie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Household contacts of patients with smear-positive pulmonary tuberculosis showed a notable bacteriologically confirmed tuberculosis burden.•Symptom-based GeneXpert Mycobacterium tuberculosis/rifampicin testing identified substantial bacteriological positivity among eligible symptomatic contacts.•Poor household ventilation, overcrowding, low household income, and cigarette smoking were associated with tuberculosis occurrence.•Higher household income and longer duration of index case treatment were associated with lower tuberculosis occurrence among contacts.•The study highlights gaps in complete bacteriological evaluation among all household contacts in contact investigations.Keywords: Smear-positive pulmonary tuberculosis, Associated factors, Adult household contacts, Southwest Ethiopia

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is an infectious disease caused mainly by Mycobacterium tuberculosis and primarily affects the lungs [1]. Despite being preventable and treatable, it remains a major public health problem, particularly in low-income countries [2]. Household contacts of patients with smear-positive pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) are at high risk of infection due to prolonged and close exposure within shared living environments [3]. The risk of transmission is influenced by factors such as the infectiousness of the index case, duration of exposure, overcrowding, and poor household ventilation [4]. As a result, households remain one of the most important settings for ongoing TB transmission in endemic regions [5].

In Ethiopia, TB remains a major public health concern, and the country is among the high-TB-burden nations in sub-Saharan Africa [6]. Although tuberculosis preventive therapy is implemented as part of national TB control strategies, it is mainly targeted at child household contacts, while adult household contacts are often not prioritized despite continuous exposure to patients with infectious smear-positive PTB [7]. This limitation in preventive coverage may contribute to sustained transmission of TB within households [8].

Moreover, implementation of routine contact investigation and follow-up remains limited in many resource-constrained settings, including Ethiopia [9]. This weakens early detection of infection among exposed household contacts and contributes to continued transmission [10].

Evidence on tuberculosis infection and its determinants among adult household contacts is essential for guiding targeted interventions; however, evidence from Southwest Ethiopia remains limited. Previous studies conducted in Eastern Ethiopia, including Haramaya District, assessed TB among household contacts using fluorescent smear microscopy and identified selected household environmental risk factors [11]. However, fluorescent microscopy has lower diagnostic sensitivity compared with GeneXpert Mycobacterium tuberculosis/rifampicin (MTB/RIF), which may influence case detection. In addition, regional differences in household conditions, socioeconomic status, and implementation of TB control activities may affect the distribution of disease and associated risk factors. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the bacteriologically confirmed TB burden and identify associated factors among adult household contacts of patients with smear-positive PTB in Kaffa Zone, Southwest Ethiopia, using the GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay.

Materials and methods

Study setting, design, and period

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among household contacts of patients with smear-positive PTB treated in the three public hospitals of Kaffa Zone, Southwest Ethiopia. Data collection was carried out from October 1 to November 1, 2025, among patients who had received treatment between May 1, 2024, and September 1, 2025. The Kaffa Zone is located in the southwestern part of Ethiopia, with Bonga as its administrative town, situated approximately 449 kilometers from Addis Ababa. Based on the 2016 Ethiopian population projection, the total population of the Kaffa Zone is estimated at 962,188, of whom 474,956 are males and 487,231 are females. The zone has three public hospitals that provide TB-related services. These include Bonga Gebre Tsadik Shawo General Hospital, located in Bonga town; Wacha Primary Hospital, situated in Wacha town of Chena Woreda, about 78 kilometers southwest of Bonga; and Tello Primary Hospital, located in Telo Woreda, approximately 45 kilometers southeast of Bonga. According to the Health Management Information System reports, these hospitals deliver inpatient and outpatient services and provide TB detection, treatment, follow-up, and household contact tracing services for their respective catchment populations.

Population

The source population comprised all adult household contacts of patients with smear-positive PTB treated in public hospitals of Kaffa Zone. The study population consisted of all adult household contacts (≥18 years) of patients with smear-positive PTB treated in the three selected public hospitals of Kaffa Zone from May 1, 2024, to September 1, 2025, who fulfilled the eligibility criteria and were available during the data collection period.

Eligibility criteria

Adult household contacts (≥18 years) living in the same house as patients with smear-positive PTB treated in the three public hospitals of Kaffa Zone and who had bacteriologically confirmed TB based on the GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay were included in the study. Adult household contacts who were critically ill, mentally unable to respond during the data collection period, or clinically diagnosed with tuberculosis without bacteriological confirmation were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination

The sample size for the first objective was determined using the single-population proportion formula based on a proportion of 7.8% from a previous study [11]. The following assumptions were used: 95% confidence level, α/2 = 1.96, and a margin of error (d) of 5%.

After adding 10% as non-response, the sample size became 123.

The sample size for the second objective was determined by using Epi Info by considering a 95% confidence interval (CI), a power of 80, an exposed-to-unexposed ratio of 1:1, and a 10% non-response (Table 1).

Sampling procedure and technique

A simple random sampling technique was used to select adult household contacts for the study. According to the Unit TB Register and Contact TB Register of the three public hospitals in Kaffa Zone, between May 1, 2024, and September 1, 2025, there were 225 patients with smear-positive PTB and 625 registered adult household contacts. The distribution of index patients across the hospitals was 64 at Gebre Tsadik Shewo General Hospital, 109 at Wacha Primary Hospital, and 52 at Telo Primary Hospital. These index patients had 198, 293, and 134 registered adult household contacts, respectively.

The sample size was allocated proportionally to the number of registered adult household contacts in each hospital using the formula ni=(n/N)×Ni. Accordingly, 132 participants were selected from Gebre Tsadik Shewo General Hospital, 196 from Wacha Primary Hospital, and 90 from Telo Primary Hospital. Finally, study participants were selected using a simple random sampling technique (lottery method) from the list of registered adult household contacts in each hospital until the required sample size was achieved.

Variables

The outcome variable was defined as bacteriologically confirmed TB among adult household contacts based on GeneXpert MTB/RIF results (Yes/No). The independent variables included sociodemographic characteristics such as age, sex, educational level, occupational status, marital status, and monthly income. Environmental characteristics included residence, housing type, number of windows, ventilation status, and living room size (crowding). Behavioral characteristics included cigarette smoking, khat chewing, alcohol consumption, and frequency of meals per day. Clinical characteristics included history of TB, previous history of TB treatment, presence of chronic illness, and contact history with a patient with TB.

Operational definition

An index patient: A person who was initially diagnosed with bacteriologically confirmed TB among family members in a specific household, regardless of age, and registered for TB treatment.

Household: People living together within the same residence and who share responsibilities and household activities in the same house.

A household contact: A person who shares the same enclosed living space in the house with the index patient diagnosed with PTB.

Smear-positive PTB: A sputum sample result that exhibits the presence of bacilli or tests positive for Mycobacterium TB during sputum analysis for TB diagnosis using TB diagnostic laboratory devices.

Chronic illness: A non-infectious disease that weakens a person’s immunity to other infectious diseases and requires health care follow-up for a long period of time.

Inadequate lighting: A house was considered to have inadequate lighting if it was not possible for data collectors to read documents written with a pencil at the center of the house during daytime observation.

Inadequate ventilation: A house was considered to have inadequate ventilation if it had no open windows or doors allowing airflow across rooms during the data collection period, as observed by data collectors.

The size of the living room: It was computed by dividing the number of household members by the number of habitable rooms and categorized as adequate (≤3 persons per room) or inadequate (>3 persons per room).

Index case treatment duration: Time since initiation of anti-TB treatment in the index patient, categorized as <6 months and ≥6 months (≥6 months indicating treatment started at least six months before data collection).

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire and direct household observation. The questionnaire was adapted from previous literature, prepared in English, and translated into Amharic for field use. A pretest was conducted on 5% of the sample at Shebench Primary Hospital, and necessary modifications were made before the actual data collection. Three nurses with Bachelor of Science degrees and three diploma-level nurses, supervised by three health officers, received training on data collection procedures, including interviewing techniques, household observation, sputum handling, and sample transportation.

Face-to-face interviews were conducted with adult household contacts, while household environmental conditions were assessed through direct observation, including living room size, ventilation, and lighting. Ventilation and lighting were assessed based on the presence of open windows and doors allowing air circulation and adequate natural light.

Sputum specimens were collected from participants who were able to expectorate sputum and were transported to the laboratory using a cold chain system and processed within five days. The GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay was used for bacteriological confirmation of tuberculosis. Participants were classified as having TB if Mycobacterium tuberculosis was detected. All participants with confirmed TB were referred to nearby TB clinics for treatment initiation, and participants with negative results were informed through their residential addresses.

During household visits, all eligible members, including children and adolescents, were screened for TB symptoms and referred for further evaluation at nearby health facilities according to national guidelines. In addition, individuals eligible for TB preventive therapy, including children under 15 years and people living with HIV, were managed in line with Ethiopian national TB program recommendations.

Data quality control

To ensure data quality, all collected sputum samples were checked by laboratory professionals (technologists and technicians) before analysis to confirm adequacy in volume (≥2 ml) and the absence of oral particles, saliva, or blood. Sample analysis was performed promptly using the rapid, automated GeneXpert assay. Supervisors and data collectors received training on proper procedures. The questionnaire was translated into the local language, and a pretest was conducted on 5% of the sample at Shebench Primary Hospital. Daily checks were performed to ensure completeness of the collected data, and the researcher closely monitored the process to verify that all data were completed before the next scheduled collection. Data were entered into EpiData version 4.7, and cross-tabulation was performed to further ensure data accuracy and quality.

Data processing and analysis

Data were entered, cleaned, and coded using EpiData version 4.7 and exported to SPSS version 27.0 for analysis. The dataset was checked for missing and inconsistent values by cross-referencing with the original questionnaires. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were computed.

Bivariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with TB among adult household contacts. Variables with P < 0.2 in bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable model, and statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (P > 0.05 indicating adequate fit). Multicollinearity was checked using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF >10 indicating multicollinearity.

Human ethics and consent to participate

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Research Ethics Review Committee of Injibara University. Permission letters were also secured from the Kaffa Zone Health Department and from Bonga G/tsadik Shawo General Hospital, Wacha Primary Hospital, and Tello Primary Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, who were not exposed to any harm. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring respect for participants’ rights, safety, and well-being. Confidentiality of individual information was strictly maintained, and data were not shared with any third parties. Participants were fully informed of their right to voluntarily participate or withdraw from the study at any time.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

Out of 418 intended adult household contacts, 417 participated in the study, yielding a response rate of 99.76%. The mean age of the participants was 32.6 ± 11.3 years, ranging from 18 to 67 years. Slightly more than half, 210 (50.4%), were male, and a majority, 319 (76.5%), resided in rural areas. About half of the participants, 212 (50.8%), were married. In relation to the index patient, 128 (30.7%) were wives, followed by husbands, 119 (28.5%); siblings, 94 (22.5%); and children, 76 (18.3%). Regarding occupation, 160 (38.4%) of the household contacts were housewives (Table 2).

Bacteriologically confirmed PTB among adult household contacts

Bacteriologically confirmed PTB was detected in 35 of 417 adult household contacts (8.4%, 95% CI 5.56-11.14). Sputum samples were collected and tested using the GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay from 167 symptomatic individuals who were able to produce sputum, among whom 35 (21.0%) were positive for PTB.

Factors associated with TB among adult household contacts

In the bivariable logistic regression analysis, marital status, ventilation status of the house, living room size, treatment duration of the index case, lighting status of the house, proximity to the index case, alcohol consumption, cigarette smoking, khat chewing, monthly income, hypertension, lack of a separate kitchen, and frequency of meals per day were associated with TB among adult household contacts of patients with smear-positive PTB at P < 0.2.

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, ventilation status of the house, living room size, treatment duration of the index case, monthly income, and cigarette smoking remained statistically significant at P < 0.05. Adult household contacts living in inadequately ventilated houses were more likely to develop TB compared with those living in houses with good ventilation (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 3.43, 95% CI: 1.37-8.59). Household contacts residing in houses with inadequately sized living rooms had higher odds of TB than those with adequate living room size (AOR = 4.26, 95% CI: 1.51-11.99). Household contacts exposed to index patients who had been on treatment for six months or longer had 62% lower odds of developing TB compared with those exposed to index patients treated for less than six months (AOR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.15-0.94). Participants with a monthly income ≥1500 birr had 71% lower odds of developing TB compared with those whose monthly income was less than 1500 birr (AOR = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.12-0.66). Similarly, household contacts with no history of cigarette smoking had 65% lower odds of developing TB compared with smokers (AOR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.14-0.89) (Table 6). The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated that the model was adequately fitted (P = 0.345). Multicollinearity was assessed using the VIF, which showed no evidence of multicollinearity among the independent variables.

Discussion

This study aimed to detect the occurrence of bacteriologically confirmed PTB and associated factors among adult household contacts of patients with smear-positive PTB in Kaffa Zone public hospitals. Bacteriologically confirmed PTB was detected in 8.4% (35/417; 95% CI: 5.56-11.14) of adult household contacts screened. This finding is comparable with the study conducted in Haramaya District, Eastern Ethiopia, which reported a prevalence of 7.8% among household contacts, suggesting a consistent burden of TB across different regions of the country [11]. Although similar findings were observed, the present study provides additional evidence from Southwest Ethiopia using the more sensitive GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay and identified additional associated factors beyond those previously reported. However, variations have been reported in other settings [10], which may reflect differences in study populations, diagnostic methods, and living conditions. Overall, the findings reinforce that household contacts remain a high-risk group for TB infection and highlight the importance of strengthening household contact tracing and early case detection strategies.

The results of this study also align with findings from another study conducted in sub-Saharan Africa (7.8%) (18) [12], and Tanzania (6.4%) (20) [13]. However, this is notably higher than those reported in Nepal (1.6%) (21) [14] and South Africa (3.9%) (22) [15]. Conversely, the findings are lower than those from Peru (34%) (23) [16] and the Philippines (12.8%) (24) [17]. The observed variations may be attributed to differences in the study populations, living conditions, lifestyle, and the diagnostic techniques employed.

Statistically significant factors included inadequate household ventilation and inadequate living room size, which were associated with increased odds of TB. In contrast, an index patient treatment duration of six months and longer, a monthly income ≥ 1500 birr, and no history of cigarette smoking were associated with reduced odds of TB.

Individuals residing in inadequately ventilated houses had higher odds of developing TB compared with those living in well-ventilated houses. This observation aligns with results from other research conducted in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia [18], and Haramaya District, Oromia Region, Ethiopia [11]. The increased risk may be attributed to inadequate ventilation, which can elevate the bioaerosol concentration within living spaces, thereby increasing exposure. Conversely, effective ventilation is associated with a reduction in the bioaerosol concentration, consequently lowering the risk of transmission [19].

Household contacts residing in inadequately sized rooms had higher odds of developing TB compared with those in adequately sized accommodations. This finding corroborates results from Metema District of Ethiopia [20] and Haramaya District, Oromia Region, Ethiopia [11]. This might be related to enclosed spaces and poor air circulation that can accelerate the transmission of TB [21].

Our study found that household contacts living with an index patient who had been on treatment for more than six months had 62% lower odds of developing TB compared with those living with an index patient treated for less than six months. This finding is similar to studies in South Africa [22], India [23], and Addis Ababa [24]. The increased risk may be linked to an inadequately suppressed bacillary load during the initial six months of treatment. However, following the completion of treatment, the likelihood of transmission diminishes [25].

Household contacts with a monthly household income ≥1500 birr had 71% lower odds of bacteriologically confirmed PTB than those with a monthly household income <1500 birr. Although comparable evidence is limited, this association may be explained by the broader influence of socioeconomic disadvantage. Lower household income is often associated with poorer housing conditions, including overcrowding and inadequate ventilation, as well as reduced access to adequate nutrition, which may increase the risk of TB.

Household contacts with no history of cigarette smoking had 65% lower odds of developing TB compared with those who had a history of cigarette smoking. This study finding is in line with studies done in China [26], Texas, USA [27], and Gedeo zone, Southern Ethiopia [28]. The harmful impact of smoking on pulmonary health and immune system functionality plays a significant role in increasing susceptibility to TB infection. The occurrence of TB has been directly associated with weakened immune responses and various impairments in immune cell activity [29].

This study has important clinical and public health implications. Clinically, the relatively high burden of bacteriologically confirmed TB among adult household contacts highlights the need for a high index of suspicion when evaluating individuals with a history of close contact with patients with smear-positive TB. Early screening and prompt use of sensitive diagnostic tools such as GeneXpert MTB/RIF should be prioritized, particularly among contacts with risk factors such as poor ventilation, overcrowded housing, low income, and cigarette smoking. From a public health perspective, the findings emphasize that household contacts remain a key source of ongoing TB transmission, underscoring the importance of strengthening household contact investigation, improving living conditions such as ventilation and crowding, and ensuring effective implementation of TB preventive and early detection strategies within TB control programs.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It was conducted in both urban and rural settings, which enhances the representativeness of the findings. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews and direct household observations, improving completeness and accuracy. Sputum samples were collected at participants’ residences and transported to nearby health facilities for GeneXpert MTB/RIF analysis, ensuring timely and reliable diagnosis. In addition, all individuals diagnosed with TB were promptly referred to TB treatment clinics according to national TB program guidelines, ensuring ethical management and public health responsiveness.

However, the study also has limitations. The study included only adult household contacts (≥18 years) and therefore did not allow comparison of TB occurrence across children, adolescents, and adults, which may limit the broader epidemiological interpretation. In addition, further diagnostic investigations, such as chest radiography, were not performed for participants with negative GeneXpert results or those unable to provide sputum samples. Individuals presenting with non-specific symptoms, including fever, weight loss, night sweats, and loss of appetite, were also not further evaluated using additional diagnostic modalities. Furthermore, bacteriological testing using GeneXpert MTB/RIF was performed only among symptomatic participants who were able to produce sputum, while asymptomatic household contacts were not microbiologically evaluated. This limits the ability to estimate a true population-based prevalence of bacteriologically confirmed PTB, and the reported 8.4% should therefore be interpreted as the minimum proportion detected through the applied symptom-based screening strategy. In addition, latent TB infection was not assessed using tuberculin skin testing or interferon-gamma release assays among household contacts who did not undergo bacteriological testing. Consequently, the study could not estimate the burden of latent TB infection or inform eligibility for TB preventive therapy among adult household contacts. These limitations may have resulted in an underestimation of the true burden of TB among household contacts.

Conclusion

Bacteriologically confirmed PTB was detected in 8.4% (35/417; 95% CI: 5.56-11.14) of adult household contacts of patients with smear-positive PTB in Kaffa Zone, Southwest Ethiopia. Inadequate household ventilation and insufficient living space were associated with increased odds of bacteriologically confirmed PTB, whereas an index case treatment duration of at least six months, higher household income, and no history of cigarette smoking were protective factors. These findings underscore the importance of strengthening household contact investigation and implementing interventions that address environmental and behavioral risk factors to reduce TB transmission.

The Zonal Health Department, TB control program, and partner organizations should strengthen household contact investigation by ensuring that health facilities have adequate resources, standardized guidelines, and regular training to improve early detection and prevention of TB among household contacts. Particular attention should be given to reducing household environmental risks through health promotion on improving ventilation and minimizing overcrowding. Health care workers should prioritize early identification and screening of household contacts, especially those living in poorly ventilated or overcrowded households, and ensure that patients with smear-positive PTB complete the recommended treatment duration to reduce household transmission. Health extension workers and community supporters should provide community-based education on improving household ventilation, maintaining adequate living space, promoting early diagnosis and treatment-seeking, discouraging cigarette smoking in households with patients with TB, and providing targeted support for low-income households that may be at greater risk of TB transmission.
