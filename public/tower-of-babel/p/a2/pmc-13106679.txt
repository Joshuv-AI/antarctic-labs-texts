HIV heart inflammation is mediated by HIV infected myeloid cells, HIV-tat secretion, and aberrant function of Connexin43-containing channels
Authors: David Ajasin, Sophia Arredondo-Anez, Jose A Gutierrez, Michelle C Ward, Karen Maass, Eliseo Eugenin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeople living with HIV (PLWH) have a 2 times higher risk of HIV-associated cardiovascular disease (HIV-CVD) compared to people without HIV, despite effective anti-retroviral therapy (ART), but the mechanism is unknown. Here, we demonstrated the presence of myeloid cells containing HIV DNA sequences (HIV+) in human ventricular heart tissues from people with HIV in the ART era. HIV+ cells show residual HIV-Tat expression that is associated with upregulation of Connexin43 (Cx43) expression, gap junctional communication, and hemichannel (HC) activity. HIV-Tat binds to the Cx43 promoter, increasing Cx43 mRNA and protein expression. Cx43 enhanced expression by HIV-Tat was localized in the intercalated disk, as well as in the lateral membrane of cardiomyocytes, resulting in Cx43-containing HC openings and release of PGE2 and ATP, as well as facilitating the secretion of inflammatory cytokines. Overall, our data demonstrated that HIV+ cells, even during ART, secrete HIV-Tat, compromising GJ and HC-mediated communication and promoting localized inflammation, which could contribute to arrhythmia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43625-2.Keywords: HIV, Heart disease, Comorbidities, Cardiomyocytes, Tat, Cx43Subject terms: Cardiology, Cell biology, Diseases, Immunology

Introduction

HIV infection has become a manageable disease due to the availability and effectiveness of anti-retroviral therapies (ART)1. However, despite the success of ART, HIV-associated comorbidities and aging-related diseases, such as cognitive impairment and cardiovascular disease, have become prevalent2. In the pre-ART era, people living with HIV (PLWH) experienced a variety of cardiovascular diseases, such as left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, myocarditis, and other cardiomyopathies3–6 that were associated with macrophage infiltration, increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis7, and high levels of cardiotoxic viral proteins such as HIV-Tat and HIV-gp1208–15. With the introduction of ART in PLWH, the incidence of cardiomyopathies has been reduced, but the echocardiography shows increased LV mass with significant wall thickness and a dilated LV1,16 as well as an abnormal electrocardiogram (ECG) with ventricular ectopic beats and non-specific ST-T waves1,17–19. More importantly, PLWH under this ART era have elevated rates of sudden death, supporting an urgent need to understand HIV associated cardiomyopathy20,21.

Under physiological conditions, the heart is highly organized to enable electrical and metabolic coordination through intercellular connections mediated primarily by gap junctions (GJs), which contain connexin 43 (Cx43), the most highly expressed Connexin (Cx) in cardiomyocytes22. In physiological conditions, Cx43 is mainly localized at the intercalated disks (IDs) between adjoining cardiomyocytes to facilitate the rapid diffusion of small molecules, ions, and second messengers up to 1.2 kDa23 to maintain cardiac rhythm and exchange of metabolites between connected cells24. However, in disease conditions, Cx43 expression and distribution are compromised24–29. While the ID-Cx43 distribution is known to be predominantly GJ and it is critical for anisotropic conduction, lateralization of Cx43, which is associated with the formation of Cx43 HC, has been associated with compromised homeostasis and inflammation30–32. However, data on gap junctional communication in HIV is limited.

HIV and some ART regimens increase the risk of arrhythmias and some other cardiac pathologies33–35. Additionally, increased open HC probability with lateralized Cx43 has been associated with pro-arrhythmogenic cardiac damage36,37. Our laboratory demonstrated that HIV triggers long-term muscle dystrophy, Cx43 enhanced expression, and abnormal accumulation of calcium35. However, the mechanisms by which HIV compromises Cx-containing channels, GJ, or HC were unknown, and this is the focus of the current manuscript.

Our development of sensitive in situ techniques to detect low levels of HIV-infected cells in tissues provides a new method for spatial identification and characterization of HIV-infected cells and/or viral reservoirs38. For example, we demonstrated in the human brain that microglia/macrophages and a small population of astrocytes are the long-term HIV+ cells39. These cells secrete residual amounts of viral proteins, including HIV-Tat, which regulates the expression of Cx43 in astrocytes. However, whether a similar mechanism of HIV-Cx43 dysregulation is present in the human heart is unknown.

In this report, we show; First, myeloid HIV+ cells are present in the heart of PLWH in the ART era; Second, these HIV+ cells are mainly macrophages that still show low-level residual viral mRNA and protein synthesis, including HIV-Tat; Third, the treatment of cardiomyocytes differentiated from human induced pluripotent cells (hiPS-CMs) with HIV-Tat resulted in Cx43 mRNA and protein upregulation; Fourth, HIV-Tat treatment enhanced GJ communication in hiPS-CMs; Fifth, Cx43 upregulation induced by HIV-Tat was associated with Cx43 HC formation and opening resulting in ATP and PGE2 release, and associated with inflammatory cytokines release. Finally, we demonstrate that the HIV-Tat protein interacts with the Cx43 promoter in hiPS-CMs. Overall, our data demonstrate that cardiac damage in PLWH is associated with the presence of HIV+ myeloid cells, residual HIV-Tat expression, increased Cx43 expression, enhanced GJ communication, and opened HC, resulting in local inflammation.

Results

Participant demographics

To identify and quantify the presence of HIV infected (HIV+) cells in the heart tissues of HIV-infected individuals under ART, left ventricle (LV) tissues from PLWH were used (Table 1:1–8 HIV negative controls; 11–14 undetectable; 9–10 low replication; 15–18 high replication). In addition, we included n = 8 uninfected individuals (Table 1, 1–8). The median ages of the HIV-infected population were 51.80 ± 12.92 years and 58.88 ± 8.92 years for the uninfected population (p = 0.2307). The HIV-positive cohort was divided into two groups of aviremic (plasma viral load < 50 copies/mL, n = 6) and viremic (plasma viral load > 50 copies/mL, n = 4). The aviremic group had an average plasma HIV RNA copy number of 11.5 ± 9.725 copies/mL for HIV (undetectable or low) and 286,750 ± 305,923 copies/mL for HIVhi (Table 1, p = 0.0092, comparing both). The average CD4+ T cell counts of the aviremic samples were 433.8 ± 300.5 cells/mm3 for HIV(un or low), 54.83 ± 91.6 cells/mm3 for HIVhi, and 859 ± 55.1 cells/mm3 for uninfected, p = 0.0144, compared to HIV(un or low)/HIVhi (Table 1). Note that the cause of death data is unavailable to correlate with these post-mortem tissues, but they were unrelated to cardiac deaths.

Myeloid HIV+ cells are present in heart tissues obtained from HIV-infected individuals on ART

Prior to the introduction of ART, PLWH experienced increased rates of HIV-associated cardiomyopathies compared to uninfected individuals19. Despite ART introduction, HIV-associated cardiomyopathies persist40, suggesting that even during controlled viremia, an alternative mechanism of cardiac damage is ongoing. We propose that long-term cardiac damage is mediated by the presence of HIV+ cells within the heart. To demonstrate this, post-mortem LV tissues from PLWH and uninfected individuals were examined.

LV heart tissues were stained for nuclei (DAPI) and Alu-repeats (Alu), as well as HIV DNA (gag or nef sequences using Peptide Nucleic Acid probes, PNA), HIV RNA (gag-pol target), and viral proteins such as HIV p24 and HIV Tat to detect HIV+ cells and residual replication. As expected, no staining for HIV-DNA, HIV-mRNA, and HIV-p24/HIV-Tat protein was observed in uninfected tissues (Fig. 1A, uninfected/control, patient 1–8, Table 1). In human hearts from HIV-infected individuals on ART with undetectable systemic replication (Fig. 1, HIVun), we identified cells containing HIV-DNA signal within the nucleus (Fig. 1B and quantification in Fig. 1C, HIVun, patient 11–14, Table 1, 0.053 ± 0.021% of the total cell number, *p ≤ 0.005 compared to control tissues). Tissues from individuals with HIV and on ART with low replication showed a higher number of HIV DNA-positive cells (HIVlow, Table 1, patient samples 9 and 10. Table 1, *p ≤ 0.005 compared to control tissues, 0.143 ± 0.025% of the total cell number). In both cases, HIVun or HIVlow, we must note that HIV+ cells were located in specific regions of the LV tissue. Most of the tissue was negative for HIV infected cells (data not represented). In contrast, analysis of heart tissues with high replication, patient samples 15–18, Table 1, and with HIV encephalitis (HIVE) from the pre-ART era (as positive control) showed a higher number of HIV-DNA positive cells compared to control/uninfected tissues and HIVun/HIVlow (HIVE, Table 1, patient samples 15–18. Table 1, *p ≤ 0.005 compared to control tissues and #p ≤ 0.001 compared to HIVun/HIVlow tissues, 2.9 ± 0.03% of the total cell number). These data indicate that the systemic viral load is correlated with the number of HIV+ cells detected in heart tissues.

Further analysis of the HIV+ cells indicated the presence of low levels of HIV-mRNA transcripts in both HIVun and HIVlow-infected individuals (Fig. 1D, 0.026 ± 0.016% and 0.054 ± 0.017% of the total cell number, respectively, *p ≤ 0.005 compared to control tissues). In contrast, the data for the HIV-mRNA levels detected in HIVE (positive control from pre-ART individuals) tissues are 2.72 ± 0.203% of the total cell number, indicating that most HIV+ cells in these tissues have robust viral mRNA transcription and possibly ongoing replication (Fig. 1D, #p ≤ 0.005 HIVE compared to HIVun/HIVlow tissues). To evaluate whether the presence of the HIV mRNA transcript translated into viral proteins, the expression of HIV p24 and HIV Tat proteins was examined. HIVun showed 0.009 ± 0.003%, and HIVlow showed 0.026 ± 0.005% of the cells expressing viral proteins, respectively (Fig. 1E, HIV-p24, *p ≤ 0.005 compared to control tissues); however, HIVE tissues maintained 2.6 ± 0.21% of the cells expressing HIV-DNA, HIV-mRNA, and HIV proteins (Fig. 1E, HIV-p24, HIVE corresponds to HIVE tissues, *p ≤ 0.005 HIVE compared to HIVun/HIVlow).

Overall, HIV+ cells are present in the LV, and may be in other areas of the heart that have not been analyzed. Residual replication (viral mRNA and protein) is detected in all tissues analyzed, and a reduction in systemic HIV replication by ART diminished the number of HIV+ cells in the heart.

Analyses of heart tissues obtained from uninfected individuals did not show any signal for HIV-DNA. Cx43 was mainly localized at the intercalated disks (ID) (Fig. 2A, Uninfected, white arrows), and macrophage numbers were minimal (Iba-1 or CD68-positive cells, Fig. 2A and Supplemental Fig. 1). However, in HIV-infected tissues, Iba-1 (Fig. 2B) and CD68 (Supplemental Fig. 1A and 1 C for quantification) positive cell numbers increased, and Cx43 expression was higher, however, its distribution changed from the ID only to being observed in the lateral membrane of cardiomyocytes (Fig. 2B, D and E, and Supplemental Fig. 1B), thereby suggesting lateralization. Quantification of the cell numbers containing HIV-DNA signals and cell markers: Iba1, sarcomeric α-actinin, and Cx43 indicate that macrophages were the main cell type harboring HIV-DNA, even in those from individuals with undetectable, HIVun, and/or low replication, HIVlow, 18.2 ± 3.4 and 41 ± 4%, respectively (Fig. 2C and Supplemental Fig. 1). Also, a small cardiomyocyte subpopulation (sarcomeric α-actinin positive cells) harboring HIV-DNA signal was identified in both HIVun and HIVlow replication, 0.158 ± 0.169 and 2.0 ± 0.9%, respectively (Fig. 2C). Nevertheless, we need to emphasize that the combination of Iba-1/CD68 and Sarcomeric α-actinin positive cells with HIV-DNA did not correspond to 100% of the HIV-DNA cells, suggesting the presence of other unidentified cell population(s) containing HIV-integrated DNA (Fig. 2C).

In conclusion, the HIV+ cells are predominantly tissue-associated macrophages, and areas with infected cells exhibit significant Cx43 mislocalization in HIV+ tissues compared to uninfected samples. We need to denote that in HIVlow and HIVun tissues, most of the tissue is negative for HIV-DNA as well as Cx43 dysregulation; thus, dysregulation is local and associated with HIV infected cells.

Residual HIV-Tat expression mediates Cx43 dysregulation

As indicated in Fig. 1, a subset of HIV+ DNA cells continues to express HIV-mRNA and viral proteins, such as HIV-p24 (Fig. 1E), as well as HIV-Tat (Supplemental Fig. 2). Furthermore, our data from the brain suggest that HIV-Tat can dysregulate Cx43 expression and function. Cx43 is essential for proper heart function22. Thus, to determine whether HIV-Tat alters Cx43 expression and/or channel function (GJ and HC), human pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes were used (hiPS-CM; the differentiation procedure was reported in41–43 and the Method section). hiPS-CMs were incubated for 24 h with 50–100 ng/mL HIV-Tat. The selection of the HIV-Tat concentration was based on the concentration that was unable to induce significant transactivation in the TZM-bL reporter cell line (Supplemental Fig. 3). After HIV-Tat incubation for 24 h, HIV-Tat staining was performed. No HIV-Tat signal was detected in untreated hiPS-CMs cultures (Fig. 3A-D). HIV-Tat treatment resulted in robust HIV-Tat intracellular accumulation in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3E to H and I to L for higher magnification; Fig. 3Q and R for quantification), but mostly in the nucleus as expected for its transactivation function (Fig. 3M to P, Q and R for quantification).

HIV-Tat treatment of hiPS-CM cultures enhances Cx43 expression

To determine whether HIV-Tat upregulates Cx43 transcription and expression, real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR, see primers Table 2), Western blot, and immunofluorescence via confocal microscopy were employed. Confocal analysis of untreated hiPS-CM cultures shows that Cx43 localization is observed at the cell-to-cell apposition membranes as expected (Fig. 4A to D, untreated). hiPS-CM cultures treated with HIV-Tat protein (50 ng/mL) for 24 h resulted in increased Cx43 protein expression in a dose-dependent manner (Figs. 4E to H and 50 ng/mL, and I to L, 100 ng/mL). However, HIV-Tat at 100 ng/mL or above reduced cardiac α-actinin (CA) expression (Fig. 4I and L), which is indicative of cardiomyocyte dysfunction. In agreement, HIV-Tat has previously been demonstrated to impact F-actin remodeling in various cell types, including RhoA, Rock1, and paxillin44,45; hence, there is the possibility that high HIV-Tat concentrations compromise cardiac α-actinin (CA) assembly, which would lead to dysregulation of cardiomyocytes46. Also, our immunofluorescence analysis suggests 100ng/mL HIV-Tat induces higher Cx43 expression compared to 50ng/mL treatment (Fig. 4G and K).

HIV-Tat binds to the Cx43 promoter region in hiPS-CMs

Our previous observation of HIV-Tat-induced upregulation of Cx43 transcription and protein in cardiomyocytes raises the question: how is the Cx43 upregulation mechanism? A previous study from our laboratory shows HIV-Tat binds to the Cx43 promoter in astrocytes to increase Cx43 expression and channel-associated function, GJ, and HC47. Here, we tested the same promoter region for HIV-Tat interaction in cardiomyocytes. We treated hiPS-CMs with either media only (Control) or 100 ng/mL HIV-Tat-Flag for 16 h to perform chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) using an anti-Flag antibody, as described in47. Figure 5A represents the experimental approach. The pull-down of DNA using HIV-Tat-Flag antibodies shows that no pull-down for GAPDH was recovered, as expected (5B, both untreated and HIV-Tat-treated, except for the genomic DNA input). The analysis of the HIV-Tat-Flag pull-down in control conditions shows no Cx43 promoter amplification, except in the input sample, as expected (Fig. 5B, Control). However, upon HIV-Tat-Flag treatment of hiPS-CM cultures, the Cx43 promoter was detected in the pull down (Fig. 5B, HIV-Tat), thereby confirming that HIV-Tat is directly binding to Cx43 promoter region.

HIV-Tat increases GJ communication in hiPS-CMs

To determine the functional consequences of HIV-Tat upregulating Cx43 transcription, GJ communication was evaluated using single-cell microinjection of hiPS-CM as described in48. First, cells were treated with either media only (control) or HIV-tat in the presence and absence of GJ blockers, AGA. Single cell microinjection (designated with *) of Lucifer Yellow (LY) resulted in LY diffusion from the microinjected cell into neighboring cells (Fig. 6A-B and G, Control Untreated). Each single cell microinjection resulted in an average of 20 ± 8 coupled cells per single microinjected cell (Fig. 6H, Control). GJ specificity was examined by adding the GJ and HC blocker, 18α-Glycyrrhetinic acid (AGA; 35 µM), into the media, resulting in ∼80% reduction in dye transfer (Fig. 6G and H, Control, AGA). In HIV-Tat-treated hiPS-CMs cultures (24 h post-Tat treatment), all microinjected cells were coupled to others (Fig. 6C-D and G) with an average number of coupled cells per microinjection of 37 ± 6 cells, indicating that the radius of GJ communication increased with HIV-Tat treatment (Fig. 6H). Enhanced GJ communication induced by HIV-Tat was AGA sensitive (Fig. 6E-F, and H, HIV-Tat + AGA). Overall, our data indicate that HIV-Tat increased Cx43 expression, but also resulted in higher GJ communication.

HIV-Tat induces hemichannel opening on the surface of cardiomyocytes

To examine whether HIV-Tat results in HC opening, Ethidium (Etd) uptake assay in hiPS-CM was used. Minimal hiPS-CMs Etd uptake was detected in untreated conditions (Fig. 7A-D, phase, and E-H, Etd fluorescence, and Y-Z correspond to the quantification of Etd uptake rate). In contrast, Etd uptake in hiPS-CM cultures treated with HIV-Tat (100 ng/mL, 24 h), increased HC opening (6–12 min and 21–24 min time points, Fig. 7I-L, phase, and M-P, Etd fluorescence, and Y-Z corresponded to the quantification). HIV-Tat-induced HC opening was blocked by AGA (a GJ and HC blocker, Fig. 7Q-T phase, U-X, Etd fluorescence, and Y-Z correspond to the quantification, *p ≤ 0.005, n = 15 experiments). Overall, HIV-Tat induces HC opening in hiPS-CMs.

PGE2 and ATP are released by HC opening induced by HIV-Tat treatment

HC opening is associated with the release of intracellular inflammatory molecules such as Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), Adenosine triphosphate (ATP), Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD), and sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P)49–53. Therefore, we examined the release of these molecules into the extracellular medium (Fig. 8). HIV-Tat treatment enhanced the release of PGE2 and ATP, both of which are inflammatory mediators (Fig. 8A and B, respectively). The enhaced release of PGE2 and ATP induced by HIV-Tat was sensitive to general GJ/HC blockers such as AGA, but also to the specific Cx43 blocking peptide (Cx43pep), thereby supporting the conclusion that Cx43 is essential to triggering inflammation (Fig. 8A, n = 3, *p ≤ 0.05 compared to control) and ATP (Fig. 8B, n = 3, *p ≤ 0.05). Controls using AGA vehicle (DMSO, data not shown) or a scrambled peptide (Scr Pep) did not change the basal or HIV-Tat-induced release of PGE2 or ATP secretion (Fig. 8A and B). In contrast, the secretion of NAD+ or S1P was not detected (data not shown). Therefore, we conclude that HIV-Tat by inducing the opening of Cx43 HC induces the secretion of PGE2 and ATP into the extracellular space.

HIV-Tat induces inflammatory cytokine levels in hiPS-CM cultures

HC opening is associated not only with the release of intracellular factors, but also with the activation of the inflammasome49–53. Thus, to evaluate the inflammasome activation, an array of pro-inflammatory cytokines was evaluated in the presence or absence of HIV-Tat treatment (24 h) using hiPS-CM cultures (Supplemental Fig. 4). IFN-γ, TNF-α, G-CSF, TGF-β, and IL-6 were elevated in HIV-Tat-treated hiPS-CMs compared to the untreated control or media only (Supplemental Fig. 4, n = 3, *p ≤ 0.05 compared to control conditions). However, there were no changes in the release of IL-13, IL-17 A, IL-4, and IL-5 detected (Supplemental Fig. 4). Thus, we conclude that HIV-Tat also triggers inflammation in cardiomyocyte cultures, potentially contributing to cardiac toxicity.

Discussion

In the ART era, HIV-CVD persists34,54, but the mechanisms behind it are poorly examined. Today, the risk and incidence of heart disease in PLWH remain approximately two-fold higher despite controlled viral replication by ART and immune reconstitution55. While few studies have shown that ART contributes to the risk of developing CVD, others have shown that HIV itself induces CVD13. In this report, using ex vivo left ventricle heart tissues from both uninfected and PLWH, and hiPS-CMs, we demonstrate first, the presence of myeloid HIV+ cells in human heart tissues from PLWH in this ART era; second, residual viral replication or viral protein synthesis in a small subpopulation of macrophages in the heart tissues from PLWH, which could potentially serve as the local source of viral proteins in the hearts of PLWH; third, HIV infection and HIV-Tat induced elevated Cx43 transcription and protein expression; fourth, enhanced Cx43 GJ communication is induced by HIV-Tat; fifth, HIV-Tat induced increase in the formation Cx43 HC expression and its opening; and lastly, HIV-Tat treatment caused local secretion of ATP, and PGE2 as well as increased pro-inflammatory cytokines release.

It is important to note that 60% of the post-mortem tissues analyzed, left ventricular muscle, in this study are from PLWH on ART with low to undetectable viral load. The remaining tissues are from PLWH with uncontrolled viremia despite ART, and those from individuals with HIV encephalitis (HIVE, in Figs. 1 and 2). Our observation of HIV proviral DNA and mRNA in heart tissues from PLWH is well established, as some studies have demonstrated similar observations in the pre-ART era88. Autopsy tissue data from early 1990–2000 demonstrated the presence of HIV-mRNA-containing cells in the hearts of PLWH56. However, we are showing in this study that despite ART, these HIV+ cells persist in PLWH in the current ART era. Interestingly, we also demonstrated in this report that the majority of the HIV-infected cells in the heart are primarily macrophages, as indicated by the presence of Iba-1+ and CD68+ cells and the positive signals for both HIV-DNA and RNA (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). However, there are still HIV-DNA-positive cell populations that we have not identified in this study and by others in the pre-ART era, suggesting other cell types in the heart or other non-heart residents, but infiltrating cells might be harboring HIV pro-viruses in the heart of PLWH57,58. Macrophages in the heart originate from two sources: one subset is of embryonic origin, and the other is derived from monocytes circulating in the blood. These two subsets have varying half-lives, but they are both long-lived with the macrophages of embryonic origin being long-lived macrophages and able to self-renew59,60. This is a critical issue, indicating that HIV promotes infiltration of monocyte/macrophages into the cardiac tissue, probably to enhance inflammation as well as perpetuate the population of cells susceptible to HIV infection.

We propose that during the peak of viremia at the early stages of primary infection, uninfected and HIV-infected monocytes infiltrate the cardiac parenchyma, as this was one of the hallmarks of HIV infection during the early years of the HIV pandemic57,61. These myeloid viral reservoirs in the heart exhibited residual expression of viral RNA and viral proteins, including HIV-Tat, which compromises surrounding uninfected cells. Recently, work on SIV-infected macaques and HIV-infected humanized mice indicated the presence of resident SIV/HIV-infected cells in the gut and other tissues, including the heart, supporting the hypothesis that there are HIV infected cells in multiple tissues in PLWH in this ART era57,62,63. We focused on the heart’s left ventricle due to data from several cohorts demonstrating that ventricular dysfunction is prevalent in this current ART era of PLWH64–66, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of comparable HIV-infected individuals has confirmed reduced ejection fractions, increased left ventricular mass, and edema/fibrosis in PLWH67,68. In addition, a humanized mouse model study recently confirmed that animals subjected to long-term HIV infection developed progressive left ventricular heart failure, a feature described in PLWH in the pre-ART era, but could persist in this ART era63. The same study observed progressive microvascular permeability and localized fibrosis, contributing to increased myocardial stiffness and disease development63. Further, it was demonstrated in the pre-ART era that most HIV-infected cells in the heart are not cardiomyocytes but rather immune or other resident stromal cells69. Note that, in this study, we have only examined the left ventricle, which is the major chamber of the heart. We have not examined other chambers or parts of the heart, such as the ventricular wall- subendocardial, mid-myocardial, subepicardial, or the perivascular regions. Future studies can examine to see if there are ventricular wall region-specific differences in this population.

Furthermore, our data indicate that, apart from the increased number of macrophages infiltrating the heart, other non-macrophage cell populations in the heart tissues contain HIV-DNA. For instance, we observed HIV-DNA-positive sarcomeric α-actinin-positive cells, which suggests some cardiomyocytes harbor HIV-DNA. This is still under investigation, as cardiomyocytes do not express the receptors required for HIV infection, but similar observations were made in heart tissues from PLWH in the pre-ART era70. This observation is suggestive that a very small subset of cardiomyocytes could be susceptible to HIV infection. The mechanism of infection is unknown, as cardiomyocytes do not express CD4, but we can not discard lateral infection via non-canonical infection pathways such as tunneling nanotubes (TNT), virological synapse (VS), or other yet-to-be-characterized pathways71–73. We do not know the effect of HIV-DNA presence in cardiomyocytes on their functionality; however, it would be interesting to examine whether these cells are dead-end hosts or if they can support productive virus replication or contribute to viral protein synthesis. Another consideration is the effect of HIV-DNA on the gene expression profile of these cardiomyocytes and how that affects their cardiac functions. However, we must be cautious because HIV-DNA-containing sarcomeric α-actinin-positive cells may not represent fully differentiated cardiomyocytes, as productive HIV infection of cardiomyocytes has not yet been demonstrated. We must consider these findings carefully, as our previous data on atherosclerotic plaques obtained from patient samples identified that HIV causes a dedifferentiation profile74.

Furthermore, another critical finding of our data is the low level of HIV mRNA and viral protein expression in HIV-DNA positive cells, even in tissues obtained from individuals under ART, suggesting a low level of viral replication and/or basal viral protein synthesis persists despite ART. Previous studies on the effect of HIV or HIV-Tat on heart function have used higher concentrations of viral proteins or animal models with uncontrolled viral protein expression, thereby representing either non-physiological scenarios or events during the pre-ART era, which further demonstrates the inherent cardiotoxicity potential of these proteins in the heart in the pre-ART era75,76. However, in this report, we have used a low concentration of HIV-Tat that does not induce transactivation in the TZMbL reporter cell line. This is important because some studies have shown that in the present ART era, viral reservoirs produce low levels of HIV-Tat compared to during robust viral replication, and consistent with what is observed presently in aviremic PLWH77,78. Therefore, using a low HIV-Tat concentration that does not transactivate viral replication in reporter cell lines is similar to how low level of HIV-Tat in vivo drives inflammation without activating robust viral replication from viral reservoirs, and our findings are in line with what is ongoing in PLWH in this ART era. Apart from this inflammation amplification observation in the heart, another study has also shown that HIV-Tat is associated with Cx43 dysregulation and dyslipidemia, and macrophage function is compromised in PLWH10,13.

Moreover, the low numbers of detected HIV+ cells cannot account for the extensive damage observed in human heart tissues in PLWH. Thus, first, we propose that persistent chronic inflammation is essential to compromise heart function; second, the direct effect of the low number of HIV-infected cells on the heart through their expression and secretion of cardio-toxic HIV proteins like Tat; and third, the indirect effects of compromised GJ and HC opening. One proposal is that viral reservoirs use GJ and HC to amplify bystander damage into uninfected cells. This observation is in agreement with our previous data in the brain, which provided the first indications that HIV uses Cx43-containing channels to amplify pro-apoptosis and pro-inflammation signals into and around neighboring uninfected astrocytes despite low to undetectable viral replication79. The data from human astrocytes and subsequently from human brain tissues indicate that HIV, specifically the HIV-Tat protein, binds to the Cx43 promoter to maintain and enhance Cx43 mRNA expression, thereby increasing Cx43 protein expression. In that study, we showed that enhanced Cx43 protein expression results in an increased radius of cell-to-cell apoptosis, and blocking GJ prevents the observed bystander apoptosis and spread of associated inflammation. In this report, we made the same observation in hiPS-CMs as demonstrated in our ChIP-qPCR experiment (Fig. 5). HIV-Tat has been shown to promote transcriptional changes in HIV-infected or HIV-Tat-treated cells45,80,81, but this is the first time it has been demonstrated in cardiomyocytes. In addition, the observation of increased Cx43 HC activity is critical, as this has been linked with arrhythmia and myocardial infarction, which has been observed in PLWH in this current ART era82,122].

Additionally, the HIV-Tat-induced dysregulation of Cx43 expression and function resulted in Cx43 being targeted to the apical plasma membrane to form hemichannels (HCs), leading to the lateralization of Cx43. The opening of these HCs allows for the release of ATP and PGE2, which have both been shown to be increased in the sera of PLWH on ART83, further contributing to systemic inflammation in suppressed individuals.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that HIV+ cells are present in the human heart, and despite ART-mediated suppression, residual viral replication is still ongoing. HIV-Tat secreted from HIV+ cells compromises Cx43-mediated communication, including GJ and HC, resulting in localized inflammation. The elimination of these viral reservoirs (HIV+ cells) in several tissues, including the heart, is essential for cure efforts and to prevent the devastating consequences of long-term infection and ART in the aging infected population.

Chromatin-immunoprecipitation of HIV-Tat-Cx43 promoter complex

hiPS-CMs at 30 d post-differentiation were re-plated in 0.1% gelatin-coated 10 cm dishes in cardiomyocyte maintenance media (DMEM, glucose and phenol red-free, supplemented with 5 mM HEPES, 1 mM Sodium pyruvate, 2 mM GlutaMAX, 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 µg/mL Streptomycin/100 IU Penicillin, and 990 mg/500 mL Galactose (Sigma G5388) and allowed to regain spontaneous contraction. Cells were treated with either 1X PBS only (control) or HIV-Tat-Flag protein (100 µg/mL) for 16 h. Cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde, incubated in 125 mM glycine, washed, and collected. Collected cells were lysed, and nuclear extracts were sonicated using a Bioruptor at the highest setting. Crosslinked extracts were precleared with normal mouse IgG plus protein A/G-agarose. Samples were immunoprecipitated with 2 µg of anti-Flag antibody, washed, and the protein-DNA complex eluted. The crosslinked protein-DNA complex was then reversed with 200 mM NaCl at 65 °C. The proteins were digested with proteinase K, and immunoprecipitated DNA was recovered with a DNA purification kit (Qiagen). The recovered DNA was quantified and, along with the same concentration of genomic DNA (input), was subjected to quantitative PCR (qPCR) using the StepOne Plus system (Applied Biosystems). As positive controls, phosphorylated RNA polymerase was used as well as astrocytes treated with HIV-tat (data not shown).

Measurement of ATP, PGE2, NAD+, and S1P release

hiPS-CMs at 30 days post-differentiation were re-plated in 6-well plates and cultured as described above until cells regained spontaneous contractile activity. hiPS-CMs were pre-treated with one of the following conditions: media only (control), Cx43 blockers (AGA or Cx43 blocking peptide), and scrambled peptide (control) for 30 min at 37 °C, and we then treated all the designated cells with HIV-Tat (100ng/mL) for 16 h. Media was collected, clarified by centrifugation at 4 °C, and stored at -80 °C. ELISA determined the quantification of each metabolite according to the manufacturer’s protocols: PGE2 (Prostaglandin E2 ELISA kit, Abcam, Cambridge, MA), ATP (ATPlite kit, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA), NAD (NAD/NADH assay kit, colorimetric, Abcam, Cambridge, MA) and S1P (Human sphingosine 1-phosphate ELISA kit, MyBioSource, San Diego, CA). Data was collected using a PerkinElmer Envision plate reader and analyzed using GraphPad software.

Measurement of cytokines in hiPS-CM media

hiPS-CMs media from above were subjected to cytokine measurement and analysis using a custom multi-analyte ELISArray kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

HC opening quantification using dye uptake

To evaluate the HC opening in control and HIV-Tat-treated hiPS-CMs, dye uptake was performed. First, contracting hiPS-CMs were pre-treated with either 1X PBS or 18α-Glycyrrhetinic acid (AGA) for 30 min. The same cultures were treated with media only (control) or Tat (100 ng/mL) for 24 h. Cells were imaged using a live cell imaging system (Zeiss Axio Observer Z1, Jena, Germany) set at 37 °C and 5% CO2, with images collected every 3 min. EtBr (5 µM) was added to the cultures, and an increase in the internal fluorescent intensity was quantified as a function of time97. We calculated the curve slope to determine the time versus Etd intensity changes described97.

Evaluation of GJ communication by dye coupling

hiPS-CMs were seeded on 0.15% gelatin-coated MatTek plates to evaluate whether HIV-Tat affects GJ activities in cardiomyocytes. Once hiPS-CMs regained spontaneous contractility, cells were first subjected to pre-treatment with 1X PBS or 20µM Glycyrrhizic acid (AGA) for 30 min, followed by media-only (control) or 100 ng/mL HIV-Tat treatment conditions for 6 h. Afterward, one cell was microinjected with a borosilicate pipette containing 5% Lucifer Yellow (diluted in 150 mM LiCl) to evaluate the diffusion of the dye from the injected cell to neighboring cells after 3–5 min. Images were captured with a Zeiss fluorescent microscope equipped with 37 °C and 5% CO2 incubation chamber. The number of cells coupled to the microinjected cell (denoted with *) was quantified as described in98. Positive cell coupling was defined as the transfer of dye to one or more adjacent cells. Each data point was obtained from 4 independent experiments, where a minimum of 10 cells were microinjected per condition.

Image analysis

Raw data for confocal analysis 3D deconvolutions were obtained using NIS Elements Advanced Research (Nikon, Japan). NIS elements and FIJI quantified the signal specificity, colocalization, intensities, numbers, and location.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (Cell Signaling, Beverly, MA) containing protease and phosphate inhibitors (Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA), and 30 µg of protein was electrophoresed on an SDS polyacrylamide gel (Bio-Rad, CA) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were incubated with primary and secondary antibodies to Cx43 and GAPDH. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageStudio software (Licor).

In situ detection of HIV+ cells in the human heart tissue of HIV-infected individuals by confocal microscopy

To address the presence and distribution of HIV+ cells within the ex vivo heart tissues (left ventricle) of uninfected and PLWH under long-term ART, we developed an imaging in situ protocol based on the detection of the HIV-DNA, viral mRNA, and immunodetection of viral and host proteins99 in the same assay to detect HIV+ cells as we recently reported in the urethra, bone marrow, and the brain94,99–101. Due to the overall low abundance of HIV-infected cells with HIV-integrated DNA (positive for DAPI, HIV-Gag or HIV-nef DNA probe, and Alu-repeat probe, all with PNA quality), we analyzed serial and large tissue pieces (6.9 ± 3.2 cm² for length and width) with an average thickness of 12 µm. Briefly, paraffin-embedded autopsy human heart tissues from HIV-infected/undetectable-low replication (n = 6, less than 30 copies/mL), high replication (n = 4, more than 30,000 copies/mL), and uninfected individuals (n = 8) were obtained from NNTC. The tissues were serially sectioned (12 µm thick) and mounted onto glass microscope slides using a cryostat, all at -20°C. Sections were first rehydrated and incubated in Sudan black (0.3%) for 2 hours at room temperature. The tissues were treated with proteinase K (20 µg/mL) and washed with 1X TBS. In addition, we performed HIV-DNA (gag or nef) and/or Alu DNA probe fluorescent in-situ hybridization (FISH) staining. Briefly, HIV Gag DNA-488 (Alexa 488-5’ AAGATTTAAACACCATGCTAA 3’, PNA Bio, Thousand Oaks, CA), or HIV Nef DNA-488 (Alexa488-5’-GACCAATGACTTACAAGGCAGCTGTAGA 3’, PNA Bio, Thousand Oaks, CA) with or without Alu DNA-647 (Alexa 647-5’ GCCTCCCAAAGTGCTGGGATTACAG 3’ or Alexa 647-5’ACCACTGCACTCCAGCCTG 3’, PNA Bio) probes were diluted in 1X TBS and added to the tissues. The tissues were incubated at 42 °C for 30 min and then at 55 °C for 50 min. The tissues were washed with 1X stringent wash (PNA Bio) solution at 55 °C for 25 min (last 15 min on a shaker) and immersed in 1X TBS for 20 s. Afterward, the samples were subjected to either HIV-gag-pol mRNA staining or immunofluorescence to stain the following proteins: Cx43, Iba-1, Sarcomeric α-actinin, HIV-Tat, and HIV-p24. For HIV-gag-pol mRNA staining, we followed the manufacturer’s protocol (ACD/Bio RNAscope 2.5 HD Detection-RED, Cat# 322360). HIV-gag-pol mRNA probe (Cat# 317691) was added to the designated samples and incubated for 30 min at 42 °C and then 50 min at 55 °C. The samples were washed with a 1X stringent wash solution for 25 min at 55 °C and immersed in 1X TBS for 20 s. The reaction was stopped immediately when we observed the samples turning red. Samples were washed five times with deionized water. For immunofluorescence staining, designated samples were blocked overnight at 4 °C and then incubated with antibodies to either one or a couple of targets, including Iba-1, CD68, Sarcomeric α-actinin, Cx43, HIV-Tat, and HIV-p24 (see Table 3 for details), also overnight at 4 °C. Samples were washed three times with 1X TBS at room temperature, followed by incubation with corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were washed five times with 1X TBS at room temperature and mounted with Prolong Gold with DAPI. The samples were imaged with a Nikon confocal microscope (Tokyo, Japan). The total cell numbers corresponded to DAPI counterstained and Alu-repeats-positive nuclei. The HIV-positive cells corresponded to HIV-Gag/Nef DNA-positive nuclei, as determined by the total number of cells (as assessed by DAPI and Alu repeat positivity). In addition, cells expressing HIV-mRNA (HIV-gag-pol mRNA probe: mRNAgagpol) and HIV-p24 protein (biotinylated antibody) were quantified in HIV-Gag/Nef DNA-positive cells without colocalization with DNA. To identify the presence of HIV-Tat protein in the heart of PLWH, heart tissues of uninfected and PLWH were sectioned, and confocal immunofluorescence using antibodies against HIV-Tat, Cardiac α-actinin, and Cx43 was performed (see Table 3 for antibody details and38, as described above. Samples were similarly imaged with a Nikon A1 confocal microscope with spectral detection (Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Information on the statistical tests used, and the exact values of n (the number of experiments), can be found in the Figure Legends. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.4.2 (GraphPad Software Inc.). The statistical tests were chosen according to the following: a two-tailed paired or unpaired t-test was applied on datasets with a normal distribution (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test), a two-tailed Mann-Whitney (unpaired test) or Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank tests were used for designated datasets, and ANOVA was also used for designated datasets (see figure legends for statistical tool for each figure. * or # p < 0.05 was considered significant.
