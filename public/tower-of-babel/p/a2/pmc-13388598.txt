How survival time reshapes prognostic risk in giant cell glioblastoma
Authors: Xiaoling Zhang, Jiajie Xia, Hua Huang, Chuanjian Tu, Zhihao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo address the lack of dynamic prognostic tools for the rare Giant Cell Glioblastoma (gcGBM), this study utilized conditional survival (CS) analysis and a population-based nomogram to provide evolving, personalized survival estimates beyond baseline diagnosis.MethodsWe identified 490 patients with gcGBM from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database (2000–2022). The cohort was split into training (n = 343) and validation (n = 147) sets. Conditional survival probabilities—CS(t|s) = S(t + s)/S(s)—were calculated to assess the likelihood of surviving an additional t years given s years already survived. A Random Survival Forest with Recursive Feature Elimination (RSF-RFE) algorithm was employed to select the most parsimonious predictors. A CS-nomogram was constructed and evaluated using calibration curves, time-dependent AUC, and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA). An interactive web-based calculator was also developed.ResultsThe baseline 5-year overall survival (OS) was 14%. However, CS analysis revealed that the prognosis improved significantly over time: for patients who survived 3 years, the probability of surviving to year 5 rose to 69%, and reached 83% for those surviving to year 4. The RSF-RFE identified six key predictors: age, sex, tumor site, surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. The resulting CS-nomogram demonstrated high discriminatory power and clinical net benefit in both cohorts. Risk stratification effectively distinguished high-risk and low-risk groups.ConclusionPrognosis in gcGBM is a dynamic process. The risk of mortality is highest in the early years post-diagnosis but decreases substantially for long-term survivors. Our validated CS-nomogram and web-based tool provide clinicians with a practical means to offer personalized, time-adjusted survival estimates, facilitating better patient counseling and refined follow-up strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05281-w.

Introduction

Giant Cell Glioblastoma (gcGBM) represents a distinct and rare histological variant of IDH-wildtype GBM, accounting for less than 5% of all cases [1–3]. Characterized by a dominance of large, bizarre, multinucleated giant cells and a prominent reticulin network, gcGBM has long been recognized as a unique clinical entity [1, 3]. Historically, it was noted for its relatively younger age at diagnosis and a slightly more favorable prognosis compared to the much more common classic glioblastoma [2, 4, 5]. However, this better prognosis remains relative; the clinical course for most patients remains aggressive, and long-term survivors are few.

While the World Health Organization classification and genomic profiling have refined our molecular understanding of gcGBM, clinical management still largely mirrors the standard protocols for classic GBM [4, 6, 7]. The rarity of the disease means that most evidence stems from small-scale institutional series or case reports, which often lack the statistical power to provide a comprehensive landscape of long-term outcomes [4, 8–12]. Furthermore, traditional survival analysis—typically focused on the prognosis from the time of diagnosis—fails to account for the evolving nature of risk [13–15]. For a patient who has already survived one or two years post-surgery, their future risk profile changes significantly. This is where conditional survival (CS) analysis becomes crucial. By providing a dynamic assessment of life expectancy as time passes, it offers more grounded, practical information for both clinicians and patients during follow-up [16].

Moreover, the heterogeneity of gcGBM outcomes suggests that a one-size-fits-all prognostic approach is inadequate. While factors such as age and extent of resection are known to be influential, there is a lack of robust, integrated tools to quantify individual risk. Most existing SEER database-based studies on gcGBM focus on simple descriptive statistics or static Cox regressions [2, 5]. There is a clear clinical gap for a validated predictive model—such as a nomogram—that can synthesize demographic, tumor-specific, and treatment-related variables into a personalized survival probability [17]. In this study, we utilized the SEER database to bridge these gaps. By analyzing a large-scale, population-based cohort, we aimed to describe the dynamic changes in prognosis through CS analysis and develop a comprehensive nomogram to offer clinicians a practical tool for personalized survival prediction. By moving beyond static baseline prognosis, this research seek to refine counseling strategies and optimize the follow-up care for patients navigating the challenges of this rare malignancy.

Methods

Data source

A retrospective population-based cohort study was conducted using data from the SEER database. We identified patients diagnosed with intracranial gcGBM between 2000 and 2022 using the ICD-O-3 morphology code 9441/3 and primary site codes C71.0–C71.9. To ensure data quality and clinical relevance, the following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) diagnosis established solely via autopsy or death certificate; (2) unknown or ill-defined surgical status; (3) missing information regarding the administration of radiation or chemotherapy; and (4) incomplete records concerning follow-up or vital status.

The variables analyzed were categorized into three dimensions: demographics and socioeconomic status (age, sex, race, marital status, and median household income), tumor characteristics (anatomical location, extension status categorized as localized or regional/distant, and sequence of malignancy—specifically whether gcGBM was the first primary tumor), and therapeutic modalities (surgical extent, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy). The primary outcome was overall survival (OS), defined as the interval from the date of histological diagnosis to death from any cause or the date of the last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

The statistical framework of this study was designed to transition from static to dynamic prognostic assessment. We first introduced the concept of CS to address the evolving risk profile of gcGBM survivors. Unlike traditional survival analysis, which calculates the probability of survival from the time of diagnosis, CS accounts for the time a patient has already survived (s). The conditional survival probability, expressed as CS(t|s) = S(t + s)/S(s), provides a more clinically relevant estimate of the likelihood of surviving an additional t years, given that the patient has already survived s years.

To develop a robust predictive model, the study cohort was randomly partitioned into a training set and a validation set. Within the training cohort, we employed Random Survival Forest with Recursive Feature Elimination (RSF-RFE) to handle potential non-linear interactions and identify the most critical prognostic predictors by minimizing the out-of-bag error [18]. The RSF model was constructed using the ‘randomForestSRC’ package in R. The forest comprised 1000 trees, with each tree grown using a random subset of candidate variables at each node split. Node splitting was based on the log-rank test statistic, which maximizes survival difference between daughter nodes. Variable importance was assessed via permutation importance, where each predictor was randomly permuted and the resulting decrease in prediction accuracy (measured by the out-of-bag concordance index) was recorded. Recursive feature elimination (RFE) was then performed by iteratively removing the variable with the lowest importance score, and model performance was re-evaluated at each step using 10-fold cross-validation of the C-index. The optimal variable subset was selected at the point where the C-index plateaued, balancing predictive accuracy with model parsimony. These identified variables were then integrated to construct a CS-nomogram, capable of providing individualized survival estimates at various milestones.

It should be noted that the nomogram is constructed using baseline covariates to estimate the overall survival function S(t), and conditional survival is subsequently derived using the standard relationship CS(t|s) = S(t + s)/S(s). This approach represents a conditional, time-reframed survival estimation rather than a fully dynamic landmark model with time-updated covariates. The dynamic nature of the approach lies in reconditioning the survival probability on the time already survived, which yields continuously updated risk estimates as follow-up progresses.

The reliability and clinical value of the model were rigorously evaluated in both cohorts. Calibration curves were used to verify the agreement between predicted and observed outcomes, while time-dependent Area Under the Curve (Time-AUC) and Precision-Recall (PR) curves were generated to assess the model’s discriminatory power across different follow-up intervals. Furthermore, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was performed to determine the clinical net benefit of the nomogram in a real-world setting. We used SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to assess how clinical and treatment factors influenced model predictions. The importance of each variable was determined by its mean absolute SHAP value. Additionally, SHAP beeswarm plots were used to show the direction and distribution of these effects, specifically how factors like age and treatment type impacted predicted survival probabilities. Based on the calculated scores, a risk stratification system was established to classify patients into distinct high- and low-risk groups. Risk stratification was performed by calculating a composite risk score for each patient based on the nomogram. The optimal cutoff value to dichotomize patients into high- and low-risk groups was determined in the training set using the maximally selected rank statistic method (surv_cutpoint function, ‘survminer’ package in R). This approach identifies the cutpoint that yields the most significant survival separation while adjusting for multiple testing. The training-derived cutoff was then directly applied to the validation cohort to independently assess its discriminatory ability. Finally, to facilitate practical application, an interactive web-based calculator was developed to provide clinicians with real-time, personalized survival predictions.

All statistical procedures were conducted using R software, with a two-sided P < 0.05 defining statistical significance.

Results

Demographic and clinicopathological characteristics

A total of 490 patients diagnosed with giant cell glioblastoma were identified and randomly assigned to the training cohort (N = 343) and the validation cohort (N = 147). The majority of the study population was characterized by age less than 60 years (52.2%), white race (86.1%), and married status (58.2%). Regarding tumor topography and pathology, most lesions were located in the supratentorial region (84.3%), staged as localized (83.5%), and represented the patient’s first primary malignancy (85.9%). In terms of therapeutic interventions, aggressive management was prevalent, with 54.5% of patients undergoing gross total resection (GTR), while radiotherapy and chemotherapy were administered in 79.4% and 67.6% of cases, respectively. Comparison of overall and conditional survival

The traditional Kaplan-Meier estimates revealed a standard decline in survival probability over time; however, the CS analysis provided a more dynamic and clinically relevant prognosis for long-term survivors [16, 19]. The dynamic CS patterns for this cohort are illustrated in Fig. 1. The 5-year OS rate at the time of diagnosis (Year 0) was merely 14%. Notably, the probability of surviving an additional year increased progressively as patients successfully surpassed each post-diagnosis milestone. For instance, for patients who had already survived 3 years, the conditional probability of surviving until Year 5 rose significantly to 69%, representing a substantial improvement over the initial baseline. By Year 4, the 5-year conditional survival reached 83%, suggesting that the excess mortality risk associated with the malignancy stabilizes over time.

Variable selection and model development

To optimize feature selection for survival analysis, this study employed the RSF-RFE algorithm, a machine learning-based approach designed to identify the most parsimonious set of predictors while capturing complex nonlinear relationships within survival data (Fig. 2). Initially, an all-inclusive RSF model was constructed to calculate variable importance scores via permutation importance. Subsequently, a recursive elimination process was implemented to iteratively remove the least contributive variables, with the model’s predictive performance monitored at each step using the C-index through 10-fold cross-validation. To achieve a balance between robust performance and model simplicity, we identified and included the top six predictors—age, sex, tumor site, surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy—as the final optimal combination (Fig. 2). Subsequently, a CS-nomogram was developed to provide individualized predictions for the 5-year CS outcomes (Fig. 3).

Model evaluation

We rigorously evaluated the nomogram’s performance and clinical utility in both cohorts. Calibration curves showed excellent alignment between predicted and actual outcomes, indicating high reliability (Fig. 4A-B). Time-dependent AUC values across different intervals demonstrated robust discriminatory power (Fig. 4C). Additionally, DCA confirmed that the nomogram provided a superior net benefit over a broad range of threshold probabilities in both cohorts (Fig. 5A-B). Precision-Recall (PR) curves further validated the model’s stability in handling the imbalanced data common in SEER studies (Fig. 5C-D). These results collectively demonstrated that the nomogram is an accurate and clinically significant tool.

SHAP analysis identified radiotherapy as the most influential predictor, followed by age, chemotherapy, and surgery (Fig. 6A). Specifically, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and advanced age positively correlated with model predictions, whereas younger age and treatment omission drove negative SHAP values. While surgery exhibited a generally positive but mixed effect, sex and tumor site contributed minimally, with values clustered near zero (Fig. 6B). Overall, treatment-related variables dominated the model, outweighing demographic and clinical site factors.

Model application

To further demonstrate the clinical utility of the model, we calculated a nomogram-derived risk score for each patient, which served as the basis for risk stratification. By applying an optimal cutoff value, patients were successfully categorized into high- and low-risk groups. The resulting Kaplan-Meier survival curves exhibited a significant separation (Fig. 7A-B, P < 0.0001), indicating that the risk score possesses powerful prognostic value and can effectively distinguish patient outcomes. To enhance the practical accessibility of our findings, we successfully developed and deployed an interactive web-based tool (Figure S1). This online calculator allows clinicians to input specific clinicopathological variables and instantly generate individualized survival estimates. By bridging the gap between complex statistical modeling and bedside decision-making, this platform facilitates the real-world application of our nomogram in personalized clinical management.

Discussion

This study approaches gcGBM from a time-aware perspective, focusing not only on survival from the moment of diagnosis but on how prognosis shifts once patients have already lived through part of the disease course. This distinction is important. gcGBM is often described as having a relatively better prognosis than classic glioblastoma [2, 5], yet such a statement is abstract and offers limited guidance during follow-up. Our CS analysis showed that mortality in gcGBM was heavily concentrated in the early years after diagnosis. Once patients passed this initial high-risk period, their outlook improved markedly. For those who survived three to four years, the likelihood of reaching the five-year mark rose sharply, suggesting that the disease behavior in long-term survivors differs meaningfully from what is observed at presentation.

This pattern likely reflected an interplay between tumor biology and treatment response. gcGBM has long been associated with distinct biological features, including a higher frequency of TP53 alterations and a tendency toward more circumscribed growth, which may partly explain why durable survival is possible in a small subset of patients [20, 21]. At the same time, long-term survivors represent a selected group: their tumors were amenable to aggressive resection, responded to adjuvant therapy, and did not follow the rapidly progressive course typical of most glioblastomas. CS analysis does not obscure this selection; instead, it translates it into clinically meaningful information that evolves over time and can be used to recalibrate expectations.

The predictors retained in our model—age, sex, tumor location, surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy—are familiar factors in high-grade glioma care. What adds value here is not their novelty, but how their combined effects are interpreted in a time-dependent framework. Younger age continues to confer an advantage, reinforcing its central role in long-term survival. The strong influence of surgical extent and adjuvant treatment highlighted that, despite its distinctive histology, gcGBM remains fundamentally dependent on multimodal therapy [2, 4, 5, 22]. The prognostic relevance of tumor location likely reflected differences in resectability and functional tolerance rather than intrinsic aggressiveness alone.

Compared with prior SEER-based studies, the contribution of this work lies less in descriptive statistics and more in methodological integration. Traditional Cox models assume proportional hazards and provide a fixed estimate of risk, an assumption that fits poorly with diseases characterized by steep early mortality and a small tail of long-term survivors. By combining RSF-RFE, we were able to account for nonlinear relationships while distilling the model to a concise and clinically interpretable set of variables. The resulting nomogram does not replace clinical judgment; rather, it formalizes what clinicians already observe in practice—that prognosis in gcGBM is not static and must be reconsidered as time passes. While the individual analytical components used in this study—conditional survival, random survival forests, and SHAP analysis—are established methods, their integrated application to gcGBM represents a novel contribution. To our knowledge, this is the first study to characterize the dynamic prognostic trajectory of gcGBM through conditional survival analysis, to employ machine learning-guided feature selection specifically for this rare subtype, and to provide a publicly accessible, interactive prognostic tool for clinical use. The value of this work lies not in methodological invention, but in the meaningful synthesis of existing tools to address an unmet clinical need in a rare and understudied tumor.

The clinical utility of this approach is most apparent during long-term follow-up. For patients who have survived two or more years, standard baseline survival statistics become increasingly uninformative, as they do not account for the favorable prognostic selection inherent in survivorship. Conditional survival estimates address this gap by providing time-updated probabilities that reflect the patient’s current status. However, these estimates should be interpreted within their methodological constraints: the model relies on baseline covariates and does not capture changes in disease status, treatment modifications, or intercurrent comorbidities that may arise during follow-up. Accordingly, the CS-nomogram is best used as a supplementary counseling tool to contextualize prognosis at specific time points, rather than as a standalone predictive instrument. For the multidisciplinary care team—including physicians, nurses, and allied health professionals—these data serve as a practical tool for patient communication and care planning. Conditional survival estimates help clinicians set realistic expectations during the early high-risk period, while also enabling them to alleviate undue anxiety among long-term survivors. By incorporating these time-updated prognostic estimates into routine follow-up, the care team can transition from a purely disease-monitoring approach to a more comprehensive model that supports patients’ quality of life, psychosocial well-being, and informed decision-making as their prognosis improves.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. As with all studies based on SEER data, detailed molecular information, performance status, recurrence patterns, and specifics of treatment regimens are unavailable, even though these factors undoubtedly influence outcome. Furthermore, the study period spans from 2000 to 2022, encompassing cases diagnosed before the 2016 WHO classification update that formally required IDH-wildtype status for a glioblastoma diagnosis. As a result, a subset of cases from this earlier era lacks molecular confirmation of their IDH status. We acknowledge that this introduces potential diagnostic heterogeneity and that our SEER-coded gcGBM population is not entirely equivalent to a contemporary, molecularly curated cohort. Future studies with prospective, molecularly annotated datasets are needed to validate our findings in the modern diagnostic framework. Additionally, the SEER database records only whether chemotherapy was administered (yes/no) but does not specify the agents, dosages, or regimens used. Given that temozolomide-based chemoradiotherapy has become the standard of care for glioblastoma since the 2005, treatment protocols likely varied substantially across the study period. This limitation precludes a detailed analysis of the impact of specific chemotherapy regimens on conditional survival outcomes. Although this represents a relatively large cohort for such a rare tumor, gcGBM remains uncommon, and external validation in independent datasets is needed to strengthen generalizability. Importantly, improved conditional survival should not be equated with cure; even among long-term survivors, continued vigilance remains essential.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reframed prognosis in gcGBM as a dynamic process rather than a fixed estimate determined at diagnosis. CS analysis demonstrated that for patients who survived the early, high-risk years, the burden of mortality decreased substantially over time. By integrating population-based data with machine learning–guided modeling, we developed a practical CS- nomogram capable of providing individualized, time-adjusted survival estimates. This tool has the potential to improve patient counseling, refine follow-up strategies, and align clinical decision-making more closely with the actual disease trajectory of gcGBM. Survival time already lived is not merely an outcome—it is one of the most informative modifiers of future risk.
