A machine learning approach to metabolomics identifies putative biomarker candidates and dysregulated pathways for distinguishing gout from asymptomatic hyperuricemia in the Zhuang population
Authors: Yuxia Wei, Xiaoqiang Qiu, Li Su, Xiaofen Tang, Yuzhu Chen, Shun Liu, Dongping Huang, Xiaoyun Zeng, Yihong Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGout typically develops from hyperuricemia (HUA), but the metabolic alterations driving this transition remain poorly understood, limiting our understanding of disease pathogenesis.ObjectivesTo identify stage-specific putative biomarker candidates and to characterize dysregulated metabolic pathways distinguishing gout from HUA.MethodsWe conducted a targeted metabolomics assay on the baseline plasma samples from a Zhuang minority cohort using LC-MS/MS. The analyzed sample set comprised 38 HUA patients, 47 gout patients, and 52 healthy controls. Sex-stratified differential metabolite analysis was performed across all participants, as well as in female and male subgroups. Pathway enrichment analysis was carried out using the KEGG database. Machine learning approaches, including the Boruta algorithm and support vector machine (SVM), were employed for putative biomarker discovery and model evaluation in male participants.ResultsAmong all participants, 24 metabolites reached nominal significance (P < 0.05), but only uric acid remained significant after FDR correction. In sex-stratified analyses, no metabolite survived FDR correction in females, whereas in males, seven metabolites (flavone, glutamine, L-2-aminoadipic acid, L-pipecolic acid, N1-methyl-2-pyridone-5-carboxamide, phenyllactic acid, and uric acid) showed significant differences among healthy controls, HUA patients, and gout patients (FDR < 0.1). These metabolites were primarily involved in nitrogen metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, D-amino acid metabolism, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, and purine metabolism. Machine learning identified four metabolites (N1-methyl-2-pyridone-5-carboxamide, flavone, glutamine, and phenyllactic acid) that distinguished gout from healthy controls, with AUCs of 0.902 and 0.800 in the training and validation sets, respectively. A second model (L-pipecolic acid, glutamine, phenyllactic acid, and flavone) discriminated gout from HUA, achieving AUCs of 0.850 and 1.000. Sensitivity analyses excluding obese or hypertriglyceridemic participants confirmed the robust performance of both models.ConclusionsThis study suggests sex-specific metabolic alterations in gout and provides robust machine learning-based models for male participants. The identified metabolite signatures appear to extend purine metabolism to involve amino acid and energy metabolic pathways. These findings provide a basis for mechanism-targeted strategies in HUA management. Introduction

Gout is a common metabolic disease characterized by acute joint inflammation resulting from the deposition of urate crystals, which is triggered by persistent hyperuricemia (HUA) (Chinese Society of Endocrinology, 2020). Although the clinical diagnosis of acute gout attacks is relatively well-established, the management of its asymptomatic precursor stage, HUA, poses a significant challenge. Most HUA patients are asymptomatic yet already in a state of systemic metabolic dysregulation, and its prevalence in the population continues to rise (reported as high as 18.4%−30%) (Li et al., 2021; Vázquez-Mellado et al., 2004). However, serum uric acid levels alone have limited value in differentiating patients with asymptomatic HUA from those who have progressed to clinical gout (Afinogenova et al., 2022; Dehlin et al., 2020). Therefore, a deeper understanding of the metabolic reprogramming distinguishing gout from HUA is crucial for improving diagnostic accuracy, elucidating potential pathogenic mechanisms, and developing mechanism-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Our previous study found that the prevalence of HUA among Zhuang residents in Guangxi was higher than the national average in China (18.1% vs. 14.0%) (Zhang et al., 2022; Zhong et al., 2022).This heightened susceptibility is consistent with the global pattern that gout disproportionately affects certain ethnic groups (Afinogenova et al., 2022; Dehlin et al., 2020). Specifically, gout is more prevalent in some ethnic populations, with genetic factors contributing to ethnic differences in gout susceptibility. Additionally, regional dietary patterns and lifestyle factors may further modulate this risk. These observations underscore the scientific importance of investigating metabolomic profiles in underrepresented minority populations such as the Zhuang, who may carry unique metabolic phenotypes reflecting the interplay of genetic and environmental risk factors that are not captured in majority-population studies.

Metabolomics, capable of systematically capturing the overall changes of small-molecule metabolites in living organisms, provides a powerful tool for uncovering the pathophysiological mechanisms of diseases. In recent years, studies have attempted to describe the metabolic features of gout or HUA (Zhang et al., 2018; Shen et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022). However, many of the discovered metabolic markers show low reproducibility, and the pathway-level regulatory logic behind them remains unclear. Importantly, no systematic metabolomic studies have focused on specific Chinese ethnic minority populations, such as the Zhuang population group, despite their distinct genetic background and high HUA prevalence.

Based on the Guangxi Ethnic Minorities Prospective Cohort, this study integrates targeted metabolomics and machine learning algorithms to systematically reveal putative biomarker candidates distinguishing healthy controls, asymptomatic HUA, and clinical gout in the Zhuang population. Our objective is not only to identify stage-specific biomarker candidates with high diagnostic performance but also to delineate a “comparative map of metabolic pathway alterations” across disease stages, thereby generating hypotheses about metabolic mechanisms underlying gout pathogenesis. We anticipate that this work will provide new scientific evidence for differential diagnosis, clinical stratification, and intervention targeting key metabolic nodes in gout.

Methods

Study participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted using data from the baseline survey of the Guangxi Ethnic Minorities Prospective Cohort of Chronic Diseases, a community-based cohort. All gout patients (n = 112) from the cohort were included for metabolomic profiling. In addition, 54 hyperuricemia (HUA) patients and 54 healthy controls, frequency-matched by age and sex distribution, were randomly selected from eligible participants in the cohort. To minimize confounding effects on the metabolomic profile, individuals with diabetes, chronic kidney disease, chronic hepatitis, cancer, or those who had used medication for more than two consecutive months within the past six months were excluded. The final analytical sample consisted of 137 participants, including 47 gout patients, 38 HUA patients, and 52 healthy controls (Fig. 1). In this study, all gout patients were diagnosed by clinicians according to the 2015 ACR/EULAR classification criteria. HUA was defined as serum uric acid > 420 µmol/L in both males and females, according to the current Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Hyperuricemia and Gout in China (2019). Healthy controls had normal serum uric acid levels and no history of HUA, gout, or other related metabolic disorders.

Flow chart of the study subjects selection. Some people met multiple exclusion criteria

All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University (Approval ID: 20170206-1).

Sample preparation and LC-MS/MS analysis

Fasting blood samples were collected after a 10–12-hour overnight fast over the period from June 2018 to October 2019 during the baseline survey of the cohort, as previously described (Zhong et al., 2022). Serum and plasma were separated.by centrifugation within 8 h of collection. Each sample was then aliquoted into five portions using 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes and immediately stored at −80 °C. For metabolomic profiling, one tube per subject was selected and analyzed in June 2021.All samples were thawed only once prior to analysis. The metabolomic assays were performed in a single batch, with all samples processed contemporaneously to minimize batch-to-batch variation.

For metabolomic profiling, plasma samples were thawed at 4 °C and processed using a methanol/acetonitrile-based protein precipitation method. Briefly, 200 µL of plasma was mixed with 200 µL of cold water and 800 µL of cold methanol/acetonitrile (1:1, v/v). After ultrasonication in an ice-water bath for 60 min and incubation at −20 °C for 1 h, samples were centrifuged at 16,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was dried under vacuum and reconstituted in 100 µL of acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v) for LC-MS/MS analysis.

Chromatographic separation was performed on a Nexera X2 LC-30AD system (Shimadzu) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm; Waters). The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Mobile phase A consisted of 5% acetonitrile in water containing 20 mM ammonium acetate (pH 9.0), and mobile phase B was 100% acetonitrile. A gradient elution was applied as follows: 0–1 min, 95% B; 1–14 min, 95%-65% B; 14–15 min, 65%-35% B; 15–17 min, 35% B; 17–17.1 min, 35%-95% B; 17.1–20 min, 95% B (isocratic). Mass spectrometric detection was conducted on a QTRAP 5500 system (AB Sciex) operating in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode with positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI). The ESI source parameters for positive ion mode were: source temperature, 550 °C; ion source gas 1 (GS1), 40 psi; ion source gas 2 (GS2), 50 psi; curtain gas (CUR), 35 psi; ion spray voltage (ISVF), 5500 V. For negative ion mode, the parameters were identical except for the ion spray voltage, which was set to −4500 V.

Targeted metabolomics panel and chemical standards

The targeted metabolomics assay was established following the high-throughput targeted metabolomics method described by Cai et al. (2015), (Cai et al., 2015). The panel comprised approximately 200 metabolites, covering major energy metabolic pathways. All chemical standards for energy metabolites were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). LC-MS grade acetonitrile (catalog No. 1499230-935) and methanol (catalog No. 144282) were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Ammonium acetate (catalog No. 70221) was purchased from SigmaAldrich, and formic acid (catalog No. 06450) was obtained from Fluka.

No isotopelabeled internal standards were used. Instead, pooled quality control (QC) samples, prepared by combining equal aliquots of all individual samples, were injected after every eight experimental samples to monitor system stability, and data normalization was performed using the median intensity of QC samples.

No commercial spectral libraries (e.g., NIST, Metlin, HMDB) were used for metabolite identification. Identification was based on authentic chemical standards and literature‑reported MRM transitions. All metabolites in the panel have corresponding authentic chemical standards, purchased from commercial suppliers (primarily Sigma‑Aldrich). Accordingly, each of the 189 metabolites reported in this study was assigned MSI Level 1 (confirmed structure) according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) guidelines (Sumner et al., 2007).

Quality control and data preprocessing

A pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by combining aliquots from all individual samples and injected throughout the analytical sequence to monitor system stability. Metabolite peaks with a relative standard deviation (RSD) > 30% in QC samples were removed. Additionally, the analytical sequence was randomized to avoid order-related bias. Raw MRM data were converted to mzML format using ProteoWizard (v3.0.6526) and processed in R (v4.3.3) with the MRMAnalyzer package for peak extraction and alignment. The final dataset was normalized to the median of QC samples prior to statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis and putative biomarker candidates discovery

Baseline characteristics were compared using Chi-square tests, ANOVA, or Kruskal-Wallis tests, as appropriate. Principle component analysis (PCA) was utilized to explore global metabolic variations among groups. Supervised orthogonal partial least-squares discriminate analysis (OPLS-DA) was used to maximize the separation of metabolic profiles between groups. To identify robust putative biomarker candidates, a two-stage feature selection strategy was employed. First, metabolites were screened across groups using ANOVA, or Kruskal-Wallis tests, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction. Metabolites with FDR < 0.1 were subjected to pairwise post-hoc comparisons using Tukey’s HSD or Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction, as appropriate. Metabolites with FDR < 0.1 were visualized using box plots and hierarchical clustering heatmaps. Pathway enrichment analysis for the metabolites with FDR < 0.1 was conducted using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database via MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Second, the Boruta algorithm, a wrapper method based on a random forest classifier, was applied to this subset to determine definitive feature importance. The algorithm was run for 10,000 iterations using the Boruta R package to ensure stability, comparing original features against shadow features to select biologically relevant metabolites.

To evaluate the diagnostic potential of the putative biomarker panels, a support vector machine (SVM) classifier was implemented using the e1071 package in R. The dataset was randomly split into training (80%) and validation (20%) sets, preserving the class distribution. Model training with repeated 10-fold cross-validation was conducted using the caret package. Diagnostic performance was assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity, with ROC curves generated using the pROC package.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

A total of 137 participants were included in the final analysis: 47 with gout, 38 with asymptomatic HUA, and 52 healthy controls. No significant differences were observed among the three groups regarding demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, including gender, age, occupation, education level, family income, or residential area. Similarly, lifestyle factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, frequency of soup consumption, desserts, livestock meat and products, poultry meat and products, aquatic/seafood products, herbal tea/beverage, and physical activity level were comparable across groups. Clinical parameters, including systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP), liver enzymes (AST, ALT), renal function (serum creatinine, Scr), and lipid profiles (total cholesterol, HDL-C, LDL-C), also showed no significant differences among the three groups.

As expected, serum uric acid, body mass index (BMI), and triglyceride (TG) levels were significantly elevated in both the HUA and gout groups compared to healthy controls, and serum uric acid levels were significantly elevated in the HUA group than in the gout group (P < 0.05). Interestingly, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) was also significantly higher in the HUA group than in the gout group (P < 0.05). The complete baseline data are summarized in Table 1.

Metabolomic data quality assessment

LC-MS/MS analysis detected 189 distinct metabolite features. System stability was rigorously monitored using repeated injections of a pooled QC sample. The distribution of the RSD for these QC samples is shown in Figure S1 [see Additional File 1]. A total of 73.5% of all quantified metabolites exhibited an RSD below 30% in the QC samples, confirming satisfactory analytical reproducibility for subsequent differential analysis.

Metabolic profiles of healthy controls, HUA, and gout participants

Unsupervised PCA was performed to evaluate the overall metabolic landscape and assess data quality across all study samples. The tight clustering of QC samples indicated a good analytical reproducibilit. In contrast, healthy controls, HUA patients, and gout patients showed less obvious separation (Figure S2). To further explore the metabolomic profiles, supervised OPLS-DA was conducted. However, the OPLS-DA score plot revealed substantial overlap among the three groups, with no clear clustering pattern observed (Figure S3).

Next, univariate analysis was performed on 139 metabolites with RSD < 30% in QC samples across the three groups.Differential analysis identified 24 metabolites with nominal P < 0.05 (Table S1); however, after Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, only uric acid remained significant at FDR < 0.1. Given the potential confounding effect of sex on metabolic profiles, stratified analyses were subsequently performed by sex. In female participants, five metabolites showed nominal P < 0.05 (Table S2), but none survived FDR correction. In male participants, 26 metabolites showed nominal P < 0.05 (Table S3), and seven metabolites exhibited significant differential abundance at FDR < 0.1, including flavone, glutamine, L-2-aminoadipic acid, L-pipecolic acid, N1-methyl-2-pyridone-5-carboxamide, phenyllactic acid, and uric acid (Table S4). Hierarchical clustering analysis of these seven metabolites revealed distinct metabolic patterns among healthy controls, HUA patients, and gout patients in males, as shown in the clustering heatmap (Fig. 2). Gout patients were clearly separated from both healthy controls and HUA patients, whereas HUA patients and healthy controls showed less obvious separation.

Boruta feature selection of healthy controls, HUA, and gout patients

To identify a robust set of putative biomarkers for distinguishing gout patients from healthy controls, the Boruta feature selection algorithm was applied to six differential metabolites. This analysis identified four metabolites as important classifiers: N1-methyl-2-pyridone-5-carboxamide, flavone, glutamine, and phenyllactic acid (Fig. 5a). Similarly, to identify metabolites discriminating gout from HUA, Boruta analysis was performed on five differential metabolites, identifying L-pipecolic acid, glutamine, phenyllactic acid, and flavone as important classifiers (Fig. 5b).

Diagnostic models to differentiate HUA, gout, and healthy controls

An SVM classifier trained on four metabolites (N1-methyl-2-pyridone-5-carboxamide, flavone, glutamine, and phenyllactic acid) demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for distinguishing gout patients from healthy controls. In the training set, the model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.902, with a sensitivity of 90.9% and a specificity of 80.8%. This performance was well preserved in the validation set, with an AUC of 0.800, 100.0% sensitivity, and 66.7% specificity (Fig. 6a). To mitigate potential confounding from obesity and dyslipidemia, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding participants with BMI ≥ 30 or TG ≥ 2.26 mmol/L. The resulting SVM classifier retained strong discriminatory power, yielding an AUC of 0.903 (93.8% sensitivity, 86.4% specificity) in the training set and an AUC of 1.000 (100% sensitivity, 100% specificity) in the validation set, suggesting that the metabolite signature is largely independent of these metabolic covariates (Fig. 6c).

ROC curves of the support vector machine diagnostic model for the training and validation sets in male participants. a ROC curve for discriminating gout from healthy controls. b ROC curve for discriminating gout from HUA patients. c ROC curve for discriminating gout from healthy controls after excluding participants with BMI ≥ 30 or TG ≥ 2.26 mmol/L. d ROC curve for discriminating gout from HUA patients after excluding participants with BMI ≥ 30 or TG ≥ 2.26 mmol/L

For distinguishing gout from HUA, an SVM model trained on four metabolites (L-pipecolic acid, glutamine, phenyllactic acid, and flavone) also showed strong diagnostic performance. The model achieved an AUC of 0.85 (77.3% sensitivity, 90.0% specificity) in the training set and an AUC of 1.000 (80.0% sensitivity, 100% specificity) in the validation set, highlighting its potential to differentiate between these two clinically relevant stages (Fig. 6b). A sensitivity analysis excluding participants with BMI ≥ 30 or TG ≥ 2.26 mmol/Lfurther confirmed the robustness of this model, with an AUC of 0.988 (93.8% sensitivity, 100% specificity) in the training set, and an AUC of 1.000 (100% sensitivity, 100% specificity) in the validation set, indicating that the discriminatory ability is largely independent of obesity and hypertriglyceridemia (Fig. 6d).

Discussion

Asymptomatic HUA and gout represent a continuum of disease progression that is frequently associated with multiple metabolic disorders. Given that most HUA patients are asymptomatic and the prevalence of HUA has risen to 18.4%–30% (Li et al., 2021; Vázquez-Mellado et al., 2004), elucidating the metabolic alterations that drive the transition from asymptomatic HUA to symptomatic gout is essential for understanding disease pathogenesis and identifying potential therapeutic targets. This integrated metabolomic and machine-learning study systematically delineates the characteristic metabolic alterations and pathway dysregulation associated with health controls, asymptomatic HUA, and gout patients in the Zhuang population. Beyond replicating known associations, our work provides two high-accuracy SVM-based models for discriminating gout from healthy controls and from HUA, offering insights into the pathophysiological understanding of gout progression and suggesting actionable targets for early intervention.

In the analysis of all participants, 24 metabolites showed nominal significance without FDR correction, but only uric acid survived stringent FDR correction. When analyzed by sex, no metabolite remained significant in females, whereas seven metabolites exhibited robust differential abundance at FDR < 0.1 in males. This suggests a potential effect of estrogen on purine metabolism and renal uric acid excretion (Zhang et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2021). Premenopausal women typically have lower uric acid levels and a lower incidence of gout. In addition, lifestyle factors such as higher alcohol consumption and red meat intake in males may amplify metabolic perturbations associated with hyperuricemia and gout (Feraco et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). The relatively small sample size in the female group may also have contributed to this observation. Furthermore, stringent FDR correction may have limited the detection of additional differential metabolites. Therefore, future studies with larger sample sizes, particularly for female participants, are needed to validate these sex-specific metabolic findings.

In male participants, seven differentially expressed metabolites (flavone, glutamine, L-2-aminoadipic acid, L-pipecolic acid, N1-methyl-2-pyridone-5-carboxamide, phenyllactic acid, and uric acid) were observed, implicating multifaceted metabolic disturbances extending beyond purine metabolism. Among these, L-pipecolic acid, glutamine, and N1-methyl-2-pyridone-5-carboxamide have been reported in previous studies in China or abroad (Shen et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2018; Li et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023), although the ethnicity of the study populations was not specified, while the remaining four have not. As expected, uric acid was significantly elevated in both the HUA and gout groups compared with healthy controls, serving as internal validation of our analytical platform. More intriguingly, six non-purine metabolites distinguished gout patients from healthy controls, five of which (flavone, glutamine, L-2-aminoadipic acid, L-pipecolic acid, and phenyllactic acid) were also significantly elevated in gout compared with HUA. The overlap of these five metabolites between the gout-vs-control and gout-vs-HUA comparisons suggests that they may reflect gout-specific pathophysiological processes rather than merely hyperuricemia, including inflammatory activation (glutamine; Cruzat et al., 2018), oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction (L-2-aminoadipic acid and L-pipecolic acid; Sato et al., 2016), gut dysbiosis (phenyllactic acid; Xu et al., 2022; Cong et al., 2023; Hanlon et al., 2022), compensatory anti-inflammatory responses (flavone; Stachelska et al., 2025), and dysregulated NAD+ metabolism (N1-methyl-2-pyridone-5-carboxamide; Lenglet et al., 2016; Dettlaff-Pokora et al., 2025). The absence of significant changes in these metabolites in the HUA group further supports that asymptomatic HUA represents an earlier, primarily metabolic stage without overt inflammation or end-organ damage, whereas gout involves a broader systemic metabolic reprogramming.

Pathway enrichment analysis of these seven metabolites suggested significant disturbances in five key metabolic pathways: nitrogen metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, D-amino acid metabolism, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, and purine metabolism. The prominent enrichment of nitrogen metabolism is particularly noteworthy, as it aligns with the observed alterations in glutamine and other amino acid metabolites. This may reflect increased nitrogen flux to support purine and pyrimidine synthesis, immune cell proliferation, and inflammatory mediator production in gout patients (Kurmi et al., 2020; Cruzat et al., 2018; Newsholme et al., 2023). Arginine biosynthesis, intimately linked to nitric oxide production, may contribute to the endothelial dysfunction and cardiovascular comorbidities frequently observed in gout (Shimabukuro, 2023). The involvement of Nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism further supports the concept of NAD+-dependent metabolic stress in gout pathophysiology (Zhu et al., 2025). Recent urine metabolomics also indicated this pathway as significantly dysregulated in gout (Zhu et al., 2025). Given that NAD+ serves as both a redox cofactor in energy metabolism and a signaling molecule regulating cellular stress responses (Covarrubias et al., 2021), this dysregulation likely reflects heightened NAD+ consumption by activated immune cells, leading to redox imbalance and compromised energy homeostasis in gout. Although Purine metabolism showed the lowest enrichment ratio among significant pathways, its inclusion supports the central role of purine dysregulation and provides context for the novel findings within established disease mechanisms.

Our study found that a compact panel of four metabolites achieves high accuracy in distinguishing gout from healthy controls and from HUA. The first SVM classifier, comprising N1-methyl-2-pyridone-5-carboxamide, flavone, glutamine, and phenyllactic acid, implicated in NAD+ metabolism, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant responses, immune cell energy metabolism, and gut microbiota-mediated immune regulation, distinguished gout from healthy controls, yielding AUCs of 0.902 and 0.800 in the training and validation sets, respectively. The second model, comprising L-pipecolic acid, glutamine, phenyllactic acid, and flavone, involved in mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, immune metabolism, and inflammatory modulation, discriminated gout from HUA, achieving AUCs of 0.850 and 1.000 in the training and validation sets, respectively. This distinction captures the distinct metabolic reprogramming between asymptomatic HUA and clinical gout. The observed metabolite signatures, involving NAD+ metabolism, mitochondrial function, inflammation, and gut microbiota-host interactions, offer insights into the pathophysiological processes driving the transition to symptomatic disease, thereby contributing to our understanding of gout pathogenesis. Importantly, the metabolite signature remained robust after excluding obese or hypertriglyceridemic participants, suggesting that it may reflects gout-intrinsic rather than comorbidity-driven metabolic reprogramming.

Limitations and future perspectives

We acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, the targeted nature of the metabolomics platform may have restricted the scope of metabolite discovery. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; therefore, we cannot determine whether the observed metabolic alterations precede gout onset or result from chronic disease and its treatment. Third, although sufficient for initial discovery, the sample size particularly in the validation cohort, may limit generalizability. These limitations necessitate validation in larger cohorts. The intriguing discrepancies between our findings and some prior reports further underscore the need for such validation in larger, longitudinal, and multi-ethnic cohorts. Future research should focus on confirming the predictive value of these metabolic signatures for progression from HUA to gout and experimentally elucidating the mechanistic roles of newly identified biomarkers and key pathways in the inflammatory cascade.

Conclusion

In summary, this study suggests sex-specific plasma metabolic disturbances in male gout patients within the Zhuang population, characterized by dysregulation of nitrogen metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, D-amino acid metabolism, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, and purine metabolism. Two four-metabolite SVM classifiers showed high accuracy in distinguishing gout from healthy controls and from HUA, with performance maintained after controlling for obesity and dyslipidemia. These findings contribute to the understanding of gout as a systemic metabolic disorder and provide a basis for further exploration of mechanism-targeted strategies in HUA management.
