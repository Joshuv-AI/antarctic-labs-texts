Timely Matters: Predictors of Delay in Oral Cavity Cancer Patients Across the Care Continuum
Authors: Nicholas Correia, Kennedy Johnson, Megan Durr, Barbara A Grimes, Ann A Lazar, Urmimala Sarkar, Patrick K Ha, Laura Chen, Katherine C Wai, Ilya Likhterov, Sue S Yom, Jason W Chan, Alain P Algazi, Hyunseok Kang, William R Ryan, Chase M Heaton, Ivan El‐Sayed, Jonathan George, Mary Jue Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveLimited literature focuses on delays in accessing head and neck cancer (HNC) care across the entire care continuum. This study aims to describe the time to care from symptom onset to treatment completion and identify predictors of delays for oral cavity cancer patients.MethodsWe reviewed patients with oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma (OCSCC) treated with curative surgery followed by adjuvant treatment at a tertiary center. Delays were defined per published thresholds for: time to treatment initiation (TTI, diagnosis to surgery), time to adjuvant treatment (stratified by whether adjuvant treatment was at the same or different facility as surgery), and total treatment package time. We also measured symptom duration, reported median times, and used logistic regression to assess predictors of delay.ResultsAmong 93 patients, median (IQR) symptom duration was 17.4 weeks (IQR: 6.8–42.8); TTI 6.9 weeks (IQR: 4.4–10.4); time to adjuvant radiation 8.4 weeks (IQR: 7.4–9.3) at the same institution as surgery was performed and 9.3 weeks (IQR: 7.4–14) at a different facility; and total treatment package time 15.4 weeks (IQR: 12.6–20) for surgery and adjuvant radiation, and 16.3 weeks (IQR: 15–18.6) for surgery and adjuvant chemoradiation. 73% of patients experienced delays in reaching a healthcare facility, 39% in TTI, 89% in starting adjuvant treatment, and 64%–81% in total treatment time. In univariate analyses, late‐stage disease, residing in a community with higher social vulnerability index (SVI), longer time from surgery to pathology report, need for free flap, and social worker interaction were associated with higher odds of delay in any care interval following diagnosis. Higher SVI was associated with delays in multivariate analyses.ConclusionHNC patients experience delays during all phases of cancer care. Interventions and future research need to address social determinants of health and health system factors that contribute to multifactorial delays across the care continuum.Keywords: delays, head and neck cancer, health disparities

Introduction

The care pathway for head and neck (HNC) patients is complex, with extended time intervals at each phase that can worsen outcomes. HNC accounts for 4.5% of cancer diagnoses globally [1], with later‐stage disease associated with poorer outcomes. Due to the absence of standardized screening, approximately two‐thirds of HNC patients present with locally advanced and late‐stage disease [2]. Furthermore, durations in time are associated with tumor upstaging and decreased survival [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Liao et al. [9] found that increasing time from diagnosis to treatment initiation (time to treatment initiation, TTI) beyond 60 days was associated with an increased risk of mortality and recurrence in an underserved urban population with HNC. Associations between prolonged time intervals and mortality also hold true for time to adjuvant treatment and total treatment package time for HNC [4, 10]. Moreover, treatment timing has been found to vary by race, ethnicity, and income status, with patients from economically disadvantaged backgrounds and with limited English proficiency experiencing longer time intervals to care [11, 12]. While some literature is equivocal on the effect of prolonged time to care on clinical outcomes, timely access to cancer care is nevertheless emphasized in care delivery, and expediting access to care across different patient populations is central to addressing the stark disparities in outcomes [13].

Current literature on HNC care delays focuses on single intervals of care. The most established measure of timely care for HNC is the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Guidelines recommendation that time from surgery to initiation of adjuvant radiotherapy is 6 weeks or less, given increased mortality associated with delays [14, 15]. Though not yet integrated into NCCN guidelines, TTI over 60 days and total treatment package time over 100 days are also associated with poorer survival [9, 10, 16]. Given that each phase of HNC care is at risk for delays from both patient and health system‐level factors, further research is needed to understand HNC care intervals across the entire patient journey to better inform interventions that improve access to timely care.

This retrospective cohort study, therefore, aims to understand time to care and predictors of delays among patients with oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma (OCSCC) presenting for subspecialty care at a tertiary referral hospital that serves both urban and rural populations. Here, we define delays as TTI over 60 days, time to adjuvant treatment over 6 weeks, and total treatment package time over 100 days. Specifically, this study characterizes time to care from symptom onset to end of treatment, proportion of patients experiencing delays at each phase of care, and factors associated with delayed care following an OCSCC diagnosis. Better characterizing delays across the entire care pathway is critical to designing and measuring multilevel interventions that improve access to timely care.

Material and Methods

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study included data from the cancer registry at the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) and was approved by the UCSF Institutional Review Board.

Population and Care Process

Patients with biopsy‐confirmed OCSCC who were diagnosed and presented to care between January 2022 and December 2023 were included. Given the varying treatment courses across head and neck subsites, we selected oral cavity cancer as our focus given the general homogeneity of the recommended treatment course involving surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy. We excluded patients who had a prior diagnosis of HNC, presented with metastatic disease, did not have a documented encounter with a specialist at the UCSF Cancer Center, had established care at the UCSF Cancer Center prior to diagnosis (e.g., dysplasia surveillance), had surgery performed outside of UCSF, or had neoadjuvant treatment.

The general pathway for oral cavity cancer patients includes referral to subspecialty care, biopsy before or after subspecialty care evaluation, and presentation of the patient's case at a multidisciplinary tumor board. Patients in this study underwent surgery at the tertiary referral center. Adjuvant treatment was determined based on pathology and input from the multidisciplinary review board and was either performed at UCSF or at a facility closer to the patient's home. Of note, the multidisciplinary head and neck cancer care team for patients at UCSF includes a social worker embedded in the team.

Data Collection

We obtained dates for the following events from the cancer registry: cancer diagnosis, presentation to the UCSF head and neck surgical oncology service, surgery, initiation and completion of radiotherapy, and initiation and completion of chemotherapy. Additional variables in the dataset included patient sociodemographic characteristics, alcohol and smoking status, insurance coverage, vital status, TNM staging (AJCC 8th edition), social work involvement, timing of radiation oncology consultation, date of final surgical path report, history of dental extraction, details of any free flap procedures, and post‐operative complications (wound healing, flap failure, infection, etc.). We calculated the distance from the UCSF Cancer Center using the patients' zip codes at the time of diagnosis. Social work involvement was defined as any meaningful and substantive contact between a patient and a social worker (LICSW) at our tertiary care center, as documented in the patient's chart.

The duration of symptoms was extracted from the UCSF specialist's notes during the patient's first evaluation at the center and was used to derive the time interval between symptom onset and the first presentation to a health facility or diagnosis. Any ambiguity in the data abstraction was rectified by the first author and senior author. In patients with chronic oral cavity inflammatory changes, the duration was calculated from the date of the most recent symptom flare. Due to incomplete documentation of dates in the patient's care trajectory, we chose to define the date of the first presentation to a health facility as the earliest of the following two dates: the date of biopsy or the earliest documented encounter with any medical care provider in the electronic medical record noting symptoms or signs of oral cavity abnormalities prior to the patient's visit to UCSF.

To determine the social vulnerability index (SVI) [17], we cross‐referenced patients' counties with the 2022 CDC/Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry SVI data file. SVI is a composite metric of 16 social factors, grouped into 4 themes: socioeconomic status, household characteristics, racial and ethnic minority status, and housing type and transportation. The percentile rank of each social factor relative to other census tracts is summed and reported as a score for each domain. These rankings are then combined and rescaled to generate a composite SVI percentile rank. Higher SVI rankings are indicative of higher social vulnerability.

Statistical Analysis

Delays in the following intervals were defined based on existing literature showing an association with survival as follows: over 60 days (8.6 weeks) from diagnosis to treatment initiation (TTI) [9], over 6 weeks from surgery to adjuvant radiotherapy [4, 18], and over 100 days (14.3 weeks) for total treatment time [10, 19]. For comparison and consistency, these delay thresholds will be referenced in weeks as follows: symptom duration of 8 weeks, TTI of 8.6 weeks, time from surgery to adjuvant treatment of 6 weeks, and total treatment package time of 14.3 weeks. Of note, the symptom duration threshold of greater than 8 weeks was obtained from a study by Bhatia et al. [20] for cancers overall without an associated survival threshold. As a result, the univariate and multivariate analyses therefore only focused on intervals of care following OCSCC diagnosis with a survival association: TTI, time to adjuvant treatment, and total treatment package time.

We used descriptive statistics to summarize patients' sociodemographic information and disease characteristics. We used Mann–Whitney and chi‐square tests to compare differences in patient demographics and tumor characteristics by clinical tumor stage and delay in healthcare interval. To assess the association between predictors and a delay in any care interval following diagnosis (primary outcome), we employed univariate logistic regression models. Because traditional logistic regression resulted in quasi‐separation of the data, we used Firth's penalized likelihood logistic regression to model the delay in timing to adjuvant radiation (at UCSF and outside UCSF). We performed separate analyses of delays for patients receiving adjuvant treatment within the UCSF system and at outside treatment facilities to allow for comparison between the two groups and identify if timelines and associations differed significantly. We pre‐specified the following predictive factors: sex, ethnicity, race, primary language, tobacco use, alcohol use, clinical stage (stage I/II defined as early and stage III/IV defined as late), distance to the tertiary care facility, duration from surgery to pathology report, social work involvement, record of dental extraction, free flap procedure status, timing of radiation oncology consultation relative to surgical resection, post‐operative complications, and SVI.

We performed multivariable logistic regression when multiple variables were found to be statistically significant and calculated odds ratios and associated 95% confidence intervals, with a 2‐sided p value less than 0.05 as the cutoff for statistical significance. Spearman's rank correlation was used to assess the correlation between SVI and social work involvement. All analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Approximately half of the patients in our sample were insured publicly through Medicare. Patients traveled a median distance of 50 miles (IQR: 22–98) to reach the subspecialty care center. Time to Care and Delays Throughout the Head and Neck Cancer Pathway

Given the limitations in retrospective data collection, we were most consistently able to extract durations for the time to presentation, TTI, time to adjuvant treatment, and total treatment package time. The median duration from the onset of symptoms to presentation at a health facility or diagnosis was 17.4 (IQR: 6.8–42.8) weeks (Figure 1, Table S1). Compared to a threshold of 8 weeks, 73% of patients had a delay in presentation to care. Once diagnosed, TTI was a median of 6.9 weeks (IQR: 4.4–10.4), with 39% of patients experiencing delays. The time from surgery to initiation of adjuvant treatment at UCSF or at an additional facility was 8.4 weeks (IQR: 7.4–9.3) and 9.3 weeks (IQR: 7.4–14), respectively. Among those receiving adjuvant treatment, 89% of patients experienced delays in care compared to a threshold of 6 weeks. Median total treatment package time for surgery with adjuvant radiation was 15.4 weeks (IQR: 12.6–20) and median total treatment package time for surgery with adjuvant chemoradiotherapy was 16.3 weeks (IQR: 15–18.6). Delays affected 64% and 81% of patients in these treatment intervals, respectively.

Factors Associated With Delays in Care Following Cancer Diagnosis

When examining a delay in any time interval following diagnosis, stage of disease (late versus early; OR = 2.56, 95% CI [1.07, 6.1], p = 0.034), need for a free flap procedure (OR = 4.77, 95% CI [1.90, 12.0], p = 0.001), social worker involvement (OR = 4.16, CI [1.72, 10.1], p = 0.002), and residing in a location in the third SVI quartile (OR = 6.75, 95% CI [1.35, 34], p = 0.020) all were associated with higher odds of care delays on univariate analyses (Figure 2). Duration from surgery to pathology report (OR = 1.10, 95% CI [1.02, 1.18], p = 0.013) was also associated with higher odds of delay following diagnosis. Multivariate analysis, including these significant variables, indicated an independent association between living in a community in the third SVI quartile (OR = 7.23, 95% CI [1.30, 40.1], p = 0.024) and odds of a delay following diagnosis (Table 3). Time from surgery to pathology report was excluded from multivariate analysis because it is structurally embedded within the outcome and its significance is not clinically or causally interpretable. Furthermore, to assess for potential association between SVI quartiles and social work involvement, a Spearman's rank correlation was calculated and demonstrated a weak correlation (p = 0.06, 95% CI [−0.15, 0.26]).

We also examined the individual intervals of care (Table S2). For TTI on univariate analyses, those with higher SVI (OR 6.49, 95% CI [1.35, 31], p = 0.019) and need for free flap (OR = 3.70, 95% CI [1.53, 8.9], p = 0.004) had higher odds of delay. Increased duration from surgery to pathology report (OR = 1.06, 95% CI [1.003, 1.11], p = 0.04) was associated with a higher odds of delay in TTI. In terms of time to adjuvant radiation for those receiving treatment at an outside facility, patients who lived further from UCSF had lower odds of delay (OR 0.78, 95% CI [0.61–0.995], p = 0.046), potentially in the context of receiving radiation closer to their home. For total treatment package time, patients with late‐stage disease had higher odds of delays compared to early‐stage disease (OR 5.42, 95% CI [2.20, 13.7], p = 0.0003), which is in line with the need for multimodality treatment. Patients who had a record of a dental extraction performed (OR: 3.73, 95% CI [1.30, 10.7], p = 0.015), those who underwent a free flap procedure (OR: 3.55, 95% CI [1.47, 8.60], p = 0.005), those with longer time from surgery to pathology report (OR = 1.07, 95% CI [1.01, 1.14], p = 0.014), and those with social work involvement in their case (OR: 8.87, 95% CI [3.0–26], p = < 0.01) were also at an increased odds of delay in total treatment time.

Discussion

Our findings reveal that delays affect a high proportion of HNC patients across the entire patient journey, greatest among time to initiation of adjuvant treatment and total treatment package time. While variations may exist in the definitions of delays at each phase of care, these data highlight that delays accumulate across the entire patient care pathway from multiple factors. Higher SVI was independently associated with an increased odds of experiencing a delay following diagnosis, underscoring the known influence of community‐level factors and structural barriers to care. Interestingly, social work involvement was associated with increased odds of having a care delay, which may reflect accurate screening for social support. While our academic medical center offers integrated social services, additional resources more comprehensively address domains captured by SVI. Furthermore, undergoing a free flap procedure and late‐stage disease were associated with delays, in line with increased multidisciplinary coordination of care. Overall, these data highlight compounded delays for patients, influenced by a combination of social determinants of health and likely complex care coordination across specialties and health systems. These findings allude to a need to holistically address cancer care delays across the entire care continuum.

Findings from this study reinforce prior literature that describe ubiquitous delays throughout the patient care pathway. Time from surgery to the start of adjuvant treatment within 6 weeks is the most established and studied HNC quality metric included in the NCCN guidelines [14]. 89% of patients in our cohort experienced delays in initiating adjuvant radiation, consistent with findings from a National Cancer Database study which reported that a 56% majority of HNC patients between 2006 and 2014 began adjuvant treatment beyond 6 weeks after surgery [15]. Our study's inclusion of oral cavity cancer patients alone may explain the higher proportions of delay in initiating adjuvant treatment, as studies have reported that patients with oropharynx, larynx, and hypopharynx tumors are statistically significantly more likely to receive timely adjuvant treatment [21]. Further literature reinforces that these challenges of delays throughout the care continuum are ubiquitous worldwide including in countries like the United States, Thailand, and Australia [3, 22, 23]. Finally, this study adds to the expanding body of literature aimed at understanding the effect of structural and social factors on the perpetuation of health inequities, reemphasizing the need to integrate social support as a backbone of care delivery [24, 25, 26, 27].

Recognizing delays across the entire patient care continuum holds implications for multidisciplinary and multilevel interventions that address an interplay of individual, community, health system, and health policy‐level factors. To promote earlier presentation, national media campaigns and targeted educational initiatives are effective in improving knowledge [28, 29], increasing earlier‐stage presentations, and reducing diagnostic delays in head and neck, breast, and other cancers [30, 31]. In addition, targeted education, decision‐making support tools, and clinical guidelines for diagnostic workup have been shown to improve diagnostic abilities and screenings for oral cancer [32, 33]. These solutions could be instrumental in supporting primary care providers, who play a critical role in initially triaging suspected malignancies. To address the complex care coordination involved in HNC, interventions such as Denmark's “package solutions,” a model that provides pre‐booked appointments for outpatient evaluation, imaging, diagnostic surgical procedures, and joint consultations should be considered; these interventions have streamlined care and reduced patient burden [34, 35]. Additionally, interventions combining provider closed‐loop communication with prospective patient tracking tools have led to earlier treatment initiation, improved rates of adjuvant radiation within 6 weeks of surgery, and reduced total treatment package times to under 100 days [36]. Finally, enhanced patient navigation programs, such as the Navigation for Disparities and Untimely Radiation thErapy (NDURE), have shown promise in targeting delays in adjuvant treatment and may be adapted to different contexts and phases of the care continuum [37]. In light of emerging evidence demonstrating the benefits of neoadjuvant therapy in HNC patients [38], increased multidisciplinary and multifacility collaboration will be central to expedite care.

Across all phases of the cancer care pathway, social determinants of health and structural barriers to care must also be systematically addressed. Health systems such as the University of Illinois Health and Kaizen Health have implemented programs to mitigate transportation insecurity by offering free rides to cancer patients, many of whom reside in communities with high SVI scores [39]. Divi and colleagues successfully integrated social drivers of health into a care pathway checklist, prompting early discussion of social support, housing, and transportation needs to minimize delays and ensure timely initiation of radiation therapy, irrespective of treatment facility [40]. Further research is needed to evaluate the impact of these interventions on health outcomes. Furthermore, studies should aim to isolate which specific SVI domains most strongly contribute to delays in the cancer care pathway. This is essential in addressing the systemic structures that continue to inflict harm on vulnerable communities and hinder delivery of equitable care.

Limitations

This retrospective cohort study has several limitations. We included patients with a specific subsite of HNC presenting to a single academic medical center; therefore, limiting the external validity and generalizability of our findings to the broader HNC population. Approximately half of the patients in our sample received adjuvant therapy; therefore, our ability to assess delays related to initiation of adjuvant therapies was limited, and delays occurring later in the continuum may be underrepresented in our analysis. Additionally, our relatively small sample size may have constrained our analysis and identification of statistically significant associations between predictive factors and delays in multivariate analyses. Limited documentation of encounters, tumor characteristics, and dates along the cancer care pathway may have also led to underreporting or over reporting of the measured time intervals. Moreover, symptom duration was patient‐reported and therefore subject to recall bias. To address this, future studies should involve larger, more diverse cohorts that encompass various HNC subsites and employ more robust methods to accurately capture time points in the care continuum.

Conclusions

From symptom appraisal to completing treatment, this study reaffirms the ubiquitous delays along the entire patient journey and intricate complexities that underlie the care trajectories for HNC patients. Given that delays are driven by both the healthcare system and patient‐level factors, future interventions ought to address these multifaceted factors and include patients, families, clinicians, and health system stakeholders in their design and implementation to fully optimize care trajectories and clinical outcomes.
