Reducing syndemics of non-communicable chronic diseases in Mayan Indigenous population through community-based participatory research: a mixed-methods study protocol
Authors: Emilio Motte-García, Cinthya Cadena-Trejo, María Fernanda Ramírez-Flores, Alfonso Gastelum Strozzi, Adalberto Loyola-Sanchez, María Victoria Fernandez-Garcia, Karen G Castillo-Hernandez, Kenia Nayrobi López-Herrera, Maarten M H Lahr, Erik Buskens, Ingris Pelaez-Ballestas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndigenous Mayan-Yucatecan communities in Mexico have a high prevalence of chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as diabetes, hypertension, obesity and rheumatic diseases (RMDs). According to the syndemic theory, these diseases combined with social, economic and cultural factors affect the quality of life. The aim of this protocol is to describe the methodological process to create, implement and evaluate a Syndemic-Based Care Model (SCM), using a Community Based-Participatory Research (CBPR) strategy in three Mayan-Yucatecan communities.Methods and analysisThis is a convergent mixed-methods protocol. The quantitative component is a before-after study, and the qualitative component is an ethnographic study. The intervention will be a SCM co-constructed with Mayan communities based on their particular needs and aiming for reduction of the negative impact of NCD/RMD through a multidisciplinary approach. We will follow four phases of the CBPR: (1) situational analysis, through community censuses and semistructured interviews to understand the prevalence of NCDs and the syndemics in these communities; (2) co-construction of the elements of a SCM based on the health priorities identified by researchers, community members and healthcare workers; (3) implementation of this SCM and (4) evaluation of the SCM through (1) statistical analyses involving the construction of a syndemic index through stepwise logistic regression of the normalised and standardised key clinical, social and economic variables; interval and ratio variables will be normalised by their z-score and categorical variables will be one-hot encoded; similarity and social networks analysis with clustering to identify syndemic subpopulations; and cost-effectiveness and cost-utility analyses using Markov modelling and (2) narrative and thematic qualitative analysis of the SCM’s implementation and impact on community members’ health, function and quality of life.Ethics and disseminationResearch ethics boards of participant institutions approved this research protocol. This project will be presented to municipal authorities, community meetings and community leaders for observation and acceptance. For people who wish to participate, informed consent will be provided written and verbally in Spanish or Mayan-Yucatecan according to the participant preferences, and it can be signed by either autograph or fingerprint. The results of this research will be disseminated to various groups: (a) local and regional authorities of the Mexican health system and municipal authorities; (b) the participating communities will be informed in an assembly of the results and (c) academic dissemination will be done through publications in public science journals and institutional press releases and will also be presented at national and international congresses or symposia.

Introduction

Non-communicable chronic diseases (NCDs) cause the highest percentage of morbidity, mortality and disability worldwide.1 2 NCDs especially impact developing countries, causing significant economic strains. Managing these conditions requires a high amount of resources and affects individuals and nations by decreasing workforce productivity.3 4

Mexico has experienced a rapid increase in the prevalence of NCDs, such as diabetes mellitus type 2 (DM2), hypertension, obesity and rheumatic diseases (RMDs). A lack of public policies and social inequities hinder the management of these health conditions, especially in the poorest regions of the country like the southern states that present the most complex health landscapes and social panoramas, such as high density of marginalised Indigenous communities.5 The Mexican state of Yucatán is a region where Mayan-Yucatecan communities make up 68% of its total population.6 7 These Indigenous communities are subject to inequities produced by discrimination and marginalisation, which is reflected in higher poverty rates (>50%),8 social lag and lack of access to healthcare services (eg, 77.6% of Mayan do not have access to health protection services).9 These social factors result in a high incidence and prevalence of NCDs in Yucatán, including a high prevalence of adult overweight (ie, 40.6%),10 DM2 (ie, 10.7%), hypertension (ie, 21.7%)11 and RMDs like lower back pain (ie, 10%), osteoarthritis (ie, 10.4%) and rheumatoid arthritis (ie, 1.1%).12

This complex health situation in Yucatan can undermine the efforts to provide an adequate standard of care for NCDs, especially in Mayan-Yucatecan communities, including diagnostic delays and lack of access to treatment in most remote communities. Often, health programmes and policies disregard the social landscape and specific needs of these communities, contributing to institutional discrimination13 14 and low cultural sensitivity that result in ineffective health interventions.

Therefore, Mayan communities face many adverse factors that worsen NCDs’ effects and require interventions based on a framework that facilitates a deep understanding of biological, social, political and environmental interactions that allows the development of more comprehensive health programmes.15 The Syndemics Theory provides a suitable framework for this as it focuses on the interactions among different diseases along with diverse socioeconomic and cultural factors to explain negative health outcomes and increased burden of disease in populations with adverse social contexts.16 17 The concept of ‘syndemics’ differs from the concepts of ‘comorbidity’ and ‘social determinants of health’ because it actively analyses the interactions between biological, sociopolitical and environmental aspects in the worsening of health at individual and population levels.18

Recognising the syndemics of NCD in Indigenous populations to create culturally sensitive interventions requires adopting a participatory research strategy, such as the Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR).19 Using the participatory lens of CBPR strategies allows for the creation of stronger context-specific knowledge on the health and healthcare needs of specific communities. These culturally sensitive interventions are expected to enhance quality of life within the Indigenous Mayan-Yucatecan population while contributing to the reduction of health inequities.

This manuscript aims to describe a methodological process to create and implement a syndemic-based care programme to reduce the NCD’s syndemics in three Mayan-Yucatecan communities, using a CBPR strategy. In addition, we aim to describe a methodology for evaluating the effects of the programme on the syndemics of these communities, developing a syndemic index that integrates biological, sociological and economic variables along with people’s narratives on their experiences of living with NCDs and the impact of this programme in their functional capability and quality of life.

Objectives

To reduce the syndemic burden of the most prevalent NCDs (ie, musculoskeletal disorders, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia and obesity) in the Maya population of the communities of Chankom, Komchén and Yaxcabá in Yucatán, Mexico, through the co-construction of a Syndemic Care Model using a community-based participatory research approach. Methods and analysis

Study population

Three Maya-Yucatecan communities were selected for this study based on three criteria: (1) high utilisation of the Indigenous language, (2) high social marginalisation indexes and (3) geographic proximity, either to each other (Chankom and Yaxunah) or to an urban centre (Komchén).

Chankom and Yaxunah are located within the Mayan jungle near the Chichén Itzá archaeological site, approximately 135 and 90 min by vehicle, respectively, from Mérida, Yucatán’s capital. These communities experience a tropical climate with consistent annual temperatures averaging 30°C, peaking during summer months.

In contrast, Komchén, a peri-urban municipality of Mérida, is accessible within 20 min and offers significantly greater infrastructure and services compared with the rural communities.

These strategically selected communities exhibit distinct sociodemographic and geographic characteristics, enabling comparative evaluation of the Syndemic Care Model (SCM) across diverse contexts. Key differences between the communities are summarised in figure 1.

Study design

This will be a convergent mixed-method study20 using a CBPR strategy in four phases (figure 2). The quantitative component will be a before-after study, and the qualitative component will be an ethnographic study. Both components will have the same methodological and theoretical weight and will be implemented simultaneously, allowing for feedback between both components to generate merged results and conclusions.

The study is scheduled to begin in January 2025 with fieldwork and the co-construction of the SCM planned for completion within the first year. Implementation and follow-up evaluations will take place from January 2026 through December 2028.

Our CBPR strategy aims to increase knowledge and understanding of health issues through an alliance between community members, service providers, decision and policy makers, and academics.15 19 This strategy will start when researchers present to local authorities and ask for their permission to start the study in their communities. Community healthcare personnel will also be informed about the project to look for alliances. Researchers will communicate the aims of the project to the population through meetings, seeking permission, and individuals will be invited to participate as community collaborators (CCs), requiring an interest in the health sector, bilingual proficiency in Mayan and Spanish, literacy skills in both languages, and sufficient time availability. Ten sessions will be conducted, each lasting approximately 2 hours.

The first three sessions focus on two critical components: (1) raising awareness of ethical considerations and privacy protection through a structured dialogue exercise. This activity reinforces participants’ commitment to maintaining confidentiality and abstaining from sharing sensitive information or research data. To ensure adherence to these principles, community collaborators will be rigorously selected based on their understanding of ethical guidelines. (2) The sessions also provide an introduction to the study’s objectives and include training on the informed consent process. During this training, collaborators will engage in role-playing exercises to practise obtaining and documenting informed consent, including proper completion and signing of consent forms among themselves.

The next three sessions will introduce measurement instruments. Each questionnaire item will be explained individually. Two paper-based practice sessions between pairs will be performed to identify ambiguities and ensure proper question interpretation.

Two training sessions will be completed in order to standardise clinical measurements such as capillary blood glucose (Accu-Chek Instant glucometer), blood pressure (Omron HEM-7120 arm monitor), weight (Omron HBF-514C scale) and height (AVANUTRI 315 portable stadiometer).

Sessions will cover basic concepts (DM, glucose, blood pressure, anthropometrics) and normal parameter ranges. Six paired measurement exercises will provide bidirectional feedback to ensure accuracy.

The final two sessions will be used to train collaborators on using digital tablets (Samsung Galaxy Tab A9) for electronic data collection (questionnaires and measurements). Collaborators’ feedback will be incorporated into the final digital questionnaire. Two practice survey exercises will be conducted.

Phase 1. Situational analysis

This phase aims to understand the health needs of each Mayan community and estimate the epidemiology of NCDs through a series of mixed methodologies (ie, quantitative and qualitative components).

The quantitative component will estimate the prevalence of NCDs and identify participants that might benefit from participating in the SCM. We will implement a census using the Community Oriented Program for the Control of Rheumatic Diseases (COPCORD) methodology.21 Because this is a census-based study in relatively small Indigenous populations, a sample size calculation is not applicable, and all population older than 18 years is expected to be surveyed.

During the COPCORD census, CCs will go house-to-house in the communities and will read the informed consent, and after acceptance, they will apply a digital questionnaire to adult subjects that meet the inclusion criteria (table 2). This questionnaire will assess the following: (1) the presence of joint pain, stiffness and inflammation in the last 6 months to detect possible cases of RMDs; (2) self-reported comorbidities; (3) sociodemographic information; (4) healthcare services utilisation and expenses generated by disease; (5) quality of life using EuroQol-5D questionnaire;22 (6) functioning, using the Health Assessment Questionnaire-Disability (HAQ-DI)23 and a validated instrument we created based on the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health that assess body functions, activities, participation and environmental factors24 25 and (7) we will also screen for NCDs (ie, DM2, hypertension, malnutrition) through capillary blood glucose and blood pressure, along with weight and height measurements. All instruments are validated in the Maya-Yucatecan population.26 The complete questionnaire and description of the variables in each questionnaire to be applied can be found in the supplementary material (online supplement 1 and 2).

Participants detected as positive cases (figure 2) will be examined by general physicians and specialists in their households. International guidelines will be used for the confirmatory diagnosis of any NCDs. Participants diagnosed with any of the conditions under study will comprise the group that will participate in the co-construction of the SCM.

Qualitative component: an ethnographic study will delve into the sociocultural and environmental processes27 that influence NCDs’ prevalence and increase their adverse health effects. Once the community accepts researchers, an anthropologist and a nurse will review their health files. Through field work for at least 1 year, ethnographic techniques and dialogues with different community members will be carried out to understand and analyse the context of the communities (figure 2).

First, focal groups will be performed with CC to get to know about communities and their healthcare needs. A convenience sample of individuals with NCDs and their family caregivers will be recruited for in-depth, semistructured interviews. Interviews will be conducted in participants’ homes until saturation criteria are achieved, ensuring comprehensive exploration of the research phenomena.28 These semistructured interviews will be conducted using an interview guide with thematic axes (table 2). These interviews will also aim to understand their functioning, quality of life, comorbidities and healthcare services utilisation to complement the results of the quantitative component. Likewise, a non-participant observation guide with the same thematic axes as the semistructured interview will be used, and the observations will be recorded in a field journal. The audio of all interviews will be recorded prior to consent, and CCs will also be translators to facilitate communication with Mayan speakers. Local healthcare personnel will be asked for semistructured interviews to understand their experiences and approach to providing care in these communities (table 3).

Phase 2. Intervention development

This phase has the objective of co-constructing an intervention model based on information from a preliminary analysis of the census, interviews, focal group and field journal results from the previous phase to address the health needs and priorities of each community. Subsequently, researchers will draft a proposal for a SCM that will be presented to the participant communities.

Through community meetings with participants and CCs, researchers will adapt and change the proposed SCM to apply solutions that will ensure appropriate attention to the particular health, social reality and values of each community. The data from the first phase will be presented in a culturally sensitive manner to community members and we will facilitate horizontal decision-making with community partners (figure 2).

The SCM will include strategies not only for NCDs’ management but also to address needs perceived by the community and observed by researchers on social and structural aspects such as multidisciplinary healthcare, connectivity within the Mexican healthcare system and shared governance. We also anticipate the SCM will address transportation barriers, insufficient medical staff and lack of access to medications based on previously documented research about health barriers in Mayan-Yucatecan communities.29 All elements of the SCM will have to be approved by the community.

Phase 3. Intervention implementation

The aim for this phase is to implement the co-constructed SCM in participant communities. We will identify community spaces to implement the components of the SCM with the guidance of community members and CCs. Initially, community meetings will be held once a week, where decisions on the SCM content will be made. This reiterative process will help maintain horizontality during the decision-making on the implementation of the intervention.

Phase 4. Intervention evaluation

This phase aims to evaluate the quantitative and qualitative impacts of the SMC interventions. The acquired data will be processed for completion; only subjects with full records will be considered for analysis.

Quantitative component: the main outcomes measured are the change in the syndemic index and in the composition of the network analysis. Secondary outcomes include quality of life (measured by the EuroQol), frequency of health services utilisation such as hospitalisation or emergency procedures related to NCDs, disability measured by HAQ-DI and clinical measures such as weight, capillary glucose and blood pressure levels. Once the SCM is implemented, CCs will carry out follow-up questionnaires to compare outcomes at 6, 12, 18 and 24 months (figure 2).

Descriptive statistics will be used to estimate the prevalence and distribution of NCDs in each population. In order to evaluate syndemics in the populations, we propose creation of the syndemic index, a metric capable of measuring the co-occurrence and interaction of health and social determinants representing an individual’s or community syndemic burden. To develop the index (online supplement 3), a stepwise logistic regression model will first be used to identify variables significantly associated with NCDs, including clinical conditions, comorbidities, socioeconomic status, healthcare accessibility and quality of life. Each selected variable will then be normalised and aggregated to generate an individual-level score ranging from 0 (no syndemic burden) to 1 (maximum syndemic burden). This index will capture the multidimensional nature of syndemics, enabling comparison across individuals and communities. Although primarily applicable to individuals with multiple co-occurring conditions, the index will also be calculated for participants without NCDs to assess the distribution of risk factors in the general population. As such, the syndemic index will serve both as a descriptive epidemiological tool and as a key outcome variable for evaluating the effectiveness of the SCM in reducing syndemic burden over time.

We will perform similarity network analyses and social network analyses (online supplement 4) using the variables included in the syndemic index to determine connections between patients and populations.29 30 Each participant will be represented as a node within the network, and connections between nodes will be established using similarity metrics, specifically the Jaccard index for categorical variables and Euclidean distance for continuous variables. The resulting similarity matrix will be processed using the Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise algorithm to detect naturally occurring clusters of individuals who share similar syndemic profiles. These clusters will then be analysed to characterise distinct syndemic subpopulations, offering insight into how certain combinations of social and health-related factors co-occur in specific community segments. By mapping these relationships, the network analysis will facilitate the identification of high-burden clusters, inform the tailoring of interventions and serve as a basis for evaluating changes in syndemic structure over time in response to the implementation of the SCM.

For the model evaluation, the before-after intervention change of all measured parameters will be estimated, including the syndemic index and dynamic network analyses. The economic evaluation comprises estimating the cost of the intervention and subsequently a cost-utility and cost-effectiveness evaluation from both a societal and healthcare perspective. The syndemic index will be used as a continuous variable to calculate the cost per unit decrease in this index.31 Network analyses will also be used to determine the cost of the SCM intervention per syndemic cluster and the cost of syndemic cluster transitions following the SCM with Markov models.31

A comparative analysis will be performed between the three communities, to account for the heterogeneity in terms of socioeconomic and geographical characteristics of each community. The analysis will be performed using R software, Python and the graphical representation of networks using Gephi software.

Qualitative analysis: semistructured, in-depth interviews will be conducted in patients using an interview guide to evaluate functioning, quality of life, comorbidities and healthcare services utilisation during the follow-ups. The CCs will translate interviews from Mayan-Yucatecan to Spanish and other CCs will verify the accuracy of the translation. Field journal notes, semistructured and in-depth interviews, and focal group records performed in the four phases will be transcribed. Then, transcripts will be processed through ATLAS.ti Software. Subsequently, thematic and narrative analyses will be carried out to reconstruct the trajectories of healthcare participants with NCDs and the elements that create a syndemic.

The thematic analysis will be done under a constructivist view and involves grouping information into relevant themes. Interviews will be read, paragraphs will be organised into free codes to form code networks, and then we will construct themes or categories.32 Once the main themes are identified, a narrative analysis will be conducted, as well as identifying underlying elements of events to establish the context of the narrative.33

Analyses will be performed for three aspects: (1) coding between researchers’ peers, (2) discussion with CCs and (3) dialogue between theoretical approaches that explain how processes of diseases interact with contexts where social and structural inequities can influence individuals’ health, like the Syndemics Theory18 or The Theory of Networks34 (figure 2).

Mixed-methods analysis: a mixed-methods analysis20 will be conducted, integrating the separate qualitative and quantitative analyses into a synthesis that allows for a complete understanding of the processes and impact of the SCM implemented. The ultimate aim will be for the qualitative results to support and lend credibility to the quantitative results through a process including (1) quantitative and qualitative data representation, (2) drawing inferences and meta inferences, (3) data transformation, (4) representation of data integration and (5) interpretation of data integration.20 Once integration is completed, estimators of change concerning the syndemic index will be analysed, using the transformed data to represent changes in syndemic indexes.

Ethics and dissemination

Research ethics boards of participant institutions, General Hospital of Mexico Dr. Eduardo Liceaga and Marista University of Merida, approved this research protocol (Ethic and Research Committee HGM- DI/23/404-B/05/22 and UMM-CE_100_2022 and UMM-CI_001_2022). The consent process will be implemented at multiple levels within participating communities. First, formal approval will be obtained through municipal authorities in Chankom, Yaxunah and Komchén, followed by community-wide endorsement during local assemblies. Subsequently, eligible participants will be invited to join the census, interviews and focus group discussions. All participants who agree to participate will provide written informed consent through either signature or fingerprint, with consent forms available in both Mayan-Yucatecan and Spanish to ensure linguistic and cultural appropriateness.

The results of this research will be shared with various groups: (a) local and regional Mexican health system authorities, as well as municipal authorities, through presentations delivered by a participating or benefited community member. Reports on the project’s cost-effectiveness will also be drafted to support potential public health policy for community interventions in Yucatán’s Indigenous populations; (b) participating communities will receive the results and programme achievements in an assembly, using didactic and audiovisual materials in Mayan-Yucatecan and Spanish, presented by a member of their own community and (c) academic dissemination will include publications in public science journals, institutional press releases and presentations at national and international congresses or symposia.

As outlined in the Methodology, community members will participate in all research phases. A community member also serves as researcher and coauthor of this project.

To ensure anonymity and data protection, all information will be collected electronically using tablets and securely stored on the servers of Azure Mexico region. Access will be restricted to authorised researchers. Prior to analysis, datasets will be fully anonymised to protect participant confidentiality and anonymity, which is especially important given the small community context.

Discussion

This mixed-methods research protocol integrates a syndemic theoretical framework with a CBPR strategy to design, implement and evaluate a SCM. We expect to improve the health, functionality, quality of life and change in the syndemic of people living with NCDs in vulnerable communities.

Applying these research and analysis methodologies, where the two methodologies feedback and merge throughout the project, will allow for a more comprehensive understanding of the health issues faced by the different participating communities. This will also help to generate interventions that more effectively address the complexity of the realities of these communities. Evaluating syndemics also requires novel methods of analysis, such as the creation of an index to measure the syndemic and its subsequent use in network and economic analyses to demonstrate cost-effectiveness. Therefore, the results of this project are expected to provide evidence to decision makers, as it could justify scaling up the intervention to other underserved Indigenous communities and promote the development of targeted, resource-efficient public health strategies.

Unlike other health interventions for vulnerable communities, our study will use community participation throughout a mixed-methods research process to create a complex intervention based on the concept of syndemics, which will put community members coresponsible for improving their health status. This project will nurture a horizontal relationship between all actors within the project, avoiding the creation of unequal power relationships between academics and community members.

The aforementioned characteristics will facilitate the SCM as a sustainable intervention by fostering collaborative relationships between three key stakeholder groups: (1) community members, (2) local healthcare providers and (3) the research team. This tripartite engagement creates a foundation for long-term implementation through established trust, cultural congruence and mutual investment in health outcomes. Furthermore, the SCM will be integrated into the existing health system, particularly at the primary care level, in alignment with current national health policy priorities that emphasise strengthening first-level care and improving coordination across levels of the healthcare system. This alignment enhances the sustainability of the project.
