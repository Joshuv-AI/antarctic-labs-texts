Central Message

A clamshell thoracosternotomy is a safe, fast, and effective way to fully expose the hilar structures, with access to both the heart and the lungs in DCD lung donors with prior median sternotomy.

Median sternotomy is the default approach for donor heart/lung procurement. Lung donors (especially donation after circulatory determination [DCD] donors) with previous cardiac surgery/sternotomy are often refused by centers with decline codes of 717 (unacceptable organ) or 750 (donor medical history) as the result of complicated access (sternal wires and plates), dense adhesions, and potential for air leaks resulting in marginal graft quality. This results in the refusal of good lungs from donors with previous sternotomy.

Redo sternotomy/parasternal access has been previously described in donation after brain death donors and DCD donors.1,2 Similarly, a clamshell approach for donation after brain death donors was reported previously.3 However, we have found no reports of clamshell thoracosternotomy incision for DCD lung donors. In addition, procuring centers often may not have the necessary redo sternotomy equipment (oscillating saw and plate removal equipment).

We present our experience in 2 consecutive cases with an emergent clamshell thoracosternotomy for lung procurement in DCD donors with previous sternotomy. This study was conducted under the currently active institutional review board for transplant data follow-up case studies (STU-2018-0346 on February 15, 2019).

Case 1

Our first DCD donor (body mass index 30.6) had previous coronary artery bypass grafting and died from an extensive hemorrhagic cerebrovascular accident. Death occurred 83 minutes after withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy. After the mandatory 5-minute wait, we performed a standard clamshell thoracosternotomy incision, entering the pleural cavities with a Kelly clamp in the fourth intercostal space as laterally as possible. Using a gentle finger sweep, we created a space for heavy curved scissors and electrocautery for dissection above the fifth rib ensuring protection of the lung. Reintubation was delayed until we safely entered the pleural cavity. The sternal plate was peeled off with a wire twister clamp, and the sternum was transversely divided with a reciprocating sternal saw. Bilateral Finochietto retractors exposed the entire thoracic cavity and mediastinum.

The left ventricle was vented with a pool suction tip as were the right chambers and the main pulmonary artery was flushed with preservation solution as previously described2 (Figure 1, B). Flimsy pleural adhesions were dissected using a combination of scissors and electrocautery close to the parietal pleura. Dense adhesions in bilateral posterior upper lobes were encountered. To avoid air leaks, a portion of the chest wall and parietal pleura were left attached to the lung parenchyma. The rest of the procurement, including back table dissection, was performed in standard fashion.4 The lungs weighed less than 800 g. A leak test was performed and there was no obvious air leak. Incision to back table took 57 minutes.

The recipient did well and was extubated on postoperative day (POD) 1 and discharged on POD10. There were small bilateral pneumothoraces that resolved after conservative management before discharge. The patient also developed atrial fibrillation that was controlled by low-dose beta blockade. Otherwise, the postoperative course was smooth and uneventful. At 3 months posttransplant, lung function was good with forced expiratory volume in 1 second at 98% and forced vital capacity at 88% predicted.

Case 2

Our second DCD donor (body mass index 31.3) had a previous cardiac transplant complicated by chronic rejection and anoxic brain injury after seizures. Death occurred 14 minutes after withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy. Clamshell thoracosternotomy (since redo saw was not available) was performed in the same fashion as case 1. The procurement was complicated by the abdominal transplant team using abdominal normothermic regional perfusion (ANRP), which necessitated clamping the descending aorta and inferior vena cava. Many pleural and diaphragmatic adhesions were carefully excised. Dense adhesions necessitated leaving portions of parietal pleura or diaphragm with the lung. We have previously described procurement when ANRP was involved.5 The heart was densely adherent to the lung, so we excised the heart and lung en bloc (Figure 2, B). On the back table, 2 to 3 small air leaks were closed using pericardial pledgeted sutures and air leak sealant. Leak test showed no further air leaks. The lungs weighed 1328 g, and the left lower lobe was “boggy” (Figure 2, B). Incision to back table took about 90 minutes, attributable to adhesions and concomitant ANRP.

Our planned recipient was found to be respiratory syncytial virus positive, so this graft was diverted to an outside hospital. We were able to get recipient updates through private communication. The recipient did well and was extubated on POD2 with an uneventful postoperative course, with discharge on POD15. At 3 months posttransplant, lung function was good with forced expiratory volume in 1 second at 81% and forced vital capacity at 90% predicted.

Informed consent was waived in both cases because of the retrospective nature of this work.

Discussion

Because DCD lung donations are increasing worldwide, there are some potential good lung donors with previous sternotomy who are often refused as the result of technical complexity and lack of expertise. In our experience, a clamshell thoracosternotomy is a safe, fast, and effective way to fully expose the hilar structures with access to both the heart and the lungs in DCD settings. It provides improved access to all areas of the chest wall and diaphragms and facilitates release of adhesions, which can be difficult from the front. This incision obviates the need for removal of all sternal wires/plates and divides sternum transversely. Care needs to be taken for possible lung parenchyma/cardiac injury during entry. We believe that this approach is expeditious, reproducible, and a useful addition to the armamentarium for procuring lungs.
