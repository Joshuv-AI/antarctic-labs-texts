Redox-mediated mitochondrial collapse drives intrinsic apoptotic signaling in ELT3 cells response to apigenin
Authors: Samak Sutjarit, Chanokchon Setthawongsin, Chainarong Sakulthaew, Nattakan Meekhanon, Kazuhiko Ochiai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUterine leiomyoma is the most common benign gynecological tumor and a major cause of abnormal uterine bleeding, pelvic pain, and infertility. Although apigenin (API) exhibits antioxidant, antiproliferative, and anticancer activities, its mechanism of action in uterine leiomyoma, particularly the involvement of oxidative stress-mediated mitochondrial apoptosis, remains unclear. This study investigated the role of oxidative stress in API-induced apoptosis using rat uterine leiomyoma ELT3 cells. Cells were treated with 50 and 100 µM API for 48 h. Oxidative stress was assessed by measuring reactive oxygen species (ROS), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione reductase (GR) activity. Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), cell proliferation, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release, and apoptosis-related gene and protein expression were also evaluated. API significantly increased ROS and MDA levels while decreasing GR activity, indicating oxidative stress. These changes were accompanied by ΔΨm collapse, reduced cell proliferation, increased LDH release, upregulation of p53, Bax, caspase-3, and caspase-9, and downregulation of Bcl-2, indicating activation of the intrinsic mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. These findings demonstrate that API induces intrinsic mitochondrial apoptosis through oxidative stress-mediated mechanisms, providing mechanistic evidence for its cytotoxic effects and supporting its potential as a phytochemical candidate for uterine leiomyoma treatment.Keywords: Apigenin, Apoptosis, ELT3 cells, Oxidative stress, Reactive oxygen species

Introduction

Apigenin (4’,5,7-trihydroxyflavone; API) is a naturally occurring dietary flavone found in a wide range of fruits, vegetables, and medicinal herbs, including citrus fruits, celery, parsley, chamomile, and various teas [1]. As a bioactive flavonoid, API has been widely recognized for its diverse pharmacological properties, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, renoprotective, antimicrobial, anticancer, hepatoprotective, and neuroprotective activities [1], [2], [3], [4]. Beyond these functional effects, API has gained increasing attention for its chemopreventive potential, particularly in hormone-responsive tissues. Notably, API functions as a phytoprogestin, capable of interacting with steroid hormone receptors and modulating signaling in progesterone-sensitive cells [5]. This activity has been demonstrated in uterine tissue as well as breast cancer models, suggesting a possible regulatory role in reproductive cell proliferation and differentiation [6].

Oxidative stress and apoptosis are central to the regulation of cellular homeostasis and are known to contribute to both physiological remodeling and pathological progression. It arises from an imbalance between Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) production and cellular antioxidant defenses, leading to lipid peroxidation, protein modification, and mitochondrial dysfunction and loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) [7]. These redox disturbances frequently converge on apoptotic signaling cascades, including Tp53 activation, Bcl−2 family modulation, and caspase-mediated execution pathways [8]. Specifically, activated caspase−9 subsequently cleaves and activates caspase−3, leading to the execution of apoptosis [9]. Therefore, intracellular ROS, ΔΨm, p53, Bax, Bcl−2, caspase−9, and caspase−3 were selected as mechanistically relevant biomarkers to comprehensively evaluate oxidative stress-mediated intrinsic apoptosis induced by apigenin in ELT3 cells. These markers collectively provide strong evidence supporting activation of the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway.Bioactive compounds, especially plant-derived flavonoids, have been reported to modulate redox homeostasis, either by enhancing antioxidant capacity or, under certain cellular contexts, promoting ROS accumulation to trigger apoptosis in aberrantly proliferating cells [7].

Uterine leiomyomas, benign smooth muscle tumors commonly identified in both human and veterinary medicine, represent a clinically relevant condition associated with abnormal reproductive function. In companion animals, particularly dogs, leiomyomas account for a large proportion of uterine tumors and often present with clinical symptoms such as irregular estrous cycles, abdominal enlargement, and pressure-related urinary disturbances [10]. Despite being benign, their size and potential to mimic malignant neoplasia complicate clinical diagnosis, requiring histopathological confirmation. Traditional antineoplastic therapies, although sometimes necessary, are often associated with systemic toxicity and secondary side effects that may require additional pharmacological management [11]. Therefore, there is growing interest in identifying plant-derived compounds with therapeutic potential that provide antitumor effects while minimizing adverse outcomes.

Given the documented hormone-associated and antiproliferative properties of API, although API has been reported to exhibit anticancer and antioxidant properties in several tumor models [9], its mechanistic effects on oxidative stress-mediated mitochondrial apoptosis in uterine leiomyoma cells remain poorly understood. Moreover, limited information is available regarding the involvement of the ROS–mitochondrial pathway and intrinsic apoptotic signaling in ELT3 cells following API treatment. its potential role in redox-mediated apoptotic regulation in uterine leiomyoma cells warrants investigation. The present study utilizes ELT3 cells as an in vitro model to examine whether API induces oxidative stress-associated apoptosis. By evaluating both oxidative stress markers and intrinsic apoptotic pathway components, this study aims to elucidate the mechanistic basis of API’s cytotoxic effects. The findings may contribute to the development of safer, phytochemical-based strategies for managing uterine leiomyomas while reducing reliance on conventional therapeutic agents associated with adverse side effects.

Materials and methods

Cells and chemicals

ELT3 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). The MTS cell proliferation assay, Lactase dehydrogenase (LDH)-cytotoxicity assay kit II, DCFDA-based ROS detection kit, lipid peroxidation (MDA) assay kit, and glutathione reductase (GR) activity kit were purchased from Abcam. API and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. A 5 mM stock solution of API was prepared in DMSO and subsequently diluted with serum-free medium to final concentrations ranging from 20 to 160 µM. For all experimental conditions, the final DMSO concentration in culture did not exceed 0.1%.

Cell culture and assays

ELT3 cells were cultured in complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F−12 (DMEM/F−12) supplemented with 5% horse serum, 2.5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 U/mL streptomycin). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. For experimental assays, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5–10 × 10 ³ cells per well in a final volume of 200 µL. Cell proliferation and cytotoxicity were evaluated using the MTS assay and LDH leakage assay, respectively. Intracellular ROS generation, MDA level, and GR activity were quantified following the procedures described by Jittapalapong [12]. ELT3 cells were exposed to API at concentrations ranging from 0 to 160 μM during the preliminary cell viability study. Based on these results, 50 and 100 μM were selected for mechanistic investigations because they produced significant but non-complete reductions in cell viability while maintaining sufficient viable cells for subsequent biochemical and molecular analyses. These concentrations also allowed evaluation of dose-dependent effects on oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis without inducing excessive nonspecific cytotoxicity.

Measurement of ΔΨm

The ΔΨm was determined using the JC−1 fluorescent probe and quantified with a fluorescence microplate reader. ELT3 cells were seeded in black 96-well plates with clear bottoms and allowed to be attached overnight. Cells were then treated with API at concentrations of 50 and 100 μM for 48 h. Control cells received vehicle only. Following treatment, culture medium was removed and cells were incubated with JC−1 dye (5 μg/mL) diluted in serum-free culture medium at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. After incubation, cells were gently washed twice with warm phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove excess dye, and fresh PBS was added to each well. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader at excitation/emission wavelengths of 485/530 nm for JC−1 monomers (green fluorescence) and 535/590 nm for JC−1 aggregates (red fluorescence). ΔΨm was expressed as the ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity. Each experiment was performed in triplicate and repeated independently at least three times. To validate the sensitivity of the JC−1 assay, a subset of cells was treated with carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP; 10 μM) for 30 min prior to JC−1 staining as a positive control for mitochondrial membrane depolarization.

Determination of apoptotic-related gene expression

To assess the expression of apoptosis-related genes, quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed. ELT3 cells were exposed to 100 µM API for 48 h prior to analysis. Total RNA was isolated using TRIZOL® reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary DNA was synthesized from 2 µg of total RNA in a 20 µL reaction mixture using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, CA). qPCR amplification was conducted to quantify the mRNA levels of Tp53, Bax, Bcl−2, Caspase−9, and caspase−3, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences for each target are provided in Table 1. Each reaction contained 50 ng of cDNA, 0.25 µM forward and reverse primers, and Luna® Universal qPCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs) in a final volume of 10 µL. Thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 44 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method after normalization to GAPDH.

Western blot analysis

Total protein lysates from ELT3 cells were prepared using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After quantification of protein concentrations, equal amounts of lysates were resolved on 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gels and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature with Tris-buffered saline containing 5% non-fat dry milk, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Tp53, Bax, Bcl−2, Caspase−9, Caspase−3, and GAPDH (loading control) at the recommended dilutions (all antibodies from Abcam, Cambridge, UK). After several washes, membranes were incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam). Immunoreactive signals were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in at least three independent replicates to ensure reproducibility. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For the MTS assay, repeated-measures ANOVA was applied to evaluate differences in cell viability across time points, and paired t-tests were performed for comparisons between matched time points within the same treatment concentration. One-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc testing was used to compare cell viability across concentrations at each time point. For LDH, ROS, MDA, and GR measurements, statistical differences among treatment groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA software version 17.0 (StataCorp, Texas, USA).

Additionally, earlier reports have applied API concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 µM for 24–114 h to evaluate cytotoxic effects in human hepatoma and leukemia cell lines [13], [14]. Consequently, 48 h were selected as the optimal exposure duration, and API concentrations of 50 and 100 µM were employed for subsequent experiments.

API induces mitochondrial membrane depolarization in ELT3 cells

The decrease in ΔΨm occurred in a concentration-dependent manner, with cells treated with 50 and 100 μM API exhibiting the most pronounced mitochondrial depolarization in Fig. 7. Treatment with the mitochondrial uncoupler CCCP, used as a positive control, resulted in a marked reduction in the red/green fluorescence ratio, confirming the sensitivity and reliability of the assay. These findings indicate that API disrupts mitochondrial membrane integrity in ELT3 cells, suggesting involvement of mitochondrial-mediated apoptotic signaling.

The effect of API on ΔΨm of ELT3 cells. CCCP was used as positive control. The results represent mean ± S.D. **p < 0.05 significantly different from control.

API induced intrinsic apoptotic pathway

API treatment induces apoptosis in ELT3 cells through an oxidative stress–dependent mechanism. As shown in Fig. 8, exposure to 50 and 100 µM API for 48 h resulted in a significant upregulation of pro-apoptotic genes, including Tp53, Bax, Caspase−9, and Caspase−3, compared with the untreated control group. In contrast, API markedly downregulated the expression of the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl−2. This profile is characteristic of activation of the intrinsic (mitochondria-mediated) apoptotic pathway, which is tightly regulated by the interplay among Tp53, Bax, and Bcl−2 [15]. The enhanced expression of Caspase−3, a critical executioner caspase responsible for orchestrating key morphological and biochemical features of apoptosis [16], further supports that API triggers programmed cell death in ELT3 uterine leiomyoma cells.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that API induces oxidative stress and apoptosis in ELT3 cells. ELT3 cells are a well-established in vitro model for studying uterine leiomyomas which derived from the uterine epithelium of Eker rats, these epithelial-like cells exhibit sensitivity to estrogenic stimulation [17]. Notably, ELT3 cells share key phenotypic and molecular characteristics with tumor-derived uterine leiomyoma cells, making them a valuable model for preclinical investigations aimed at identifying potential therapeutic agents [18]. Utilizing this model allowed us to examine the cellular responses to API in a controlled environment and to assess its potential relevance for leiomyoma treatment. Our findings provide evidence that API promotes oxidative stress and activates the intrinsic apoptotic pathway in ELT3 cells, supporting its potential as a phytochemical candidate for further evaluation in the context of uterine leiomyoma management.

Cytotoxicity assays are fundamental for determining the capacity of a compound to induce cell death [19]. In this study, two complementary approaches were employed: the MTS assay and the LDH leakage assay. The MTS assay provides a convenient, single-step method for evaluating cell viability directly within the culture medium, avoiding the multiple processing steps required in the traditional MTT assay. In contrast, loss of plasma membrane integrity, a hallmark of necrotic cell death, can be quantitatively assessed by measuring LDH released into the culture supernatant [20]. Our findings demonstrate that API exerts cytotoxic effects on ELT3 cells in both a dose- and time-dependent manner, as evidenced by a progressive reduction in cell viability with increasing compound concentration and prolonged exposure. These observations are consistent with previous studies reporting API-mediated suppression of cell proliferation in various human and animal cell lines, including hepatocellular carcinoma (Hep3B, HepG2), TM4 Sertoli cells, leukemia cells, cholangiocarcinoma cells, and breast carcinoma cells [12], [13], [14], [21], [22], [23].) Furthermore, the LDH leakage assay corroborated the cytotoxic action of API by revealing a significant elevation in LDH release, indicating membrane damage and cell death in API-treated ELT3 cells.

Oxidative stress occurs when the generation of ROS exceeds the cellular antioxidant capacity, leading to molecular damage and impaired cellular function. Elevated ROS production and enhanced lipid peroxidation are widely recognized indicators of oxidative stress [24]. In the present study, API treatment markedly increased intracellular ROS levels in ELT3 cells, consistent with previous reports showing API-induced ROS generation in multiple human and animal cell types, including TM4 Sertoli cells, human hepatocarcinoma HepG2 and Hep3B cells, human leukemia cells, and human breast carcinoma cells [12], [13], [14], [21], [22], [23]. Furthermore, API significantly elevated MDA levels, a key lipid peroxidation end-product, indicating enhanced oxidative degradation of membrane lipids. This increase in MDA confirms that API promotes lipid peroxide, thereby contributing to oxidative stress in ELT3 cells [25]. The GR enzyme plays a pivotal role in maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis. Previous studies have highlighted that the balance between ROS generation and antioxidant defense systems is a critical determinant of cell survival or death [26]. In the present study, API-treated ELT3 cells exhibited a significant reduction in GR activity. This decline in enzymatic activity suggests that API compromises the antioxidant defense capacity of the cells, thereby intensifying the oxidative stress observed in this model.

Programmed cell death (PCD) comprises several regulated mechanisms characterized by distinct morphological and biochemical signatures [27]. Although the present study did not systematically investigate all PCD modalities, our findings strongly support that API induces apoptosis in ELT3 cells. Apoptosis represents a tightly regulated cell death pathway essential for preserving tissue homeostasis. Other PCD pathways such as ferroptosis, an iron-dependent oxidative death [28], necroptosis, a regulated form of necrosis mimicking apoptotic features [29], and autophagy, a stress-adaptive recycling process [30] may also interplay with oxidative signaling. Previous evidence demonstrates that API possesses anticarcinogenic activity by modulating oxidative stress responses and promoting apoptosis and autophagy [31]. Notably, API has been shown to induce both apoptotic and autophagic cell death in HCT116 colon cancer cells [32]. Taken together, the current results highlight apoptosis as the predominant PCD mechanism activated by API in ELT3 cells, while the potential involvement of additional PCD pathways warrants further investigation.

Apoptosis, a form of PCD, is essential for removing unnecessary cells and maintaining tissue homeostasis. It is primarily regulated through two major signaling routes: the extrinsic or death receptor–mediated pathway, which is initiated at the cell surface, and the intrinsic or mitochondrial pathway [33]. The present study demonstrates that API induces mitochondrial dysfunction in ELT3 cells, as evidenced by a significant collapse of ΔΨm. Loss of ΔΨm is currently recognized as a critical initiating event in mitochondrial intrinsic apoptosis and reflects irreversible mitochondrial injury preceding caspase activation [34], [35]. Recent evidence indicates that oxidative stress plays a central role in mitochondrial depolarization and apoptosis initiation. Excessive ROS disrupts mitochondrial electron transport and lipid membranes, leading to dissipation of ΔΨm and activation of downstream apoptotic signaling [34], [36] In the present study, API-induced ΔΨm collapse occurred in parallel with oxidative stress alterations, supporting the concept that redox imbalance acts upstream of mitochondrial dysfunction. Mitochondrial depolarization promotes mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and subsequent release of cytochrome c into the cytosol, a hallmark event of intrinsic apoptosis [37]. The mitochondrial impairment observed following API exposure is therefore consistent with a caspase-dependent intrinsic apoptotic mechanism. Regulation of mitochondrial permeability is tightly controlled by Bcl−2 family proteins [38], [39]. From a veterinary perspective, mitochondrial oxidative stress has been increasingly implicated in reproductive disorders in animals, including uterine and smooth muscle abnormalities [40]. Naturally occurring flavonoids such as API have been shown to modulate mitochondrial redox homeostasis and apoptotic signaling, highlighting their potential relevance in veterinary reproductive health and disease modulation [41]. The p53 protein, a central tumor suppressor, is known to induce apoptosis through multiple transcription-dependent and transcription-independent mechanisms [42]. Further clarification of the downstream targets regulated by p53 in API-treated ELT3 cells would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the intrinsic apoptotic mechanism involved. Both the extrinsic and intrinsic pathways incorporate transcriptional regulations [43]. Various methodological approaches are available to assess apoptosis, and these have been broadly categorized into six major groups [27]. Among the commonly used strategies, caspase-based assays remain fundamental due to the essential role of caspases in orchestrating the execution phase of apoptosis [27]. Additionally, real-time PCR provides a robust approach for quantifying transcriptional changes associated with apoptosis. This method enables the assessment of key pro-apoptotic genes, such as Bax, as well as anti-apoptotic regulators like Bcl−2, offering critical insight into the dynamics of the apoptotic signaling cascade [44]. In the present study, API treatment led to a marked upregulation of Tp53 expression, accompanied by a reduction in Bcl−2 levels. Together, these findings indicate that API disrupts the balance between pro- and anti-apoptotic factors, thereby shifting ELT3 cells toward apoptosis through a p53-mediated intrinsic pathway. In this study, Tp53 was found to enhance API-induced ROS generation, which subsequently activated Bax expression while suppressing Bcl−2 expression. Excessive ROS primarily target mitochondria, and mitochondrial damage is a well-established trigger of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway [45]. Mitochondrial disruption facilitates the activation of the Caspase−9/Caspase−3 cascade, ultimately leading to apoptosis [46]. API-treated cells exhibited a marked upregulation of Caspase−9 and Caspase−3 gene expression, confirming the activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. In this study, Western blot analysis provides mechanistic confirmation that API induces apoptosis in ELT3 cells through activation of the intrinsic, mitochondria-mediated pathway. Consistent with the oxidative stress data, API treatment markedly increased the protein abundance of p53, Bax, Caspase−9, and Caspase−3 in a concentration-dependent manner, while simultaneously decreasing the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl−2. These protein-level changes align with the transcriptional responses observed in our qPCR analysis, supporting the notion that API disrupts intracellular redox homeostasis and shifts the cellular balance toward pro-apoptotic signaling. Previous studies have reported that API induces apoptosis in several tumor cell lines through the mitochondrial-mediated pathway, primarily driven by intracellular ROS generation as an initiating signal [47]. In contrast, Sanjay [48] demonstrated that API was relatively safe for normal cells when compared with human prostate carcinoma cells. The pro-apoptotic response observed in ELT3 cells in the present study suggests that API may selectively induce apoptosis depending on the cellular context. Although API is generally recognized as a natural antioxidant, its biological activity is highly context dependent [7], [8], [9]. In cancer cells, which typically maintain elevated basal ROS levels and altered redox homeostasis, higher concentrations of API may exceed the cellular antioxidant capacity and promote ROS accumulation [9], [49]. This oxidative shift results in mitochondrial dysfunction, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, activation of p53, alteration of the Bax/Bcl−2 ratio, and subsequent activation of the intrinsic caspase cascade [7], [8], [49]. Such dual antioxidant/pro-oxidant behavior has also been reported for several dietary flavonoids and is considered one mechanism underlying their selective anticancer activity [8], [9], [49].

The present study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, only ELT3 cells were investigated; therefore, the selective cytotoxicity of API toward leiomyoma cells relative to normal uterine smooth muscle cells could not be determined. Future investigations should compare the responses of leiomyoma cells with those normal uterine smooth muscle cells to determine whether API preferentially targets tumor cells while sparing normal uterine tissue. Consequently, the therapeutic window and safety profile of API cannot be fully established based solely on the present in vitro findings. Second, the concentrations of API used in this study (50 and 100 μM) represent pharmacological exposure and are higher than the plasma concentrations generally achieved through dietary intake because of the limited bioavailability and extensive metabolism of API [1]. Nevertheless, comparable concentrations have been widely used in mechanistic in vitro studies to investigate intracellular signaling pathways [50]. Future studies should determine whether similar biological effects can be achieved at clinically relevant concentrations using optimized formulations or targeted delivery systems, followed by validation in appropriate animal models before clinical translation. Third, antioxidant rescue experiments using ROS scavengers were not performed to directly establish the causal role of ROS in API-induced apoptosis. Future studies using ROS scavengers, such as N-acetyl-L-cysteine or MitoTEMPO, together with mitochondrial or caspase inhibitors, may help determine whether inhibition of ROS generation or mitochondrial signaling attenuates apoptosis, thereby providing stronger mechanistic validation of the proposed pathway [51], [52]. Fourth, although other forms of regulated cell death, including autophagy, ferroptosis, and necroptosis, have been reported to participate in cellular responses to oxidative stress, these pathways were not investigated in the present study. Therefore, their potential contribution to API-induced cytotoxicity cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, the coordinated increases in mitochondrial membrane depolarization, caspase−9/caspase−3 activation, Bax/Bcl−2 dysregulation, and p53 upregulation collectively support the activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway as the predominant mechanism under the present experimental conditions. Future studies incorporating pathway-specific biomarkers and inhibitors will be valuable for distinguishing apoptosis from other forms of regulated cell death. Finally, assessment of cytochrome c release and direct measurement of caspase−3 and caspase−9 enzymatic activities were not performed, which could have provided additional mechanistic evidence supporting mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis. Despite these limitations, our findings consistently demonstrated increased intracellular ROS generation, mitochondrial membrane depolarization, upregulation of Tp53, Bax, caspase−9, and caspase−3, together with downregulation of Bcl−2, providing converging evidence for activation of the oxidative stress-mediated intrinsic mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. These results are also consistent with the established mechanism of mitochondrial apoptosis reported in previous studies. Nevertheless, cellular responses to API may vary depending on cell type, treatment concentration, redox status, and other experimental conditions. Therefore, future studies should include normal uterine smooth muscle cells or immortalized myometrial cells to evaluate the selective cytotoxicity, therapeutic window, and safety profile of API. Additional investigations incorporating ROS scavenger rescue experiments, cytochrome c release, caspase activity assays, and molecular markers of alternative regulated cell death pathways will further clarify the molecular mechanisms of API and strengthen its translational potential as a therapeutic candidate for uterine leiomyoma.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that API induces oxidative stress and activates apoptosis in ELT3 cells predominantly through the intrinsic pathway. API exposure resulted in reduced cell viability, increased LDH release, and elevated intracellular ROS and MDA levels, indicating enhanced oxidative damage. Consistently, antioxidant capacity was impaired, as reflected by decreased GR activity. At the molecular level, API significantly upregulated pro-apoptotic genes (Tp53, Bax, Caspase−9, Caspase−3) while suppressing the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl−2, supporting mitochondrial-mediated apoptotic signaling. Collectively, these findings suggest that API exerts cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic effects on ELT3 cells through oxidative stress–driven mechanisms. API may represent a potential phytochemical candidate for managing uterine tumors; however, further in vivo investigations are required to validate its therapeutic potential and safety profile. Moreover, elucidating the specific regulatory interactions between API, p53 activation, and downstream apoptotic mediators will be essential for future drug development and translational applications.
