Financial precarity and mental health among French health students: a nationwide cross-sectional study
Authors: Marie-Pierre Tavolacci, Philippe Berthelot, Jean Dellamonica, Loic Josseran, Benoît Veber, Joel Ladner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to describe the prevalence and determinants of financial precarity and examine its associations with mental health, healthcare avoidance and academic outcomes among French health students.DesignNationwide cross-sectional study based on an online self-administered questionnaire. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to identify factors associated with financial precarity.SettingAll 34 French health universities.ParticipantsA total of 12 565 health students participated, including medical (56%), paramedical (21%), midwifery, odontology, pharmacy or physiotherapy students (12%) and first-year health students (11%).Primary and secondary outcome measuresFinancial precarity was defined as an indicator combining financial insecurity, frequent overdrafts and foregoing essential purchases. Primary outcomes included depressive symptoms, anxiety and emotional exhaustion. Secondary outcomes included healthcare avoidance and academic outcomes.ResultsAmong 12,565 respondents, 56% were medical students, 21% were paramedical, 12% midwifery, odontology, pharmacy or physiotherapy and 11% first-year health students. Financial precarity varies by academic fields of health, ranging from 4.6% in first-year health students to 12% in paramedical students. Adjusted analyses showed lower odds of precarity among medical (aOR=0.69; 95% CI 0.52 to 0.83), midwifery, odontology, pharmacy or physiotherapy (aOR=0.55; 95% CI 0.43 to 0.72) and first-year health students (aOR=0.54; 95% CI 0.38 to 0.77) than paramedical students. Risk factors included very low parental socio-economic status (aOR=2.96; 95% CI 2.33 to 3.89) and student loans (aOR=2.78; 95% CI 2.33 to 3.32). Financial precarity was strongly associated with depressive symptoms (aOR=4.90; 95% CI 4.13 to 5.80), anxiety (aOR=3.84; 95% CI 3.13 to 4.52), emotional exhaustion (aOR=8.49; 95% CI 5.98 to 12.06), renouncing healthcare (aOR=6.21; 95% CI 5.01 to 7.70) and repeating a year (aOR=1.80; 95% CI 1.54 to 2.10).ConclusionsFinancial precarity among health students is shaped by economic and academic factors, with family support protective of and low socio-economic background increasing vulnerability, and is associated with poorer mental health, reduced healthcare access and academic difficulties.Keywords: Cross-Sectional Studies; Education, Medical; MENTAL HEALTH; PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

By pursuing higher education rather than entering working life, university students expose themselves to a form of temporary precarity in the hope of a better future.1 While the student condition was historically characterised by strong dependence on family support for housing and financial resources, many contemporary students no longer live at home, and family support may be limited or inconsistent.2 Although student precarity is widespread, it remains largely under-recognised by public authorities, particularly among health students, who are rarely classified as a vulnerable population. Unlike poverty, which is a more stable and measurable condition, financial precarity is by definition transient, fluctuating between periods of relative stability and crisis. Students’ financial situations vary widely: some must combine paid employment with academic demands, whereas others remain dependent on family but face sudden changes in household income. Between these extremes lies a spectrum of situations shaped by interrelated factors such as income, housing, food security and access to support. Recent events have exacerbated this precariousness. In France, 69% of students reported major financial difficulties in the month preceding one study, including difficulties paying rent or covering basic expenses.3 Recent events have intensified student financial vulnerability. The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted employment, reduced family support and complicated access to public aid.4,6 More recently, the 2022–2023 inflation surge has likely increased financial strain, particularly for students lacking stable financial support.7 Financial insecurity can have cascading effects on students’ well-being and academic trajectories. It has been associated with increased risks of stress, anxiety and depression and may discourage students from seeking healthcare.8 Students working to finance their studies may struggle to balance employment with academic demands, and reliance on loans may increase long-term financial stress.9 Moreover, to finance their studies, students may resort to loans, increasing their level of indebtedness and long-term financial stress.10 11 Food insecurity, closely linked to financial hardship, may further exacerbate these difficulties.12 Evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship between food insecurity and emotional health, each factor increasing the risk of the other and potentially undermining academic success.13

These challenges are particularly pronounced among health students, whose academic paths involve intense workloads, clinical rotations and limited flexibility for employment.14 The demanding nature of healthcare education, including long hours, emotional exposure and frequent evaluations, contributes to high stress and emotional exhaustion. Cultural norms emphasising resilience may further discourage seeking mental health support despite high levels of distress.15 Consequently, health students may face greater financial and psychological vulnerability than peers in other disciplines.16

International research consistently shows that financial precarity is common among university students, although its magnitude varies across educational systems. In countries with high tuition fees such as the United States, Canada and Australia, studies report particularly high levels of financial strain among health students, often exceeding 50% and strong associations with mental health problems and academic difficulties.17 18 Evidence from publicly funded or lower-tuition systems also indicates substantial vulnerability. Norwegian students experiencing financial difficulties report significantly higher levels of depressive symptoms, anxiety and poorer academic outcomes,19 whereas financial concerns have been shown to predict subsequent deterioration in mental and physical health among UK students.20 Similarly, 25% of university students reported a worsening financial situation during the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly among those with limited financial support or who had to pay tuition fees themselves or rely on loans.21 Despite these international findings, data on financial precarity among health students in France remain limited, where tuition fees are low but academic and clinical demands may increase vulnerability.22

This study aimed to address this gap by focusing on university health students in France. Its objectives were to (1) describe financial resources including grants, paid employment, loans and family support; (2) assess the prevalence of financial precarity; and (3) identify factors associated with financial precarity, including socio-demographic characteristics, mental health status and the role of financial resources and family support.

Methods

All 34 universities in France were invited to participate, and all agreed to take part. Within each university, all students were eligible to receive the survey invitation. This national study was based on a cross-sectional design with an online link providing access to a self-administered questionnaire. The invitation was sent out by each university to its students with several follow-up reminders sent between 1 February 2024 and 31 March 2024. The study population consisted of university students enrolled in health-related programmes (residents were not included).

Data collection

Socio-demographic characteristics

Socio-demographic characteristics were collected using study-specific items including age, gender, academic year, weight and height (to calculate the body mass index (BMI) using the standard formula (BMI weight (kg)/height [m2]) and was classified as underweight (<18.5), normal weight (18.5–24.9), overweight (25.0–29.9) and obesity (>30.0)). Academic fields of health were categorised into four groups: 1, medical students; 2, students in related health programmes (midwifery, dentistry, pharmacy and physiotherapy), grouped together due to similarities in curriculum structure and academic demands; 3, paramedical group, including nursing, speech therapy, occupational therapy and psychometry; and 4, first-year health access students, enrolled in a competitive initial year designed for entry into medicine, pharmacy, dentistry or midwifery programmes. Students indicated whether they benefited from being admitted using the ‘first-year students Passerelles’ procedure, which allows holders of certain titles or diplomas to directly enter into the second or third year of one of the four healthcare courses in France (medicine, pharmacy, dentistry and midwifery) without going through the first-year programme. Living accommodation was also collected and has been classified into three categories: hosted by a third party (parents and family), rental or sublease or student residence. The employment category of both parents was requested and then classified by parental households: a high household consists of parents who hold high-level professions, such as a combination of two high-level professions (higher intellectual professions) or a high-level and an intermediate-level profession (farmers, artisans and intermediate professions). An intermediate household includes parents with intermediate-level professions or a mix of intermediate and high or low professions (employees and manual workers). A low household is composed of parents with low-level professions or a combination of low and intermediate-level professions, and a very low household is characterised by parents who are either unemployed or only one parent has a low-level active profession; this category also includes households in which one or both parents are unemployed or deceased.

Student’s resources

Student resources were assessed using study-specific items adapted from previous national student surveys and included students’ job, and if so, whether it was in the health sector, whether it was held year-round or part of the year, or weekly hours (half time is 17 hours per week) and whether the job was essential for covering living expenses (housing, food, etc.). If the job served as a supplementary income for leisure activities with five possible responses ranging from ‘not at all’ to ‘completely’. Students were also asked whether they received a grant and its level (from 0bis (lowest) to 7 (highest)). Students were asked whether they had taken out a student loan to finance their studies and if they had a Public Service Commitment Contract (CESP) (for the medicine and odontology students: in return for a monthly allowance of €1,200, the beneficiaries undertake, for a number of years equal to that during which they will have received the allowance, and carry out their duties, from the end of their training, in specific places of practice in an area). They were also asked about financial support from their families for rent, groceries, clothing and leisure activities, with the possibility of responding to each item with ‘yes, fully’ (2 points), ‘yes, but partially’ (1 point) or ‘no, no support’ (0 points). An overall score for family financial support was then calculated: 0–2 indicating low support, 3–5 indicating moderate support and 6–8 indicating high support. The final question was about having to repeat a year at a university in the past.

Mental health

Anxiety and depression were assessed using the validated Hospital Anxiety and Depression (HAD) scale. The HAD scale consists of 14 items (seven related to anxiety and seven to depression). Each item is scored ranging from 0 to 3, where 0 indicates the absence of symptoms and 3 reflects maximum severity of the symptoms. The scores are interpreted as follows: 0–7, no significant anxiety or depression; 8–10, mild anxiety or depression; 11–21, clinically significant anxiety or depression.23 Burnout was assessed using the validated Maslach Burnout Inventory Emotional Exhaustion subscale (MBI-EE). Respondents are asked to rate their experiences ranging from 0 (never) to 6 (every day). The results of MBI-EE were categorised as follows: low (5–13), moderate (14–22) and high (23–30) emotional exhaustion.24

Access to healthcare

Access to healthcare was evaluated using study-specific questions regarding healthcare consultations and forgone care for financial reasons. Students were asked if they had consulted a psychologist, psychiatrist or general practitioner (GP) in the past 12 months and whether the reason for the GP visit was related to stress and anxiety. They were also asked if they had forgone visits to a dentist, GP, specialist, psychologist, prescription glasses, over-the-counter medicine, prescription drug or contraceptive method for financial reasons.

Precarity

Financial precarity

Questions were asked about financial sufficiency (‘Do you have enough money to cover your monthly needs?’), financial anxiety (‘Does your financial situation make you anxious?’ (five possible responses ranging from ‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree’), bank overdraft history (‘Have you ever had a bank overdraft?’ (never, yes but not every month and yes every month)). The frequency of foregoing purchases due to financial constraint for each of these categories was also asked (five responses ranging from ‘very often’ to ‘never’): leisure, clothing, food, hygiene products, household maintenance and digital items (phone, computer, etc.). They were also asked about their use of food aid (never, a few times in the past 12 months, a few times a month or every week or more often).

Financial precarity was assessed using study-specific items derived from previous literature and national surveys, including perceived financial sufficiency, bank overdraft frequency and food purchase restrictions. These items reflect complementary dimensions of economic vulnerability (financial instability, material deprivation and subjective financial strain) and were selected based on evidence from a recent systematic review25 and French studies in student populations identifying these indicators as common and valid measures of financial strain.26 Equal weights were applied to ensure a balanced representation of each dimension. This choice was guided by the absence of evidence supporting differential weighting. A financial precarity indicator was constructed based on the following: 1 point was assigned for reporting insufficient money to cover monthly needs (‘strongly disagree’ or ‘disagree’), monthly bank overdraft and frequent food purchase restrictions (‘often’ or ‘very often’), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 3. Levels of financial precarity were defined as 0 (none), 1 (low) and 2–3 (high). Items were reviewed by public health experts for content validity and pre-tested in a small student sample to ensure clarity and relevance.

Social deprivation

Social deprivation was measured, with the EPICES27 score used in its simplified version: a situation of social vulnerability was identified whenever ≥4 unfavourable responses were given out of the 11 questions.

Food precarity

Food security was assessed using the validated six-item Food Security Survey Module: a score of 0–1 indicated food security; 2–4, low food security; and 5–6, food insecurity.28

Data analysis

Data were analysed using descriptive and inferential statistics to explore the relationships between variables and student financial precarity. Percentages and means with SD were calculated to present the demographic and academic characteristics of the sample. χ2 tests and Student t-tests were conducted to identify significant associations between qualitative and continuous variables, respectively. Two sets of logistic regression analyses were performed. The first set focused on socio-demographic and academic factors, and the second introduced variables related to health status and healthcare-seeking behaviours. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to identify independent predictors of financial precarity. Model 1 included socio-demographic variables identified a priori as potential confounders: gender, academic fields of health and academic year, which were retained instead of age in multivariate models because both variables were strongly related. These were selected based on literature suggesting their association with both exposure and outcome. Model 2 extended this analysis by incorporating variables related to financial resources, namely, student grants, loans, job and family financial support. In all models, university was included as a fixed effect to account for between variability in response rates and student characteristics. This stepwise approach aimed to assess the specific contribution of financial support mechanisms to the risk of financial precarity, beyond basic socio-demographic factors. The rationale for variable inclusion was based on theoretical relevance and statistical significance in univariate analyses (p<0.20). Model performance and validity were assessed through several diagnostic procedures. Multicollinearity between predictors was examined using variance inflation factors, with all values remaining below the commonly accepted threshold of 5, indicating no significant collinearity. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Adjusted OR (aOR) with 95% CIs were calculated to quantify the strength of associations, and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Spearman correlation coefficients were used to assess the relationship between study and university reported proportions, treating percentages as continuous variables. Bland–Altman analysis was used to assess agreement between sources.

A total of 12 565 students were included; 55.6% were medical students; 20.7%, paramedical students; 12.2%, related health programmes students; and 10.8%, first-year health access students. Concerning health factors (table 4), being overweight or obese was associated with a higher likelihood of financial precarity than having a normal BMI. Mental health issues, such as depressive symptoms (aOR 4.90; 95% CI 4.13 to 5.80), anxiety (aOR=3.84; 95% CI 3.13 to 4.52) and emotional exhaustion (aOR=8.49; IC95% 5.98–12.06), were strongly associated with financial precarity. Social vulnerability was also associated with financial precarity. Students in precarious situations consult GPs less often, but more often for reasons of stress and anxiety. Consultations with a psychotherapist or psychologist were also associated with financial precarity. Financial precarity was also linked to a higher likelihood of forgoing healthcare (general medical and psychological consultations) for financial reasons. All these factors remained significant in model 2.

Representativeness of the sample

Response rates of medicine students were moderate overall (16.4%) and showed marked variability across universities (3.8%–42.1%) (online supplemental table S1). Response rates could not be calculated for the other health student groups due to the lack of comparable denominators. Based on 17/34 universities with available scholarship data, the proportion of scholarship students reported by universities was broadly consistent with that observed in our study population. A strong correlation was observed between study and universities data (r=0.82; p<0.001), with good agreement in Bland–Altman analysis. Detailed comparisons of scholarship rates by university are provided in online supplemental figure S2).

Discussion

Our findings provide new insights into the multidimensional nature of financial precarity among health students defined using a measure of at least two of the following: monthly overdraft, insufficient funds to cover basic needs and frequent food deprivation. Overall, nearly 30% of students were affected by financial precarity, comprising 10% with significant precarity and 20% with low precarity, particularly among paramedical students and men. Family financial support appeared to constitute a structural protective resource against financial precarity, whereas student employment and grants seemed to provide only partial compensation, and student loans remained indicative of persistent financial vulnerability. Financial precarity was associated with multiple adverse outcomes. It was linked to mental health challenges, including anxiety, depression and especially emotional exhaustion, as well as barriers to healthcare access. Additionally, financial precarity negatively affected academic outcomes, highlighting the multidimensional nature of student vulnerability.

By using multiple indicators, this study offers a more robust estimate than prior research relying solely on self-reported financial difficulties.3 Male students appeared more at risk of financial precarity when financial support was excluded from multivariate models, which may indicate gender-related differences in the mobilisation of support resources.29 Financial precarity increased progressively across academic years, suggesting that financial strain accumulates over time as students advance through training, while the persistence of this association in the sixth year, corresponding to the final stage of medical training, after adjustment for financial support indicates that structural factors, such as increasing academic demands, reduced opportunities for paid employment and costs related to clinical rotations, may independently contribute to heightened vulnerability.30 Our study highlights that financial precarity is more pronounced in non-medical health programmes; another French study22 reported that 37% of nursing students and 11% of medical students experienced high subjective financial difficulties, corroborating our findings. Medical and other traditional health students have a lower risk of financial precarity, independent of personal or family resources, suggesting that structural elements of these programmes such as paid internships and structured curricula provide a protective effect. Students from lower household socio-economic backgrounds were at higher risk of financial precarity, likely due to limited family resources and greater cumulative constraints; this risk was attenuated after accounting for financial support, suggesting that scholarships, loans or other aid can partially mitigate but do not fully eliminate the impact of socio-economic disadvantage. Our study also points to the protective role of family financial support; family contributions accounted for approximately 50% of students’ monthly income in our sample, compared with 40% across European surveys.31 Job plays a complex role: although jobs can provide financial relief, one-third of students reported needing a job to cover living expenses, which rises to half in national surveys.32 Both grants and student employment were associated with financial precarity in partially adjusted models, but these associations disappeared after adjustment for other financial resources, suggesting that they mainly reflect underlying socio-economic disadvantage rather than independent determinants of financial precarity.33 Student loans likely reflect unmet financial needs that persist despite other available resources. Regarding academic impact, repeating a year was associated with financial precarity, suggesting a potentially bidirectional relationship in which academic delays increase financial strain due to prolonged tuition costs and living expenses, while financial precarity may in turn contribute to delays by increasing stress, limiting study time due to paid work and restricting access to learning resources.17

This study highlights the association between financial precarity and both mental health and indicators of physical health, such as high BMI, among French health students. Students experiencing financial precarity were more likely to have high BMI (overweight or obesity), as limited financial resources may reduce access to balanced food and constrain lifestyle choices. Studies reported that financial stress has been associated with food insecurity in university populations, which may in turn influence diet quality and weight status.34,36 During the COVID-19 pandemic, the French government offered student meals for €1, a measure extended in 2023 for students receiving grants or experiencing financial precarity.37 In this study, depression and anxiety affected 15%–24% and 40%–50% of students, respectively, indicating higher prevalence than pre-pandemic international estimates and broadly consistent with recent French data.38 Regarding mental health, students experiencing financial difficulties were significantly more likely to report depression and anxiety, highlighting a cyclical relationship in which financial stress and psychological distress reinforce each other.39 Financial precarity was also linked to social vulnerability. Students facing financial difficulties were more likely to struggle with friendships and feel excluded from social activities, amplifying isolation, which may in turn contribute to poorer academic performance, substance use (eg, alcohol) or even suicidal ideation.40,42 Rather than indicating direct causality, our findings support the possibility of a bidirectional relationship in which financial stress and mental distress reinforce one another. Additionally, major barriers to healthcare access were identified. Students experiencing financial precarity were significantly more likely to forgo medical or psychological consultations due to cost, and this tendency worsened progressively with increasing levels of financial precarity, suggesting a potential causal effect. These outcomes persisted even after adjusting for personal and family financial resources, indicating that factors beyond absolute income, such as high mandatory expenses, academic constraints, administrative limitations, anticipated debt or perceived stress, contribute to the impact of financial precarity on health and care avoidance.43 Finally, social and healthcare-related mechanisms may also play a role. Financial difficulties can limit participation in social activities and delay healthcare use, potentially increasing vulnerability through reduced social support and unmet care needs.44

Strengths and limitations

The study includes more than 12 000 respondents from all 34 French universities, providing substantial statistical power and broad national coverage of health students. The findings offer a comprehensive understanding of the issue, underscoring the complex interplay between multiple psychosocial, behavioural and contextual factors across students from different academic years and health disciplines. Response rates were overall moderate and varied considerably between universities, which may limit the representativeness of the sample. Although several strategies were implemented at the design stage to mitigate non-response bias, including wide dissemination and neutral survey framing, participation bias cannot be ruled out. The absence of individual-level data on non-respondents prevented a direct assessment of selection bias. However, indirect comparisons with available external data at both national and institutional levels showed reasonable consistency for key characteristics such as gender distribution45 and scholarship status, although these comparisons were limited to a subset of universities. While prevalence estimates should therefore be interpreted with caution due to potential selection bias and heterogeneity in response rates, the consistency of associations after multivariable adjustment suggests that the observed relationships are likely to be robust. Nonetheless, residual confounding cannot be excluded, as some relevant variables were not measured. In particular, factors such as family background, prior academic performance and individual resilience may also influence financial precarity among students. Finally, although the study population is diverse and drawn from multiple institutions, the findings may not be fully generalisable to other cultural or institutional contexts, particularly in countries with substantially different higher education financing systems, higher tuition fees or less comprehensive student health coverage.

Practical and research implications

This study highlights the multidimensional nature of financial precarity among French health students, encompassing economic vulnerability, mental health challenges, limited access to healthcare, social exclusion, variation across health academic programmes and adverse academic outcomes. Our findings emphasise the need for targeted and sustainable interventions, particularly for students lacking family support or balancing employment with demanding academic programmes. Measures such as enhanced financial aid, improved access to mental health services and institutional monitoring of at-risk students are essential to promote student well-being, academic success and the resilience of the future healthcare workforce. Further research is needed to understand causal pathways and explore mechanisms in depth, including longitudinal studies to examine how financial precarity evolves over time and affects mental health and academic outcomes, as well as comparisons across health academic programmes and institutional contexts to elucidate the role of structural factors.

Conclusion

Overall, this study highlights the multidimensional nature of financial precarity, shaped by individual, structural and contextual factors—including gender, academic programme, family socio-economic status and cumulative stress over the educational trajectory—and its consequences for both health and academic outcomes, pointing to areas where targeted policy interventions could have the greatest impact
