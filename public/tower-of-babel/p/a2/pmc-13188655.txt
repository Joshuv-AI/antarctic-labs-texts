Genome and antimicrobial compounds analysis of Bacillus subtilis M51 as a potential biocontrol agent against Fusarium oxysporum
Authors: Li Zhang, Lizhu Shao, Boshen Wang, Boyuan Zhang, Yuting Li, Shuhong Song, Heng Zhao, Yilei Pu, Minxuan Rui, Zhongke Sun, Chengwei Li, Na Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFusarium oxysporum is a common fungal pathogen of wheat that severely impacts plant growth and causes grain yield loss. The identification of potential biocontrol agents is important to complement agricultural management practices and reduce the use of chemical fungicides. This study investigated the potential mechanism of Bacillus subtilis strain M51 inhibition of F. oxysporum and analyzed secondary metabolite-related compounds based on M51 whole-genome sequencing results.ResultsThe strain M51 was experimentally isolated from healthy wheat roots and identified as B. subtilis. This strain showed significant antagonistic effects against seven phytopathogenic fungi, with the highest inhibition rate of 64.35% observed against F. oxysporum YJ-2. Fusarium oxysporum treated with a crude extract of M51 fermentation broth produced rough and abnormally twisted mycelium, an increased number of branch nodes, and disruption of mycelial cell membranes. The activity of peroxidase in F. oxysporum treated with the crude extract was significantly reduced, whereas reactive oxygen species accumulation and malondialdehyde content were increased. Genome sequencing revealed that M51 contained a single circular chromosome 4,425,418 bp in length, with 4498 coding sequences and an average GC content of 43.05%. Genomic analysis revealed that it contained protein-encoding genes for the production of cellulase, lipopetides, indole-3-acetic acid, and iron carriers, for example, which assist in plant defense against pathogen infection and promote plant growth. Six lipopetides (surfactin, fengycin, bacillaene, bacillibactin, bacilysin, and subtilosin A) and small secondary metabolites (dimethyl carbate, aviglycine hydrochloride, butroxydim, D-limonene, latilure, and oryctalure) were detected from the M51 crude extract by UHPLC-QTOF-MS/MS. In addition, a pot experiment indicated that the sterile filtrate from M51 fermentation broth can promote the growth of wheat seedlings.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate the potential of M51 as a biological control agent against wheat root-rot fungi and provide a foundation for the development of novel secondary metabolites that effectively inhibit pathogenic fungi.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12827-3.Keywords: Bacillus subtilis, Biological control, Secondary metabolites, Fusarium oxysporum, Wheat

Introduction

Wheat is a globally important food crop and is planted on a large scale worldwide [1]. However, during wheat cultivation, diseases caused by root-rot fungi result in severe losses in grain yield and thus infection by such pathogens poses a serious threat to global food security [2]. Fusarium oxysporum is a soil-borne pathogen known to be a common causal agent of root rot in wheat. Fusarium oxysporum is widespread and highly pathogenic, and is ranked fifth among the world’s ten most serious phytopathogenic fungi [3].

Currently, chemical methods are widely used to control root rot of wheat, but these methods pollute the environment, harm the soil, and readily lead to the emergence of resistance in pathogenic microorganisms [4]. In recent years, organic farming techniques have received increasing consideration and the development of effective biocontrol agents is important to complement agricultural management practices and reduce the use of chemical fungicides [5]. The identification of effective microbial biocontrol agents and their active metabolic substances is an important focus of ongoing research.

Bacillus species are common in the environment and are often found in the soil, human gut, and fermented foods. Some Bacillus species are recognized as safe commercial starter cultures. Several Bacillus species produce bacteriocins, inhibitory substances, and extracellular enzymes, such as amylases and cellulase, which have broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties [6]. Bacillus species can produce indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), siderophores, and proteases, and harbor mineral phosphate decomposition activity [7]. For these reasons, many studies have been conducted on the use of Bacillus strains as effective biocontrol agents for plant diseases, and genome sequencing and secondary-metabolite analysis are important approaches to provide insights into the biology of their antagonistic activity [8].

In previous studies, Bacillus velezensis BV01 has shown significant control of wheat root rot and wilt of pepper, and produces biotropic substances, such as IAA and iron carriers, which have a significant probiotic effect on wheat and pepper [9]. Bacillus subtilis GYUN-2311, isolated from the rhizosphere soil in an apple orchard, exhibits antagonistic activity against 12 fungal pathogens, whole-genome analysis identified biosynthetic genes for secondary metabolites such as subtilomycin, bacillaene, fengycin, bacillibactin, and pulcherrimin [10]. Zhao et al. reported that the crude lipopeptide extracted from B. velezensis NEAU-242-2 showed strong antifungal activity against Bipolaris sorokiniana, causing mycelial wrinkling, fracture, and disruption of the cell structure, and leading to cellular malformation and the disappearance of most membrane-like structures [11]. Yi at al. reported that the sterile filtrate of Bacillus amyloliquefaciens DB2 had a significant inhibitory effect on B. sorokiniana mycelia, and significantly promoted the growth of wheat seedlings [12]. Bacillus velezensis YL2021 was demonstrated to produce bacillibactin among its metabolic products through light chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry analysis and had broad inhibitory effects on plant pathogens [13]. Bacillus amyloliquefaciens SQ-2 secretes bacillibactin, surfactin, and bacillaene, and shows potential for crop protection by inhibiting plant pathogens [14].

Bacillus species have attracted substantial attention because of their excellent biocontrol potential. Previous studies found various Bacillus species exert biocontrol and growth-promoting effects on plantshave focused on lipopeptides, growth-promoting substances such as IAA in metabolites analyzed, and the effects of fermentation broth on the growth of pathogenic fungi. Building on the aforementioned findings this study isolates a B. subtilis strain M51 with good antagonistic activity against F. oxysporum and systematically investigates its inhibitory effect on this pathogen. This study further analyzed small-molecule antibacterial active substances in the secondary metabolites of M51, the impact of crude extracts on reactive oxygen levels and DNA damage in hyphae, and conducted an analysis of the antifungal mechanism. The study also explore the potential of B. subtilis M51 as a source of bioactive secondary metabolites by leveraging whole-genome sequence data and expanding the understanding of biocontrol-related metabolites in B. subtilis. In addition, the growth-promoting properties of M51 were evaluated using wheat seedlings in a greenhouse pot experiment. This work provides a theoretical basis for the development of biocontrol agents effective against root rot in wheat.

Materials and methods

Screening of antagonistic strains

Root samples for isolation and screening of antagonistic bacteria were collected from healthy wheat plants growing at Shangqiu, Henan Province, China. The wheat pathogens used comprised F. oxysporum YJ-2, Botrytis cinerea, Fusarium graminearum, Fusarium solani, Rhizoctonia cerealis, Bipolaris sorokiniana, and Alternaria alternata. All microbial cultures used in this study were maintained in the laboratory of the School of Biological Engineering, Henan University of Technology. Potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium (2% dextrose, 20% potato, 2% agar, pH 7.0) and potato dextrose broth (PDB) medium (2% dextrose, 20% potato, pH 7.0) were sterilized at 115 °C for 30 min.

The plate confrontation method was used for screening of the isolated bacterial strains for antagonistic effects against F. oxysporum YJ-2 [15]. Fusarium oxysporum YJ-2 was precultured on PDA medium at 30 °C for 5 d. A 0.8-cm-diameter plug taken from the pathogen colony was placed at the center of a 9-cm-diameter PDA plate, and two pure bacterial colonies were cultured at equal distance from the pathogenic plug. Plates not inoculated with any other strain served as controls. All plates were cultured for 5 d at 30 °C. The antagonistic activity of the screened strains was quantified as the inhibition rate. The inhibition rate (%) was calculated as (C − T)/C × 100, where C is the radius of the control fungus and T is the radius of the treated fungus [16]. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Biocontrol potential of strain M51

The antagonistic bacterial strain, designated M51, was further tested for their biocontrol potential by in vitro dual culture of the bacterium on PDA medium with a pathogenic fungus. Antagonism tests were conducted against Bo. cinerea, F. graminearum, F. solani, R. cerealis, A. alternata, and Bi. sorokiniana.

Strain M51 was grown in PDB at 30 °C for 12 h. PDA medium was inoculated with a 5 µL drop (OD600 = 3 × 106 CFU/mL). The three inoculation points on the medium were equidistant from the centre of the plate. A 0.8-cm-diameter fungal pathogen plug was placed in the center of the petri dish. The control plates were pathogen-free. Each plate was incubated at 30 °C. The inhibition rate was calculated as described in "Screening of antagonistic strains" section. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Identification of antagonistic bacterial strain M51

Morphological, physiological, and biochemical identification

Morphological analysis, and physiological and biochemical tests were used to identify the bacterial species for strain M51. The morphological analysis primarily involved observation of colony characteristics, comprising color, size, growth characteristics, microscopic morphology, and Gram staining. Physiological and biochemical identification comprised tests for citrate utilization, sugar catabolism, nitrate reduction, hydrogen sulfide gas production, indole production, and Gram staining [17].

Whole-genome sequencing and comparative genomic analysis

The experimental procedure was conducted following the standard protocols for the Illumina NovaSeq and PacBio Sequel platforms. This workflow includes critical steps such as sample quality evaluation, library preparation, library quality assessment, and sequencing [18]. Large-fragment DNA was recovered using the BluePippin automated nucleic acid recovery system. For PacBio Sequel sequencing, a library was constructed according to the SMRTbell® template prep protocol. For Illumina NovaSeq sequencing, libraries were prepared using the corresponding Illumina DNA prep kits. Sequencing was performed on the PacBio Sequel system to generate long reads and on the Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate high-accuracy short reads. Following quality assessment, raw reads from both platforms were processed: Illumina short reads were trimmed with Trimmomatic (v 0.39) after FastQC (v 0.11.9) inspection, and PacBio Sequel subreads were extracted and filtered (length > 1,000 bp) using SMRT Link v 11.1, yielding high-quality data for downstream genome assembly. The total raw bases were calculated by multiplying the number of paired-end reads by their average length. The PacBio long reads were error-corrected and assembled using the Canu assembler (v 2.0) (https://github.com/marbl/canu) [19]. The resulting assembly was then polished with the Illumina short-read data using Pilon (v 1.22) (https://github.com/broadinstitute/pilon) to produce a high-accuracy final genome sequence. The quality and completeness of the assembled genome were assessed using QUAST (v 5.1.0, http://quast.sourceforge.net/quast).

To independently assess the completeness and coverage of the final assembly, a K-mer analysis was performed. The quality-filtered Illumina reads were analyzed using Meryl (v 1.4.0, https://github.com/marbl/meryl) to generate a K-mer frequency spectrum. The estimated genome size derived from this K-mer distribution was compared with the size of the assembled genome. The ratio (assembled genome length / K-mer estimated genome length) approached 1.0, indicating that the assembly achieved near-complete coverage and effectively captured the genomic content present in the sequencing data. To further quantify the sequencing coverage, the filtered reads were mapped back to the final assembly using BWA-MEM (v 0.7.17, https://github.com/lh3/bwa), and the resulting alignment files were processed with SAMtools (v 1.9, https://github.com/samtools/samtools/releases/). The sequencing depth (527×) was then determined by dividing the total number of aligned bases by the estimated genome size, as calculated from the K-mer distribution.

Coding genes were predicted using Prodigal v 2.6.3. RepeatMasker v 4.0.5 was used to screen for repeat sequences. Prophages were predicted using PhiSpy v 2.3. CRISPR domains were identified using CRT v 1.2. Functional annotation of protein-coding genes was conducted based on gene comparisons with the GO (v 20180910), KEGG (v 201703), COG (v 4.0), NR (v 2019-03), SwissProt (v 2019-07-31), and Pfam (v 32.0) databases. AntiSMASH (v 7.1.0) was employed for rapid genome-wide identification, annotation, and analysis of biosynthetic gene clusters.

Prokka v 1.14.6 was used to re-annotate all genome sequences [20]. Synteny plots were generated using TBtools v 2.357 with default settings [21]. Four tools were applied to conduct pan-genome analysis, including Roary v 3.11.2 [22], Bacterial Pan Genome Analysis (BPGA) pipeline (v 1.3) [23], panX software [24], and OrthoMCL v 2.0.9 pipeline [25]. The OrthoVenn3 web platform was employed for comparative analysis and visual annotation of orthologous clusters among different strains [26]. All the aforementioned tools were utilized for comparative genomic analyses.

Phylogenetic analysis

The 16S rRNA gene of strain M51 was amplified using the universal primers 27F (5’-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3’) and 1492R (5’-GGTTACCTTGTGTTACGACTT-3’), which preliminarily identified it as belonging to the genus Bacillus. The strain YJ-2 was identified as Fusarium oxysporum based on the amplification and sequencing of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region using universal primers ITS1 (5’-TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG-3’)/ITS4 (5’-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC3’) and corroborated by its morphological characteristics (Figure S1). To further resolve species-level taxonomic assignment, genomic sequences of related strains were selected on the basis of 16 S rRNA sequence homology. Using the UBCG v3.0 extracted and concatenated 92 core genes, and constructed a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree with FastTree [27]. In addition, FastANI v 1.1 [28] and DNA-DNA hybridization (dDDH, https://ggdc.dsmz.de/ggdc.php, Formula 2) analyses were performed, further supporting the classification of strain M51. All strains analyzed in this part are typical strains.

Antifungal activity of bacterial fermentation filtrates

To investigate the antifungal activity of the fermentation broth filtrate, M51 was inoculated into PDB (250 mL) and cultured at 30 °C for 48 h. The fermentation broth was centrifuged at 8150 g for 10 min to remove the bacteria and the supernatant was sterile-filtered with a 0.22 μm membrane. The filtrate was mixed with PDA medium to achieve final concentrations of 5%, 10%, 20%, 25%, and 50% (v/v). The media containing different concentrations of the filtrate were poured into culture dishes and inoculated with F. oxysporum at the center. PDA medium supplemented with sterile water was used as the control treatment. After incubation for 7 d (by which time the F. oxysporum colony covered the plate in the control treatment) photographs were captured and the colony diameter was measured. The inhibition rate (%) was calculated as (A − a)/A × 100, where A is the diameter of the control fungus and a is the diameter of the treatment fungus. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Preparation of crude extracts from M51 culture filtrate and its inhibitory effect

The strain M51 was inoculated into PDB (250 mL) and cultured at 30 °C for 48 h. The fermentation broth was centrifuged at 8150 g for 10 min to remove bacteria. Subsequently, the resulting culture supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to ensure sterility. The pH of the culture filtrate was adjusted to 2.0 with 1 mol HCl and incubated overnight at 4 °C to precipitate crude extracts. After centrifugation at 4 °C and 10,000 g for 10 min, the collected precipitate was resuspended in methanol at 1/100 of the original volume. The pH was adjusted to 7.0 with 1 mol NaOH. The supernatant was collected and filtered with a 0.22 μm membrane to obtain the crude extract [29].

The crude extract was mixed with PDA medium to a final concentration of 2% (v/v). The medium supplemented with the crude extract was poured into petri dishes and inoculated with F. oxysporum at the center. PDA medium supplemented with 2% methanol was used as the control treatment. After incubation for 7 d (by which time the F. oxysporum colony covered the control plate), photographs were captured and the colony diameter was measured to calculate the inhibition rate. The inhibition rate was calculated as described in "Antifungal activity of bacterial fermentation filtrates" section.

Detection of small-molecule secondary metabolites in the crude extract of strain M51

The crude extract was obtained using methanol as the sole solvent. The same methanol-extracted crude extract was analyzed for the presence of antimicrobial metabolites using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography–quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC-QTOF-MS/MS) following a previously reported method [30]. Mass spectrometry was performed on a Waters Xevo G2-XS system and the chromatography column was a Waters Acquity UHPLC BEH C18 (100 mm × 2.1 mm × 1.7 mm). The MassLynx (v 4.2.0) and UNIFI (v 1.9.13.9) scientific information systems were used to process the mass data and perform structural analysis. Injection parameters were as follows: volume 4 µL, flow rate 0.30 mL/min, and column temperature 40 °C.

Effect of crude extract on reactive oxygen species and cell membrane integrity

Fusarium oxysporum was inoculated into 250 mL conical flasks containing 100 mL PDB and incubated at 30 °C for 48 h. An appropriate amount of mycelium was collected and resuspended in 980 µL PDB, followed by the addition of 20 µL crude extract. The mixture was cultured at 30 °C for 12 h. The control group was treated with an equal volume of PDB medium.

The treated mycelium was collected, rinsed twice with PBS, and then incubated in reactive oxygen species (ROS) staining solution in the dark for 10 min. The ROS staining kit was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The mycelium was washed twice and then observed under a fluorescence microscope. A propidium iodide (PI) staining kit (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was used to evaluate the impact of the crude extract on cell membrane integrity. After washing twice with PBS, the treated mycelium was resuspended in PBS and stained with PI (10 µg/mL) for 10 min in the dark. After staining, the treated mycelium was washed twice and observed using a fluorescence microscope.

Effect of crude extract on antioxidant enzyme activity of F. oxysporum

The impact of different concentrations of the crude extract on the activity of peroxidase (POD) and malondialdehyde (MDA) content in F. oxysporum was investigated. A sample (0.1 g) of F. oxysporum mycelium was added to 2% crude extract and supplemented with PBS up to a final volume of 1 mL. The addition of an equal volume of PBS served as the control. The mixtures were incubated at 30 °C for 12 h. The determination of POD activity and MDA content was conducted in accordance with previously reported methods [31]. The POD and MDA assay kits were purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.

Detection of hydrolytic enzymes and growth-promoting substances in strain M51

The activities of hydrolases, specifically proteases, cellulase, and amylases, were determined using skim milk agar medium, carboxymethyl cellulose agar medium, and starch hydrolysis medium, respectively, in accordance with previously reported methods [32]. Protease activity of strain M51 was determined by inoculating the strains in skim milk agar medium. Incubation was carried out in the dark at 30 °C for 48 h. Cellulase activity of strain M51 was determined by inoculating the strains in carboxymethyl cellulose agar medium. Incubation was carried out in the dark at 30 °C for 48 h. Amylases activity of strain M51 was determined by inoculating the strains in cstarch hydrolysis medium. The strain M51 was inoculated on the medium followed by incubation 30 °C for 48 h. The zone of degradation of starch was determined.

The production of IAA and siderophores, and solubilization of phosphorus were evaluated to determine whether strain M51 has the ability to promote plant growth [33, 34]. The ability of the bacterium to produce IAA was assessed using Salkowski’s colorimetric assay. The strain M51 was grown in LB broth with the addition of 0.5 mg/L tryptohan. After incubation, the broth was centrifuged at 6000 × g for 10 min and the supernatant was collected. 1 mL of supernatant and 4 mL of Salkowski’s reagent were mixed at room temperature and incubated for 30 min in the dark. The absorbance of developed pink color was spectrophotometrically determined at 530 nm. A calibration curve was determined using IAA pure (Merck, USA), as a standard. The strain was inoculated onto Chrome Azurol S assay medium for detection of siderophore production. Siderophore production was assessed on Chrome Azurol S (CAS) agar medium by the presence of an orange halo zone around the bacterial colony on blue agar, after 48 h of incubation at 30 °C. For the evaluation of phosphate solubilization, the strain M51 was grown on the National Botanical Research Institute’s phosphate growth (NBRIP) medium, containing bromophenol blue (BPB) and tricalcium phosphate as the single phosphorus source and incubated for 7 days at 30 °C. The clear halo zone around the bacterial colony indicated the phosphate solubilizing activity of the tested strain.

Pot experiment

The growth-promoting effects of strain M51 on wheat were investigated in a pot experiment. Wheat seeds (Yushengmai 20242033, Henan Qiule Seeds Technology Co., Ltd., Zhengzhou, China) were surface-sterilized with 0.5% sodium hypochlorite solution for 30 min, rinsed thoroughly with sterile distilled water three times, and subsequently sown in plastic pots (110 mm × 80 mm) under a controlled temperature of 25 ± 2 °C and a 12 h light/12 h dark photoperiod [35]. Ten wheat seedlings were grown in each pot, with three pots forming one replication. The pots were irrigated with water or fermentation broth supernatant, respectively, with three replicates per treatment. Irrigation was conducted at 5-day intervals (50 mL was supplied per irrigation event). After growth for 14 days, the height, root length, and fresh weight of the wheat seedlings were measured [36].

Statistical analysis

Results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three triplicate experiments, with preliminary SD calculations in Microsoft Excel 2021. Normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and variance homogeneity (Levene’s test) were confirmed for all datasets (p > 0.05) in SPSS 27.0.1 prior to one-way ANOVA, which was performed using SPSS 27.0.1 and GraphPad Prism 10. Post-hoc multiple comparisons were conducted via Duncan’s multiple range test (p < 0.05), and significant intergroup differences are marked with different lowercase letters above the bars.

Results

Screening for antagonistic bacterial strains

A total of 29 bacterial strains were isolated from the wheat roots. All isolates were subjected to plate confrontation assays with F. oxysporum YJ-2 and five strains (M29, M6, M11, M8, M51) had inhibitory effects on F.oxysporum ( Fig. 1A ). Among the five strains, M51 showed the strongest antagonistic effect against F. oxysporum, with an inhibition rate of 64.35%. Given the effective inhibitory impact of M51 on F. oxysporum, M51 was selected for further identification and analysis.

Antifungal activity of strain M51

The strain M51 was observed to have strong antifungal activity, as assessed with simultaneous dual assays, against six fungal pathogens of wheat. The inhibition rates of strain M51 against Bo. cinerea, F. solani, F. graminearum, Bi. sorokiniana, R. cerealis, and A alternata were 44.44%, 51.11%, 34.07%, 40.00%, 69.63%, and 39.26%, respectively (Fig. 1B). These results confirmed that strain M51 showed broad-spectrum antifungal activity against all tested phytopathogenic fungi.

Identification and physiological and biochemical characterization of strain M51

After incubation for 48 h on PDA medium, strain M51 formed round, reddish colonies with a generally smooth surface, rough edges, and an opaque appearance (Fig. 2A). Strain M51 is a Gram-positive bacterium, was unable to produce hydrogen sulfide gas, but had the ability to consume citrate, ferment glucose, and liquefy gelatin (Table 1). The 16 S rRNA gene sequence of strain M51 was deposited in the GenBank database (Genbank accession no. PQ394939) showed 99.94% similarity to that of B. subtilis IAM 12,118 (NR112116.2). Following identification based on 16 S rRNA gene homology, a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using 92 core genes with the UBCG toolset, which placed strain M51 in a cluster with B. subtilis IAM 12,118 (= DSM 10) and 168 (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, ANI values between M51 and closely related B. subtilis strains reached up to 99.9% (Fig. 2B). The genomic relatedness was further substantiated by dDDH values of 100.00% between M51 and B. subtilis DSM 10 and 168, respectively (Table S1). Based on these morphological, physiological, biochemical, and genetic characteristics, strain M51 was identified as B. subtilis.

Inhibitory effect of sterile filtrate from strain M51 on F. oxysporum

Supplementation of PDA medium with 5%, 10%, 20%, 25%, or 50% sterile filtrate of strain M51 had a distinct inhibitory effect on F. oxysporum. The inhibitory rates on the seventh day of growth were 22.76%, 31.93%, 41.10%, 44.71%, and 49.99%, respectively (Fig. 3). These results showed that, compared with the control, all tested filtrate concentrations had strong antifungal activity. The M51 sterile filtrate concentrations of 20% – 50% most strongly prevented normal growth of F. oxysporum.

Effects of crude extract on mycelium and intracellular ROS of F. oxysporum

To further verify the inhibitory effect of M51 metabolites on F. oxysporum, the inhibitory activity against F. oxysporum of the crude extract from the M51 fermentation filtrate was determined (Fig. 4B). The results inferred that addition of 2% crude extract to PDA medium had a significant antifungal impact and the inhibition rate was 23.90%, 33.02%, and 36.26% on the first, third, and fifth days of treatment, respectively. The antifungal activity was highest on the seventh day and the inhibition rate was 47.78%.

Diacetyl dichlorofluorescein (DCFH-DA) is a fluorescent probe used for detection of ROS within cells. It is hydrolyzed into a non-fluorescent compound by esterases within the cell, and then oxidized by ROS into a fluorescent product, which can be detected using a fluorescence microscope. DCFH-DA is widely used for monitoring the redox state of cells and assessing the degree of oxidative stress. Based on the production of blue fluorescence in living cells, the amount and changes in the cellular ROS content can be determined. As shown in Fig. 4A, the blue fluorescence in the mycelium of the treated group was stronger than that of the control, indicating that the crude extract of strain M51 induced the accumulation of ROS in the mycelial cells of F. oxysporum. It was speculated that the extract from M51 may disturb the redox homeostasis of the cells, resulting in oxidative stress, thereby inhibiting the growth of F. oxysporum and inducing its apoptosis.

Effects of crude extract on cell membrane integrity and antioxidant capacity of F. oxysporum

After treatment with the crude extract from the M51 fermentation filtrate, the mycelium of F. oxysporum became rough and showed abnormal distortions, cell enlargement, and an increased in the number of branches, whereas the untreated mycelium was more uniform in morphology and did not show the above abnormalities (Fig. 5A). After PI staining, the treated F. oxysporum mycelium showed these obvious red fluorescence, whereas the untreated mycelium showed no red fluorescence signal. The results indicated that the crude extract of strain M51 may disrupted the cell membrane of F. oxysporum and, as a consequence, the fluorescent substrate could enter the cell and red fluorescence was produced.

The POD activity and MDA content in F. oxysporum mycelium were investigated after treatment with different concentrations of the crude extract of M51. The POD activity of the 2% crude extract-treated groups was significantly lower than that of the control (untreated group); compared with that of the control group, POD activity decreased by 54.76% after treatment (Fig. 5B). The activity of oxidoreductase in the mycelium decreased, so free radicals within the cells could not be removed timeously and the redox homeostasis in the mycelium cells was disrupted, therefore the mycelial growth and development was inhibited.

MDA is one compound produced by the oxidative degradation of unsaturated fatty acids on the membrane system in the presence of oxygen radicals. Compared with the control group, the treated group showed a significant increase in MDA content with increase in crude extract concentration; the MDA content increased by 24.52% after treatment (Fig. 5B). These results indicated that the lipids on the cell membrane were oxidized and the cell membrane was disrupted.

Whole-genome sequencing and analysis of strain M51 and Comparative genomic analysis

A graphical circular map of the M51 genome, showing the genome structure and functions, is presented in Fig. 6A. The complete genome of M51 was sequenced at 527x coverage and comprised a circular 4,425,418 bp chromosome with 43.05% GC content. Genome map analysis showed there were 4498 coding sequences (CDS), 108 tRNAs, 30 rRNAs, 3 prophages, and 4 CRISPR domains. The total length of the 4498 CDS was 3,896,370 bp, accounting for 88.05% of the total genome sequence.

Analysis based on annotations in the COG database revealed that 3300 CDS were classifiable into 21 functional categories, accounting for 74.03% of the total CDS (Fig. 6B). This indicated that strain M51 may have a strong biological metabolism capacity, which is associated with high activity of coding genes associated with transcription, as well as amino acid transport and metabolism in the genome.

To identify candidate genes associated with the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites in the bacterial genome (Table 2), antiSMASH v 7.1.0 was employed for rapid genome-wide identification, annotation, and analysis of biosynthetic gene clusters in the strain M51 genome. It was possible to identify 15 gene clusters that are involved in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. Three of these clusters are involved with the generation of non-ribosomal peptides, including bacillibactin, fengycin, and surfactin. One cluster is responsible for the biosynthesis of polyketides, specifically bacillaene. Two clusters are responsible for terpene biosynthesis, while four clusters code for ribosomal peptides, namely bacilysin, sublancin 168, subtilosin A, and sporulation killing factor. In addition, one cluster is responsible for pulcherriminic acid and three gene clusters encode T3PK, CDPS, and RRE-containing proteins, respectively.

Following the annotation of the M51 genome, a comparative pangenome analysis was employed to assess its genetic makeup relative to other Bacillus strains with documented activity against F. oxysporum. Specifically, to investigate the biocontrol potential of strain M51 against F. oxysporum YJ-2, comparative pangenome analysis of M51 and nine representative Bacillus strains, all documented for their antagonistic activity against this pathogen (Table S2). The results revealed that the core gene repertoire of M51 was largely consistent with that of the model strain B. subtilis 168 (Fig. 7). To further delineate the genetic differences between M51 and its conspecific model strain 168, TBtools and OrthoVenn3 were used to compare and analyse the orthologous clusters based on protein sequences. The comparative analysis identified two unique clusters in M51 relative to strain 168. These clusters were functionally annotated as serine-type endopeptidase activity (GO:0004252) and viral tail assembly (GO:0098003) (Table S3). Furthermore, secondary metabolite prediction in strain 168 yielded results consistent with those obtained for M51, suggesting a conserved potential for the biosynthesis of antimicrobial compounds (Table S4). Collectively, this comparative analysis confirms that M51 shares the fundamental genetic foundation for antagonism with established biocontrol strains, while also pinpointing unique genes that could underlie specialized adaptations. These findings imply that while the core metabolic and antagonistic machinery is shared, the unique genetic elements in M51 may contribute to specialized functional adaptations, warranting further investigation into their specific roles in pathogen inhibition.

Discussion

The prevalence of wheat root rot has caused substantial losses in agricultural production and led to severe economic losses. Currently, chemicals are predominantly used to achieve disease control and to maintain yields. However, excessive and repeated use of chemical fungicides in agriculture has led to environmental pollution, a threat to human health, and the emergence of resistant pathogens. These outcomes are incompatible with the concept of green and sustainable agriculture [49]. A previous study reported that B. amyloliquefaciens BCB16 showed strong antagonistic activity against F. oxysporum, which significantly reduced the incidence of disease caused by F. oxysporum [50]. The B. velezensis BA was isolated by Jahan et al. and showed broad-spectrum inhibitory activity against a diverse range of pathogenic fungi, including F. oxysporum [51]. In the present study, the strain M51 was isolated from healthy wheat roots and was identified as B. subtilis based on its morphological, physiological, biochemical, and molecular characteristics. The present experiment testing the antagonistic effects of M51 against seven phytopathogenic fungi (F. oxysporum, F. graminearum, F. solani, Bi. sorokiniana, Bo. cinerea, R. cerealis, and (A) alternata) showed that M51 had notable antagonistic effects against all of the tested fungi and that the strongest inhibitory effect was against F. oxysporum and the inhibition rate was 64.35%. The antifungal rate of strain M51was similar with biocontrol strains (B) subtilis BS-58 (inhibition rate 68.25%), also exhibits good antifungal effect [52].

By studying the inhibitory mechanisms of antagonistic bacteria, the antagonistic effects can be exploited more effectively. Bacillus spp. are effective antagonistic bacteria because the inhibition of plant pathogens is due to the production of a variety of secondary metabolites and antibiotics. Antagonistic bacteria are able to secrete secondary metabolites that disrupt the cellular integrity of the pathogen and suppress its antioxidant system [53]. A previous study identified genes for the synthesis of lipopeptides in the genome of B. subtilis DHA41, and treatment of F. oxysporum mycelium with crude extracts significantly inhibited mycelial growth and disrupted mycelial cell membranes [54]. The present research showed that addition of the M51 crude extract to the culture medium significantly inhibited the growth of F. oxysporum mycelium, the hyphae were abnormally twisted, the cells were enlarged, and the cell membrane was damaged. It can be inferred that one important mechanism by which bacteriostatic substances inhibit the growth of pathogenic fungi is the destruction of their cell membrane system.

The accumulation of ROS causes oxidative damage to biological cells, but ROS can be scavenged through the action of antioxidant enzymes to maintain cell stability [55]. In the present study, it was found that treatment of F. oxysporum with the M51 crude extract from the fermentation broth decreased the activity of mycelial antioxidant enzymes, leading to ROS accumulation, which causes cellular damage to pathogenic fungal mycelium. Meanwhile, the activity of F. oxysporum POD was significantly reduced and the MDA content was increased. POD can catalyze the oxidation of phenols and amines by hydrogen peroxide, serving a dual role in eliminating the toxicity of hydrogen peroxide, phenols and amines. MDA is a major product of lipid oxidation in membrane systems [56]. Therefore, it can be speculated that the crude extract disrupted the original redox system of the pathogen and damaged the mycelial membranes, thus inhibiting the growth of the pathogen. This research preliminary studied the inhibitory mechanism of strain M51 against F. oxysporum. Nevertheless, the rapid evolution of F. oxysporum resistance under field conditions currently constrains biological control efficacy, and the mechanism of strain-induced resistance remains incompletely understood-this constitutes a key direction for future extension of the study. Subsequent transcriptome analysis to delineate the expression characteristics of resistance-related genes in F. oxysporum under strain M51 treatment will further enhance the applied value of this research.

Bacillus spp. also has the potential to promote plant growth and indirectly aid plants to defend against pathogen attack. Defense-related enzymes such as chitinase, glucanase, cellulase and protease inhibit the growth and multiplication of pathogens by hydrolyzing key components of fungal and bacterial cell walls [57]. The B. stercoris B.PNR1 genome contains gene clusters of non-ribosomal peptide synthetase, polyketide synthase and exhibits activities such as producing hydrolytic enzymes, antimicrobial compounds, and the potential for disease control under greenhouse conditions [58]. B. velezensis Q-426 genome includes genes associated with secondary metabolite biosynthesis based on whole-genome sequencing results, including surfactin, iturin, and fengycin, among 12 lipopeptides [59]. Genomic analysis of B. velezensis 1–10 revealed gene clusters encoding bacillibactin, iturin, and fengycin, and the presence of these substances was confirmed through antimicrobial experiments and mass spectrometric analysis [60]. In the present genomic analysis of M51, gene clusters encoding surfactin, fengycin, bacillaene, bacillibactin, subtilosin A, and bacilysin were identified, and the production of these substances was verified through UHPLC-QTOF-MS/MS. The results indicate that M51 is capable of producing a variety of synergistic antimicrobial lipopeptides to inhibit the growth of plant pathogens. The whole-genome sequencing analysis confirmed that the M51 genome contained multiple genes associated with phosphorus solubilization, protease production, siderophore production, and synthesis of lipopeptides, which are involved in antagonistic and plant growth-promoting activities. Strain M51, inheriting the prominent phenotypic traits of this genus, exhibits considerable potential for large-scale industrial production. Regarding formulation stability, although systematic stability assessments have not yet been conducted for strain M51, the inherent stress tolerance conferred by the spore structure of Bacillus subtilis has been sufficiently validated, providing robust evidence to support the stability of M51-based formulations [61]. In terms of safety for commercial application, most small-molecule secondary metabolites from B. subtilis can be used as nutraceuticals, providing both nutritional and pharmaceutical functions, and as therapeutic/antibiotic agents. Bioactive peptides such as cyclic lipopeptides (e.g., surfactin) are active against microbial infections [62]. Combined with the intrinsic safety characteristics of Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS)-category strains, these findings collectively demonstrate that strain M51 possesses favorable potential for commercial development [63]. In addition, the strain M51 produced cellulose, amylase, and IAA, which can stimulate cell proliferation and elongation of plant roots and thus regulate the growth of plant roots. In the pot experiment, the height, fresh weight, and root length of wheat seedlings were increased after irrigation with the fermentation broth supernatant from M51 and 168 broth, confirming that M51 has plant growth-promoting effect. These results demonstrate that strain M51 possesses strong antagonistic and growth-promoting potential, thereby providing crucial theoretical support for subsequent field trials. Although Bacillus species are generally recognized as environmentally benign biocontrol agents, large-scale soil application may alter the structure and diversity of soil microbial communities via nutrient competition and the secretion of antimicrobial metabolites [64]. For instance, the antimicrobial metabolites produced by strain M51 may exert non-target inhibitory effects on beneficial soil microorganisms. Additionally, the strain’s dominant colonization in the rhizosphere might reduce microbial community evenness and compromise ecosystem stability [65]. Subsequent studies will systematically evaluate these non-target effects, thereby providing empirical support for the large-scale application of the M51 strain in green agricultural disease management.

In summary, an endophytic bacterium, strain M51, was isolated from wheat roots and identified as B. subtilis. Strain M51 showed the stronger inhibitory activity against F. oxysporum but also had pronounced broad-spectrum antifungal activity. The strain exerted strong biocontrol inhibition of Fusarium root rot and promoted the growth of wheat seedlings. Genome sequencing revealed that M51 contains a single circular chromosome 4,425,418 bp in length, with 4498 coding sequences and an average GC content of 43.05%. A large number of genes associated with antifungal and plant growth-promoting traits were identified in the genome. Strain M51 was capable of producing IAA, lipopeptide synthetases, siderophores, hydrolytic enzymes, and phosphorus solubilization. Crude extracts from M51 caused mycelial wrinkling, disruption of cell membranes, reduction in antioxidant capacity, and increase in oxidative damage. In addition, we identified some small secondary metabolites, such as dimethyl carbate, D-limonene, and latilure, which provide additional means for the prevention and control of fungal diseases. The findings imply that B. subtilis M51 had the potential promote the growth of the wheat seedlings. In the present study, only a pot experiment was conducted, and thus field experiments are needed to verify the effects of M51 on disease prevention and growth promotion. Although several secondary metabolites were detected in this study, the specific antifungal activity and its mechanism were not analyzed. Therefore, future investigations will examine the signaling pathways that are associated with the antifungal effects of the secondary metabolites.
