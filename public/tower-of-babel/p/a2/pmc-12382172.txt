Bulgarian exploratory analysis of time from diagnosis to treatment of lung cancer patients (Beat) - a retrospective database study on the patient pathway and time from diagnosis to treatment
Authors: Assen Dudov, Nikolay Conev, Mila Petrova, Ivan Tonev, Martina Nacheva, Maria V Dimitrova, Maria J Dimitrova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobocan data for 2020 rank lung cancer as the third most common malignancy (12.1%) and the deadliest cancer (18.3%) in Bulgaria. The current study aims to analyze lung cancer patient pathways and time to treatment from diagnosis based on real-world data.MethodsWe conducted an observational database study based on the secondary use of data collected for other purposes for 3 years (2020–2022). The main outcomes of interest were time from diagnosis to therapy measured in days on national and regional levels, stratified by therapy type and year and number of newly diagnosed patients with lung cancer in each of the years for the observed period. Data were analyzed through descriptive statistics.ResultsThroughout the observed period, 8,585 patients were newly diagnosed with lung cancer, averaging approximately 2,861 diagnoses per year. Most patients’ records reported non-small cell lung cancer (4536, 64%), Furthermore, most patients were diagnosed with advanced stages of cancer, with 61% (4297) being diagnosed with Stage IV. Time from Diagnosis to First Treatment (median) outcome showed variance by regions and type, from 7 to 40 days in the case of surgical treatment and 26 to 85 days in the case of combination therapy. The time from Diagnosis to First Systemic Treatment indicator shows a median delay between diagnosis to treatment of 42 days.ConclusionsThe study confirms that dynamic patient registers are crucial for performing real-world studies of treatment patterns and identifying gaps and inequities on a national level. There is a significant need to address the lack of screening programs for lung cancer in Bulgaria and ensure more equitable distribution of oncology centers.Keywords: Lung cancer, Patient pathway, Real-world data, Time to treatment, Diagnosis

Introduction

GLOBOCAN data rated lung cancer as the second most common malignancy (12,1%) and the deadliest cancer (18.3%) in Bulgaria in 2020. Lung cancer induced an estimated 4,300 incidence cases (12.1%) with a relatively low 5-year prevalence of 5,296 compared to other localizations. For example, colorectal cancer has a high 5-year prevalence and breast cancer with 13,674 cases [1].

In recent years, we have observed significant progress in innovative health technologies and access to more precise diagnostics and treatment of lung cancer based on the development of novel assays and therapies targeting molecular and immune mechanisms [2]. Oncologists are now able to provide a more individualized approach based on more accurate staging, gene mutation identification, molecular subtype determination, treatment regimen choice and assessment of risk of recurrence or progression, thus increasing the chance of overall survival and quality of life of patients with lung cancer [3, 4].The effectiveness of novel therapies, however, is difficult to prove if there is no adequate access to structured data for all patients assigned to therapy and the needed diagnostic tests.

In this respect, patient records and population-based registries may be very useful in predicting cancer patients’ treatment outcomes and survival rates. They could also provide evidence from real-life treatment practice and improve health resources and cost allocation-related decision-making. In recent years some cancer registries started to collect population-based information on treatment patterns, progression, survival and mortality status, thus providing overall mapping of the patient pathway [5].

Lung cancer diagnosis and treatment in Bulgaria are managed according to the European guidelines and recommendations have been adopted into the national pharmacotherapeutic guideline on medical oncology [6].

However, since 2013, the National Cancer Registry in Bulgaria has not been properly managed and cannot be used as a relevant source of information. That lack of national data significantly increases the need for alternative sources. This raised interest in analyzing cancer patient pathways and time to treatment from diagnosis based on real-world data, emphasizing the gaps in patients’ access to timely diagnostics and treatment [7].

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a 3-year non-interventional retrospective database study on lung cancer (ICD: C34) based on secondary use of data collected for other purposes between 2020 and 2022. No testing or evaluation of patients was specifically conducted for this protocol, and informed consent was not required in this context. The data collection and analysis scope excluded safety and patient involvement reconciliation; therefore, informed ethical permission was not required for the study.

For the data collection and analysis, we used the electronic artificial intelligence platform of Sqilline - Danny Platform (www.sqilline.com) which aggregates and harmonizes heterogeneous data sources from oncology practices. Medical information was collected from the central information system used by the medical site to process and store clinical data. For the analysis, data were collected from 36 major hospitals, which guarantees a high degree of population coverage and the representativeness of the sample.

Danny Platform collects data continuously every month from all major hospitals in the country and stores it in a database. Encryption and data anonymization are applied on every step of the data transfer - starting with the origin, going through our data centers and finally reaching the front-facing users of the Danny Platform system. Collected data includes oncology protocols, ambulatory procedures, hospitalizations, administered drug therapies from hospital pharmacies and dispensary observations. While the Danny Platform collects both structured (parameterized) and unstructured (free-text) information, the information used for this analysis is entirely structured, meaning that the data analyzed is that provided by the medical sites; it does not undergo additional processing for information extraction and is therefore not subject to a processing error.

Study population

The study population included all newly diagnosed lung cancer patients (stage I, II, III and IV) aged 18 and older in each of the years from 1st of January 2020, to 31st of December 2022. A two-year look-back period (January 1st, 2018, until December 31st, 2019) was included in case patients didn’t get the C34 code during this period. Patients diagnosed with other cancer types were excluded from the analysis. Therapies without a preceding examination recommending the prescribed treatment and the time gap between the first examination and treatment exceeding 6 months were excluded from the analysis.

Outcomes of interest

The main outcomes of interest were defined as:

The number of newly diagnosed lung cancer patients from 2020 to 2022, categorized by stage and histology.

The number of newly diagnosed lung cancer patients from 2020 to 2022, categorized by stage and histology.

The time (in days) from diagnosis to the initiation of radiotherapy, surgery, and/or drug therapy (first treatment initiation) or a combination of therapies, presented at both the national and regional levels, and stratified by therapy type and year.

The time (in days) from diagnosis to the initiation of radiotherapy, surgery, and/or drug therapy (first treatment initiation) or a combination of therapies, presented at both the national and regional levels, and stratified by therapy type and year.

The time (in days) from the ambulatory procedure/protocol to the initiation of radiotherapy, surgery, and/or drug therapy (first treatment initiation) or combination therapy, also presented at both the national and regional levels, and stratified by therapy type and year.

The time (in days) from the ambulatory procedure/protocol to the initiation of radiotherapy, surgery, and/or drug therapy (first treatment initiation) or combination therapy, also presented at both the national and regional levels, and stratified by therapy type and year.

Data validation

Data was analyzed using descriptive statistics, stratified by therapy type and region. The median and interquartile range (IQR) were reported for time to treatment, as the data distribution was skewed, making the median and IQR more robust against outliers.

Limitations of the study

A key limitation of this study is the lack of outcome data, which prevents assessment of the clinical effectiveness or long-term impact of the treatments examined. However, the primary focus of this analysis is to characterize treatment patterns and time to therapy initiation, providing valuable insights into real-world clinical practice. Future research incorporating outcome data will be essential to fully understand the implications of these treatment approaches.

Results

Patient’s characteristics

In total, 8,585 patients were newly diagnosed with C34 throughout the observed period. On average, approximately 2,861 patients were diagnosed per year. Of these, 7,098 had no other carcinomas and were included in the current analysis.

Most patients’ records reported non-small cell lung cancer (N = 4,536, 64%), and a smaller number reported small cell lung cancer (N = 890, 13. Most patients were diagnosed with advanced stages of cancer, with the majority of whom (N = 4,297, 61%) being diagnosed in Stage IV. In the case of some patients (N = 1,672, 23%), disease type was missing and not reported, and they were excluded from the analysis. For some of the patients there was also missing data for the disease stage. The median days passed between Ambulatory Procedure 05 (a local regulated medical activity that creates treatment plans for patients with malignant diseases, allowing hospitals to report services to the National Health Insurance Fund (NHIF) for reimbursement) and the first treatment, as surgery, was 10 days. Times in 2020 were higher than in 2021 and 2022, likely due to the COVID-19 pandemic. However, the sample sizes are relatively small, and results are therefore subject to uncertainty. For the same reason, while breakdowns by region are also not very reliable, the longest waiting times were observed in the North-eastern (19 days), followed by South-central (16 days), and the shortest in the South-western (5 days). An interquartile range of 30 days indicates a relatively widespread distribution.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first non-interventional study on treatment patterns and patient pathways in lung cancer in Bulgaria based on real-world data.

Nevertheless, the results of the study are more preliminary, they confirm that lung cancer is still mostly diagnosed in the advanced stages of the disease, as more than 60% of the patients for the observed period were diagnosed at stage IV. The most prevalent subtype is non-small lung cancer, which was found in 64% of patients. The delayed diagnosis might be attributed to the lack of a national screening program for lung cancer in Bulgaria and the lack of equal distribution to oncology centers and oncology specialists among the population and geographical regions. Our suggestions are in line with a published study conducted by Petrov M et al. The authors explored the feasibility of implementing national lung cancer screening programs in the Balkan region. The study also aimed to identify the potential challenges and possibilities of such implementation. The results show that only Croatia has a fully implemented lung screening program (LSP), while Greece is in progress, and Bulgaria and Serbia lack such programs. Key barriers identified by the authors include financial constraints with low-dose CT, a shortage of trained radiologists, insufficient political will, disagreement among healthcare professionals, and low public awareness [8]. The disparities identified in this area could be significant for optimizing national policies regarding access to healthcare professionals, medical services, and medications, particularly in regions far from major oncology centers.

Our study also examines treatment times across different geographical regions. The results revealed significant geographical variation, particularly in areas where only one distant regional city has an oncology center. These results are in line with other published studies with the same focus. In 2017, a study by Rohova M examined the distribution of healthcare personnel across Bulgaria. It found that the Southeast and North Central regions lag behind areas with university hospitals and medical universities. The investigation also demonstrated that the availability of radiologists has declined in recent years [9].

In Bulgaria, genetic testing is currently an out-of-pocket expense as public expenditures do not yet cover it. This situation could impact the time needed to diagnose and initiate treatment and could be one of the possible reasons for disparities in treatment times found in our study. In the last months, the National Health Insurance Fund has initiated changes in the policy concerning coverage of genetic testing, which might address this gap.

Our study also found that systemic therapy experienced the longest delay from diagnosis to treatment, which could be influenced by the lack of genetic testing reimbursement and issues with drug shortages. Shortages of oncology medicines are primarily due to exclusion from the Positive Drug List, driven by administrative burdens and financial constraints from the National Health Insurance Fund. To address these issues, regulatory authorities proposed measures including changes to registration and pricing procedures, as well as separate budgeting for essential oncology medicines to ensure full coverage in cases of overspending [10, 11].

These results support other published studies analyzing the time from diagnosis to treatment. A recent study in Poland assessed the time elapsed from diagnosis to first treatment and the factors that impact the delay. The authors assess the waiting time for therapy as too long and mostly delayed by decisions on the type of cancer and specific features related to the health care system. 27% of the patients included in the study report out-of-pocket expenditures for diagnostic procedures. A significant correlation between age, education, professional activity, marital status and use of private medical services is also found. Oncologists in Poland are also few per the general population and are overloaded with paperwork [12].

Published data also show that in some countries like Great Britain, the Czech Republic and the Scandinavian countries, the waiting time from diagnosis to first treatment is regulated by law. In Great Britain, the maximum waiting time from diagnosis to treatment is 31 days [13].In Germany, waiting times are used as a tool to control access to hospital services. However, the study shows that patients with private health insurance have shorter waiting times [14].

A study by Tsiligianni I et al. analyses the barriers and gaps in the lung cancer patient journey in Greece. The results from the applied mixed method approach show that the Greek lung cancer patients also had delays in treatment time, mainly due to physicians’ missteps, the COVID-19 pandemic, and a lack of population-based interventions and patient awareness about risk factors [15].

Time lag to treatment initiation in Bulgaria, especially with innovative therapies, may be also attributed to the fact that there is a gap between inclusion in the positive drug list and the actual payment with public expenditures by the National Health Insurance Fund due to regulatory requirements for discount negotiations between the marketing authorization holder and the Fund [16].

A study by Vancoppenolle J et al., published in 2024, analyses time to patient access to innovative cancer medicines in Hungary, Italy, the Netherlands, Belgium, Switzerland and France. The study shows that some of the new cancer therapies reached the patients in late stages of the disease due to national differences in the pricing and reimbursement requirements [17].

The COVID-19 pandemic also contributed to the delay in oncology treatment initiation. Roderburg C et al. conducted a study to assess the prevalence and the factors related to treatment delay in gastrointestinal (GI) cancer in Europe due to the Pandemic. The results among 30,171 GI cancer patients in EU 5 (Germany, France, UK, Spain and Italy) show that delay was documented in 45% of the studied population. The highest delay was observed in France (6.7%) and Italy (6.5%), and the lowest in Spain (1.9%, p < 0.0001). This is not surprising because Italy and France were among the very first countries affected the most by the COVID-19 pandemic [18].

The COVID-19 pandemic also negatively contributed to the delay in the implementation of Europe’s Beating Cancer Plan [19].A recently published meta-analysis shows that the implementation of lung cancer screening in Europe is still a challenge for the healthcare systems of the member states. Some activities coming from medical societies such as the European Respiratory Society (ERS), the European Society of Radiology (ESR) and the European Society of Thoracic Imaging (ESTI) are aiming at implementing indicators of screening for lung cancer among risk groups such as smokers and radiation injury but still, we need a more structured approach to overcome the identified barriers as operational, psychological and financial [20].

An OECD report characterizes waiting times as one of the most frequent barriers to accessing health services and treatment. The report shows that countries, like Japan and South Korea, where a fee-for-service method is applied, tend to have above-average consultation rates, while countries, like most of the European countries (i.e. Sweden), with mostly salaried doctors tend to have below-average rates. In countries like Spain, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Scandinavia and the UK, elective hospital care is related to high waiting times. high waiting times for elective hospital care. The report also outlines some practices that could be considered in terms of regulating waiting times – i.e. setting maximum waiting time for specialist appointment or planned medical care [21–23].

Viberg et al. define waiting times as one of the most important health policy tools to monitor time to access health services and treatment. The authors focus on the importance of analyzing international waiting times. They define this process as vital for countries aiming to enhance their healthcare policies. Such a kind of analysis not only provides valuable insights for policymakers but also empowers patients to make well-informed decisions about their care options. In Europe, this is particularly important, as patients have the legal right to seek treatment in different countries if they face undue delays in accessing necessary medical services. By fostering transparency in waiting times, individuals can better navigate their healthcare choices and find timely interventions [24].

The results of the study raise some important issues regarding the need for establishing indicators for regulatory control of waiting times not only in hospital and medical services but also in terms of timely treatment.

The results are also subject to several limitations. While we consider the sample of patients to be representative of the population, as most hospitals in the country are included, it is possible that for certain patients, the patient’s profile is incomplete. This risk would materialize if the patient received treatment in a hospital not included in our sample. Due to the high data coverage, we assess this risk to be low and do not expect it to significantly affect the results. Furthermore, as we include all patients for whom we have available data of C34 diagnosis, we do not assess that there is selection bias which may skew the results. Moreover, we assess limitations associated with confounding variables to be inapplicable since our results are descriptive. However, the underlying causal variables of the reported trends may be unmeasured.

The current study highlights the need for improved lung cancer diagnosis to detect earlier stages of the disease. This requires a shift in political will to implement the National Anticancer Plan in line with the European Cancer Beating Plan and EC Recommendations. It’s essential to raise public awareness, launch educational campaigns, and involve general practitioners (GPs). Successful programs, such as Slovenia’s breast and colorectal cancer screenings and Croatia’s lung cancer initiative, can serve as models.

To address the gaps in waiting times for therapy initiation, the Ministry of Health, the Health Insurance Fund, and the National Council of Prices and Reimbursement have already taken several steps. These measures include regulatory changes to reduce administrative burdens and budget allocations aimed at minimizing financial strain due to overspending. Other possible measures include adopting good practices by setting deadlines in legislation for treatment initiation after diagnosis, as in Great Britain.

Inequalities in oncology centres’ geographical allocation and access to specialists should also be addressed based on legislative changes and financial resource allocation.

Further investigations are needed to address the potential of the measures and their cost-effectiveness and affordability.

Conclusions

Early diagnosis of lung cancer still poses a significant burden in Bulgaria. Our study shows that currently, most of the patients are diagnosed at advanced stages of the disease. The study also confirmed that dynamic patient registers are of great importance in performing real-world studies of treatment patterns and in the process of identifying gaps and inequities on a national level. Significant measures need to be implemented to address the deficiency of screening programs for lung cancer in Bulgaria and the uneven distribution of oncology centers.
