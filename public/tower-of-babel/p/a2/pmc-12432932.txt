Fear of cancer recurrence and associated factors in Chinese patients with colorectal cancer: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lijuan Xu, Wenli Dai, Li Chen, Lifeng Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is rising globally, with China bearing the highest burden. Fear of cancer recurrence (FCR) significantly impacts postoperative patients’ well-being but remains understudied in China. The roles of illness perception, emotional distress, and social support in FCR are unclear. This study aims to assess FCR levels and identify influencing factors to guide early clinical intervention.MethodsFrom November 2023 to May 2024, a cross-sectional survey was conducted among 314 postoperative CRC patients at a tertiary cancer hospital in Shanghai. Eligible adults (≥18 years) with confirmed CRC who had completed primary treatment were recruited via convenience sampling. Validated questionnaires were completed independently or with staff assistance. These included the Fear of Cancer Recurrence Inventory (FCRI), the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ), the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS), the 15-Item Social Constraints Scale (SCS-15), and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine associations among FCR, illness perception, social factors, and psychological symptoms. Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression was conducted to identify independent predictors of high FCR.ResultsOf the 330 questionnaires distributed, 314 were deemed valid, yielding a response rate of 95.2%. The sample included 193 males (61.5%) and 121 females (38.5%), with a mean age of 59.7±12.3 years. Participants showed a high level of FCR, with a mean FCRI score of 69.64±27.10 and severity dimension score of 13.91±5.996. Univariate analysis revealed significant differences in age, monthly income, and residence between high and low-to-moderate FCR groups (P<0.05). The high FCR group had greater illness perception (BIPQ), higher social constraints (SCS-15), elevated anxiety and depression (P<0.001), and lower social support (SSRS; P=0.01) compared to the low-to-moderate group. Correlation analysis showed that FCR was significantly positively correlated with illness perception (r=0.548, P<0.01), social constraints (SCS-15; r=0.275, P<0.01), and psychological distress, including anxiety (r=0.596, P<0.01) and depression (r=0.426, P<0.01), highlighting the interplay among cognitive, social, and emotional factors. Subsequent binary logistic regression analysis identified illness perception and anxiety symptoms as significant independent predictors of FCR in patients with CRC.ConclusionsFCR is common among postoperative CRC patients and is strongly linked to illness perception and anxiety. These findings underscore the need for routine psychological screening and tailored communication. Interventions addressing maladaptive beliefs and emotional distress, along with integrated psychoeducational and psychosocial support, may enhance resilience and improve quality of life in survivorship care.

Highlight box

Key findings

• A total of 58.9% of patients with postoperative colorectal cancer (CRC) in China experienced a high level of fear of cancer recurrence (FCR). FCR was significantly associated with negative illness perception, elevated anxiety and depression, and lower levels of social support.

What is known and what is new?

• FCR is a prevalent psychological concern among cancer survivors and is known to negatively impact quality of life and treatment compliance.

• This study provides novel data from a Chinese patient population, identifying key psychosocial correlates of FCR, including illness perception and emotional distress, in the context of CRC.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Routine screening for FCR and its psychological and cognitive risk factors should be integrated into postoperative care. Tailored interventions addressing illness beliefs, emotional regulation, and social support may help mitigate FCR and enhance the long-term well-being of patients with CRC.

Introduction

Cancer remains a significant global health issue, with increasing rates of both incidence and mortality. Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer globally by incidence and the second by mortality (1,2). CRC trends are particularly alarming in China, where the incidence of CRC has increased significantly, with an age-standardized incidence rate of 17.3 per 100,000 and a mortality rate of 7.8 per 100,000 (3). Notably, gender differences have emerged: the incidence and mortality rates have declined in females while they continue to rise in males. In 2022, approximately 4.82 million new cancer cases and 2.57 million cancer-related deaths were reported in China (4). The 5-year survival rate for patients with CRC in China is 56.9%, the lowest in the Asia-Pacific region, indicating a serious public health challenge (5). Despite improved survival rates among CRC patients following surgery, many continue to experience substantial psychological challenges, including anxiety about recurrence and depression, with fear of cancer recurrence (FCR) being one of the most prominent concerns (6).

Surgery, often supplemented by radiotherapy and chemotherapy, is the cornerstone of CRC treatment. However, the anatomical specificity of CRC lesions frequently results in physical trauma and altered appearance, compounding patients’ psychological distress. Advances in screening and treatment have improved early detection and survival rates, yet the persistent threat of recurrence and metastasis remains a significant challenge (7). Consequently, FCR—a pervasive psychological response characterized by heightened vigilance toward bodily symptoms—profoundly impacts patients’ quality of life (8).

Moderate FCR may enhance awareness of recurrence risks and promote healthier behaviors. However, excessive FCR fosters uncertainty, amplifies symptom perception, and can precipitate severe mental health conditions, such as somatic symptom disorder or posttraumatic stress disorder (6). Furthermore, elevated FCR disrupts social functioning, future planning, and overall well-being (9). Influencing factors for FCR span demographic, disease-related, and treatment-related domains, with a growing recognition of psychosocial contributors—such as illness perception, social support, and anxiety—being supported by cognitive behavioral models (10,11). Previous studies emphasize that modifiable cognitive and social factors outweigh static demographic variables in shaping FCR (12,13). To date, studies on FCR have primarily focused on demographic and clinical factors, with limited investigation of cognitive and social determinants among postoperative CRC patients in China (6,14). Additionally, prior research often employed small, non-representative samples, limiting generalizability (15,16). This study addresses these gaps by comprehensively assessing psychosocial factors influencing FCR in a larger Chinese cohort.

This study was thus conducted to examine the prevalence of FCR and its psychosocial correlates among Chinese patients with CRC, with the aim of informing targeted interventions and improving patient outcomes. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jgo.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jgo-2025-503/rc).

Methods

Study design and patient population

This cross-sectional study used convenience sampling to recruit CRC patients from two colorectal surgery wards at a specialized cancer hospital in Shanghai, China, between November 2023 and May 2024. The patients were enrolled from the Xuhui and Pudong campuses. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) age 18–80 years (with a cutoff at 80 years to exclude individuals with severe cognitive impairment); (II) histologically confirmed primary CRC based on WHO diagnostic criteria, with curative surgery performed 6 months to 5 years before survey participation to ensure sufficient postoperative experience and minimize recall bias; (III) ability to read and understand Chinese questionnaires and communicate verbally; and (IV) voluntarily participation and expressed willingness to provide informed consent.

Participants were grouped into three age categories: 18–39, 40–59, and ≥60 years. Although the cohort’s mean age exceeded 60 years, the younger age brackets were delineated according to epidemiological conventions to represent distinct developmental stages that may differentially affect psychological outcomes, particularly FCR.

The exclusion criteria were the following: (I) patients unaware of a CRC diagnosis; (II) presence of clinical, laboratory, or radiological signs suggestive of recurrence or metastasis or a previous history of recurrence or metastasis; (III) physical weakness prohibiting participation in the survey; (IV) cognitive impairment, mental illness, or a history of psychological treatment; (V) and concurrent enrollment in other interventional studies.

Procedure

We conducted a pilot study involving 18 patients with CRC to assess the reliability of the evaluation tools. Due to practical constraints in the clinical setting and specific patient characteristics, convenience sampling was employed to recruit eligible CRC patients from outpatient clinics at Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center. Two senior clinical nurses worked in collaboration with CRC specialists at Fudan University Cancer Hospital in Shanghai to screen and identify eligible participants. Participants, guided by the researchers, completed questionnaires that included clear instructions and an explanation of the study’s purpose.

After the participants completed the questionnaires, they were reviewed, and participants were requested to provide any necessary missing information. Questionnaires with multiple unanswered items, instances in which participants refused to supply additional details, or cases in which most answers were identical were deemed invalid.

Validated and reliable instruments were selected based on a thorough literature review and expert consultation. Detailed instructions were provided to ensure data quality. Data were entered using SPSS 26.0, with discrepancies resolved by cross-checking against original questionnaires.

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (No. 2404294-Exp4). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. All participants provided written informed consent and were made aware of their right to withdraw from the study at any time.

Measures

The tools used in this study comprised the Chinese versions of the Fear of Cancer Recurrence Inventory (FCRI), Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ), Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS), the 15-item Social Constraints Scale (SCS-15), Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), and a general demographic and disease statistics questionnaire.

FCRI

The FCRI, developed by Simard et al. (17) in 2009, evaluates the multidimensional FCR experienced by patients with cancer. This scale is grounded in the cognitive behavioral model of FCR. Totaling 42 items, it comprises seven dimensions: triggers, severity, coping strategies, psychological distress, functional impairment, insight, and reassurance-seeking. The FCRI uses a 5-point Likert scale (0–4), with total scores ranging from 0 to 168, with higher scores indicating a greater FCR. The severity dimension independently assesses FCR, with a maximum score of 36. A score of 13 or higher indicates a high level of fear, while a score of 13 or lower indicates a moderate to low level of fear. The scale demonstrates excellent reliability, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.95, and the coefficients for each dimension range from 0.71 to 0.94, which indicates strong validity and reliability.

BIPQ

The BIPQ was developed by Broadbent et al. (18) in 2006 and includes items on various aspects of illness perception, such as illness consequences, timeline, personal control, treatment control, symptom identity, concern, emotional response, illness understanding, and perceived causes. It consists of one dimension and nine items. Items 1–8 are scored on a 0–10 numerical scale, with items 3, 4, and 7 being reverse-scored. The total score ranges from 0 to 80, with higher scores indicating more negative perceptions of illness. The final item is an open-ended question asking respondents to list the three most important causes of their disease. The Chinese version of the BIPQ demonstrates good reliability and validity, with an internal consistency coefficient of 0.831, test-retest reliability of 0.931, and criterion-related validity of 0.640.

SSRS

The Chinese version of the SSRS was developed by Xiao in 1986 and has since been widely used in various studies within Chinese communities, demonstrating strong validity and reliability (19). The scale consists of 10 items that evaluate 3 dimensions of social support: objective support, subjective support, and support utilization. Objective support refers to tangible or actual assistance received in the past, while subjective support encompasses the emotional experience of feeling respected, supported, and understood within the community. Support utilization assesses the behaviors associated with seeking social support. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 12 to 66. Higher scores indicate a greater level of social support.

SCS-15

Lepore et al. developed the SCS-15 to assess the frequency with which patients encounter social constraints from spouses, family members, or friends in response to specific stressors over the previous month (20). The scale comprises 15 items, each rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating “not at all” and four indicating “most of the time”. The total score ranges from 15 to 60, with higher scores representing more significant levels of social constraints. The Chinese version of the scale has shown strong reliability, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.92.

HADS

The HADS, developed by Zigmond and Snaith, is commonly used to evaluate anxiety and depression in patients within medical settings (21). As a specialized psychological assessment tool, HADS measures symptoms of anxiety and depression and is widely applied to assess the mental health of patients across diverse medical conditions. It consists of 14 items, divided into 2 subscales, including anxiety (items 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, and 13) and depression (items 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14), each with seven items. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 to 3. The score ranges for each subscale are as follows: 0–7 (no symptoms), 8–10 (suspected symptoms), and 11–21 (definite symptoms). The scale demonstrates strong internal consistency and external validity, exhibiting high sensitivity and specificity. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the anxiety and depression subscales are 0.914 and 0.897, respectively, indicating excellent reliability and validity.

Statistical analysis

The minimum sample size required for linear multiple regression was calculated based on the requirements of multivariate statistical analysis, which recommend a sample size of 10 to 20 times the number of variables. Considering a 10% expected loss to follow-up and including 22 variables in the study, we enrolled 314 patients.

All statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were applied to describe the participants’ demographic and disease characteristics, as well as their scores on the FCRI, BIPQ, SSRS, SCS-15, and HADS scales. For inferential statistics, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to clarify the relationships between FCR, illness perception, social factors, and anxiety-depression in patients with malignant CRC. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at 0.05. To identify factors independently associated with FCR, variables significant in univariate analysis were included in a binary logistic regression model. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. All tests were two-tailed, with P<0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Of the 330 distributed questionnaires, 314 were deemed valid, resulting in a response rate of 95.2%. Participants included 193 males (61.5%) and 121 females (38.5%), with an average age of 59.72±12.32 years (8.6% aged 18–39 years, 33.4% aged 40–59 years, and 58.0% aged ≥60 years). Over half (53.8%) had a junior high school education or below, and 97.8% of the participants were diagnosed within the previous year. The majority (86.3%) underwent sphincter-preserving surgery, 42.7% had chronic diseases, and 10.8% reported a family history of CRC (Table 1).

FCR, illness perception, social factors, and psychological symptoms

The mean FCRI score was 69.64±27.10, with a severity subscale score of 13.91±5.996, indicating a high prevalence of FCR (58.9% above the cutoff). Coping strategies had an average score of 17.36±8.247, suggesting a moderate level of usage. In contrast, reassurance-seeking (5.44±2.852) and insight (4.33±2.979) were low, indicating limited external support-seeking and self-awareness. The average illness perception was 35.42±11.175 (moderate), with the highest scores being illness concern (7.41±2.926), followed by disease effects (5.31±2.97), emotional response (5.22 ±2.90), symptom recognition (4.55±2.73), disease understanding (3.87±2.93), course (3.73±2.82), and personal control (3.18±2.67), with treatment control receiving the lowest score (2.14±2.25). The mean social support score was 42.17±6.988 (moderate), with subjective support scoring highest (25.69±4.720), followed by objective support (9.03±2.56) and support utilization (7.45±1.97). The mean social constraint score was 24.97±7.897. According to the SSRS classification, 8.2% of participants reported low support, 58.0% reported moderate support, and 33.8% reported high support. Regarding psychological status, the mean anxiety and depression scores were 6.77±4.28 and 6.36±4.27, respectively. Among participants, 23.9% (n=75) exhibited probable clinically significant anxiety symptoms, while 18.8% (n=59) meeting criteria for definite anxiety. For depression, 24.2% (n=76) showed possible clinical symptoms, while 15.9% (n=50) had confirmed significant depressive symptoms.

Univariate analysis

Patient participants were categorized by FCRI severity (high: ≥13, n=185; moderate to low: <13, n=129). Univariate analysis revealed statistically significant differences in age, monthly per capita income, and place of residence between groups (P<0.05). Patients in the high FCR group showed significantly greater illness perception (BIPQ), higher levels of social constraints (SCS-15), and elevated anxiety and depression scores compared to those in the low-to-moderate FCR group (P<0.001). Moreover, social support (SSRS) scores were significantly lower in the high FCR group (P=0.01) (Table 2).

Multivariate analysis

Binary logistic regression analysis revealed that FCR in CRC patients was significantly associated with both illness perception and anxiety symptoms. Specifically, higher BIPQ scores, indicating more negative perceptions of illness, were linked to greater FCR (OR =1.073, P<0.001). Similarly, higher levels of anxiety were associated with increased FCR (OR =1.352, P<0.001), suggesting that cognitive and emotional factors play a key role in the development of FCR in this population (Table 4).

Discussion

Level and characteristics of FCR in patients with CRC

This study primarily enrolled older adult patients with limited education who were in the early stages of their disease, typically within 1 year of diagnosis. As these patients age, a decline in physical health can erode their confidence, making them particularly vulnerable to FCR (15). Additionally, their limited educational background can hinder their ability to fully comprehend their condition, leading to a lack of understanding regarding the disease and its treatment. This often results in feelings of panic and anxiety, further exacerbating FCR (6). Furthermore, patients who are retired, unemployed, or have a low income are more likely to experience anxiety related to the financial burden of treatment and disease management (6). Early-stage patients, in particular, are preoccupied with the potential for cancer recurrence and metastasis, which intensifies their fear due to the unpredictable nature of these outcomes. In our study, we found that the total FCR score among patients with CRC was 69.64±27.10, with the mean score for the severity dimension of the FCRI being 13.91±5.996. Notably, the reassurance seeking and insight dimensions scored lower, reflecting insufficient external support and a lack of self-awareness. These findings are consistent with those of Koch-Gallenkamp et al. (22), which highlight the significant role of social factors in FCR. An increased level of social constraints may exacerbate FCR, while lower levels may help alleviate it. Previous studies have shown that a strong support network can significantly improve psychological well-being, increase patients’ confidence in coping with the disease, and ultimately reduce levels of FCR (14,23).

Univariate analysis revealed statistically significant differences in age, monthly income, and place of residence between patients with high FCRI scores and those with low to moderate scores (P<0.05). Notably, older adults exhibited higher levels of FCR than younger patients, consistent with findings by Zhao et al. (16). This elevated FCR in the elderly may reflect greater psychological vulnerability, including heightened anxiety, self-blame, and concerns about becoming a burden to their families.

To enable more precise analysis of age-related variations in FCR, patients were stratified into three groups: 18–39, 40–59, and ≥60 years. Although the cohort’s mean age exceeded 60 years, this classification was based on established epidemiological standards to reflect distinct developmental stages—early adulthood, middle age, and late adulthood—that may shape illness perception, emotional regulation, and coping capacity. Notably, younger patients reported lower FCR levels, potentially reflecting greater psychological resilience, stronger motivation for recovery, and a heightened sense of family and work-related responsibility. These findings highlight the need for age-specific psychosocial interventions in FCR management.

From an economic perspective, patients with lower incomes tend to experience higher levels of FCR compared to those with higher incomes. This finding aligns with the research conducted by Cai et al. on patients with postoperative CRC (6). Financial status significantly influences mental well-being, likely because individuals with higher incomes have more significant economic resources and access to social support, which enables them to cope with financial challenges more effectively. Conversely, financial hardship often leads to adverse outcomes, including missed follow-up appointments and poor medication adherence. These behaviors not only increase the risk of anxiety and depression but also negatively affect patients’ lifestyles, social interactions, family roles, and relationships (24).

In addition to age and income, habitual residence was significantly associated with FCR levels. Urban patients reported higher FCR, potentially due to greater psychological stress and heightened health awareness driven by increased access to medical information and healthcare services. In contrast, rural patients showed lower FCR, possibly reflecting limited disease awareness or differing cultural attitudes toward illness and health. This urban-rural disparity underscores the need for geographically and demographically tailored psychosocial interventions.

Clinical characteristics such as tumor stage and surgical modality were not significantly associated with FCR levels in this study. Although biological recurrence risk varies markedly across disease stages, FCR scores remained relatively uniform. This disconnect may result from inadequate communication about personalized recurrence risk, leading to generalized anxiety regardless of prognosis. Additionally, many patients may lack a clear understanding of disease staging and its implications, particularly in the absence of structured survivorship education. These findings highlight the need to improve physician-patient communication by delivering accurate, comprehensible, and empathetic information about recurrence risk. Future interventions should integrate stage-specific psychoeducation into survivorship care, tailored to patients’ clinical profiles, psychological needs, and health literacy to mitigate unnecessary distress and foster adaptive coping.

Disease perception and characteristics in patients with CRC

The mean illness perception score for patients with CRC was 35.42±11.175, indicating a moderate level of disease awareness. This result aligns with findings from Li et al.’s study on illness perception in patients with cancer (15). Most participants in our study were in the early stages of CRC and within 1 year of their diagnosis. As newly diagnosed patients, concerns about cancer recurrence are prevalent. The physical toll of cancer and its treatments makes it challenging for patients to maintain an objective view of their condition. Instead, they often form a subjective, one-sided understanding of their illness, which can lead to negative perceptions that significantly affect their quality of life (25). Although patients can recognize the symptoms associated with CRC during diagnosis and treatment, their sense of control over their treatment remains relatively low. This may result from a lack of information or limited involvement in the decision-making process regarding their care, leading to diminished confidence in their ability to manage and overcome the disease.

Social factors and characteristics in patients with CRC

The study found that the mean social support score for patients with CRC was 42.17±6.988, indicating a moderate level of social support. The score for social constraints was 24.97±7.897, suggesting that participants experienced moderate levels of social constraints from family, friends, or spouses in response to specific stressors over the previous month. This may reflect the limited social resources available to them as they cope with the disease. However, the relatively higher score for subjective support indicates that despite a moderate overall level of social support, patients felt cared for and encouraged by their loved ones. Additionally, the variability in the social Constraints Score suggests that patients may perceive social restrictions differently depending on the situation. In certain circumstances, these perceived restrictions could suppress or alter the way they express their feelings or deal with distressing events (26). Excessive social constraints can hinder patients’ autonomy and decision-making abilities, potentially hurting their recovery. Research by Zhang et al. on patients with breast cancer and their spouses showed that lower levels of disease-related communication between couples were associated with higher levels of FCR in patients (27). Similarly, Chen et al. reported that encouraging mutual self-disclosure between couples effectively reduced the FCR in patients with breast cancer (28). These findings suggest that fostering a supportive social environment that encourages emotional expression and open communication can help alleviate FCR.

Anxiety and depression levels in patients with CRC

The mean anxiety score among patients with CRC was 6.77±4.279, while the mean depression score was 6.36±4.273. A total of 151 patients were identified via preliminary screening as potentially exhibiting significant symptoms of anxiety and depression, among whom 109 were subsequently confirmed to have clinically significant symptoms through diagnostic assessment. Several factors may contribute to these elevated levels of anxiety and depression, including the emotional challenges patients face while managing their condition, the uncertainty and fear associated with the diagnosis and treatment process, physical discomfort, concerns about the future, and a lack of adequate social support, all of which can intensify these negative emotions (29).

Correlation between FCR and illness perception in patients with CRC

There was significant positive correlation between FCR and illness perception in patients with CRC (r=0.548; P<0.01). This finding suggests that patients’ cognitive and emotional responses to their illness are crucial, with more negative perceptions and emotions associated with higher levels of FCR. These results align with Li et al.’s research, which also identified a positive correlation between FCR and illness perception in patients with CRC (15). Our univariate analysis demonstrated a significant difference in the total BIPQ score between the high-FCRI and moderate-to-low FCRI groups (P<0.001), indicating that patients who perceive the disease as a more significant threat tend to experience higher levels of FCR. This finding is consistent with Zhou et al.’s study, which found that negative beliefs about one’s illness are associated with elevated levels of FCR (30). Patients with an increased ability to recognize symptoms may become more sensitive to bodily changes, leading to heightened concerns regarding disease progression and recurrence, which results in a greater FCR. This creates a cycle in which anxiety and fear distort patients’ understanding of their condition, potentially causing misinterpretation of symptoms and exaggerated responses. Furthermore, a lack of control over the disease and insufficient confidence in treatment outcomes may heighten FCR. Therefore, healthcare professionals must address patients’ need for accurate information, promote open communication to correct misconceptions, reduce negative perceptions of the illness, and foster a more optimistic attitude toward treatment and daily life (31).

Correlation between FCR and social factors in patients with CRC

Univariate analysis revealed significant differences in social support (P=0.014) and social constraints (P<0.001), indicating their impact on FCR in patients with CRC. Patients who received better social support and demonstrated enhanced self-coping abilities experienced notably lower levels of FCR, consistent with previous research (23). This relationship can be explained by the central effect hypothesis of social support (32), which posits that regardless of stress levels, increased social support promotes better mental health and resilience, enabling individuals to manage psychological strain and uncertainty more effectively. Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed a positive relationship between social constraints and FCR, suggesting that patients with lower social support and more significant social constraints tend to experience higher levels of FCR. These findings align with the research by He et al. (33), which indicates that higher perceived social constraints is associated with a perception of greater illness severity. These restrictions can prevent individuals from engaging in critical social interactions where they express emotions and thoughts, thereby exacerbating their FCR (26).

Correlation between FCR and anxiety-depression levels in patients with CRC

Univariate analysis revealed significant differences in anxiety and depression levels between the high-FCRI group and the moderate-to-low-FCRI group (P<0.001). The high-FCRI group exhibited greater severe anxiety and depression symptoms, emphasizing the strong connection between mental health issues and FCR. This study, FCR was significantly positively correlated with both anxiety (r=0.596; P<0.01) and depression (r=0.426; P<0.01), identifying anxiety as a key driver of FCR. This finding is consistent with Wu et al.’s study, which demonstrated that higher levels of anxiety and depression are linked to a greater FCR (34). Anxiety in cancer patients can exacerbate feelings of uncertainty and fear regarding future health. Meanwhile, depressive states can heighten feelings of helplessness and pessimism, making patients more likely to worry about cancer recurrence. This creates a vicious cycle in which depression worsens FCR and FCR and, in turn, amplifies depressive symptoms. Quan et al. also noted a close relationship between anxiety, depression, and FCR, emphasizing that high levels of anxiety and depression can increase patients’ fears and worries, even occasionally leading to death-related thoughts, which further elevate FCR levels (35). Consequently, it is crucial to help patients maintain an optimistic outlook. Healthcare providers should be attentive to the impact of anxiety and depression on FCR. Early identification and proactive intervention for negative psychological states can significantly improve patients’ FCR levels, enhancing their overall well-being and mental resilience.

Although most participants reported relatively low levels of anxiety and depression, correlation analyses revealed significant associations with FCR, suggesting that even mild emotional distress can meaningfully contribute to cancer-related fears. Consistent with prior research, subclinical symptoms of anxiety and depression influence illness perception and undermine coping among cancer survivors (36,37). These findings underscore the importance of recognizing the psychological burden of mild affective symptoms. Early detection and targeted psychosocial interventions may help mitigate FCR and enhance patients’ overall psychological resilience.

Multivariate analysis of FCR in patients with CRC

Our study demonstrated that higher illness perception scores were significantly associated with greater FCR among CRC patients. Negative perceptions of illness may heighten symptom vigilance and anxiety, leading to a vicious cycle in which benign bodily sensations are misinterpreted as signs of recurrence. These findings are consistent with those reported by Wang et al., who also identified illness perception as a key contributor to FCR in elderly patients with early-stage esophageal cancer (38).

Anxiety was a significant predictor of FCR in our multivariate analysis (β=0.302, P<0.001; OR =1.352), suggesting that higher anxiety levels substantially increase the risk of FCR. This finding supports prior studies highlighting anxiety’s central role in the onset and persistence of FCR (37,39). Anxiety may heighten vigilance toward bodily sensations and trigger misinterpretations of benign symptoms as signs of recurrence, reinforcing fear over time. From a cognitive-behavioral perspective, anxious individuals are more likely to engage in threat overestimation and maladaptive coping. Moreover, recent evidence suggests that self-efficacy and social support may buffer the impact of anxiety on psychological distress in cancer survivors (40), offering potential targets for intervention.

Although multiple sociodemographic, clinical, and psychosocial variables were examined, only illness perception and anxiety emerged as independent predictors of FCR. These results emphasize the importance of addressing cognitive and emotional factors in psychological care. Routine assessment of illness perception and anxiety could facilitate early identification of high-risk individuals, while tailored cognitive-behavioral strategies may effectively reduce psychological burden and perceived recurrence threat.

Conclusions

FCR is common among patients with CRC and is significantly influenced by illness perception, anxiety, and a range of sociodemographic and psychosocial factors. This study identified illness perception and anxiety as independent predictors of FCR, underscoring their pivotal role in shaping patients’ psychological responses to cancer. Patients with more negative illness beliefs or elevated anxiety were more likely to report severe FCR, irrespective of clinical variables such as tumor stage or surgical approach.

FCR was also positively associated with depressive symptoms, social constraints, and financial stress, whereas greater social support was linked to reduced fear. Variations in FCR by age, income, and residential setting further highlight the need for individualized, context-sensitive psychosocial interventions. Notably, even subclinical levels of emotional distress were significantly correlated with FCR, emphasizing the importance of early psychological assessment and timely support.

These findings underscore the necessity of integrating cognitive and emotional screening into routine survivorship care for CRC patients. Targeted interventions focused on restructuring maladaptive illness beliefs, reducing anxiety, and strengthening social support networks may effectively mitigate FCR and enhance overall psychological well-being and quality of life.

Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to track the progression of FCR and assess the effectiveness of tailored psychosocial interventions. Multicenter studies are also needed to validate these findings and inform evidence-based survivorship care.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers a rigorous and multidimensional evaluation of FCR among patients with CRC by incorporating sociodemographic, clinical, psychological, and social variables. The targeted inclusion of older adults within one year of diagnosis enhances the clinical relevance of the findings, particularly for early survivorship planning. Importantly, the identification of illness perception and anxiety as independent predictors highlights the central role of cognitive and emotional factors in shaping FCR, providing actionable targets for psychosocial intervention. The use of both univariate and multivariate analyses strengthens the validity of the results, while alignment with prior research supports their generalizability. Additionally, the study emphasizes the value of early risk stratification and patient-centered communication, offering clear implications for improving psychological care and long-term quality of life in this population.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences; although anxiety and illness perception were significant predictors of FCR, the directionality of these associations remains uncertain. Second, the sample was drawn from a single center and primarily included older patients with early-stage CRC, which may reduce the generalizability of the findings to broader or more diverse populations. Future research should include multicenter studies with larger and more diverse populations to enhance the generalizability and robustness of the results. Third, important variables such as coping strategies, cultural beliefs, and family dynamics were not assessed and may have influenced FCR outcomes. Fourth, the small size of certain subgroups (e.g., high-income individuals, religious minorities) may have limited statistical power to detect subtle effects. Fifth, reliance on self-reported instruments (e.g., BIPQ, SSRS, SCS-15) may introduce recall or social desirability bias. Lastly, the lack of significant associations for variables such as depression and social support in the multivariate model may reflect measurement limitations, sample heterogeneity, or residual confounding, highlighting the need for future longitudinal and multicenter studies.
