An exploration of actigraphy in the context of ketamine and treatment‐resistant depression
Authors: Claire Punturieri, Wallace C Duncan, Dede Greenstein, Gavi Shandler, Carlos A Zarate, Jennifer W Evans
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study explored the potential of non‐parametric and complexity analysis metrics to detect changes in activity post‐ketamine and their association with depressive symptomatology.MethodsIndividuals with treatment‐resistant depression (TRD: n = 27, 16F, 35.9 ± 10.8 years) and healthy volunteers (HVs: n = 9, 4F, 36.4 ± 9.59 years) had their activity monitored during an inpatient, double‐blind, crossover study where they received an infusion of ketamine or saline placebo. All participants were 18–65 years old, medication‐free, and had a MADRS score ≥20. Non‐parametric metrics averaged over each study day, metrics derived from complexity analysis, and traditionally calculated non‐parametric metrics averaged over two weeks were calculated from the actigraphy time series. A separate analysis was conducted for a subsample (n = 17) to assess the utility of these metrics in a hospital setting.ResultsIn HVs, lower intradaily variability was observed within daily rest/activity patterns post‐ketamine versus post‐placebo (F = 5.16(1,15), p = 0.04). No other significant effects of drug or drug‐by‐time or correlations between depressive symptomatology and activity were detected.ConclusionsWeak associations between non‐parametric variables and ketamine were found but were not consistent across actigraphy measures.Clinical Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT00088699.Keywords: actigraphy, circadian, ketamine, recurrence quantification analysis, treatment‐resistant depression

INTRODUCTION

Actigraphy is a non‐invasive method of measuring physical activity that has been extensively used for behavioral monitoring in mood disorders (Dogan et al., 2017; Fuster‐Garcia et al., 2015; Germain & Kupfer, 2008; Minaeva et al., 2020; Monteleone & Maj, 2008; Reinertsen & Clifford, 2018; Salgado‐Delgado et al., 2011). Actigraphic metrics can summarize rest‐activity cycles either continuously or during specified intervals (e.g., daytime vs. night‐time, sleep vs. wake, etc.). Thus, it is well suited for investigating circadian disturbances or examining specific behaviors (e.g., daytime naps, night‐time arousals, exercise, etc.) as well as the impact of pharmacologic treatments on daytime activity and sleep interruptions (Burton et al., 2013; Luik et al., 2015; McCall, 2015; Medina et al., 2014; Reichert et al., 2015; Tazawa et al., 2019). In major depressive disorder (MDD), pharmacological investigation with actigraphy has generally centered on traditional antidepressant options (e.g., selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)) (Burton et al., 2013). However, recent work has also used actigraphy to examine the impact of rapid‐acting antidepressants like the glutamatergic modulator ketamine on circadian functioning. Ketamine, which is known to produce much more rapid antidepressant effects than SSRIs (Kishimoto et al., 2016), also appears to improve sleep quality in individuals with MDD (Duncan et al., 2017; Duncan, Sarasso, et al., 2013; Duncan, Selter, Brutsche, Sarasso, & Zarate, 2013). In this context, several recent studies have suggested that the circadian rhythm biology changes induced by ketamine may play a pivotal role in understanding its antidepressant effects (Alitalo et al., 2021; Kohtala et al., 2021).

Rating scales like the Montgomery‐Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS; (Montgomery & Åsberg, 1979)) are considered the gold standard for measuring treatment response in ketamine efficacy trials. However, these scales are designed to evaluate symptoms at discrete intervals (e.g., over 24 h to about a week). In contrast, actigraphy data are continuous over time, are collected passively, reduce patient burden (Hills et al., 2014; Jacobson et al., 2019), and have been found to correlate with the severity of depressive symptoms (Spulber et al., 2022). Actigraphy may thus reveal unique features of treatment response to rapid‐acting antidepressants that have not yet been achieved with conventional approaches. This is especially important given that self‐tracking health and activity devices and applications have been commercially available for decades and provide a wealth of day‐to‐day data that could be used to inform clinical decision‐making (Areàn et al., 2016). Harnessing actigraphy to track treatment response is particularly valuable because translating these widely accessible and financially viable tools into the community could have an enormous impact on public health.

Nevertheless, no consensus presently exists regarding the best summary report metrics for measuring treatment response in MDD. Many actigraphy studies calculate non‐parametric values averaged over weeks or months (Burton et al., 2013). Calculation of non‐parametric actigraphy values does not make assumptions regarding the shape of the activity time series, in contrast to other analyses that characterize rest‐activity rhythms (e.g., cosinor analysis), which fit a cosine function to the data (Gonçalves et al., 2014). However, examining non‐parametric measures calculated over a long period of time may still fail to capture rapid treatment response. In contrast, examining activity over a shorter timescale (i.e., over days) might allow researchers to capture the acute impact of ketamine on activity.

Recently, Recurrence Quantification Analysis (RQA) has been applied to actigraphy data in an attempt to differentiate between healthy volunteers (HVs) and individuals with MDD (George et al., 2021). A subset of complexity analysis, RQA quantifies various aspects of the underlying dynamics of time series data such as transitions and repeated patterns in the signal (Marwan et al., 2018), with the trade‐off being poorer temporal resolution. RQA has previously been used to study psychopathology in a variety of physiological signals, including heart rate variability (Gregório et al., 2020) and actigraphy (Martín‐Martínez et al., 2012). In the context of antidepressant treatment response, RQA could potentially be used to assess shifts in rest/activity patterns that coincide with change in symptoms or with rest/activity patterns that rating scales fail to capture, thus providing insight that differs from that provided by non‐parametric variables.

The present study collected actigraphy data from HVs and individuals with treatment‐resistant depression (TRD) who were participating in an inpatient, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, crossover study of ketamine. Because of the limited research into which actigraphic metrics are most useful in the context of specific conditions, the primary goal of this study was to explore the relationship between drug condition and rest/activity patterns quantified in different ways. Non‐parametric metrics averaged over each study day (relative amplitude (RAp), most active 10‐h period (M10p), least active five‐hour period (L5p)) were examined with the goal of characterizing associations between actigraphy metrics and MADRS total score. Another major aim was to replicate and extend RQA results drawn from an analysis by George and colleagues (George et al., 2021) by analyzing additional non‐parametric values (intradaily variability (IV) and interdaily stability (IS)) alongside variables extracted from recurrence plots calculated from actigraphy time series (laminarity (LAM), determinism (DET), and their ratios), in association with MADRS scores. It was hypothesized that activity levels and symptomatology would be associated in individuals with TRD.

METHOD

Participants

Data were drawn from an inpatient, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, crossover study of the effects of ketamine in TRD (NCT00088699). Cohort and behavioral details have previously been published (Nugent et al., 2019). Thirty‐six participants (HV: n = 9, 4 female, 36.4 ± 9.59 years old; TRD: n = 27, 16 female, 35.9 ± 10.8 years old; Table 1) from the larger trial completed the actigraphy substudy (i.e., opted to wear their watches for the 28‐day duration of the study). Because some individuals left the unit (i.e., went “out on pass”) in between study procedures, separate analyses were performed for the subset of TRD participants who were inpatients throughout the entire duration of the study (n = 17; 9 female; 36.9 ± 12.0 years; see Supporting Information S1).

All TRD participants were between 18 and 65 years old, had not responded to at least one previous medication trial, were currently experiencing a major depressive episode lasting at least four weeks, and had scored ≥20 on the MADRS at the time of screening, per inclusion criteria. The diagnosis of MDD was confirmed via the Structured Clinical Interview for Axis I DSM‐IV‐TR Disorders–Patient Edition (SCID‐IV; (First et al., 2002)). The Antidepressant Treatment History Form (Sackeim, 2001) was used to confirm failure to respond to a minimum of one previous antidepressant trial. Participants were free from psychotropic medications for at least 2 weeks prior to randomization (3 weeks for aripiprazole, 5 weeks for fluoxetine).

Healthy volunteers were between 18 and 65 years old, with no Axis I disorder and no family history of Axis I disorders in first‐degree relatives. Healthy volunteers were also free of any medications that affect neuronal function or cerebral blood flow/metabolism. Participants in both groups were in good physical health, as determined by medical history, physical exam, electrocardiogram, chest x‐ray, blood labs, urinalysis, and toxicology.

This study was conducted at the NIMH Clinical Research Center Mood Disorders Research Unit in Bethesda, MD. All research procedures were approved by the Combined Neuroscience Institutional Review Board of the National Institutes of Health. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study participation. A clinical research advocate assigned to each participant from the NIMH Human Subjects Protection Unit oversaw the consenting process and monitored the participant over the course of the study.

Data collection and processing

Infusions of 0.5 mg/kg ketamine and placebo were administered to participants over a 40‐min period, two weeks apart, in a double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, crossover design (see Figure 1). The order of infusions was randomized. The MADRS was used to assess treatment response and, in order to examine both the acute and sustained effects of ketamine, was administered at 60 min prior to each infusion (“baseline”), and at one, two, and 11 days post‐infusion.

Schematic demonstrating participant randomization and data collection procedures. Non‐parametric and recurrence plot variables were extracted from actigraphy data collected throughout the study.

Actigraphy data were collected throughout the duration of the study in one‐minute epochs. All participants were fitted with an Actiwatch (AW64; Philips, Amsterdam, the Netherlands) on their non‐dominant wrist at baseline. To account for variations in watch placement times across participants, analyses presented here began at midnight on Day −1 (approximately 33 h prior to the infusion). Participants used a handwritten log to track removal of the watch (e.g., for showering or research procedures such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans) and time spent off the inpatient unit.

Non‐parametric circadian rhythm variables per period

Before calculating non‐parametric variables, data masking was used to account for periods of inactivity, research procedures, and spurious bursts of activity. Periods of inactivity and research procedures were manually identified and verified using calendar and watch diary entries when possible. Data were thresholded using a slow‐walking count (3003) (Freedson et al., 1998) to create an automatic mask that was then visually inspected; this minimized data spikes from events irrelevant to assessment, such as participants dropping the watch. Data were split into one‐day periods, beginning at 1 day prior to infusion (Day −1) through 12 days post‐infusion, for a total of 14 days per arm of the trial and 28 days total. Three non‐parametric variables were calculated per one‐day period: the most active 10‐h period (M10p), the least active five‐hour period (L5p), and the relative amplitude (RAp). The M10p measure was the average of the activity during the most active 10 consecutive hours per one‐day period and could be influenced by daytime napping (Witting et al., 1990). The L5p measure was the one‐day period average of the activity during the least active five consecutive hours and could be influenced by movement during sleep or night‐time arousal (Witting et al., 1990). The RAp measure was derived from the most active 10‐h period and the least active five‐hour period (RAp = M10p – L5p/M10p + L5p), with higher values indicating higher amplitude (Van Someren et al., 1999). All metrics were calculated using pyActigraphy (Hammad et al., 2021).

Recurrence quantification analysis

RQA can be used to uncover subtle repeated patterns of variables (linear or nonlinear) within signals where the data are known to be noisy, irregular, or highly dimensional (Riley & Turvey, 2002). In actigraphy, RQA can be used to assess the duration and length of activity within time series data. Briefly, RQA quantifies what is visually shown in a recurrence plot. A recurrence plot depicts repetitions in data in a binary manner, with “1” indicating a repetition and “0” indicating novelty (in this case, a “1” is a dark point on the plot). If the observed time series data are non‐random, the plot will have clear patterns of diagonal and horizontal lines. An example recurrence plot for one HV and one TRD participant is shown in Figure 2. After recurrence plots were calculated for each participant, complexity metrics were extracted, and their means were examined across drug conditions.

An example of normalized data (top) and corresponding recurrence plot (RP; bottom) for a healthy volunteer (HV) and an individual with treatment‐resistant depression (TRD). Blue = ketamine arm, green = placebo arm. The infusion for each arm took place at the 33‐h mark (approximately 9:00am).

Similar analytic procedures to those laid out by George and colleagues (George et al., 2021) were used. Raw activity data were first averaged over 10‐min bins and then rank transformed to normalize the data across participants and account for watch‐removal periods. RQA was then conducted for each arm of the study for each participant using a recurrence threshold of about 0.05 (i.e., a fixed recurrence rate or density of 5%). Complexity measures, derived from the generated recurrence plots, were compared within participants in each arm of the study over 14 days (i.e., for each drug condition). The metrics calculated were as follows: laminarity (LAM), determinism (DET), and the ratio of laminarity to determinism (LAM/DET). In the recurrence plot itself, LAM is the proportion of recurrent points that form vertical lines (number of points that form vertical lines/total number of recurrent points) and DET is the proportion of recurrent points that form diagonal lines (number of points that form diagonal lines/total number of recurrent points) (Zbilut and Webber, 1994). Thus, the two are analogous but capture different dimensions of the data. In the context of the actigraphy time series, higher levels of LAM represent a higher number of long‐lasting activities whereas higher levels of DET indicate more deterministic patterns in the time series (in other words, less varied and more consistent circadian rhythms). For instance, a person who regularly exercises for an hour at the same time every day and has a generally consistent schedule overall will theoretically have both higher LAM and DET values than a person who does not have regular periods of movement and who has a less consistent (more varied) schedule. Their ratio can be used to understand the stability of the time series (in other words, whether the pattern of the time series remains similar from day to day). Significant clusters for these metrics were restricted to clusters of >2 recurrence points. All metrics were calculated using the free standalone software TOCSY (Marwan et al., 2007; Thiel et al., 2004) (https://tocsy.pik‐potsdam.de/pyunicorn.php).

Traditionally calculated non‐parametric circadian rhythm metrics

To provide comparator metrics against which to measure per period and complexity analyses, two standard circadian rhythm actigraphy metrics were computed: IV and IS (Witting et al., 1990). IV represents fragmentation of the circadian rhythm, with higher values indicating a more fragmented rhythm (which could reflect frequent daytime naps or interrupted sleep). IS quantifies the consistency of rest/activity patterns day‐to‐day. Here, higher values represented greater circadian rhythm stability or how well an individual's rest‐activity rhythm tracked the 24‐h light‐dark cycle from one day to the next. Data were averaged over 14‐day periods following masking. These measures were also calculated using pyActigraphy (Hammad et al., 2021).

Statistical analysis

For measures calculated three times per arm (M10p, L5p, RAp), mixed effect models were used with main effects of time (Day 1, Day 2, Day 11) and drug (ketamine, placebo), and a drug‐by‐time interaction to test our hypotheses. The models also included sex, age, infusion number (i.e., first or second), period‐specific baseline, and average baseline (to avoid cross‐level bias (Kenward & Roger, 2010)) as covariates, and a random intercept per person. Because an unstructured covariance matrix resulted in convergence problems, a first order autoregressive covariance structure was used. A mixed model was used for measures averaged over the two‐week post‐infusion period (LAM, DET, LAM/DET, IV, IS), with a main effect of drug to test our hypothesis, a random intercept per person, and infusion number, sex, and age as covariates. All models were performed for TRD and HV groups separately, as some evidence suggests that participants with TRD may respond differently to ketamine than HVs (Nugent et al., 2019) and because the study did not have the power to detect higher order interactions with diagnostic group (e.g., drug‐by‐time‐by‐diagnostic group). Residual plots (residual vs. fitted, qq‐plots) were used to screen for assumption violations and, in several instances of possible heterogeneity of variance, sandwich estimators were used for the variance‐covariance matrix. However, in these instances, the pattern of results was not meaningfully altered; for the sake of simplicity, these results are not reported. Alpha was set at 0.05 given the exploratory nature of this study. An identical set of mixed model analyses was performed for the subsample of TRD participants who remained on the unit for the duration of the study.

Pearson correlation coefficient calculations were carried out to estimate per time point/drug relationships between MADRS total score and actigraphy metrics in the TRD sample. SAS 9.4 was used to perform all mixed model analyses (SAS Institute Inc.).

RESULTS

Relation between drug condition and rest/activity patterns

In HVs, IV was reduced post‐ketamine (model estimated mean = 0.38 (SE = 0.04)) versus post‐placebo (model estimated mean = 0.43 (SE = 0.04); (F = 31.91(1,7), p = 0.0008); no other HV ketamine‐placebo differences were observed (see Table 2 for per period measures and Table 3 for aggregate measures, and Figures 3 and 4). No significant associations were observed between drug condition and activity levels in this inpatient subsample with either grouping of metrics (see Table S2A, S2B, and Figures S1 and S2). Results from the mixed effects models did not support any main effects of drug or drug‐by‐time interactions in the overall or inpatient TRD sample.

Association between actigraphy metrics and MADRS scores

The pattern of TRD correlations between MADRS total scores and activity metrics at baseline, Day 1, Day 2, and Day 11 in the ketamine and placebo conditions did not support an overall relationship between depression symptomatology and activity measures. Specifically, no significant correlations were observed between MADRS and by‐day metrics (L5p, M10p, RAp). However, scatterplots revealed a restricted range in MADRS scores at baseline and post‐placebo conditions, which may have downwardly biased correlation coefficient estimates (Supporting Figure S3).

DISCUSSION

The primary goal of this study was to explore the relationship between drug condition and rest‐activity patterns quantified using a variety of analytic techniques. A secondary aim was to replicate and extend RQA results previously reported by George and colleagues (George et al., 2021) by analyzing these values in association with depressive symptom scores over the course of treatment. Results indicated reduced IV values in HVs following ketamine administration. No significant relationship was noted between any actigraphy measures and depressive symptomatology ratings.

Ketamine has been shown to induce mood symptoms in HVs (Nugent et al., 2019), raising the question of whether these mood symptoms would potentially lead to rest/activity patterns similar to those seen in depressed patients. Interestingly, this study found reduced IV (less varied or more consistent rest/activity patterns day‐to‐day) following ketamine administration in HVs, though further investigation is needed to see whether this finding paralleled increases in negative mood symptoms (Nugent et al., 2019). No other associations were observed, perhaps because reported depressive symptoms were more acute and more likely to be observed on the day of the infusion itself in HVs (Nugent et al., 2019). Given that infusion also occurs during a heavy research procedure day, it is possible that otherwise observable activity differences were obscured by a combination of the watch needing to be removed for research procedures (e.g., MRIs), the sedentary nature of certain procedures (e.g., blood draws), and compliance with study procedures that limited our sample size.

Previous studies found that individuals with depression exhibit decreased daytime activity and increased sleep disturbances compared to HVs and that these may resolve with treatment (Tazawa et al., 2019). George et al. (2021) observed that their MDD group had significantly lower levels of complexity with respect to duration of recurrent activity and diversity of duration relative to their non‐depressed participants, but significance was lost following a Bonferroni correction (George et al., 2021). In this study, no other associations between actigraphy metrics (both non‐parametric and complexity) and ketamine were observed, nor any associations with MADRS scores. However, interpreting our results requires some consideration. First, both our and George and colleagues' findings were limited by small sample sizes, so group comparisons may have been lost following several testing corrections (George et al., 2021). Second, while the present study had 4 weeks of continuous actigraphy data available for analysis—as in previous research (George et al., 2021)—our data were divided by treatment arm (i.e., ketamine or placebo) in order to more accurately assess treatment effects. Additionally, not all TRD patients in our sample responded to ketamine (as defined by a 50% reduction in MADRS score). Thus, it is possible that—in addition to increased sample size—longer periods of continuous actigraphy data and a sample with more a homogeneous response to treatment could increase the power needed to calculate outcomes on an individual level. Third, our findings could also be influenced by differences in mobility on an inpatient unit compared to a naturalistic setting, as underscored by the finding observed only in inpatients; studying this technique in a longer‐term, outpatient setting may produce different results.

Our study has several limitations. First, actigraphy data were collected in an inpatient setting without naturalistic baseline data as a comparator. These metrics would benefit from incorporating naturalistic baseline data to account for circadian rhythm changes artificially induced by the hospital environment. In addition to collecting extended baseline data prior to participants' arrival on the inpatient unit, future work would also benefit from assessing circadian rhythms following participants' departures. This would supplement a growing literature suggesting that actigraphy has potential as a non‐invasive clinical tool and help investigators understand long‐term response to ketamine, especially following repeated infusions. Second, TRD participants in this study were required to have a MADRS score greater than 20, which limited the dynamic range of depressive scores at baseline and may have further obscured our ability to understand the relationship between depressive symptomatology and actigraphy. Finally, future efforts within inpatient settings should focus on comparing daytime‐centric and night‐time‐centric metrics to better understand the impact of the hospital environment on actigraphy metrics. Because improved sleep quality may predict treatment response to ketamine in individuals with TRD (Duncan, Sarasso, et al., 2013; Duncan & Zarate, 2013), it may be a worthwhile avenue of future research. Such night‐time metrics may be particularly useful when daytime activity is confounded by the research environment, such as on an inpatient unit in a research hospital. Continuing to compare the viability of actigraphy metrics across the rest‐activity cycle will help solidify which metrics are most appropriate under certain conditions.

Despite these limitations, this exploratory study lays important groundwork across several domains. First, the study expanded previous research using complexity analysis applied to the actigraphy time series by using it to examine rapid‐acting antidepressant treatment effects (George et al., 2021). It also included an alternative to several traditional non‐parametric circadian rhythm variables (L5p, M10p, RAp) by calculating these variables on a 24‐h basis, thereby extending this novel approach into the depression literature. Because no guidelines exist regarding which analyses or metrics should be conducted with actigraphy data acquired from different populations under a variety of conditions (Burton et al., 2013), robust exploratory work is imperative in order to develop such recommendations. Little research using actigraphy data has been conducted to understand treatment effects, and multiple analytic techniques should be used to determine if and how rapid‐acting therapeutic response can best be quantified using these metrics. The use of complexity measures in addition to traditional quantifiers is a major strength of the present study, as both capture distinct aspects of the time series, and these findings also provide a foundation for examining these novel methods within an inpatient setting. Finally, this paper explores methodological considerations crucial to translating these potential markers of remittance and relapse from the lab into wearables already in common use in the general population.

CONCLUSIONS

This study used non‐parametric actigraphy metrics averaged over study day and complexity metrics to explore the associations between ketamine and physical activity in an inpatient sample of HVs and individuals with TRD and whether these associations were related to depressive symptoms. The exploratory analyses suggested that HVs have more consolidated rest/activity patterns following ketamine administration. As research continues to explore the role of physical activity in understanding treatment for depression, the development of actigraphy metrics to track clinical course in real‐world settings will be key.
