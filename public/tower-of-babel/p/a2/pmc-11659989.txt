Ready-To-Use Microwave Sensor Modified by Antibody-AuNPs Nanoconjugate for Highly Sensitive and Selective Detection of the SARS-CoV-2 Virus
Authors: Jin Wang, Weijia Cui, Carolyn L Ren, Emmanuel A Ho
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe COVID-19 outbreak has led to notable developments in point-of-care (POC) diagnostic devices, as they can be valuable resources in identifying and managing the spread of the pandemic. Currently, the majority of techniques demand advanced laboratory equipment and professionals to execute precise, efficient, accurate, and sensitive testing. In this work, we report a new method to significantly enhance the sensitivity of microwave sensing of the SARS-CoV-2 virus by functionalizing the sensor surface using anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike antibody-gold nanoparticle (AuNPs) conjugates. AuNPs were surface-functionalized with the antispike antibody by EDC/NHS chemistry via PEG as a linker to form the conjugate (Ab-PEG-AuNPs). The Ab-PEG-AuNPs nanoconjugate was then coated onto the sensor through APTES and used for selectively capturing the spike protein on the SARS-CoV-2 virus. The sensing performance of the modified sensor was demonstrated via both experimental measurements and numerical simulations. Our sensor exhibited high sensitivity, achieving a limit of detection of 1,000 copies/mL of the SARS-CoV-2 virus within a 60 min time frame while requiring a minimal sample volume of 100 μL. The sensor exhibits outstanding specificity in distinguishing SARS-CoV-2 from other viruses, including influenza A and B, SARS-CoV-1, and MERS-CoV. Overall, this sensor provides a sensitive and label-free alternative for COVID-19 POC diagnosis.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2, gold nanoparticles, nanoconjugate, antibody, microwave sensor

Introduction

Since the declaration of the COVID-19 outbreak in January 2020, detection of the SARS-CoV-2 virus has become crucial for patient management and global pandemic response,1 leading to the rigorous development of detection techniques in different test settings. Clinical laboratories worldwide have conducted more than 3 billion COVID-19 molecular tests, most commonly utilizing the real-time reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR).2 RT-PCR tests are the gold standard and were mandatory during the pandemic to reduce the risk of importing COVID-19 cases from international travel. However, RT-PCR tests possessed major drawbacks including high-cost, long wait times, high potential for false-negatives, and the need for trained personnel and specialized equipment for quick pandemic control responses.3 Since the reopening of schools, businesses, workplaces, and social events, rapid and precise COVID-19 detection, which can be conducted in point-of-care (POC) settings, have extended to the community level and urgent care facilities. Commercial antigen-based rapid diagnostic tests (Ag-RDTs) offer inexpensive and rapid detection in lab-independent settings but often exhibit insufficient sensitivity and specificity,4 typically only detecting 104–106 genome copies of virus per mL in clinical specimens.5 The sensitivity of a diagnostic test is important to accurately and rapidly diagnose a disease, especially during a pandemic. In this regard, advanced POC testing strategies are being explored to achieve a more rapid, accurate, and straightforward COVID-19 testing.

Biosensors serve as ideal POC platforms capable of offering label-free and rapid detection of various analytes, including biomolecules, pathogens, and chemical compounds.6−9 Microwave sensors, characterized by their low cost, high accuracy, and compact design, have shown significant advantages for pathogen detection. They enable direct detection of pathogens in complex samples (cell and bacteria medium) with minimal risk of exposure using reagent-free and extraction-free procedures.10 For example, Zhang et al. developed a microwave sensor for differentiating colorectal cancer cells based on their differences in permittivity and observed a global permittivity increase correlating with the malignant grade of the cells.11 The growth of pathogenic bacteria Escherichia coli (E. coli) has also been monitored with microwave sensing based on the variations of dielectric property of the bacteria surrounding the sensor. Piekarz et al. developed a microwave sensor for the detection of various concentrations of E. coli0111 with the detection limit at 103 CFU/mL.12 Various microwave sensing methods have also been adopted to measure viruses such as aseroviruses,13dengue virus,14 adeno-associated virus (AAV),15 and SARS-CoV-2 virus.16

In a previous study, we developed a microwave sensor integrated into a microfluidics device for the rapid detection of SARS-CoV-2 antigen and virus16 without the need for reagents and extraction procedures. In this method, antibodies were directly coated on the microwave sensor surface to capture the virus present in the sensing region. While our sensor achieved high selectivity and sensitivity in detecting 4000 genome copies per mL of the SARS-CoV-2 virus, further enhancement of sensitivity is necessary for widespread use. This can be achieved through sensor design improvements and robust fabrication processes; however, a more effective strategy likely involves increasing the binding efficiency between the antibodies and virus through antibody modifications.

Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) are among the most popular nanomaterials used to enhance the binding efficiency between antibodies and antigens in biosensing applications, thanks to their unique electronic, magnetic, and optical properties arising from their distinct size and structural composition.17,18 First of all, the high surface-to-volume ratio of AuNPs provides a large interface for immobilizing specific chemical or biological molecules, enabling selective recognition and signal amplification of biological analytes such as metal ions, toxins, biomolecules, and microorganisms. Second, AuNPs are highly polarizable particles due to their small size and large surface area,19 which allows them to interact strongly with electrical fields and induce a local increase in the electrical field. When introduced into a medium, AuNPs can alter its permittivity through interactions with the electrical field generated by the nanoparticles.20,21 This unique ability makes AuNPs valuable for a variety of applications, including microwave sensing and imaging.

This study aims to develop a ready-to-use microwave sensor coated with AuNPs-modified antibodies that enables direct and more sensitive detection of the SARS-CoV-2 virus without the need for virus extraction. To achieve this, we utilized AuNPs that were surface-functionalized with anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike antibodies via a poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) linker to form Ab-PEG-AuNPs nanoconjugates using N-(3-(dimethylamino)propyl)-N-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) chemistry. Subsequently, the Ab-PEG-AuNPs conjugates were modified onto the sensing region of the microwave sensor chip through APTES and used to selectively capture the spike protein in the SARS-CoV-2 virus. Experimental results demonstrated that our modified sensor successfully recognized the SARS-CoV-2 virus within the range of 50,000 copies/mL and achieved a limit of detection (LOD) of 1,000 copies/ml within 60 min with an optimized amount of nanoconjugate modification. Moreover, the sensor exhibited excellent specificity in recognizing only the SARS-CoV-2 virus when compared to Influenza A and B and SARS-CoV-1 and MERS-CoV viruses.

Experimental Section

Materials

Tetrachloroauric acid trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O), sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate (Na3Ct·2H2O), carboxylpoly(ethylene glycol) thiol (HS- PEG12- COOH), 4-Morpholineethanesulfonic acid (MES), EDC, Sulfo-NHS, cupric sulfate (CuSO4), boric acid (MH3BO3), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), ferric chloride (FeCl3), 3-Triethoxysilylpropylamine (APTES), acetone, and rabbit anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike protein antibody were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. mPEG-SH was purchased from Abbexa. The NanoOrange Protein quantitation kit was obtained from Invitrogen. The SARS-CoV-2 virus (isolate: USAWA1/2020, Cat: NATSARS (COV2)-ST), SARS-CoV-1 (cat. NATSARS-ST), MERS-CoV (cat. NATMERS-ST), and Influenza A and B (cat. NATFLUA/B) were acquired from ZeptoMetrix.

Synthesis of Gold Nanoparticles (AuNPs)

AuNPs were synthesized using the citrate reduction of HAuCl4·3H2O reported by Turkevich et al.22 with slight modification. Briefly, after a 50 mL 0.125 mM solution of HAuCl4·3H2O reached the boiling point, 919 μL of a 34.0 mM (1.0 wt %) NaCt solution was freshly prepared and rapidly added to the HAuCl4 solution under vigorous stirring. The solution quickly changed color from yellow to dark purple to red. The solution was then kept at 100 °C and stirred for another 20 min to complete the reaction.

Preparation of PEG-Coated AuNPs (PEG-AuNPs)

Bare AuNPs were cooled to room temperature and modified with HS-PEG12-COOH. For this purpose, 2 mg/mL HS-PEG12-COOH was incubated with bare AuNPs by 1:1 volume ratio for 15 min at room temperature under gentle stirring. Following incubation, the PEG-coated AuNPs were centrifuged at 9000g for 10 min to remove excess PEG. PEG-AuNPs were then resuspended in 0.1 M MES solution (pH 3.9).

Preparation of Anti-SARS-CoV-2 Spike Protein Antibody Functionalized PEG-AuNPs (Ab-PEG-AuNPs) Nanoconjugates

Antibody conjugation was conducted according to the method reported by Pramanik et al.23 with modifications. The rabbit anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike protein antibody was conjugated to PEG-AuNPs by EDC/NHS chemistry. Briefly, PEG-AuNPs were activated by adding 33.3 mmol/L EDC and 416.7 mmol/L NHS in 0.1 M MES buffer (pH 3.9) and incubated at 4 °C under rotation for 30 min. The suspension containing excess EDC/NHS was then removed by centrifugation at 9000g for 10 min. Then, the activated PEG-AuNPs were resuspended in 1 mL of 0.1 M MES solution (pH 7.0). 7.5 to 25 μL of antibody (1 mg/mL) was added to the activated PEG-AuNPs and mixed by rotating for 30 min at 4 °C. Antibody-conjugated AuNPs were then separated from unbound antibody by centrifugation for 10 min at 9000g. The unbound antibody was collected to determine the antibody conjugation efficiency. Finally, 5 μL of mPEG-SH (200 mg/mL) was added to stabilize the Ab-PEG- AuNPs for long-term use.

Characterization of Ab-PEG-AuNPs Nanoconjugate

The size distribution and surface charge of all AuNPs were characterized by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and ζ-potential using a Zetasizer Nanoseries (Malvern, U.K.) at room temperature. Morphology of AuNPs was acquired using a Libra 200MC TEM, Zeiss. The particle and size distribution of each sample was obtained by image analysis using ImageJ. AuNP surface plasmon resonance (SPR) was measured with a Varioskan LUX multimode microplate reader. AuNPs were characterized by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Bruker Tensor 27 FTIR) over the wavelength range of 4000–400 cm–1.

Quantification of Antibody Conjugation Efficiency

The NanoOrange protein quantitation kit was adopted to measure antibody concentration and for the indirect determination of antibody conjugation efficiency. For this purpose, when purifying Ab-PEG-AuNPs from the unbound antibody, AuNPs were centrifuged at 9000g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected for quantification of unbound antibody with AuNPs. Standard solution (2–10 μg/mL) was prepared with rabbit anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike protein antibody. The NanoOrange reagent was freshly prepared and added to all samples according to manufacturer’s instructions. The mixtures were incubated at 90 °C for 10 min and then cooled to room temperature. Fluorescence of the NanoOrange reagent interacted with the antibody and was measured by exciting the samples at 485 nm. Emission was measured at 590 nm with a Varioskan LUX multimode microplate reader.

Sensor Fabrication

The fabrication of our microwave sensor is based on a 500 Å copper-coated glass slide (EMF Corp.) using both photolithography and electroplating techniques. It starts with coating positive photoresist S1813 onto a copper glass slide at 1500 rpm for 60 s and followed by baking in a hot plate for 120 s. The microwave sensor design is then patterned on a photoresist-coated copper glass slide via UV exposure, followed by the removal of the exposed photoresist using a developer solution. This step defines the areas where copper traces for the sensor design will be exposed. Subsequently, the copper traces are exposed and their height is increased using electroplating techniques, involving the following procedures: immersing the slide in a solution containing 0.2 M CuSO4, 0.1 M H3BO3, and 0.1 M H2SO4 for specific durations and current densities (5 min at 2 mA, 5 min at 5 mA, and 10 min at 7 mA). Finally, acetone and FeCl3 solutions are used to remove the extra positive photoresist and base copper layer, respectively.

Chip Fabrication and Ab-PEG-AuNPs Nanoconjugate Coating

The polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) mold was made by mixing PDMS base with the curing agent at a 10:1 mass ratio, followed by degassing in a vacuum oven. The mixture was then put on a hot plate at 95 °C to cure for 60 min and cut into the size that fit the glass slide. A reservoir was created by punching the PDMS mold to hold the sample above the microwave sensor. It was carefully aligned so that the capacitive gap of the inner loop is centered within the reservoir, as depicted in Figure 2B. The reservoir measured 4 mm in diameter and 8 mm in height, providing a volume capacity of 100 μL (100 mm3). To coat the antibody conjugate, the glass substrate with the microwave sensor was treated with oxygen plasma at 29.7 W and 500 mTorr for 20 s to remove any organic contamination and improve surface adhesion. Then, 2% (v/v) APTES was dropped into the reservoir for 30 min for salinization followed by air-drying for 5 min. Twenty-five μL of gold-antibody conjugate was dropped onto the reservoir and air-dried for 2 h to finish the coating process. Finally, a SubMiniature version A (SMA) connector was soldered to the outer loop of the microwave resonator to connect to a vector network analyzer (VNA) for analyzing the microwave signal.

Sensing Procedure

Samples containing virus were prepared in buffer solutions with concentrations ranging from 1000 to 50,000 copies/mL. Prior to testing, the reservoir was primed with PBS for 5 min to stabilize the microwave signal. Subsequently, 100 μL of the viral sample solutions were introduced into the reservoir to replace PBS. Throughout the testing process, microwave signals were recorded using a VNA. Experimental data were collected continuously for 60 min until the frequency shift reached a plateau. This duration was chosen based on our observations and prior study,16 which indicated that the frequency shift, reflecting virus binding to the sensor, stabilized within this time frame. Shorter detection times could compromise sensitivity at lower concentrations, while excessively long durations are impractical for rapid testing for COVID-19. After achieving saturation and stability in binding (at 60 min), microwave signals were recorded, and the resonance frequency shift was calculated and reported as the mean ± standard deviation. To ensure experimental reliability and reproducibility, each measurement was independently conducted and repeated five times (n = 5) with five individual sensors. A new sensor was employed for each independent measurement to prevent potential sample cross-contamination. The resonance frequency shift was calculated and reported as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA was performed to evaluate the consistency and significance of the measurements across different samples.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Ab-PEG-AuNPs Nanoconjugate

In this study, the anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike protein antibody, which is specific to the spike protein present on the virus surface, was functionalized on AuNPs via HS-PEG12-COOH as a linker (Figure 1A). For this purpose, bare AuNPs were first synthesized by the reduction of HAuCl4·3H2O with sodium citrate. TEM analysis confirmed that bare AuNPs possessed a spherical shape with an average diameter of about 14.88 nm and a narrow size distribution (Figure 1B). Bare AuNPs were monodispersed with a hydrodynamic diameter of 21.71 ± 0.07 nm as measured by DLS (Table 1). In order to functionalize AuNPs with the antibody, PEG containing a thiol group and a terminal carboxyl group as linkers was used to form PEG-AuNPs. The thiol group (-SH) was used for PEG self-assembly onto the surface of bare AuNPs via the Au–S bond. EDC/NHS bioconjugation has the advantage of providing a stable covalent bond to connect polymers with biomolecules. In this work, the terminal carboxyl group on PEG-AuNPs was activated with EDC/NHS for covalent coupling with the antibodies’ amine group. An increase in hydrodynamic diameter to 36.58 ± 0.537 nm was observed as a result of PEG coating. After conjugating the antibodies to AuNPs, we found that the particle diameter was further increased to 52.49 ± 5.342 nm. This result indicated successful antibody conjugation to the carboxyl group of PEG that was coated on the AuNPs, considering the size of the IgG molecule reported to be around 4.0–14.5 nm.24 Meanwhile, the ζ-potential of the AuNPs positively shifted from −33.60 ± 1.089 mV to −12.40 ± 0.964 mV after PEG coating since the charge strength of the carboxyl groups of PEG is weaker than that of citrate on the surface of AuNPs. After antibody conjugation, the surface charge shifted to −36.73 ± 0.331 mV, indicating the stability of Ab-PEG-AuNPs nanoconjugate.25

AuNPs display unique optical properties due to their strong interaction with visible light. While interacting with light, excitation of free electrons in gold atoms induces surface plasmon resonance (SPR), enabling AuNPs to absorb and scatter visible light based on their size, shape, and agglomeration state.26,27 In our study, the bare AuNPs showed maximum absorption and yielded a characteristic peak at 519 nm. After PEG coating, the peak slightly shifted to 522 nm (Figure 1C). This shift suggests successful surface coating of PEG which may be due to the PEG polymer layer changing the refractive index around AuNPs.28 After antibody functionalization, absorption peak of AuNPs showed a modest shift to 523 nm as previously described.23,28

To further confirm the successful conjugation of AuNPs with antibodies, typical FTIR spectra of lyophilized bare AuNPs, PEG-AuNPs, and Ab-PEG-AuNPs are shown in Figure 1D. Compared to bare AuNPs, PEG-AuNPs showed OH stretching (3340 cm–1) and COC stretching (1180 cm–1) bands, which indicated that SH–PEG12–COOH was successfully coated on the surface of AuNPs. Ab-PEG-AuNPs nanoconjugate displayed a characteristic peak at 1600 cm–1 which is assigned to the amide I band and mainly associated with C=O stretching vibration in the amide bond. Amide II and III bands showed at 1420 and 1220 cm–1 which are associated with C–N and N–H bending vibration in the amide bond. Amide A was also shown at 3300 cm–1 which is assigned to NH stretching. These results again indicate the successful conjugation of antibodies with AuNPs.

To determine the amount of anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike protein antibody attached to AuNPs, a fluorescent-based method using NanoOrange reagent was employed to determine the excess antibody in the Ab-PEG-AuNPs supernatant after antibody conjugation. This conjugation method achieved a high antibody conjugation efficiency (95.24 ± 2.36%) with 23.94 ± 0.456 μg of anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike protein antibody covering the AuNPs surface via PEG as a linker, as shown in Figure 1E.

Detection of SARS-CoV-2 Virus with the Ab-PEG-AuNPs Nanoconjugate-Modified Microwave Sensor

The entire sensing platform (including VNA, the SMA connector, and microwave sensor chip for the detection of SARS-CoV-2 virus) is presented in Figure 2A. Our compact-sized sensor chip is based on microwave sensing features with cocenter split ring resonators (SRR) as the sensing component and an on-chip reservoir as the sample container as displayed in Figure 2B. Compared with the conventional microchannel design involving continuous flow pumping, this sensor is further miniaturized with an on-chip static reservoir which also can be easily removed making the microwave sensor reusable. In addition, the input sample volume can be reduced with a reservoir, therefore achieving better portability of the POC application. In the experiments, the samples were added into the reservoir chamber, which is located above the sensing region for detection. The proposed microwave sensor’s design parameters were optimized to display a sharp sensitive resonance, which is ideal for sensing. The focal sensing area in this sensor lies within the inner ring gap, showing the maximal magnitude of the electric field, as illustrated in Figure 2C. Our previous work has successfully demonstrated that this sensor design can detect the presence of the SARS-CoV-2 spike antigen as well as virus particles when the sensing region is modified with the anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike protein antibody alone.16 Once captured by the sensor, the antigen and virus in the liquid samples induce significant changes in the electromagnetic properties in the sensing region, herein inducing a resonant frequency shift. In this study, we further explored the possibility of introducing Ab-PEG-AuNPs nanoconjugates on the sensor region to directly measure the SARS-CoV-2 virus and improve the performance of this sensor.

To demonstrate the performance of the Ab-PEG-AuNPs nanoconjugate-modified microwave sensors for virus detection, we treated the capacitive gap on the inner loop of the microwave sensor with APTES to bind with the antibody on Ab-PEG-AuNPs. Figure 2D illustrates the sensing region of unmodified and modified microwave sensors and a comparison of the frequency shift with and without Ab-PEG-AuNPs modification. Without modification, a minor resonance frequency shift (0.53 ± 0.1155 MHz) was recognized on the bare sensor; whereas for Ab-PEG-AuNPs-modified microwave resonator, we observed a significantly greater frequency shift of 6.83 ± 0.475 MHz for 10,000 copies/mL of the SARS-CoV-2 virus. The performance of this sensor is significantly influenced by the quantity of the Ab-PEG-AuNPs nanoconjugate. To achieve an optimized frequency response, a particularly precise amount of Ab-PEG-AuNPs should be coated in the sensor region. Within 0.189 mg of the Ab-PEG-AuNPs nanoconjugate, we observed an increase in frequency shift that is contingent on the quantity of the nanoconjugate applied to the sensor in experimental measurements for detecting a viral concentration of 10,000 copies/mL for SARS-CoV-2. Notably, an observed short circuit of the SRR is evident in experimental results upon exceeding this threshold, as demonstrated in Figure 2E. Correspondingly, a numerical model using High-Frequency Structure Simulation (HFSS) software was constructed with boundary conditions provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S1). By tracking the simulated changes of S11, HFSS simulation demonstrated the occurrence of a short circuit when excess AuNPs are applied to the sensor, as shown in Figure 2F. However, with modification with an optimized amount of AuNPs, the S11 resonance frequency of the fabricated sensor appeared normal.

In this study, numerical simulation also highlighted the capability of the designed sensor to detect SARS-CoV-2 viruses based on the dielectric property variation over the ring gap of the sensing resonator. As shown in Figure 3A, a resonance frequency shift around 9.5 Hz is anticipated in the presence of the SARS-CoV-2 virus in the simulation, as opposed to in the absence of the virus. In experimental measurements, the sensitivity of the Ab-AuNPs-modified microwave sensor was determined by exposing different concentrations of SARS-CoV-2 virus (1000–50,000 copies/mL in buffer solution) to the sensing region within the reservoir (Figure 3B). All microwave sensors modified with Ab-PEG-AuNPs exhibit negligible shifts in the resonance frequency for the buffer solution, which served as a control group. A constant increase in the resonance frequency shift (from 2.37 ± 0.25 MHz to 12.81 ± 1.643 MHz) was observed as the concentration of the SARS-CoV-2 virus increased (corresponding to 1000–50,000 copies/mL) at 60 min of detection. The increasing frequency shift could be due to the change in the electromagnetic properties on the sensor, since the amount of the target virus bound to the capture antibody has increased. Here, the incorporation of Ab-PEG-AuNPs nanoconjugates on the sensor platform significantly enhanced the frequency shift of the microwave sensor responding to the virus (Figure S3 provided in the Supporting Information). The LOD of the modified sensor for the SARS-CoV-2 virus is 1000 copies/mL within 60 min of detection. In contrast to our previous sensor design, where the sensor was merely coated with the anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike protein antibody and achieved a detection limit of 4000 copies/mL within 90 min,16 notable improvements in detection time and LOD were achieved after Ab-PEG-AuNPs modification in this work. The sensor proposed in this study also demonstrates an exceptional LOD when compared to recent SARS-CoV-2 sensors (refer to Table S5). As opposed to commercial Ag-RDTs which usually detect a viral load above 1.2–2.2 × 106, this sensor is also considered highly sensitive.29 This sensor only requires a low sample volume of 100 μL, equivalent to 2–3 drops of liquid,30 while achieving sensitive detection for the SARS-CoV-2 virus.

In contrast to our previous sensor, which was optimized for identifying SARS-CoV-2 antigens from other coronavirus antigens (MERS-CoV and SARS-CoV-1 antigens),16 a huge improvement of this sensor is to directly target SARS-CoV-2 virus particles without the need for prior sample processing. In order to assess the specificity of our sensor to detect the SARS-CoV-2 virus, a series of virus-controlled experiments were conducted with other respiratory interference viruses. The sensor was exposed to various nontarget strains, including noncoronavirus entities such as Influenza A and B, and coronavirus strains, namely SARS-CoV-1 and MERS-CoV. The concentrations of all viral loads were intentionally upheld at 10,000 copies/mL, a deliberate choice made to evaluate the sensor’s specificity under conditions where the viral loads are commonly seen in clinically positive patients.31,32 This aims to underscore the sensor’s precision and accuracy in detecting the target SARS-CoV-2 virus and emphasize its robustness in scenarios of clinical relevance. As shown in Figure 4, the most significant frequency shift recorded at 60 min was only observed when the sensor was exposed to the SARS-CoV-2 virus. The modified sensor exhibited negligible reactivity to all non-SARS-CoV-2 virus analytes, although a slightly elevated response was shown compared to the control buffer solution. This sensor confidently recognizes SARS-CoV-2 virus when compared to Influenza A and B, SARS-CoV-1, and MERS-CoV virus, despite their similarity in protein sequences, size, and overall morphology. Collectively, this sensor exhibits exceptional specificity by selectively detecting SARS-CoV-2 virus while discerning it from other respiratory interference strains.

Conclusions

COVID-19 detection is an important public health tool during the pandemic and is crucial for controlling the spread of the virus and reducing the burden on healthcare systems. As detection techniques have evolved over time, the development of affordable POC detection with robust, rapid, accurate, and user-friendly features on portable platforms is important. In this work, a sensor platform with the potential to improve SARS-CoV-2 detection and complement current reagent-free virus tests has been designed and constructed by using microwave sensing technology. SARS-CoV-2 virus particles were detected directly by the sensor without a laborious and expensive sample extraction process, which significantly saves time and cost for COVID-19 diagnostics. The sensing platform incorporates the anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike antibody-AuNPs nanoconjugates for recognition of SARS-CoV-2 virus and fabrication of the sensor; this sensor allows for sensitive measurement of different concentrations of SARS-CoV-2 virus and achieves a limit of virus detection of 1000 copies/mL within 60 min with an optimized amount of nanoconjugates modification, all without the need for additional reagents during the sensing process. Requiring only 100 μL of sample, this sensor could potentially overcome the limited amount of clinical samples, such as throat swabs, nose swabs, saliva sputum, and blood collected from patients. Without cross-sensitivity, this sensor demonstrated higher selectivity in recognizing the target SARS-CoV-2 virus than other structurally similar viral stains (SARS-CoV-1, MERS-CoV) and structurally different stains (Influenza A and B). In comparison to our previous work and recent SARS CoV-2 sensors, this sensor platform exhibits notable advancements in sensitivity, efficiency, and virus detection specificity, accompanied by a reduction in the required sample input.16 In summary, a new POC testing strategy is demonstrated in this work to provide high-precision and ready-to-use COVID-19 detection and is very suitable for community testing and household screening. The sensing strategy described in this research provides a robust foundation for more in-depth studies concerning not only viruses but also other pathogens by conjugating a suitable recognition antibody with our proposed functionalization methodology for the construction of microwave sensors. This platform opens the opportunity beyond biomedical applications and encompasses adaptability for food, agricultural, and environmental applications.
