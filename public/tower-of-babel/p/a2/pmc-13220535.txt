A novel TAp63γ–Airn regulatory axis governs early myogenic gene networks
Authors: Veronica Ciuffoli, Maria Cristina Piro, Alessia Angelin, Giulio Colasanto, Huiqing Zhou, Jieqiong Qu, Anna Maria Lena, Gerry Melino, Eleonora Candi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Skeletal muscle development relies on tightly coordinated transcriptional programs, yet the contribution of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) to myogenesis remains largely unexplored. We previously demonstrated that the transcription factor TAp63γ is upregulated during myoblast differentiation and regulates the expression of genes involved in early stages of myogenic maturation. Here, combining transcriptome profiling with p63 ChIP-seq analysis in differentiating mouse myoblasts, we identify the lncRNA Airn as a direct transcriptional target of TAp63γ. Our findings position Airn as a previously unrecognized regulator of myogenic commitment that modulates both MyoD and MyoG expression at the mRNA and protein levels. Our work uncovers a novel TAp63γ–Airn axis essential for proper skeletal muscle differentiation and with potential relevance to muscle-wasting diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00801-8.

Introduction

Skeletal muscle accounts for nearly 40% of body weight and performs a wide range of essential physiological functions, including movement and structural support. A detailed understanding of the developmental principles that regulate skeletal muscle formation and function is indispensable for elucidating muscle pathologies and guiding the development of new therapeutic strategies for conditions such as developmental disorders, rhabdomyosarcoma, sarcopenia, muscular dystrophies, or acute injuries. Myogenesis, the process by which muscle tissue is formed, proceeds through a series of distinct developmental stages. Throughout both embryonic and postnatal phases, myogenesis is governed by a diverse set of signaling molecules that activate intracellular cascades driving the expression of a series of transcriptional regulators and chromatin-modifying enzymes. Specifically, the identity of precursor myogenic cells derives from the hierarchical and spatio-temporal induction/repression of muscle regulatory transcription factors (MRF) such as Myf5, MyoD, MRF4 (Myf6), and myogenin (MyoG), which specify the myogenic lineage [1]. The execution of this highly orchestrated myogenesis program, which includes also regulatory loops of cytokines, microRNAs, and long non-coding RNAs, finally leads to cell cycle arrest, fusion of myoblasts into multinucleated myotubes, and expression of specific muscle proteins, such as myosin heavy chain [2].

TP63 and TP73 proteins belong to the p53 family of transcription factors [3, 4]. Both TP63 and TP73 are expressed in multiple protein isoforms due to the presence of two alternative promoters (P1 and P2) and/or alternative splicing at the 3’ ends of their mRNA [5]. While the TA isoforms include the N-terminal transactivation domain, its absence characterizes the truncated ΔN isoforms. In addition, both TA and ΔN-p63/p73 mRNAs can be alternatively spliced at their 3’-end generating the α-, β-, γ- and other minor isoforms [6].

Members of the p53 family have already been implicated in the regulation of myogenic differentiation; see [7] for a review. In fact, TAp63 is essential for in vitro cardiogenesis and heart development in vivo [8]. Moreover, the knockout mice for p63 show severe defects in embryonic cardiac development, including dilation of both ventricles, a defect in trabeculation, and abnormal septation; accordingly, embryonic stem cells demonstrate endodermal expression of TAp63 with modulation of GATA-6 cardiogenic rescue, see below [9]. In this case, TAp63 seems to act in a non-cell-autonomous manner by modulating the expression of endodermal factors. An unexpected role for TAp63 in myofibrillogenesis has been uncovered during cardiogenesis in TAp63-null mice; these knockout animals exhibit impaired cardiac development, characterized by pronounced myofibrillar disarray and mitochondrial disorganization [9]. Moreover, during myogenic differentiation, a synergistic and complementary role was shown between all p53 family members, where p63 and p73 cooperate via the CKI p57 to maintain RB protein in the active hypo-phosphorylated state required for the permanent cell cycle exit [10]. Interestingly, our group previously showed that the TAp63γ isoform is the only member of the p53 family that accumulates during in vitro myoblast differentiation [11] where it also controls proliferation and mitochondrial basal respiration [12]. Furthermore, silencing of TAp63γ leads to a delay in terminal differentiation and a marked reduction in the myoblast fusion index [11].

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are RNAs longer than 200 nt with little or no coding potential generated by the pervasive and dynamic transcription of most of the genomes of plants and animals. By interacting with DNA, RNA, or proteins, lncRNAs exquisitely regulate gene expression, chromatin structure and accessibility as well as RNA stability, splicing, and translation. Since their initial discovery, the idea that long non-coding RNAs play a prominent role in fine-tuning the organization of gene expression during cell differentiation and development has become increasingly prevalent [13]. In this regard, some lncRNAs have already been described as regulators of muscle differentiation and myogenesis, such as linc-MD1 [14], H19, Malat1, Neat 1 [15], MyHC IIA/X-AS [16], lncMyoD [14, 17], or linc-RAM [18]. Nevertheless, this fascinating and still partly enigmatic landscape continues to evolve as newly discovered lncRNAs are progressively being recognized as pivotal regulators of myogenesis. Indeed, evidence is mounting describing lncRNAs as integral components of the muscle development regulatory system. In light of this and given the regulatory implications of p63 during muscle cell differentiation, here we aimed to identify p63-dependent lncRNAs whose expression was modulated during in vitro myogenesis. Specifically, we centered our attention on Airn lncRNA, which, in a recent and robust bioinformatic analysis, has been recognized as one of five lncRNAs, along with Xist, Gas5, Pvt1, and Meg3, endowed with regulatory potential in the context of skeletal muscle development [19]. Here, we describe the first molecular pathway by which TAp63 affects in vitro myogenesis in mouse cells.

Results

Airn is a p63-dependent lncRNA expressed during myoblast differentiation

To assess the transcriptional impact of p63 silencing in myoblasts, we first performed whole-transcriptome profiling of differentiating C2C7 cells transfected with either a p63 (sip63) or a scramble (siSCR) control siRNA, covering 14,050 coding and 703 non-coding genes, thus implementing our previous data [11, 12]. The importance of TAp63γ contribution in muscle cell development is shown in Fig. 1A, B, and Table S1, resulting in the significant modulation of 1109 coding genes and 47 non-coding genes (cut-off |log₂FC| ≥ 1, p-value < 0,05). Specifically, the volcano plots in Fig. 1A and B reveal that p63 depletion leads to the significant upregulation of 550 mRNAs and downregulation of 559 mRNAs, as well as the upregulation of 17 ncRNAs and downregulation of 30 ncRNAs. Similarly, among the most significantly downregulated non-coding transcripts was 430431A17Rik, a lncRNA previously associated with muscle myogenesis [20] and Airn, an lncRNA which displayed a marked logFC = − 2.013 following p63 silencing (P = 0,000257, Table S1, Fig. 1B). Therefore, we focused our analysis on Airn expression, whose role in myoblast development was still unexplored.

TP63 binds Airn gene and controls its transcription during myoblast differentiation

To explore whether p63 could directly regulate Airn transcription through binding to its genomic region, we analyzed previously published TAp63γ ChIP-seq data obtained from a murine ES endodermal cell line (END2) [8]. This analysis revealed two prominent TAp63γ binding peaks/signals within the second intron of the Airn gene Fig. 1C. In parallel, we performed H3K27ac ChIP-seq during C2C7 differentiation at 0, 36, and 72 h, and observed that only one of the two putative TAp63γ binding sites identified by ChIP-seq exhibited increased H3K27ac signal during differentiation. We then used the p63scan software [21] to detect potential p63 binding motifs within the Airn locus. Consistent with our ChIP-seq data, we found a canonical p63 binding motif within the second intron of Airn, precisely overlapping the first putative TAp63γ binding signal and co-localized within the H3K27ac (Fig. 1C). We then validated both p63 transcriptomic and p63 ChIP data. By real-time PCR analysis, we detected a sixfold increase in Airn expression in C2C7 cells after 36 h of differentiation (Fig. 2A). At the same time point, silencing of TAp63γ resulted in up to a 40% reduction of Airn expression level in the same cells (Fig. 2B). Moreover, TAp63γ overexpression in Tet-ON C2C12 cells [12] generated an approximately threefold increase in Airn levels at 36 h of differentiation and in the presence of doxycycline induction (Fig. 2C). The specific binding of TAp63γ to the Airn genomic region bearing the p63 binding motif was then further validated through both ChIP-PCR in C2C12 Tet-On induced and differentiated cells and luciferase assay in H1299 cells, Fig. 2D, E. These results confirmed that, in murine myoblasts, lncRNA Airn expression increases during differentiation under the direct binding and control of p63.

Airn modulates MyoD and MyoG levels during myoblast differentiation

We then wondered if lncRNA Airn might also play an active role during myoblast differentiation by modulating both MyoG and MyoD mRNA and protein levels. To answer this question, we knocked down Airn expression using two specific siRNAs, sh1-Airn and sh2-Airn (Fig. 3A), and induced differentiation of C2C7 cells for 36 h. Our hypothesis was corroborated by real-time PCR (Fig. 3B, C) and western blot analyses (Fig. 3D-F), which showed that Airn silencing significantly reduced both MyoD and MyoG mRNA levels (by 70% and 90%, respectively) and protein levels (by 60% and 80%, respectively).

Discussion

This study, for the first time, analyzes TP63-dependent Airn expression in muscle cells and explores its potential and novel function during myogenesis. TP63 exists in multiple isoforms, with diverse functions, from regulating development, tissue homeostasis, and cell fate determination to influencing senescence and aging [22, 23]. In this paper, we unveil that the isoform TAp63γ controls muscle cell development through the action of both long ncRNA and myogenic basal transcription factors. Myoblast whole-transcriptome profiling after TP63 silencing revealed a profound reshaping of the muscle cell transcriptome affecting both coding and non-coding genes. Loss of TAp63γ in mouse myoblasts led to the strong repression of coding genes crucial for myocyte and myofiber structure and development, including Myh3, Myh8, Fgf21, and MyoG, suggesting also an important impact on gene regulatory networks involved in cell fate control. Notably, the deregulation of the noncoding transcriptome added an additional layer of complexity. Within this framework, the significant modulation of the lncRNA Airn during myoblast differentiation and upon TP63 silencing stimulated us to dissect the myogenic pathway downstream of TAp63γ. Here, we demonstrated that the lncRNA Airn is a direct target of TAp63γ during in vitro murine myogenic differentiation. We provide evidence that TAp63γ specifically binds a site in the intronic region of the Airn gene exhibiting increased H3K27ac enrichment, consistent with an enhancer-like function [24]. We have previously identified MyoG and MyoD as proteins whose levels were modulated by TAp63γ expression during differentiation of C2C7 muscle cells [11]. The finding that Airn silencing reduces significantly both MyoG and MyoD mRNA and protein levels reinforces the idea that Airn acts as an important regulator during muscle cell development. Moreover, Airn appears to phenocopy the action of TAp63γ in muscle cells, suggesting that Airn is, at least in part, a mediator of the pro-differentiative effects of TAp63γ in this context.

This is a new feature of Airn, because Airn so far has been studied in mouse ESCs (Embryonic Stem Cells) as a paternally expressed, nuclear and unspliced imprinted transcript arising from the antisense strand of the second intron of the Igf2r gene, mediating cis-silencing of nearby maternal alleles such as Igf2r, Slc22a2, and Slc22a3 [25, 26]. In cardiomyocytes, Airn interacts with the RNA-binding protein IGF2BP2, modulating mRNA translation, cellular stress response, migration, and survival [25]. Airn has also been linked to various pathological conditions, such as liver injury [27], diabetic cardiac fibrosis [28], podocyte injury [29], and maintenance of liver sinusoidal endothelial cell differentiation [30], although these functions are not yet fully understood. It is, however, long known that many lncRNAs, such as H19, Neat1, Malat1 [15] are considered multifunctional because they can act in different roles depending on the cellular context, the proteins with which they interact, or their localization.

Most lncRNAs involved in myogenic processes exert their function by modulating gene expression at the epigenetic and transcriptional levels, largely through interactions with proteins such as DNA methyltransferases, histone-modifying enzymes, and chromatin-remodeling complexes [31]. Specifically, some lncRNAs are activated or induced by MyoD upon early differentiation [17, 32, 33], while others promote MyoD expression or its binding to its target loci, such as MyoG [18, 34–37].

The Airn-dependent upregulation of MyoD and MyoG that we observed suggests that Airn may promote myogenesis by inducing MyoD, an early factor that recruits chromatin modifiers [38], which in turn activates MyoG, a gene essential for the late fusion of myocytes into multinucleated myotubes (Fig. 3G). In myoblasts, MyoD has been reported to indirectly engage the transcriptional coactivator BRD4. Indeed, in muscle cells, BRD4 is a reader of acetylated histones that promotes transcriptional elongation and facilitates activation of gene programs, including myogenic genes such as MyoG [39, 40]. For this reason, during myogenic differentiation, MyoD co-localizes with BRD4 at active enhancers [41]. In a hypothetical scenario, while we have shown that p63 induces Airn, which in turn upregulates MyoD and MyoG, it is tempting to speculate that BRD4 could participate in this pathway, as BRD4 has also been reported to regulate TP63 expression and to physically interact with TP63 in epithelial cells [42, 43]. Of course, although plausible,this hypothesis awaits full experimental validation in the muscle context.

One limitation of this study is that TAp63γ ChIP-seq was performed in END2 cells, where TAp63 is expressed [8]as its low abundance in differentiated myoblasts limits ChIP sensitivity; nonetheless, the TAp63 key binding site was validated in myoblasts and supported by H3K27ac ChIP-seq, confirming its relevance in the muscle context. Another limitation is that the mechanism by which Airn regulates MyoD and MyoG remains unclear. LncRNAs may act at the transcriptional level, for example, by scaffolding chromatin remodeling complexes or facilitating promoter–enhancer interactions, or at the post-transcriptional level by stabilizing mRNAs [44]; however, the precise mechanism underlying Airn function in this context has yet to be defined.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings support a previously unrecognized role for the lncRNA Airn as a direct p63 target, functioning as a novel key regulator during early stages of murine myoblast differentiation. Nonetheless, additional detailed investigations will be essential to define its structural/functional characterization, its intracellular localization, and molecular partners in muscle cells. We believe that these insights may help unravel the intricate molecular networks governing muscle differentiation and ultimately support the development of novel and effective therapeutic approaches for muscle-wasting disorders.

Materials and methods

Reagents and resource sharing

Further information and requests for resources and reagents should be directed to Eleonora Candi (candi@uniroma2.it).

Study design, cell lines, and transfection

C2C7 and C2C12 myoblasts [45] were grown in DMEM supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/ml). Myoblasts were differentiated by switching them to a differentiation medium consisting of DMEM supplemented with 2% horse serum and penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/ml). C2C12 Tet-ON myoblasts were generated as previously described [11, 12]. For p63 and Airn silencing by siRNAs in C2C7 cells, cells were seeded and transfected with Lipofectamine RNAiMax (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For transfection of p63 siRNA, Qiagen, GeneSolution siRNA #1,027,416, Mm_Trp63_11, and #SI04953760, Mm_Trp63_8, #SI04953739; for transfection of Airn siRNA, Qiagen, FlexiTube siRNA, #SI01485267, Mm_Airn_1, and #SI01485274, Mm_Airn_2. 48 h after transfection, myoblasts were switched to differentiation medium. Cells were collected at 0 h (proliferating myoblasts) and after 36 h of differentiation (myocytes). For luciferase assays, the H1299 cell line has been used. The number of experimental replicates and statistics are indicated in the figure legends.

Western blotting

Myoblasts were lysed in RIPA buffer (50mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP40, 0.25%Na-deoxycolate, 1mM AEBSF, and 1 mM DTT). 15–40 µg of total protein extracts were analysed by SDS PAGE and blotted onto Hybond PVDF membrane (GE Healthcare). Primary antibodies used were anti-MyoD (sc-304, Santa Cruz, 1:300), anti-MyoG (sc-576, Santa Cruz, 1:300), and anti-αtubulin (Sigma, 1:10000). After appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies incubation (Bio-Rad), signal detection was performed with the ECL chemiluminescence kit.

RNA-extraction, RT-qPCR, and whole-transcriptome profiling

Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. 1 µg of total RNA was used for cDNA synthesis by the GOScript Reverse Transcription System Kit (Promega). mRNAs relative quantification by RT-qPCR was performed using the GOTaq Real-Time PCR System (Promega) in the Applied Biosystem 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystem). GAPDH was used as a housekeeping gene for normalization. Gene expression was defined from the threshold cycle (Ct), and relative expression levels were calculated by using the 2− ΔΔCt method. Primers used for RT-qPCR are reported in Table S2.

For the whole-transcriptome analysis of p63-silenced C2C7 cells, myoblasts were seeded and transfected with specific mouse p63 siRNA (Qiagen, GeneSolution siRNA #1027416, Mm_Trp63_11, #SI04953760) or scramble siRNA (siSCR), using Lipofectamine RNAiMax Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, #13778150) and then differentiated for 36 h. Total RNA from N = 3 biological replicants was isolated using the Rneasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Whole-transcriptome profiling was performed by Biogazelle (Ghent, Belgium) as previously described [12]. cRNA was hybridized to a SurePrint G3 Mouse GE 8 × 60 K Microarray (Agilent). Only genes with log2(FC) > + 1, log2(FC)< -1 and p-value < 0.05 were included in the analysis. Volcano plots for both coding and non-coding genes were generated by analyzing the full lists of modulated genes in sip63 compared with siSCR at 36 h of differentiation (Table S1), using R-project software. Red points indicate downregulated and upregulated genes in sip63 with -log10(p-value) > 1.3 and log2(FC) > + 1, log2(FC)< -1; grey points indicate non significantly modulated genes.

Cloning and luciferase assays

Enhancer region Airn containing p63 putative responsive element (chr17:12983940–12985233, mm9) was amplified from C2C7 myoblasts genomic DNA using specific primers (Table S2). PCR products were digested by KpnI/XhoI restriction enzymes (New England Biolabs) and subcloned into the pGL3-Promoter vector (Promega). The construct generated was completely sequenced. For the luciferase activity assay, a total of 1.2 × 106 H1299 cells were cultured in 12-well plates and transfected at 60% confluence. 100 ng of pGl3 constructs 2 ng of pRL-CMV vector (Promega) and 300 ng of pcDNA-HA-TAp63α, pcDNA-HA-TAp63γ or empty pcDNA-HA vector (as a control) were co-transfected using Effectene transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Qiagen). 24 h after transfection, luciferase activity was quantified by using a Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) in cellular extracts; light emission was measured over 10 s using a Lumat LB9507 luminometer (EG&Berthold). The transfection efficiency was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)- PCR

1 × 106 TAp63γ C2C12 Tet-On myoblasts were induced to overexpress HA-TAp63γ for 24 h by doxycycline addition and induced to differentiate for 36 h, and used for ChIP-PCR. Cells were collected, fixed in 1% formaldehyde, and sonicated to shear DNA. The chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed with an anti-HA antibody (Abcam, ab9110) or nonspecific immunoglobulin G (IgG, Invitrogen), as a negative control, using MAGnify ChIP Kit (Invitrogen). Specific primers were used to amplify the putative TAp63 response elements identified in genomic regions in Airn intronic regions (mm9 chr17:12984458-12984661). Primers used for the ChIP-PCR assay are reported in Table S2.

H3K27ac chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) sequencing and analysis

C2C7 cells were seeded and collected at 0, 36, and 72 h of differentiation. Antibodies against H3K27ac (Diagenode, C15410174, 1.2 µg) were used in ChIP-seq of H3K27ac. Resulted DNA fragments from four independent ChIP assays were purified. Afterward, these DNA fragments were pooled and used to construct libraries using the KAPA Hyper Prep Kit (Kapa Biosystems #KK8504) according to the standard protocol. The prepared libraries were then sequenced using the NextSeq 500 (Illumina) according to standard Illumina protocols.

Approximately 15–20 × 106 reads were sequenced for each sample. Sequencing reads were aligned to the human genome assembly mm9 (NCBI version 37) using BWA [46]. Mapped reads were filtered for quality, and duplicates were removed for further analysis. The bamCoverage script was used to generate and normalize bigwig files with the RPKM formula. Peak calling was performed using MACS2 [47] against a reference input sample from the same cell line, with standard settings and a q-value of 0.05. The broad setting (--BROAD) was enabled for detecting H3K27me3 peaks. Only peaks with a P-value < 10e-5 were used for differential analysis using Manorm [48]. Association of peaks to genes and associated GO annotation was performed with GREAT [49], with the ‘single nearest gene within 1 Mb’ association rule. P values were computed with a hypergeometric distribution with FDR correction.
