Evaluating urbanization effects on biomass density using a hybrid AI model: a case study
Authors: Buket İşler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrbanisation profoundly influences both socioeconomic structures and ecological systems worldwide, highlighting the critical need for integrated approaches to sustainable urban planning and environmental management. This study investigates the effects of population growth and urban expansion on natural vegetation cover in Fethiye, Muğla, Turkey—a coastal region exemplifying the delicate balance between urban development and ecological preservation. To model vegetation dynamics, Normalised Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) values are projected up to the year 2032 using Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks applied to time series data. Land Surface Temperature (LST) and Normalised Difference Built-up Index (NDBI), derived from MODIS and Landsat satellite imagery spanning 2013–2023, are used as input parameters. The predictions are interpreted within the framework of anticipated urban growth, demographic trends, and stable climate assumptions. Furthermore, a hybrid model combining Discrete Wavelet Transform with LSTM (DWT-LSTM) is proposed to enhance predictive performance, resulting in a 9.1% improvement in NDVI accuracy. This improvement has been validated against CORINE land cover data, demonstrating the model’s robustness and generalisability. The findings offer crucial insights for urban planners and environmental policymakers. By quantitatively linking urbanisation indicators with ecological degradation, this study provides a data-driven foundation for mitigating the adverse environmental impacts of rapid urban development. The results underscore the utility of advanced AI-based models in forecasting ecological change and supporting informed, sustainable land-use strategies.Keywords: Urbanization, Remote sensing, LSTM, Discrete wavelet transformSubject terms: Environmental impact, Climate-change impacts, Sustainability

Introduction

Urbanisation is a complex and transformative process that reshapes the spatial structure of human settlements and exerts profound impacts on ecological and economic systems. Accelerating since the second half of the 20th century, urbanisation has resulted in the majority of the global population residing in urban centres. While it brings certain advantages—such as enhanced economic productivity, technological development, and improved infrastructure—it also imposes critical challenges including environmental degradation and unsustainable land exploitation.

One of the most critical dimensions of this transformation is its impact on land use and land cover (LULC). Urbanisation drives drastic changes in LULC patterns by replacing agricultural land, forests, wetlands, and other natural surfaces with impervious urban structures. This process alters local ecosystems and hydrological cycles, disrupts biodiversity, and accelerates climate-related vulnerabilities. Particularly in regions experiencing tourism-led urban growth, such as Mediterranean coastal areas, rapid changes in LULC are often observed due to increased population density, housing demand, and infrastructure development. Similar transformations have also been documented by Altuwaijri et al.1 in the case of Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. Their study revealed that three decades of urban expansion significantly altered land surface conditions and ecosystem services in arid coastal settings. As a consequence, monitoring LULC changes through robust techniques has become indispensable for sustainable urban planning and environmental governance. In this context, remote sensing technologies play a pivotal role. Advances in satellite observation systems have enabled continuous, large-scale, and high-resolution monitoring of the Earth’s surface. Spectral indices derived from satellite imagery—such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), the Normalized Difference Built-up Index (NDBI), and Land Surface Temperature (LST)—offer critical insights into the spatial and temporal dynamics of LULC. NDVI quantifies vegetation health and biomass density2,3, while NDBI highlights the extent and density of built-up areas4,5. LST reflects thermal surface conditions, which are often exacerbated by urban land cover changes, reduced green spaces, and heat-retaining artificial materials—leading to the intensification of the urban heat island effect6. Similarly, the integration of NDVI and related spectral indices with machine learning algorithms for long-term environmental vulnerability prediction has been demonstrated by Kafy et al.7 in a rural context. Their study on the semi-arid Barind region of Bangladesh utilised remote sensing data and hybrid algorithms (Cellular Automata–Artificial Neural Network, CA-ANN) to assess and forecast agricultural drought vulnerability. Although their focus was on a rural and agricultural setting, their approach exemplifies the methodological potential of combining vegetation indices with AI-driven predictive frameworks, which resonates with the current study’s objectives in an urban context.

Urbanisation also brings significant social challenges such as unequal access to housing and services, increased cost of living, and the displacement of vulnerable populations. These pressures are particularly acute in tourism-driven regions, where rapid development can exacerbate pre-existing social inequalities. Thus, the study of urbanisation through the lens of LULC change—supported by geospatial indicators and time-series modelling—offers a comprehensive framework for understanding the ecological implications of spatial development. This approach not only reveals how urban expansion alters land surfaces but also provides critical foresight for mitigating adverse environmental and social impacts. However, to fully leverage the temporal richness and complexity of remote sensing data in LULC monitoring—particularly in predicting long-term vegetation trends under dynamic urban conditions—traditional statistical methods may fall short. In response to this limitation, deep learning techniques have emerged as powerful alternatives capable of capturing nonlinear relationships and temporal dependencies in time-series data. Among these, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks have shown significant advantages due to their capacity to retain long-term contextual information and identify complex temporal patterns. LSTM models have been effectively employed in modelling vegetation dynamics and NDVI time series, demonstrating superior performance over traditional methods such as ARIMA and SARIMA in capturing temporal dependencies and forecasting environmental variables8–10. These models are particularly suitable for analysing LULC-related vegetation changes under urbanisation pressures. Recent studies suggest that further improvements can be achieved by combining LSTM with complementary methods such as Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT).

Hybrid models that integrate LSTM with complementary techniques have shown strong performance in time series prediction. Among these, the fusion of LSTM with DWT is particularly effective, as it enables signal decomposition into multiple frequency components—thereby reducing noise and enhancing relevant features. For instance, Rhif et al.11 proposed an improved wavelet-based LSTM model (I-WT-LSTM) using 18 different mother wavelets and Adam-optimised learning, which demonstrated high forecasting accuracy on satellite-derived data. Building upon this approach, Long et al.12 confirmed the predictive advantages of DWT-LSTM in modelling vegetation trends from MODIS EVI time series. Furthermore, Abbes et al.13 applied a multiresolution wavelet-enhanced LSTM model (MRA-WT-LSTM) for drought prediction, achieving superior accuracy compared to traditional machine learning methods such as ANN, SVR, RFR, and standalone LSTM.

Unlike previous studies, this research applies the DWT-LSTM model to predict long-term changes in vegetation cover in the rapidly urbanising region of Fethiye, Muğla. As one of Türkiye’s most visited tourist destinations, Fethiye has experienced significant post-pandemic population growth. Coupled with tourism-driven land-use pressures, this trend has intensified urban expansion into ecologically sensitive areas—making Fethiye a representative case for studying the environmental impacts of urbanisation in Mediterranean coastal zones. To capture this transformation, NDVI trends between 2024 and 2032 were predicted using deep learning techniques, incorporating NDBI and LST data. Analyses were conducted using the LSTM model, followed by a novel hybrid method that combines DWT and LSTM, which significantly improved vegetation density forecasting accuracy. While previous studies have applied DWT-LSTM to MODIS or EVI datasets at large spatial scales, few have tested its performance against regional reference datasets such as CORINE—particularly in Mediterranean coastal regions. This study addresses that gap by integrating CORINE-based LULC data for model validation, thereby strengthening the relevance and applicability of the findings to local land-use policy. This study makes two primary contributions: First, it demonstrates the application of NDVI and LSTM for forecasting long-term vegetation trends in a rapidly urbanising Mediterranean context. Second, while the DWT-LSTM hybrid model is not entirely novel, its adaptation to the specific urban and ecological dynamics of Fethiye constitutes a region-specific methodological contribution. Rather than claiming methodological invention, the study enhances predictive accuracy through contextual refinement and offers empirical insights for sustainable land-use strategies in similarly pressured environments.

Materials and methods

Study area

Fethiye is located in the southwestern part of Türkiye, along the Mediterranean coast (Fig. 1). This geographical positioning within the Mediterranean basin plays a central role in shaping its climate, vegetation, and urban development dynamics.

Location of the case study area (Fethiye: 36°37’34.04” N − 29°6’33.23” E).

Fethiye is one of the largest districts of Muğla province, with an area of 3,055 km². This district is neighbouring the Gulf of Fethiye in the west, Mendos Mountain in the south, the heights of Babadağ and the Aya Cape Peninsula. To the east and north are the western parts of the Taurus Mountains14. Fethiye has weather conditions typical of the Mediterranean climate, making it an attractive destination for tourists throughout the year. The population of Fethiye was 195,419 in 2012 and decreased to 140,509 in 2013 when the eastern part of the district became a separate district as Seydikemer. It then increased by 4.30% to 177,702 in 2022. The highest annual population increase was recorded in 2008, with 4.61%; the most significant decrease was realized in 2013, with 28.10%15. Figure 2 presents the population growth of the region graphically.

Population of Fethiye from 2013 to 2022.

Fethiye district’s Mediterranean climate (average annual temperature 18.8 °C, ranging from 10.6 °C in January to 27.9 °C in July-August) and precipitation patterns (993.4 mm annually, with 62% winter concentration) create baseline vegetation conditions that are increasingly threatened by rapid urbanization, as demonstrated by the steady population growth from 140,000 in 2013 to 178,000 in 2022 shown in Fig. 2—a 27% increase that correlates with urban expansion patterns within the study area mapped in Fig. 1. This demographic pressure drives continuous habitat conversion and infrastructure development that fragments the region’s characteristic drought-resistant maquis communities (laurel, olive, thyme, sage, lavender) and altitudinal forest transitions (red pine and juniper at higher elevations)16,17.

The population trajectory illustrated in Fig. 2 provides the anthropogenic forcing function for the study’s hybrid AI models, as this demographic growth directly translates to built-up area expansion captured through NDBI increases and corresponding NDVI declines across the 2013–2023 study period. The region’s topographical precipitation gradient (1200–2000 mm from 500 m to 2000 m elevation) that naturally sustains diverse vegetation zones is being systematically disrupted by this population-driven urban sprawl, creating heat island effects measured through LST indices and altered drainage patterns that threaten species in riparian corridors where moisture-loving shrubs concentrate biodiversity hotspots18.

Data used

Satellite data

Satellite imagery provides essential data for land-use and vegetation analyses. Recent developments in cloud computing technologies have significantly enhanced the acquisition and processing capabilities of large-scale satellite datasets. Cloud-based platforms, notably Google Earth Engine (GEE), facilitate the rapid and efficient analysis of extensive archives of remote sensing data, enabling users to avoid computational overload. GEE is particularly advantageous for evaluating changes in vegetation, forests, water bodies, and agricultural landscapes using diverse satellite imagery sources, including LANDSAT and MODIS19,20.

This study utilizes data derived from Landsat 8, launched by the US Geological Survey (USGS) and NASA in 2013. Landsat 8 provides detailed spectral and visual images through its Operational Land Imager (OLI) and Thermal Infrared Sensor (TIRS). It operates at a medium spatial resolution of 15–100 m, acquiring data every 16 days across visible, near-infrared (VNIR), shortwave infrared (SWIR), and thermal infrared (TIR) bands21. LST data used in this study were provided by the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) sensor developed by NASA. MODIS was deployed on the Terra satellite in 1999 and the Aqua satellite in 2002 to monitor changes in the Earth’s surface closely. Both satellites have 36 spectral bands at wavelengths between 0.4 μm and 14.4 μm and a field of view of 2330 km, collecting data at 250 m, 500 m and 1000 m resolutions. Completing daily imaging of the Earth’s surface, MODIS measures particles in the Earth’s surface and atmosphere, as well as land cover, land use and various environmental and climatic parameters. Methodolgy

Figure 5 illustrates the methodology of the study. The first step involved collecting satellite data required for the forecasting analysis. These data were obtained as LANDSAT-derived NDVI and NDBI indices, as well as MODIS-based LST measurements, through the Google Earth Engine platform. Additionally, CORINE LULC data, which were used to validate the analysis results, were collected during the same stage. Prior to modelling, all datasets were preprocessed to enhance quality and reliability. This preprocessing included noise reduction and correction of erroneous values. Subsequently, the data were scaled to a [0, 1] range using min–max normalization, which facilitates the comparison of features on different scales and improves the learning performance of the model27. Two different modelling approaches were used for NDVI estimation. The first approach is based on LSTM networks suitable for time series forecasting. The second approach involves DWT-LSTM integration, where the data were first preprocessed with DWT and then analyzed with LSTM. This method enables the model to capture temporal and frequency information from the data more effectively. The performance of both models in predicting NDVI values was compared. Once the most successful model was identified, it was used to simulate temporal changes in biomass density (NDVI) until 2032. Finally, the predictions of this model were compared with CORINE LULC data to confirm its accuracy. The analysis results are evaluated to explain how changes in vegetation cover are shaped by urbanization and demographic changes.

Modelling

As mentioned earlier, LSTM networks are effective in time series forecasting in the literature. In this study, a hybrid model is developed that aims to enhance the analytical capabilities of LSTM by combining it with the wavelet transform. While wavelet analysis reveals features in the dataset more clearly, LSTM plays a vital role in predicting future values. This hybrid method, applied to analyse and predict NDVI changes in the Fethiye region until 2032, is crucial for assessing the future state of vegetation in the area.

LSTM model

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks are an advanced form of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) designed to overcome the vanishing gradient problem that limits the ability of standard RNNs to capture long-term dependencies in sequential data. This limitation arises from the difficulty RNNs face in retaining and propagating relevant information across extended time intervals. LSTM addresses this issue through a gated architecture that regulates the flow of information over time.

LSTM networks employ three primary gates—input, forget, and output—that collectively determine how information is stored, updated, or discarded within the network. This gating mechanism allows the model to maintain relevant temporal information over longer sequences, making LSTM particularly well-suited for complex time-series forecasting tasks such as NDVI prediction. The structure and functioning of LSTM models have been extensively validated in the literature for capturing nonlinear temporal dependencies28–30.

Discrete wavelet transform (DWT)

DWT is a powerful mathematical method for analysing the time–frequency characteristics of signals, especially in signal processing and time series analysis. Introduced in the 1980s by Jean Morlet and Alex Grossmann, DWT enables multiresolution analysis by decomposing a signal into distinct frequency bands. This is achieved by selecting an appropriate wavelet function and applying it through scaling and shifting operations to extract localised frequency and temporal information. The resulting wavelet coefficients, representing signal features at different resolutions, are often organised into a wavelet transform table to visualise the signal’s time–frequency structure. Hybrid DWT-LSTM model

This study’s developed hybrid model is based on decomposing input data, such as NDBI and LST, into seven subcomponents using DWT. This decomposition is performed by dividing the data dimension into subcomponents equal to the square root of the original data, aiming to obtain more meaningful and reduced-dimensional components. One of the critical steps affecting the model’s performance is the selection of the appropriate mother wavelet and decomposition level based on the data characteristics. In this context, the ‘d4’ wavelet from the Daubechies family has been selected due to its ability to separate patterns and structures at different scales within the data. This wavelet is widely recognized in the literature for its effectiveness in various analysis applications, and its use has significantly improved the model’s performance31.

After DWT decomposition, the output features were statistically processed and reanalyzed as input parameters for the LSTM model. This approach enhances the model’s ability to capture and process the data characteristics more effectively, thus improving overall performance. Components with a correlation value greater than 0.2 with the original NDVI data were selected through correlation analysis and reintroduced into the LSTM model, as this threshold is not only supported by previous studies32–34, but was also empirically validated during preliminary tests of the current study. Lower thresholds such as 0.1 were tested but yielded less stable model performance.

LSTM model

In the scope of this research, data collected every month over 11 years from 2013 to 2023 for the Fethiye region comprises a total of 132 monthly data points. In this study, the dataset has been divided into three subsets for training, testing, and validation purposes to evaluate the performance of the LSTM model. The ratios of data allocated to each subset are set as 60% for training, 20% for testing, and 20% for validation, respectively. A chronological time-series split was applied to preserve temporal dependencies and prevent data leakage, ensuring that future observations did not influence earlier model learning.

In order to improve the model’s accuracy, the number of hidden neurons was incrementally increased. The findings are detailed in Table 5. A maximum of 15 neurons was selected for the hidden layer to balance model complexity with the limited dataset size. Furthermore, hyperparameter optimisation was conducted by empirically tuning the learning rate (0.001–0.01), batch size (16–64), and number of epochs (100–300), aiming to minimise the validation loss and ensure stable convergence, as recommended in recent time-series forecasting studies. To strengthen the model’s generalisation ability and reduce overfitting, rolling-origin time-series cross-validation was also implemented. This technique has been shown to produce reliable error estimates in sequential data settings and was thus deemed suitable for the current application.

DWT- LSTM model

The DWT-LSTM hybrid model was developed by integrating Discrete Wavelet Transform with a Long Short-Term Memory network to improve the forecasting accuracy of NDVI. This approach allows for simultaneous analysis of both frequency components and temporal dependencies in environmental data. The implementation steps followed the methodology described in Sect. “Hybrid DWT-LSTM model”. Input variables (NDBI and LST) were first decomposed using the Daubechies-4 (db4) mother wavelet. This wavelet was selected due to its proven ability to represent non-stationary signals commonly found in satellite-derived environmental time series. The decomposition level was determined heuristically using the square root of the total number of monthly observations (√132 ≈ 11), and seven subcomponents were retained for further analysis. To prevent dimensional inflation and preserve signal relevance, only subcomponents exhibiting a Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.2 with the NDVI series were selected. This selection strategy was validated against previous studies32 and refined during preliminary testing.

Following decomposition, the selected components were normalised and used as inputs to the LSTM model. A chronological split was applied to divide the dataset into training (60%), testing (20%), and validation (20%) subsets, ensuring that the temporal order of observations was maintained and that future values did not influence earlier stages of learning. Hyperparameters such as learning rate (0.001–0.01), batch size (16–64), and number of epochs (100–300) were empirically optimised by minimising validation loss through iterative testing. In addition, rolling-origin cross-validation was employed to assess the generalisability of the model, which is particularly important in time-series forecasting.

Model performance across different hidden neuron configurations is presented in Table 7. The best results were obtained with 6 hidden neurons, achieving high accuracy during training (MSE = 0.002, R = 0.95), testing (MSE = 0.003, R = 0.94), and validation (MSE = 0.001, R = 0.96). Configurations with higher neuron counts (e.g., 12 and 15) resulted in perfect fits during training (MSE = 0, R = 0.98) but a notable drop in validation performance (R = 0.78), indicating overfitting.

Discussion

Environmental dynamics and urbanization

This study proposes an innovative hybrid methodology based on remote sensing data for predicting long-term changes in the vegetation cover of Fethiye, one of Türkiye’s most popular holiday destinations in the Muğla province. CORINE LULC data and demographic indicators were utilised to gain an in-depth understanding of urban–ecological interactions. This multidimensional framework enabled the analysis of how urban expansion has altered the natural landscape between 2013 and 2023, using remote sensing indices such as NDVI, LST, and NDBI.

Since the 1980s, tourism incentives and associated investments along Türkiye’s Mediterranean coast have significantly transformed the Fethiye district. Between 1980 and 2021, the population increased by 26.8%, placing Fethiye among the top ten districts in Türkiye in terms of demographic growth35. The resulting tourism-led urbanisation has triggered considerable internal migration and spatial restructuring of settlement patterns. A comparable phenomenon was observed by Qian et al.36 in Zhapu, China, where tourism investments spurred population growth and infrastructure expansion, illustrating a broader global pattern linking tourism and urban growth.

This tourism-led demographic growth has produced observable spatial consequences for land use patterns in Fethiye. To quantify the extent of landscape transformation associated with urban expansion, CORINE LULC data were analysed for the period between 2006 and 2018. The results reveal that artificial surfaces in the district increased by 16.1%, while agricultural land decreased by 2% and wetlands declined by 21.43%. These shifts directly support the remote-sensing-based NDVI projections and indicate that the expansion of artificial areas constitutes the principal driver of the projected 14% decline in NDVI. NDVI values were derived from Landsat and MODIS imagery and forecast through the DWT–LSTM model; CORINE classifications were subsequently employed to evaluate the environmental validity of the model outputs. In particular, the strong negative correlation between artificial-surface expansion and NDVI (r = − 0.93) clearly demonstrates that urban-induced land-cover conversions lead to vegetation loss. The concomitant reductions in agricultural and wetland classes further corroborate this transformation. The model’s low MAPE value of 5.2% confirms its statistical accuracy, while the spatial correspondence with CORINE data substantiates the ecological coherence of the prediction. Consequently, the expansion of artificial surfaces emerges as the predominant determinant of the anticipated NDVI decline.

In addition to land use changes, climatic variables have also exerted a significant influence on vegetation patterns in the region.Climatic drivers, particularly rising temperatures and altered precipitation regimes, have also played a critical role in shaping vegetation dynamics. According to TSMS data, the average annual temperature in the region increased by approximately 1.3 °C between 2006 and 2023. This warming trend has intensified evapotranspiration and depleted soil moisture, particularly during dry seasons. In parallel, erratic precipitation patterns and frequent dry spells—especially between 2014 and 2020—have contributed to the degradation of vegetation cover. These shifts coincide temporally and spatially with the observed NDVI decline, underscoring the compounding impact of climatic stressors on land use change. Hence, climate change should be regarded as a co-driver of vegetation decline alongside urban pressures.

To mitigate the vegetation decline projected by the DWT-LSTM model—particularly in low-elevation areas adjacent to expanding urban zones—ecological restoration strategies such as afforestation are essential. Empirical studies have demonstrated that afforestation can effectively reverse fire-related vegetation losses and facilitate long-term ecosystem recovery37,38. The current model outputs corroborate these findings, indicating that areas adjacent to artificial surfaces are particularly vulnerable. Accordingly, spatially-informed afforestation efforts guided by predictive modelling should be prioritised to enhance the region’s ecological resilience under mounting anthropogenic and climatic pressures. In addition to ecological degradation, the spatial consequences of urbanisation in Fethiye also point to rising social pressures that merit further examination. Although this study primarily focused on vegetation dynamics, rapid urban expansion and declining NDVI values may indirectly reflect increased inequality in access to green spaces, growing infrastructure strain, and the displacement of vulnerable communities—particularly in tourism-driven zones. The strong correlation between artificial surface expansion and ecological degradation may be a proxy for broader socioeconomic transformations. Therefore, future research should incorporate social indicators—such as housing accessibility, land use conflicts, and environmental justice metrics—to holistically assess urbanisation’s dual impact on ecological and human systems.

In order to statistically substantiate the observed landscape trends, a Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between CORINE LULC classes and satellite-derived indices (NDBI, NDVI, and LST) for the years 2012 and 2018. The results revealed a strong positive correlation between artificial surfaces and NDBI (r = 0.93), as well as with LST (r = 0.87). In contrast, artificial surfaces exhibited a strong negative correlation with NDVI (r = − 0.93), indicating a consistent decline in vegetation health associated with urban expansion. While CORINE data alone did not display dramatic shifts in land cover proportions, the satellite-based indices captured subtle yet ecologically meaningful changes in surface conditions. These correlation findings reinforce the predictive power of the hybrid deep learning models and lend empirical support to the projected NDVI decline, highlighting the role of increasing anthropogenic pressures.

The results align with documented ecological pressures in other Mediterranean coastal cities. For instance, remote sensing analysis in Belek (Antalya coast) reported a significant NDVI decline, associated with expanding tourism-related urbanisation in golf resort areas39. Similarly, a study in Konyaaltı demonstrated that urban green spaces lowered land surface temperatures in Antalya by up to 0.77 °C—highlighting the cooling benefits of green infrastructure40. Furthermore, broader regional tracking of Mediterranean coastal zones has shown strong negative correlations between built-up area expansion and vegetative health (i.e., lower NDVI), mirroring the patterns observed in this study41. In comparison, Fethiye’s predicted 14% reduction in NDVI by 2032 places it among the most ecologically vulnerable Mediterranean holiday destinations, underscoring the acute environmental pressures it faces.

In addition to vegetation decline, urban land cover transformation often triggers broader environmental consequences, including deteriorating air quality, intensified urban heat island effects, and decreased carbon storage. Saha et al.42 highlighted these dynamics in emerging South Asian cities, where rapid land use changes were found to significantly impact air pollution levels and urban ecosystem services. Although the present study focuses primarily on vegetation dynamics, the observed reduction in NDVI and expansion of impervious surfaces in Fethiye may similarly signal rising environmental pressures beyond ecological degradation alone. This reinforces the need for integrated land-use planning approaches that account for multiple, interconnected urban sustainability challenges. In this context, it is recommended that local authorities in Fethiye adopt proactive urban planning policies that limit further impervious surface expansion, incorporate green infrastructure, and prioritise ecological buffers around high-risk zones.

Furthermore, Kafy et al.43 provided additional evidence on how urbanisation, surface temperature increase, and carbon stock depletion interact in coastal regions. Their study utilised machine learning algorithms to dynamically model LULC change, LST elevation, and carbon loss in Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh, demonstrating that coastal proximity moderates temperature increases and that regions with high urbanisation exhibit steep declines in vegetation and carbon reserves. These findings reinforce the relevance of applying machine learning-based forecasting approaches in data-scarce coastal zones and validate the integrated methodological framework adopted in the current study.

Hybrid modelling for future predictions

This study focuses on the predictions of vegetation biomass in the Fethiye region up to the year 2032. The primary objective of the research is to accurately predict vegetation biomass by effectively addressing the complexity introduced by environmental variability. In this context, modeling has initially been conducted using LSTM algorithm. LSTM is a method that has shown high success in the analysis of time series data and in learning long-term contexts. The selection of this method is aimed at overcoming the challenges encountered in prediction studies and taking into account the effects of environmental variability44.

However, in the later stages of the study, it was considered that the 87% success rate provided by the LSTM algorithm could be further improved. To this end, a hybrid model integrating Wavelet analysis with LSTM has been developed to enhance the accuracy and robustness of the model. Wavelet analysis, with its ability to distinguish high and low-frequency information within data, has the potential to increase the predictive success of the LSTM model on time series data. This integration has enabled the model to learn both short-term fluctuations and long-term trends more effectively.

The 95% success rate obtained as a result of hybrid modeling demonstrates the effectiveness of this innovative approach. This rate represents a 9.1% increase in success compared to the LSTM model. This increase indicates that the hybrid model is better equipped to handle the challenges brought by environmental variability and has provided a significant improvement in predicting vegetation biomass in the Fethiye region45,46.

In conclusion, the findings of this study highlight the methodological innovation and superiority provided by hybrid modeling in vegetation biomass predictions. Furthermore, the advantages offered by the hybrid model in more accurately addressing the complexities associated with environmental variability will serve as an important reference point for future similar studies. Therefore, the importance of employing hybrid modeling approaches in ecological and environmental research should be emphasized.

Given the demonstrated success of the DWT-LSTM model in capturing vegetation dynamics within the Mediterranean context of Fethiye, it is suggested that this hybrid approach may offer analytical value when adapted to other bioclimatic zones, such as semi-arid inland basins, temperate coastal belts, or rapidly urbanising subtropical regions. While further empirical validation is required, it is anticipated that the model’s capability to integrate temporal complexity and spatial heterogeneity could be beneficial in forecasting vegetation changes across diverse ecological settings. Therefore, the methodological framework proposed in this study may contribute to broader environmental modelling efforts and inform decision-making processes in regions facing similar anthropogenic and climatic pressures.

Conclusions and future work

This study has been conducted to analyze the changes in vegetation biomass in the Fethiye region over the years and to predict its future up to 2032. The research proposes a hybrid model developed with LSTM and Wavelet analysis integration to address the complex effects of environmental variability effectively. The findings indicate that the hybrid modelling approach achieved a prediction accuracy of 95%, with a 9.1% increase in success compared to the application of the LSTM algorithm alone. These results demonstrate the hybrid model’s capacity for more accurate predictions on time series data and its robustness against environmental variability.

This study has provided significant insights into the long-term predictions of vegetation biomass in the Fethiye region; (i) The hybrid modelling approach has proven its effectiveness for long-term predictions of vegetation biomass; (ii) The model has provided critical data for the conservation and restoration of the region’s natural habitat by analyzing the effects of unexpected environmental changes, such as the forest fire in Fethiye in 2021; (iii) Additionally, the importance of restoration efforts, such as afforestation, in significantly contributing to the re-establishment of the region’s ecological balance has been emphasized.

For future research, several objectives will be explored: (i) An in-depth examination of the impact of various environmental factors diversifying the model’s performance is planned. This is a critical step to assess the model’s sensitivity and adaptability to different environmental variables; (ii) Following advancements in artificial intelligence and machine learning, a comparative analysis of the effects of alternative algorithms and broader and more diverse datasets on the model’s learning and prediction capacity is anticipated. This approach aims to transcend the limits of current modelling techniques and maximize prediction success; (iii) Testing the applicability and generalizability of the model in regions with different climatic conditions and physiographic features is intended. These tests will determine how flexible and responsive the model is to various environmental scenarios, thereby revealing the model’s potential for universal application.
