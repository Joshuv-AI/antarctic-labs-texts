Genome-wide analysis of tobacco NtTOM1/TOM3 gene family and identification of NtTOM1a/ NtTOM3a response to tobacco mosaic virus
Authors: Xuebo Wang, Yalin Bai, Zhan Shen, Xinyao Zhang, Changchun Cai, Chan Qiao, Caihong Jiang, Lirui Cheng, Dan Liu, Aiguo Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTOBAMOVIRUS MULTIPLICATION 1 (TOM1) and its homolog TOBAMOVIRUS MULTIPLICATION 3 (TOM3) play a prominent role in the multiplication of tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) in higher plants. Although homologs of NtTOM1/TOM3 genes have been identified in several plant species, little is known about the characteristics and functions of NtTOM1/TOM3 at the genome-wide level in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.).ResultsIn this study, we performed genome-wide identification and expression pattern analysis of the tobacco NtTOM1/TOM3 gene family. Twelve NtTOM1/TOM3 genes were identified and classified into four groups based on phylogenetic analysis. Sequence and conserved domain analyses showed that all these genes contained a specific DUF1084 domain. Expression pattern analysis showed that NtTOM1a, NtTOM1b, NtTOM1d, NtTOM3a, NtTOM3b, and NtTOM3d were induced by TMV at 1-, 3-, and 9 dpi, whereas the expression of other genes was not responsive to TMV at the early infection stage. TMV virion accumulation showed no obvious difference in either nttom1a or nttom3a mutants compared with the wild type. However, the virus propagation was significantly, but not completely, inhibited in the nttom1atom3a double mutant, indicating that other gene family members may function redundantly, such as NtTOM1b and NtTOM1d. In addition, overexpression of NtTOM1a or NtTOM3a also inhibited the TMV replication to some extent.ConclusionsThe present study performed genome-wide analysis of the NtTOM1/TOM3 gene family in tobacco, and identified NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a as important genes involved in TMV multiplication based on functional analysis. These results provide a theoretical basis for further improving TMV resistance in tobacco.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05632-1.Keywords: Tobamovirus multiplication protein, TOM1, TOM3, Tobacco mosaic virus, Tobacco

Background

Tobamoviruses, of which there are more than 30, including tobacco mosaic virus (TMV), tomato mosaic virus (ToMV), and youcai mosaic virus (YoMV, also known as TMV-Cg), pose a huge threat to host plant growth, fruit quality, and grain yield [1]. Among these, TMV is widespread and threatens many economically important crops, including tobacco, tomato, and pepper [2, 3]. Agricultural and biological controls are usually time-consuming, environmentally hazardous, or slow to take effect [4, 5]. To better control the TMV damage in crops, identifying susceptible host factors and engineering them without altering other traits would be an ideal strategy.

Viruses recruit or co-opt host factors for multiplication to complete their infection cycle. Recessive mutations or quantitative changes to host factors lead to a wide range of effects on viral multiplication. For example, the mutation of yeast DED1 gene block the replication of the brome mosaic virus (BMV) RNA [6, 7]. The TMV genome consists of a positive single-stranded RNA (+ ssRNA) of approximately 6395 nucleotides [8] which encodes at least four proteins: 126 kDa and 183 kDa replication proteins, a 30 kDa movement protein (MP), and a 17 kDa coat protein (CP) [9–11]. The 126 kDa and 183 kDa replication proteins are necessary for viral replication and the 126 kDa replication protein can also suppress the gene silencing effects of host defenses [12]. Several host factors, including Eukaryotic translation elongation factor 1 A (eEF1A) [13–15], Eukaryotic translation elongation factor 1B (eEF1B) [16], TOBAMOVIRUS MULTIPLICATION 1 (TOM1) [17], TOBAMOVIRUS MULTIPLICATION 3 (TOM3) [18], and TOBAMOVIRUS MULTIPLICATION 2 A (TOM2A) [19] have been shown to participate in tobamovirus multiplication.

The TOM1 and TOM3 genes of Arabidopsis thaliana (AtTOM1 and AtTOM3, respectively), encode proteins which are essential constituents of the replication complex of tobamoviruses and function in parallel to tether the replication complex to the cell membranes [20]. AtTOM1 interacts with the helicase domain of the tobamovirus replication protein and is necessary for the efficient intracellular multiplication of tobamoviruses. Efficient multiplication of TMV-Cg and TMV-L was inhibited in an A. thaliana tom1 loss-of-function mutant [17]. However, mutation of AtTOM1 cannot completely inhibit the replication of TMV, indicating that other genes may also play a positive role in tobamovirus multiplication. AtTOM3 interacts with both AtTOM1 and the helicase domain of the tobamovirus replication protein and plays a parallel role in assisting the replication of TMV [18]. In the A. thaliana double mutant tom1-1tom3-1, the TOM1 gene carried a single nuclear recessive mutation and TOM3 gene carried a nonsense mutation in the first exon, TMV replication is almost completely inhibited [18]. TOM2A, which is not a homolog of TOM1 or TOM3, can also interact with TOM1 and is an integral constituent of the replication complex [4, 19]. The A. thaliana tom2a mutant, which has a single nuclear recessive mutation, the multiplication of TMV-Cg and ToMV is significantly reduced [21–23]. In ToMV replication, TOM1 and a host small GTP-binding protein ARL8 can form a complex along with ToMV replication proteins, this complex can play crucial role in the RNA synthesis activation and the replication proteins capping function [24].

In common tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.), two homologs of AtTOM1 and AtTOM3 have been characterized as NtTOM1 and NtTOM3 [20]. By inhibiting the NtTOM1 and NtTOM3 expression simultaneously with RNA interference (RNAi), TMV replication was almost completely inhibited in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.) [20]. However, there may be functionally complementary or redundant homologous NtTOM1 and NtTOM3 genes since N. tabacum is an allotetraploid species (2n 4x 48) that arises from hybridization between N. tomentosiformis (TT) and N. sylvestris (SS). Although previous studies have shown that the role of NtTOM1 and NtTOM3 is to assist TMV replication, it remains unclear how many homologous genes perform the functions of TOM1 or TOM3 in tobacco.

Here, we performed genome-wide identification of all NtTOM1/TOM3 genes in the tobacco genome (Nicotiana tabacum L.) [25] by investigating the phylogenetic relationships, domain structures, and analyzing their expression patterns under TMV infection. Moreover, we clarified the function of two NtTOM1/TOM3 homologs by using the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (CRISPR/Cas9) system. We further explored the functions of NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a by inducing overexpression under the control of the cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35 S promoter. Our results suggested that the NtTOM1/TOM3 genes have the similar functions, and other gene family members may compensate for the function of these two genes. The present study provides insights into future functional studies of NtTOM1/TOM3 genes that may be valuable for tobacco breeding.

Results

Identification of tobacco NtTOM1/TOM3 gene family

Twelve putative NtTOM1/TOM3 genes were identified in the tobacco genome and named NtTOM1a–NtTOM1h and NtTOM3a–NtTOM3d, according to the homology between AtTOM and NtTOM (Table 1). The characteristics of these 12 genes, including the exon number, coding sequence (CDS) length, protein length, molecular mass (kDa), and isoelectric point (pI), were analyzed. The exon numbers ranged from four (NtTOM3b) to 15 (NtTOM1h). The CDS lengths ranged from 357 bp (NtTOM3b) to 1332 bp (NtTOM1h), with corresponding polypeptides of 119–444 amino acids. The NtTOM1h protein possessed the highest MW 49.13 kDa, while the smallest MW was found in NtTOM3b (13.89 kDa). The theoretical pI ranged from 8.7 (NtTOM1c) to 9.99 (NtTOM3a) (Table 1). In addition, subcellular localization of NtTOM1/TOM3 proteins was predicted using Cell-PLoc 2.0 [26] (Table 1). The predicted locations of the eight NtTOM1/TOM3 proteins were the cell membrane. NtTOM1g and NtTOM1h were predicted to be localized to multiple organelles, including the cell membrane, chloroplasts, and nucleus. NtTOM3b was predicted to be localized to the chloroplasts and nucleus, whereas NtTOM1b was predicted to be localized to the chloroplasts only.

Phylogenetic analysis of the NtTOM1/TOM3 gene family

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the NtTOM1/TOM3 proteins were divided into four groups: I, II, III, and IV (Fig. 1). Group I contained four tobacco proteins (NtTOM3a, NtTOM3b, NtTOM3c, and NtTOM3d), which were clustered closely to AtTOM3, AtTHH1, SlTOM1a, and SlTOM1d. Group II contained four tobacco NtTOM1/TOM3 proteins (NtTOM1a, NtTOM1b, NtTOM1c, and NtTOM1d), which were clustered closely to AtTOM1, SlTOM1c, and SlTOM1e. Group III contained two tobacco proteins (NtTOM1e and NtTOM1f) which clustered closely with SlTOM1b. Group IV contained two tobacco proteins (NtTOM1g and NtTOM1h), which clustered closely with SlTOM1.

The 2,000 bps upstream of the start codon were analyzed for cis-regulatory elements of tobacco NtTOM1/TOM3. The results showed that the promoters of the NtTOM1/TOM3 gene family contain a large number of stress-related cis-elements, including abscisic acid, MeJA, salicylic acid, gibberellin, low temperature, drought, and defense and stress responsiveness element, and anaerobic induction element, (Fig. 2C) (Table S3). To elucidate the structural characteristics of NtTOM1/TOM3 genes in tobacco, we analyzed the exon-intron organization of NtTOM1/TOM3 genes. The results showed that NtTOM1/TOM3 gene structures varied from gene to gene, suggesting gene diversification in the process of evolution (Fig. 2D).

Chromosomal distribution of the NtTOM1/TOM3 genes

The chromosomal positions of the NtTOM1/TOM3 genes were determined based on their physical location in the tobacco genome. Only six genes were located on the chromosomes: NtTOM1g on chromosome Nt03, NtTOM1e on chromosome Nt07, NtTOM3d on chromosome Nt09, NtTOM3b on chromosome Nt12, NtTOM1a on chromosome Nt17, and NtTOM1f on chromosome Nt19. Six other genes were located on different scaffolds owing to the incomplete assembly of the tobacco reference genome (Version: N. tabacum v1.0 Edwards 2017) (Fig. S3).

Expression pattern analysis of NtTOM1/TOM3 genes response to TMV infection

Previous studies have shown that AtTOM1 and AtTOM3 are involved in tobamovirus replication [18]. To identify the response of tobacco NtTOM1/TOM3 genes to TMV infection, we detected the expression of individual members of the NtTOM1/TOM3 gene family exposed to TMV infection at 0-, 1-, 3-, and 9 dpi. To confirm the TMV accumulation of the test and control samples, we detected the expression of TMV CP using RT-PCR, and virus accumulation was only detected in test samples at 1-, 3-, and 9 dpi (Fig. S4). The expression data showed different expression patterns between the four groups, indicating the diverse functions of the NtTOM1/TOM3 genes in response to TMV infection in tobacco (Fig. 3). Members of Groups I and II were strongly responsive to TMV infection compared to the other two groups (Fig. 3). The expression of NtTOM1a, NtTOM1b, NtTOM1d, and NtTOM3a, NtTOM3b, and NtTOM3d was induced at the initial infection stage and was maintained at a relatively high level. The expression of NtTOM3a, NtTOM3b, and NtTOM3d increased at 3 dpi. The expression of genes in Groups III and IV was induced to a lesser extent by TMV infection. NtTOM1e, NtTOM1f, and NtTOM1g were induced at 9 dpi, whereas the expression of NtTOM1h did not change with TMV infection (Fig. 3). Among these genes, NtTOM1a, NtTOM1b, NtTOM1d, NtTOM3a and NtTOM3d showed continuous responses to TMV infection, the expression was induced on the 1st, 3rd, and 9th day after virus infection. NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a correspond to NtTOM1 (Genbank Accession No. AB193039) and NtTOM3 (Genbank Accession No. AB193040), respectively. To further investigate the function of these two genes under TMV infection, we chose NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a for further functional analysis.

Subcellular localization of NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a

The predicted location of the NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a proteins was at the cell membrane (Table 1). To further investigate the subcellular localization of NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a, 35S:: NtTOM1a-eGFP and 35S:: NtTOM3a-eGFP expression vectors (Fig. 4A) were transformed into N. benthamiana epidermal leaves to determine the localization of the GFP fluorescence signal. The resulting fluorescence signals of NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a colocalized with the mCherry signal from the membrane marker (Fig. 4B). These results demonstrated that NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a are localized to the membrane.

Functional identification of NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a under TMV infection

To study the role of NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a in the TMV infection response, gene knockout and overexpression tobacco lines were constructed (Fig. S5A, B). Initially, two single-gene mutants (designated nttom1a and nttom3a) (Fig. 5A) were used for TMV inoculation analysis. At 9 dpi, the systemic leaves of nttom1a and nttom3a mutants displayed TMV symptoms (Fig. 5B), and western bloting (WB) analysis demonstrated that the TMV CP contents were lower than the wild type (Fig. 5C), indicating that neither NtTOM1a nor NtTOM3a knockout confers efficient TMV resistance. Two independent double mutant lines (designated nttom1atom3a-1 and nttom1atom3a-2) were selected to analyze TMV resistance (Fig. 6A). The double mutant lines exhibited no obvious symptoms in systemic leaves, whereas the systemic leaves of the wild type showed distortion and tobacco mosaic symptoms (Fig. 6C). To test virus accumulation, the CP content in inoculated leaves and systemic leaves was detected using WB. The CP content was strongly reduced in inoculated leaves at 3 dpi and in systemic leaves at 9 dpi in double mutants compared to that in the wild type (Fig. 6D). After 40 dpi, however, the two pieces of systemic leaves of the double mutants showed severe distortion and tobacco mosaic symptoms, like the wild type (Fig. S6). These results indicated that the inhibition of TMV replication mediated by loss-of-function of NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a is not sufficient to produce durable resistance to TMV.

Discussion

Virus multiplication requires multiple host factors to cooperate with the factors encoded by the virus genome [20]. Previous studies have shown that TOM1 and TOM3 are host factors that contribute to TMV replication in plants [18]. However, the NtTOM1/TOM3 gene family members and their functions in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.) need to be further explored. In the current study, genome-wide identification revealed 12 NtTOM1/TOM3 genes in Nicotiana tabacum, including 2 more clusters of NtTOM1/TOM3 genes than in Arabidopsis thaliana (Fig. 1). Thus, ancestral genes of Groups III and IV appeared to be lost in the Arabidopsis lineage. This indicated that NtTOM1/TOM3 could have evolved unique adaptations in tobacco. All 12 genes possess the DUF1084 domain (Fig. 2B), members of this domain are part of the G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR) superfamily and are involved in stress tolerance [28]. These results indicated that the NtTOM1/TOM3 protein family may be important for responses to biotic and/or abiotic stress, which is necessary for gene function in stress tolerance [28].

Subcellular localization refers to the localization of a protein or expression product to a specific location in the cell, thus providing research directions for understanding the mechanisms of gene function [29]. Most of the NtTOM1/TOM3 gene family members were predicted to localize specifically to the cell membrane, while NtTOM1b, NtTOM1g, NtTOM1h, and NtTOM3b were predicted to be localized to the chloroplasts and/or nuclei (Table 1). The cell membrane localization of the two proteins, NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a, was further verified using fluorescence signals (Fig. 4). The different subcellular localizations indicated that tobacco NtTOM1/TOM3 family members could play diverse functions in tobacco. Analysis of cis-acting elements in gene promoter regions can reveal the potential biological functions of genes [30]. Our results showed that NtTOM1/TOM3 genes contained a variety of hormone and abiotic responsiveness elements (Fig. 2C), indicating that tobacco NtTOM1/TOM3 family members could play important role in regulating stress responses or be regulated by biotic and abiotic stresses. In addition, our results demonstrated that different NtTOM1/TOM3 family members respond differently to TMV infection (Fig. 3). Four members showed a significant induction of expression with TMV infection, and the expression level increased at 1- and 3 dpi, and then decreased at 9 dpi. In contrast, expression of NtTOM1e, NtTOM1f, and NtTOM1g was induced at 9 dpi. These results suggested that Groups I and II members might play vital role in TMV replication in tobacco and that different members function at different stages of TMV infection.

TMV is a widely distributed viral disease that causes considerable yield loss in tobacco, and several resistance genes against TMV have been identified in Solanaceae. For example, Tm-1 is a semidominant resistance gene isolated from tomato plants, which prevents the formation of a replication complex by binding to ToMV and TMV replication protein [31–33]. Tm-2 confers resistance to TMV by interacting with viral CP to restrict its function [34]. Tm-22, isolated from tomato plants, confers extreme resistance against TMV by recognizing TMV movement protein (MP) [35–37]. Tm-1, Tm-2, and Tm-22 have been introduced into commercial tomato cultivars to protect them from infection [38]. In tobacco, the main TMV resistance gene used in breeding is the gene N which is dominant and temperature-sensitive [39, 40]. Recessive resistance generated by loss-of-function mutations has proved to be a stable and reliable strategy to prevent virus infection. In this study, we precisely edited the NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a genes belonging to Groups II and I, respectively, to study the functions of these two genes during TMV infection. nttom1a and nttom3a mutants showed tobacco mosaic symptoms at 9 dpi, like those seen in the wild type (Fig. 5B). Simultaneous editing of NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a genes resulted in delayed virus accumulation at the early stage of infection (Fig. 6C and D) but could not inhibit TMV replication at 40 dpi (Fig. S6). Besides, the mosaic phenotype has always existed. These results indicated that NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a may function in parallel to support TMV replication, and that other NtTOM1/TOM3 family members might be involved in assisting TMV replication. Our expression pattern analysis indicated that other members such as NtTOM1b and NtTOM1d were also induced by TMV infection. Therefore, these two genes may be also involved in TMV replication. Previous studies have shown that simultaneous RNAi against NtTOM1 and NtTOM3 in N. tabacum leads to almost complete inhibition of tobamovirus without affecting plant growth [18, 20]. A recent study showed that the knockout of four SlTOM1 genes in tomato plants (S. Lycopersicum) simultaneously using CRISPR/Cas9 conferred complete resistance to Tomato brown rugose fruit virus (ToBRFV) [41]. Knockout of both SlTOM1a and SlTOM3 (also known as SlTOM1d) results in an asymptomatic response to ToBRFV but has no influence on TMV and ToMV infection [42]. Editing of NtTOM1 gene using CRISPR/Cas9 in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum cv. Petit Havana) confered high TMV resistance after 20 days of TMV inoculation [43], which was different from our research. Perhaps due to the different genotypes of germplasm collections, the resistance to TMV also varies. The TMV phenotype of the wild type in Jogam’s study was not typical actually [43]. The tobacco germplasm Nicotiana tabacum cv. K326 displayed typical mosaic phenotype in our study (Figs. 5B and 6C, S6), implied that K326 is more appropriate for the research of NtTOM1/TOM3 genes. Our results predicted that simultaneous knockout of the NtTOM1/TOM3 genes indicates a potential route to generating recessive resistance to TMV. These results provide a theoretical basis and technical support in the development of TMV resistance techniques.

In most cases, overexpression of host factors for virus multiplication enhances plant sensitivity to pathogens. However, a previous study showed that the TOM1 overexpression inhibits ToMV propagation by enhancing RNAi in tobacco [27]. The soluble form of the TMV 126 kDa replication protein in ToMV serves as a viral suppressor of RNAi to confront host RNAi defense. When TOM1 was overexpressed, replication of the soluble form of 126 kDa decreased, and host RNAi was strengthened [27]. Plant RNAi-mediated resistance is one of the most well-studied mechanisms for plant resistance to viruses. RNA-dependent RNA polymerase 1 (RDR1) and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase 6 (RDR6) protect against viral infection by synthesizing a new population of virus-specific dsRNA, which is then cleaved by DICER-LIKE (DCL) proteins and processed into sRNA of 18–25 bases long. Mature sRNA binds to Argonaute proteins to form an RNA-induced silencing complex to direct the cleavage of viral genomic RNA that is complementary to viral sRNA, in order to silence target genes [44–47]. Dicer enzymes such as DCL1 and DCL3 function at the core of RNA silencing mechanisms to defend against exogenous RNA or to regulate endogenous genes [48, 49]. In the present study, both NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a overexpression lines showed attenuated TMV CP accumulation and disease symptoms compared to the wild type (Fig. 6C and D), which is consistent with previous research results [27]. Furthermore, the relative expression levels of NtRDR1, NtRDR6, NtDCL1, and NtDCL3 were significantly increased in upper systematic leaves, leading to enhanced host RNAi defense.

Materials and methods

Plant material and virus inoculation

The tobacco cultivar Nicotiana tabacum cv. K326 was grown in a greenhouse with a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod, 26/20°C day/night temperatures, and 70% relative humidity at the Tobacco Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Qingdao, China. Six-week-old seedlings were used for viral inoculation and expression analyses. The common strain of tobacco mosaic virus (TMV-U1) was used for virus inoculation, and mechanical inoculation was performed as described by Holler [50].

Identification of NtTOM1/TOM3 gene family

The genome annotation file for common tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.) [25] was downloaded from the Sol Genomics Network (SGN) (https://www.sgn.cornell.edu/organism/Nicotiana_tabacum/genome). The AtTOM1 (At4g21790) and AtTOM3 (At2g02180) protein sequences were used as query sequences for BlastP searches with an E-value threshold of < 1E-20 against the N. tabacum genome database. The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) configuration file for the THH1/TOM/TOM3 domain (PF06454) from the Pfam database (https://pfam.xfam.org/) was also used as a query to identify all THH1/TOM/TOM3 domain-containing sequences in tobacco. TBtools software [51] was used to obtain the sequences for NtTOM1/TOM3 gene family. The online programs NCBI-CCD (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd) and SMART [52] (http://smart.emblheidelberg.de/) were used to determine gene family members. The online program ExPASy-ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) was used to analyze the isoelectric point (pI), molecular mass, and exon numbers of these 12 genes.

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis

The tobacco NtTOM1/TOM3 protein sequences together with the protein sequences of AtTOM1, AtTOM3, and AtTHH1 (At1g14530) ( the homolog of AtTOM1 and AtTOM3) in A. thaliana, and SlTOM1 (XP_010316915), SlTOM1a (Solyc04g008540), SlTOM1b (Solyc01g105270), SlTOM1c (Solyc02g080370), SlTOM1d (Solyc01g007900), and SlTOM1e (Solyc09g005240) in tomato plants (Solanum lycopersicum) were used to investigate the phylogenetic relationships (Table S2). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using ClustalW program in MEGA11.0 software, and the phylogenetic tree was constructed using the maximum likelihood (ML) with 1000 bootstraps replications. The tree was visualized using EVOLVIEW [53] (https://evolgenius.info/).

Analysis of gene structure and cis-acting elements

The online program MEME (http://meme-suite.org/) was used to identify conserved protein motifs in the NtTOM1/TOM3 family. The conserved domains of the NtTOM1/TOM3 proteins were determined using CD SEARCH of NCBI. For cis-acting element analysis, DNA sequences of the region 2,000 bp upstream from the 5’ end of the gene coding region were extracted from the tobacco reference genome database [25] and analyzed using the PlantCARE database [54] (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/). Gene structures were analyzed using the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS2.0) [55] (http://gsds.gao-lab.org/index.php). The resulting maps were drawn using TBtools software [51].

Chromosome location

The tobacco (N. tabacum) reference genome gff3 file [25] was downloaded from the Sol Genomics Network (SGN) (https://www.sgn.cornell.edu/organism/Nicotiana_tabacum/genome), and NtTOM1/TOM3 gene identity files were used to analyze the chromosome positions of the NtTOM1/TOM3 gene family using the TBtools software [51].

RNA extraction, reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR), and reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The inoculated leaves were harvested at 0-, 1-, 3-, and 9 days post inoculation (dpi) for the determination of virus accumulation and gene expression. The TMV-free tobacco leaves were harvested at 0-, 1-, 3-, and 9 days in the same condition as a control. Total RNA was extracted using the Plant Total RNA Kit ZP405 (Beijing Zoman Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration of total RNA was determined using the NanoDrop ND-2000, 1 µg of total RNA was used to synthesize first-strand cDNA using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, China). The primers used for RT-PCR and qRT-PCR (Table S1) were designed using the Primer-BLAST online tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/index.cgi). RT-PCR was conducted by using 2 × Phanta Flash Master Mix (Dye Plus) (Vazyme, China) and qRT-PCR was performed using the ChamQ SYBR Color qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The gene expression levels were normalized to those of the tobacco actin gene and quantified using the 2−∆∆CT method [56]. The results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates, each sample performed three technical repeats.

Vector construction

The gene editing vector pkse401 was a gift from Qijun Chen (Addgene plasmid # 62202), and pkse401-NtTOM1aTOM3a was constructed according to the method of the giver [57] to edit NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a using CRISPR/Cas9. Single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting a region within exon 1 of NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a CDS sequences were amplified from the N. tabacum cv. K326 and cloned into the pEASY®-Blunt-Zero vector [58] (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). To construct the overexpression vector, the CDS sequences of NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a were cloned into the BGV008 expression vector. For subcellular localization analysis, the CDS sequences of NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a without a stop codon were cloned into the N-terminus enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) driven by the CaMV 35S promoter. Different constructs were individually transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. All the primers used for vector construction are listed in Table S1.

Subcellular localizations of NtTOM1a and NtTOM3a

The Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain containing 35S::NtTOM1a-eGFP, 35S:: NtTOM3a-eGFP constructs, and AtPIP2-mCherry (cell membrane marker) [59] were resuspended in infiltration buffer (1 M MgCl2, 1 M MES and 0.2 mM acetosyringone). 35S::NtTOM1a-eGFP and 35S:: NtTOM3a-eGFP were then co-infiltrated with AtPIP2-mCherry into N. benthamiana epidermal leaves. Approximately 48–60 h after infiltration, the transfected leaves were examined using a LEICA Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope (TCS SP8) [60].

Agrobacterium tumefaciens transformation of tobacco

Tobacco transformation was performed using the leaf disc method as described previously [61]. Leaf discs from five-week-old tobacco plants were infected with Agrobacterium strain EHA105 harboring the indicated vector for constructing transgenic tobacco. The T1 generation of overexpression lines and T2 generation of knockout transgenic lines were used for virus inoculation analysis. Specific primers used for gene expression detection and targeted mutant identification are listed in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Inoculated leaves at 3 dpi and upper systemic leaves at 9 dpi were collected to isolate the total protein. Total proteins for the western blot (WB) assay were extracted from leaf tissues using a Thermo Scientific Protein Extraction Kit ( Thermo Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The TMV coat protein (CP) was detected using a specific antibody against TMV CP (Agdia, USA), and β-actin was used as an internal reference. WB was performed according to the method described by Guo and Yang [62, 63]. The intensity of each band was measured using ImageJ [64].

Statistical analysis

Values are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For expression analysis upon TMV infection, the data were subjected to Student’s t-test analysis using statistical software Prism8 (version 8.2.1. GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All the other statistical analysis were performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) of Prism8 software (version 8.2.1. GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Significance is labeled as: **** (p < 0.0001), *** (p < 0.001), ** (p < 0.01), and * (p < 0.05).

Conclusion

In this study, 12 NtTOM1/TOM3 genes were identified in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.), and their characteristics were systematically analyzed, this included their evolutionary relationships, gene structures, conserved domains, conserved motifs, cis-acting elements, and chromosomal locations. The expression pattern analysis demonstrated that the expression of members of Groups I and IV was induced by TMV infection. Simultaneous knockout of NtTOM1a or NtTOM3a results in reduced TMV accumulation in the early stages of infection. Overexpression of NtTOM1a or NtTOM3a attenuated TMV accumulation in a similar way to the nttom1atom3a mutant. This study provides valuable information for the systematic analysis of the function of NtTOM1/TOM3 genes in N. tabacum and provides a strong foundation for further investigations on the function of NtTOM1/TOM3 genes in response to TMV infection in tobacco.
