Dapsone induced hemolysis and drop in saturation in normal glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase level patients – alarming presentation of poor man’s drug: A series of 12 cases and review of literature
Authors: Swetalina Pradhan, Gaurav Dash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Dapsone-induced hemolysis is well described in the literature, especially in glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) enzyme deficiency. However, it is reported rarely with normal G6PD levels.OBJECTIVES:The objective of the study was to describe the association between dapsone and fall in oxygen saturation (SpO2) and hemoglobin after intake of the drug.MATERIALS AND METHODS:All outpatients and inpatients receiving dapsone who presented with breathlessness, fall in SpO2, hemolysis, and fall in hemoglobin were included in the study. Patients who were started on dapsone were asked to measure the SpO2 regularly and observed for any breathlessness. Complete blood count (CBC) and liver function test (LFT) were repeated, and a reticulocyte test was done after 1 week of dapsone.RESULTS:Twelve cases (5 female and 7 male) with a mean age of 47 years, ranging from 22 to 83 years, presented with various dermatological conditions treated with dapsone. SpO2 fell in all cases, of which eight cases developed breathlessness after starting dapsone. Nine cases developed a reduction in hemoglobin (Hb) with increased reticulocyte counts. The mean decrease in Hb was 2.74 mg/dL. LFT was deranged postdapsone in six cases. Dapsone was stopped in all cases, following which Hb level improved, and there was relief of breathlessness with normal SpO2. Confirmation was done by drug provocation with dapsone.CONCLUSION:Baseline CBC and SpO2 with frequent repetition every week, along with reticulocyte count, are essential to look for dapsone induced hemolysis and methemoglobinemia.

Introduction

Dapsone has a versatile mechanism of action with anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties.[1] It has been used as a primary or alternative drug in several dermatological conditions. It is the only drug without any alternative in some diseases like leprosy. Hemolysis is a known side effect of dapsone in patients, especially with abnormal glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) enzyme deficiency due to the hydroxylamine metabolite.[1] It also causes methemoglobinemia, which can cause fall in oxygen saturation (SpO2). So, baseline G6PD level (antioxidant enzyme which scavenges oxidants like free radicals) is essential before initiation of dapsone.[2] However, dapsone induced hemolysis can occur even with normal G6PD levels, which have been reported in the literature, especially in immunocompromised (transplant recipients and renal failure) patients.[3456] We are reporting 12 immunocompetent cases with normal G6PD levels, some of which had a dip in hemoglobin (Hb) and some developed breathlessness with a fall in SpO2 postdapsone therapy. All patients were managed symptomatically, and dapsone was stopped in all cases, following which the Hb level improved. We also provoked all patients with dapsone to confirm our diagnosis.

Aim and objective

The aim of the study was to describe the association between dapsone and fall in SpO2 and hemoglobin after intake of the drug.

Materials and Methods

A descriptive analytic study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital. All outpatients and inpatients receiving dapsone 100 mg once daily who presented with breathlessness/fall in SpO2, and hemolysis/fall in hemoglobin were included in the study. Outpatient cases who were started on dapsone were asked to measure the SpO2 weekly and observe for any breathlessness, following which they should report to the nearby hospital. SpO2 was measured daily in admitted patients. Complete blood count (CBC), liver function test (LFT), and renal function tests were repeated after 1 week of dapsone. Confirmation was done by drug provocation.

Results

Twelve cases (5 female and 7 male) with a mean age of 47 years, ranging from 22 to 83 years, presented with various dermatological conditions (3 bullous pemphigoid, 2 immunoglobulin A [IgA] pemphigus, 2 lepromatous leprosy, 2 borderline tuberculoid leprosy, 1 borderline lepromatous Hansens, 1 pyoderma gangrenosum, and 1 epidermolysis bullosa acquisita [EBA]). Case details are mentioned in Table 1. Baseline CBC, LFT, and G-6PD estimation were within normal limits in all patients. Baseline O2 saturation at room air was also normal. Viral markers (human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis B surface antigen, and hepatitis C virus) were negative in all. All patients received dapsone 100 mg once daily. 8 cases (case 1, 2, 3, 4, 7, 10, 11, 12) developed breathlessness after 2–25 days of starting dapsone. Postdapsone, SpO2 fell in all cases, ranging from 76% to 88% after 2–60 days (average of 31 days). All patients were immediately started on nasal oxygen at a rate of 6 L/min, and arterial blood gas (ABG) was sent. Following this, all had restoration of SpO2 (99%), and ABG was normal. Serum methemoglobin could not be measured due to a lack of facilities. Only 1 patient (case 8) developed headache, vomiting, weakness, jaundice, and loss of appetite after 1 month, while 4 patients (case 2, 5, 6, and 9) did not develop any symptoms. CBC, LFT, and reticulocyte count were repeated in all these cases weekly. Nine cases (1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12) developed a reduction in Hb (0.3–5.4 mg/dL) with increased reticulocyte counts (2.4% to 16.1%) after 2–60 days of dapsone. The mean decrease in Hb was 2.74 mg/dL. All had normal iron stores, serum B12, and folate levels. LFT was deranged postdapsone in cases 1, 3, 5, 8, 10, and 12. Details of investigations are mentioned in Table 2. All patients were managed symptomatically, and dapsone was stopped in all cases, following which Hb level improved, and there was relief of breathlessness with normal SpO2. Case 3 was started on ursodeoxycholic acid 300 thrice daily after gastroenterology consultation. To confirm the findings, all patients were provoked with dapsone 100 mg orally in the inpatient department (IPD) with proper monitoring and informed consent. An emergency tray with drugs was kept ready. ICU beds were kept ready with an anesthetic on call, aware in case of an emergency. In all cases, there was a drop in SpO2 on oral provocation with dapsone. The standard protocol was followed after a fall in SpO2 just below 90%. All patients had normal SPO2 following this.

Discussion

Dapsone, being cheap (poor man’s drug), is widely prescribed by physicians, especially on an outpatient basis in various dermatological problems such as leprosy, urticaria, and uncommon rare like vasculitis. It acts via inhibition of the folic acid pathway, reducing the synthesis of folate thus useful in leprosy.[1] Thus, it is the only effective drug, along with clofazimine and rifampicin, in leprosy. It also has a potent anti-inflammatory agent by inhibiting myeloperoxidase in neutrophil thus reducing the oxidative free radical damage to normal tissue.[17] So, indicated in neutrophilic dermatoses such as Sweet’s syndrome, pyoderma gangrenosum, pemphigus (IgA pemphigus, pemphigus foliaceus), and subepidermal vesicobullous (bullous pemphigoid, linear IgA dermatosis, dermatitis herpetiformis, EBA).[17] Usually, patient are not monitored for fall in hemoglobin and SpO2. Side effects of dapsone are ranging from mild, such as nausea, vomiting, to severe, such as drug hypersensitivity syndrome. Known side effects are hemolytic anemia, methemoglobinemia, and hepatitis. Breathlessness and fall in saturation are reported rarely. It is a lipid-soluble drug which is metabolized in the liver to hydroxylamine metabolite from N-hydroxylation which is responsible for rare dose-related hematologic side effects like hemolytic anemia and methemoglobinemia, especially in G6PD deficiency. So, dapsone is usually given when G6PD is normal. G6PD enzyme in red blood cells (RBC) has an essential role in protecting against oxygen-free radicals by maintaining optimal reduced glutathione. In case of G6PD deficiency due to mutation, RBC is more prone to oxidative damage from free radicals, resulting in hemolysis. But there is significant individual variation in susceptibility to this effect (oxidative damage), resulting in significant hemolysis even in some individuals having a normal G6PD assay.[89] There is a decrease in hemoglobin with the presence of at least one laboratory marker of hemolysis and an improvement to baseline Hb after discontinuation of dapsone.[10] There are a few case reports of dapsone induced hemolysis occurring in immunocompromised cases, even with normal G6PD levels.[3456] Accidentally, 3 indoor patients (cases 1, 2, and 3) with normal G6PD levels developed breathlessness with a fall in SpO2 while on dapsone. There were also features of hemolysis and fall in hemoglobin in laboratory reports. Subsequently, we observed all patients on dapsone both the outpatient department and IPD. Similar findings were noted, of which cases 4, 7, 10, 11, and 12 developed breathlessness, possibly due to methemoglobinemia. Case 8 developed headache, vomiting, weakness, jaundice, and loss of appetite. Case 4, 11 developed a reeling of the head. Our cases (cases 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, and 12) which were immunocompetent despite having normal G6PD levels, developed dip in hemoglobin levels with increased reticulocyte counts. LFT was dearranged in 6 cases. All patients were immediately started on nasal oxygen at a rate of 6 L/min, and ABG was sent. Following this, all had restoration of SpO2 (99%), and ABG was normal. Dapsone was stopped in all these cases, following which Hb level improved, and there was relief of breathlessness with normal SpO2. We also provoked all patients with dapsone, and all of them had a fall in SpO2. As per the author’s knowledge, this is the first case series of 12 immunocompetent cases having dapsone induced hemolysis occurring even with normal G6PD levels. CBC (Hb) is repeated weekly for 4 weeks, then every 2 weeks for 3 months, and reticulocyte count if required initially to monitor for hemolysis as per guidelines. But it is not stringently followed by dermatologists in real-world practice. We propose to do baseline CBC, then frequent repetition every week, and reticulocyte count to look for dapsone induced hemolysis as seen in our case series. Furthermore, baseline SpO2 by pulse oximetry should be recorded and then repeatedly measured for a few days for early detection of dapsone induced methemoglobinaemia (breathlessness and fall in SpO2). Further studies needed to be done with larger sample size to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

Baseline CBC with frequent repetition every week, along with reticulocyte count are essential to look for dapsone induced hemolysis. Furthermore, baseline SpO2 by pulse oximetry with frequent monitoring for a few days will be helpful for early detection of dapsone induced methemoglobinaemia.
