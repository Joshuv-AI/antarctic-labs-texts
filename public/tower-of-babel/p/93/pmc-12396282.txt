Staged reconstruction of the traumatic large soft-tissue defect in the leg
Authors: Jaehyun Park, Seungyup Shin, Gyeonggyu Choi, Sungho Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
CaseWe present a case of an elderly female patient with a chronic lower leg wound involving bone exposure in the tibia. Despite the absence of overt infection, low-grade osteomyelitis was suspected. Given the patient's age and overall fitness, we opted for a staged reconstructive approach using negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT), followed by sequential pedicled muscle flaps and skin grafting. This method was favored over free flap reconstruction due to patient-specific factors and the aim of minimizing donor site morbidity. The outcome was functionally and aesthetically satisfactory, with no signs of flap failure or recurrent infection.ConclusionThis case highlights the utility of staged reconstruction using local muscle flaps combined with NPWT in elderly patients, offering a limb-salvaging alternative to free tissue transfer in selected cases.

Introduction

Reconstruction of extensive soft-tissue defects in the lower extremity is challenging [1]. This type of wound usually requires flap transplantation to cover critical structures such as exposed joints, bones, and tendons. The most suitable type of tissue and the decision between local and free flap coverage still remain under discussion. Planning for soft tissue reconstruction requires careful consideration of several factors, including patient comorbidities, wound size and location, exposed underlying structures, and, in the case of possible free flap, patency of donor and recipient vessels. Single-stage reconstruction of large soft-tissue defects is preferred as it allows the restoration of lower extremity function and prevents infections [2,3]. Free flaps are one of the feasible options for single-stage reconstruction. However, these flaps require a high level of experience and are technically challenging, and the donor site morbidity is also a concern [4]. In selected cases, the medial head of the gastrocnemius and hemi-soleus flap, in combination with skin grafting, can be a successful limb salvage procedure instead of free tissue transfer, such as free flaps [5,6]. Therefore, there is no “gold standard.” Herein we report a case of large soft-tissue defect of the lower leg successfully treated with a split-thickness skin graft following the medial gastrocnemius and hemi-soleus flap incorporating NPWT.

Case report

The patient, a 74-year-old female, presented to the emergency room with large soft-tissue defect resulting from a traumatic injury two months ago (Fig. 1). The patient didn't visit clinic and waited for spontaneous healing of the wound. The wound gradually progressed to soft-tissue necrosis and a large soft-tissue defect. On arrival, vital signs were stable except a mild fever of 37.4 °C. Clinical examination revealed a large soft-tissue defect, approximately 20 × 10 cm, with exposed tibial cortex measuring 4 × 2 cm in the middle third with no signs of active infection in the leg. The neurovascular examination was normal. The patient had no significant past medical history including diabetes mellitus. Blood glucose levels and hemoglobin A1C were within normal limits. Thorough debridement was performed under general anesthesia, removing necrotic tissue (Fig. 2). Given the absence of gross purulence but suspicion of low-grade osteomyelitis based on clinical signs and bone appearance, bone drilling and curettage were conducted (Fig. 2), and samples were sent for histopathology and microbial culture. No pathogens were isolated; however, empirical antibiotics were initiated. NPWT was applied at −125 mmHg continuous pressure for 3 days. A second look revealed healthy granulation over most of the wound bed except for the tibial bone exposure. Following an additional session of debridement and NPWT (interval of 3 days), a pedicled medial gastrocnemius flap was rotated to cover the middle third, and a hemi-soleus flap was tunneled to cover the mid-third and distal-third (Fig. 3). The distal tip remained uncovered due to limited arc of rotation. Given the favorable periosteal condition, it was deemed suitable to provide healthy granulation tissue following NPWT for subsequent skin grafting. Discussion

The treatment of large soft tissue defects of the lower leg remains a challenge. Although free flaps are considered the gold standard for distal-third leg defects due to their robust vascularity and coverage versatility, they are not always feasible, particularly in elderly or comorbid patients. Studies such as by Rovere et al. [7] highlight that while free flaps provide reliable outcomes, they are associated with higher operative times, technical complexity, and potential donor site morbidity. In contrast, local muscle flaps, such as the gastrocnemius and soleus, offer shorter operative durations and reduced perioperative risks, especially when combined with negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT).

Franken et al. [8] reported that in the case of large or serious defects, free flaps remain the only possibility for reconstruction, whereas local flaps can be used in less serious or smaller defects. However, staged soft tissue reconstruction, incorporating NPWT, local muscular flaps, and subsequent skin grafting can facilitate successful wound closure and functional restoration in select cases [5,6]. Historically, the gastrocnemius and soleus muscle flaps have provided dependable coverage of lower limb defects [5,9]. The gastrocnemius local flap with subsequent skin graft has been widely used for reconstruction of knee and proximal leg defects [5] and the soleus muscle flap can be elevated and transposed into wounds of the proximal, middle thirds, and even the distal part of the leg [5,6]. These local muscular flaps can improve blood flow to the defect and provide a surface for subsequent skin grafting. Distal third wounds of the lower leg especially remain a challenge because of the limited possibilities for local muscle transposition due to its limited volume and reach [8]. Free flaps are frequently used for defects of the distal third of the leg and often remain the only possibility for treatment [8]. Therefore, the classic reconstructive armamentarium suggests the use of local muscle flap for the upper two-thirds of the leg and free tissue transfers for the distal leg and foot. [10] However, fasciocutaneous flaps such as anterolateral supramalleolar flap and reverse sural artery flap can offer coverage the distal leg. Furthermore, other studies recommend initial treatment with a vacuum-assisted closure system. These studies describe vacuum-assisted closure being applied either as a bridge to surgery by diminishing the surface area of the wound and inducing tissue granulation or as a definitive treatment in combination with skin grafting [[11], [12], [13]]. Typically, skin grafts are used to cover deep partial-thickness skin defects, full-thickness skin defects, or placed over muscle. Furthermore, they can also survive on any wound bed with vascularity, including the bone with intact periosteum [14,15]. As seen with this case, skin grafting was successfully taken on the healthy granulation tissue from the intact periosteum. This case also highlights the efficacy of NPWT in preparing the wound bed for reconstruction, even in the elderly patient.

In our case, a staged approach involving NPWT followed by local flaps and skin grafting achieved complete healing with preserved function and no recurrence over twelve months. This mirrors the findings of Rovere et al. [16] who demonstrated comparable healing rates with local flaps in selected patients, particularly when meticulous debridement and granulation bed preparation are ensured. Furthermore, potential complications such as flap necrosis, graft failure, or wound infection were carefully avoided through staged NPWT, rigorous wound bed preparation, and selective coverage. The use of muscle flaps enhances local perfusion and resistance to infection, which is especially beneficial in potentially osteomyelitic wounds. The patient showed no clinical or radiographic recurrence of infection postoperatively. From a functional perspective, although pedicled muscle flaps can compromise plantarflexion strength, this was not clinically significant in our elderly patient who resumed normal daily activities without deficits. This aligns with studies like Rovere et al. [17], which emphasize balancing functional outcomes with reconstructive success.

This case underscores the viability of local flaps augmented with NPWT as a limb-salvaging option in elderly patients, particularly when free tissue transfer is not optimal. Comparative literature suggests that with proper technique and patient selection, staged reconstruction can yield outcomes comparable to those of free flaps while minimizing morbidity.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that in elderly patients with distal lower limb soft-tissue defects and limited reconstructive options, a staged approach involving serial debridement, NPWT, pedicled muscle flaps, and skin grafting can yield excellent functional and aesthetic outcomes. This strategy offers a viable alternative to free flap reconstruction, especially when patient comorbidities, surgical risks, or personal preferences preclude microsurgery. Our experience underscores the importance of individualized reconstruction planning and highlights the potential generalizability of this method to similar high-risk patient populations.
