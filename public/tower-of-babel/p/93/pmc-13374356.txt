Effective removal of toxic methylene blue dye using ZnO/nanolignin composite: kinetic, isotherm and thermodynamic study
Authors: Abdelrahman A. Badawy, Suzan Saber, Mona H. Abdel Rehim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lignin nanoparticles (LNp) were prepared by the solvent-non-solvent method from lignin extracted from bagasse. Investigation of particle size revealed that the obtained LNp has size of 282 nm. However, it was used as a bio-template during the synthesis of ZnO to form ZnO/LNp. Characterization of the formed materials was performed by FTIR, XRD, and XPS. A scanning electron microscope is used to study the morphology of both LNp and ZnO/LNp. The prepared nanocomposite is tested as an adsorbent for cationic dye from contaminated water. The results confirmed the efficiency of the nanocomposite to remove methylene blue (MB) from water as ZnO/LNp removed nearly 97% of MB while LNp showed only 93% dye removal. Moreover, the investigation of adsorption kinetics confirmed that the adsorption can be described by pseudo-second-order mechanism. The uptake of MB was increased with elevated temperature, indicating that the adsorption of MB is an endothermic process, and Freundlich isotherm is more applicable than Langmuir isotherm. The low value of ΔHo was found to be lower than 40 kJ/mol. Moreover, Dubinin–Radushkevich (D-R) relations are utilized to determine the nature of adsorption. The parameters of D-R isotherm revealed that the value of E = 3.9 and 4.9 (kJ/mol) which indicated that poor correlation coefficient and confirming that the MB adsorption process occurs via a physical adsorption mechanism.

Introduction

Synthetic dyes are used worldwide in many industries such as textiles, paper leather, paints and packaging coloring, among others [1]. These massively produced chemicals are discharged in the environment in high quantities, causing water coloring even at very low concentrations [2]. Moreover, synthetic dyes do not undergo biodegradation, are highly stable to light and temperature, and are difficult to eliminate with the conventional water treatment methods [3, 4]. From these synthetic dyes, methylene blue (MB) emerges as one of the most used dyes as a colorant in textiles, paper, food, and pharmaceutical industries [5]. In addition, MB has been shown to have certain therapeutic effects; these only materialize when taken as directed by a medical professional [6]. However, there could be numerous health concerns associated with the discharge of partially or untreated MB dye-loaded wastewater from any of the aforementioned sectors. For example, MB dye can cause several illnesses in people, including vomiting, cyanosis, Heinz body formation, tissue necrosis, jaundice, and an accelerated heartbeat [7]. Additionally, the presence of MB can be a significant problem for plants, as evidenced by growth suppression, pigment, and protein content reductions in some microalgae [8]. Therefore, cleaning of wastewater from MB before releasing it into physical environment is a must.

A variety of treatment techniques have been used to remove dye from the environment, including physico-chemical techniques (mostly adsorption), chemical techniques, and biological techniques using enzymes and microorganisms [9]. However, research has indicated that the majority of these traditional approaches have disadvantages, as high costs, high electricity consumption, and significant production of hazardous waste [10]. However, it has been demonstrated that the adsorption technique is an efficient way to remove MB from waste water and has minimal drawbacks. Numerous benefits of adsorption technology have been identified, including simplicity in design, convenience of use, insensitivity to dangerous contaminants, efficiency, affordability, and accessibility of natural adsorbents, and absence of hazardous byproducts [11]. In the adsorption process, molecules from a bulk liquid or gas phase are focused onto the surface of a solid adsorbent. Because the surface has a higher energy state than the bulk material, adsorbates accumulate there. This adsorption process is driven thermodynamically by a decreased Gibbs surface free energy [12–21]. However, this effect does not permeate the adsorbent’s interior structure; rather, it is restricted to its exterior [22].

Traditional synthetic adsorbents for water treatment have many drawbacks such as high operation cost; difficult regeneration, and secondary pollution after disposal. The shift toward natural adsorbents on the other hand enables utilization of low cost materials since they are mostly agricultural residues like rice husk or banana peels. They have high adsorption capacity, contain large number of functional groups that can be chemically modifies and biodegradable [23–26].

Lignin is an aromatic biopolymer present in cellulosic plants and can be obtained as a byproduct during paper and pulp industry. It is regarded as a promising natural resource of significant potential for the production of commodities with a high market value. Due to its extensive availability, lignin offers intriguing prospects for use in the creation of novel applications and environmentally friendly technologies. The chemical structure of lignin contains many active functional groups, allowing its utilization in many applications. Lignin is therefore an appropriate low-cost and environmentally benign adsorbent material for wastewater dye removal. The presence of hydroxylic, phenolic, and carboxylic groups enables its binding to dye molecules through complex formation via ion exchange mechanisms [27]. In contrast to commercial absorbents, pristine lignin (PL) shows comparatively little pollutant removal capabilities, despite the potential adsorption ability of lignin that can be attributed to its complicated structure and varied three-dimensional architecture [28]. Doping of lignin with ions or chemical modification of its structure by esterification or sulfonation to enhance its adsorption capability is studied [29–32]. Studying the kinetics of the adsorption process revealed that it mostly follows a pseudo-second order mechanism [33]. Behera et al. investigated the removal of basic dye using nanolignin (LN) extracted from agricultural waste [34]. The results showed that NL could remove ranges of 3.5 mg/g to a final of 24 mg/g, 2.5 mg/g to 42.5 mg/g, and 1.5 gm/g to 5.5 mg/g, for methylene blue (MB), phenol red (PR), and brilliant green (BG) dye from aqueous solutions, respectively. Moreover, removal of MB from its aqueous medium was studied by Pourbaba et al. using pristine lignin and nanolignin [35]. Characterization of the materials’ surfaces revealed that NL has a higher surface area with low roughness. On the contrary, PL showed lower surface area and high roughness. The adsorption experiments showed that NL is a superior adsorbent compared with pristine lignin (PL). The kinetic studies revealed that chemisorption is the predominant mechanism and the rate constant in case of NL is one order of magnitude higher than that of PL.

Hereinafter, we describe enhancing adsorption capability of LN as a natural material through incorporating ZnO nanoparticles in its matrix. The chemical structure of the prepared nanocomposite is studied using different analytical methods. The prepared material is examined as a potential adsorbent for methylene blue dye (MB).The adsorption experiments were performed under various test conditions and their kinetics were discussed using different models.

Experimental

Materials

Zinc acetate dehydrate [Zn(O2CCH3)2(H2O)2] (99.99%) and ethanol were purchased from Sigma Aldrich, USA. Lignin sodium sulphonate extracted from Bagasse was used as a source for lignin nanoparticles (LNp). Bagasse pulp was supplied from Edfo Company, Egypt. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 98%) and sodium bisulfate (NaHSO4, 95%) were supplied by Fluka Chemie, Germany. All chemicals were used as received without further treatment.

Methodology

Synthesis of lignin nanoparticles (LNp)

A solution of lignin (2 g) in 200 mL THF is prepared and water, as a non-solvent, is added in a rate of 4 mL/min. To collect NL particles as a colloid, THF is removed by rotary evaporator.

Synthesis of ZnO/LNp

100 mg of lignin was dissolved in 5 ml of 0.2 M NaOH and the volume of solution was increased to 50 ml using absolute ethanol. Then 250 ml of zinc acetate (0.024 M) was added drop wise until pH = 8. The reaction mixture was stirred at 75 °C for 2 h then washed using deionized water.

Adsorption studies

Adsorption experiments

Solution of methylene blue (MB) dye was synthesized by dissolving a definite quantity of dye powder in bi-distilled water. The desired concentrations for the adsorption studies were gotten by diluting the solution. In each experiment, the concentration is measured via single beam UV-visible spectrophotometer (JASCO V-730, Japan).

The batch equilibrium strategy was used to remove reactive MB dye solution from an aqueous solution. After shaking100 mL of dye solution at an initial concentration with 0.1 mg of the adsorbent, the solution settled for three hours at a definite pH (3, 7, and 9 ± 0.1 °C). Equilibrium isotherms were investigated at varied beginning concentrations ranging from 10 to 100 ppm. Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Dubinin–Radushkevich (DR) isotherms models were employed to investigate adsorption process.

Effect of dye concentration

50 mg of adsorbent, contact durations ranging from 0 to 3 h, stirring at 150 rpm, at 25 °C,, and pH = 7, the influence of dye concentrations varied between 10 and 100 ppm was examined. Before being analyzed with UV spectroscopy at the appropriate wavelength (664 nm), the solutions were spun for 10 min at 7000 rpm.

Adsorption mechanism

Predicting rate-determining steps is crucial to the design’s objective. Either intra-particle diffusion, external mass transfer, or both are commonly employed to characterize the solute transfer in a solid-liquid adsorption process. The adsorption dynamics can be clarified by the three successive processes listed below: The first step involved transporting the solute from the bulk solution to the adsorbent outer surface via the liquid layer. The second step involved solute diffusion within the adsorbent’s pore, with the exception of a tiny amount of sorption on the external surface. Solute sorption on the inside surfaces of the adsorbent’s pores and capillary gaps, was the last stage. The overall rate of sorption would be controlled by the slowest mechanism, which may be either pore diffusion or film diffusion.

Results and discussion

Determination of (LNp) size

Solvent-non-solvent technique is used to prepare LNp in which water was the non-solvent used to swap THF. The addition rate of the non-solvent affects to a large extent the size of the formed particles via nucleation-growth mechanism, since slower water addition leads to nanoparticles of larger diameters [36, 37]. The size of lignin nanoparticles determined by dynamic light scattering technique was found to be an average of 282 nm, and a PDI value of 0.46 (Fig. 1A). Morphology investigations carried out by TEM showed that the lignin nanoparticles have irregular structure. (Fig. 1B) However, the high magnification (X= 30 K) displayed the smooth and nano-porous surface of the nanoparticles.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The crystalline structure of ZnO/LNp is studied by XRD to investigate the effect of LNp on the crystal structure of ZnO. The obtained diffraction pattern revealed an amorphous structure with no distinct peaks characteristic of ZnO (Fig. 2).

The weak but sharp peaks at 31.9, and 34.4 and 36.2 are in close agreement with ZnO peaks (as shown in the inset), where the amorphous nature of lignin is dominant [38]. This weakness of the ZnO peaks might be attributed either to embedding of them in the broad peak related to LNp and/or presence of ZnO in small crystallite size, which couldn’t be detected by the X-ray diffractometer. However, these results confirmed the good incorporation of ZnO in the LNp matrix.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR)

The obtained spectrum for LNp showed an absorption band at 3328 cm− 1 related to OH stretching (cf. Figure 3). Bands were noticed at 2900 cm− 1 and 2838 cm− 1, corresponding to aliphatic C-H stretching and tertiary C-H groups, respectively. The nanoparticles also exhibited vibration bands at 1690 cm− 1 that can be attributed to C = O stretching. Moreover, LNp depicted vibration bands at 1218 cm− 1 and 1024 cm− 1 which can be attributed to C-O stretching aromatic and C-O deformation of the methoxy group, respectively [16]. However, the spectrum of ZnO/LNp depicted sharp and strong bands found in LNp but in shifted positions. The hydroxyl group absorption band appeared at 3301 cm− 1, and those related to aliphatic C-H stretching and tertiary C-H groups could be found at 2935 and 2855 cm− 1, respectively. The shift in bands’ positions can be attributed to hydrogen bonding between ZnO and LNp. Nevertheless, the carbonyl band is masked under the strong absorption bands of aromatic skeletal vibration bands at 1558 cm− 1 and 1949 cm− 1 [39, 40].

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The surface morphology of lignin after reaction with zinc oxide (ZnO) was investigated using scanning electron microscope (SEM) to examine changes in structure and distribution of ZnO particles (cf. Figure 4). SEM provides high-resolution images that reveal topographical and microstructural details at the nanoscale. SEM images of pure lignin typically exhibit an amorphous, irregular, and fibrous morphology due to its heterogeneous polymeric structure. After reaction with ZnO, significant changes in surface texture were observed. ZnO particles were found to be deposited on the lignin surface, appearing as nanoparticles. These particles were either embedded within or adhered to the lignin matrix, indicating successful interactions or binding between lignin and ZnO. The presence of ZnO altered the surface roughness and introduced distinct particulate features, suggesting enhanced surface area and potential modifications in physicochemical properties. The distribution and morphology of ZnO on lignin also offer insight into the efficiency of the synthesis process and nature of the LNp-ZnO interaction. These observations confirm the successful incorporation of ZnO into the lignin structure and are crucial for understanding the nanocomposite’s potential applications. Furthermore, elemental analysis of ZnO/LNp by EDX revealed that the prepared nanocomposite is composed of C, O, N and Zn, confirming the successful preparation step (Table 1).

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is a surface-sensitive technique used to analyze the elemental composition and chemical states of materials. It works by irradiating a material with X-rays, causing the ejection of photoelectrons from the atoms on the material’s surface. The energy and intensity of the emitted photoelectrons are measured to provide information about the elements present and their chemical environments. XPS of lignin provides us with valuable insights into the elemental composition and oxidation states of the functional groups of the lignin structure. Lignin is primarily composed of carbon (C), oxygen (O), and hydrogen (H), with minor amounts of other elements such as nitrogen or sulfur depending on the lignin source or any treatment the material may have undergone.

The XPS spectrum (Fig. 5) shows strong peaks corresponding to the core-level binding energies of these elements. Carbon (C1s) revealed strong peaks at 284.3 eV due to C–C and C–H bonds. The C1s spectrum is of lignin is typically has one main peak at 284.3 representing the aromatic rings and the side chains. Additional peaks or shoulders is observed at higher binding energies (around 286–288) corresponding to C–O, O = C, or C–O–C bonds in oxygen-containing functional groups. Oxygen (O1s) has a characteristic peak at 532 eV from O–C bonds in various oxygen containing groups such as hydroxyl, ether, or carbonyl groups. The O1s spectrum is typically shows a primary peak at 532 and two other sub-peaks at 531–533 which suggest the presence of more oxidized species that may be formed due to the oxidation of lignin.

Hydrogen (H) is not directly detected by XPS since its binding energy is too low to ionize electrons from its core levels. Nitrogen typically appears in the N1s region with a binding energy of around 399 eV. Sodium is a highly reactive alkali metal and can appear in various forms of lignin samples, especially if lignin has been treated with sodium salts during isolation, extraction, or a chemical modification process. The sodium Na 1s peak typically appears at a binding energy of around 1071 eV as shown in Fig. 5 this indicates the presence of sodium salts or impurities in the treatment of lignin. The Cl 2p peak in X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) corresponding to the binding energies of chlorine (Cl) atoms in the sample. The Cl 2p peak typically appears around 198-200 eV for Cl 2p3/2, which is the more prominent spin orbit splitting component. A satellite peak for Cl 2p1/2 appears at slightly higher energies (around 200–202), but the main peak is observed at 198. Chlorine may be introduced to lignin during chemical treatment or the inclusion of chlorine-based chemicals in lignin or its nanocomposites.

Adsorption mechanism

The important step for designing an adsorption mechanism is determining the rate-determining step. It is well known that the solute in an adsorption process is transferred by one of two methods: external mass transfer or intra-particle diffusion, or both [20, 21]. This can be pronounced by three steps as follows:

Through liquid film, the movement of the solute from the bulk solution to the exterior surface of the adsorbent.With the exception of a tiny quantity of sorption on the outside, solute diffusion carried out inside the adsorbent’s pore; the intra-particle transport mechanism of surface diffusion carried out concurrently.The solute is absorbed on the inside surfaces of the adsorbent’s capillary gaps and pores. The slowest process, either pore diffusion or film diffusion, will regulate the overall rate of sorption. Nonetheless, the step that determines the rate may be split between external and intra-particle transport mechanisms. In either scenario, the sorption process will entail external diffusion. It was clear from Fig. 12’s intra-particle diffusion plot that the adsorption process consists of two phases. The diffusion in the boundary layer is represented by the first linear section, which is followed by a curve and the intra-particle diffusion by the second linear section. There have been reports of a similar investigation elsewhere [46]. The thickness of the boundary layer can be estimated from the value of the constant Ki as shown in Table 4. However, Fig. 13 demonstrates that the plot is linear and does not cross the origin, indicating that the rate of the response data is mostly controlled by the external mass transfer.

Conclusion

This work describes the synthesis of a nanocomposite based on lignin nanoparticles (ZnO/LNp) and its application as an environmentally friendly dye adsorbing material. Characterization of the prepared materials was performed by FTIR, XRD, and XPS and its morphology is studied by SEM equipped with EDX. The nanocomposite is tested as an adsorbent for methylene blue (MB) from contaminated water. The adsorption experiments performed under different conditions, namely pH, temperature, contact time, and dye concentration confirmed that ZnO/LNp removed nearly 97% of MB. Investigation of adsorption kinetics demonstrated that the adsorption can be described by a pseudo-second-order mechanism and the dye uptake is increased at high temperatures, indicating that the adsorption of MB is an endothermic process. By applying different models, it was found that the Freundlich isotherm is more applicable than the Langmuir isotherm. Moreover, the value of ΔHo was found to be lower than 40 kJ/mol. Dubinin–Radushkevich relations are utilized to ensure the nature the adsorption process. The parameters of D-R isotherm revealed that the value of E = 3.9 and 4.9 (kJ/mol) indicating the poor correlation coefficient. However, all obtained data b applying different relations confirmed that the adsorption process occurs via a physical adsorption mechanism. In conclusion, the prepared nanocomposite based on lignin nanoparticles can be employed as an effective adsorptive material for removing toxic cationic dye (MB) from aqueous solutions.
