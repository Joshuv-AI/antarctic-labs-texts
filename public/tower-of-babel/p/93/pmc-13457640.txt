Repetitive temperature cycles with DMSO sustain RNA replicating liposome compartments via fusion mediated content exchange
Authors: Gakushi Tsuji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCompartmentalization by phospholipid membranes was a crucial step in the origin of life, enabling primitive cells to evolve through gene replication and diversification. Periodic freeze-thaw cycles are known to induce fusion and fission of phospholipid compartments; however, these processes typically involve membrane disruption followed by reassembly. Here, we report a dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)-mediated fusion mechanism that enables liposome fusion at -20 °C without freezing either the internal or external aqueous phases, thereby preserving membrane integrity. Using this approach, we achieved repetitive liposome fusion coupled with RNA replication for at least seven consecutive cycles at -20 °C, under non-freezing conditions. We further show that membrane lipid exchange occurs even at 4 °C, whereas efficient internal content mixing depends on lipid phase behavior, indicating a decoupling between membrane exchange and content mixing. This system enables redistribution of molecular substrates and genetic material across compartments, supporting recursive replication under fluctuating conditions.Subject terms: Synthetic biology, Membranes, Phospholipids

Introduction

Reconstructing the evolution of primitive life through a bottom-up approach using cell-like compartments bounded by lipid membranes remains a major challenge. This approach is essential for understanding how lipid membranes contributed to the emergence of biological complexity by continuously supplying nutrients to primitive compartments1,2. Biological membranes act as boundaries that separate the interior from the exterior, generating localized nonequilibrium environments that resist dilution3–5. These properties are fundamental for maintaining genotype–phenotype linkage and for enabling evolution through natural selection5,6.

Fatty acids, which can be synthesized under prebiotic conditions, were initially proposed as primitive membrane components7. However, fatty acid vesicles show limited stability against changes in physicochemical parameters such as pH and temperature, making them unsuitable for sustaining biochemical reactions within compartments8,9. Recently, the non-enzymatic synthesis of diacyl phospholipids has been demonstrated10–12, supporting the hypothesis that primitive cells may have possessed phospholipid-based membranes. Yet, phospholipid vesicles are largely impermeable even to small molecules such as nucleic acids and amino acids, resulting in unstable biochemical reactions within the vesicles. Moreover, horizontal transfer of genetic material between vesicles, which is essential for the spread of genetic innovation, rarely occurs in such systems.

Cyclic physical or chemical perturbations, including pH fluctuations, temperature changes, and dehydration–rehydration cycles, have been shown to transiently increase membrane permeability13–15. Other approaches for vesicle fusion and macromolecular exchange include pH- or charge-induced fusion16, electrofusion17,18, freeze-thaw (F/T) cycles19–24, and the use of fusion-promoting compounds such as polyethylene glycol25, amphiphiles26, peptides27, cations28,29, and magnetic nanoparticle coated with oleic acid30. Among these, F/T cycles driven by temperature fluctuations could have naturally occurred on the prebiotic Earth, allowing repetitive fusion and fission of compartments that supported internal RNA replication19, and ribozyme self-replication23. However, fusion efficiency during F/T cycles is low at −20 °C and requires freezing at −80 °C or in liquid nitrogen for efficient fusion19,20.

Here, we show that dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) induces internal content mixing between phospholipid vesicles at −20 °C without freezing either the internal or external solutions. In this study, DMSO is used as a representative small molecule that modulates membrane interactions under non-freezing conditions. DMSO is an organic sulfur compound that can arise from oxidation of reduced sulfur species31 and may have been present under prebiotic conditions, where diverse sulfur compounds were abundant and actively involved in prebiotic chemical networks32–34. DMSO is widely used as a cryoprotectant for cell35 and liposomes36 preservation and has been reported to enhance membrane stability by interacting with lipid headgroups at various temperatures37. We found that membrane phospholipid exchange occurs at 4 °C in the presence of DMSO, whereas efficient internal content mixing depends on lipid phase behavior, indicating a decoupling between membrane exchange and content mixing. Importantly, internal content mixing was detectable even at relatively low DMSO concentrations, although higher concentrations enhanced the robustness of the process. Using this DMSO-mediated fusion-fission cycle, we demonstrated RNA replication within liposomes and the subsequent distribution of replicated RNA into daughter vesicles, where it served as a template for further replication. These results establish a mechanism by which membrane fusion can mediate the redistribution of both genetic information and molecular resources across compartments without loss of structural integrity. This provides a experimental system for recursive, compartment-based replication cycles and suggests a plausible route by which protocells could have sustained and propagated genetic information through repeated fusion events under fluctuating environmental conditions.

Results

DMSO-mediated liposome fusion coupled with inner content and membrane mixing

We developed a DMSO-mediated liposome fusion method that enables the exchange of both internal contents and membrane lipids. To test whether DMSO, an antifreeze agent known to interact with lipid membranes38, induces internal content exchange between adhered liposomes, we used a water-in-oil (w/o) emulsion transfer method39 to prepare giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs). GUVs resemble living cells in both size and lamellarity and are widely used as models for artificial cells19,22,40. Two populations of POPC liposomes were prepared, one encapsulating transferrin conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (TA488) and the other encapsulating Alexa Fluor 647 (TA647). The two solutions were mixed and exchanged with a fusion buffer containing DMSO, followed by centrifugation to concentrate the liposomes. The resulting pellets were incubated at either 4 °C or −20 °C for 1 h, then exchanged with a DMSO-free outer solution (Fig. 1A). Fluorescence was analyzed by flow cytometry (FCM). The expected phase states of the inner and outer solutions at each DMSO concentration are summarized in Supplementary Table 141,42, indicating that both phases were expected to freeze at up to 30% DMSO, whereas 50% DMSO was expected to remain unfrozen at −20 °C. Inner content mixing, detected as red dots in the FCM scatter plots (Fig. 1B), was not observed in samples incubated at 4 °C, with or without DMSO. In contrast, GUVs incubated at −20 °C with DMSO contained both fluorescent proteins, referred to as double-positive GUVs. The fraction of double-positive GUVs was significantly higher at −20 °C than at 4 °C (Fig. 1C), indicating that content mixing was efficiently induced under freezing conditions. In addition, a significant effect of DMSO concentration (p < 0.001) and a significant interaction between temperature and DMSO concentration (p < 0.001) were observed, suggesting that the effect of DMSO depends on the temperature condition. Under −20 °C conditions, higher DMSO concentrations, particularly at 50%, significantly increased the fraction of double-positive GUVs compared to 0%, indicating enhanced content mixing.

GUV ratios, determined by light scattering43, did not change significantly under any condition (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of incubation temperature on the GUV fraction (p < 0.05), whereas no significant effect of DMSO concentration (p = 0.59) or interaction (p = 0.85) was observed, indicating that the temperature-dependent changes in GUV fraction were independent of DMSO concentration. Because FCM cannot distinguish between fused and aggregated liposomes, we further confirmed by fluorescence microscopy that the dual-fluorescent GUVs resulted from genuine internal content mixing rather than adhesion (Fig. 1D).

Leakage of internal contents was then quantified by comparing fluorescence intensities after incubation at −20 °C relative to those after incubation at 4 °C. No significant leakage was detected in samples without DMSO (Fig. 1E, 0%), whereas a ~60% decrease in fluorescence intensity was observed at −20 °C in the presence of DMSO (Fig. 1E). A decrease in fluorescence reflects both leakage of encapsulated molecules and dilution resulting from internal content mixing. To evaluate the effect of DMSO concentration on fluorescence retention, one-way ANOVA was performed using the −20 °C/4 °C fluorescence ratios. No significant effect of DMSO concentration was observed for TA488 (p = 0.224), whereas a significant effect was detected for TA647 (p < 0.05). However, post hoc Tukey’s multiple comparison test did not identify any significant pairwise differences between individual DMSO concentrations for either fluorescence channel. In addition, FSC–FITC analysis further confirmed that the double-positive population was distributed across a broad FSC range and was not restricted to unusually high-FSC events (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Moreover, the FITC intensity of double-positive liposomes was generally lower than that of FITC-only liposomes, consistent with dilution of encapsulated tracers following internal content mixing.

To further assess potential size changes, FSC distributions were analyzed. FSC distributions showed modest condition-dependent changes after incubation at −20 °C, including slight shifts toward higher FSC values under some DMSO concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Notably, similar shifts were also observed in the absence of DMSO, suggesting that these changes are primarily temperature-dependent rather than DMSO-specific effects. However, no distinct emergence of an anomalously high-FSC population was observed (Supplementary Fig. 2B), indicating that the observed content mixing was not accompanied by the appearance of a strongly enlarged vesicle population.

To determine whether DMSO-mediated content mixing differs from the rupture–reformation mechanism reported for conventional freeze-thaw cycles22, we performed experiments at −80 °C, where both inner and outer solutions are frozen. The double-positive GUV ratio tended to be higher under freeze–thaw conditions than at −20 °C for 0% and 10% DMSO, but lower for 30% and 50% DMSO (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). When all temperature conditions were compared, both −20 °C and −80 °C showed significantly higher fractions of double-positive GUVs than 4 °C (Tukey’s test, p < 10⁻¹²), whereas no significant difference was observed between −20 °C and −80 °C. Notably, the effect of DMSO differed between these freezing conditions. At −80 °C, increasing DMSO concentration did not enhance content mixing and instead tended to reduce the fraction of double-positive GUVs compared to lower DMSO concentrations. GUV ratios remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 3C, D). In addition, no internal content mixing was observed at intermediate temperatures (25 °C and 37 °C), indicating that this phenomenon is not promoted by increasing temperature but is specific to low-temperature conditions. These results suggest that DMSO-mediated content mixing occurs within a distinct temperature range and is mechanistically different from conventional freeze-thaw induced fusion.

We next examined lipid exchange between liposomes. Phospholipid membrane mixing was negligible at both 4 °C and −20 °C without DMSO (Fig. 2A), but occurred at −20 °C in the presence of 30% or 50% DMSO. Notably, 50% DMSO induced membrane mixing even at 4 °C (Fig. 2A). The fraction of membrane-mixed (double-positive) GUVs (Fig. 2A, red dots) increased at −20 °C with 30% DMSO relative to 4 °C, whereas similar values were obtained for 4 °C and −20 °C at 50% DMSO (Fig. 2B). Unlike content mixing, membrane mixing showed strong dependence on both incubation temperature and DMSO concentration. Two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of incubation temperature (p < 0.01) and DMSO concentration (p < 0.01), as well as a significant interaction between these factors (p < 0.01), indicating that the effect of DMSO on membrane mixing depends on temperature. Post hoc analysis (Tukey’s test) showed that membrane mixing was significantly enhanced at −20 °C compared to 4 °C, particularly at higher DMSO concentrations (p < 0.001). At −20 °C, increasing DMSO concentration significantly promoted membrane mixing, with 30% and 50% DMSO showing markedly higher fractions of double-positive GUVs than 0% DMSO (p < 0.001). In contrast, at 4 °C, DMSO concentration had little or no significant effect on membrane mixing. Fluorescence microscopy confirmed that these signals reflected true membrane mixing rather than adhesion between liposomes (Fig. 2C).

To test whether this effect was specific to DMSO or general to cryoprotectants, we examined glycerol, which can also permeate phospholipid membranes and acts as a cryoprotectant for cells44. Glycerol failed to promote either internal content or membrane mixing at 4 °C or −20 °C (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B) and destabilized GUVs (Supplementary Fig. 4C).

Together, these results indicate that DMSO specifically promotes liposome–liposome fusion accompanied by internal content and membrane mixing without the need for freezing and thawing.

Phase transition of the membrane is required for inner content mixing

To examine the relationship between lipid phase transition temperature and DMSO-mediated liposome fusion, we compared 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC) liposomes with POPC liposomes. The phase transition temperatures of POPC and DOPC are −3 °C45 and −16.5 °C46, respectively. The ratio of internal content mixing was markedly lower in DOPC liposomes than in POPC liposomes, even at high DMSO concentrations (Fig. 3A, B). Moreover, there was little difference in the degree of internal content mixing between −20 °C and 4 °C (Fig. 3B), indicating weak temperature dependence. Consistently, GUV ratios showed no significant dependence on either incubation temperature (p = 0.60) or DMSO concentration (p = 0.18), with no significant interaction between these factors (p = 0.48) (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B), suggesting that vesicle stability was largely maintained under all conditions.

In DOPC vesicles, internal content mixing exhibited a distinct behavior compared to POPC vesicles. Two-way ANOVA revealed no significant effect of incubation temperature (p = 0.27) and no interaction between temperature and DMSO concentration (p = 0.57), indicating that content mixing in DOPC vesicles was largely independent of temperature. In contrast, a significant effect of DMSO concentration was observed (p < 0.01). Post hoc analysis (Tukey’s test) showed that only the highest DMSO concentration (50%) significantly increased the fraction of double-positive GUVs compared to both 0% and 30% DMSO (p < 0.01), whereas lower DMSO concentrations had little effect, suggesting a threshold-like response.

Flow cytometry analysis indicated that a small fraction of DOPC liposomes exhibited content mixing even at 4 °C under 50% DMSO conditions (Fig. 3A, B), consistent with fluorescence microscopy observations (Fig. 3C). Notably, in DOPC liposomes, the fluorescence intensity of TA647 varied widely, and many double-positive liposomes exhibited relatively weak signals, indicating partial and heterogeneous content mixing rather than complete equilibration (Fig. 3B, C). Because double-positive events in FCM are defined based on threshold values, liposomes with low but detectable fluorescence in both channels are still counted as mixed, whereas fluorescence microscopy directly reflects signal intensity. This difference in detection criteria explains why the mixed population appears comparable in FCM but shows reduced fluorescence intensity in microscopy images. Thus, the overall mixing efficiency remained substantially lower than that observed in POPC vesicles.

We next analyzed membrane lipid mixing in the presence and absence of DMSO and found that it was primarily dependent on DMSO concentration rather than incubation temperature (Fig. 4A, B). In contrast to internal content mixing, membrane mixing was strongly enhanced by DMSO concentration but showed no significant dependence on temperature. Two-way ANOVA revealed no significant effect of incubation temperature (p = 0.46) and no interaction between temperature and DMSO concentration (p = 0.78), whereas a significant main effect of DMSO concentration was observed (p < 0.001). Post hoc analysis (Tukey’s test) demonstrated that membrane mixing increased significantly at 30% and 50% DMSO compared to 0% DMSO (p < 0.001), with further enhancement at 50% relative to 30% (p < 0.001). These results indicate a strong DMSO-dependent promotion of membrane mixing in DOPC vesicles. Fluorescence microscopy confirmed that, as in POPC liposomes, fluorescent lipids on DOPC liposomes were exchanged between vesicles without aggregation (Fig. 4C). Notably, this DMSO-dependent but temperature-independent behavior was consistent with the results obtained for internal content mixing (Fig. 3), although the magnitude of the effect was markedly different. While membrane mixing was efficiently induced at ≥30% DMSO, internal content mixing remained limited and required higher DMSO concentrations.

Taken together, these results suggest that membrane phase transitions in the presence of DMSO contribute to internal content mixing (Figs. 1B and 3B), whereas membrane lipid exchange is primarily governed by DMSO concentration and the physicochemical properties of the phospholipid (Figs. 2B and 4B). Thus, in DOPC vesicles, membrane mixing can occur independently of freezing but does not necessarily result in efficient internal content mixing.

RNA replication and propagation after DMSO-mediated fusion

RNA replication within liposomes was achieved by supplying nutrients through DMSO-mediated fusion. Reconstitution of recursive fusion for nutrient delivery must be compatible with the replication of genetic information inside the liposomes. As a model RNA replication system, we used a modified RNA encoding Qβ replicase (Rep-RNA)47, together with NTPs and Qβ replicase provided as substrates. A previous study employing freeze–thaw fusion used liposomes containing substrate concentrations five times higher than those required post-fusion19. Because DMSO-mediated fusion caused minimal leakage, we prepared substrate liposomes at either the reaction concentration (1× nutrients) or twice that concentration (2× nutrients). In addition, liposomes composed of POPC were used for the RNA replication experiments, as POPC vesicles exhibited higher internal content mixing efficiency under DMSO-mediated fusion conditions compared to DOPC vesicles.

To assess whether RNA replication could occur in the presence of external DMSO, GUVs encapsulating the replication system were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h with or without DMSO in the external solution (Supplementary Fig. 6). SYBR Green II, a membrane-permeable dye that fluoresces upon binding to RNA, was then added to visualize replication products inside liposomes, and the fluorescence was measured by FCM. Previous work demonstrated a linear relationship between Rep-RNA concentration and SYBR fluorescence intensity measured by FCM19. Replication efficiency was nearly identical regardless of whether DMSO was present externally.

We next performed DMSO-mediated fusion between Rep-RNA containing liposomes (RNA liposomes) and liposomes encapsulating replicase and substrates (nutrient liposomes) under the same conditions used in content-mixing experiments. Initially, no RNA replication was detected. To address this, we optimized the outer and fusion buffers by replacing HEPES-KOH with Tris-HCl and adding Mg²⁺ and bovine serum albumin (BSA). Under these conditions, internal content mixing was confirmed (Supplementary Fig. 7A), and the GUV ratio remained stable (Supplementary Fig. 7B).

Liposomes encapsulating 1 nM or 5 nM Rep-RNA were then mixed with nutrient liposomes containing 1× or 2× substrate concentrations. The mixtures were centrifuged, incubated at −20 °C in the presence of DMSO, and then exchanged into fresh buffer and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h (Fig. 5A). GUVs were defined as SYBR Green II–positive when their FITC fluorescence intensity exceeded a predefined threshold (FITC-A ≥ 102.3), which was determined based on the fluorescence distribution of control samples lacking RNA replication (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. 8A, green dots), thereby indicating successful RNA replication within GUVs. The number of SYBR Green II-positive liposomes increased from 0 to 2 h under 40% and 50% DMSO conditions, whereas no increase was observed without DMSO (Fig. 5C). The mean SYBR fluorescence intensity of active liposomes further confirmed successful RNA replication, except in samples lacking DMSO during the −20 °C incubation (Fig. 5D). RNA liposomes containing 5 nM template RNA and 2× nutrients fused under 50% DMSO exhibited the highest replication efficiency (Fig. 5D). The fraction of GUVs remained unchanged across all conditions (Supplementary Fig. 8B, three-way ANOVA, p > 0.1 for all factors), indicating that vesicle integrity was preserved during the reaction. In contrast, the fraction of SYBR Green II–positive GUVs was significantly affected by multiple factors. Three-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of initial RNA concentration (p < 0.001), DMSO concentration (p < 0.001), and nutrient concentration (p < 0.05), as well as a significant interaction between initial RNA concentration and DMSO concentration (p < 0.05). These results indicate that RNA replication efficiency depends on both the availability of RNA templates and molecular substrates, as well as the efficiency of fusion-mediated mixing between compartments. Consistently, the mean fluorescence intensity of SYBR-positive GUVs was also significantly influenced by DMSO concentration (p < 0.001) and nutrient concentration (p < 0.01), further supporting that both fusion efficiency and substrate availability contribute to productive RNA replication.

To quantify the extent of RNA propagation following fusion, we estimated the minimum number of nutrient GUVs that received RNA based on the observed SYBR Green II–positive fraction relative to the theoretical dilution-only expectation (Fig. 6A). After replication in liposomes, resultant liposomes and 2× nutrient liposomes were mixed at defined particle ratios (1:1, 1:9, and 1:99) and fused by DMSO-mediated fusion. The resulting replication activities were compared with those expected from the dilution ratios. Replication occurred in 1.5- to 4-fold more compartments than predicted (Fig. 6B), implying that one mother liposome distributed RNA to approximately 1.4, 2.1, and 4.3 daughter liposomes at mixing ratios of 1:1, 1:9, and 1:99, respectively. One-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of the nutrient GUV ratio on the estimated propagation number (p < 0.01), indicating that increasing the excess of nutrient GUVs enhances RNA propagation across compartments. Post hoc analysis (Tukey’s test) showed that the propagation number was significantly higher in the 1:99 condition than in both the 1:1 (p < 0.01) and 1:9 (p < 0.05) conditions, whereas no significant difference was observed between the 1:1 and 1:9 conditions.

These results indicate that RNA propagation does not increase linearly with the number of nutrient GUVs but instead requires a sufficiently large excess of nutrient GUVs to achieve efficient spreading. However, such conditions also imply stronger dilution of replicated RNA among recipient compartments. Taken together, these results suggest a trade-off between RNA propagation and maintenance of replication-positive compartments. While a large excess of nutrient GUVs promotes broader RNA distribution, it also dilutes replicated RNA among too many compartments, reducing the likelihood that individual GUVs remain replication-positive. Therefore, a 1:1 mixing ratio was selected for the passaging experiment (Fig. 7) to balance efficient RNA transfer with sustained replication activity across generations.

Repetitive fusion-reaction cycles via DMSO-mediated fusion

To examine whether DMSO-mediated GUV fusion can repeatedly supply substrates for RNA replication and enable serial RNA propagation, we performed a cyclic fusion–reaction experiment. GUVs encapsulating all components necessary for the Qβ replicase reaction and 5 nM RNA were used as the starting population (generation 0). After RNA replication, 2× nutrient liposomes were added and fused under 50% DMSO conditions, followed by incubation at 37 °C (generation 1) (Fig. 7A). To examine whether RNA replication would cease upon nutrient depletion, we also performed fusion with liposomes lacking substrates (0× nutrient liposomes). This process was repeated for seven consecutive generations. Samples from generations 1 and 4 were stored overnight at 4 °C, and samples from generation 5 were stored at 4 °C for 12 days before further analysis. RNA was stained with SYBR Green II, and fluorescence intensities of GUVs before and after replication, as well as after storage, were analyzed by FCM (Supplementary Fig. 9).

The GUV ratio remained nearly constant throughout all generations (Fig. 7B). The proportion of active liposomes also remained stable at ~20% after 2 h of incubation in each generation supplied with 2× nutrient liposomes (Fig. 7C). In contrast, green fluorescence dropped to 0% when 0× nutrient liposomes were supplied (Fig. 7C). Fluorescence microscopy of fifth-generation GUVs confirmed RNA synthesis within liposomes (Fig. 7D).

We next analyzed the average fluorescence intensity of all GUVs before and after the −20 °C fusion treatment and subsequent incubation at 37 °C. Fluorescence decreased after the −20 °C step but increased in each generation following the replication reaction, indicating that RNA was diluted by fusion with 0× or 2× nutrient liposomes and subsequently amplified through replication. Moreover, RNA replication was accelerated after storage at 4 °C for overnight for generations 1 and 4, and for 12 days for generation 5. Remarkably, long-term storage at 4 °C enhanced the stability and reproducibility of RNA replication during subsequent fusion-reaction cycles (Fig. 7E, generations 6 and 7).

Discussions

In this study, we achieved serial fusion of GUVs encapsulating an RNA replication system through non-freezing, DMSO-mediated liposome fusion. DMSO promoted efficient fusion of POPC liposomes at −20 °C, inducing both internal content and membrane mixing without freezing. This effect was specific to DMSO and was not observed with glycerol, which did not induce detectable content or membrane mixing under the same conditions. The efficiency of inner content mixing depended on the lipid phase transition temperature, whereas membrane exchange primarily reflected DMSO concentration and phospholipid properties. Importantly, detectable internal content mixing was observed even at relatively low DMSO concentrations (e.g., 10%), indicating that the phenomenon is not restricted to high solvent concentrations. DMSO likely facilitates fusion by competing with water for interactions with phospholipid headgroups, thereby promoting membrane contact even under non-freezing conditions48–50. In contrast to conventional freeze–thaw fusion19, which involves membrane disruption and reformation22, the DMSO-mediated system exhibits distinct mixing behaviors depending on temperature and solvent concentration, with improved preservation of GUV integrity. This suggests a mode of compartment interaction that allows molecular exchange while maintaining structural continuity, which may be relevant for protocell models. Notably, this fusion behavior is not a general property of cryoprotectants but depends on specific physicochemical interactions between small molecules and lipid membranes. Lipid composition also played a critical role in determining fusion outcomes. In contrast to POPC vesicles, DOPC vesicles exhibited substantially lower internal content mixing efficiency and little temperature dependence, even under high DMSO conditions. Although membrane lipid mixing was observed in DOPC vesicles, it did not lead to efficient internal content mixing, indicating a decoupling between membrane exchange and content mixing. These differences highlight that efficient biochemical reactions in liposome-based systems require not only membrane fusion but also sufficient internal content mixing, which is strongly dependent on lipid composition.

Using this method, we successfully supplied substrates for RNA replication and demonstrated the propagation of replicated RNA into empty liposomes. In this study, replicating RNA is treated as the central informational entity, while vesicle fusion provides a mechanism for replenishing the molecular resources required for continued activity. This system can therefore be interpreted as a minimal model in which genetic information persists through repeated redistribution of both informational molecules and shared resources. Although enzymes are not consumed during catalysis, their effective concentration decreases due to redistribution across liposomes during fusion, necessitating continuous supply to sustain RNA replication. From this perspective, nucleotides and protein components function as a shared resource pool that supports RNA-based replication. Consistent with this view, RNA transfer between liposomes is expected to be bidirectional, reflecting stochastic redistribution of encapsulated components through fusion-mediated content mixing.

Serial fusion-replication cycles were achieved for at least seven generations without loss of liposome integrity. Unlike conventional freeze-thaw fusion, the GUV ratio remained stable, indicating that DMSO mediates fusion without compromising membrane structure. These results demonstrate that DMSO-mediated fusion enables sustained recursive activity while preserving compartment integrity. In the present passaging experiments, nutrient liposomes were supplemented at each generation because repeated DMSO-mediated fusion is accompanied by dilution and partial leakage of encapsulated components. Consistent with this, in the absence of additional nutrient liposomes (0× condition), RNA replication increased only during the first fusion cycle, likely due to residual substrates carried over from the previous generation, but did not increase further in subsequent cycles. These results indicate that while carryover activity can transiently support replication, it is insufficient to sustain propagation across multiple generations. Therefore, repeated nutrient supplementation was adopted to enable reliable evaluation of sustained passaging.

RNA dilution through leakage and redistribution highlights the importance of optimizing liposome ratios and temperature cycles for sustained replication. Molecular size is likely to affect exchange efficiency in this system. Smaller molecules may be more prone to leakage, whereas larger molecules may be exchanged less efficiently. At the same time, redistribution of Rep-RNA (2041 nt) indicates that relatively large RNA molecules can be transferred through DMSO-mediated liposome fusion. Compared with conventional freeze–thaw fusion19, the extent of RNA redistribution observed here is more limited, consistent with a milder exchange process. In contrast to emulsion-based transfer systems, where parasite RNAs dominate47,51,52, partial leakage in this system may influence RNA population dynamics, potentially biasing the system toward more stable or self-sustaining RNA species, representing an important direction for future investigation.

Overall, DMSO-mediated liposome fusion offers a stable and repeatable platform for studying recursive RNA replication and molecular evolution under prebiotic-like conditions, providing new insights into how genetic information could have persisted and propagated within primitive membrane-bound compartments. By enabling repeated redistribution of both genetic information and molecular resources across compartments, this system provides a minimal experimental model for understanding how such activity could be sustained through cycles of fusion and resource sharing.

Materials and methods

Materials

1-Palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) was derived from Nippon Fine Chemical (Osaka, Japan). 1,2-Dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC) was purchased from Yuka-Sangyo Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Liquid paraffin (0.86–0.89 g/mL at 20 °C), chloroform, dimethyl sulfoxide, and glycerol were purchased from Fujifilm Wako (Osaka, Japan). SYBR Green II was purchased from TaKaRa Bio (Shiga, Japan). RNase inhibitor was purchased from Promega (Madison, WI, USA). ATTO-633 conjugated 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE), and ATTO-488 conjugated DOPE were purchased from ATTO-TEC (Siegen, Germany). Transferrin Alexa Fluor 488 conjugate (TA488) and Transferrin Alexa Fluor 647 conjugate (TA647) were purchased from Life Technologies (Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Liposome preparation

Giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs) were prepared using the water-in-oil (w/o) emulsion transfer method19,39,53. Briefly, POPC was dissolved in chloroform at 0.1 mg/μL and mixed with liquid paraffin to obtain a 5 mg/mL lipid solution. The mixture was heated at 80 °C for 30 min, and ATTO633-DOPE was added to a final concentration of 5 μg/mL.

For inner content mixing experiments, 20 μL of the inner aqueous solution (50 mM HEPES–KOH, pH 8.0; 350 mM potassium glutamate; and 500 mM sucrose) containing 1.5 μM transferrin–Alexa Fluor 488 (TA488) or TA647 was added to 400 μL of the lipid–oil mixture to form the w/o emulsion. The emulsion was incubated on ice for 10 min. Subsequently, 400 μL of the emulsion was gently layered onto 200 μL of outer solution (50 mM HEPES–KOH, 350 mM potassium glutamate, and 1 M glucose) in a fresh tube and incubated on ice for another 10 min. The sample was centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, which was sufficient to sediment micrometer-sized vesicles despite the modest density difference between internal and external solutions. The liposome pellet was collected by puncturing the bottom of the tube with a needle, followed by centrifugation at 18,000 × g for 10 min. The resulting pellet was resuspended in incubation buffer (50 mM HEPES–KOH and 1 M glucose).

For RNA replication experiments, we used the RNA template encoded a modified β-subunit of Qβ replicase with enhanced replicability (Rep-RNA; 2041 bp)47. Liposomes were prepared with an inner solution containing 25 mM NTPs, 100 nM Qβ replicase, 125 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 5 mM MgCl₂, 0.01% BSA, Rep-RNA, 350 mM potassium glutamate, RNase inhibitor, and 500 mM sucrose. The outer solution contained 125 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 350 mM potassium glutamate, and 1 M glucose. The prepared liposomes were suspended in incubation buffer (125 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, and 350 mM potassium glutamate).

Rep-RNA replication inside liposomes

Rep-RNA replication inside liposomes was performed at 37 °C using purified Qβ replicase. The purified enzyme was kindly provided by Dr. Norikazu Ichihashi54. Liposomes were prepared with an inner solution containing 125 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 5 mM MgCl₂, 0.01% BSA, 500 mM sucrose, 350 mM potassium glutamate, 1.25 mM of each NTP, 20 nM purified Qβ replicase, and 5 nM Rep-RNA. The liposome suspensions were then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h for RNA replication assays.

Liposome fusion mediated by DMSO

Liposome suspensions containing equal numbers of liposomes were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was replaced with a fusion buffer consisting of 1 M glucose, 50 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 8.0), and 0–50% (v/v) DMSO. The samples were then incubated at −20 °C for 1 h, centrifuged again at 18,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was replaced with incubation buffer.

For Qβ-replicase experiments, the fusion buffer was optimized to contain 0.01% BSA, 5 mM MgCl₂, 125 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 1 M glucose, and 0–50% (v/v) DMSO. For RNA liposomes, the inner solution contained 125 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 5 mM MgCl₂, 0.01% BSA, 500 mM sucrose, 350 mM potassium glutamate, 50 mM HEPES–KOH (pH 7.6), and 5 nM Rep-RNA. Nutrient liposomes contained the same buffer composition, except that Rep-RNA was replaced with 6.25 mM of each NTP and 100 nM purified Qβ replicase. After fusion, the outer solution was replaced with incubation buffer (125 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, and 350 mM potassium glutamate), and the samples were subsequently incubated at 37 °C for 2 h.

Measuring RNA in liposomes by SYBR Green II

SYBR Green II was used to stain Rep-RNA encapsulated within liposomes. Before fluorescence measurement by flow cytometry or microscopy, SYBR Green II was added to the outer solution of liposomes at a final dilution of 1:10,000 and incubated on ice for 15 min. The outer solution was then replaced with fresh buffer to remove unbound dye.

Flow cytometry

Each liposome was analyzed by flow cytometry (FCM) using a FACS Canto II and FACS Aria II (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Data acquisition was performed using FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences). Flow cytometry data (.fcs files) were analyzed in R using custom scripts based on standard packages, including prada and ggplot2. Forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) were recorded to collect liposome data, and the region corresponding to giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs) was determined as previously described43,55.

Fluorescence from SYBR Green II and TA488 was excited with a 488 nm semiconductor laser, and the emission was detected using a 530 ± 15 nm band-pass filter (FITC-A). Fluorescence from ATTO633-DOPE lipids and TA647 was excited with a HeNe laser (633 nm) and detected with a 660 ± 10 nm band-pass filter (APC-A).

The GUV ratio was calculated by dividing the number of liposomes within the GUV region by the total number of detected particles. The total number of GUVs in each sample was then obtained by multiplying this ratio by the total number of detected particles measured under identical preparation conditions.

For inner content mixing assays, fluorescence intensities of TA488 and TA647 were measured for individual liposomes within the predefined GUV gate (Supplementary Fig. 1). Threshold values for encapsulated proteins were determined based on the fluorescence distributions of liposomes containing only one fluorescent protein. Specifically, thresholds were set at FITC-A ≥ 102.3 and APC-A ≥ 102.1. Thresholds were chosen to minimize false-positive detection based on control samples. Liposomes exhibiting fluorescence intensities above both thresholds were defined as inner content-mixed liposomes, and their proportion within the GUV population was reported as the inner content mixing ratio.

Similarly, the lipid mixing ratio was calculated as the proportion of liposomes within the GUV gate in which the fluorescence intensities of both fluorescent lipids exceeded their respective thresholds relative to the total GUV population. The fluorescence ratio between samples incubated at 4 °C and −20 °C was calculated as follows; for each fluorescent protein of interest, the average fluorescence intensity of liposomes containing that protein (i.e., those with fluorescence intensities above the threshold) after incubation at −20 °C was divided by the average fluorescence intensity of the same population after incubation at 4 °C.

Fluorescence microscopy

Fluorescence images of liposomes were acquired using an Axio Observer Z1 microscope (Carl Zeiss) equipped with a 63× Plan-Neofluar oil-immersion objective lens (NA = 1.4). SYBR Green II and TA647 fluorescence were excited using an LED light source at 470 nm and detected with a 525 ± 25 nm band-pass filter. ATTO633-DOPE fluorescence was excited with an LED at 590 nm and detected with a 629 ± 31 nm band-pass filter.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times (n = 3), and data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), unless otherwise noted. Each replicate corresponds to an independently prepared liposome sample subjected to identical experimental procedures, including liposome formation, DMSO treatment, and subsequent analyses.

Flow cytometry data were acquired for at least 100,000 events per sample, and analyses were restricted to events within a predefined GUV gate to exclude debris and non-vesicular particles. Fluorescence thresholds for defining positive populations (e.g., SYBR Green II–positive or double-positive liposomes) were determined based on control samples lacking the corresponding fluorescent signal.

Statistical analyses were performed using R. For comparisons involving two independent variables, two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. For experiments involving three independent variables, three-way ANOVA was applied. When a significant main effect or interaction was detected, post hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. For single-factor comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test was used. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. The sample size was chosen based on common practice in the field and previous studies using similar experimental systems. All reported experiments were successfully reproduced with consistent trends across independent replicates.
