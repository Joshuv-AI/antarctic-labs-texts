Solid organ transplantation in people with HIV: Outcomes, obstacles and opportunities
Authors: David W J Griffin, J H McMahon, D A Ruschena, J F Hoy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePeople with HIV experience disparate access to solid organ transplantation (SOT), despite mounting evidence for comparable outcomes. This review aims to understand the outcomes following SOT, obstacles to SOT and opportunities to improve access and outcomes following SOT.MethodsWe conducted a narrative review of the literature.ResultsSOT is safe and effective for people with HIV, and increasing evidence indicates that people with HIV may safely donate organs to others with HIV. People with HIV experience a higher risk of acute rejection following SOT, without a higher risk of graft loss or death, when compared with appropriately matched controls. Those with HIV experience systemic and structural barriers to SOT, in addition to barriers that occur at the level of healthcare providers and consumers. Opportunities exist to improve access to SOT, and to improve SOT‐related care for people with HIV.ConclusionSOT should be considered standard of care for people with HIV when indicated. Further efforts are required to identify and address gaps in the SOT‐related cascade of care; and to improve SOT outcomes for people with HIV.Keywords: comorbidities, health equity, HIV, solid organ transplantation

INTRODUCTION

People with HIV are living longer due to effective antiretroviral therapy (ART) [1]. Contemporary data indicate that the life expectancy of people with HIV approaches and, in some models, exceed that of the general population [2, 3]. However, people with HIV experience a greater burden of age‐associated comorbidities, likely due to a combination of lifestyle factors, immune dysregulation, chronic inflammation, long‐term side effects and legacy effects of ART [4, 5, 6]. Moreover, people with HIV can experience organ disease attributable specifically to HIV infection [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Therefore, there is a rising prevalence of end‐stage organ disease (ESOD) among people with HIV, warranting consideration of solid organ transplantation (SOT).

People with HIV were historically excluded from SOT due to poor outcomes associated with HIV infection, concern that transplant immunosuppression may accelerate HIV disease progression, that transplanted organs would have reduced survival in people with HIV, and would not be an appropriate use of scarce organs [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Some jurisdictions, including the United States and Australia, specifically prohibited people with HIV from donating blood, tissue and/or organs through legislation and regulatory frameworks [18, 19].

SOT improves life expectancy and quality of life for people with ESOD, and people with HIV experience similar benefits to those without HIV. Kidney transplantation (KTx) conferred an almost 80% reduction in mortality at 5 years for people with HIV who had end stage kidney disease compared with those remaining on dialysis [20] with similar benefits after liver transplantation (LTx) [21].

Despite the potential benefits of SOT in people with HIV with a higher incidence of ESOD and improved life expectancy with ART, people with HIV still experience additional barriers to SOT [22]. This review aims to explore the outcomes following SOT, the obstacles to SOT experienced by people with HIV and how access to this life‐saving intervention could be improved.

OUTCOMES FOLLOWING SOLID ORGAN TRANSPLANTATION IN PEOPLE WITH HIV

Kidney

Early studies of KTx among people with HIV demonstrated comparable graft and recipient survival at one and three years after KTx with other recipients in the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (SRTR) [23]. However, a higher rate of acute rejection in people with HIV compared with controls was observed at one year (31% vs. 12.3%). Factors predicting rejection included cyclosporin use, anti‐thymocyte globulin induction, delayed graft function and hepatitis C virus (HCV) co‐infection, while having a living donor was protective [23]. A higher incidence of acute rejection, especially in the first year following KTx, has been consistent across studies of people with HIV, but remains mechanistically poorly understood [23, 24, 25, 26]. Cyclosporin use was associated with an almost three‐fold increased risk of rejection compared with tacrolimus, [27] and HCV co‐infection predicted graft loss and death in other studies [28, 29]. Identifying these predictors of poorer outcomes presents opportunities to improve SOT‐related care for people with HIV.

Subsequent SRTR data demonstrated an increasing frequency of KTx among people with HIV in the United States, along with a 45% reduction in graft loss and 41% reduced mortality in those undergoing transplantation after 2008; [28] this decrease appeared unrelated to transplant centre‐level experience, but instead was associated with improvements in HIV care, including the introduction of integrase inhibitors.

Liver

Early studies exploring LTx outcomes in a cohort of people with HCV suggested that HIV infection increased the risk of graft loss and death [30]. However, Sawinski and colleagues demonstrated that while HCV/HIV co‐infection more than doubled the risk of graft loss and death in LTx recipients, HIV mono‐infection did not [31]. A subsequent study exploring LTx outcomes for people with HCV/HIV coinfection corroborated that the observed poorer outcomes for people with HIV following LTx likely relate to HCV/HIV coinfection rather than HIV itself and highlighted the importance of HCV treatment. The authors demonstrated that HIV/HCV coinfection was associated with an 85% higher risk of graft failure prior to the introduction of directly acting antivirals (DAAs) for HCV but that the risk was comparable to the general population after the introduction of DAA therapy [32]. Moreover, this study found that HIV mono‐infection was not associated with graft loss in either the pre‐ or post‐DAA periods and highlighted the increasing frequency of LTx for people with HIV with time [32]. Importantly, DAAs are safe, with comparable effectiveness to the general population in people with HIV following LTx [33]. LTx for people with HIV with hepatitis B virus (HBV) coinfection confers a relatively good prognosis due to the availability of HBV antiviral treatment, with outcomes similar to those without HIV [34]. Although liver retransplantation is uncommon in people with HIV, previous data illustrate that HCV infection confers a poor prognosis, while outcomes are acceptable among those with HIV with HBV and who have negative polymerase chain reaction for HCV [35].

Consistent with the general population, non‐viral indications for LTx surpassed viral hepatitis in people with HIV in 2018 [36]. Outcomes for people with HIV following LTx for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) are also comparable to non‐HIV‐infected controls, with similar rates of HCC recurrence and overall survival observed [37]. In some people with HIV with HCC, hepatectomy may be a reasonable alternative to LTx, although perhaps with an additional risk of disease recurrence [38].

While those with HIV with a model of end stage liver disease (MELD) score ≥ 15 experience a 90% reduction in mortality compared with those remaining on the waitlist, this benefit was not seen in those with a MELD score of <15 [21, 37]. Recent data show the long‐term graft survival among people with HIv with KTx and/or LTx appears comparable with matched controls extending beyond 10–15 years, when adjusted for HCV infection [39].

Lung

Although the experience with thoracic organ transplantation in people with HIV is lagging, emerging evidence indicates that this is also safe and effective [40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48]. A study of 59 lung transplant (LuTx) recipients with HIV compared with propensity‐matched controls demonstrated that people with HIV had comparable short‐ and medium‐term survival up to three years, despite a higher lung allocation score (LAS) at transplantation [49]. The LAS was used to prioritize people in the United States for LuTx based on clinical urgency for and likelihood of benefit from LuTx [50].

A retrospective analysis of registry data in the United States compared outcomes of primary LuTx in 67 people with HIV with 17 420 controls [51]. The frequency of LuTx for people with HIV had increased with time, with 64% of transplants occurring after 2020. Despite those with HIV having higher pulmonary arterial pressures and higher LAS than controls, [51] there was no difference in graft or recipient survival between groups over 3 years [51]. While the higher incidence of pulmonary hypertension in people with HIV may explain the higher pulmonary pressures observed, [52] the higher LAS seen in people with HIV may reflect poorer access to LuTx, in that a higher LAS may reflect a greater clinical urgency due to longer transplant waiting time.

Heart

The available literature on heart transplantation (HTx) among people with HIV is also reassuring [53, 54, 55]. A study of 41 people with HIV undergoing HTx from 2004 to 2017 demonstrated similar survival compared with 41 propensity‐matched controls at 30 days, 1 year and 5 years of 97.6%, 85.9% and 77.3%, respectively [48]. The rate of acute rejection at one year (39%) was higher than expected, while cardiac allograft vasculopathy (32%) and malignancy (19%) occurred at similar rates to controls [48] in keeping with other studies [41, 56, 57]. Longer hospital length of stay, higher rejection rates, but fewer infection‐related hospitalizations have been reported following HTx for people with HIV compared with controls [56].

These data indicate that HIV should not be considered a contraindication for SOT.

People with HIV as organ donors

Utilizing organs from donors with HIV (HIV D+) for KTx was pioneered in South Africa, due to limited dialysis resources and growth in the proportion of deceased donors with HIV [58, 59]. Graft survival of 96% and 79% and overall survival of 87% and 84% at 1 and 5 years was reported in 51 people with HIV in receipt of HIV D+ organs [60, 61]. Although donor virus was detectable in plasma samples from 32% of recipients, sustained superinfection was not. No transmitted drug resistance or ART modification for virologic failure occurred [60].

The US HIV Organ Procurement Equity (HOPE) Act reversed a ban on the donation of organs from people with HIV in the United States in 2013 [18, 62]. This initially allowed SOT between people with HIV under research protocols, with the first HIV D+ HOPE transplant occurring in 2016 [62, 63]. Excellent HIV and KTx outcomes among recipients with HIV, regardless of donor status, are reported, albeit with a trend to higher rejection in HIV D+ recipients compared with donors without HIV [64, 65]. Overall and graft survival, graft function, infections and malignancy are similar, regardless of donor status [64]. Those receiving kidneys from people with HIV had three‐fold higher likelihood of detectable HIV viraemia post‐transplant, but only one person was recorded to experience superinfection, without needing modification of their ART [65]. Although uncommon, living‐donor KTx appears safe, with good outcomes for both recipients and donors [66, 67, 68, 69, 70].

Reports of HIV D+ LTx have also been reassuring [71, 72, 73]. Outcomes of 24 HIV D+ LTx with 21 HIV donor negative (D−) transplants among people with HIV revealed around half of HIV D‐ livers were from people with false‐positive HIV tests [74]. Donors with HIV were more frequently seropositive for cytomegalovirus (CMV) (88% vs. 62%, p = 0.08) and human herpes virus‐8 (25% vs. 0%, p = 0.15), while HIV D+ recipients experienced more opportunistic infections (OIs); predominantly CMV viraemia in high‐risk CMV mismatch without prophylaxis and Kaposi's sarcoma [74]. There was no difference in rejection‐free survival or graft failure between groups; however, overall and cancer‐free survival appeared reduced in those receiving HIV D+ livers. However, more data are warranted.

To date, only one HIV D+ HTx with concurrent KTx has been reported, with good outcomes [75].

OBSTACLES TO SOLID ORGAN TRANSPLANTATION FOR PEOPLE WITH HIV

SOT for people with HIV is now a realistic treatment option for ESOD in multiple jurisdictions [25, 26, 36, 43, 47, 76, 77, 78, 79] and is standard of care for people with HIV where indicated [77, 79, 80, 81, 82] However, people with HIV face additional barriers to transplantation [83, 84, 85] (Figure 1).

In the United States, people with HIV who might benefit from SOT are less likely to be referred, less likely to be waitlisted, more likely to be de‐listed, wait longer for their first transplant offer and receive fewer offers overall [22, 83, 86]. People with HIV who do undergo SOT frequently wait longer for poorer quality organs and receive fewer organs from living donors than controls [83, 84, 86]. One study suggested that this disparity was not due to comorbid disease or rurality alone, with the authors proposing that it might relate to socioeconomic factors or hesitancy among transplant programmes and healthcare providers (HCPs) relating to HIV [85]. Previous studies have identified non‐HIV barriers to SOT for people with HIV, including inability to complete the evaluation process, higher immunological sensitization, substance use and schizophraenia [84, 86, 87, 88, 89]. Similarly, the presence of ART resistance and use of protease inhibitors have been associated with a lower likelihood of transplantation, perhaps due to drug–drug interactions with transplant immunosuppression, while others speculate that it might relate to the added risk of rejection or relapse of HIV‐associated nephropathy [85, 87]. This hesitancy is consistent with historical care inequities experienced by people with HIV [90, 91].

Clinical considerations for solid organ transplantation in people with HIV

People with HIV should be assessed for SOT in accordance with standard criteria for prospective recipients without HIV. Careful planning, including optimizing ART to mitigate drug–drug interactions; addressing HCV and HBV co‐infections pre‐transplant; preventing OIs and screening for malignancies, affords an opportunity to improve outcomes following SOT for people with HIV [80, 92, 93, 94, 95]. Contemporary data suggest that modern integrase inhibitor‐based ART has no significant impact on the pharmacokinetics of commonly used immunosuppressants, like tacrolimus and mycophenolate, [96] though the impact of newer injectable agents is uncertain [97]. However, it is unclear whether the risk of OIs or malignancies is elevated among people with HIV post‐SOT [93, 98]. Additionally, for recipients of HIV D+ organs, the relevance of HIV superinfection remains uncertain [74, 94, 99, 100, 101].

HCP barriers to transplantation for people with HIV

HCPs play a central role in identifying organ dysfunction, referral to transplant specialists, the evaluation of SOT candidates, decision‐making around waitlisting for transplantation and the transplant process [102, 103]. The selection of recipients often follows a utilitarian approach, aiming for the best use of scarce organs, by need and expected benefit. However, decision‐makers and guidelines are frequently risk averse, leading to the underutilization of otherwise ideal donors, even when the residual risk of blood‐borne virus transmission is low, contributing to disparate access to SOT for people with HIV [83, 88, 104, 105, 106]. Understanding HCP knowledge and attitudes towards SOT for people with HIV is crucial. Some HCPs and programmes still perceive HIV to be a contraindication to SOT [107]. In a 2017 survey of US transplant centres, 30% of adult and 26% of paediatric centres considered HIV an absolute contraindication to SOT, and 35% and 51% considered HIV a relative contraindication, respectively [108]. The majority of LuTx programmes (59%) considered HIV an absolute contraindication, while 17% of kidney centres did. Higher volume centres were 80% less likely to consider HIV a contraindication [108]. A survey of US nephrologists demonstrated heterogeneity in the interpretation of SOT evaluation criteria, though 38% indicated that HIV was a relative contraindication to KTx [109].

A discrete choice experiment among organ procurement organization (OPO) staff in the United States revealed that HIV infection was associated with a lower likelihood of pursuing a donor than HCV infection, being unregistered to donate or a donor with lesser organ potential [110]. Subsequent semi‐structured interviews among OPO staff revealed multiple barriers for people with HIV [111]. These included stigmatizing generalizations about housing insecurity; complex family dynamics; concern and confusion about the need for disclosure of HIV to a prospective donor's next of kin; additional financial risk; and clinical burden in assessing a donor with HIV [111]. Some participants described ‘looking’ for reasons to exclude or for not ‘moving forward’ with donors with HIV, and a fear of occupational transmission of HIV to OPO or HCPs [111].

Occupational transmission of HIV is exceedingly rare in the setting of universal precautions, treatment as prevention, and post‐exposure prophylaxis [112, 113]. Nevertheless, an early survey among 371 transplant surgeons reported that the risk of HIV acquisition from a percutaneous exposure was frequently overestimated, and participants expressed greater fear and potential harm from HIV than from other blood‐borne viruses [107]. Surgeons estimated that people with HIV had a reduced survival following SOT, comparable to recipients with HCV, while 40% of respondents thought people with HIV were potentially appropriate SOT candidates; significantly less than for people with HBV or HCV. This is consistent with more contemporary data, where those with HIV were 25% less likely to receive an organ [83]. Older clinicians and thoracic surgeons were less likely to believe people with HIV were appropriate SOT candidates, as were those with incremental fear of and previous occupational exposure to HIV [107]. These findings highlight inherent biases prohibiting people with HIV from accessing SOT [83].

People with HIV face additional practical barriers to SOT, given the need to satisfy HIV‐specific criteria, including viral suppression, CD4 T lymphocyte count ≥200 cells/μL, optimized ART, additional infection risk assessment and mitigation and the absence of some previous OIs [88, 114].

Providers play an important role in counselling those with HIV about their ability to donate; though the additional effort, limited time and the efficiency of processes by which people with HIV are registered as donors may impact the ability to increase donor registration [115].

Consumer barriers to solid organ transplantation

Although numerous studies explore public perceptions around organ donation and transplantation, [116, 117, 118, 119] there are limited data exploring the attitudes of people with HIV about organ donation and transplantation. Important in this is their willingness to donate and accept organs from increased risk donors (IRD), deemed to be at higher likelihood for infection with viral hepatitis and HIV infection and donors with HIV.

Several studies in high income countries demonstrate that the willingness of those with HIV to donate organs is high. A survey of 114, mostly African American people with HIV in Baltimore, USA, showed that almost 80% of participants were willing to donate their organs, while over 60% were willing to be living donors [120]. Although 93% had disclosed their HIV status, only 15% had discussed donation with their family. Only 20% were registered as donors, possibly reflecting the limited knowledge of pathways to donation in this cohort [120].

Another study in a US Ryan White clinic (which primarily serves people with HIV who are uninsured or underinsured) found only 11% of clinic attendees were registered organ donors, with 21% indicating HIV precluded them from donating, while others indicated non‐HIV comorbidities, religion or distrust in the health system as barriers [115]. A separate survey in the United States identified altruism as an important factor in willingness to donate, but a small proportion of participants were concerned about the additional financial burden of donation for them or their family, disfigurement of their body prior to burial and about their organs being harvested prior to death [120]. Nevertheless, more than 80% trusted the medical system to handle the donation process [120]. Factors associated with reluctance to be living donors included needing to change ART, needing surgery or fear that HIV would increase the likelihood of poor health following donation [120]. Similar concerns were described elsewhere [121].

A United Kingdom (UK) study, conducted in 2016, indicated that 62% of people with HIV would donate to others with HIV, while 22% were uncertain about their willingness to donate [122]. In this small study, ‘Black‐African’ ethnicity was associated with 3.8‐fold higher uncertainty about donating compared with Caucasian participants [122]. In Taiwan, 72% of 1010 people with HIV were willing to donate their organs posthumously [123]. Predictors of higher willingness to donate included tertiary education, being registered to donate and knowledge of HIV D+ SOT in the United States [123].

A US qualitative study found that people with HIV interested in living donation emphasized altruism, reducing organ wait‐times and faith‐based motivations [121]. HIV‐specific motivations included ‘solidarity’ with others with HIV, a desire to reduce HIV‐related stigma, and a sense of indebtedness to science [121].

A survey of 116 people with HIV in the United States indicated willingness to accept organs from deceased (84%) and living (87%) HIV D+ to be high [124]. Moreover, 70% of people with HIV found organs from IRD to be acceptable. Approximately 30% reported concerns about HIV superinfection, though most (71%) indicated that they would still accept an HIV D+ organ [124]. This is higher than an earlier survey of people with HIV attending a UK sexual health clinic, where 55% indicated that they would consider receiving an HIV D+ organ, while 27% were unsure [122]. Concerns among those not willing to accept HIV D+ organs included the belief that HIV D+ organs were less safe and would not function as expected, that they might acquire another infection from their donor and that donation may cause harm to the living donor with HIV. Nevertheless, almost all (99%) participants believed that people with HIV should have the choice to donate their organs, that HIV D+ transplantation should be researched (96%) and that it could reduce HIV‐related stigma (76%), [124] similar to previous work [120]. Factors influencing willingness to accept IRD organs include expected equivalent organ performance, discussions with the transplantation team and belief that IRD transplantation reduces waitlist times for themselves and others.

Conversely, a recent study among people with HIV in Northern Uganda reported a lower willingness to donate (35%) than higher income settings [125]. Most participants were aware of SOT (80%) and agreed that it was ‘needed’ (87%) and beneficial (89%); personal and family experience was uncommon; and most reported limited education. Female sex, higher income, tertiary education, knowledge about SOT and attendance at a SOT campaign were associated with increased willingness to donate. Participants not willing to donate their organs indicated body disfigurement (32%), misuse or trafficking of their organs (13%) and religious beliefs (4%) as their main barriers. Other respondents felt that their HIV or health status precluded them from donating [125].

Systemic and structural issues driving reduced opportunities for solid organ transplantation for people with HIV

Social and structural determinants of health are important drivers of the disparate access to SOT experienced by people with HIV. African American and Hispanic individuals are overrepresented among those with HIV in the United States. However, non‐Caucasian race is associated with reduced survival on renal replacement therapy and access to transplantation for people with HIV [51, 89]. Among people with HIV on haemodialysis, non‐Caucasian race was associated with 45% decreased survival compared with controls, despite African American and Hispanic dialysis recipients apparently having a paradoxical survival advantage compared with their white counterparts in the general dialysis population. This disparity may reflect reduced access to transplantation (especially living kidney donation) among non‐Caucasians, differences in nutritional and inflammatory covariates, disparate access to healthcare and other selection biases [126, 127]. Others have described how intersectionality exacerbates disparate SOT access among people with HIV of Black race. In a data linkage study, HIV reduced access to KTx by 50%, while Black race reduced access by 12%. However, this effect was additive; Black people with HIV experienced a 65% lower likelihood of SOT, even after adjustment for important confounders [128]. Subsequent work by this group showed that a large proportion of this disparity remained unexplained [129].

Several studies have demonstrated geographical differences in access to SOT among people with HIV [48, 83]. A French study demonstrated that most people with HIV on dialysis undergoing KTx accessed care in the Parisian area, compared with other regions [86]. Similarly, of 140 HTx centres in the United States, only 24 (~17%) performed HTx for people with HIV over a 13‐year period, with approximately one third conducted in New York and surrounds [48]. A similarly small proportion of transplant centres (10%) had local protocols to perform SOT between people with HIV, [130] and though more had protocols planned, 56% were in eastern United States [131]. Centres planning HIV D+ protocols had higher transplant volumes, a proportion of IRD organs and county HIV prevalence, and more people with HIV waitlisted at their institution [131].

The acceptability of people with HIV as organ donors and recipients has improved, [29, 132] and guidance has become more inclusive for people with HIV over time. However, the language used in guidelines and medical literature frequently describes HIV as ‘ruling‐out’ or ‘contraindicating’ donation and SOT and uses risk‐based and stigmatizing language, though some acknowledge the use of conservative estimates around the likelihood of HIV transmission, disregarding the high effectiveness of biomedical prevention modalities [82, 133, 134, 135, 136]. Similarly, language used in public‐facing information can be confusing, exclusionary and stigmatizing [137, 138]. For example, the UK National Health Service Blood and Transplant website indicates that donors that ‘have or are suspected of having’ HIV cannot donate organs, but that people with HIV can donate in rare instances and should consider registering [138, 139]. The language used by HCPs plays an important role in stigma perpetuation and impacts healthcare quality for individuals [137]. The impact of stigma on healthcare engagement, disease‐related outcomes and quality of life is well‐described in the HIV‐literature, [140, 141], with organizations, including Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) [142] and the Centres for Disease Control [143] committing to the use of person‐centred language.

Ethical and legal considerations

While SOT from donors with HIV to recipients without HIV (HIV D+/R−) is generally an accepted contraindication, it has occurred both inadvertently and intentionally, without compromise to graft function or survival. Although donors are routinely screened for blood‐borne viruses, cases of inadvertent HIV transmission have occurred following SOT, when a donor has been tested within the HIV window period, acquired HIV between screening and donation or due to laboratory error [144, 145, 146]. Nucleic acid‐based testing reduces but does not eliminate this possibility, compared with serology [105]. Intentional LTx from a mother with HIV to her child without HIV was performed safely in South Africa, [147] where HIV D+/R− transplantation can occur, within a formalized research programme [147, 148]. The risk of HIV transmission through SOT, in the setting of a donor with virological suppression and early initiation of ART in the recipient, remains unknown [77]. Regardless, some proponents suggest that this could become a therapeutic option for some recipients, who might otherwise not receive SOT [138]. Clinicians and SOT recipients would need to weigh up the risk and potential impact of HIV infection, with the potential benefits of reduced time to transplantation, waitlist mortality and quality of life [149]. In some countries, legislative barriers have existed to specifically prohibit HIV‐positive organ donation [18, 132, 150, 151]. Moreover, despite the HOPE Act removing a federal ban on organ donation from people with HIV in the United States in 2013, some state laws continue to specifically restrict or criminalize this practice, limiting implementation and highlighting the challenges with legal inconsistencies across jurisdictions [152, 153].

Key legal considerations about the use of organs from people with HIV, especially HIV D+/R− in jurisdictions where this is not specifically prohibited, would be the legality of intentional HIV exposure, validity of consent and what ‘informed’ consent entails [149]. Laws in numerous jurisdictions criminalize transmission or exposure to HIV or have applied other legal provisions in local cases of sex, biting or spitting. These laws are often related to historic fear and stigma around HIV, rather than contemporary evidence [154, 155]. However, it is unclear how these laws would be influenced by the doctrine of necessity; where SOT and possible HIV transmission may be necessary to save a recipient's life. Historically, consent to HIV transmission was limited by the rule that one could not validly consent to an activity that inflicted grievous physical injury [150]. There are now several examples in case law in Commonwealth countries that confirm that a fully informed person can consent to the risk of HIV transmission [156, 157, 158].

Although data on the acceptability of HIV D+/R− transplantation are lacking, studies of SOT recipients without HCV who are offered or receive organs from donors with HCV report prospective transplant candidates having positive experiences [159, 160, 161]. These studies highlight the important role that effective treatment of HCV, earlier access to SOT and improved life expectancy for organ recipients have in the acceptability of organs from donors with HCV. Conversely, recipients raised concerns about the additional costs of therapy and the impact of potentially acquiring HCV infection on relationships and intimacy [159, 160, 161]. There is growing interest in Xenotransplantation, whereby organs from other species could be used to overcome human organ shortages; though concerns about zoonotic infections, including retroviruses, have been raised [162, 163]. While data around public perceptions are limited, a 2019 US KTx centre study suggests that acceptability may be high if outcomes are similar to standard SOT and highlights potential provider biases that may impact implementation [164]. Though the use of organs from people with HIV in the general population is unlikely to overcome the organ shortage in most jurisdictions, it seems there is a relative aversion to the use of organs from people with HIV in favor of the use of organs from different species, where the risks are unknown.

Ethical issues around organ donation from people with HIV have been explored elsewhere [148, 165, 166, 167]. Briefly, key issues include the need to balance beneficence with non‐maleficence, that is, expanding the donor pool to ensure people with HIV and others who may benefit have timely access to life‐saving SOT, with the additional benefit of potentially reducing HIV stigma, while understanding and mitigating additional individual and public health harms, not limited to HIV transmission or superinfection, which might complicate HIV D+ SOT [165, 166]. Secondly, supporting autonomous people with HIV to register and ultimately volunteer as organ donors, while also empowering and respecting the autonomy of informed transplant candidates to consent to receive organs from a person with HIV without coercion [167]. Finally, the principle of justice, whereby people with HIV have equitable access to SOT, along with equal distribution of benefits and burdens [165, 167].

OPPORTUNITIES TO IMPROVE ACCESS TO SOLID ORGAN TRANSPLANTATION FOR PEOPLE WITH HIV

Important practical and research opportunities exist to optimize access to SOT and transplant‐related care for people with HIV (Table 1). Briefly, SOT access could be enhanced by identifying and addressing barriers faced at a structural, consumer and provider level (Figure 1) and may vary by region. Awareness among people with HIV and HCPs should be increased through targeted education and engagement with relevant professional and community organizations about the eligibility of people with HIV to receive and potentially donate organs, the comparable outcomes observed in people with HIV following SOT, and to dispel myths that may contribute to HIV‐related stigma in healthcare. Moreover, guidelines should reflect contemporary evidence and use clear, permissive and non‐stigmatizing language. HCPs and OPOs should be cognizant of implicit and explicit biases affecting people with HIV. Moreover, clinicians should optimize HIV‐related care pre‐ and peri‐transplant, involving a multidisciplinary team of clinicians with experience in HIV medicine, transplant infectious diseases and pharmacists in collaboration with the transplant team. This is important to navigate the nuances of transplant‐related care for people with HIV, to minimize toxicities, prevent infectious complications and advocate for those with HIV who may require SOT or wish to donate.

The use of HIV D+ organs may provide a niche pool of donors for people with HIV, while reducing demand on the remaining donor pool. Recent evidence highlights HIV D+ SOT as an opportunity to enhance access to KTx for people with HIV. Waitlist candidates with HIV willing to accept kidneys from other people with HIV were 3‐fold more likely to undergo KTx, received higher quality organs from donors with fewer comorbidities and, on average, received their organs 50 months earlier than standard waitlist candidates. In this study, 20% of organs were from donors with false‐positive HIV tests [168] which would previously have been excluded from transplantation, a phenomenon reported elsewhere [169, 170].

Though experience is limited, HIV D+/R− transplantation may become routine. While unlikely to solve organ shortages in many countries, this practice may have a more substantial impact in countries where HIV prevalence is high, especially if the risk of HIV transmission from a virologically suppressed donor to a recipient given pre‐ or a defined period of post‐exposure prophylaxis is low or zero [132, 147, 148]. In some individuals, it may provide an additional opportunity to obtain a life‐saving transplant and help to reduce HIV stigma.

CONCLUSION

Overwhelmingly, evidence supports SOT for people with HIV when indicated as standard of care, and a growing body of evidence indicates that D+/R+ SOT is safe and effective. As HCPs, we have a responsibility to ensure that people with HIV, who have a life expectancy approaching that of the general population, have equitable access to SOT by identifying and addressing structural and systemic barriers and those that exist among providers and consumers (Figure 1).
