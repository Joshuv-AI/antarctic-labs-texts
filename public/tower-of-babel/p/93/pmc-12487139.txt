EEG microstates, spectral analysis, and risk prediction in epilepsy comorbid with mild cognitive impairment: alteration in intrinsic brain activity
Authors: Shenzhi Fang, Shenggen Chen, Lizhen Chen, Hanbin Lin, Changyun Liu, Chunhui Che, Wenting Xiong, Yuying Zhang, Juan Li, Luyan Wu, Xinming Huang, Huapin Huang, Wanhui Lin, Chaofeng Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the differences in electroencephalogram (EEG) microstates and power spectrum between patients with epilepsy (PWE) comorbid with (without) mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and to develop a machine learning model to predict the risk of MCI comorbidity in PWE.MethodParticipants were classified into PWE comorbid with MCI (EPMCI) and PWE comorbid without MCI (EPNMCI). The microstate parameters and power spectral density (PSD) of both groups were compared. We combined different types of variables and constructed models using Support Vector Machine (SVM), Neural Network (NNET), Random Forest (RF), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Naive Bayes (NB). An ideal predictive model was selected to evaluate the risk of MCI comorbidity in PWE.ResultA total of 627 PWE were included in this study, of whom 106 had MCI and 521 did not. Significant differences were observed between the two groups of patients in microstates A, B, C, D, and PSD. Among various machine learning models and multiple variable groups, we selected the NNET model based on microstate variables as the optimal model. It demonstrated the second-highest ROCAUC value (0.93), the highest accuracy (0.89), the lowest standard error (0.11), and superior calibration metrics, including the highest discrimination index (D = 0.724), the lowest Brier score (0.084), and the smallest unreliability index (U = 0.006). Finally, we compared this model with the traditional MMSE decision curve analysis (DCA) and found that it exhibited a wider range of applicable thresholds and a greater overall net benefit, demonstrating enhanced clinical utility.ConclusionDifferences in EEG microstates analysis and spectral analysis provide evidence for the mechanisms and dynamic changes associated with epilepsy comorbid with MCI. The development of a predictive model offers guidance for the assessment of MCI in specific populations with epilepsy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07023-y.Keywords: Epilepsy, Cognition, Microstate, Spectrum, Machine learning

Introduction

Patients with epilepsy (PWE) frequently present with comorbid behavioral and cognitive issues that interact in complex ways, adversely influencing treatment, prognosis, and healthcare costs [1, 2]. Studies have found that epilepsy can lead to cognitive decline. Multiple factors may contribute to this phenomenon. Firstly, it relates to the underlying etiologies of epilepsy, which include acquired causes such as traumatic brain injury, cerebral infarction, and encephalitis, as well as congenital causes like Dravet syndrome and fragile X syndrome [6]. Secondly, epilepsy itself may induce cognitive impairments, potentially due to alterations in brain networks [3–5]. Cognitive function relies on complex neural networks, and abnormal neuronal discharges in epilepsy can disrupt the integrity of these networks [7]. Additionally, studies have identified excessive tau protein phosphorylation in epilepsy patients, further impairing cognitive function [8]. Epilepsy affects various cognitive domains, including memory, executive function, naming, and psychomotor skills [9–11]. Therefore, following the diagnosis of epilepsy, it is crucial to detect cognitive decline early to develop effective treatment plans. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is characterized by cognitive decline that falls below expectations based on an individual’s age and education, without significant impairment in daily functioning and not meeting the diagnostic criteria for dementia [12]. Compared to healthy individuals, patients with MCI have an increased risk of progressing to dementia. Moreover, the prognosis of MCI is uncertain, as some patients may revert to normal cognitive function. Therefore, early identification of MCI is essential for preserving cognitive health [13]. Currently, commonly used cognitive impairment assessment methods in clinical practice include the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). However, both MMSE and MoCA have certain limitations: assessing certain populations, such as those under 18, individuals with communication impairments, illiterates, and elderly individuals with low educational levels, poses difficulties. Therefore, an alternative assessment method is needed for identification.

In recent years, an increasing number of studies have focused on resting-state Electroencephalography (EEG), which is recorded without any active sensory, motor, or cognitive tasks [14]. Compared to EEG during seizure phases, resting-state EEG is safer and can avoid electromyographic interference during seizures. However, EEG signals exhibit multidimensionality and randomness, which necessitate the use of additional analytical methods to quantify EEG features. Commonly employed techniques include microstate analysis and spectral analysis.

Microstate analysis represents scalp EEG signals through topographical maps. These topographical maps remain stable for a short period (80–120 milliseconds) before rapidly transitioning to subsequent EEG topographies. Each brief, discrete EEG topography, known as a “microstate,” is analyzed using correlational computations, thereby reflecting the discontinuities and nonlinear changes in the brain with high temporal resolution. Currently, four commonly used microstates (A, B, C, and D) can account for most of the variance in global brain topographies. These four states are associated with language processing, visual processing, subjective experience processing, and attentional and analytical processing, respectively [15]. fMRI-EEG studies have shown that microstate A is typically associated with the auditory network (AN), microstate B with the visual network (VN), microstate C with the salience network (SN) and the default mode network (DMN), and microstate D with the dorsal attention network (DAN) [15]. Currently, only a few studies have identified differences in microstates between epilepsy patients with comorbid cognitive impairment and those without cognitive impairment [16].

Spectral analysis utilizes the Fourier transformation to convert EEG signals into power spectral density (PSD), thereby allowing for the comparison of changes in EEG energy across different frequency bands and locations [17]. Current research has identified differences in EEG power spectra between PWE and cognitive impairment compared to normal individuals. Jedynak, Pons, and others found that before the occurrence of epileptic-like activity, the spectral power in the theta and delta frequency bands is enhanced [18]. In adolescents with myoclonic epilepsy, resting-state EEG exhibits higher PSD at low alpha frequencies [19]. Multiple studies have found significant changes in PSD at the alpha, delta, and theta frequency bands in patients with MCI and dementia, primarily demonstrated by an increase in the PSD of alpha and delta and a decrease in theta [20]. Furthermore, some studies have identified that changes in the power of the beta band lead to cognitive impairment [21, 22]. However, current spectral analysis research related to cognitive impairment primarily focuses on Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, multiple sclerosis, and various psychiatric disorders [23, 24]. Research on EEG spectral analysis of MCI comorbid with epilepsy is relatively scarce.

In addition to the EEG changes that may occur in epilepsy comorbid with MCI, various factors are also related to this condition. Therefore, by combining multiple relevant indicators, it is possible to predict the risk of MCI comorbidity in PWE, facilitating early detection and intervention. This can be achieved through machine learning. Currently, machine learning is widely used in the medical field for disease diagnosis, risk factor screening, and prognosis analysis [25]. Different machine learning algorithms exhibit varying classification capabilities for the same data types, and their performance may differ; thus, selecting the appropriate machine learning algorithm is essential for prediction.

In summary, the primary aim of this study is to compare the differences in EEG microstates and PSD between PWE comorbid with MCI and without MCI. This study aims to identify general electrophysiological markers associated with comorbid cognitive impairment in epilepsy. However, due to the limited sample size, we focused solely on changes in overall cognitive function among patients with epilepsy. Furthermore, this study aims to develop an ideal predictive model with a predictive capacity comparable to that of the MoCA for assessing the risk of MCI comorbidity in specific populations of PWE.

Materials & methods

Study design and participants

Figure 1 illustrates the experimental design and procedure of this study. This retrospective study utilized consecutive sampling to analyze patient data collected from 24-hour video EEG recordings at Fujian Medical University Union Hospital between October 2019 and August 2024. Inclusion criteria included: (1) a diagnosis of epilepsy confirmed by the International League Against Epilepsy; (2) age between 18 and 80; and (3) no seizure lasting at least 72 h. Exclusion criteria were: (1) non-epilepsy; (2) poor-quality video EEG data; (3) inability to complete the MoCA or MMSE; (4) missing clinical information. Diagnostic criteria for MCI included: (1) subjective cognitive decline; (2) cognitive testing showing declines in one or more domains (MoCA scores of ≤ 19 for ≤ 6 years of education, ≤ 22 for 6–12 years, ≤ 24 for > 12 years) [26]; (3) ability to perform daily activities; (4) preservation of general cognitive function; and (5) non-dementia status [27]. A total of 627 PWE were analyzed and divided into two groups: those with MCI (EPMCI, n = 106) and those without MCI (EPNMCI, n = 521). The study received approval from the hospital’s ethics committee, and all patients provided signed informed consent.

EEG acquisition

Before the EEG measurement, the physician instructed the patient to sit comfortably and relax their facial muscles. The dimly Lit room minimized noise to create an optimal testing environment. Throughout the procedure, the patient remained awake with eyes open. Electrodes were positioned according to the International 10–20 System, with precise distances measured using a quantitative ruler. EEG was obtained using a 20-channel system (EEG-1200: NIHON KOHDEN CORP, Tokyo, Japan, band-pass filter: 0.5–70 Hz, sampling rate: 500 Hz, impedance < 100 MΩ).

EEG preprocessing

All patients underwent EEG testing, during which 10 min of continuous EEG data with minimal or no electromyographic interference were collected while they were awake. The data were preprocessed using the EEGLAB (version 13.0.0b) toolbox in MATLAB (version 2013b; The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA). Interpolation was conducted on the Oz electrode, references A1 and A2 were removed, and unnecessary electrodes were eliminated. A total of 20 electrodes (Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, F7, F8, C3, C4, P3, P4, T3, T4, T5, T6, O1, O2, Fz, Cz, Pz, Oz) were used for further processing. The EEG data were subjected to band-pass filtering (1–40 Hz) and notch filtering (48–52 Hz), followed by re-referencing and segmentation into 2-second time slices. Segments with severe electromyographic interference or poor electrode recordings were manually removed, and eye movement artifacts were then automatically eliminated using independent component analysis (ICA). The final analysis included data from at least 90 segments of 2 s each.

EEG microstate analysis

Microstate analysis was performed using the Cartool plug. First, the preprocessed data was downsampled to 250 Hz and band-pass filtered between 2 and 20 Hz to eliminate high-frequency noise interference. The analysis consisted of three steps: (1) Calculation of the time series of global field power (GFP); when the GFP peaks, the EEG topography is stable, and the corresponding topography is considered a microstate. (2) Collection of topographies at GFP peaks for k-means clustering analysis, which indicates that the optimal number of clusters is four (microstates A, B, C, and D) [28]. (3) Calculation of parameters associated with the four microstates, including coverage (the ratio of time spent in each microstate to total microstate time), duration (the time a given microstate is stable), occurrence (the number of times a microstate occurs per second), and transition probability (the probability of transitioning from one microstate to another).

EEG spectral analysis

EEG spectral analysis was conducted using EEGLAB in MATLAB to calculate the power spectrum of five frequency bands: delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and gamma (30–40 Hz). For each frequency band, the EEG signal was transformed into PSD using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) with a Hanning window The Hanning window was applied with a sampling rate of 250 Hz, a length of 500 points, and 50% overlap between windows to enhance frequency resolution and reduce spectral leakage. This process generated spectral topographies for five frequency bands.

Covariates

In addition to Microstate and PSD indicators, we included other factors that may be associated with epilepsy comorbid with MCI, including age, sex, whether the patient has drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), body mass index (BMI: Normal: BMI ≤ 25, Overweight: BMI > 25 and ≤ 30, Obese: BMI > 30), Educational level (≤ 6 years, >6 years and ≤ 12 years, >12 years), family history of epilepsy, age at first seizure, duration of epilepsy, Type of epilepsy (Focal, Generalized, Combined Generalized and Focal, and Unknown), seizure frequency (number of seizures in the past year, including daily seizures, monthly seizures, annual seizures (fewer than 12 seizures in the past year), irregular seizures), etiology (hereditary, structural, infectious, autoimmune, idiopathic), history of generalized tonic-clonic seizures, history of nocturnal seizures, history of febrile seizures, types of anti-seizure medication (ASM) (None or monotherapy, multidrug therapy), MRI findings (lesions unrelated to seizures or normal, lesions related to seizures), history of brain injury and brain surgery, history of status epilepticus (SE), EEG (normal, abnormal background, epileptiform discharges).

Machine learning

Preprocessing

Before implementing machine learning, it is essential to preprocess the data. Initially, missing values are imputed, followed by the application of the Yeo-Johnson method to normalize the sampled continuous variable data, thereby reducing heteroscedasticity and enhancing normality [29]. Subsequently, feature scaling is performed on all continuous variables to standardize their value ranges, ensuring optimal model performance and convergence. In our dataset, the proportion of EPNMCI is approximately five times greater than that of EPMCI, resulting in significant class imbalance. This imbalance causes machine learning algorithms to favor the EPNMCI group during training, leading to suboptimal performance. To address this issue, we applied the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), an oversampling method that generates synthetic samples for the minority class to balance class distribution. Following the SMOTE application, the EPMCI group increased to 284 patients [30]. Finally, feature selection is conducted to identify variables that significantly contribute to the outcome of interest. Data were partitioned at the patient level: each patient was assigned a unique identifier, and the dataset was split into training (70%) and test (30%) sets based on these identifiers. Only the training dataset was used for variable selection and model development, whereas the validation dataset was reserved exclusively for model evaluation. This ensures complete independence of the test set. In this study, the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression analysis is employed for variable selection, focusing on identifying variables most strongly associated with the outcome [31]. LASSO enforces that the sum of the absolute values of the regression coefficients is less than the tuning parameter lambda (λ), resulting in certain variables being penalized to zero, thereby achieving effective variable selection [32]. The optimal value of lambda is determined using 10-fold cross-validation to mitigate model overfitting. Feature selection is performed separately for seven groups of variables: (1) the clinical variable (CV) group (comprising variable without duration, occurrence, coverage, transition probability and PSD), (2) the microstate variable (MV) group (including duration, occurrence, coverage, transition probability), (3) the PSD group, (4) the microstate and PSD variable (MPV) group (including duration, occurrence, coverage, transition probability and PSD), (5) the clinical and microstate variable (CMV) group (comprising clinical variable, duration, occurrence, coverage, transition probability), (6) the clinical and PSD variable (CPV) group (comprising clinical and PSD variable), (7) the clinical, microstate and PSD variable (CMPV) group (encompassing all variables). The selected variables from these groups are subsequently utilized for model development.

Model construction, training, and validation

The selected variables are analyzed using five machine learning algorithms: Support Vector Machine (SVM), Neural Network (NNET), Random Forest (RF), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Naive Bayes (NB). The dataset is divided into a training set (70%) and a validation set (30%). Initially, the optimal hyperparameters for each algorithm are determined through 5-fold cross-validation on the training set. The training set is randomly partitioned into five subsets, with four subsets used for grid search to optimize the hyperparameters and train the model, while the remaining subset evaluates the model’s performance with the selected hyperparameters. This process is repeated five times, and the hyperparameters yielding the highest performance across the iterations are selected as the optimized parameters. Subsequently, the model is trained on the training set using the optimized hyperparameters, and its performance is assessed on the test set. The test set is reserved exclusively for evaluation to minimize overfitting.

Performance evaluation and comparison of the model

The performance of all machine learning models is evaluated and compared using the validation set. First, accuracy, standard error, recall, specificity, and sensitivity are computed to assess performance differences among the models. Next, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves are generated and visualized to evaluate model accuracy. We comprehensively compared these metrics and selected models from different variable groups exhibiting high ROC area under the curve (AUC), recall, accuracy, and low standard error for further evaluation. Finally, calibration curve, and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) of the selected models in selected groups are compared to determine the best-performing model as the ideal predictive model. The calibration curve assesses the model’s calibration, measuring the consistency between predicted and actual probabilities, while DCA evaluates the clinical utility of the model in practice.

Analysis of model interpretability

In practice, when using models, it is crucial to understand which feature variables contribute the most to the model’s predictive performance (referred to as “feature importance”). We used permutation feature importance (PFI), partial dependence plots (PDP), individual conditional expectation (ICE) plots, and Shapley values to reflect feature importance. PFI measured the change in model performance before and after feature permutation. PDP and ICE correspond to local feature effect methods and global feature effect methods, respectively. Shapley values estimated the contribution of each input feature to the final prediction for a single instance (after subtracting the average prediction) [33]. Finally, to further investigate the contribution of these variables to individual patients and their clinical relevance in machine learning models, we randomly selected a patient from the validation cohort and provided a visual interpretation of the model for that specific individual.

Statistical analysis

Binary classification variables were described in terms of counts and percentages and assessed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests. For continuous variables that conform to a normal distribution, t-tests were used for comparison; for non-normally distributed data, the Wilcoxon rank sum test was applied. The software R version 4.2.0 was used for data analysis and presentation. The p-value was adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). The p-value < 0.05 is considered statistically significant. We employed analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to control for potential confounding effects by including all baseline variables demonstrating significant between-group differences (p < 0.05) as covariates.

Machine learning

Feature selection

Supplementary Fig. 1A–F presents the results of LASSO regression analyses across different variable groups. In the CV group, the variables selected for subsequent model construction were age, duration of epilepsy, whether the patient has DRE, type of epilepsy, and BMI. For the MV group, we chose the duration of microstates A, B, and D, the occurrence of microstates B and D, and TPDA for further model development. In the CMV group, the selected variables included whether the patient had DRE, age at first seizure, Duration of epilepsy, family history of epilepsy, type of epilepsy, duration of microstates A, B, C, and D, occurrence of microstates D, and TPDA. The CPV group included whether the patient has DRE, age at first seizure, duration of epilepsy, type of epilepsy, and the PSD of the delta frequency band in the T5 brain region for model building. In the MPV group, the variables selected were the duration of microstates A, B, and D, the occurrence rate of microstate D, TPDA, and the PSD of the delta frequency band in the T5 brain region for subsequent model construction. For the CMPV group, the selected variables were age at first seizure, duration of epilepsy, whether the patient has DRE, family history of epilepsy, type of epilepsy, the duration of microstates A, B, C, and D, the occurrence rate of microstate D, TPDA, PSD of the delta frequency band in the T5 brain region. However, in the PSD variable group, only two variables—PSD of the delta frequency band in the T5 and Pz brain regions—were identified; consequently, this group was excluded from subsequent model construction.

Figures 4C-F display the calibration curves of the four machine learning models. The dashed lines represent the relationship between the predicted probabilities of the original models and the observed event probabilities, while the solid lines correspond to the predictions after calibration with logistic regression. We found that both the original and logistic regression-calibrated curves for all four models closely approached the ideal calibration line depicted in gray. Further comparison of metrics revealed that the calibration curve of the MV group exhibited the highest discrimination index (D = 0.724), the lowest Brier score ( 0.084), and the smallest unreliability index ( U = 0.006), indicating the best calibration performance. In addition, the NNET model in the CMV and CMPV groups involved a larger number of variables (CMV = 11, CMPV = 12), which increases both computational time and the difficulty of acquiring variables. Therefore, based on these considerations, we selected the NNET model in the MV group as our optimal model.

To further evaluate the clinical utility of the NNET model in the MV group, we performed decision curve analysis (DCA) as shown in Fig. 4G. DCA quantifies the net clinical benefit of the model across varying risk thresholds. The x-axis represents the threshold probability (Pt, 0–100%), and the y-axis denotes the net benefit. The model’s decision curve remains above both the “intervene all” and “intervene none” strategies between threshold probabilities of 1% and 100%, indicating superior performance relative to these two extreme benchmarks throughout most of this interval. Furthermore, the curve does not exhibit any negative net benefit, implying that the model’s decisions can increase patient benefit while minimizing harm. To further assess the clinical utility of the model, we employed MMSE as the predictor variable (MMSE ≤ 26 defining MCI) to construct a decision curve analysis (DCA), with the binary MMSE predictions on the x-axis and MoCA-confirmed MCI as the outcome. Comparison of the two curves revealed that the MMSE curve was slightly higher than the MV model at low threshold probabilities (3–50%) but declined below the Treat-All strategy at 76%, indicating a narrower clinical applicability range. The area under the decision curve for the MV model was significantly larger than that of MMSE, suggesting that the MV model yields a greater cumulative net benefit across the entire threshold range. These results indicate that, relative to traditional MMSE, the MV model may provide enhanced clinical utility in predicting MCI risk, offering patients increased net benefit over a wider range of thresholds.

Model interpretation and application

We utilized PFI, PDP, ICE, and SHAP to assess how variables influence the risk of comorbid MCI in PWE. Figure 5A illustrates the PFI of the variables, which quantifies their importance and relative weights within the model. Microstate D duration is identified as the most influential variable in the model. Figure 5B shows the PDP and ICE for various variables. We found that, the duration of microstates A, B, and D, the occurrence of microstate B and D, and TPDA exhibit a linear relationship with epilepsy comorbid with MCI, The ICE curves for most patients align with the trends observed in the PDP. Figure 5C presents the SHAP values for the model variables, with the feature ranking (y-axis) indicating the significance of each predictor. The SHAP values (x-axis) provide a unified measure of the influence of each feature on the model’s predictions. In each feature importance row, the attribution of the outcome for all patients is depicted using colored points, where yellow indicates high risk and purple indicates low risk. In PWE, the lower duration of microstates A and D, the lower occurrence of microstate B, the higher duration of microstate B, the higher occurrence of microstate D, and the higher TPDA, all contribute to an increased probability of comorbid MCI. To further explore the contribution of these features for individual patients and their clinical application within the model, we randomly selected one patient from the validation cohort. Figure 5D offers a visual explanation of the model’s predictions. The model predicts a 99.4% probability that this PWE has MCI and displays the contribution of each variable.

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the differences in microstates and power spectra between PWE comorbid with and without MCI. Then, we developed and validated an ideal predictive model to predict the probability of MCI comorbidity in PWE. Finally, the model interpretation was conducted, and the risk prediction for the disease was visualized. Our analysis guided the study of the risks of MCI comorbidity in specific populations of epilepsy.

Microstate analysis

Epilepsy is a brain network disorder that alters the FC of the RSN. These changes can be accurately identified by microstates [34]. Microstates are utilized to display RSN-related topographies, reflecting different RSNs. Recent research indicates that the FC of RSN is altered in PWE or cognitive impairment, thereby establishing a theoretical foundation for our study.

Several studies indicate that microstate A may serve as a biomarker for the early stages of disease in patients with MCI. The relevant indicators of microstate A are significantly elevated in these patients [35, 36]. These findings align with our results. The primary brain regions associated with microstate A encompass the lateral superior temporal gyrus, temporal pole, temporal plane, Heschl’s gyrus, and posterior insula, which are primarily involved in auditory processing, speech processing, and arousal [37, 38]. Previous research has identified a specific reduction in FC within the AN among patients with MCI [39]. Furthermore, Sendi et al. reported that Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patients exhibit decreased FC in the AN when compared to healthy individuals [40]. Moreover, alterations in FC in AN have also been documented in PWE [41, 42]. We propose that these changes may result from the combined effects of epilepsy and MCI on the auditory network, contributing to the modifications observed in microstate A.

Our research indicates that PWE comorbid with MCI exhibit increased duration, coverage, and occurrence of microstate B compared to those without MCI. Similar findings have been documented in studies focusing on cognitive impairment [16, 43, 44]. Microstate B is primarily linked to visual function, with the relevant brain regions predominantly comprising the bilateral occipital lobes [45]. Nong et al. reported that patients with insulinoma had significantly lower MoCA scores than healthy individuals and observed a reduction in FC within their VN [46]. Wu et al. demonstrated that following computerized cognitive training aimed at improving cognitive function, patients experienced decreased abnormal structure/function coupling values within the VN, as well as enhancements in visual memory and recall abilities [47]. Changes in FC within the VN have also been noted among various types of PWE [41, 48, 49]. Consequently, we propose that epilepsy and MCI collectively influence the visual network, resulting in alterations to the associated indicators of microstate B.

Studies have found that the microstate C index is negatively correlated with cognitive function [35, 50, 51]. Our study found a similar alteration in the duration of microstate C. Several studies have identified overlapping brain regions between microstate C and the DMN as well as the SN [52–55]. The DMN is involved in self-referential processing, emotional cognition, and emotion regulation. Key regions of the DMN include the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vMPFC), dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dMPFC), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), and bilateral angular gyrus. The SN plays a crucial role in attention and emotion regulation, as well as in integrating and filtering sensory, autonomic, and emotional information, encompassing the anterior insula (AI) and the dorsolateral anterior cingulate cortex (dACC) [56–58]. It was found that FC between PCC and ACC was reduced in patients with MCI [59]. In addition, Yeager, B.E., et al. found that the lower the score of MOCA, the lower the connectivity between SN and DMN [60]. Also, studies have found altered FC in DMN and SN in PWE. Together, these results suggest that epilepsy and MCI collectively influence emotion regulation networks.

Previous studies have demonstrated that patients with MCI exhibit reduced indicators of microstate D (i.e., duration, coverage, occurrence) compared to healthy individuals [44, 61]. Our research similarly identified alterations in these metrics. Microstate D is associated with the DAN and is linked to executive function, with relevant brain regions encompassing the frontal and parietal lobes [37]. Roy et al. found that a ketogenic diet can enhance FC within the DAN in patients with MCI [62]. Conversely, other studies reported that MCI groups exhibited diminished FC in the DAN compared to the control group [63, 64]. Furthermore, alterations in FC within the DAN have also been observed in patients with temporal lobe epilepsy and drug-resistant epilepsy [65, 66]. These findings indicate a combined influence of MCI and epilepsy on the executive function network.

Alterations in transition probabilities signify shifts between intrinsic brain FC, which may impact cognitive function. Our findings indicate that PWE comorbid with MCI exhibit elevated levels of TPAC, TPAB, TPBA, and TPBC compared to those without MCI. TPCD, TPDC, TPDA, TPDB demonstrate the opposite result. Similar results have been reported in previous studies. Lian et al. found that both MCI and AD presented higher levels of TPAB and TPAC compared to healthy individuals [43]. Li and Qu reported that TPBA and TPAB levels in MCI patients were significantly greater than those in the healthy group [44]. Hanoglu et al. similarly documented comparable findings [67]. Transition probabilities are associated with changes in these two microstates. Our research confirmed that the indicators of microstates A and B are elevated in patients with epilepsy and MCI compared to those without MCI, microstate C and D are degraded in patients with epilepsy and MCI compared to those without MCI. We speculate that the synchronous changes in the two microstates contribute to increases in TPAB and TPBA and decreases in TPCD and TPDC. Conversely, alterations in TPAC, TPBC, TPDA, and TPDB may depend on which microstate is in a dominant role, reflecting alterations in FC between brain regions, i.e., relationships between different neural networks. Previous studies have identified similar alterations. For example, Yue et al. found that, compared to healthy individuals, patients with cognitive impairment exhibit changes in FC between the VN and the AN, as well as between the DMN and VN, and between DMN and AN [68]. Moreover, prior research has shown that changes in FC between the AN and DMN mediate the relationship between hearing loss and impaired information processing speed, contributing to cognitive impairment [69]. Previous studies have also identified FC alterations in DAN and DMN in AD and MCI [70].

In summary, our findings indicate that PWE comorbid with MCI exhibit changes in microstates and transition probabilities. These changes reflect alterations in FC both within and between neural networks, affecting primary sensory networks (visual and auditory) as well as higher cognitive networks (emotional regulation and executive function). These results offer new insights for further investigations into the pathogenesis of epilepsy comorbid with MCI. This highlights a promising area for future research. Collectively, our studies support the notion that microstates A, B, C, and D, along with transition probabilities, may serve as potential biomarkers for assessing cognitive function in PWE.

Spectral analysis

Numerous studies have concentrated on the spectral analysis of cognitive impairment. Said and Göker discovered that patients with MCI had a lower PSD in the 1–49 Hz range compared to healthy individuals [71]. Other studies have primarily focused on the PSD of alpha, theta, and delta frequency bands. Research on alpha frequency bands has produced consistent findings [72–74], related to a decline in cholinergic function. Alterations in the PSD of the alpha band arise from the interaction between the cortex and thalamus, regulated by cholinergic signaling [72]. Choline is known to enhance cognitive function and protect the brain from neurodegenerative diseases [75]. Additionally, epilepsy may lead to mutations in cholinergic-related genes, disrupting cholinergic synthesis pathways that contribute to cognitive decline [76]. These findings support our results.

Our study also revealed significant differences in PSD among various brain regions, particularly in the the posterior temporal and parietal regions, between the two groups. Previous studies support our findings. Furthermore, other research has shown differences in brain activity intensity between patients with MCI and healthy individuals in the hippocampus and temporal lobe [77, 78]. Future research could investigate metabolic functional changes across various brain regions in PWE comorbid with MCI.

In conclusion, our study indicates that the PSD across different frequency bands in distinct brain regions may act as indicators for evaluating cognitive function in individuals with epilepsy.

Machine learning

Previous studies have employed feature selection and machine learning methods to predict epilepsy comorbidity with cognitive impairment [79–81]. However, these approaches constructed only one machine learning model. Additionally, the selected variables primarily focused on clinical factors. Our study compares the performance of models constructed with different categories of variable combinations and different machine learning algorithms. Furthermore, our study is the first to integrate EEG microstates and power PSD to develop a machine learning model aimed at predicting the risk of MCI comorbidity in PWE. This methodology offers a non-invasive, cost-effective, and reproducible approach for early cognitive screening and detection in populations unable to complete the MoCA.

In models constructed with various variable groups, we found that models including microstate variables exhibited higher performance. The CMV group models, which added microstate variables, outperformed the CV group models composed solely of clinical variables (ROCACUCMV:0.94 VS ROCACUCV:0.80). Also, the CMV group models, which composed clinical and microstate variables, outperformed the CPV group models composed of clinical and PSD variables (ROCACUCMV:0.94 VS ROCACUCPV:0.84). Both of them demonstrated that microstate variables have superior predictive accuracy compared to clinical variables and the PSD variable. The NNET model in the MV group exhibited the second-highest ROCAUC (0.93), the highest accuracy (0.88), the lowest standard error (0.12), optimal calibration, and an appropriate number of variables. We additionally found that the MV model offers superior clinical utility compared to the traditional MMSE scale in predicting the risk of epilepsy-associated cognitive impairment, characterized by a wider applicable threshold range and greater overall net benefit. Consequently, we selected the MV group model as the optimal predictor for comorbid mild cognitive impairment (MCI) risk in epilepsy patients, offering a potential auxiliary tool for screening this specific population. Our study also demonstrates that, compared to clinical variables and PSD variables, microstate variables can better predict the risk of MCI comorbidity in PWE.

This study has several limitations: (1) The sample size of PWE comorbid with MCI is relatively small, necessitating larger samples in future research. (2) The diagnosis of MCI was primarily based on self-assessment scales; future studies should incorporate additional measures to improve diagnostic accuracy. (3) Our single-center sample is predominantly Asian and recruited from a hospital; thus, findings may not generalize to rural or ethnically diverse populations. Larger, multi-center studies are needed to confirm external validity. (4) The lack of analysis on seizure focus localization and laterality, due to incomplete or missing data in the retrospective medical records. Given the potential influence of seizure laterality on cognitive and neurophysiological outcomes, future prospective studies with more comprehensive clinical documentation are warranted to address this factor.

Conclusion

This study examines the differences in EEG microstates and power spectrum between PWE comorbid with and without MCI, utilizing machine learning algorithms to predict the risk of such comorbidities. Significant differences were observed in the duration, coverage, occurrence, and transition probability of microstate and power spectral density between the two groups. These findings provide a novel perspective for further investigation into the pathogenesis of MCI comorbidity in epilepsy. We integrated diverse variable categories to develop models employing various machine learning algorithms. The combination of microstate and PSD variables enhanced model performance indicators, such as accuracy and calibration. This integrated model outperformed those based solely on clinical or microstate variables, demonstrating the practical utility of microstate and PSD variables for predicting the risk of MCI. The model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) exceeding 0.9. demonstrates predictive accuracy comparable to that of MoCA, allowing for the prediction of the risk of comorbid MCI in specific epilepsy populations. This approach offers valuable insights for the early identification and treatment of comorbid conditions in epilepsy.
