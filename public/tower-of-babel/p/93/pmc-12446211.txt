Associations of Physical Frailty, Social Deficits, and Cognitive Impairment with Risk of 45 Incident Non-Communicable Diseases: The Mediating Role of Accelerated Biological Aging—A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Bing Pei, Yue Yu, Xinyuan Shen, Yanjie Jia, Jianping Wang, Yihan Zhang, Ying Shi, Wenjing Wang, Fulan Hu, Fan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Physical frailty, social deficits, and cognitive impairment increase the risk of incident NCDs.•Multi-domain impairments confer higher NCD risk than single-domain impairments.•Accelerated biological aging partially mediates the relationship between these impairments and NCD risk.

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) account for more than half of the global disease burden and cause approximately 70% of deaths [1]. Their burden will continue to increase in the next 20 years, especially in low- and middle-income countries, plunging millions into poverty [2]. In this context, identifying modifiable risk factors and developing effective preventive strategies is critical.

Beyond traditional risk factors, the British Geriatric Society highlights that identifying frailty is essential for early prevention NCDs [3]. Frailty is characterized by reduced physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors [4], and has been associated with elevated risks of multiple diseases, including depression [5], type 2 diabetes [6], and cancer [7]. However, most evidence derives from independent and heterogeneous studies, lacking outcome-wide analyses within a single prospective cohort. Moreover, frailty is increasingly recognized as a multidimensional construct encompassing dysfunction across physical, cognitive, and social domains, rather than being a purely physical condition [8,9]. Assessments of multidimensional impairments can better reflect an individual's overall health status and improves the prediction of adverse outcomes [10]. Despite this, research linking social and cognitive impairment to incident NCDs risk remains limited. In addition, studies on multidimensional impairments often rely on deficit accumulation models to calculate a total score [11], which overlook heterogeneity in disease risk arising from distinct domain-specific combinations. Furthermore, the mechanisms linking frailty to NCDs remains unclear.

Aging is a complex and irreversible biological process that impairs cells and organs integrity, ultimately leading to disease and death [12]. Compared with chronological age (CA), biological age (BA) can accurately reflect an individual’s aging rate [13]. The difference between BA and CA reflects the degree of "accelerated aging". Frailty is closely related to biological aging. However, existing studies are mostly based on the unidirectional hypothesis that "aging causes frailty" [14], with limited exploration of another complementary hypothesis whereby frailty will in turn accelerate biological aging. Studies showed that physically frail individuals usually show reduced activity, decreased muscle mass and strength [15], and chronic inflammation [16], all of which may further accelerate aging. Besides, long-term loneliness [17] and cognitive decline [18] have also been shown to accelerate epigenetic aging. These findings provide support for the pathway of "frailty-accelerated biological aging-NCDs", but further verification through systematic research is warranted.

To address these gaps, this study systematically explored the associations of physical frailty, social deficits, cognitive impairment, and their combinations with risk of 45 NCDs, and investigated the role of accelerated biological aging. We aimed to strengthen the scientific evidence on the impact of frailty and biological aging on NCDs and provide theoretical support for developing strategies to prevent and control NCDs.

Methods

Study population

The present study was part of UKB Project 81680. UKB is a prospective cohort study that recruited 502,411 participants from the UK population by mailed invitation between 2006 and 2010. Participants underwent a nurse-led interview at one of 22 assessment centers in England, Scotland, and Wales, completed physical examinations, touchscreen questionnaires, and provided biological samples for testing. Participants were limited to age 40–60 years, but some older people were also included in practice. All participants had signed informed consent.

A total of 427,026 participants with physical frailty data, 171,269 with social deficits data, and 49,820 with cognitive function data were included to explore the association between a single function impairment with the risk of NCDs. Among them, 145,055, 47,369 and 27,206 participants had biological aging data, respectively, and were included in the analysis of the mediating role of accelerated biological aging in these associations. In addition, a total of 41,336 individuals with complete data across all three domains were included to further examine the impact of multi-domain impairments on the risk of NCDs. Participants with a diagnosis of the respective NCDs at baseline or within 30 days of enrollment were excluded from its outcome-specific analysis.

Assessment of physical frailty, social deficits and cognitive impairment

Physical frailty: Fried [19] initially assessed frailty based on five physical frailty phenotypes, including weight loss, decreased physical activity, fatigue, decreased grip strength, and decreased walking speed. Subsequently, Frances [20] adapted these items based on data from UK Biobank to allow for assessment using existing data (Table S1). Participants were divided into physically frail (meeting ≥3 criteria), pre- physically frail (meeting 1–2 criteria), and non- physically frail (meeting 0 criteria).

Social deficits: There is no unified definition of social frailty, which can be understood as the accumulation of social deficits across multiple domains, increasing vulnerability to adverse outcomes [21]. Existing assessment methods include the Makizako Social Frailty Index [22], the Tilburg Frailty Indicator [23], and the Social Frailty Screening Index [24]. However, given the heterogeneity of population and the differences between the questionnaires collected in the UKB and the above scales in terms of assessment items and measurement indicators, these tools may be difficult to directly apply to this study. Therefore, this study integrated the content of the above three assessment tools and extracted eight relevant questions from the UKB questionnaire, covering social resources, self-management abilities, and behavioral activities, to construct a Social Deficits Index (Table S2). Although the index is not a validated social frailty scale, its items are highly consistent with the social frailty and can be used as an alternative indicator in analysis. Participants were categorized into none (0 deficits), moderate (1–3 deficits), and high (≥4 deficits) groups according to social deficits index.

Cognitive impairment: Cognitive frailty refers to the co-occurrence of cognitive impairment and physical frailty. In this study, cognitive impairment was analyzed independently. Donald M. Lyall et al. proposed a method for constructing cognitive assessment scores based on UKB data. Specifically, the researchers extracted the first principal component using principal component analysis based on four cognitive indicators, including visual memory, reaction time, numerical reasoning and number memory, as a general cognitive ability (g factor) score [25]. Subsequently, with reference to the a priori prevalence distribution and cognitive scores, participants were classified into cognitive impairment (scores < 20%), mild cognitive impairment (MCI) (scores 20%–40%), and non-cognitive impairment (scores ≥ 40%).

Assessment of biological age

We quantified BA based on clinical and blood test data using three algorithms from the R package ‘BioAge’: the Homeostatic Dysregulation(HD) [26], PhenoAge [27] and Klemera-Doubal Method (KDM) [28]. Specifically, we trained algorithm using 18 biomarkers (Table S3) from the NHANES III dataset to calculate the biological age for individuals, then validated their reliability in the NHANES Ⅳ dataset, and finally applied the algorithm to the UK Biobank to assess the BA of the entire cohort. These algorithms have previously been employed in studies such as that by Jonathan K. L. Mak [29], where they demonstrated strong predictive power for cancer incidence. In addition, we calculated the difference between CA and BA using a natural cubic spline regression analysis with three degrees of freedom, which was interpreted as ‘age residuals’. Given that HD intrinsic has already quantified deviations relative to the reference population, residuals were not further estimated. Due to its skewed distribution, a logarithmic transformation was applied to the HD variable.

Covariates

Participants’ demographic characteristics, lifestyle information, and physical measurements were collected using a standardized touchscreen questionnaire and on-site measurements. Demographic characteristics included gender (male, female), educational attainment (college, vocational, high school, middle school, unknown), age (continuous), race (white, other), employment status (employed, unemployed), Townsend deprivation index (continuous), assessment center (categorical), and income level (categorical: <£18000, £18000-£30999, £31000-£51999, £52000-£100000, >£100000). Lifestyle information included drinking (never, former, current), sleep duration (<7 h, 7–8 h, >8 h), and smoking (never, former, current). Physical activity was assessed according to the U.S. Adult Activity Guidelines [30], and determine whether it meets the recommended activity level (yes/no). Depressive symptoms were assessed by asking participants about the frequency of lack of interest or enthusiasm and experiencing depressed mood during the past two weeks, rated as ‘not at all’ (0), ‘a few days’ (1), ‘over half of the days’ (2), and ‘almost every day’ (3). The scores for the two questions were added together to obtain a total depression score (continuous; range: 0–6). In addition, three physical measurement indicators were included, including body mass index (BMI, kg/m², categorized as thin, normal, overweight, mildly obese, moderately and severely obese), systolic blood pressure (SBP, continuous) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP, continuous).

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were 45 incident NCD events, coded according to the ICD-10 (Table S4). Cases were classified by comparing the date of first diagnosis with the enrollment date: those with a first diagnosis prior to enrollment were pre-existing cases, while those with a diagnosis after enrollment were incident cases. Notably, due to sample size constraints, the analysis of multi-domain impairments was limited to examining their associations with eight disease categories. Follow-up time was defined as the interval from the baseline assessment to the earliest occurrence of a study-terminating event, including clinical diagnosis, death, or lost follow up at the designated endpoint (January 2022).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as N (%), and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation. Baseline characteristics were described according to impairment status, and intergroup differences in categorical and continuous variables were assessed using chi-square and Kruskal-Wallis tests, respectively. The number and proportion of missing values for each covariate are presented in Supplementary Table S5. Missing values were subsequently imputed using the random forest algorithm (ntree = 100).

Cox proportional hazards models were constructed to assess the associations between physical frailty, social deficits and cognitive impairment, accelerated biological aging, and 45 incident NCDs, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Models were adjusted for education attainment, assessment center, gender, race, age, employment status, Townsend deprivation index, BMI, SBP, DBP, smoking, depression score, physical activity, sleep duration, and alcohol consumption. All analyses met the proportional hazards assumption, which was tested using Schoenfeld residual. To control for type I error, P values in all analyses were adjusted using the Bonferroni method. The robustness of the results was assessed by several sensitivity analyses. First, to avoid the reverse causality, we excluded individuals who developed NCDs within two years after recruitment. Second, we excluded individuals with missing covariates that had been imputed by random forests to rule out the potential effects of the imputation method on the results. Third, the Fine&Gray models were constructed to assess the impact of competing events on these associations. Finally, stratified analyses were constructed by age (<60 years, ≥60 years) and gender and used likelihood ratio tests to assess the interaction between these function impairments and stratification variables on NCD risk.

The PAFs for NCDs attributed to frailty was calculated using the following formula.PAF=∑Pi*RRi-1/[∑(Pi*RRi)]

Pi: the proportion of the population in exposure i; RRi: the HR for exposure i.

Multivariate linear regression model was constructed to evaluate the association of physical frailty, social deficits and cognitive impairment with accelerated biological aging (β estimate). Furthermore, we used the survival mediation analysis framework proposed by VanderWeele, based on a counterfactual model, combined with Cox and logistic regression, to evaluate the direct and indirect effects of function impairments on the risk of NCDs through accelerated biological aging, and estimated the proportion of the mediation effect.

All statistical analyses and plotting were conducted using R software (version 4.1.1). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided α < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among the 427,026 participants with physical frailty data, the mean age was 56.42 ± 8.11 years, and 46.6% were male. Of these, 5.2% were physically frail, 49.4% were pre-physically frail, and 45.2% were not physically frail. In the social deficits analysis, the 171,269 participants had a mean age of 56.74 ± 8.14 years, with 45.8% being male. Among them, 32.5% were classified as none-social deficits, 58.7% as moderate-social deficits, and 8.8% as high social deficits. For cognitive impairment, the mean age of the 49,820 participants was 56.45 ± 8.26 years. Association of physical frailty, social deficits, and cognitive impairment with NCDs

A higher proportion of individuals with impairment developed NCDs compared to their respective non-impairment counterparts: 34.5% vs. 30.9% for physical frailty, 34.9% vs. 30.9% for social deficits, and 37.2% vs. 31.9% for cognitive impairment (Pall < 0.001) (Table1, Table S6). Specifically, compared with non-physically frail individuals, physically frail individuals had a higher risk of 27 incident NCDs (HR > 1; Padjustment < 0.05), with the strongest associations observed for multiple sclerosis (HR = 10.942 [8.472, 14.133]; PAF = 38.5%), lupus erythematosus (HR = 4.887 [3.401, 7.024]; PAF = 23.8%), and rheumatoid arthritis (HR = 4.096 [3.686, 4.552]; PAF = 25.3%). High social deficits was associated with an increased risk of 17 incident NCDs (HR > 1; padjustment < 0.05) and a reduced risk of prostate cancer (HR = 0.734 [0.623, 0.866]; PAF = −6.0%). The strongest associations were observed with schizophrenia (HR = 8.093 [4.513, 14.512]; PAF = 60.2%), depression (HR = 2.183 [1.993, 2.392]; PAF = 19.7%), and dementia (HR = 2.154 [1.815, 2.555]; PAF = 19.8%). Compared to cognitively normal individuals, those with cognitive impairment had a higher of 7 incident NCDs (HR > 1; Padjustment < 0.05), with the strongest association observed for schizophrenia (HR = 5.312 [2.228, 12.666]; PAF = 51.8%). Mediation analysis showed that accelerated biological aging partially mediated the associations of physical frailty, social deficits, and cognitive impairment with NCDs. Among the 27 NCDs significantly associated with physical frailty, KDM residuals, PhenoAge residuals, and HD mediated the associations of 21, 25, and 26 NCDs, respectively, with mediation proportions ranging from 7.935% to 44.045%, 5.268%–48.793%, and 5.587%–30.526% (Table S13–S15). Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, we identified three key findings. First, physical frailty, social deficits, and cognitive impairment were associated with an increased risk of incident NCDs, and these associations were also observed in individuals with pre-physical frailty and moderate social deficits. Second, individuals with impairments in multiple domains have a higher risk of diseases than individuals with impairments in single domain. Finally, accelerated biological aging plays an important mediating role in the relationship between these function impairments and NCDs.

While the relationship between physical frailty and NCDs has been more extensively studied compared to other domains of functional impairment, significant research gaps remain. In terms of cardiovascular metabolic diseases, Zhu et al. found that higher frailty scores significantly increased the risk of type 2 diabetes, coronary artery disease, and stroke [6]. Di et al. reported that as frailty progressed, the risk of cardiovascular disease increased significantly, whereas recovery from it led to a reduction in risk [31]. Our study was the first to identify an association between physical frailty and the risk of incident atrial fibrillation and thyroid disease. In the field of oncology, Jonathan et al. found that the higher the frailty score, the higher the risk of lung cancer, but the lower the risk of melanoma and prostate cancer [7]. However, in our study, no association between physical frailty and reduced risk of melanoma was observed, and the association with prostate cancer risk was not statistically significant after adjustment. These discrepancies may be due to differences in exclusion criteria and the adjustment for confounders. Regarding mental illness, studies by Leslie [32] and Pinar [33] found an association between frailty and the incidence of depression, while Melissa [34] reported that frailty was linked to increased anxiety symptoms in older people. However, since these studies used a cross-sectional design, their results have certain limitations in establishing causal inferences. Our study supplemented the longitudinal evidence linking physical frailty with anxiety and depression, while also, for the first time, uncovering its association with sleep disorders and schizophrenia risk. Importantly, our study also provided prospective associations between physical frailty and less studied NCDs such as chronic liver disease, diverticulitis, periodontal disease, rheumatoid arthritis, lupus erythematosus, gout, and chronic kidney disease (CKD). Several mechanisms may explain these associations. First, physical frailty is accompanied by a systemic inflammatory response, manifested by increased levels of inflammatory cytokines, neutrophil and monocyte counts [35]. Second, physical frailty disrupts energy metabolism, leading to abnormalities in the tricarboxylic acid cycle, increased levels of glycolytic intermediates, reactive oxygen species, and neurotransmitters, thereby affecting myocardial energy production, oxygen homeostasis, and cellular electrophysiological function [36]. Finally, physical frailty may lead to changes in brain structure, such as reduced brain volume, increased cerebral microbleeds, and increased white matter hyperintensities, which may increase the risk of neurological diseases [37,38].

Impairment in the social domain often precedes physical, so early intervention targeting it may be more effective in preventing NCDs. However, research on the association between social deficits and disease remains limited. Tsutsumimoto et al. [39] found that social frailty was linked to a higher risk of Alzheimer's disease, while Zongke et al. showed that it increased the risk of death in older patients with heart failure [40]. In line with these findings, our study found that high social deficits were significantly associated with an increased risk of 14 NCDs and a decreased risk of prostate cancer. The mechanisms include pathophysiological changes and the accumulation of risk factors. For example, Huang et al. found that socially frail older people had less dietary intake, poor diet quality, and were often accompanied by malnutrition [41]. Pothier et al. found that social frailty increased the levels of inflammatory markers, specifically elevated C-reactive protein and IL-6 [42]. Moreover, loneliness and social isolation, two key components of social deficits, are closely linked to unhealthy lifestyles (such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical inactivity) and harmful biological processes (including oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction). These factors further increase the risk of onset NCDs [43,44]. However, these inferences are based on indirect evidence and need to be further verified through population studies and animal experiments in the future.

Cognitive impairment is considered to be a transition stage from normal cognitive function to dementia and is an important indicator of poor prognosis for many NCDs. Martin's study found that, in a high cardiovascular risk population, baseline cognitive impairment was associated with the risk of congestive heart failure, stroke, and cardiovascular mortality, but showed no association with coronary heart disease events [45]. Marina et al. found that MCI was a key risk factor for anxiety, depression, and dementia [46]. Our study extended previous research by providing longitudinal evidence of its association with 7 NCDs, including diabetes, autism, heart failure, COPD, and CKD. The specific mechanism between them is still unclear. One plausible explanation is that the two may be associated with each other through common physiological pathways, including oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammatory response [47]. In addition, cognitive impairment may lead to a decrease in social support, which can result in depression and reduced quality of life, further increasing the risk of disease [48].

In this study, we observed that individuals with physical frailty or high social deficits reported relatively low alcohol consumption but had a significantly higher risk of liver disease. Several contributing factors may account for this paradoxical finding. First, although alcohol consumption is a well-established risk factor for liver disease and the difference in the proportion of drinkers between the two groups was statistically significant, the absolute difference was less than 10%, and the effect size (Cohen’s h = 0.185) fell within the range of a small effect, resulting in a limited explanation of disease risk. Second, frailty is typically characterized by multisystem dysfunction and an increased susceptibility to external stressors [19], such as falls, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Therefore, even with lower levels of alcohol consumption, frail individuals may be more susceptible to liver injury.

Frailty is a cumulative and progressive process. Individuals classified as pre-physical frail or moderate social deficits accounted for more than 45% of the total population. Previous studies have shown that individuals in the pre-frail stage have a 2.6-fold higher risk of developing frailty than healthy people [19], highlighting the importance of early identification and intervention. Furthermore, individuals with impairment in multiple domains had a higher risk of NCDs. A similar effect was observed in a study by Lee et al. [10], which showed that individuals with multiple frail characteristics were more likely to have adverse health outcomes, with a sub-hazard ratio of 3.48 for hospitalization and a hazard ratio of 3.95 for death. The finding suggests that impairments across different domains have a cumulative effect on health outcomes, possibly due to simultaneous exposure to a broader range of disease-associated risk factors. Therefore, in the prevention and management of NCDs, a systematic assessment of impairment across multiple domains is warranted.

Another key finding of our study was that accelerated biological aging mediated the associations between physical frailty, social deficits and cognitive impairment and NCDs. Contrary to traditional studies, we did not consider these function impairments as a result of aging, but rather as a potential influencing factor. Aging is influenced by multiple factors, including genetics, lifestyle, diet, and environmental exposures. Physical frailty is often accompanied by a variety of unhealthy lifestyles, such as nutritional deficiencies, obesity, and low physical activity, which may accelerate aging. SI and loneliness can lead to persistent activation of the HPA axis, which can impair metabolic function, increase oxidative stress and inflammation, disrupt glucose metabolism, and thus accelerate aging [49]. In addition, research has found that older individuals who lack social support tend to be less physically active, which may further exacerbate the aging process [50]. As biological ageing accelerates, organ function gradually declines, which in turn increases the risk of disease and death. Animal studies have further verified this finding: transplanting senescent cells into young mice can induce an aging phenotype and increase the occurrence of aging-related diseases, while using quercetin to eliminate senescent cells can reverse this process [51]. In summary, the results of this study together with existing evidence support the potential biological pathway of "frailty-accelerated biological aging- NCDs".

This study has several limitations. First, there is currently no validated social frailty scale based on the UKB database. Therefore, we constructed a Social Deficit Index by referencing existing scales and aggregating relevant items to approximate an individual's social functioning. However, this index has not been formally validated and should not be considered equivalent to a standardized social frailty measure. Future studies should develop validated instruments to verify these findings. Second, although the study provided evidence that physical frailty, social deficits and cognitive impairment accelerated aging, potential reverse causality could not be ruled out because the conclusions were drawn from a cross-sectional study. Third, considering that this was an observational study, we could not completely avoid residual confounding effects. Fourth, compared with individuals without functional impairments, those with impairments may have deviations in access to and utilization of medical services due to differences in health status and social resources, which may lead to early or delayed diagnosis of the disease. Although this study has controlled the relevant confounding factors of medical resource accessibility as much as possible, due to the lack of specific medical use data, it is still impossible to completely rule out its potential impact on the results. Therefore, future research incorporating more comprehensive healthcare utilization data is warranted to further validate this association. Fifth, the assessment of social deficits and cognitive impairment was based on self-reported data, which may be subject to reporting bias, potentially resulting in misclassification and thus underestimating the risk of them on NCDs.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our results suggest that impairments in physical, social, and cognitive functions are associated with an increased risk of NCDs, and that individuals with impairment in multiple domains face higher health risks. These findings emphasize the importance of systematically assessing frailty in the management of NCDs and support the development of targeted interventions across different functional domains to alleviate the growing burden of disease. In addition, our data showed that accelerated biological aging may be a key biological mechanism linking frailty and NCDs, warranting further verification through experimental studies.
