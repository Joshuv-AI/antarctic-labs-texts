Introduction

Cardiac device impedance provides critical information on the overall integrity of a cardiac implantable electronic device. In a pacing lead, high impedance alerts are typically associated with a conductor fracture, incomplete pin insertion, header-lead pin mismatch, or header-bond failure (failure of the adhesive that bonds the header to the generator). Historically, some centers would use a hybrid system (generator and leads from a different company) to leverage the advantages of battery or lead longevity. Issues with these hybrid systems have been reported. An important issue is high impedance alerts, which may trigger a polarity switch to unipolar sensing or pacing, potentially leading to myopotential oversensing and inappropriate inhibition of ventricular pacing.1 We present here a case of a lead impedance switch owing to the presence of a hybrid system in a patient with neurocardiogenic syncope. We discuss the differential diagnosis and management of this finding.

Case presentation

A 56-year-old woman with a medical history including focal epilepsy, autoimmune thyroid disorder, and neurocardiogenic syncope with ictal asystole for which she underwent implantation of a dual-chamber pacemaker approximately 18 years ago. She had a Medtronic E2DR01 EnPulse generator implanted, with a Medtronic 5592 right atrial lead and Medtronic 5076 right ventricular (RV) lead. At implantation, the RV lead impedance was 445 Ω and had an adequate sensing value and pacing threshold. 9 years after the initial implantation, the patient underwent a generator exchange during which a Boston Scientific Accolade DR L301 pacemaker generator was implanted. 9 years after the generator change, patient presented to the clinic for a pacemaker device check after a device alert for a polarity switch event. Device interrogation demonstrated a transiently high bipolar RV lead impedance of greater than 2000 Ω with a safety switch to unipolar sensing and pacing (Figure 1). High impedance switch raises concern for potential conductor fracture or connection issue related to incomplete pin insertion or header pin mismatch. Other considerations, although extremely rare with modern-day devices, include loose set screws, setscrew misalignment, and header-bond failure.

Impedance trends with a transient impedance spike in the right ventricular (RV) lead (March 2024). Note the decrease in impedance after the spike owing to the change to unipolar pacing.

Clinic evaluation showed normal RV lead sensing (fixed bipolar sensing 2.0 mv) and thresholds (unipolar threshold 1.2 V/0.4 ms; bipolar threshold 1.8 V/0.4 ms). There was no noise at baseline or with isometric movements. Furthermore, there was normal unipolar (350 Ω) and bipolar RV lead impedance (450 Ω). Chest radiograph showed a stable lead position with no radiologic evidence of incomplete pin insertion. Owing to our suspicion of this intermittent increase in impedance being caused by lead-header interaction causing terminal ring oxidation, we subsequently reprogrammed her device to a bipolar sensing or unipolar pacing configuration, thereby eliminating the ring electrode from the pacing circuit and preventing the safety switch. Given that our patient was not pacemaker dependent and was being atrially paced 30% of the time and ventricularly paced 5% of the time, we also turned off the automatic polarity switch. Patient has subsequently been followed up remotely for approximately 1.5 years now, with no further evidence of intermittent high impedance alert.

Discussion

The differential diagnosis for sporadic high lead impedance includes lead fracture or issues with the lead-header connector. A sudden, extreme increase in impedance or noise oversensing with normal impedance trends typically suggests lead fracture.2 Sudden high impedance or noise oversensing occurring soon after implant, followed by a return to baseline, is indicative of a lead-connector problem.2 Swerdlow et al2 proposed an algorithm to differentiate between lead fracture and lead-connector issues. They suggest that high impedance or noise oversensing within 30 days of implantation, high impedance returning to baseline after 45 days, and impedance normalization within 45 days in devices implanted less than 200 days ago are signs of a connection issue.2 However, none of these factors applied in our case, which occurred almost 9 years after generator exchange, highlighting the challenges of diagnosing lead-connector problems. We present a case of lead-header interaction causing terminal ring oxidation leading to RV high impedance alert and lead impedance switch in a patient with a hybrid pacing system with a Boston Scientific generator and Medtronic leads.

A retrospective analysis of 395 patients by Karnik et al,1 with pacemakers implanted between 2015 and 2017, found that 9% of Boston Scientific pacemakers paired with Abbott or Medtronic leads exhibited transient out-of-range impedances with safety switching compared with 0% in other device-lead combinations (P = .0089). This relatively high incidence of intermittent high impedance in hybrid systems involving Boston Scientific generators has been attributed to the “setscrew-less” multipoint terminal ring connector used in contemporary pacemakers.1 Older Boston Scientific devices, as well as current Medtronic models, use a multibeam leaf-spring connector, providing 10 points of contact with the IS-1 terminal ring.3 In contrast, newer Boston Scientific and Abbott devices use a multipoint canted spring connector, with up to 70 contact points.3 Small axial or radial movements of the terminal ring can lead to microscarring and the accumulation of oxidative particles, which can impair electrical connectivity between the spring and the terminal ring.3 This is likely the cause of intermittent high impedance, recurrent safety switching to unipolar configuration, and lead oversensing in our case. The issue may be exacerbated by the newer canted spring configuration, which has 5 times the number of contact points compared with older leaf-spring models.1 This interaction is found to be more pronounced with non-Boston Scientific leads, likely owing to the greater microscopic surface roughness of the terminal ring and increased axial/radial movement in Medtronic and Abbott leads than Boston Scientific leads.1 In addition, although Boston Scientific pacemakers use a subthreshold pulse of 80 μA (0.04 V, assuming a 500 Ω impedance) for impedance testing, Abbott pacemakers use a pulse of 2 or 4 V, and Medtronic pacemakers use a pulse of 5 V, both of which can overcome elevated resistance caused by terminal ring oxidation more effectively.1 As a result, both Abbott and Medtronic devices are less likely to experience high impedance issues. Although Boston Scientific pacemakers use subthreshold pulses for routine impedance checks, actual pacing occurs at higher voltages and currents, which are less affected by microparticles and oxides, explaining why lead parameters remained stable during in-office interrogation, as in our case. However, these explanations remain hypothetical and unproven. In addition, the unusually low ventricular intrinsic signal might also be taken as a finding that questions lead integrity after an already long implant time. Moreover, despite the absence of fracture potentials during follow-up, microfracture of the lead with intermittent disconnection (eg, during extension) cannot completely be ruled out.

Potential solutions include reprogramming the device to a bipolar sensing/unipolar pacing configuration, thereby excluding the ring electrode from the pacing circuit and preventing safety switching (as was done in our case). Turning off the automatic polarity switch (also done in our case, given that the patient was not pacemaker dependent) and disabling thoracic and pacing impedance checks to inhibit automatic polarity switching are other options, although this was not performed in our case, given that it would limit the ability to monitor lead impedance trends and diagnostics in the future.4 Although rare, there is a possibility of loss of capture (with bipolar pacing configuration) at the time of high impedance alert, which can be life threatening and therefore, should not be performed in a pacemaker-dependent patient.

Our case underscores the challenges and nuances of accurately identifying lead-connector interface issues as a potential cause of sporadic high lead impedance, given that these issues can often be resolved through simple reprogramming, thus avoiding unnecessary lead extraction.
