Expressivity attributed to music affects the smoothness of bowing movements in violinists
Authors: Nicola Di Stefano, Daniela Lo Presti, Luigi Raiano, Carlo Massaroni, Chiara Romano, Emiliano Schena, Marc Leman, Domenico Formica
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Playing music is a complex task that relies on the combination of musicians’ technical and expressive skills. While the literature has investigated the effects of musical expressivity on the listeners, the way how technical difficulty and emotional expressivity affect musicians during playing has surprisingly received no attention. In an attempt to fill this gap in the literature, we collected behavioral and physiological data from twelve violinists playing 29 pieces that included both technical exercises and excerpts from classical repertoire for violin. After playing each stimulus, participants rated it for emotional expressivity and technical difficulty. During the entire session, cardiac parameters and electrodermal activity were collected, together with the kinematic parameters of the bowing gesture. A set of Linear Mixed-Effect (LME) models suggested that emotional expressivity attributed to music affected the fluidity of bowing (i.e., smoothness), with the excerpts rated as more expressive being performed in a less smooth way. In contrast, LME models revealed no effects of expressivity and technical difficulty on any of the physiological parameters of violinists. Our results offer novel insights into the psychophysiological dynamics that link motor parameters with musical expressivity. These findings could influence educational practices in music and deepen our understanding of aesthetic emotions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73593-4.

Introduction

Imagine a violinist playing Massenet’s Thais Meditation. To accomplish this complex musical goal, the violinist faces two distinct challenges, namely, playing all the notes correctly (i.e., with correct intonation and timing), and conveying the emotional meanings of the music. These challenges refer to technical difficulty and (musical) expressivity1–3. Remarkably, while mastering technical skills is the prerequisite for a correct performance, musicians consider expressivity to be one of the most important, when not the most important, aspects of performance2. As Lehmann and colleagues have observed: “Expressive performance is how performers display the deepest and most personal aspects of their work”3, [p. 85]. This is even more true for solo performance: going back to the opening example, should all the notes of the Thais be played correctly, but not expressively, the result would be retained as musically unsatisfactory or insufficient.

In the literature, technical difficulty and emotional expressivity have been investigated separately across different fields. While technical difficulty has been early addressed in methods for music learning [e.g.,4,5 for violin], only a few studies have investigated it empirically using physiological measurements6–8. Williamon and colleagues6, for example, monitored the physiological parameters of a pianist playing Bach’s English Suite in A Minor (BWV 807) in two different conditions (low stress vs. high stress, with stress being induced by the presence of audience). Results showed that some heart rate parameters (e.g., heart rate variability) were affected by the technical difficulty of the musical passages and the presence of the audience. More recently, focusing on musicians’ health, Blasco-Lafarga and colleagues7 investigated the physiological parameters of wind instrument players while playing pieces with different degrees of physical and cognitive demands. Results confirmed that the hardest tasks induce changes in heart rate variability.

In contrast with technical difficulty, the issue of musical expressivity has attracted the interest of empirical scientists soon after, if not in parallel with, its original conceptualization within the field of philosophical aesthetics [e.g.,9–11 for a review on early empirical findings]. A solid body of evidence has demonstrated that music expressivity induces changes in the autonomic responses of the listeners12–25. For example, Krumhansl16 found that emotionally expressive music decreases heart rate and skin temperature while increasing blood pressure and respiratory rate. Emotionally engaging music experiences were shown to elicit greater galvanic skin conductance response20,21 as well as to increase pupil size in the listeners21,23. Additionally, a number of studies have also addressed the question of how musicians move their bodies to express emotional intentions during performance26–28. For instance, Thompson and Luck28 asked eight pianists to play Chopin’s Prelude in E Minor (Op. 28, No.4) in four different conditions varying in the degree of expressivity and bodily movement, showing that pianists’ bodily gestures are inherently associated with both the structural element of music and how music is performed (i.e., expressively or not). At the same time, however, much less investigated is how performers’ fine motor gestures and micromovements contribute to conveying musical expressivity. Finally, studies have focused on the musical aspects that performers tend to manipulate when playing expressively. For example, Gabriellson and Juslin29 had nine musicians performing short melodies using different instruments to convey specific emotional characters to the listeners. Results showed that musical features such as dynamics, articulation, tempo, and vibrato were most affected by the emotional intention of the performer.

Given such widespread interest in the study of technical difficulty and musical expressivity, it might be surprising that the question of how musical expressivity affects performers during playing remained almost unexplored, especially in interaction with technical difficulty. A possible exception could be the study by Nakahara and colleagues30, in which the authors compared the physiological measures of thirteen pianists during expressive vs. nonexpressive piano playing (and listening). Results showed that the expressive conditions produced significantly higher levels of heart rate and low-frequency component of heart rate variability, with a greater modulation of these revealed also for performance than perception. Importantly, however, in the study by Nakahara and colleagues, expressivity is conceived and empirically investigated as an attribute of the performance rather than a property of the music itself. On the other side, lots of anecdotal knowledge on the expressivity of music and musical gestures could be obtained from the voice of soloists, who practice every day to refine their own ability to convey musical expressivity. For example, in one of his masterclasses, the world-class violinist Maxim Vengerov has identified the fluidity of bowing movements as a key parameter for playing espressivo31. Unfortunately, however, no empirical study has ever investigated whether and how musical expressivity affects kinematic parameters of bowing in relation to technical difficulty.

In this paper, we collected physiological and motion data from expert violinists while playing pieces with varying degrees of emotional expressivity and technical difficulty. Importantly, in our study, both technical difficulty and emotional expressivity are conceived and operationalized as features of music by asking violinists to rate each excerpt from 1 to 10 for difficulty and expressivity. Based on the reviewed findings, one might expect pieces rated as more expressive to induce changes in those physiological responses traditionally associated with (musical) emotion arousal (e.g., heart rate, heart rate variability, and electrodermal activity), while pieces rated as more technically demanding to engage the motor system more significantly, thus primarily affecting movement parameters. Contrary to this expectation, our results suggest that musical expressivity attributed to music affects violinists’ motion parameters (i.e., the smoothness of bowing), while no effects of perceived expressivity or technical difficulty were found on any of the physiological parameters of violinists. We discuss these findings in terms of motor control and bowing movements during music performance, drawing some consequences for music education and the debate on the nature of music emotions, and outlining future directions for empirical research in music expressivity and performance.

Results

First, we tested whether emotional expressivity and technical difficulty of musical excerpts were consistently rated across subjects. To this aim, we used the Krippendorff’s alpha coefficient32, which showed that ratings were overall consistent across subjects (alpha values were 0,78 for emotional expressivity and 0,68 for technical difficulty). Krippendorff set the minimum acceptable value of alpha to 0.66732. The lower agreement on technical difficulty might be explained by considering the slight differences in technical ability among participants, who may therefore perceive the same excerpt as more or less difficult according to their technical level. However, and importantly, excerpts marked as “fast” were consistently rated as significantly more difficult than the same excerpts marked as “comfort” tempo (t-test, p = .0003) (see Methods section and Supplementary Information for details on musical excerpts).

To test the effects of perceived technical difficulty and emotional expressivity on physiological and motion parameters, we run a set of Linear Mixed-Effect models (LME models hereafter)33, having the sensor indices as linearly dependent variables, and technical difficulty (TD), emotional expressivity (EE) ratings and their interaction as fixed effects, and subjects as random effect. The LME models were implemented using the MATLAB function “fitlme”. The fitting was performed using the Restricted Maximum Likelihood Estimation method for estimating the parameters. Discussion

Bow and motor control

Results suggest that more expressive music tends to be played less smoothly than less expressive music. This result confirms the general link between the kinematic features of movement35,36 and the quality of musical performance that has been broadly investigated in the literature37–39. Among the various kinematic variables, smoothness, as defined by SPARC index, seems to be particularly relevant for bow instruments40. In their seminal paper, Balasubramanian and colleagues34 considers a movement to be smooth “when it happens in a continual fashion without any interruptions”. Remarkably, continuity and fluency in movement have been retained as crucial for optimal bow control that, in turn, is fundamental to play virtuoso30,37,40. In contrast with other indexes of smoothness, SPARC is not affected by other movement features, such as velocity, amplitude, and duration34, thus making it a suitable parameter to study violinists’ bowing movements while playing excerpts with different tempi and duration. Testing a small sample of apprentice and advanced cellists (n = 3) and drummers (n = 3), Gonzalez-Sanchez and colleagues37 found that SPARC index was mostly consistent across participants and tempi, with both drummers and cellists increasing smoothness at faster tempi and decreasing smoothness at slow tempi. The results also suggested that musical expertise affects smoothness, such that more skilled participants were able to maintain a consistent smoothness across tempi while less experienced participants showed greater variability.

The crucial role of fluency of bowing is further supported by the evidence showing that, in addition to proximal muscles and joints (e.g., fingers, wrist, elbow), musicians act on distal elements to increase the smoothness of bowing. For instance, Rozé and colleagues40 showed that cellists use a set of primary postural directions to develop fluid kinematic bow features that prevent the production of poor-quality sounds. Given that mass balancing oscillations help avoid static muscle tension in fingers, hand, arm, and shoulder41, violinists might use head movements to ensure the smooth transmission of fine motor control all the way down to bowing42.

Considering this solid body of empirical evidence, how to account for our findings showing that expert violinists perform less smooth bowing movements while playing espressivo? We put forward two intertwined explanations, based on the assumption that more expressive excerpts are more demanding to play. First, we hypothesize that musicians might feel a state of performance-induced anxiety when playing expressive excerpts, and this can result in muscular tension43,44 that might, in turn, decrease movement smoothness. Second, as demonstrated in the literature on motor control, the demand for higher accuracy and precision in movements’ execution increases muscle co-contraction45,46. Extensive research on motor learning also demonstrates how co-contraction and movement fluidity co-vary during training, with an initial increase of muscle activation connected to a decrease of smoothness during the first part of the learning curve, followed by a gradual decrease when the movements are refined to that specific task47,48. Shifting to music playing, given that playing espressivo requires extensive and dedicated training sessions, we might speculate that the need for more accurate bowing movements in playing expressive excerpts in our protocol increased violinists’ co-contraction while decreasing smoothness. Additionally, we might also observe that expressive excerpts tend to be played at slower tempi than less expressive excerpts, and literature on motor control suggests that it is hard for humans to execute smooth rhythmic movements slowly49.

One might speculate that technical exercises, though technically challenging, lack interpretative complexity, which is closely tied to expressivity. A potential explanation could be the polarization of musical excerpts: smoothness in bowing technique is primarily learned and assimilated through exercises with minimal musical discourse, while greater expressive and interpretive complexity may lead to increase muscle contraction.

Physiological measures

Our study did not uncover any significant differences in the physiological parameters of musicians, which contrasts with previous findings that demonstrated technical difficulty affecting cardiac parameters6,7. Specifically6, explored stress induced by difficulty or audience presence, indicating that challenging passages likely contribute to stress and alter cardiac responses. However, methodological differences may partly explain these discrepancies. Whereas6 focused on a single lengthy and stylistically coherent piece (BWV 807), our study included a larger number of shorter and stylistically diverse excerpts, assessing difficulty across the entirety of each piece. Regarding Blasco-Lafarga et al.’s7 study on wind instrumentalists, their findings may not be directly comparable due to the relationship between respiratory and cardiac activities.

Music education

The foundations and handcraft underlying bowing fluency are particularly interesting from an educational perspective. Since music training often has a particular focus on the auditory output (i.e., the sound) rather than the musical gestures that produced it, the acquisition of optimal movement patterns, such as bowing gestures, typically takes time and effort and can be experienced, especially by children, as a slow and frustrating process50. Additionally, teachers often rely on their idiosyncratic vocabulary (e.g., verbal imagery and metaphors) to transfer motor instructions to the learner51. However, these verbal instructions can only provide a rough approximation of the target movement52, increasing the risk of resulting in ambiguous instructions. Our findings prove that a fundamental aspect of bowing technique, namely the smoothness of movement, can be easily monitored through a single, cheap, easy-to-use and -wear, magnetoinertial sensor opportunely placed on the wrist of the right harm. Implementing such a device in traditional learning paths would allow both the teacher and the learner to monitor a kinematic parameter of bowing that has been proven to impact the quality of sound, thus complementing the verbal instructions of the teacher with quantitative measures [e.g.,53].

Furthermore, highlighting the importance of the fluency of bowing movements for musical expressivity, our findings impact the debate on how to teach and learn expressivity54,55, drawing attention to the basic motor components of music performance rather than to the higher-level aspects related to music interpretation, such as articulation and dynamics, which have traditionally been credited with great importance when it comes to playing espressivo29.

Music emotions

Finally, our findings can impact the debate on the nature of emotions induced by music expressivity [e.g.,56]. While most scholars agree that aesthetic emotions can elicit changes in the physiological parameters of subjects who experience them, the existence of a motor component is controversial [e.g.,57,58]. Showing that the emotional expressivity of music affects motor behaviors of violinists seems to suggest that the link between emotion and movement, which is retained as crucial for the definition of primary or basic emotions59,60, plays a role also in the experience of aesthetic emotions. However, given the role of complex sensorimotor dynamics that characterize music performance in our study, further investigation is needed to assess to what extent our findings can be considered as a generalized response to the emotional expressivity of music.

Limitations

The limitations of the study mainly relate to the way the experiment has been implemented. First, we decided not to add electromyography (EMG) sensors to our system, as these would have been likely perceived by participants as annoying, when not negatively impacting bowing gestures, given the presence of the IMU on the wrist. EMG data could have provided precious indications on how to interpret the relationship between kinematic variables of bowing (e.g., velocity and acceleration), music properties (e.g., expressivity and difficulty) and muscular tension, an issue that therefore remains open for future investigation. However, given the nature of movements involved in the specific task and protocol, concerns might arise regarding the reliability of EMG data. In fact, while the activity of the muscles responsible for shoulder and elbow movements is more straightforward to record, EMG signals of forearm muscles involved in wrist rotation and forearm prono-supination suffer from crosstalk, difficulties in correctly placing the electrodes and strong movement artefacts61. Moreover, the correct setup of EMG electrodes that requires direct skin contact with the main muscles of arm and torso would have strongly impacted the feasibility of such “ecological protocol”. Second, given our focus on expressivity and technical difficulty as objective properties of music, we did not take into account any variables related to the actual musical output of the performance, namely, the quality of violin performances. Despite similar analysis might have provided additional information on how expressivity and technical difficulty affect the musical performance of violinists (e.g., intonation, rhythmic precision, vibrato), such information would have not helped to answer our main research question, which focused on how objective properties of music affect physiological parameters and motor behavior of performers, rather than their performance. Another potential limitation is the lack of information on how familiar the violinists were with the pieces they performed, such as whether they had practiced or performed them live before. This familiarity could potentially impact the results.

Future directions

Given that the concepts that form the theoretical background of the present study are allegedly universal in music performance (e.g., musical expressivity, technical difficulty, fluidity of musical gesture), the present investigation can be extended to other musical instruments. As far as concerns strings, we expect that similar results would be obtained from violists, who have biomechanical constraints similar to violinists, while it might be interesting to test the protocol with cellists and double-bass players, who perform similar bowing movements in different conditions (sitting or standing up) to verify whether the observed effect of expressivity on smoothness is generalizable, or at least independent of some fundamental biomechanical parameters. Moreover, similar protocols could be used to compare soloists, who are accustomed to playing expressive pieces under stressful conditions (e.g., concertos), with expert violinists (e.g., conservatory students). This comparison could test whether motor learning reduces co-contraction in music performance, as has been widely demonstrated for simple motor tasks47. If this is the case, soloists would exhibit higher SPARC values compared to the control group of experts. Additionally, future protocols might investigate the effect of familiarity with the pieces on the smoothness of performers’ movements. This could be done by comparing violinists who practiced the excerpts in advance with a control group playing them for the first time during the protocol. It might be worth extending the investigation of the relationship between smoothness and expressive performance also to non-stringed instruments, such as keyboards. Given the importance of reducing stiffness and promoting flexibility of the hand-arm unit to achieve better sound quality, especially while playing expressive melodic lines62, similar protocols on pianists could provide useful insights into the link between smoothness and expressivity. Finally, one might also consider examining the results from the opposite perspective: why technical (non-expressive) pieces tend to prioritize smoothness. This could lead to the development of new experimental protocols focused exclusively on technical difficulty and smoothness.

Materials and methods

The experimental protocol

12 violinists (7 females, 5 males; MeanAge = 24.67 SD = 2.50) were enrolled in the study. To ensure a homogeneous group, participants were selected based on a similar level of musical training. We verified this homogeneity by assessing the internal consistency of participants’ ratings on the “musical training” items of the Goldsmiths Musical Sophistication Index (Goldsmith-MSI). The calculated Intraclass Correlation Coefficient was 0.60, indicating a moderate degree of agreement among subjects’ ratings, with a highly significant p-value (< 0.001), suggesting that the ratings are consistent. At the beginning of the session, participants were invited to wear the wearable devices as follows: the ZEPHYR™ Bioharness v3 around the thorax, the SHIMMER 3 GSR + Unit with the electrodes in contact with two fingertips of the right hand, and two XSENS DOT, one on the elbow and the other one on the wrist of the right arm (see Fig. 3).

The experimental session started with a baseline in which the subject was standing in a relaxed position with open (1 minute) and closed eyes (1 minute). During the baseline, HR, HRV, and EDA data were gathered. Then, the playing session started, and the violinist was asked to play a sequence of 29 excerpts of different degrees of difficulty and expressivity, randomly ordered. The excerpts were presented to violinists through a 50’’ LCD TV screen placed at a comfortable distance in front of the participants. Participants were allowed to take a general look at the score before starting to play but were then asked to play the piece entirely from beginning to end. After playing each excerpt, participants were asked to rate it (from 1 to 10) for technical difficulty and musical expressivity.

Music excerpts included 5 orchestral and solo excerpts from the Western classical repertoire for violin (e.g., Bach, Mozart, Paganini) and 12 technical exercises and etudes (see Supplementary Information). The latter were presented twice with different tempo indications, namely “comfort tempo” and “fast tempo”, allowing us to manipulate the technical difficulty of the excerpts. Participants were instructed to play the excerpts labelled as “comfort tempo” in such a way that they could easily perform the whole excerpt; in contrast, they were asked to play “fast” excerpts faster than comfort ones, in such a way that the speed of execution was perceived as challenging, although not preventing them to play the entire excerpt at a consistent tempo. The average duration of the excerpts was 65.55 s. The mean duration of Comfort and Faster tempo excerpts was 64.80 s and 57.94 s, respectively. The shortest excerpt (n = 18, see supplementary materials) lasted an average of 40.33 s, while the longest (n = 19, see supplementary materials) lasted 90.88 s.

The whole session lasted about 45 min. Participants were allowed to take breaks if they wished. However, none of them reported fatigue or requested a break during the procedure.

All methods were carried out following relevant guidelines and regulations and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s) for publication of identifying information/images in an online open-access publication. The study received ethical clearance from the Campus Bio-Medico University Ethical Committee (project name: HUROB, reference number: 25.21). The informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s) for this study.

The wearable devices

The following three wearable devices were used in the experimental session.

1) the ZEPHYR™ Bioharness v3 System (ZS commercialized by Medtronic). It consists of a wearable chest strap integrating two dry electrodes for 1-lead ECG trace monitoring. The device is intended to be worn around the thorax in contact with the skin surface.

2) the SHIMMER 3 GSR + Unit (SH commercialized by Shimmer Sensing). It consists of two electrodes to be placed in contact with two fingertips of the hand to monitor the galvanic skin conductivity (GSC) signal.

3) the XSENS DOT Platform (XS commercialized by Xsens). It consists of a motion tracker integrating a 3-axis accelerometer, gyroscope, and magnetometer. The platform is intended to be mounted on specific body joints to measure join accelerations and angular velocities along the three axes (X, Y, and Z).

The wearable system composed by the above set of sensors allowed us to record physiological signals – namely, heart rate and heart rate variability (HR and HRV), and the tonic skin conductance components – and the movement metric for assessing the smoothness (i.e., the Spectral Arc Length – SPARC of the angular speed profile). Data of the ZS were stored on board while data from ZS and XSs were sent via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) to a dedicated device.

Resampling

The wearable devices recorded data at different sampling rates. The ZS records ECG and a 3-axis accelerometer at 250 Hz and 100 Hz respectively, while both the SH and the XS acquire data at 60 Hz. Hence, all the synchronized signals were resampled at a common sampling frequency, i.e., 100 Hz (fc).

3.Windowing

3.Windowing

Windowing

To easily identify the moment in which each violinist run the protocol, two types of event markers (i.e., square waves and pulses) were generated by using the ConsensysPRO software (Shimmer Sensing). This software enabled the acquisition of data from the SH sensor and the generation of a trigger signal which allows to add markers synchronized with the recorded GSC signal. In more detail, two square waves were generated at the beginning and the end of each experimental session (that included overall 2 baselines, i.e., open and closed eyes, and 29 excerpts), while two pulses were generated at the beginning and the end of each baseline or excerpt to allow splitting the recorded signals into 31 (2 baselines + 29 excerpts) separated data series. A total of 62 pulses were generated in order to obtain the 31 distinct data series. In this way, the signals belonging to each time window were analyzed while the other parts were discharged (see Fig. 4).

Time-domain and frequency-domain analyses

The physiological biomarkers and movement metrics under investigation were extracted from each windowed signal using custom algorithms developed in MATLAB environment.

While most of the relevant data analysis is reported in the main manuscript, hereafter we only report some additional details related to the pre-processing of the ECG signal.

HR was estimated using a time-domain analysis consisting of two main steps: filtering and peaks detections. The ECG signal was bandpass filtered using a first-order Butterworth filter (cut-off frequency 5–26 Hz) to make more evident the R peaks on the normalized ECG trace. Then, an automatic R peak detection was carried out using findpeaks function with the following parameters: “MinPeakHeigth” corresponding to the signal average value and “MinPeakDistance” corresponding to 0.7*(1/f0)*fc. The value of f0 is obtained from the frequency value corresponding to the maximum peak in the frequency spectrum of each windowed signal, obtained by performing the power spectral density (PSD) analysis based on the Welch method. Then, the interbeat intervals (IBI) were calculated as the time elapsed between consecutive R peaks (see Fig. 5).

2.RMMSD estimation

2.RMMSD estimation

RMMSD estimation

Among the various hearth rate variability indexes, we estimated the root mean square of differences between successive RR intervals (RMSSD) (61). The RMSSD is defined as the marker of the parasympathetic system, and low RMSSD values indicate stress: the lower the RMSSD value, the higher the sympathetic predominance because of parasympathetic withdrawal63.

The RMSSD was calculated by using the following formula, where IBI indicates inter-beat intervals:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:RMSSD\:\left[ms\right]=\:\sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum\:_{i=1}^{N}{{(IBI}_{i}-{IBI}_{i-1})}^{2}}$$\end{document}
