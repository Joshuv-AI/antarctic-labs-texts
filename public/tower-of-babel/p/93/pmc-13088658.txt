Application of lower limb exoskeleton robots in elderly patients with ICU-acquired weakness: a prospective clinical study
Authors: Xing Jin, Xiu-rong Wang, Kui Wang, Meng-yi Ren, Kang Yang, Bo Chen, Daejung Yang, Zhao-xiang Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the application effects of lower-limb exoskeleton robots in elderly patients with intensive care unit-acquired weakness (ICU-AW).MethodsA total of 114 elderly patients with ICU-AW admitted to Yangzhou Second People’s Hospital between May 2023 and May 2025 were enrolled and randomly assigned using a random number table to either a control group (n = 57) or an experimental group (n = 57). The control group received conventional rehabilitation interventions, while the experimental group underwent lower-limb exoskeleton robot-assisted training in addition to conventional rehabilitation. Outcome measures compared before and after the intervention included scores on the Chelsea Critical Care Physical Assessment Tool (CPAx), Medical Research Council (MRC) muscle strength scale, and Barthel Index (BI); muscle quality parameters (fat fraction, cross-sectional area, T2 mapping); bone mineral density (T-score and classification); serum levels of creatine kinase (CK), osteocalcin (OC), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP); and the incidence of complications.ResultsCompared with baseline, both groups showed significant increases in CPAx, MRC, and BI scores, muscle cross-sectional area, T-scores, and serum OC levels, along with significant decreases in fat fraction, muscle water content T2, prevalence of osteoporosis, and serum CK and ALP levels (all P < 0.05). The improvements in all these outcome measures were significantly greater in the experimental group than in the control group (all P < 0.05). The overall complication rate was significantly lower in the experimental group [7.02% (4/57)] compared with the control group [21.05% (12/57)] (P = 0.031).ConclusionRehabilitation training using a lower-limb exoskeleton robot effectively improved muscle quality and bone mineral density in patients with ICU-AW, promoted physical functional recovery, enhanced quality of life, and demonstrated a favorable safety profile.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07259-3.Keywords: Critical illness polyneuropathy, Aged, Robotics, Muscle weakness, Bone density, Rehabilitation

Introduction

With the intensifying population aging, the proportion of elderly patients in intensive care units (ICUs) continues to rise, making ICU-acquired weakness (ICU-AW) a critical clinical issue affecting patient prognosis [1, 2]. The incidence of ICU-AW among elderly ICU patients reaches 40–60%, leading to muscle atrophy and functional impairments that not only prolong hospital stays but also increase long-term disability risks, significantly exacerbating healthcare burdens [3–5]. ICU-AW development involves multiple mechanisms including inflammatory responses, metabolic disorders, and muscle disuse [6–8]. Elderly patients are particularly vulnerable to severe muscle weakness due to sarcopenia and underlying conditions. Studies indicate that over 50% of elderly ICU-AW patients still exhibit significant functional impairments six months after discharge [9]. Although early rehabilitation has been proven effective in improving outcomes, the unique ICU environment-characterized by limited access to medical tubing and hemodynamic instability-poses challenges for standardized implementation of traditional rehabilitation methods [10].

Lower-limb exoskeleton robotics offers an innovative solution to this challenge. Through intelligent mechanical support and precise gait control, these devices enable early standardized training while reducing healthcare worker burdens [11]. While exoskeletons have demonstrated promising results in neurological rehabilitation [12], their application research in elderly ICU-AW patients remains relatively underdeveloped. The age-related characteristics of elderly patients, such as osteoporosis and limited joint mobility, combined with the unique environment of ICU settings, impose heightened demands on the safety and applicability of this technology.

This study aims to investigate the rehabilitation efficacy of lower-limb exoskeleton robots for elderly ICU-AW patients, providing new evidence for intelligent rehabilitation in ICU-AW scenarios. The findings hold significant clinical value for improving prognoses in critically ill elderly patients.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective, randomized, parallel-group, superiority clinical trial employed a 1:1 allocation ratio with repeated-measures design. Conducted at Yangzhou Second People’s Hospital from May 2023 to May 2025, we enrolled 114 elderly patients diagnosed with ICU-acquired weakness (ICU-AW). Using a random number table generator, participants were allocated to either a control group (n = 57) receiving conventional rehabilitation or an experimental group (n = 57) receiving lower-limb exoskeleton robot-assisted therapy. The study protocol was prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500106047). This trial adhered to CONSORT 2010 guidelines; participant screening and flow are detailed in the CONSORT Flow Diagram ( Figure S1).

Assessment time points

Baseline evaluations were completed within one week prior to intervention initiation. Follow-up assessments occurred at 8 weeks ± 3 days post-intervention. Long-term follow-up beyond the immediate post-intervention period was not conducted, which we acknowledge as a study limitation. A 4-week telephone follow-up was performed post-intervention for safety monitoring purposes only.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria: (1) ICU length of stay ≥7 days; (2) Meeting British Medical Research Council (MRC) diagnostic criteria for ICU-AW (total MRC score <48 persisting >24 hours); (3) Age 60–80 years; (4) Stable vital signs, conscious, and capable of effective communication; (5) Able to tolerate interventions; (6) Signed informed consent for voluntary participation.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Unstable medical condition or risk of deterioration; (2) History of neuromuscular disorders (e.g., stroke) or other muscle-related pathologies; (3) Immunosuppression or long-term systemic corticosteroid use; (4) Congenital limb dysfunction or requirement for limb immobilization; (5) Active malignancy; (6) Severe anemia or malnutrition; (7) Coma, severe cognitive impairment, or obtundation; (8) Contraindications to MRI/DXA or musculoskeletal conditions preventing assessment.

Interventions

The control group received a standard rehabilitation protocol comprising routine clinical management, nursing care, nutritional support, and comprehensive limb rehabilitation training (including joint mobility exercises, muscle strengthening, electric bed exercises, aerobic activity, respiratory therapy, occupational therapy, neuromuscular electrical stimulation, and traditional Chinese rehabilitation). Training was administered for 2 h daily, 5 days per week, over 8 weeks. In the experimental group, participants received conventional rehabilitation therapy supplemented with the UGO220 exoskeleton robot (Chengtian Technology, Hangzhou, China). Technical specifications of the device are detailed in Supplementary Material (Supplementary Table 1). Equipment parameters (thigh length 128 mm adjustable range, calf length 128 mm adjustable range, hip width 110 mm adjustable range) were individually adjusted for optimal fit. The device features motor-driven assistance at hip/knee joints with maximum assistance torque of 30 N·m at hip, 25 N·m at knee, and 15 N·m at ankle. The device operator was solely responsible for technical operation and did not deliver rehabilitation instructions or provide patient encouragement, thereby minimizing attention bias. The progressive protocol began with three 30-minute sessions weekly, advancing to daily 30-minute sessions (5 days/week) over 8 weeks based on patient tolerance (Supplementary Table 2). Training intensity was progressively increased weekly by 10%, with initial parameters set at walking speed 0.2 m/s and stride length 0.3 m. Heart rate and oxygen saturation were continuously monitored via portable pulse oximetry to ensure safety. Real-time monitoring data was displayed on the device’s human-machine interaction screen, with emergency stop response time of 0.5 s. A photograph depicting the UGO220 exoskeleton robot in use during a patient-assisted gait training session is provided in Supplementary Figure S2.

The training protocol was structured in three phases: preparation, active training, and emergency response. During preparation, the system was activated and patients were carefully positioned with individualized adjustments to leg length and hip width. The training phase involved configuring parameters (walking speed 0.3–0.8 m/s, stride length 30–50 cm, weight support 30–50%), securely transferring the patient into the exoskeleton, initiating gait training with continuous vital sign monitoring (heart rate and oxygen saturation), and concluding with patient transfer back to the wheelchair, thorough skin inspection, and documentation. In case of clinical deterioration, the emergency response protocol mandated immediate activation of the stop button and rapid patient transfer to safety. Detailed step-by-step procedures are available in the Supplementary Material.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

Change in Chelsea Critical Care Physical Assessment Tool (CPAx) score from baseline to post-intervention.

Secondary outcomes

Changes in Medical Research Council (MRC) sum score, Barthel Index (BI), muscle quality parameters (fat fraction, cross-sectional area, T2 mapping), bone mineral density T-score, and serum biomarkers (CK, OC, ALP).

Physical Function Recovery Assessment: The Chelsea Critical Care Physical Assessment Tool (CPAx) [13] was used to evaluate physical function recovery pre- and post-intervention. This 10-item assessment (respiratory function, bed mobility, sitting balance, etc.) uses a 0–5 scale per item (total range 0–50), with higher scores indicating greater functional independence. Assessments were conducted by two senior physical therapists (each with > 8 years ICU rehabilitation experience and professional certification), who were independent from the intervention team and blinded to group allocation.

Muscle Quality Assessment: Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) assessments were performed pre- and post-intervention using a United Imaging uMR580 1.5T superconducting scanner (Shanghai United Imaging Healthcare, China) with an integrated lower extremity dedicated coil. Imaging parameters: For fat fraction imaging, mDixon-Quant sequence was used at mid-thigh axial plane; for T2 mapping, multi-echo spin-echo sequence was applied; for structural imaging, short-tau inversion recovery (STIR) coronal sequence was performed. All subjects were required to avoid strenuous physical activity for 24 h before scanning. During scanning, subjects were placed in standard supine position with feet-first orientation, aligning the limb axis with the magnetic field center. T1WI (TR 500ms, TE 15ms, slice thickness 5 mm, gap 0 mm) and T2 mapping (TR 1500ms, TE 20/40/60/80ms) were acquired. Fat fraction was calculated using a 3-point Dixon technique with automated muscle-fat segmentation via United Imaging uWS-MR post-processing software (v2.5.3). Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually delineated by a board-certified radiologist (10 years MRI diagnostic experience) at the mid-femur over a 30 cm segment. The radiologist was blinded to group allocation. Measurement reliability was confirmed via intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC = 0.92). ROI delineation was performed at the mid-femur level (defined as the midpoint between the greater trochanter and lateral condyle) on axial images of bilateral quadriceps femoris (integrating rectus femoris, vastus medialis, vastus lateralis, and vastus intermedius) at the most muscular belly region. ROIs strictly avoided intermuscular fascia, visible vessels, and bone areas. Image analysis was independently completed by a physician with over five years of musculoskeletal MRI analysis experience under blinded conditions. Fat fraction and muscle T2 values were automatically calculated by the scanner’s post-processing software; muscle cross-sectional area was manually outlined on corresponding axial sections of coronal STIR images and calculated using ImageJ software via pixel counting method. To ensure consistent measurements, the same radiologist repeated measurements on 10 randomly selected samples at a 2-week interval.

Bone Mineral Density (BMD) Testing: BMD was measured using a PinYuan DXA-800E dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scanner (PinYuan Medical Technology, Jiangsu, China) that underwent weekly standard phantom calibration (coefficient of variation < 1%). Measurements were obtained at the distal one-third of the forearm (~ 10 cm below the olecranon). Measurement protocol included: (1) Pre-measurement skin preparation: alcohol swab cleaning of forearm skin at 1/3 distal site, followed by uniform application of coupling gel to ensure full contact between skin and probe; (2) Patient positioning: supine position with forearm naturally extended on detection platform, adjusting limb position to align bone densitometer with limb central axis; (3) Scan parameters: cone-beam imaging technology, scanning range of distal 1/3 forearm region, scan speed 5 mm/s, resolution 0.1 mm; (4) Data output: automatic generation of T-score, Z-score, bone mineral density (BMD), bone mineral content (BMC), and bone quality index parameters. Forearm site selection was justified because: (1) ICU patients are typically bedridden—spine/hip positioning requires patient transport and increases dislodgement risk; (2) Elderly patients often have hip arthroplasty or degenerative disease compromising accuracy; (3) The forearm is cortical-rich and mechanically responsive, reflecting systemic bone metabolism; (4) WHO technical reports (2007) recognize forearm as a feasible alternative for ICU populations. T-scores were calculated using the Chinese Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS 2018) reference database, which includes forearm BMD data from 20 to 80 year-old Han Chinese participants stratified by sex. Classification followed WHO criteria (T-score ≥ -1.0 = normal; -2.5 < T-score < -1.0 = osteopenia; T-score ≤ -2.5 = osteoporosis) [15]. Scans were performed by a nuclear medicine technologist (5 years DXA experience) following standard operating procedures, with the technologist blinded to group allocation. The device underwent daily standard phantom calibration using EUR Spine Phantom and weekly deep calibration, with calibration error allowance ≤ 1% and traceable calibration records.

Randomization and blinding

An independent statistician generated the allocation sequence using SAS 9.4 software. Allocation concealment was achieved through sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes stored by the study coordinator and opened only after participant enrollment.

This study employed assessor blinding. Group allocation information was managed by independent statistical staff; assessors (physical therapists, radiologists, nuclear medicine technologists) received only numbered assessment instructions and were not involved in randomization or intervention delivery.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was estimated using pilot data from 20 participants. Based on the observed mean improvement in CPAx scores—14 ± 4 points in the experimental group compared to 9 ± 4 points in the control group—a statistical power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 software. Assuming a two-tailed test with an alpha level of 0.05 and a power of 0.90, a minimum of 51 participants per group was calculated. To account for an estimated 15% attrition rate, we enrolled a total of 114 participants, with 57 in each group.

Missing data handling

One participant in the experimental group withdrew due to medical instability. Additionally, laboratory data were incomplete for one participant in each group as a result of failed venous access. Missing laboratory values were imputed using the Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) method. Primary outcome analyses were conducted following the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, with complete case analysis applied for key variables.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 22.0 software. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Primary analyses employed mixed-design repeated measures ANOVA (Group × Time) for each outcome. When Mauchly’s test indicated violation of sphericity (P < 0.05), the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. The Group × Time interaction term was the primary focus. Significant interactions (P < 0.05) were followed by post-hoc independent samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests on change scores, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Effect sizes were calculated as Cohen’s d for parametric data and r = |Z|/√N for non-parametric data. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 114 elderly patients with ICU-acquired weakness were enrolled and randomized into the experimental group (n = 57) or the control group (n = 57). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences in age, gender distribution, or body mass index (BMI) (all P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Functional recovery outcomes

Before the intervention, no significant differences existed between groups in Chelsea Critical Care Physical Assessment Tool (CPAx) score, Medical Research Council (MRC) sum score, or Barthel Index (BI) score (all P > 0.05). After 8 weeks of intervention, both groups showed significant improvements in all functional scores compared to their respective baselines (all P < 0.001). However, the magnitude of improvement was significantly greater in the experimental group than in the control group (all P < 0.001). The median changes in CPAx, MRC, and BI scores for the experimental group were 15.0, 10.0, and 24.0 points, respectively, which were substantially larger than the corresponding changes of 10.0, 3.0, and 17.0 points observed in the control group (Table 2).

Muscle quality parameters

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) assessments revealed significant Group × Time interactions for all muscle quality parameters (all P < 0.001). Post-intervention, the experimental group exhibited a significantly greater reduction in muscle fat fraction, a larger increase in muscle cross-sectional area (CSA), and a more pronounced decrease in muscle water content (T2 mapping) compared to the control group (all P < 0.05) (Table 3).

Bone mineral density

Bone mineral density (BMD) T-scores and classification were similar between groups at baseline (both P > 0.05). After the intervention, a significant Group × Time interaction was observed for T-scores (P < 0.001). The experimental group showed a significantly greater improvement in T-scores compared to the control group (P = 0.031). Furthermore, the distribution of BMD classification (normal, osteopenia, osteoporosis) differed significantly between the two groups post-intervention (P = 0.030), with a more marked reduction in the prevalence of osteoporosis observed in the experimental group (from 22.8% to 1.8%) than in the control group (from 33.3% to 15.8%) (Table 4).

Discussion

The key finding of this study is that lower-limb exoskeleton training serves as a powerful adjunct rather than a replacement for conventional rehabilitation in elderly patients with ICU-acquired weakness. The experimental group demonstrated superior improvements across functional recovery, muscle quality, bone density, and bone metabolism parameters, along with a significantly lower burden of complications. These results confirm our hypothesis that mechanized gait assistance enhances rehabilitation efficacy through controlled mechanical loading and standardized motor pattern training. Notably, the intervention proved safe in the ICU setting, with no training-related adverse events. The meaningful attenuation of osteoporosis progression further reinforces the public health value of this intervention.

Our findings corroborate and extend the existing literature in this emerging field. Xu et al. [16] previously demonstrated that exoskeleton-assisted lower-limb training improves muscle thickness and MRC scores beyond conventional therapy, while Castelli et al. [17] reported enhanced motor function and quality of life using robotic devices integrated with virtual reality. Our study builds on this foundation by simultaneously evaluating multi-dimensional outcomes—functional, musculoskeletal, and metabolic—specifically in an elderly ICU-AW population, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of the intervention’s effects. Specifically regarding muscle structure, the experimental group showed greater improvements in MRI-derived metrics, including reduced fat fraction and increased cross-sectional area. However, it is important to acknowledge that the 8-week intervention period, while sufficient to detect significant between-group differences, is a relatively short timeframe for substantial structural muscle remodeling. Although we employed standardized imaging protocols, a blinded assessor, and demonstrated high measurement reliability (ICC = 0.92), part of the observed change could be attributable to the inherent measurement variability of MRI techniques. Although consistency across studies strengthens the evidence base, our focus on the previously understudied demographic of elderly ICU-AW patients carries distinct clinical relevance.

The contribution of our study lies in its mechanistic and translational insights. While prior research established feasibility and short-term efficacy, we elucidated specific pathways through which exoskeleton training may exert its effects: mechanical stress-induced protein synthesis mediated via mTOR pathway activation, bone mechanostat stimulation through controlled loading, and motor relearning driven by neuroplasticity. These mechanisms collectively explain the concurrent improvements in muscle hypertrophy and bone formation—an outcome rarely achieved with conventional rehabilitation alone. Furthermore, the significant reduction in complications suggests that technological precision in dose control may offer greater safety than traditional manual training, potentially indicating a paradigm shift in ICU rehabilitation philosophy.

From a health economics perspective, the GUT220 exoskeleton demonstrates promising cost-effectiveness potential. Amortized over its lifespan with high patient throughput, the per-patient equipment cost becomes minimal compared to the substantial savings in therapist time. Each training session costs significantly less than manual gait training, accumulating considerable savings over an 8-week program. However, initial capital investment, maintenance, and training requirements remain considerable barriers to widespread adoption, necessitating technological maturation and healthcare policy support. Regarding ICU implementation feasibility, this study confirms that exoskeleton training can be safely delivered in the ICU environment under continuous monitoring without adverse events. Successful implementation requires adequate space, trained operators, and real-time availability of rehabilitation physicians—resources typically accessible in well-resourced tertiary centers but potentially limiting for smaller hospitals. Safety protocols and staff training curricula will be essential for successful translation.

Several methodological limitations constrain interpretation and generalizability. First, the single-center design, modest sample size, and cohort homogeneity—predominantly Han Chinese patients with strong family support—limit external validity, as adherence may exceed levels typical of Western healthcare systems. Second, although outcome assessors were blinded, functional evaluators could not be masked to group allocation, introducing potential detection bias; this limitation, combined with the inability to implement double-blinding, may have biased functional assessments despite partial mitigation by objective measures. Third, differential operator interaction may have created an attention effect despite matched training duration; future studies should incorporate sham-exoskeleton controls to isolate technological from interpersonal effects. Fourth, the absence of long-term follow-up precludes assessment of durability and post-intensive care syndrome impact, limiting evaluation of true clinical value. Fifth, diagnostic heterogeneity (mixed medical, surgical, and trauma ICU diagnoses) and lack of severity-stratified subgroup analysis hinder identification of optimal patient candidates and prevent determination of which severity phenotypes benefit most from exoskeleton training. Sixth, forearm BMD measurements, while practical for bedbound patients, may not reflect axial skeletal health. Seventh, retrospective trial registration (ChiCTR2500106047, July 16, 2025) falls short of prospective registration standards for human intervention studies. Eighth, the substantial financial burden (≈ 200,000 CNY device cost plus maintenance contracts) and operational complexity (dedicated ICU space and trained operators) present major implementation barriers warranting further health-economic evaluation.

In conclusion, lower-limb exoskeleton-assisted training represents a safe and effective augmentation strategy that amplifies the benefits of conventional rehabilitation for elderly ICU-AW patients. The intervention concurrently improves muscle quality, bone density, functional independence, and complication rates through mechanistically plausible pathways. Despite study limitations and practical implementation challenges, the compelling clinical and public health implications justify further multicenter trials with extended follow-up, cost-effectiveness analyses, and severity-stratified cohorts to optimize patient selection and integrate this technology into routine ICU rehabilitation practice.
