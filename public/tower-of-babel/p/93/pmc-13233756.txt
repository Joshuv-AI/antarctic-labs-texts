Clinical characteristics and IgE-binding protein profiles of patients with buckwheat allergy in China☆
Authors: Sainan Bian, Zhenjing Geng, Yanjun Zhou, Rui Tang, Ruiqi Wang, Jinlyu Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo characterize patients with buckwheat allergy in China and to investigate the associations between clinical manifestations and different buckwheat IgE-binding proteins.MethodsPatients diagnosed with buckwheat allergy at the Department of Allergy, Peking Union Medical College Hospital, were followed up between 2023 and 2024 and retrospectively enrolled. Serum allergen-specific IgE (sIgE) levels were measured using the ImmunoCAP system, and buckwheat IgE-binding proteins were identified by immunoblotting.ResultsA total of 46 patients with buckwheat allergy were included, with a mean age of 26 ± 11 years. 45 patients (97.8%) developed immediate allergic reactions after buckwheat ingestion. Anaphylaxis occurred in 33 patients (73.3%). Seventeen patients (37.0%) experienced respiratory allergic symptoms upon exposure to buckwheat husks. The median f11-sIgE level was 10.75 kUA/L (IQR, 3.01–20.38). Fifteen IgE-binding proteins with different molecular weights were identified, among which the 18–19 kDa protein showed the highest positivity rate (100%). The 14 kDa protein was detected more frequently in patients without anaphylaxis than in those with anaphylaxis induced by buckwheat consumption (25% vs 0%, P = 0.016). Sensitization to the 22–24 kDa protein was more common in patients with buckwheat husk allergy than in those with allergy limited to buckwheat ingestion (52.9% vs. 24.1%, P = 0.048). Patients positive for the 22–24 kDa protein had significantly higher f11-sIgE levels than those who were negative (21.85 [5.19–38.05] vs 7.53 [2.84–12.95] kUA/L, P = 0.009). Multivariate logistic regression analysis further showed that sensitization to the 22–24 kDa protein was independently associated with concomitant buckwheat husk allergy (P = 0.027, OR = 5.22, 95% CI: 1.21–22.60).ConclusionPatients with buckwheat allergy may present with anaphylaxis, and respiratory symptoms upon exposure to buckwheat husks. Sensitization to the 22–24 kDa protein was associated with concomitant buckwheat husk allergy and higher f11-sIgE levels.Keywords: Buckwheat allergy, Food allergy, IgE-binding protein, Anaphylaxis

Introduction

Buckwheat has gained increasing popularity as a health food and is commonly used as an alternative staple, particularly among individuals with diabetes. Buckwheat allergy is a clinically significant food allergy and poses a significant health concern in Asia, where buckwheat consumption is relatively common.1 Immediate-type food allergy and food-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis (FDEIA) have been reported as major clinical phenotypes of buckwheat allergy.1 In addition to ingestion-related reactions, allergic symptoms may also occur following inhalational exposure, as buckwheat husks are traditionally used as pillow fillings in some Asian countries.2

Previously, we reported the first case series of patients with buckwheat allergy in China.3 In earlier work, our group also identified an 18 kDa buckwheat protein, which was subsequently designated as Fag t 6.4 To date, several allergenic components of common buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum) have been described.1 However, the IgE-binding protein profile of buckwheat has not been fully characterized, particularly with respect to its association with specific clinical manifestations, including anaphylaxis and buckwheat husk–related allergy.

Therefore, the present study aimed to characterize the clinical features of patients with buckwheat allergy in China and to investigate the associations between clinical phenotypes and different buckwheat IgE-binding proteins.

Methods

Patients

Patients diagnosed with buckwheat allergy, including allergy induced by food ingestion and inhalational exposure, at Department of Allergy, Peking Union Medical College Hospital were followed up between 2023 and 2024 and retrospectively enrolled. The diagnosis of buckwheat allergy was based on a compatible clinical history together with positive results of buckwheat-specific IgE (sIgE) testing and/or skin prick testing using buckwheat dough, after exclusion of other potential culprit allergens. Clinical characteristics, allergen-sIgE levels, and buckwheat IgE-binding protein profiles were collected and analyzed.

Allergen-specific IgE measurement and buckwheat IgE-binding protein immunoblotting

Serum allergen-sIgE levels were measured using the ImmunoCAP system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Uppsala, Sweden). A value > 0.35 kUA/L was considered positive.

Buckwheat proteins were extracted using phosphate-buffered saline at 4°C, followed by agitation, centrifugation, and collection of the supernatant. Protein extracts were denatured with sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) loading buffer and separated on Bis–Tris gels. Proteins were then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes and blocked with non-fat milk. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with diluted patient sera (1:10, v/v), followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated goat anti-human IgE. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using a chemiluminescent substrate and analyzed by densitometry.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages, and continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were compared using the independent samples t-test, whereas non-normally distributed data were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate factors associated with concomitant buckwheat husk allergy. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

General characteristics of patients

A total of 46 patients with buckwheat allergy were enrolled, including 23 females (50%) and 23 males (50%), with a mean age of 26 ± 11 years. Twenty-seven patients had a history of allergic rhinitis (58.7%), 19 had asthma (41.3%), 8 had eczema (17.4%), and 12 had urticaria (26.1%). Eleven patients reported allergic reactions to other foods (23.9%), and 4 reported a history of drug allergy (8.7%).

Among the 45 patients (97.8%) who developed immediate allergic reactions after buckwheat ingestion, cutaneous manifestations were observed in 33 (73.3%), respiratory symptoms in 35 (77.8%), and gastrointestinal involvement in 21 (46.7%). Anaphylaxis occurred in 33 patients (73.3%), including anaphylactic shock in 7 (15.6%). In addition, 17 patients (37.0%) experienced upper or lower respiratory allergic symptoms upon exposure to buckwheat husks, presenting as isolated rhinitis in 1 case (5.9%) and asthma in the remaining 16 cases (94.1%) (Table 1).

Buckwheat-sIgE and IgE-binding protein profiles

All 46 patients were positive for buckwheat-sIgE (f11). The median f11-sIgE level was 10.75 kUA/L (IQR, 3.01–20.38), with a mean sIgE class of 3 ± 1. The median total IgE level was 180 kU/L (IQR, 99–368).

Fifteen buckwheat IgE-binding proteins with distinct molecular weights were identified by immunoblotting. The 18–19 kDa protein showed the highest positivity rate (100%), followed by the 34 kDa (59%) and 69–70 kDa (39%) proteins (Table 2).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically characterize the clinical features and IgE-binding protein profile of patients with buckwheat allergy in China. By integrating clinical phenotypes with immunoblot-based IgE-binding protein profiling, our findings provide new insights into the heterogeneity of buckwheat allergy and its potential molecular correlates.

The prevalence of buckwheat allergy has been estimated to range from 0.1% to 0.4% in Asian populations.2 Buckwheat can induce IgE-mediated allergic reactions through multiple exposure routes, including oral ingestion as a food allergen, occupational inhalational exposure during processing or handling, and household environmental exposure via buckwheat husk pillows.2

In this study, cutaneous manifestations, respiratory symptoms, and gastrointestinal involvement after buckwheat ingestion were observed in 73.3%, 77.8%, and 46.7% of patients, respectively. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that skin (30.0–77.8%), respiratory (30.0–61.1%), and gastrointestinal (55.6–70.0%) symptoms are common clinical features of buckwheat allergy.5,6 Approximately 70% of patients in our cohort experienced anaphylaxis, which is comparable to reports from Korea and Australia.7,8

The median buckwheat-sIgE (f11) level in our study was 10.75 kUA/L, exceeding the previously proposed diagnostic cut-off of 1.26 kUA/L, which has been reported to provide high sensitivity and specificity.9 However, no significant difference in the f11-sIgE/total IgE ratio was observed between patients with and without anaphylaxis induced by buckwheat ingestion. This finding is in line with previous studies suggesting that the predictive value of the specific IgE/total IgE ratio for clinical severity or oral food challenge outcomes remains controversial.5,10

Fifteen buckwheat IgE-binding proteins with molecular weights ranging from 10 to 170 kDa were identified in this study (10, 13, 14, 16, 18–19, 22–24, 34, 36, 38, 40–50, 54, 56, 69–70, 100–130, and 130–170 kDa). Several of these proteins have been reported previously, including bands at approximately 9, 10, 14, 16, 19, 24, 30, 34–38, 40–50, 69–70, 75, and 100 kDa.2,11, 12, 13 By contrast, the 13 kDa protein has not been described to date, to our knowledge. Owing to its very low positivity rate, association analyses with f11-sIgE levels or clinical severity could not be performed, and further characterization in larger cohorts is warranted. Similarly, a previous study identified the 14 kDa buckwheat protein via N-terminal sequencing, and its sequence shared partial homology with a rice protein (phenylalanine ammonia lyase).14 These findings indicated that low-molecular-weight buckwheat IgE-binding proteins in the 13–14 kDa range may represent uncharacterized protein isoforms or enzyme-related components with cross-species homology, rather than well-documented classic allergens.

Several well-characterized buckwheat allergens have been reported to match these molecular weight ranges in previous studies. For reference, the 24 kDa Fag e 1 (β-subunit of 13S globulin), 16 kDa Fag e 2 (2S albumin), and the 19 kDa Fag e 3 (vicilin-like protein) have been widely documented.15 In addition, a 3.9 kDa hevein-like protein (Fag e 4) and a 55 kDa vicilin-like antimicrobial polypeptide (Fag e 5) have also been identified in prior research.16 Fag e 1 and Fag e 2 are considered the major allergens of common buckwheat, whereas Fag e 3, Fag e 4, and Fag e 5 are regarded as relevant allergens contributing to buckwheat allergy.16,17 In the present study, reactive bands were classified only by molecular weight. Definitive identification of specific allergens within these molecular weight ranges requires further verification, such as mass spectrometry.

Sensitization to the 22–24 kDa protein was significantly associated with concomitant buckwheat husk allergy, and remained an independent risk factor after adjustment for f11-sIgE levels. This finding implies that this protein may potentially participate in inhalation-related sensitization, instead of only mediating ingestion-triggered reactions. Proteins in this molecular weight range might possess physicochemical properties that could plausibly facilitate aerosolization or prolonged mucosal exposure, which may partially account for their association with respiratory allergic reactions to buckwheat husks. Exposure to buckwheat husks can trigger respiratory allergic symptoms, suggesting that inhalational routes likely contribute to non–oral buckwheat allergy. A previous review shows that the lipocalin family, with a molecular weight of 17–25 kDa, includes multiple respiratory allergens, such as Bla g 4, Bos d 2, Can f 1, Can f 2, and Fel d 7.18 Aerosolized food proteins, including plant-derived allergens, have been shown to provoke rhinitis, asthma, or systemic reactions in sensitized individuals, and occupational studies indicate that high–molecular-weight proteins can act as inhalant allergens.19, 20, 21 Their physicochemical properties, such as stability and particle behavior, may influence their potential to become airborne and interact with the respiratory mucosa.22 In this context, sensitization to the 22–24 kDa buckwheat protein may reflect characteristics that favor inhalational exposure, which could explain its association with husk-related respiratory allergy, although mechanistic studies are needed. However, these findings remain preliminary and require further validation in a larger cohort.

The 18–19 kDa protein showed universal IgE binding in our cohort, suggesting that it represents a major IgE-binding protein of buckwheat in this population. However, its lack of association with clinical severity or specific phenotypes indicates limited utility for risk stratification. This molecular interval covers 2 major buckwheat allergens: Fag t 6 (18.4 kDa) identified by our group4 and the classic 19 kDa allergen Fag e 3 with a reported positive rate of 83.3%.23 These findings support that this reactive band most probably represents natural allergenic components, though potential mixed components cannot be entirely ruled out. Previous studies have reported regional differences in immunoblot recognition patterns. A Japanese study identified a 24 kDa protein as the most frequently recognized component,24 while other reports suggested that the 19 kDa allergen may discriminate clinical reactivity to buckwheat.23,25 These findings collectively suggest geographic heterogeneity in buckwheat sensitization profiles.

Interestingly, sensitization to the 14 kDa protein was observed exclusively in patients without anaphylaxis, while none of the patients with anaphylaxis showed IgE reactivity to this component. However, this finding relied on merely 3 positive cases, which limited the robustness of this finding. This preliminary result should be interpreted with great caution. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate this finding. A study in Korean children with buckwheat allergy suggested that IgE reactivity to a 40–50 kDa protein may be associated with moderate-to-severe clinical symptoms.11 Moreover, 2S albumin allergens have been recognized as clinically relevant components in IgE-mediated immediate hypersensitivity reactions to buckwheat, including anaphylaxis.15,26

In the present study, no significant differences in the positive rates of IgE-binding proteins were observed among patients presenting with cutaneous, respiratory, or gastrointestinal manifestations. In contrast, an Italian study reported distinct immunoblot patterns, with a 16 kDa band predominantly associated with gastrointestinal symptoms, a 25 kDa band with cutaneous manifestations, and a 40 kDa band with anaphylaxis, suggesting that component–phenotype associations may vary across populations.27 Similarly, a study in Japanese children with suspected buckwheat allergy showed that measurement of sIgE antibodies to Fag e 3 improved diagnostic accuracy.28 Other investigations have highlighted the potential role of combined sensitization patterns. One study demonstrated that concomitant sensitization to legumin, Fag e 2, and Fag e 5 was predictive of buckwheat allergy, whereas Fag e 4 appeared to be particularly relevant for latex cross-reactivity.16 In addition, Chinese researchers identified 2 non-specific lipid transfer proteins (nsLTPs), FtLTP1a and FtLTP1b, from Tartary buckwheat, with FtLTP1b showing cross-reactivity with Artemisia pollen.29 Given the high prevalence of Artemisia pollen allergy in northern China,30 the potential cross-reactivity between buckwheat and Artemisia pollen warrants further investigation.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center retrospective investigation with a relatively small sample size. As all participants were enrolled from a tertiary referral hospital, potential selection bias may exist, because patients admitted to tertiary centers typically present with more severe and complex conditions, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to primary care populations or mild cases. Second, the small cohort size reduced the statistical power for subgroup analyses, and relevant subgroup results should be interpreted with caution. Third, multiple comparisons may increase the risk of type I error. Considering the exploratory nature of this study and the limited sample size, we did not perform multiple testing correction. Findings require further validation in larger cohorts. Forth, immunoblotting provides only semi-quantitative information on IgE binding and does not allow precise quantification or functional assessment of individual allergenic proteins. To date, direct evidence confirming the existence of the 22–24 kDa protein in buckwheat husk extracts remains lacking. Further research will be performed in the future to verify this point and provide more supporting data.

In conclusion, this study provides the first systematic summary of the clinical characteristics and IgE-binding protein profiles of patients with buckwheat allergy in China. Sensitization to the 22–24 kDa protein was associated with higher buckwheat-sIgE levels and an increased risk of concomitant buckwheat husk allergy, highlighting its potential clinical relevance. These findings underscore the heterogeneity of buckwheat allergy and suggest that component-level analysis may contribute to improved phenotypic characterization and risk assessment in affected patients.
