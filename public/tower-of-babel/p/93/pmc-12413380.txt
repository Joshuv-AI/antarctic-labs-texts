Efficacy and Safety of 0.01% Atropine Eye Drops and Novel Lenslet-ARray-Integrated Spectacle Lenses for the Prevention of Myopia Progression Among Children with Premyopia: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Mingjun Gao, Yanhua Hou, Yutong Lu, Zhanhua Shi, Qi Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPrevious studies have suggested that premyopia shows high prevalence and high risk of progression to myopia among children. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the efficacy and safety of 0.01% atropine eye drops and novel positive Lenslet-ARray-Integrated spectacle lenses (LARI) in slowing myopia onset and myopic shift among children with premyopia in China.MethodsThis was a prospective randomized controlled trial. A total of 400 children aged 6–12 years with premyopia in both eyes were enrolled. Subjects were randomly divided into four groups in equal proportions: 0.01% atropine eye drops group (group A), LARI group (group S), combination group (group AS), and control group without any clinical intervention (group E). Measurements were conducted at baseline and at 3-, 6-, and 12-month follow-up. The incidence of myopia and the percentage of patients with fast myopic shift over 1 year were the primary outcomes. The secondary outcomes were changes in cycloplegic spherical equivalent refraction (SE) and axial length (AL). Changes in uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA), corneal curvature (CC), subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT), and intraocular pressure (IOP) were the exploratory outcomes. Adverse events were also recorded.ResultsThe incidence of myopia and the percentage of patients with fast myopic shift in groups A, S, and AS were significantly lower than those in group E. Treatment with 0.01% atropine, LARI, and their combination decreased myopia progression by 55.88%, 64.71%, and 77.94%, and axial elongation by 65.00%, 75.00%, and 77.50%, respectively. Further analyses were conducted based on age stratification. SFCT in intervention groups increased significantly. No serious adverse events were detected.ConclusionsThese 1-year results suggest that, combined with the progression of SE, the reduction in AL, and the increase in SFCT, 0.01% atropine and LARI can be used as effective and safe treatments for the prevention of myopia progression in children with premyopia. Combination therapy showed superior efficacy in this cohort, but long-term effects beyond 12 months remain unknown.Trial Registration: ChiCTR2500098887.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-025-01214-y.Keywords: Premyopia, 0.01% atropine, Optical approaches, Myopia progression, Choroidal thickness

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Currently, premyopia shows high prevalence and risk of progression to myopia. Therefore, early prevention of premyopia in young children and reducing the risk of myopia-related complications appear to be crucial.Randomized clinical trials are thus warranted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of different clinical interventions for the prevention of myopia progression in premyopic children.What was learned from the study?Combined with the progression of cycloplegic spherical equivalent refraction (SE), the reduction in axial length (AL), and the increase in subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT), 0.01% atropine and novel positive Lenslet-ARray-Integrated spectacle lenses (LARI) can be used as effective and safe treatment for the prevention of myopia progression in children with premyopia throughout 12 months.Combination therapy showed superior efficacy in this cohort, but long-term effects beyond 12 months remain unknown.The findings provide supporting evidence for early intervention in the clinical management of premyopic children.

Introduction

Myopia is a prevalent refractive error affecting an increasing proportion of the global population. High myopia increases the risk of vision impairment caused by glaucoma, cataracts, and retinal detachment [1]. By 2050, it is expected that half of the world's population will be affected by myopia, and nearly 1 billion people will have high myopia, making myopia the leading cause of vision loss worldwide [2, 3]. Over the past few decades, several studies have been conducted to prevent and control this condition. The earlier myopia occurs, the faster it is likely to progress and, ultimately, the higher the degree of myopia in adults [4, 5]. Therefore, early prevention of myopia in young children and reduction of the risk of myopia-related complications are crucial.

For the prevention of myopia, International Myopia Institute (IMI) White Papers published in 2019 defined premyopia as “a refractive state of ≤ +0.75 D and > −0.50 D.” Children with premyopia, when combined with quantifiable risk factors, demonstrate an increased likelihood of future myopia development, warranting preventive interventions [6]. Studies have found that the refraction and axial length of the eye change rapidly in children with premyopia, but the speed of change gradually slows after myopia has developed [7, 8]. Premyopia represents the optimal stage for early myopia control, enabling high-risk identification and reduction of both incidence and ultimate severity [9, 10].

Various control interventions can be performed in premyopic children, including environmental, pharmacological, and optical approaches [11–13]. Clinical studies have found that increased outdoor time is an effective preventive measure for children with high hyperfarsightedness reserves. However it has a limited effect on slowing the progression of myopia among those who are already affected [14]. Consequently, behavioral interventions show constrained effectiveness in premyopic children, warranting further clinical intervention.

Currently, low-concentration atropine eye drops play an important role in the pharmacological treatment of myopia. The ATOM2 clinical trial demonstrated comparable efficacy and safety profiles across varying atropine concentrations, with 0.01% atropine being the optimal concentration for clinical use [15, 16]. Its effectiveness and safety in children with premyopia require further studies [17–19]. Optical approaches such as orthokeratology, multifocal myopia control contact lenses, and peripheral defocus spectacles are commonly used to control myopia [20–23]. However, the first two modalities are not applicable to premyopia. The peripheral defocus spectacles employed in this study are positive lenslet-array-integrated spectacle lenses (LARI), and the lens degree was from +1.00 to −12.00 D, which made the optical intervention in premyopia possible [24]. In addition, previous studies have reported the efficacy of optical intervention combined with 0.01% atropine in controlling myopia [25, 26]. Further research should evaluate the clinical necessity of combination therapies for premyopic children and their potential synergistic effects.

Recent studies have also evaluated the efficacy of low-level red-light therapy (LLRL) in premyopic children [27–29]. However, the safety of LLRL remains incompletely characterized, necessitating a comprehensive investigation. In response to the small number of patients with retinal photoreceptor damage associated with LLRL, the National Medical Products Administration of China implemented enhanced regulatory measures governing the clinical application of LLRL devices in July 2024. Consequently, this study excluded LLRL.

Therefore, we conducted this clinical trial to investigate the efficacy and safety of 0.01% atropine, LARI, and their combination in slowing the progression of myopia in premyopic children. In light of the escalating severe global myopia, it is hoped that this study can provide feasible evidence for early intervention in clinical practice.

Methods

This 12-month prospective randomized controlled trial was conducted at the Second Hospital of Dalian Medical University between November 2023 and February 2025. The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board of The Second Hospital of Dalian Medical University (KY2024-206-01). The trial was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (http://www.chictr.org.cn/) with registration number ChiCTR2500098887, due to unavoidable post-pandemic institutional review backlog and administrative complexities beyond the investigators’ control. Despite retrospective registration, the pre-specified statistical analysis plan was adhered to, and there were no changes in the protocol or methods after trial commencement. All outcomes registered match those reported. The trial original protocol is available in the Supplementary Material. For subjects under 8 years of age, we obtained informed written consent from their legal guardians. For subjects aged ≥ 8 years, we obtained the informed consent of the subjects themselves and their legal guardians.

Sample Size

The sample size was calculated based on previous research [12, 17, 19]. We assumed that clinical interventions reduced myopia shift by at least −0.36 D (δ = 0.36), with a standard deviation of 0.70 D (σ = 0.70), assuming a power of 90% (β = 0.10) with a two-sided test of 5% (α = 0.05). Eligibility Criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 6–12 years; (2) cycloplegic spherical equivalent refraction ≤ +0.75 D and > −0.50 D in both eyes; (3) astigmatism < 1.00 D; (4) uncorrected visual acuity 0.20 logMAR or better in both eyes; (5) intraocular pressures ≤ 21 mmHg; and (6) cycloplegic spherical equivalent refraction of at least one parent ≥ −3.00 D. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) anisometropia ≥ 1.00 D in both eyes; (2) other eye conditions (strabismus, amblyopia, cloudy refractive media, severe inflammation, or eye tumors); (3) previous or current treatment for controlling myopia progression; (4) allergy to atropine; or (5) inability to complete the research visit plan.

Randomization and Interventions

A total of 400 children with premyopia were included and randomly divided into four groups at a ratio of 1:1:1:1 using a spreadsheet generator (Excel; Microsoft). Owing to the nature of the intervention, both the participants and their parents were aware of the study grouping.

In addition to routine clinical knowledge education on the prevention of myopia (including no less than 10 h of outdoor activities per week) in the four groups, group A received single-dose packaged 0.01% atropine eye drops daily, one drop at a time before bed (Sinqi Pharmaceutical, Shenyang, China; approved by the National Medicine H20240320). Group S was given positive Lenslet-ARray-Integrated spectacle lenses, which were worn for 12 h per day. All the lenses were prescribed with 0.00 diopters (manufacturer: Vision Eye Care Technology, Guangdong, China; registration number: 68736330). Both treatments were administered in the combination group (group AS). No clinical intervention was administered to the control group (group E).

Measurements were conducted at baseline and at 3-, 6-, and 12-month follow-up. During follow-up, children were asked to wear single-vision spectacles if their uncorrected visual acuity (logMAR) in both eyes was higher than 0.2 in groups A and E. Instead, the prescription was updated in groups S and AS.

Outcomes

All inspections were performed by designated operators in accordance with standardized operating procedures. Personnel were blinded to randomization records. The primary outcomes included the incidence of myopia (defined as a cycloplegic spherical equivalent of at least −0.50 D) and the percentage of participants with fast myopic shift (defined as a spherical equivalent myopic shift of at least 0.50 D) over 1 year. The secondary outcomes were changes in the spherical equivalent refraction (SE) and axial length (AL). Changes in uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA), corneal curvature (CC), and subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT) were the exploratory outcomes. The intraocular pressure (IOP) and adverse events were recorded to assess the safety of 0.01% atropine eye drops. The safety of the LARI was measured by collecting the wearing experience.

SFCT was performed using the Zeiss Cirrus HD-OCT [high-definition optical coherence tomography] (Carl Zeiss, Germany). The SFCT was measured from the Bruch’s membrane to the chorio–scleral interface perpendicularly in the center of the fovea [30]. All OCT images were obtained at similar time points to minimize the impact of daytime changes on choroidal thickness.

The comfort and frequency of visual symptoms and the quality of vision after wearing the LARI were measured through questionnaires. The comfort and frequency of visual symptoms were scored from 1 to 10 (Table 1). The visual quality scores with different visual distances ranged from 1 to 10 points (Table 2).

Statistical Analysis

SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corporation), R 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), and GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software) were used for data processing and visualization. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to evaluate the normality of the continuous variables, which are represented by (x¯ ± s). One-way analysis of variance or independent-samples t-tests were used for intergroup comparisons. Non-normally distributed variables were expressed as medians (quartiles) and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis H test, while (n/%) was used to represent count data, and the χ2 test was used to compare the groups. At the time of follow-up, a general linear model was employed to analyze the intergroup and intragroup changes in the evaluation indicators in each group. All analyzed data were obtained for the right eye. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Myopia Incidence and Percentage with Fast Myopic Shift

At 6 months, the incidence of myopia in groups A, S, AS, and E was 3.61%, 1.25%, 1.23%, and 24.44%, respectively. At 12 months, it was 13.35%, 10.00%, 2.47%, and 60.00% (Fig. 2). The proportions of fast myopic shift over 1 year in the four groups were 37.35%, 25.00%, 11.11%, and 78.89% (Fig. 3) (all P < 0.05). The incidence of myopia and the proportions of fast myopic shift in the intervention groups were significantly lower than those in the control group. LARI alone reduced the incidence of myopia more than atropine alone. Combination therapy showed superior efficacy.

Changes in SE and AL

At 1 year, the mean myopic shifts in SE were −0.30 (95% CI −0.11 to −0.49) D in group A, −0.24 (95% CI −0.05 to −0.43) D in group S, −0.15 (95% CI −0.01 to −0.29) D in group AS, and −0.68 (95% CI −0.43 to −0.93) D in group E (P < 0.001; Table 4, Fig. 4). Further analyses were conducted based on age stratification. For the 6 to < 8-year-old subgroup, the combined group was significantly better than groups A and S in controlling the progress of SE. For the 8 to < 10-year-old subgroup, Groups S and AS were superior to those treated with 0.01% atropine alone. For the 10 to 12-year-old subgroup, groups A and S and the combination group exhibited significantly better outcomes than group E, with no significant differences among the three groups.

Changes in Exploratory Outcomes

UCVA (logMAR) in group E was significantly higher than that in the other three groups after 6 months (F = 5.88, P < 0.001) and 12 months (F = 25.76, P < 0.001). After 3 months (F = 3.49, P < 0 0.001), 6 months (F = 12.09, P < 0 0.001), and 12 months (F = 22.31, P < 0.001), SFCT of the three intervention groups was significantly higher than that of the E group. The SFCT increased from the baseline level in groups A, S, and AS, and decreased from the baseline level in group E. In addition, there were no significant differences among the four groups in terms of CC (Table 4).

Safety Evaluation

Intraocular pressure was within the normal range in all four groups during the study period. The average scores of the subjects for daily wearing and exercise gradually increased with an increase in wearing time. After 6 months, both wearing experience scores were ≥ 8 points. The average scores for the clarity of vision for distance, intermediate distances, and near tasks performed well from the baseline (Table 5). No serious adverse events were observed in this study.

Discussion

In this study, we found that once-nightly 0.01% atropine, LARI, and their combination significantly reduced the incidence of myopia by 77.75%, 83.33%, and 95.88%, with relative reduction of 55.88%, 64.71%, and 77.94% in myopia progression. To our knowledge, this is the first randomized clinical trial to demonstrate the efficacy of multiple interventions in premyopic children.

Low-concentration atropine is one of the interventions that can be initiated in high-risk non-myopic children to delay myopia onset [17, 31, 32]. Our study showed that 0.01% atropine could effectively and safely delay the development of myopia, reducing the progression of myopia by 0.38 ± 0.04 D and axial elongation by 0.26 ± 0.01 mm. Recent randomized controlled trials have reported that peripheral defocus lenses may be as effective as atropine for myopia control [20, 33]. However, comparable studies for preventing myopia onset in premyopia are absent, which is a gap addressed by our research. Furthermore, we found that LARI was superior to atropine, reducing myopia progression by 0.45 ± 0.02 D and axial elongation by 0.31 ± 0.01 mm. This might be because optical intervention for controlling myopia is more natural than atropine, by normalizing the plane of the peripheral image shell nearer to the retina, rather than acting directly on tissue systems like pharmacological agents [34].

Combination therapy has demonstrated further reduction in axial elongation for myopia management [35, 36]. In our study, we observed analogous therapeutic effects among premyopic children. The atropine–LARI combination slowed refractive progression by 0.52 ± 0.02 D and axial elongation by 0.32 ± 0.01 mm. Younger children experience faster progression of myopia [37]. Our analysis further revealed the significantly superior efficacy of combined intervention versus monotherapy in premyopic children aged 6–8 years. For older cohorts (10–12 years), a single clinical intervention may be sufficient.

In 2023, IMI discussed the potential effect of the choroid on myopia and speculated that short-term choroidal thickening can be employed as a biomarker of myopia control therapy [38]. The greatest AL-associated choroidal thinning may occur at myopia onset. As a muscarinic receptor antagonist, atropine may partly control myopia by inhibiting serotonergic receptors [39]. Previous studies have measured the effect of local atropine on choroidal thickness, and most studies have reported choroidal thickening [40–43]. The LAMP study reported that atropine thickening of the choroid showed a dose-dependent effect, as did its effect on myopia progression. Some studies have found that atropine has a choroidal thickening effect at low doses [41, 43], but other studies have expressed different views [44, 45]. Our study showed that 0.01% atropine increased choroidal thickness in children with premyopia. The mean SFCT in group A increased by 3.36 μm at 3 months, 4.76 μm at 6 months, and 5.91 μm at 12 months. Furthermore, it was confirmed that defocus can change the length of the ocular axis and the thickness of the choroid, and the change in choroidal thickness after defocus may control the changes in the ocular axis [46], which is consistent with our findings. We also observed that LARI monotherapy increased SFCT significantly more than atropine alone (5.91 μm vs. 11.39 μm, P < 0.001), but combination therapy (13.49 μm) showed no added benefit over LARI alone. This also explains the different therapeutic effects of 0.01% atropine and LARI. Furthermore, SFCT in the intervention groups increased significantly at 3 months but demonstrated progressive deceleration over time, suggesting that SFCT may be used as a potential short-term biomarker for predicting treatment response in children with premyopia.

Limitations

First, the study population was homogeneous and all participants were Chinese, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other populations. Second, due to the significant differences in intervention measures, a double-blind design could not be adopted in this study, which might have led to experimenter bias and expectation bias. Third, this was a single-center study that lacked external validation from multiple centers. Fourth, the information on wearing experience and treatment compliance was collected through self-reports of children and their parents, which might have led to recall bias. Fifth, although we adhered to the IMI consensus definition of premyopia (spherical equivalent ≤ +0.75 D and > −0.50 D), emerging evidence indicates that age-specific refractive thresholds may better stratify myopia development risk [47]. The uniform threshold applied in this trial could have resulted in heterogeneous baseline risk profiles, particularly between younger (6–8 years) and older (11–12 years) participants. Nevertheless, our age-stratified analyses consistently demonstrated significant intervention effects in the treatment groups compared to the control group, supporting the robustness of our primary findings. Finally, our study was not long enough to measure the long-term effects of 0.01% atropine and LARI on myopia prevention. Therefore, the next stage was to conduct a prospective multicenter clinical study with a large sample size to provide evidence-based guidance for clinical treatment.

Conclusions

Among premyopic children aged 6–12 years, 0.01% atropine eye drops and novel lenslet-array-integrated spectacle lenses (LARI) significantly slowed the onset of myopia and fast myopic shift. The combination was significantly more effective than a single intervention in younger cohorts. No major adverse events were observed in terms of safety. Combined with the progression of SE, the decrease in AL, and the increase in SFCT, 0.01% atropine eye drops and LARI can be employed as effective and safe treatments to prevent the progression of myopia among children with premyopia.
