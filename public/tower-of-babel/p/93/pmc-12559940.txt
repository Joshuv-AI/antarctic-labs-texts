The Therapeutic Effect of High-definition 1-Hz Transcranial Alternating Current Stimulation in PTSD: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Boshra Hatef, Gila Pirzad Jahromi, Ali Khaleghi, Shokofeh Radfar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePost-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a serious mental health challenge that usually lacks effective responses to conventional therapies. High-definition transcranial alternating current stimulation (HD-tACS) is gaining attention as a potential therapeutic approach for neuropsychiatric disorders. This study aimed to investigate the effects of 1-Hz tACS on PTSD symptoms.MethodsA randomized controlled trial involving 40 PTSD patients was designed. Patients were randomly divided into sham and real stimulation groups and underwent 10 HD-tACS sessions. We used 1-Hz HD-tACS with the central anode positioned at T8 and the ring cathodes at P8, C4, F8 and EX10 (targeting right lateral temporal cortex). Clinical symptoms of patients were evaluated through the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5). Also, we measured salivary cortisol levels and serum brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels before and after treatment.ResultsThe real stimulation resulted in a more significant reduction in PTSD symptoms as measured by the mean PCL-5 score (56.25 ± 12.13 to 39.40 ± 13.91) compared to the sham stimulation (p < 0.05). The mean salivary cortisol level increased significantly in the real stimulation group (0.053 ± 0.04 μg/dl to 0.10 ± 0.07 μg/dl) compared to the sham (p < 0.001). In contrast, serum BDNF levels showed no significant changes in either group.ConclusionPreliminary findings suggest that HD-tACS in delta band can significantly alleviate PTSD symptoms, indicating its potential as a therapeutic intervention for PTSD.Keywords: Post-traumatic stress disorder, Transcranial alternating current stimulation, Temporal lobe, Cortisol, Brain-derived neurotrophic factor

INTRODUCTION

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a multifaceted psychiatric disorder that can be caused by experiencing traumatic events, including warfare, natural catastrophes, sexual violence, or other perilous events [1]. It is marked by a range of symptoms, such as intrusive recollections, heightened arousal, avoidance of trauma-related triggers, and adverse changes in mood and thinking [2]. Approximately 3.9% of the world’s population has experienced PTSD at some point in their lives [3]. PTSD prevalence can vary significantly based on factors like trauma type, exposure levels, and socioeconomic status [4]. Studies indicate a prevalence of around 20% for PTSD during the COVID-19 pandemic [5]. PTSD can lead to a range of mental health conditions, including depression, anxiety disorders, and substance use disorders. It can affect social relationships, leading to isolation, irritability, and difficulty forming and maintaining connections. Individuals with PTSD often face increased medical costs and reduced productivity, contributing to a significant economic burden [6]. PTSD has a profound effect on the quality of life and daily functioning of those affected, while also placing a substantial strain on healthcare systems globally [7,8].

The current standard treatments for PTSD include pharmacotherapy, primarily with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and cognitive-behavioral therapies (CBTs) [9]. Although these interventions have been effective in reducing PTSD symptoms, a significant number of patients have failed to respond adequately or experience residual symptoms despite treatment [10,11]. This necessitates the exploration of alternative and adjunctive therapies to improve clinical outcomes for individuals with PTSD. One promising approach is transcranial electrical stimulation (TES) as a non-invasive neuromodulation method that uses a mild electrical current for brain stimulation and cortical excitability modification [12,13]. TES has been investigated for various neuropsychiatric conditions, including autism, depression, substance use disorder, and chronic pain, with emerging evidence supporting its efficacy [14-18]. Given that PTSD is characterized by alterations in brain areas crucial for emotion regulation, memory processing, and fear response—most notably the prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and amygdala—TES techniques can serve as a therapeutic tool to help restore functionality in these regions [19,20]. A recent systematic review showed that tDCS techniques can effectively alleviate PTSD symptoms [21]. However, the small number of trials conducted has made it challenging to draw definitive conclusions in this area.

PTSD is characterized by distinct neurocircuitry abnormalities centered on fear processing and emotion regulation networks. The amygdala shows hyperactivity to threat cues, driving exaggerated fear responses, while hypoactive ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) fails to sufficiently inhibit this response [22]. The hippocampus, crucial for contextualizing memories, often exhibits reduced volume and impaired function, contributing to intrusive recollections [23]. Notably, the right lateral temporal cortex (LTC)—our stimulation target—plays a key role in integrating sensory components of traumatic memories and shows altered connectivity in PTSD patients [24]. This network dysfunction leads to hallmark symptoms: amygdala hyperactivity perpetuates hypervigilance, vmPFC deficits impair fear extinction, and hippocampal/LTC abnormalities foster intrusive memories [25]. Current treatments aim to normalize these circuits, but many patients remain symptomatic, creating a need for targeted neuromodulation approaches.

High-definition TES (HD-TES) is an advanced neurostimulation technique that involves delivering electrical currents to the scalp using a high-density electrode array. This method allows for a more precise targeting of specific brain regions compared to traditional TES, which typically employs fewer electrodes. The primary advantages of HD-TES include improved spatial resolution, enabling researchers and clinicians to selectively modulate localized areas of the brain, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of the intervention. Additionally, HD-TES can reduce unwanted stimulation of surrounding tissues, minimizing side effects and providing a more comfortable experience for the patient. Poydasheva et al. [26] highlighted the greater stimulation accuracy of HD-tDCS compared to standard tDCS, indicating its potential for more targeted therapeutic effects. Among different HD-TES techniques, high-definition transcranial alternating current stimulation (HD-tACS) is an advanced technique that uses specialized electrodes to deliver more focal and targeted stimulation, potentially enhancing its therapeutic impacts with minimal adverse effects [27]. In addition to its focal nature, the integration of specific brain wave frequencies during stimulation, such as delta waves, has gained attention due to their association with restorative and regulatory processes in the brain. Delta waves have a significant impact on neural synchronization, emotional processing, and autonomic regulation, making them particularly relevant for addressing the dysregulation observed in PTSD [28,29]. There are promising studies in support of using brain stimulation techniques in delta band in neuropsychiatric diseases [30]. A recent study on schizophrenia showed that delta HD-tACS is an effective therapeutic modality than HD-tDCS to mitigate symptoms in patients [31]. In a study of patients with Alzheimer’s disease, delta band tACS significantly improved cognitive function [32].

Despite advances, the use of HD-tACS in PTSD treatment remains underexplored. The goal of the current study is to fill this gap by finding the effects of HD-tACS on clinical symptoms, salivary cortisol levels, and serum brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels in PTSD patients. Applying 1-Hz HD-tACS with a central anode positioned at T8 and ring cathodes located at P8, C4, F8, and EX10 is a promising approach for treating PTSD due to its targeted modulation of brain activity in the right LTC. The selection of a low-frequency stimulation of 1-Hz is hypothesized to entrain neural oscillations associated with emotional regulation and memory processing, which are often disrupted in individuals with PTSD. The right LTC plays a crucial role in processing emotional memories and integrating sensory information [33], making it a key area for intervention. By positioning the anode over T8, we can enhance excitability in this region, potentially facilitating a normalization of altered neural circuits linked to traumatic memories. The ring cathodes extend the stimulation field, allowing for a broader influence on adjacent areas involved in emotional processing and regulation. This targeted stimulation approach, grounded in neuromodulation principles, aims to alleviate PTSD symptoms by promoting adaptive neural responses, enhancing emotional resilience, and fostering neuroplasticity, ultimately leading to improved clinical outcomes for affected individuals.

METHODS

Participants

The study includes 40 patients diagnosed with PTSD based on the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) criteria from Jannat Psychiatric Hospital and Mehregan Rehabilitation Center in Shiraz, Iran, and Baqiyatallah Hospital in Tehran, Iran. The general inclusion criteria consist of adults aged 18 to 65 years who are diagnosed with PTSD based on the PCL-5, have had stable clinical symptoms over the past 4 to 6 weeks without significant changes in medication for at least 4 weeks prior to the trial, have intact scalp skin without wounds, infections, or lesions, and possess the capacity to provide informed consent. The general exclusion criteria consist of having neurological or psychiatric disorders unrelated to PTSD, having metallic implants, a history of epilepsy or high-risk seizures, having pacemakers or other implanted devices, a history of substance abuse, pregnancy, breastfeeding, and skin sensitivity.

The sample size was determined using Cohen’s table for statistical power analysis, ensuring adequate power (95%) considering medium effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 0.4) at a significance level of p < 0.05. We obtained informed consent from all the patients. The project aligns with ethical principles and adhere to national regulations and standards for conducting medical research in Iran with registration number IR.BMSU.BAQ.REC.1403.068, registered 29 June 2024. We registered the trial protocol with www.irct.behdasht.gov.ir under protocol registration number IRCT20171128037666N4, registered 11 August 2024.

Study Design

The study is a RCT whose participants were divided randomly into the real or the sham stimulation group with a 1:1 ratio. We used a computer-generated randomization sequence to ensure allocation concealment. The sequence was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque envelopes opened only after baseline assessments. Research assistants enrolling participants had no access to the randomization sequence until after written consent was obtained. The individuals who took part in the study engaged in a total of 10 sessions. To determine eligibility, inclusion and exclusion criteria were evaluated by the study coordinator. During this first session, participants provided their consent and filled out various questionnaires and experiments (demographic and outcome measures). After confirming their eligibility, they came back for five consecutive treatment days per week for two weeks. All baseline evaluations were conducted on the first session. During the initial visit, participants had their clinical symptoms evaluated as part of a baseline assessment, which was succeeded by either an active or sham HD-tACS sessions. We obtained the patients’ basic demographic information, including age, sex, educational attainment, marital status, employment status, economic level, internet usage, medication and Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI) score upon enrollment into the study. PSQI has shown acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.70–0.85) and variable test-retest reliability across components (r = 0.30−0.84). Its global score correlates with other sleep measures, supporting its validity [34]. At the end of the treatment period (i.e., 10 sessions of active or sham intervention), a post-evaluation was conducted. The participants and assessors were kept unaware of the group assignments. The blinding was verified by prompting participants to make guesses about their group assignments following the intervention.

High-definition Transcranial Alternating Current Stimulation

The HD-tACS protocol involved applying alternating current using a Liv V L550 HD-TES device (Liv Company) with 5 × 7 cm electrodes. The central anode was positioned at the T8 to maximize current delivery to the LTC, while ring cathodes (P8, C4, F8, EX10) were arranged to constrain stimulation within the target region (based on 10-20 standard). This montage targeted the right LTC without affecting other brain regions. The stimulation frequency was set to 1 Hz in the delta band to influence cortical plasticity and normalize disrupted neural rhythms associated with PTSD, particularly within the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and sleep-related pathways [35]. Indeed, this configuration leveraged the LTC’s connectivity with the amygdala and hippocampus to modulate fear memory networks, while delta-frequency (1-Hz) stimulation was chosen to engage slow-wave oscillations associated with memory reconsolidation. The stimulation was carried out in a calm office setting. Before each session, a trained researcher thoroughly examined the patients’ head for any signs of skin injury or abnormalities, none of which were found throughout the study. The researcher then measured and marked the patients’ heads to ensure precise electrode placement. During the procedure, patients were instructed to sit or recline comfortably and remain at ease. The researcher was present in the room to monitor the process but refrained from speaking unless the patient had inquiries. The researcher calibrated the stimulation intensity to 2 mA and delivered for 20 minutes per session. Afterward, he removed the electrodes, and inspected the electrode areas for any lesions. Patients underwent a series of 10 sessions consisting of the first five days of a week over two running weeks. In the sham group, the researcher administered an initial brief stimulation, gradually diminishing to 0 mA within 30 seconds, creating a similar sensory experience to the active stimulation without any therapeutic effect.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome measure was identified as the variation in PTSD symptoms assessed by the PCL-5, comparing baseline scores to those obtained at the end of 10 treatment sessions. The PCL-5 is a 20-item questionnaire to measure the severity of PTSD symptoms that has demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.94) and strong test-retest reliability (r = 0.82). Its convergent validity is supported by correlations with related measures (r = 0.74−0.85) [36].

The secondary outcome measures were salivary cortisol level and serum BDNF. The neurobiological basis of PTSD involves disruption of the HPA axis and changes in neuroplasticity. HPA axis is key in the body’s response to stress, with cortisol being a primary biomarker of HPA axis activity [37]. Cortisol, a crucial glucocorticoid hormone, is central to the stress response by influencing the HPA axis and supports energy mobilization, regulates immune activity, and helps maintain balance during both acute and chronic stress [38]. Dysregulated cortisol levels, often observed in PTSD patients, are associated with increased symptom severity and impaired recovery; therefore, interventions that normalize cortisol levels may have therapeutic potential in PTSD [39]. Moreover, BDNF is a key neurotrophin for synaptic modification, neuronal survival, and neurogenesis [40]. Reduced serum BDNF levels correlate with the pathophysiology of various neuropsychiatric disorders, including PTSD. Enhancing BDNF expression through therapeutic interventions could, therefore, support neuroplasticity and recovery in PTSD patients [41]. Salivary cortisol levels were measured using the DiaMetra (DKO020) ELISA kit. Patients collected early morning (8 AM) saliva samples at baseline (pre-treatment) and a day after the final HD-tACS session (post-treatment). They were prevented from eating, drinking, and brushing their teeth for at least an hour before sample collection to avoid contamination. Samples were collected using Salivettes and kept at −20°C till analysis time. Furthermore, serum BDNF levels were measured using the Cusabio (CSB-E04501h) ELISA kit. A trained phlebotomist drew blood samples at baseline and post-treatment. Samples were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 minutes to separate serum, then aliquoted and stored at −80°C until analysis. The Cusabio ELISA protocol was strictly followed to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Safety and Side Effect

Adverse effects were evaluated following each stimulation session including headache, itching, tingling, burning sensation, sleepiness, worsening of mood and so on. Moreover, the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) was conducted at pre- and post-assessment to evaluate potential cognitive changes in patients.

Statistics Analysis

We used SPSS software version 27.0 (IBM Corp.) for statistical analyses. The mean and standard deviation were used for summarizing continuous variables which were compared by cross tab and chi-square test. Frequency and percentage were used to present categorical variables. We undertook a rigorous statistical approach to evaluate the data. Normality of distributions for primary, secondary, and change-related variables was confirmed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Baseline differences between groups were assessed using independent t-tests for continuous variables (e.g., age, PCL-5, cortisol, BDNF) and chi-square tests for categorical variables (e.g., sex, medication status). Spearman’s rank correlation was utilized to assess the relationship between behavioral changes and biological outcomes. Bartlett’s test was utilized to evaluate the equality of variances. Primary and secondary outcomes were analyzed using linear mixed models (LMMs) to account for repeated measures, with fixed effects for group (active/sham), time (pre/post-treatment), and their interaction, while adjusting for baseline values as covariates. A random intercept for participant ID addressed within-subject dependencies. To confirm robustness, we conducted ANCOVAs on post-treatment scores with baseline values as covariates, which yielded consistent results with the LMM approach. For significant interactions, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni correction. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d for between-group differences and partial η2 for LMM effects. We utilized Kenward–Roger approximations to determine p values and performed F-tests for each factor and their interactions within the mixed model. No missing data occurred in primary and secondary outcomes due to complete follow-up. The effectiveness of the blinding concerning treatment assignment was evaluated using chi-square tests of independence. p < 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Demographic Characteristics

A total of 40 patients were enrolled randomly, with an equal allocation to the sham and real stimulation groups (20 patients per group, Fig. 1). Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the real and sham HD-tACS groups. The mean age observed in the sham group (50.95 ± 13.37 years) and the real group (52.30 ± 10.35 years) were not significantly different (p = 0.723). Among the patients, 23 (57.5%) were male, distributed as 12 in the sham group and 11 in the real group. The sex distribution was not significantly different between groups (p = 0.834). Marital status analysis indicated that 32 patients (80%) were married, with a higher proportion in the real stimulation group (85%) in comparison to the sham group (75%) without any significant difference (p = 0.621). Employment status was similar between the groups, with 21 patients (52.5%) employed (p > 0.05). Socioeconomic assessments revealed that 20 patients (50%) reported a medium income level, while 16 (40%) reported a low one. Educational attainment was distributed evenly, with 11 patients (27.5%) holding undergraduate degrees (p > 0.05). Daily internet usage patterns were consistent across groups, with 31 patients (77.5%) less than 19 hours per week (p > 0.05). Notably, 70% (14/20) of the sham group and 50% (10/20) of the real group were receiving routine pharmacological treatment for PTSD at the time of the study without any significant differences among groups (p = 0.414). The mean PSQI score for all patients was 9.63 ± 2.94 at baseline, with minimal variation between groups (p > 0.10). These demographic characteristics underscore the balanced distribution of key variables between the sham and real stimulation groups, ensuring comparability and minimizing potential confounders.

Primary Outcome (PTSD Checklist for DSM-5)

The mean baseline PCL-5 score for all participants was 58.8 ± 6.45. Post-treatment, this score decreased significantly to a mean of 43.75 ± 5.98 with p < 0.01. In the sham group, the mean PCL-5 score decreased from 60.7 ± 6.12 to 48.1 ± 6.24, representing a significant improvement (t = 6.44, p < 0.01). Also, the real stimulation group demonstrated a significant reduction in PCL-5 score after intervention with mean scores decreasing from 56.25 ± 6.54 to 39.4 ± 5.72 (t = 9.10, p < 0.01) (Fig. 2). Moreover, there was a significant difference in these changes in terms of condition (F124.56 = 12.82, p < 0.01), assessment times (F131.27 = 14.23, p < 0.01) and their interaction (F144.63 = 10.91, p = 0.022). Post-hoc comparison revealed significant group differences, with a greater reduction observed in the real stimulation group compared to the sham group (t = 2.80, p < 0.01, Cohen’s d = 1.72). While sham responses are common in neuromodulation trials, the active group’s improvement (ΔPCL-5=16.85) not only exceeded the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) threshold (5−10 points) but showed a 34% greater reduction than sham. This suggests HD-tACS offers clinically meaningful augmentation beyond nonspecific effects.

Secondary Outcomes (Salivary Cortisol and Serum Brain-derived Neurotrophic Factor Levels)

Salivary cortisol levels exhibited an overall significant increase from 0.10 ± 0.02 μg/dl at baseline to 0.13 ± 0.03 μg/dl post-treatment (t = 5.26, p < 0.001). The sham group experienced a modest non-significant rise from 0.155 ± 0.02 μg/dl to 0.167 ± 0.03 μg/dl (t = 1.48, p = 0.14), while the real stimulation group exhibited a significant increase from 0.05 ± 0.01 μg/dl to 0.10 ± 0.02 μg/dl (t = 10.00, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). Moreover, there was a significant difference in these changes in terms of condition (F129.68 = 19.73, p < 0.001), assessment times (F144.20 = 18.29, p < 0.001) and their interaction (F115.93 = 18.64, p < 0.001). Post-hoc comparison revealed significant group differences, with a greater increase observed in the active stimulation group compared to the sham group (t = 3.46, p < 0.01, Cohen’s d = 1.17).

Serum BDNF levels demonstrated minimal overall change (p = 0.621). It increased slightly from 14.35 ± 1.34 ng/ml to 14.65 ± 1.41 ng/ml in the sham group without any significance (t = 0.69, p = 0.494). Also, serum BDNF levels decreased marginally from 16.3 ± 1.52 ng/ml to 16.11 ± 1.49 ng/ml in the real group with no significance (t = 0.39, p = 0.692) (Fig. 4). Moreover, there was no significant difference in these changes in terms of condition (F154.61 = 0.31, p = 0.782), assessment times (F181.52 = 0.45, p = 0.711) and their interaction (F174.37 = 0.38, p = 0.651). While baseline serum BDNF levels appeared higher in the real stimulation group (16.3 ± 1.52 ng/ml) compared to the sham group (14.35 ± 1.34 ng/ml), this difference was not statistically significant (t = 1.92, p = 0.062, Cohen’s d = 0.61). To account for potential confounding, an ANCOVA adjusting for baseline BDNF levels was performed, confirming no significant group differences in post-treatment BDNF changes (F37 = 0.22, p = 0.643).

Association between Behavioral and Biological Outcomes

To evaluate the relationship between changes in PCL-5 scores and changes in salivary cortisol levels, Spearman’s rank correlation was performed. There was no significant linear correlation between changes in PCL-5 scores and salivary cortisol levels (r = −0.20, p > 0.05). These findings indicate that changes in PTSD symptoms measured by PCL-5 scores were not linearly associated with changes in salivary cortisol levels.

Blinding and Safety

Participants provided self-report feedback indicating their perceptions of whether they believed they received the actual stimulation, with options for responses being “Yes,” “No,” or “I’m unsure.” Based on responses, 90% of participants were unaware or unsure of the stimulation conditions. In addition, patients from each group indicated experiencing few side effects, and there were no significant differences noted among the groups, except for the occurrence of “flickering lights” (p = 0.006) (Table 2). Three adverse events were recorded during the research; however, after careful examination, it was concluded that neither event was linked to the study or the intervention. Additionally, all patients demonstrated a slight cognitive enhancement from baseline to end of treatment, as evaluated by the MMSE. There was no significant difference in the level of improvement associated with different conditions across sessions (F185.40 = 1.115, p = 0.381).

DISCUSSION

For the first time, this study highlights the efficacy of HD-tACS in delta band to reduce PTSD symptom severity, as evidenced by significant reductions in PCL-5 scores, particularly within the real stimulation group. While both active and sham stimulation groups demonstrated significant reductions in PCL-5 scores, the greater improvement observed in the active HD-tACS group (16.85-point reduction vs. 12.6-point reduction in sham) suggests a specific therapeutic effect beyond placebo. This difference exceeds the established 5-10 point MCID for PCL-5 [42], indicating that HD-tACS provided meaningful additional benefit. The treatment also elicited notable changes in salivary cortisol levels, suggesting potential modulation of stress-related neuroendocrine activity. However, HD-tACS in delta band had limited effects on serum BDNF levels, indicating that the observed clinical benefits may operate through mechanisms independent of neurotrophic factor modulation. In fact, the results of this study emphasize the capability of HD-tACS in delta band targeting LTC as a novel treatment for PTSD. Based on these findings, 1-Hz HD-tACS in our study was able to effectively modulate neural oscillatory activity and address the stress-related neural dysregulation observed in PTSD. Employing low-amplitude, spatially distributed exogenous extracellular electric fields with varying frequencies and intensities could impact the oscillatory dynamics of low-frequency spiking activity in the brain [43]. The positive effects of brain stimulation in the delta band have been mentioned in other studies. In a study focused on fibromyalgia, home-based tDCS targeting delta frequency bands reduced pain perception by altering connectivity between the right insula and left ACC. These changes correlated with improvements in emotional and cognitive processing [44].

In this trial, we selected a novel protocol targeting the right LTC based on its established role within the extended fear network and its strong association with PTSD symptoms, particularly re-experiencing and hyperarousal. While our HD-tACS montage primarily targeted the right LTC through the T8 anode with surrounding cathodes, current spread to adjacent regions (particularly C4 over parietal cortex and F8 near inferior frontal gyrus) may have contributed to therapeutic effects. The LTC was selected for its role in fear memory processing and sensory integration of traumatic stimuli, with modulation potentially reducing re-experiencing symptoms by disrupting maladaptive memory traces. However, concurrent stimulation of nearby regions involved in emotional regulation (via F8) and interoceptive awareness (via C4) might have synergistically improved hyperarousal and avoidance symptoms. Future studies should incorporate computational current modeling to quantify regional specificity and EEG to verify frequency-specific effects. Additionally, while we hypothesized that delta-band stimulation would promote memory reconsolidation, the precise mechanisms linking LTC modulation to specific symptom clusters (e.g., intrusions vs. negative cognition) remain theoretical. Targeted neuroimaging before and after stimulation could clarify these pathway-specific effects. Prior studies, including findings by [45], demonstrated hyperactivity and increased connectivity of the right LTC in PTSD patients. Furthermore, a study on 44 healthy individuals analyzed electroencephalographic recordings during and after the Trier social stress test, revealing a substantial increase in the relative delta band frequency during acute stress [46]. Therefore, we decided to employ HD-tACS in the delta band with the central anode positioned at T8, while the ring cathodes at P8, C4, F8 and EX10 modulating this region activity to alleviate PTSD symptoms. This protocol’s use in earlier studies further validated its potential efficacy, allowing us to explore its impact on the symptom improvement in PTSD [47].

This trial could offer significant insights into managing PTSD patients, addressing the need for safe and effective treatment options for individuals who show inadequate responses to traditional anti-anxiety medications, which can be seen in 30−50% of patients and is expected to improve daily functioning, often impaired in individuals with PTSD [48]. Our findings demonstrate significant reductions in PTSD symptoms like intrusions, avoidance, cognition change and hyperarousal measured by PCL-5 following 10 sessions of HD-tACS interventions.

The observed increase in salivary cortisol following active HD-tACS in our study contrasts with the hypocortisolism typically reported in PTSD. While baseline levels in our real stimulation group were notably low (0.053 μg/dl), resembling profiles of patients with enhanced glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity [49], the post-treatment rise (0.10 μg/dl) may reflect partial HPA axis normalization. A study conducted on 22 veterans with treatment-resistant PTSD provides preliminary evidence suggesting that cortisol levels measured before and after sessions could be used as a biomarker to predict responses to exposure-based treatments in PTSD. Patients who responded to treatment had higher average cortisol levels both before and following the sessions compared to non-responders [50]. Additionally, elevated cortisol levels before and after sessions were found to be significant indicators of greater reductions in PTSD symptoms [51]. Future studies should stratify patients by baseline cortisol to test whether this biomarker predicts HD-tACS response. This indicates that while both psychological and physiological responses to treatment were observed, they were not linearly linked. It is possible that the combination of other biomarkers of neuroplasticity and even considering wider range of PTSD symptoms specially the stress related responses rather than PCL-5 score may provide more explicit connection to the effects of HD-tACS in future studies.

Serum BDNF is crucial for stress responses, supporting neural plasticity, resilience, and adaptive coping mechanisms [52]. Our findings revealed no significant changes in BDNF levels following the intervention, consistent with previous research on patients with depression, which found no significant associations between BDNF levels and clinical or psychopathological variables related to depression [53]. Notably, baseline serum BDNF levels were moderately higher (though not statistically significant) in the real stimulation group compared to sham (d = 0.61), which may reflect random variability or subtle group imbalances despite randomization. While ANCOVA adjusting for baseline BDNF confirmed the lack of treatment effects, this imbalance underscores the importance of stratification by baseline biomarkers in future trials. The absence of BDNF modulation post-treatment aligns with prior studies showing delayed neurotrophic responses to neuromodulation [54,55], suggesting longer follow-ups may be needed to capture such effects.

Boundaries and Prospective Developments

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size (n = 40) may restrict the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Although a priori power analysis suggested adequate power (95%) to detect medium effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 0.4), the modest sample increases the risk of Type II errors (false negatives) and limits subgroup analyses (e.g., differential responses based on trauma type, comorbidities, or medication status). Replication in larger cohorts is essential to confirm the robustness of these preliminary results. Second, the absence of long-term follow-up due to budgetary constraints prevents conclusions about the durability of HD-tACS effects. PTSD often involves fluctuating symptoms, and while acute improvements were observed, it remains unclear whether these benefits persist beyond the immediate post-treatment period. Future studies should incorporate extended follow-ups (e.g., 3−6 months) to assess relapse rates and potential need for maintenance sessions. Third, the lack of a healthy control group or a PTSD group receiving standard interventions (e.g., CBT or SSRIs) limits comparative analyses. Including such controls would help differentiate HD-tACS-specific effects from placebo or natural symptom fluctuations. Additionally, while PCL-5 is a validated measure, combining it with clinician-administered scales (e.g., Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5) could strengthen diagnostic and outcome assessments. While there was no significant difference between groups in medication use, the specific regimens (types, dosages, duration) were not systematically controlled or analyzed. This heterogeneity in pharmacological treatments may have influenced outcomes, particularly given potential interactions between neuromodulation and psychotropic drugs. Finally, while salivary cortisol showed significant modulation, the lack of correlation with PCL-5 scores suggests that PTSD symptom reduction may involve mechanisms beyond HPA axis normalization. Future work should explore additional biomarkers (e.g., amygdala reactivity via functional magnetic resonance imaging, inflammatory markers) and consider more granular symptom clusters (e.g., dissociative vs. hyperarousal subtypes) to clarify therapeutic mechanisms. Additionally, optimizing stimulation parameters, such as intensity and electrode positioning, could improve efficacy [56]. Using a more diverse questionnaire than PCL-5 as a diagnostic and follow-up tool is recommended.

Although our study demonstrated significant changes in salivary cortisol and serum BDNF following HD-tACS, the interpretation of these biomarkers is constrained by their measurement at only two timepoints (pre- and post-intervention). Cortisol exhibits diurnal variability and acute stress reactivity, while BDNF fluctuates with factors like sleep, exercise, and metabolic state. A single morning measurement cannot capture dynamic trends—such as the cortisol awakening response or BDNF’s temporal kinetics—which may be critical for understanding PTSD-related dysregulation. Future studies should incorporate repeated sampling across multiple days and controlled conditions to distinguish sustained treatment effects from transient biological noise. Following serum BDNF for a longer period and also adding other neurobiological biomarkers can be beneficial. Portable, home-based TES devices, as explored in recent studies for PTSD and other neuropsychiatry disorders [57], represent another promising direction to improve accessibility and adherence.

Conclusion

This pilot randomized controlled trial provides preliminary evidence that 1-Hz HD-tACS targeting the right LTC may reduce PTSD symptoms and modulate stress-related physiology, though these findings require replication in larger, confirmatory studies. While the observed improvements in PCL-5 scores (with medium-to-large effect sizes) and normalization of cortisol levels are promising, several limitations, including the modest sample size (n = 40), warrant cautious interpretation. The absence of long-term follow-up and comprehensive biomarker sampling further underscores the need for more extensive investigation. Future research should prioritize: (I) multi-center trials with adequate power to detect clinically meaningful effects; (II) mechanistic studies incorporating neuroimaging to verify target engagement; and (III) optimization of stimulation parameters. These preliminary results nevertheless highlight HD-tACS as a potential therapeutic approach worthy of further exploration in PTSD treatment, particularly for patients with inadequate response to first-line therapies.
