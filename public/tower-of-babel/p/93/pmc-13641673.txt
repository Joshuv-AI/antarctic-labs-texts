Chronic viral hepatitis with concurrent metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease: dual etiology challenges
Authors: Shang-Chin Huang, Yi-Fen Shih, Chun-Jen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe overlapping epidemics of chronic viral hepatitis and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) present a complex challenge in modern hepatology. In chronic hepatitis B, a unique “steatosis paradox” emerges: while isolated hepatic steatosis provides a suppressive microenvironment that promotes hepatitis B viral clearance, the concomitant systemic cardiometabolic burden acts dose-dependently to drive fibrogenesis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Conversely, chronic hepatitis C and host metabolic dysfunctions exhibit synergistic destruction. Hepatitis C virus actively hijacks lipid metabolism and induces insulin resistance. Even after viral eradication via direct-acting antivirals, residual steatosis and glycemic abnormalities remain formidable drivers of HCC. Consequently, the traditional virocentric paradigm is no longer sufficient. Clinical management should pivot toward precision risk stratification and a multidisciplinary dual-target approach, equally prioritizing sustained viral suppression and aggressive metabolic remission. This review summarizes the divergent virus-MASLD interactions, optimal clinical strategies, current challenges and future opportunities.Keywords: Chronic hepatitis, Hepatitis B virus, Hepatitis C, Fatty liver, Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease

INTRODUCTION

Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and chronic hepatitis C (CHC) have historically been the leading causes of chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) globally [1,2]. However, as the implementation of widespread vaccination programs and highly effective antiviral therapies continues to alter the trajectory of viral hepatitis, the global landscape of hepatology is undergoing a profound paradigm shift [3,4]. Parallel to the global obesity epidemic and aging populations, the prevalence of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has surged dramatically [5,6]. Consequently, hepatologists are increasingly encountering patients harboring both chronic viral hepatitis and metabolic liver disease, bringing the clinical challenges of “dual etiology” liver disease to the forefront of modern hepatology [7,8].

The intersection of viral and metabolic etiologies within the liver does not result in a simple additive pathology; rather, it triggers a highly complex and dynamic web of virus-host interactions. In CHB, the concurrent presence of MASLD orchestrates a unique clinical paradox: while hepatic steatosis itself may suppress viral replication and promote favorable virological outcomes, the associated systemic cardiometabolic burden simultaneously drives fibrotic progression and hepatocarcinogenesis [9,10]. Conversely, in CHC, viral-induced lipid hijacking and host metabolic dysfunctions often synergize to exacerbate hepatic injury, presenting a sustained risk for HCC even after achieving a sustained virologic response (SVR) in the post-cure era [11,12]. Therefore, this review aims to comprehensively elucidate the epidemiological trends, intricate molecular mechanisms, and divergent clinical impacts underlying the viral-metabolic interplay. By unraveling these dual etiology challenges, we seek to provide mechanistic insights and translational perspectives to optimize risk stratification and guide tailored therapeutic strategies for this rapidly growing patient population in the modern era.

PREVALENCE OF MASLD IN CHRONIC VIRAL HEPATITIS

MASLD (formerly termed nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, NAFLD) has become one of the most common chronic liver diseases worldwide, with recent meta-analyses estimating a pooled global prevalence of approximately one-third of the adult population [13,14]. Within this context, concurrent MASLD is now frequently encountered in both CHB and CHC.

In CHB, hepatic steatosis affects roughly one third of patients. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 54 studies comprising 28,648 patients reported a pooled steatosis prevalence of 32.8% (95% confidence interval [CI] 28.9–37.0) [15], and a more recent global meta-analysis estimated a prevalence of 36% (95% CI 32–40) among individuals with hepatitis B virus (HBV) monoinfection; higher rates are consistently observed in those with cardiometabolic risk factors and greater body mass index [16].

Hepatic steatosis is even more frequent in CHC. The same global meta-analysis reported a steatosis prevalence of 49% (95% CI 47–51) among patients with hepatitis C virus (HCV) monoinfection, with a particularly strong predilection for genotype 3 infection.16 When the contemporary MASLD definition is applied, a nationwide Taiwanese registry of 5,840 patients with CHC found that 34.0% met MASLD criteria before treatment, a proportion that remained essentially unchanged (34.8%) after viral eradication [17].

PATHOGENESIS OF MASLD-RELATED AND VIRUS-RELATED HCC

Although MASLD and chronic viral hepatitis can each culminate in HCC, the underlying routes of hepatocarcinogenesis are fundamentally distinct. In CHB, the virus contributes directly to malignant transformation: integration of HBV DNA into the host genome drives insertional mutagenesis, chromosomal instability, and alteration of cancer-driver genes such as TERT, TP53, and MYC; consequently, HBV-related HCC may arise even in the absence of cirrhosis [18]. In CHC, hepatocarcinogenesis is comparatively indirect, reflecting decades of chronic necroinflammation, oxidative stress, and progression to cirrhosis [19]. On the other hand, HCC related to MASLD and its progressive form, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), is propelled by a chronically deranged metabolic milieu, including lipotoxicity, insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia, chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, which together foster a pro-tumorigenic hepatic niche [20,21]. MASH-related HCC is also characterized by impaired immune surveillance and the accumulation of exhausted, auto-aggressive CD8+ T cells; in preclinical models and clinical trials, this immunological state was associated with a blunted response to immune checkpoint inhibitors, such that non-viral (including MASH-related) HCC may benefit less from anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy than virus-related HCC [22]. These etiology-specific drivers are reflected in distinct molecular and immunogenomic landscapes [21,23].

THE INTERSECTION OF CHB AND MASLD: THE STEATOSIS PARADOX

The impact of concurrent steatotic liver disease (SLD) on the natural history of CHB has been a subject of intense debate. While some cohorts suggested that the presence of hepatic steatosis accelerated fibrotic progression and increased the risk of adverse liver outcomes [9,24-26], others reported a seemingly protective effect, observing lower viral loads and reduced incidences of cirrhosis, HCC, and even mortality in this patient subpopulation [27-35]. The recent paradigm shift in nomenclature to MASLD, alongside the accumulation of high-quality longitudinal data, has provided critical insights into resolving this controversy. The discrepancies may stem from treating MASLD as a singular, homogenous pathogenic entity. To accurately decipher the impact of MASLD on the trajectory of CHB, it is essential to conceptually disentangle MASLD into two distinct biological elements: “simple hepatic steatosis” and “systemic metabolic dysfunction” [31,36]. Hepatic steatosis as a suppressive microenvironment for HBV

Accumulating clinical and virological evidence has unveiled the paradoxical role of simple hepatic steatosis as a suppressive microenvironment for HBV [37]. Large-scale longitudinal studies have consistently demonstrated that patients with CHB and concurrent hepatic steatosis exhibit significantly lower serum hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and HBV DNA levels compared to their non-steatotic counterparts, with a substantially higher likelihood of achieving HBsAg seroclearance and subsequent seroconversion [25,27,32,38]. In a Taiwanese cohort study with 4,084 treatment-naïve patients, those with concurrent MASLD had a 1.43-fold higher probability of achieving functional cure after adjustment for potential confounding factors. In the same cohort, patients with moderate or severe SLD consistently showed a higher chance of HBsAg loss than those with mild hepatic steatosis [38]. In another individual patient data meta-analysis including 13,262 patients, the presence of fatty liver was associated with a significantly higher 10-year incidence of HBsAg seroclearance, 16.8% versus 11.7% [32].

Beyond its profound impact on promoting viral clearance, simple hepatic steatosis, when carefully disentangled from the deleterious effects of systemic metabolic dysfunction, confers a paradoxical protective effect on long-term adverse liver outcomes. Recent comprehensive cohort studies have revealed that the isolated presence of hepatic steatosis is independently associated with a significantly reduced risk of cirrhosis, HCC, and mortality [27-33,35]. For example, a propensity score matching analysis (n=9,414) in a large hospital-based cohort showed that SLD patients had a 38% lower mortality risk than non-SLD patients.35 Crucially, this protective phenomenon against HCC development is not limited to treatment-naïve patients; it has also been robustly observed in CHB populations receiving long-term nucleos(t)ide analogue therapy [29,34]. The persistence of this protective effect even under potent pharmacological viral suppression suggests that the steatotic microenvironment may trigger unique, intrinsic host mechanisms that synergistically mitigate HBV-driven hepatocarcinogenesis. The possible mechanisms will be discussed in another section.

Importantly, the impact of simple steatosis is different from its histological progression to MASH, the necroinflammatory form of MASLD that drives fibrogenesis and hepatocarcinogenesis [20]. For example, in a cohort of 906 patients undergoing curative resection for HCC (53.9% positive for HBsAg), those with steatosis (MASLD without MASH) had the highest five-year overall survival, whereas patients with MASH fared substantially worse, with MASH independently predicting poorer survival (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 1.95) [39]. These results indicate that simple steatosis and MASH may exert different prognostic effects in the CHB population.

The detrimental impact of metabolic dysfunction

In contrast to the virological benefits conferred by simple hepatic steatosis, the cardiometabolic risk factors embedded within the MASLD criteria, such as type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, and obesity, act as risk factors for disease progression in CHB, including fibrosis progression, HCC, and mortality [31,33-36,40-47]. Pathophysiologically, systemic metabolic dysfunctions are highly correlated with a state of chronic low-grade inflammation, profound insulin resistance, and elevated oxidative stress [48-50]. This lipotoxic and pro-inflammatory milieu synergistically amplifies HBV-mediated hepatic injury, accelerating fibrogenesis and establishing a robust pro-oncogenic microenvironment for HCC development [50-52].

Clinically, the detrimental impact of these metabolic derangements operates in a dose-dependent manner. Comprehensive analyses have clearly delineated that a higher number of concurrent cardiometabolic traits correlates with a stepwise increase in the risks of cirrhosis, HCC, liver-related and all-cause mortality [31,33,35,45]. For example, in a cohort study, the risk of liver-related mortality increased by 23% per cardiometabolic risk factor fulfilled at baseline [35]. Furthermore, studies highlighting the temporal dynamics emphasize that this metabolic burden is not static. Beyond pre-existing baseline conditions, the development of new-onset metabolic dysfunctions, especially incident DM, during the disease course profoundly exacerbates the risks of subsequent cirrhotic complications, HCC, and mortality [33,35,40,41]. As a result, the prognostic weight of systemic metabolic dysfunctions often overrides the potential protective effects of simple hepatic steatosis. When evaluating long-term survival, a heavy metabolic burden significantly elevates the rates of adverse outcomes in the CHB population.

Disentangling steatosis from cardiometabolic burden

Hepatic steatosis and systemic metabolic dysfunctions should be considered to play two different roles in CHB. The clinical trajectory of a patient with CHB and MASLD is ultimately dictated by the net effect of two opposing biological forces: the localized virological suppression conferred by hepatic steatosis and the systemic pro-inflammatory drive fueled by metabolic dysfunction [7,10,36]. This delicate balance resolves the conflicting findings in earlier epidemiological literature. In patients harboring a heavy cardiometabolic burden, the detrimental impacts of profound insulin resistance and lipotoxicity typically overwhelm the protective virological benefits of steatosis, propelling the liver toward cirrhosis and HCC [31,33]. Conversely, in individuals presenting with isolated hepatic steatosis and minimal metabolic derangements, the protective “shield” against HBV predominates, yielding a favorable long-term prognosis [35]. Therefore, the contemporary management of CHB-MASLD should pivot toward precision risk stratification. Clinicians may quantify and monitor the longitudinal cardiometabolic burden rather than fixating solely on the degree of hepatic fat accumulation. This conceptual disentanglement emphasizes that the aggressive optimization of systemic metabolic parameters remains the true cornerstone for improving survival and mitigating liver-related complications in this unique patient population.

Coinfection with hepatitis D virus (HDV)

A distinct subset of patients with CHB is also infected with the HDV, a defective satellite agent that depends on HBsAg for its propagation. HBV-HDV coinfection is widely regarded as one of the most aggressive forms of chronic viral hepatitis, accelerating progression to cirrhosis and conferring a substantially higher risk of HCC than HBV monoinfection [53,54]. In contrast to the expanding literature on HBV and MASLD, however, the interplay between HDV and MASLD remains largely uncharacterized: whether concurrent steatosis alters the risk conferred by HDV, or whether the intense necroinflammation driven by HDV overrides the impact of hepatic steatosis, is presently unknown. With the recent introduction of HDV-specific therapy [55], clarifying how metabolic comorbidity modifies outcomes in this particularly high-risk group represents an important and as-yet unmet research need.

THE INTERACTION BETWEEN CHC AND MASLD

In contrast to the paradoxical suppressive effects observed in the HBV-MASLD axis, the interplay between HCV and hepatic lipid metabolism is predominantly characterized by synergistic destruction. HCV is uniquely recognized as a steatogenic virus, possessing a life cycle that intricately depends on and actively hijacks the host’s lipid pathways [56]. When chronic HCV infection encounters host metabolic dysfunction, a bidirectional vicious cycle ensues: the viral infection exacerbates systemic metabolic derangements, while the pre-existing MASLD accelerates the progression of hepatic fibrosis and hepatocarcinogenesis (Fig. 2) [11,57]. Viral vs. metabolic steatosis

The pathogenesis of hepatic steatosis in CHC is broadly categorized into two distinct, yet frequently overlapping, biological entities: viral (virus-induced) steatosis and metabolic steatosis [58]. Viral steatosis is most prominently exemplified by HCV genotype 3 infection. In this context, the accumulation of hepatic fat represents a direct cytopathic effect of the virus [56,59]. Molecular studies have demonstrated that the HCV core protein exhibits an exceptionally high affinity for lipid droplets (LDs), mediated by specific sequence motifs such as the phenylalanine 164 residue, which facilitates viral assembly while directly inducing profound intracellular lipid retention [60]. Consequently, the severity of steatosis in genotype 3 infection correlates strongly with the viral load and typically resolves following successful viral eradication [61,62].

Conversely, hepatic steatosis observed in non-genotype 3 infections (such as genotype 1) is predominantly driven by host metabolic factors, closely mirroring the pathogenesis of MASLD [59,63]. However, the coexistence of HCV and MASLD triggers a formidable synergistic pathology rather than a simple superposition of two diseases. HCV actively perturbs host metabolic homeostasis by impairing intracellular insulin signaling pathways, such as the degradation or inhibition of insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1), thereby precipitating profound systemic insulin resistance [64,65]. This virus-induced metabolic derangement synergizes with host-derived adiposity and lipotoxicity, cementing the role of MASLD as a critical accelerator of HCV-related liver disease [12,66].

Impact of MASLD in the post-cure era

The advent of direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) has revolutionized the landscape of hepatology, enabling SVR rates that exceed 95% across all HCV genotypes [67,68]. However, achieving virological cure does not equate to the complete abrogation of liver-related risks. Residual MASLD and metabolic dysfunctions have emerged as a formidable and burgeoning clinical threat in the post-cure era, sustaining a pro-oncogenic microenvironment that continues to drive liver fibrosis and HCC development [12,66,69-74]. In a multi-center cohort study enrolling 1,598 post-DAA patients with biannual alpha-fetoprotein and liver imaging surveillance, MASLD was a risk factor for HCC (aHR 2.07; 95% CI 1.36–3.16). Moreover, the causal mediation analysis showed that MASLD significantly mediated cardiometabolic risk factors for HCC development [70].

In addition, the prognostic evaluation of this residual steatosis and metabolic burden requires a highly nuanced approach. Rather than assuming all degrees of steatosis and cardiometabolic traits confer equal risk, recent evidence underscores the necessity for precision risk stratification. A multi-center cohort study including 700 post-SVR patients with concurrent MASLD elucidated that the simplistic enumeration of metabolic criteria is insufficient for optimal risk prediction. This study revealed a clear oncogenic trajectory: advanced hepatic steatosis (S3 versus S1, aHR 2.15; 95% CI 1.25–3.69) and profound glycemic abnormalities (encompassing pre-diabetes and DM, aHR 2.33; 95% CI 1.38–3.94) were identified as the most potent, independent determinants of HCC risk after HCV cure for those with concurrent MASLD [12]. This finding is compatible with an earlier study showing steatosis and DM were the risk factors for HCC in non-genotype 3 CHC patients with SVR [71].

These findings highlight a clinical caveat: viral eradication merely eliminates one half of the dual-etiology equation. The persistent lipotoxic and hyperinsulinemic milieu takes over as the primary post-SVR oncogenic driver. Clinicians should evaluate the grade of residual steatosis and aggressively optimize specific cardiometabolic parameters to mitigate the long-term risk of HCC in this population.

The HBV-MASLD axis

The accumulation of lipids within hepatocytes profoundly alters the intracellular microenvironment, transforming it into a hostile niche for the HBV [37]. First, lipid accumulation triggers robust intra-hepatic immune modulation. The influx of saturated fatty acids actively stimulates Toll-like receptor 4 signaling. This activation initiates a downstream inflammatory cascade that culminates in the release of endogenous antiviral cytokines, such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, which potently suppress HBV replication [75-77]. Moreover, lipotoxicity in CHB impairs Rab7 expression, causing the accumulation of stimulator of interferon genes in macrophages, thereby facilitating antiviral activity [78]. On the other hand, the presence of steatohepatitis may facilitate Fas expression and subsequent death receptor-mediated apoptosis [79], as well as the increase of unconventionally activated CD8+PD-1+ T cells and possible hepatocyte injury [22]. These studies indirectly support the hypothesis that the presence of MASLD leads to a higher efficacy of intrahepatic HBV reservoir clearance by the immune system.

Second, severe intracellular lipid overload inevitably provokes ER stress [80]. The heightened ER stress response severely impairs the organelle’s capacity to properly fold and traffic viral envelope proteins [81]. Consequently, HBsAg and mature viral particles are trapped intracellularly, preventing their secretion into the bloodstream [82,83].

Third, the steatotic microenvironment hampers viral egress through autophagy inhibition. HBV heavily hijacks the host’s autophagic machinery to facilitate nucleocapsid maturation and envelopment [84,85]. However, the accumulation of LDs, accompanied by the marked upregulation of lipid-stabilizing proteins like perilipin 2 on their surfaces, severely impedes autophagic flux [86]. This disruption deprives the virus of the essential vesicular transport network required for its life cycle. Furthermore, stearoyl coenzyme A desaturase 1 also regulates autophagy under high lipid conditions, leading to the inhibition of HBV replication [87].

Fourth, hepatic steatosis orchestrates a profound metabolic reprogramming that starves HBV at the transcriptional level. Within the nucleus, the steatotic state significantly downregulates the expression and activity of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator-1α [88]. This downregulation leads to a diminished co-activation of hepatocyte nuclear factor-4α, an indispensable transcription factor that drives HBV gene expression [89,90]. Furthermore, the systemic reduction in adiponectin, a common feature of MASLD and obesity, further weakens the critical structural interaction between the HBV polymerase and pregenomic RNA, thereby crippling the viral replication engine from its core [91].

Despite these compelling mechanistic insights, it is crucial to acknowledge that the current evidence remains largely fragmented. No single molecular pathway can independently and comprehensively account for the multifaceted in vivo interactions or the clinically observed steatosis paradox. The overarching suppressive effect on HBV is likely the culmination of a highly complex, synergistic network rather than a solitary cellular mechanism. Therefore, future comprehensive translational studies are urgently warranted to fully decipher these intricate virus-host metabolic cross-talks.

The HCV-MASLD axis

In contrast to the suppressive environment generated by the HBV-MASLD axis, the molecular interactions between HCV and host lipid metabolism are synergistic and destructive. A hallmark of this viral-metabolic interplay is the hijacking of LDs. The HCV core protein possesses unique structural domains that direct its localization to the surface of host LDs [92,93]. Rather than being suppressed by lipid accumulation, HCV utilizes these lipid-rich organelles as essential physical platforms for viral nucleocapsid assembly and infectious virion production [56,94]. By densely coating the LDs, the core protein not only displaces essential host lipid- regulating proteins but also directly inhibits lipid turnover; this virus-driven LD hijacking directly induces intracellular lipid retention (steatosis) and essentially transforms the host’s metabolic storage into a dedicated viral factory [95,96].

Beyond structural hijacking, HCV orchestrates molecular insulin resistance [97]. The presence of HCV proteins, particularly the core protein and non-structural protein 5A, disrupts the host’s intracellular insulin signaling cascades [98,99]. Mechanistically, these viral proteins promote the pathological ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of IRS-1 [100]. They simultaneously impair the downstream phosphoinositide 3-kinase/Akt signaling pathway, effectively blinding the hepatocyte to insulin [101]. This virus-induced intracellular insulin resistance creates a devastating ripple effect. It may precipitate compensatory systemic hyperinsulinemia and lipotoxicity, which are associated with hepatic stellate cell activation [102]. Consequently, the molecular synergy between HCV-driven metabolic disruption and preexisting host MASLD establishes a fibrogenic and pro-oncogenic microenvironment, aggressively propelling the liver toward cirrhosis and HCC [103,104].

CLINICAL MANAGEMENT AND THERAPEUTIC PERSPECTIVES

Precision risk stratification in routine care

Historically, the clinical management of chronic viral hepatitis has been predominantly virocentric, focusing heavily on suppressing viral replication, achieving serological endpoints, and monitoring hepatic fibrosis. However, as the global prevalence of MASLD continues to escalate among patients with CHB and CHC, this traditional virocentric paradigm is no longer sufficient [105]. The management of dual-etiology liver disease necessitates a holistic approach, pivoting toward precision risk stratification that incorporates systemic metabolic parameters into routine hepatology care [12,35]. The fundamental step in this paradigm shift is the comprehensive assessment of cardiometabolic risk factors at the initial clinical encounter [106]. However, risk stratification should not be viewed as a static, one-time evaluation. Routine virological surveillance should be integrated with longitudinal cardiometabolic monitoring [33]. By dynamically quantifying the cumulative cardiometabolic burden over time, clinicians can precisely identify a highly vulnerable subset of patients who are at an elevated risk for adverse liver outcomes.

Beyond these conventional cardiometabolic parameters, two additional host determinants warrant incorporation into risk assessment. The first is sarcopenia, which shares core pathogenic pathways with MASLD, including insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, physical inactivity, and dysregulated myokine-adipokine signaling, and it independently predicts adverse outcomes across the spectrum of chronic liver disease [107-109]. For example, in a multi-center cohort study with 1,624 cirrhotic patients, sarcopenia was associated with a higher mortality risk (aHR 1.56; 95% CI 1.23–1.98) [109]. Because muscle status is both prognostically informative and modifiable, body-composition assessment can sharpen risk stratification in viral hepatitis with concurrent MASLD.

The second determinant is host genetic background. Common variants that impair hepatic lipid handling, including PNPLA3 (rs738409, I148M), TM6SF2 (rs58542926, E167K), and MBOAT7 (rs641738), modulate steatosis severity, steatohepatitis, fibrosis progression, or hepatocarcinogenesis in SLD [110,111]. In CHB, the PNPLA3 I148M allele has been linked to greater risks of long-term adverse outcomes including cirrhosis and HCC [30], and the MBOAT7 rs641738 variant is associated with increased necroinflammation and fibrosis [112]. In CHC, PNPLA3 and TM6SF2 variants are associated with fibrosis progression that may persist after viral eradication [113], and a hepatic fat-genetic risk score predicts HCC in cirrhotic patients cured with DAAs [74]. Incorporating such genetic information may further refine individualized risk prediction in dual-etiology liver disease. Multidisciplinary management of MASH and cardiometabolic burden

Because the longitudinal cardiometabolic burden influences the prognosis in patients with viral hepatitis, actively improving these systemic parameters is crucial for optimizing clinical outcomes. This multifaceted challenge necessitates a highly coordinated interprofessional team approach. Hepatologists remain central for advanced disease management, focusing on comprehensive fibrosis staging, prescribing stage-specific treatments, and conducting rigorous HCC surveillance [63]. However, for patients with dual-etiology liver disease, these specialists should work in continuous tandem with endocrinologists and primary care physicians, especially for those with DM since optimal glycemic control contributes to better prognosis in this population [114,115]. Furthermore, a healthy lifestyle remains the absolute foundation of treatment for patients with concurrent MASLD [116]. Dietitians play an indispensable role in delivering tailored nutritional therapy and recommendation, aiming to achieve substantial weight reduction (5–10%) [117]. Concurrently, behavioral modification teams and exercise physiologists are critical for designing structured physical activity [118]. In summary, deploying a proactive, multidisciplinary team to mitigate metabolic dysfunctions is necessary for improving long-term prognosis in the dual-etiology population.

KNOWLEDGE GAPS AND UNSOLVED ISSUES

Pharmacological interventions for MASH in CHB and CHC

Given that systemic metabolic dysfunction serves as the primary driver of disease progression in dual-etiology liver disease, host-targeting metabolic therapies represent a highly promising therapeutic frontier. Emerging pharmacological agents, such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists [119], peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor agonists [120,121] and liver-directed thyroid hormone receptor-beta agonists (resmetirom) [122], hold immense potential to ameliorate the pro-inflammatory and fibrogenic host microenvironment. However, the translation of these novel therapeutics into routine viral hepatitis care is currently hindered by an evidence gap. A critical methodological limitation in contemporary hepatology research is that pivotal phase 3 clinical trials evaluating these advanced metabolic agents routinely exclude patients with active or chronic HBV and HCV infections. Consequently, the long-term safety, histological efficacy, and potential unintended consequences on viral dynamics of these medications within the unique viral-metabolic microenvironment remain largely uninvestigated. For example, whether the correction of MASLD by GLP-1 receptor agonists results in elevated levels of HBV DNA is unknown [7]. The establishment of dedicated, large-scale real-world prospective cohorts is essential to validate the safety and efficacy profiles of these metabolic agents in dual-etiology populations.

Optimizing non-invasive tests (NITs) and HCC surveillance models

The accurate staging of hepatic fibrosis is necessary for risk stratification and determining treatment eligibility. However, the diagnostic performance of widely used NITs, such as the Fibrosis-4 index and transient elastography, remains highly controversial in the context of dual-etiology liver disease. The established clinical cut-offs for these modalities were predominantly validated in mono-etiological cohorts (either pure viral hepatitis or pure MASLD) [123], highlighting an urgent need to establish and validate specific, dual-etiology-adjusted cut-offs for NITs in routine practice.

In addition, current prognostic models utilized to predict the risk of HCC, such as the PAGE-B [124] or REACH-B [125] scores for CHB, are fundamentally virocentric. These models weigh baseline viral and demographic parameters but not hepatic steatosis, cardiometabolic burden, or the other non-viral factors that aggravate disease progression in CHB and CHC, as summarized in Table 2 [126-138]. Therefore, an unmet need is the development and validation of HCC prediction models that capture both virological and steatotic/metabolic parameters, thereby enabling personalized surveillance for patients with concurrent viral hepatitis in the MASLD era.

Steatosis paradox for developing novel therapeutics against CHB

Perhaps the most compelling unsolved puzzle within the dual-etiology paradigm is the exact in vivo mechanism of the HBV-MASLD steatosis paradox. While robust clinical data confirm that steatosis facilitates HBsAg seroclearance, and in vitro studies point toward mechanisms like metabolic reprogramming and ER stress, the comprehensive molecular network operating within the human liver remains incompletely understood. Decoding this cross-talk presents a translational opportunity. If researchers can precisely map the specific cellular pathways responsible for viral suppression, it may be possible to develop innovative host-targeting therapeutics [38]. Such agents could pharmacologically “mimic” the suppressive steatotic microenvironment by selectively starving the virus or blocking its secretion without subjecting the patient to the pro-fibrotic consequences of actual lipotoxicity. Finally, harnessing the steatosis paradox could unveil a potential therapeutic target to achieve the functional cure for CHB.

CONCLUSIONS

The overlapping epidemics of chronic viral hepatitis and MASLD represent an inescapable challenge in contemporary hepatology. Rather than a simple superposition of two morbidities, their convergence yields highly divergent and complex pathophysiological interactions. In CHB, a distinct paradox emerges: while isolated hepatic steatosis uniquely provides a suppressive microenvironment that favors viral clearance, the concomitant systemic cardiometabolic burden acts in a dose-dependent manner to relentlessly drive fibrogenesis and hepatocarcinogenesis. Conversely, in CHC, the viral-metabolic interplay is fundamentally synergistic and destructive. HCV actively exacerbates host metabolic dysfunctions, accelerating insulin resistance and liver injury. Crucially, even after the achievement of virological cure via DAAs, residual advanced steatosis and profound glycemic abnormalities persist as formidable, independent drivers of de novo HCC.

In the modern era of highly effective antiviral therapies and DAA-mediated cures, a strictly virocentric approach is no longer adequate to eliminate liver-related mortality. The future of hepatology must embrace a holistic, dual-target strategy, one that equally prioritizes sustained viral suppression and aggressive metabolic remission. By integrating dynamic, precision risk stratification and targeted cardiometabolic interventions into standard virological care, clinicians can effectively neutralize the residual metabolic threats and fundamentally optimize the long-term prognosis for this highly vulnerable patient population.
