Synthesis of Conjugated Linear and Cyclic Polyynes by Selective Alkyne Metathesis
Authors: Julien Escudero, Jonathan Long, Dylan Bouëtard, Jennifer Morvan, Mehdi Koohgard, Angelika Neitzel, Dirk Bockfeld, Shedrack Nwachukwu, Thierry Roisnel, Rik R Tykwinski, Matthias Tamm, Marc Mauduit, Yann Trolez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe formation of polyynes with an odd number of conjugated triple bonds is synthetically demanding, particularly for complex architectures, since most established methods rely on irreversible C–C bond‐forming reactions and operate under kinetic control. To address this problem, we have developed the synthesis of triynes via molybdenum‐catalyzed alkyne metathesis of diynes, which allows thermodynamic control through reversible cleavage and formation of carbon–carbon triple bonds. We demonstrate the potential of this method through the synthesis of challenging, more complex products, including triyne precursors to [7]cumulenes, a pentayne, a heptayne, and dehydrobenzannulene macrocycles containing triynes. The key to the success of the proposed methodology is achieving a balance between the selectivity and reactivity of the molybdenum catalyst with the diyne precursors.Keywords: alkyne metathesis, cumulene, heptayne, macrocycle, pentayne

Introduction

Polyynes are molecules containing multiple conjugated carbon–carbon triple bonds [1]. This important family of compounds is found in nature, as natural products such as caryoynecin and collimonin [2], as well as in the interstellar medium (Figure 1) [3]. Furthermore, polyynes have been utilized in the synthesis of innovative organic materials [4]. Long chains of conjugated triple bonds have been synthesized over the last 15 years to create compounds that mimic carbyne, an infinite chain of alternating single and triple bonds [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Although carbyne remains an elusive allotrope of carbon, it is predicted to possess exceptional properties, including superior conductivity, strength, elastic modulus, and stiffness, surpassing any known material [12]. Cyclo[n]carbons are also carbon allotropes containing only sp‐hybridized carbon atoms. Although they are poorly stable, some examples have been characterized by mass spectrometry [13], generated on surfaces at low temperature, or recently characterized in solution via catenation [14, 15, 16].

Selected examples of relevant molecules featuring conjugated polyynes.

Over the past decades, strategies have been developed to connect two carbon–carbon triple bonds to afford a conjugated diyne (Figure 2A). The formation of symmetrical derivatives, i.e., di‐ and tetraynes, through oxidative acetylenic homocoupling reactions dominates this chemistry. The most famous of these methods is Glaser coupling combined with the modifications of Eglington and Hay, which all employ copper salts (and in some cases palladium) to induce Csp─Csp bond formation from terminal alkynes [17]. The Cadiot–Chodkiewicz coupling is commonly used for the formation of unsymmetrical diyne products via a heterocoupling reaction that links a terminal alkyne with a halogenated alkyne [18]. More recently, new strategies to synthesize unsymmetrical 1,3‐diynes have been developed in order to improve yields and to overcome the limitations of the above‐mentioned methods [19, 20, 21, 22, 23].

(A) Common methodologies for the synthesis of conjugated triynes. (B) Synthesis of conjugated triynes via alkyne metathesis and structure of four Mo‐catalysts. (C) Synthesis of challenging molecules via selective diyne metathesis (this work).

Moving beyond diynes, the formation of conjugated triynes is substantially more tedious since a third triple bond is at stake, which typically forces the less selective coupling reaction of an alkyne and diyne. Even though often used [17], Glaser‐type couplings are typically inefficient for this purpose as they either require an excess of the less reactive substrate or produce several products without selectivity. This results in a substantial amount of byproducts in addition to the target compounds. Consequently, Cadiot–Chodkiewicz couplings are preferred, although brominated diynes can be unstable in some cases, and they are prone to dimerization under the reaction conditions, complicating purification [24, 25]. Both the Glaser‐type and Cadiot–Chodkiewicz reactions necessitate the use of a potentially unstable terminal diyne as a precursor, which limits applications. The Fritsch–Buttenberg–Wiechell (FBW) rearrangement is frequently used to construct both symmetrical and unsymmetrical triynes [26, 27], but its reliance on strong bases such as n‐butyllithium limits its applicability. Among these and other methods reported in recent years [28, 29], none have achieved consensus as the preferred “go‐to” method although all of them enabled the synthesis of conjugated triynes over the last decades in variable yields. Beyond these practical considerations, it is important to recognize that most established approaches to conjugated triynes rely on irreversible C–C bond‐forming reactions. As a result, product distributions are governed by kinetic control, offering no opportunity for error correction once undesired coupling events occur. In contrast, alkyne metathesis proceeds through reversible cleavage and formation of carbon–carbon triple bonds, enabling thermodynamic control over acetylenic bond formation. Thus, the synthesis of triynes, and more generally polyynes with an odd number of conjugated triple bonds, remains a scientific challenge. In this context, we recently described the synthesis of various triynes by alkyne metathesis of diynes (Figure 2B) [30]. Previous studies indicated that, perhaps counterintuitively, diyne metathesis typically yields diynes [20, 23, 31]. On this basis, we hypothesized that steric shielding of one of the two carbon–carbon triple bonds of the diyne precursors should prevent or limit the formation of diynes and favor triynes. This strategy was validated using the Fürstner catalyst [32] and bulky end‐groups like triphenylmethyl (Tr) that afforded the desired triyne in good yield and full selectivity [30]. However, as the bulkiness of the end‐group was reduced, the selectivity toward the triyne decreased. The presence of the triisopropylsilyl (TIPS) end‐group provided 95% of the triyne and 5% of the diyne, while the triphenylsilyl and triethylsilyl end‐group resulted in dramatically lower selectivity of 40:60 and 2:98, respectively, favoring formation of the diyne. Consistent with this trend, the use of the trimethylsilyl end‐group led to only formation of the diyne.

Notably, modifying the catalyst structure significantly improved selectivity for triynes over diynes. Preliminary findings show complete selectivity for the triyne using the catalysts MoF9 and MoSi*F9 (Figure 2B) with the TIPS and mesityl end‐groups [33], while it was not the case with the Fürstner catalyst. These results encouraged us to use alkyne metathesis to tackle the challenging synthesis of more complex products such as [7]cumulenes, a pentayne, a heptayne, and macrocycles containing triynes, all representing significant synthetic challenges (Figure 2C). For this project, we also used a fourth catalyst, MoF6, which is one of the most active alkyne metathesis catalysts, toward optimizing the balance between the selectivity and the reactivity that originate from steric hindrance around molybdenum and electronic factors [34, 35, 36, 37]. In this report, we demonstrate that alkyne metathesis is a reaction that complements existing traditional methods for the synthesis of odd polyynes and their derivatives that would be difficult or impossible to form with current routes.

Results and discussion

Synthesis of [7]cumulenes

The synthesis of [n]cumulenes is motivated by a number of interesting goals, most recently as models for the allotrope carbyne [38], the prospect of helical orbitals [39], and as interesting synthetic precursors [40]. Shorter derivatives, [3]‐ and [5]cumulenes, are typically quite stable and thus routine to synthesize. The synthesis of the next longer homologue, a [7]cumulene, is complicated by both the need for a triyne as a precursor and instability of the cumulene product. The Cadiot–Chodkiewicz coupling [41] and the Fritsch–Buttenberg–Wiechell rearrangement [27] have been used in the past, but the syntheses are tedious and yields often low. Thus, alkyne metathesis offers an opportunity to greatly simplify the synthesis of triyne precursors to [7]cumulenes since bulky substituents are required to stabilize the final structure. The synthesis started with the reaction of ester 1 with the respective aryl lithium reagent 2a‐d at –78°C over two hours. The resulting lithium alkoxide was subsequently quenched with dimethyl sulfate to afford compounds 3a‐d [42, 43, 44]. The trimethylsilyl or triisopropylsilyl groups were then removed through the reaction with potassium carbonate (in THF/MeOH) or TBAF (in THF), respectively, yielding compounds 4a‐d. A Sonogashira coupling between compounds 4a‐d and 1,2‐dichloroethylene under standard conditions afforded compounds 5a‐d. The dichloroolefins 5a‐d were then treated with n‐butyllithium (2.1 equiv) followed by quenching with methyl iodide (10 equiv) to afford pentadiynes 6a‐d. Diyne metathesis of compounds 6a‐d was subsequently explored (Scheme 1). Using MoF6 or MoF9, the corresponding triynes 7a‐d were efficiently formed in 47%–81% yield. A suitable single crystal of 7a revealed its solid‐state structure by x‐ray diffraction. To complete the synthesis, reductive elimination [45] of 7a afforded the tetraphenyl[7]cumulene 8a, which decomposed in either solution or the solid state under ambient conditions (within minutes to a few hours), but could be characterized by UV–vis spectroscopy (see Supporting Information for details). However, the steric shielding provided by the ortho‐substituted end‐groups afforded stable [7]cumulenes 8b‐d, which were isolated in 48–58% yield. Furthermore, x‐ray diffraction from suitable crystals of 8b and 8d confirmed the structures. The cumulenic bond lengths of 8b and 8d are in the same range as the two [7]cumulenes for which crystallographic characterization has been reported [46]. On the other hand, a number of bond angles of 8b and 8d, at 174 and 176°, respectively, deviated slightly more from linearity than those previously reported (all > 177.5°) [46].

Synthesis of [7]cumulenes 8a–d via selective diyne metathesis and solid‐state structures of [7]cumulene 8b and 8d (ellipsoids are drawn with 50% probability and hydrogens are omitted for clarity).

Overall, the use of alkyne metathesis in the construction of triyne precursors to [7]cumulenes represents a significant advancement compared to traditional routes, such as the Cadiot–Chodkiewicz cross‐coupling or Fritsch–Buttenberg–Wiechell rearrangement. A more detailed discussion is provided in the supporting information that describes the drawbacks of these two methods relative to alkyne metathesis (Figures S1–S3).

Synthesis of a Conjugated Penta‐ and Heptayne

Building on the synthesis of triynes, we explored the feasibility of assembling more challenging species such as conjugated pentaynes and heptaynes. To tackle the synthesis of a pentayne, we took advantage of the initial formation of a dicobalt complex masking group [47, 48, 49] in order to provide (i) the required steric hindrance provided by the coordination of the two cobalt atoms for the selective formation of a conjugated triyne and (ii) the possibility of generating additional carbon–carbon triple bonds under mild conditions to afford a conjugated pentadiyne. To this end, diyne 9 was synthesized according to a procedure from the literature [50], and then functionalized with a dicobalt complex using dicobalt octacarbonyl Co2(CO)8 and bis(diphenylphosphino)methane (dppm) ligand to afford compound 10 in 47% isolated yield. As described by the groups of Diederich and Anderson [48, 49], the bulky TIPS group prevents the cobalt from reacting with the adjacent C≡C triple bond. The TIPS group was subsequently removed using tetrabutylammonium fluoride (TBAF) to give compound 11 in 91% yield. Compound 11 was then subjected to a Cadiot‐Chodkiewicz coupling with iodopropyne [51], affording diyne 12 in 33% yield. Isolation of compound 12 from the residual starting material 11 proved impossible, and an additional Hay coupling was thus performed to convert the remaining reactant 11 into its dimer, which could be readily removed by column chromatography. Diyne 12 was subjected to alkyne metathesis (Scheme 2), but no conversion of 12 to triyne 13 was observed using MoSi*F9 catalyst. It is likely that cobalt complex is incompatible with a hindered catalyst, highlighting the need to balance the selectivity and reactivity of the catalyst and the diyne precursor. The triyne 13 was isolated in 46% yield using the less hindered catalyst MoF6. Removal of the cobalt complex from compound 13 using I2 at room temperature in CH2Cl2 afforded pentayne 14. Single crystals of 14 were grown and allowed unambiguous characterization by x‐ray diffraction. The chain of C≡C triple bonds is not perfectly linear but shows a very slightly distorted S‐shaped geometry. This pentayne 14 is remarkably insoluble in most organic solvents, making its NMR spectroscopic characterization particularly difficult.

Synthesis of 1,3,5,7,9‐pentayne 14 and its x‐ray structure (ellipsoids are drawn with 50% probability and hydrogens are omitted for clarity).

To illustrate the effectiveness of this methodology, the synthesis of a conjugated heptayne was tackled (Scheme 3) since these species have been rarely synthesized previously and only using specific protocols [52]. Starting from diyne 15 (see supporting information for synthetic details), reaction with diyne 16 (2 equiv) in the presence of MoSi*F9 catalyst afforded triyne 17, which was then functionalized with a dicobalt complex using dicobalt octacarbonyl Co2(CO)8 and bis(diphenylphosphino)methane (dppm) ligand, selectively affording compound 18 in 68% yield, thanks to the presence of two bulky capping groups. The TIPS group was then removed with TBAF, yielding compound 19 quantitatively. Compound 19 was then subjected to a Cadiot‐Chodkiewicz coupling with iodopropyne to afford compound 20 in 76% yield, which was subsequently reacted with the MoF9 catalyst. The corresponding conjugated triyne 21 was isolated in 72% yield. In this case, MoSi*F9 did not yield the expected triyne 21 (as was also observed for triyne 13) presumably because it is too bulky for the cobalt complex. This is the reason why MoF6 or MoF9 had to be used for the synthesis of triynes 13 and 21, highlighting the need to strike the right balance between the bulkiness of the catalyst and that of the substrates.

Synthesis of 1,3,5,7,9,11‐heptayne 22 and x‐ray structure of compound 20 (ellipsoids are drawn with 50% probability and hydrogens are omitted for clarity).

Removal of the two cobalt complex fragments with I2 (10 equiv) afforded heptayne 22. Thanks to the nature of the end‐groups, compound 22 is significantly more soluble in chlorinated solvents than pentayne 14, greatly facilitating its characterization by NMR spectroscopy and thus showing the expected 7 13C NMR signals for the Csp carbon atoms in the range 63–81 ppm.

Macrocycle Synthesis

Conjugated macrocycles have been a recurring synthetic topic since the seminal work of Sondheimer [53], offering fascinating structures, opportunities for unique semiconductors, and models for studies of aromaticity [54]. The number of conjugated macrocycles bearing a triyne unit reported in the literature is very small [55, 56, 57, 58], a fact that is undoubtedly linked to the scarcity of methodologies for accessing conjugated triynes (vide supra). Dehydrobenzannulenes (DBAs) featuring diyne and tetrayne moieties have often been reported in the past based on oxidative acetylenic homocoupling reactions of terminal alkynes [59]. These previously established methods are not, however, applicable to DBAs constructed from triyne building blocks. We hypothesized that alkyne metathesis, which is regularly used for the synthesis of macrocycles [32, 60, 61, 62], could solve this problem with our Mo‐catalysts. In this context, the reversible nature of alkyne metathesis is particularly beneficial as it allows macrocyclization to proceed under thermodynamic control. Undesired oligomeric or mismatched coupling products can re‐enter the catalytic cycle, ultimately favoring formation of the most stable cyclic architectures over kinetically trapped by‐products. To this end, two triangular macrocycles were targeted. The first, compound 23, was synthesized via a double Sonogashira coupling with 1,2‐dichloroethylene (8 equiv), affording compound 24 in 29% yield. Double elimination with n‐butyllithium followed by quenching with methyl iodide gave compound 25. In the presence of MoSi*F9 in toluene for four hours (Scheme 4), the ortho‐substituted arene 25 reacted selectively to give the trimer, the formally anti‐aromatic dehydrobenz[24]annulene 26, along with trace amounts of byproducts that were tentatively identified by mass spectrometry (see Supporting Information for details) as the isosceles triangles 27 and 28. Although suitable crystals were grown of 26, all attempts to characterize the compound by x‐ray crystallography resulted in the decomposition of the crystals due to radiation damage.

Selective cyclotrimerization catalyzed by MoSi*F9 complex affording macrocycle 26 and trace amounts of the corresponding isosceles triangles.

For the second macrocycle, an alternative strategy was employed. Dibromoolefin 29 was synthesized by known methods [63, 64] and treated with n‐butyllithium and zinc dichloride to generate a solution of organozinc reagent 30, which was immediately used for a double Negishi coupling with compound 31 [65], providing tetrayne 32 in 25% yield. Under the same metathesis reaction conditions as used for macrocycle 26 with MoSi*F9, macrocycle 33 was isolated in 77% yield. Notably, no trace of smaller macrocyclic by‐product was detected, which likely accounts for the improved yield. While the importance of ortho‐substituents in the selectivity of arene‐substituted diynes metathesis has been described [30, 33, 66], the synthesis of 33 suggests that substituents remote from the reactive diyne core may influence the selectivity of the macrocyclization reactions. In addition to usual techniques, macrocycle 33 was characterized by single‐crystal x‐ray diffraction (Scheme 5). The structure of the dehydrobenz[24]annulene is slightly bowl shaped, rather than planar (Figure S9). While there is slight bending in some of the triyne segments (176°–179°), bond angles and lengths are consistent with analogous acetylenic macrocycles [55, 67]. Additional details regarding the packing can be found in the Supporting Information (Figures S106–S108).

Conclusion

We demonstrate that alkyne metathesis with properly designed Mo‐catalysts can be used efficiently to assemble linear and cyclic conjugated triynes that are challenging or impossible to synthesize by usual metal‐catalyzed cross‐coupling reactions. A key conceptual advantage of this strategy lies in the reversible nature of alkyne metathesis, which enables thermodynamic control over triple‐bond formation and fundamentally distinguishes this approach from conventional irreversible coupling reactions commonly employed in polyyne synthesis. While MoSi*F9 catalyst generally offers the best selectivity, its bulkiness precludes the formation of the most sterically hindered derivatives. Suitably functionalized triynes are formed, isolated, and studied as precursors to [7]cumulenes. Strategic use of a dicobalt complex as an alkyne masking group allows the use of metathesis to form triynes that are readily converted to a pentayne and a heptayne by removal of the masking group. Finally, the metathesis reaction of ortho‐substituted bis(pentadiynyl)arenes enables unprecedented formation of the antiaromatic dehydrobenz[24]annulenes in high yields. In combination, these results demonstrate the synthesis of molecules bearing triyne, pentayne, and heptayne moieties that would be difficult to form by any other methods. With appropriate matching of the steric requirements presented by the diyne substrate and catalyst, the formation of complex triyne acetylenic architectures is now within reach, and work is currently in progress toward the synthesis of cyclic and acyclic new targets using this methodology.
