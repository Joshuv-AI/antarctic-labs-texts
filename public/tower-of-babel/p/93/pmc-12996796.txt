Excessive ER-mitochondria coupling: A DRP1-driven mechanism underlying mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired autophagy in stress-induced depression-like behavior in mice
Authors: Jia-Rui Zhang, Jia-Yan Zhang, Qing-Ya Sun, Chang Chen, Jing-Wei Yang, Nan Cheng, Zi-Wen Guo, Ruo-Nan Shuang, Wei Gu, Meng-Ying Zhai, Jin-Ao Duan, Wei-Wei Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression is a common psychiatric disorder characterized by heightened stress exposure and disruptions in neuronal signaling. Growing evidence suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to its pathophysiology. In particular, mitochondrial dynamics regulated by Dnm1l/Drp1 are critical for neuronal homeostasis, and their dysregulation may lead to cellular impairment. Additionally, mitochondrial-endoplasmic reticulum contact sites (MERCs) are crucial for maintaining cellular function and require precise regulation. However, the role of Drp1 in modulating MERC structure and function in the context of depression remains unclear.MethodsWe quantified protein changes via 4D-FastDIA proteomics. MERC alterations were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and proximity ligation assay (PLA). Mitochondrial metabolism was assessed with the Seahorse XF Analyzer. Autophagy was visualized through tyramine signal amplification and Imaris-based 3D reconstruction. The causal relationship was tested using Vglut2-Cre mice combined with specific flox-virus mediated Drp1 manipulation and pharmacological inhibition of autophagy. Depression-like behaviors were evaluated after chronic social defeat stress (CSDS).ResultsDrp1 activation disrupts mitochondrial-endoplasmic reticulum contact sites (MERCs), leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired autophagy, and ultimately promoting depressive-like behaviors. Inhibiting the MERC tethering protein GRP75 or enhancing mitophagy pharmacologically alleviated these neuronal and behavioral deficits. These findings identify Drp1-mediated MERC disruption as a key mechanism in depression and suggest therapeutic strategies targeting MERC integrity and autophagy.ConclusionOur results provide novel mechanistic evidence that Drp1-mediated dysfunction at MERCs and impaired mitochondrial quality control contribute to the pathogenesis of depression. These findings underscore the importance of endoplasmic reticulum-mitochondrial crosstalk in depression and suggest potential therapeutic targets for modulating cellular resilience in stress-related disorders.Keywords: Depression, Mitochondrial-endoplasmic reticulum contact sites (MERCs), Mitochondrion, Mitophagy, drp1

Introduction

Depression is a prevalent psychiatric disorder whose pathogenesis is strongly associated with increased exposure to external stressors [[1], [2], [3]]. The hippocampus, a key component of the limbic system, closely related to emotions, learning, and memory. Neuropathological studies have demonstrated alterations in hippocampal neural plasticity in individuals with depression [4,5]. Furthermore, emerging research indicates that hippocampal function critically influences susceptibility to depressive symptoms, particularly in modulating stress and emotional responses [6,7]. Consequently, the hippocampus remains a major focus of research in mood disorders.

Accumulating evidence further suggests a connection between mitochondrial dysfunction, bioenergetic deficits, and major depressive disorder (MDD). Neuroimaging studies have reported decreased cerebral ATP levels in depressed patients [8]. Notably, one clinical study found that 63% of patients with mitochondrial disease meet the criteria for a psychiatric illness, among whom 58% were diagnosed with MDD [9]. Collectively, these findings underscore the central role of mitochondrial impairment in the pathophysiology of depressive disorders.

Given their high energy demands, neurons are particularly dependent on mitochondrial function [10] and are thus vulnerable to mitochondrial dysfunction [11,12]. Dysregulated neuronal signaling has been implicated in the pathogenesis of depression [13,14] and neurophysiological alterations have been observed in neurons from MDD patients [15]. Therefore, maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis is crucial for neuronal integrity. However, mitochondria do not function in isolation but through dynamic interactions with other organelles [16]. The communication between mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), in particular, is essential for mitochondrial function. The involvement of mitochondrial-ER contacts in neurons presents a promising framework for investigating how mitochondrial regulation influences depressive phenotypes.

The specialized contact sites between mitochondria and the ER, known as mitochondria-ER contacts (MERCs) or mitochondrial-associated membranes (MAMs), typically span 10-100 nm. These sites are facilitated by protein complexes that tether the two organelles, promoting the exchange of molecules and signals, including Ca2+, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and lipids [17]. Several tethering complexes have been identified, including the inositol 1,4,5-triphosphate receptor (IP3R) - glucose-regulated protein 75 (GRP75) - voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC) complex, mitofusin 1/2 (MFN1/2), and other MERC regulatory proteins [[18], [19], [20]]. Our previous work identified GRP75 (Hspa9) as a brain-enriched protein primarily localized to the mitochondrial outer membrane, where it bridges the IP3R3-GRP75-VDAC1 complex to link the ER and mitochondria [21,22].

The relationship between mitochondrial structure and function is well-established [23]. Mitochondrial dynamics are regulated by specific proteins, and mutations in these proteins can lead to structural and functional deficits [24]. Dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1), a key regulator of mitochondrial fission, is implicated in several human diseases, including depression [25]. Functional modifications of Drp1 can create a platform between the ER and mitochondria, stabilizing calcium flux at MERCs [26,27]. Recent evidence suggests that in yeast and mammalian cells, the ER associates with mitochondria at the site where Dnm1 (yeast) or DRP1 (mammals) is recruited by mitochondrial receptors [28]. Assembled Dnm1/DRP1 forms helical structures that constrict mitochondria, initiating fission and autophagy [29]. However, the mechanism by which Drp1 regulates MERCs and mitophagy in neurons remains unclear.

In this study, we observed specific enrichment of MERCs in hippocampal neurons of mice subjected to chronic social defeat stress (CSDS), concomitant with increased Drp1 expression. Using neuron-specific viral tools, we demonstrated that CSDS recruits Drp1 to disrupt mitochondrial-ER tethering, impair mitophagy, and induce neuronal dysfunction, ultimately mediating depressive-like behaviors. Furthermore, by generating conditional Hspa9 knockout mice with GRP75 deficiency in neurons, we found that disrupting the mitochondrial-ER tethering complex ameliorated the pathological cascade triggered by Drp1 recruitment. Our findings provide direct evidence for the critical roles of Drp1 and MERCs in depression-related neuronal pathology.

Methods and materials

Animals

C57BL/6J wild-type male or female mice (6-week), CD1 male mice (7-month) andVglut2Cre mice [B6. Vglut2-ires-cre] were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, China). Conditional vglut2-specific Hspa9 knockout mice were generated using the Cre/loxP system. The Hspa9flox/+ mice (C57BL/6J background; Strain NO. T008726, GemPharmatech) were generously provided by the Yu Laboratory at Fudan University. Mice were housed under standard conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle, an ambient temperature of 22 ± 1 °C, and relative humidity of 30-80%, with ad libitum access to food and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. 202304A054) and performed in accordance with the NIH guidelines.

Primary neuron culture

Hippocampal tissues were digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA at 37 °C for 15 min, triturated, and resuspended in Neurobasal medium supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 0.5 mM l-glutamine, and 2% B-27 (Gibco, New York, USA). After filtration through a 70 μm cell strainer, cells were seeded onto poly-d-lysine-coated glass-bottom dishes (50 μg/mL, 1 × 105 cells/cm2, WHB, China) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. The medium was partially replaced every 72 h. Cytosine arabinoside (Ara-C, 5 μM, Sigma, USA) was added at 48 h to inhibit glial cell proliferation. Neurons were used for experiments after 7–10 days in culture.

Chronic social defeat stress (CSDS)

The CSDS procedure was performed as previously described [30]. experimental C57BL/6J mice (intruders) were exposed to a novel aggressive CD1 mouse (aggressor) for 5-10 min daily over 10 consecutive days. Intruder mice consistently exhibited submissive postures (e.g., defensive upright, fleeing, or freezing). After each physical encounter, mice were separated by a perforated plexiglass divider, allowing sensory contact for the remaining 24 h.

Subthreshold social defeat stress (sub-SDS)

For the sub-SDS protocol [21,31], Vglut2-Cre mice were subjected to three 5-min attack sessions by CD1 aggressors, spaced 15 min apart, in a single day. After the final session, mice were individually housed. Social interaction tests were performed 24 h post-stress. This paradigm induces stress susceptibility without provoking significant social avoidance.

Shock-induced depression (SID)

We employed a standard learned helplessness paradigm, a well-established model of depression [32]. Briefly, rats were placed in a chamber (13 × 16 × 26 cm) and subjected to 60 inescapable, unpredictable foot shocks (1 mA, 15 s duration, 50 Hz) delivered irregularly at an average of one per minute. Control mice were housed in an identical chamber for 1 h without receiving any foot shock.

Virus injection

For sparse neuronal labeling, NCSP-YFP-2E5 (BC-SL001, Brain Case) was used. To knockdown Drp1 (Dnm1l) in hippocampal Vglut2 neurons, we used an AAV2/9 vector carrying a Cre-dependent EGFP-tagged shRNA under the CMV promoter. Control vectors expressed a scrambled shRNA sequence. To overexpress Drp1, we used an AAV2/9 vector with a Cre-dependent EGFP tag. For stereotactic delivery, anesthetized mice (2% pentobarbital) were placed in a stereotactic frame (RWD Life Sciences). Viruses were bilaterally injected into CA1 region (AP -2.1 mm, ML ± 2.11 mm, DV -1.65 mm) using a micro-syringe pump at 100 nL/min. The injection needle was retained for 10 min post-injection before withdrawal. Surgical procedures were performed on a heating pad with postoperative monitoring.

Social interaction test (SIT)

The SIT was conducted 24h after the final CSDS session. Testing arenas were cleaned with 75% ethanol between sessions. The apparatus consisted of a 44 × 44 cm arena with a 10 × 6 cm wire mesh enclosure. The test comprised two 2.5-min phases: a habituation period without a target mouse, followed by a test phase with a novel CD1 mouse confined in the enclosure. An interaction zone (14 × 26 cm) surrounding the enclosure was defined. The social interaction ratio was calculated as (time in interaction zone with target)/(time in interaction zone without target) × 100%.

Sucrose preference test (SPT)

Mice were acclimated f to two identical bottles containing either 2% sucrose solution or pure water for 24h, with bottle positions switched after 12h. After acclimation, mice were food- and water-deprived for 24h. The 8-h test was conducted under standard conditions, with bottle positions switched every 4h. Sucrose preference was calculated as: (sucrose intake/total fluid intake) × 100%.

Forced swimming test (FST)

Mice were placed in a clear plexiglass cylinder (30 cm height × 15 cm diameter) filled with 15 cm of water maintained at 25 ± 1 °C. Sessions lasted 5 min, and immobility time (passive floating with minimal movement to stay afloat) was automatically quantified using the ANY-maze video tracking system (Stoelting Co., USA).

Protein extraction

Frozen tissue samples were r pulverized in a liquid nitrogen-chilled mortar. Powdered tissue was homogenized in ice-cold lysis buffer (8 M urea containing 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitors) using ultrasonic disruption. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for protein quantification using the BCA method.

Trypsin digestion

Protein aliquots were normalized, precipitated with ice-cold acetone, and washed. Pellets were reconstituted in 200 mM TEAB and sonicated. Trypsin digestion was performed overnight at a 1:50 (w/w) enzyme-to-protein ratio. Peptides were reduced with 5 mM DTT (56 °C, 30 min) and alkylated with 11 mM IAA (room temperature, 15 min in the dark).

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis

Peptides were resuspended in 0.1% formic acid and purified using Evotip columns. Chromatographic separation was performed on an Evosep One UHPLC system with a 60 samples/day gradient. Eluted peptides were ionized and analyzed on a timsTOF Pro 2 mass spectrometer in dia-PASEF mode.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Mice were anesthetized and perfused sequentially with warm saline, primary fixative (0.25% glutaraldehyde), and ice-cold glutaraldehyde. Brains were post-fixed overnight, before sectioning. And hippocampal vibratome sections (100 μm) were prepared. Sections were post-fixed with 1% OsO4, dehydrated, and resin-embedded. Ultrathin sections (50 nm) were cut and imaged. Referring to the statistical methods in published literature [18,22], cell images were randomly selected in CA1 region and analyzed in a blinded manner using ImageJ and GraphPad Prism 8.

Western blot analysis

Tissue or cell lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay. (Sigma, USA). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% non-fat milk, and probed with primary and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Bands were detected by chemiluminescence (Immun-Star™ Western C™ HRP kit) and imaged (Tanon 4600 system, Shanghai).

Cycloheximide chase assay

The primary neurons were isolated and seeded into a six well plate. After the confluence of cells reached about 90%, 100 μg/ml Cycloheximide (CHX) was added to the cultured cells and the timing was started. Then cells were collected at the set time points (0h, 2h, 4h, 8h, 12h). Finally, the expression level of the target protein at each time point was detected by Western blot analysis.

Co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP)

Hippocampal tissue or primary neurons were lysed with mild lysate containing deubiquitinate inhibitor and proteasome inhibitor (RIPA lysate). Protein A/G magnetic beads were washed with buffer. Then, 1.5 mg protein solution, 10 μl specific antibody and 30 μL protein a/g magnetic beads were mixed and placed overnight at 4 °C. After the magnetic beads were washed, the LDS sample buffer 4× was added to the magnetic beads at 95 °C for 10min. Finally, the supernatant was collected by the following methods: magnetic separation for Western blot analysis.

Tyramine signal amplification (TSA)

Brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and cryopreserved in OCT compound (Thermo NEG-50). Multiplex staining was performed using TSA-based immunofluorescence with primary antibodies: Drp1 (CST 12741S, 1:100), MAP2 (ab32454, 1:1000), GFAP (CST 12389, 1:400), IBA1 (ab178846, 1:1000), Vglut1 (CST #27181, 1:800), Vglut2 (ab216463, 1:250), OLIG2 (CST #65915, 1:400), and LAMP1 (CST 99437S, 1:100). Following incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (ab205718), signal amplification was achieved using tyramide conjugates (YF® 488 and 594 Tyramide, UElandy). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI before imaging with a confocal microscope (Olympus FV4000, Japan) and subsequent analysis using ImageJ or cellSens software.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

Brain sections were processed for PLA using the Duolink® system (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Sections were blocked (37 °C, 1 h) before overnight incubation (4 °C) with primary antibodies against IP3R-3, GRP75, and VDAC1 (1:200). After Buffer A washes, PLA probes (secondary antibodies in Duolink® diluent) were applied (37 °C, 1 h). Subsequent ligation (37 °C, 30 min) and amplification (37 °C, 100 min, dark) steps were performed, followed by Buffer B washes. DAPI-counterstained sections were imaged using an FV4000 confocal microscope, with PLA signals quantified as red fluorescent puncta using ImageJ.

Mitochondrial respiration assay

Hippocampal mitochondria were isolated using a commercial kit (Beyotime, China) [33]. Oxygen consumption was measured using a Seahorse XFp analyzer (Agilent, USA) with 3 mg protein/well. Two assay paradigms were employed: Coupling assays assessed State 3ADP respiration using 10 mM succinate (+2 mM rotenone) with sequential additions of 4 mM ADP, 3 mM oligomycin, 4 mM FCCP, and 4 mM antimycin A; Electron flow assays evaluated Complex I activity with 10 mM pyruvate/2 mM malate, followed by 2 mM rotenone, 10 mM succinate, 4 mM antimycin A, and 10 mM ascorbate/0.1 mM TMPD to probe Complexes II-IV function, as previously describe [34].

Dihydroethidium (DHE) staining

Tissue samples were cry sectioned at 12 μm thickness, and incubated with DHE (2 μmol/L) to detect superoxide production. Specificity was confirmed by pre-treatment with PEG-SOD.Fluorescence imaging was performed using a microscope (Olympus, Japan) with a 20 × objective. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity was conducted using ImageJ software to determine average superoxide levels.

ATP content measurement

Hippocampal tissues were homogenized on ice (Q55 QSONICA homogenizer, 30 s) and ATP levels were determined using a bioluminescent assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Samples were mixed with assay reagent and incubated for 3 min before luminescence measurement (Synergy HT microplate reader, BioTek, USA). ATP concentrations were determined from a standard curve.

Mitochondrial transmembrane potential (MMP) assay

Hippocampal mitochondria were isolated using a commercial separation kit (Beyotime, China) and incubated with JC-1 dye (5 μg/mL) in 96-well plates (37 °C, 30 min, dark) [35]. Fluorescence was measured at 560/595 nm (J-aggregates) and 485/535 nm (monomers) using a microplate reader (SpectraMax Paradigm). MMP was expressed as the ratio of J-aggregate to monomer fluorescence.

mtDNA copy number quantification

Total DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) following standard protocols [36]. DNA samples were combined with SYBR Green (Thermo Fisher, USA) and target-specific primers for quantitative PCR analysis. Amplification reactions were performed using established primer sequences, with DNA content determined through standard curves generated from serial dilutions of mtDNA and nucDNA reference samples. Relative quantification was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The sequences of genes primer pairs were listed as follows:

mtDNAF:CTTTACGAGCCGTAGCCCAAR:GTATGGTGGTACTCCCGCTGnucDNAF:CGTCTGCCCTATCAACTTTR:CCTCGAAAGAGTCCTGTATTG

Electrophysiology

Following isoflurane anesthesia, mice were perfused with 30 ml of oxygenated ice-cold dissection buffer (in mM: 110 choline chloride, 25 NaHCO3, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 0.5 CaCl2, 7 MgSO4, 25 d-glucose, 11.6 sodium ascorbate, 3.1 sodium pyruvate). Hippocampal slices were recovered in oxygenated ACSF (in mM: 126 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 26 NaHCO3, 2 CaCl2, 2 MgCl2, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 10 d-glucose) at 35 °C for 30 min before room temperature storage. For sEPSC recordings, slices were transferred to a perfusion chamber on an Olympus microscope stage, with electrodes filled with internal solution (in mM: 130 K-gluconate, 10 HEPES, 4 KCl, 4 Mg2+-ATP, 2 NaCl, 0.3 Na3GTP; pH 7.2, 295-300 mOsm).

ER-mitochondria colocalization test

Primary neurons cultured in glass-bottom dishes (WHB) were labeled with 20 nM MitoTracker Red and 100 nM ER-Tracker Green (Thermo Fisher, USA) for 20-30 min at 37 °C, followed by 5 μM Hoechst 33342 nuclear staining for 10 min. After washing with live-cell imaging solution, cells were imaged using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope with a 63 × oil objective. Colocalization analysis was performed using Leica LAS and ImageJ software.

Mitochondrial Ca2+ signaling analysis

Primary neurons cultured in 12-well plates were treated with CORT in the bathing medium. To measure mitochondrial calcium, cells were loaded with 5 μM Rhod-2 AM (YEASEN, Shanghai, China) in Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS; Corning, New York, USA) and incubated in the dark at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 30 min. Following two washes with HBSS, the cells were further incubated in fresh HBSS at room temperature for 30 min. Prior to imaging, the cells were maintained in HBSS. Fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (CKX53, OLYMPUS, Shinjuku, Japan) with an excitation/emission wavelength of 549/578 nm. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Lyso-Tracker staining

Lysosomes were labeled with Lyso-Tracker Red (Beyotime, China). Following treatment, primary neurons cultured on sterile dishes were incubated with 50 nM LysoTracker Red at 37 °C for 15–30 min. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove unbound dye. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Olympus, Japan).

3D reconstruction

For spine quantification, lentivirus-infected dendritic segments (50-150 μm from soma) were randomly selected and imaged at 250 × total magnification (100 × objective with 2.5 × zoom). Z-stacks were acquired at 0.39 μm intervals and reconstructed in Imaris 9.0 (Bitplane), where spine density and morphology were quantified using the surface rendering module. Immunofluorescence data were similarly processed for colocalization analysis through surface reconstruction.

Molecular docking

The three-dimensional structures of Drp1 (UniProt: O00429), GRP75 (UniProt: P38646), and VDAC1 (UniProt: P21796) were retrieved from the AlphaFold database. Structural preparation, including hydrogen addition and charge distribution, will be carried out in Discovery Studio 2023. Docking simulation was performed using the LibDock module, where the GTPase domain of Drp1 was defined as the receptor site, while the substrate binding domain of GRP75 and the N-terminal region of VDAC1 were designated as the ligand interface. Adopting a “high-quality” docking mode, ranking is based on Docking Score and Confidence Score scores. Conducted three independent runs to ensure repeatability.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Proteomic data were analyzed using Spectronaut (v17.0) for spectral library matching and quantitative processing, followed by statistical evaluation in Perseus (v2.0.6.0) with log2 transformation, significance testing (t-test, p < 0.05), and fold-change filtering (≥1.5). Proteins were identified against UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot (2023_03) and functionally annotated through Metascape using GO, KEGG, and Reactome databases (FDR<0.05). All statistical comparisons were conducted in GraphPad Prism 8.0 using t-tests or ANOVA as experimental design dictated, with significance set at p < 0.05. Data represent mean ± SEM unless noted.

Results

CSDS induces MERCs remodeling and impairs autophagy in hippocampal neurons

To investigate proteomic changes in the hippocampus following stress exposure, we subjected mice to CSDS and performed 4D-FastDIA-based quantitative proteomics (Fig. 1A). CSDS elicited depression-like behaviors, including social avoidance, anhedonia, and behavioral despair (Fig. 1B and C). Proteomic analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes were enriched in pathways related to mitochondrial function, oxidative phosphorylation, and autophagy (Fig. 1D, E, F, Fig. S1A). Seahorse XF analysis demonstrated impaired mitochondrial respiration in the hippocampus of CSDS-exposed mice (Fig. 1G). Consistent with these findings, we observed elevated ROS levels, reduced ATP content, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), and decreased mtDNA copy number (Figs. S1B, C, D, E).

Given the established link between mitochondrial function and MERCs [37], we next examined mitochondrial ultrastructure in hippocampal neurons using TEM. CSDS induces morphological changes in CA1 neuron mitochondria, such as a significant decrease in perimeter or area and a significant reduction in distance between mitochondria and ER, while the ratio of ERMICC (the ratio of MERC length to product of mitochondrial perimeter and MERC distance) increases (Fig. 1H), indicating enhanced ER mitochondrial contact. In contrast, no significant changes were observed in the DG or CA3 regions (Fig. S1F). Proximity Ligation Assay (PLA) further confirmed an increase in the IP3R3-GRP75-VDAC1 complex in the CA1 region (Fig. 1I). Concurrently, TEM revealed a decrease in autophagosome number (Fig. 1J), and WB analysis indicated dysregulation of mitophagy-related proteins (Fig. 1K). In the foot-shock-induced depression model in female mice (Fig. S2A and B), stress stimulation was also found to activate the mitophagy signaling pathway (Fig. S2C). These results suggest that CSDS-induced mitochondrial dysfunction may contribute to depressive-like behaviors through remodeling of MERCs architecture and impaired mitophagy.

Drp1 activation mediates hippocampal neuronal damage in CSDS mice

Further analysis of MERCs regulatory proteins revealed that Dnm1l/Drp1 expression was significantly increased in CSDS-exposed mice confirmed by proteomic analysis and Western blotting (Fig. 2A and B, Fig. S2D). At the same time, we used corticosterone (CORT, a classic depression in vitro model) [38] treatment in primary neurons in vitro, and CHX tracking experiment found that CORT administration could inhibit the degradation of Drp1 caused by CHX over time (Fig. S3A). In order to further explore the mechanism of Drp1 increase, we also used CO-IP to detect the ubiquitination of drp1, and found that CSDS and CORT can reduce the ubiquitination level of Drp1 in vivo and in vitro (Fig. 2B, Fig. S3B).

In addition, the expression levels of mitochondrial fusion proteins were assessed using proteomic data and further validated in hippocampal tissue (Fig. S4A and B). Immunofluorescence analysis showed prominent Drp1 co-localization with neurons, with minimal signal in oligodendrocytes, microglia, and astrocytes (Fig. 2C, Fig. S5). And the dendritic spines of CA1 neurons also undergo changes (Fig. 2D). Simultaneously, Drp1 was primarily co-localized with Vglut2-positive neurons (Fig. 2E, Fig. S6).

To assess the functional role of Drp1, we injected a Cre-dependent Dnml1 knockdown AAV into the CA1 of Vglut2-Cre mice (Fig. 3A and B). Drp1 knockdown significantly ameliorated CSDS-induced depressive-like behaviors in the SPT and FST (Fig. 3C). Conversely, Drp1 overexpression in the CA1, combined with a sub-SDS paradigm [39] (Fig. 4A and B), enhanced stress susceptibility, leading to social avoidance, anhedonia, and increased immobility (Fig. 4C). Electrophysiological recordings from Vglut2+ neurons in the CA1 revealed that CSDS significantly reduced sEPSC frequency, an effect rescued by Dnm1l knockdown (Fig. 3D). These results indicate that Drp1 activation is critical for stress-induced synaptic deficits in Vglut2 neurons.

We also assessed autophagic activity. WB and TEM analyses indicated that Drp1 knockdown restored mitophagy, which was impaired by CSDS (Figs. S7B, C, Figure 6D). Immunofluorescence for the autophagosome marker LAMP1 confirmed reduced autophagic flux in Vglut2 neurons after CSDS, which was rescued by Drp1 knockdown (Fig. 6E). Disruption of MERCs attenuates Drp1-dependent depressive-like behaviors and autophagy deficits

It is well known that Glucose regulated protein 75 (GRP75) functions as a critical adaptor, bridging the mitochondrial outer membrane protein VDAC1 with the ER protein IP3R3 to form a stable complex essential for the establishment of functional MERCs [18,40]. To directly assess the role of MERCs, we generated Vglut2 neuron-specific GRP75 knockout mice (Fig. 7A, Fig. S8A and B). Compared to controls, GRP75 knockout mice exposed to CSDS exhibited reduced depressive-like behaviors, including improved social interaction, sucrose preference, and decreased immobility (Fig. 7B and C). TEM confirmed that GRP75 deletion normalized mitochondrial perimeter, mitochondrial area, ER-mitochondria contacts and ERMICC ratio (Fig. 7D). Electrophysiological and mitochondrial respiration analyses showed that GRP75 knockout improved synaptic function (Fig. 7E) and restored the mitochondrial energy metabolism (Fig. 7F) and ROS levels (Fig. S8C). Furthermore, GRP75 knockout enhanced autophagy in neurons (Fig. 7G, H, Fig. S8D). These results highlight the importance of MERC structural integrity in maintaining mitochondrial function and neuronal resilience under stress.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that Drp1 activation triggers extensive remodeling of mitochondrial-ER contact sites (MERCs) architecture, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired autophagy, and subsequent depressive-like behaviors. Disrupting the MERC tethering protein GRP75 or pharmacologically enhancing mitophagy with idebenone attenuated Drp1-mediated neuronal deficits and alleviated depressive phenotypes. These findings reveal a novel mechanism whereby Drp1 regulates MERC integrity in depression pathogenesis, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting MERC dynamics and autophagy. Accumulating evidence implicates aberrant MERCs in neurological disorders, including Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and depression [19,22,45,46]. MERCs regulate essential processes such as calcium signaling, lipid metabolism, ER stress, and mitochondrial quality control [47,48]. Under stress conditions, MERC dysregulation can trigger neuronal apoptosis and contribute to neurodegeneration [49]. In line with previous observations [22], we found extensive mitochondrial damage and MERC enrichment in hippocampal CA1 neurons of CSDS-exposed mice, accompanied by Drp1 activation. Although Drp1 dysregulation has been reported in other neurological conditions [50,51], its role in depression-associated mitochondrial pathology remains underexplored.

Our study revealed an upregulation of Drp1 protein in the CSDS model, indicating abnormal mitochondrial fission. This finding warrants further investigation given the potential functional interplay between Drp1 and MERCs. Previous evidence suggests that Drp1 can be recruited to mitochondria through direct interaction with VDAC1, which is localized on the mitochondrial side of MERCs [[52], [53], [54]]. Therefore, Drp1 may form a polymeric complex on the mitochondrial membrane (Molecular docking reveals that Drp1 has interaction sites with GRP75 or VDAC1, Fig. S10), which can serve as a structural core, actively recruit endoplasmic reticulum components, and promote the maturation of MERC. Our functional experiments showed that inhibiting Drp1 activity not only blocked mitochondrial fission, but also significantly reduced MERC formation, suggesting that Drp1 is necessary for maintaining such contact. The post-translational regulation behind the increase of drp1 protein level after stress stimulation has also been preliminarily explored. It has been found that stress stimulation reduces the ubiquitination level of Drp1, resulting in abnormal mitochondrial function of neurons, which is consistent with previous research reports. In Parkinson's disease (PD), it plays a neuroprotective role by increasing the ubiquitination level of the protein [55], which also provides a new strategy for the treatment of nervous system diseases.

Mitochondrial fission, mediated by Drp1, is a prerequisite for efficient mitophagy, as it facilitates the segregation and subsequent targeting of damaged mitochondrial segments for autophagic clearance [[56], [57], [58]]. In our study, Drp1 knockdown likely hindered this fragmentation, impairing the physical separation of dysfunctional components and ultimately compromising mitophagic flux. This deficit is particularly critical under stress conditions such as CSDS, where mitochondrial quality control is essential. Notably, autophagy—a key process supported by MERCs [59,60]—is vital for neuronal survival [61], and impaired mitophagy has been implicated in depression [62,63]. Our findings confirm that autophagy participates in Drp1-mediated depressive behaviors and demonstrate that IDBN rescues CSDS-induced mitophagy deficits, thereby underscoring the functional linkage between Drp1-dependent fission, mitophagic efficiency, and behavioral outcomes.

GRP75, a mitochondrial chaperone, has diverse cellular functions [[64], [65], [66]]. Additionally, our study investigated how changes in MERC distance may affect cellular signaling. TEM and PLA data showed shortened ER-mitochondria contact in CSDS microglia, along with increased IP3R3-GRP75-VDAC1 complex formation. This closer apposition could facilitate more efficient ER-to-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer, resulting in mitochondrial Ca2+ overload. Excess mitochondrial Ca2+ may then reduce membrane potential, increase ROS, and further upregulate Drp1, promoting mitochondrial fragmentation and dysfunction. This cascade—linking MERC structural changes to Ca2+ dysregulation and impaired mitochondrial dynamics—helps explain the mitochondrial and neuronal alterations seen in CSDS. By generating Vglut2-specific Hspa9 knockout mice, we identified a protective role for GRP75 deletion in stress-induced neuronal dysfunction. Disrupting the IP3R3-GRP75-VDAC1 tether improved MERC structure, mitochondrial respiration, and synaptic function.

Oxidative stress, resulting from mutant SOD1-induced ROS, impairs MERC-mediated oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) [67,68], suggesting that MERC structural changes can drive oxidative damage. We found that CSDS induced oxidative stress in CA1 neurons alongside MERC remodeling, both of which were reversed by idebenone. In the future, we will conduct further research to dissect the precise mechanism of action of IDBN. Although the function of MERCs still requires further investigation, our research results provide groundbreaking evidence for MERC-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction in depression.

In summary, ER-mitochondria communication is increasingly recognized in neurological disorders [69,70]. MERCs, abundant in neurons, are implicated in depression through functional dysregulation [71]. Drp1 is required for MERC integrity, with inhibition reducing MERC formation and enhanced fission increasing MERC abundance [72]. We obtained similar results in Vglut2 neurons of CSDS mice, proposing a novel mechanistic insight: in stressed neurons, Drp1 activation drives mitochondrial fission and MERC remodeling, contributing to depression pathogenesis. This study analyzed stressed mice as a cohort without separating “susceptible” and “resilient” subgroups, potentially diluting inter-group differences. Future work will investigate stress susceptibility subgroups.

This study identifies profound mitochondrial pathology in neurons during depression development. Drp1 upregulation leads to mitochondrial damage, MERC restructuring, and impaired mitophagy, ultimately disrupting synaptic transmission. Targeting neuronal ER-mitochondria contacts represents a promising therapeutic strategy for depression, offering valuable insights for future treatment development.
