Urticaria as a dermatologic manifestation of Giardia infection: a systematic review of clinical, diagnostic, and therapeutic features
Authors: Javad Mahdavi, Kimiya Ahmadifar, Reza Ghasemikhah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGiardiasis is recognized as the most prevalent enteric protozoal infection worldwide. Although gastrointestinal symptoms are the most common manifestations of giardiasis, several studies have reported cases of urticaria associated with this infection. Urticaria is a common mast cell-dependent disorder characterized by wheals, angioedema, or both. The aim of this study was to conduct the first systematic review to comprehensively synthesize the clinical, diagnostic, and therapeutic features of urticaria as a dermatologic manifestation in patients with giardiasis.MethodsThis review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar were searched without language restrictions up to 2025 to identify articles reporting patients with urticaria and giardiasis.ResultsThirteen papers describing a total of 23 patients (14 males and 9 females, aged 4 to 56 years) were included. The locations of urticarial lesions varied, involving the head, trunk, and extremities. The duration of urticaria ranged from 8 h to 8 months. Other clinical manifestations reported in the patients included diarrhea, pruritus, abdominal distension, arthropathy, anorexia, headache, vomiting, abdominal pain, and dysphagia. Increased Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP), elevated Immunoglobulin E (IgE), eosinophilia, stool examination, and Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) were among the diagnostic tests utilized. Metronidazole, tinidazole, and quinacrine hydrochloride were the most commonly prescribed medications for treatment.ConclusionsOur review highlights the diversity of clinical presentations and diagnostic approaches, and, for the first time, provides a consolidated resource for clinicians encountering this uncommon extraintestinal manifestation of giardiasis. In the initial evaluation of patients presenting with urticaria and a history of gastrointestinal symptoms, particularly those residing in areas with poor hygiene, potential exposure to contaminated water, or recent travel to such regions, parasitic infections, especially giardiasis, should be considered among the differential diagnoses. By addressing this under-recognized association, our study fills a significant knowledge gap regarding urticaria associated with giardiasis, ultimately facilitating earlier diagnosis, appropriate treatment, and improved patient prognosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11484-3.

Introduction

Giardiasis is an intestinal infection caused by the flagellated zoonotic protozoan parasite Giardia lamblia (syn. G. duodenalis, G. intestinalis) [1]. It is recognized as the most common enteric protozoal infection worldwide, affecting about 8% of children and 2% of adults in developed countries, while an estimated one-third of people in developing countries have experienced giardiasis [2].

The clinical presentation and severity of the manifestation of giardiasis vary widely. Although approximately 50–75% of infected individuals are asymptomatic, there is usually an incubation period of 1 to 3 weeks in those who develop symptoms, which may include diarrhea, abdominal cramps, weakness or malaise, nausea, weight loss, anorexia, abdominal distension, flatulence, vomiting, and fever [3, 4].

Giardiasis can lead to various complications, such as functional dyspepsia, post-infectious irritable bowel syndrome, growth retardation, impaired cognitive function, myopathy, and reactive arthritis [5]. Additionally, one of the other extraintestinal manifestations reported in Giardia infection is urticaria [6–18].

Urticaria is a common inflammatory allergic disorder that presents with wheals (hives), angioedema, or both. Its lifetime incidence is estimated to be up to 20% worldwide [19]. Wheals are skin lesions ranging in size from a few millimeters to several centimeters, appearing as round or irregular shapes with central edema surrounded by erythema. They are commonly pruritic and transient, resolving within 24 h without sequelae [20, 21]. Angioedema is a sudden localized edema of the deep dermis, subcutaneous tissue, or mucous membrane, usually subsiding within 72 h. It is accompanied by sensations of tingling, tightness, burning, pain, and, less commonly, itching [19, 22].

Urticaria is sub-categorized, based on its duration, into two types: acute and chronic. Acute urticaria lasts six weeks or less, while chronic urticaria persists for more than six weeks. In addition, urticaria can be classified as spontaneous, with no identifiable trigger, or inducible, in which a specific eliciting factor, such as cold, heat, vibration, pressure, and UV/visible light, is required [22].

Infections are among the factors that cause or aggravate urticaria. The association between various pathogens, including bacteria, viruses, parasites, and fungi, and both acute urticaria and chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) has been described in articles [22, 23].

The aim of this study was to conduct the first systematic review to comprehensively synthesize the clinical, diagnostic, and therapeutic features of urticaria as a dermatologic manifestation in patients with giardiasis. This review addresses a significant gap in the literature regarding the spectrum and management of urticaria associated with giardiasis.

Methods

Search strategy

This review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and ScienceDirect databases were searched using combinations of the following key terms: “urticaria,” “urticarial lesions,” “hives,” “wheals,” “angioedema,” “Giardia,” “Giardia lamblia,” and “giardiasis,” without language restrictions up to March 2024. Google Scholar was also used to find further relevant studies. The detailed search strategy, including all search terms and databases, is provided in Supplementary Material 1. Furthermore, reference lists of studies meeting the inclusion criteria were manually screened and included when appropriate. Finally, the search was updated in February 2025.

Study selection

All articles identified through the aforementioned sources were imported into EndNote 20.2.1 (Bld 15749) software, where duplicate publications were automatically recognized, manually checked, and finally excluded. Two researchers (KA and JM) independently screened titles and abstracts to exclude papers that did not meet the inclusion criteria. Also, all papers considered likely to contain relevant data were reviewed in full to assess eligibility. Any discrepancies regarding the included studies were resolved by a third researcher (RGH). Additional studies were identified through manual screening of the reference lists of included papers. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklists, which are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Data extraction and data items

Relevant data were extracted by one researcher (KA) and independently verified for accuracy by another researcher (JM). Any discrepancies in data extraction were resolved by a third researcher (RGH). The following information was collected from the included studies: first author, year of publication, number of patients, age and sex of patients, clinical manifestations, location of urticaria, duration of urticaria, past allergic history, history of recent travel, paraclinical findings, and prescribed medications.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria consisted of studies including case reports, case series, cohort studies, editorial letters, and conference abstracts describing urticaria in patients with giardiasis worldwide up to 2025. There were no restrictions on language or study location. Exclusion criteria included duplicate publications, studies that did not involve both urticaria and giardiasis, and studies that lacked sufficient data. Insufficient data was defined as the absence of diagnostic criteria for either condition or inadequate clinical details to assess a potential relationship.

Results

After searching the mentioned sources, 2359 studies were identified. Following a review based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 13 papers describing 23 patients (14 males and 9 females, aged 4 to 56) were included in this study (Figs. 1 and 2).

Discussion

According to our study, urticarial lesions associated with giardiasis are typically generalized. They may appear on various areas of the head, trunk, and extremities, including the face, scalp, abdomen, back, chest, legs, hands, arms, forearms, and thighs. In addition, giardiasis can be associated with both acute and chronic urticaria, with a relatively high prevalence of the chronic type. The shortest duration of urticaria was just 8 h, reported in the youngest patient who was 6 years old, while the longest duration was 8 months in a 47-year-old man who had no gastrointestinal symptoms [10, 15].

The predominance of chronic urticaria in patients with giardiasis may be preliminarily related to the prolonged nature of the infection. Giardiasis often presents with mild or asymptomatic gastrointestinal symptoms, which can lead to delayed or missed diagnosis. Moreover, its gastrointestinal manifestations are frequently subtle or mistaken for other conditions, further postponing appropriate treatment. This persistent infection sustains ongoing immune activation, such as elevated IgE levels and mast cell degranulation, thereby promoting chronic rather than acute urticaria.

The coexistence of gastrointestinal symptoms and urticarial lesions should prompt clinicians to consider parasitic infections, particularly giardiasis, as a possible underlying factor in patients presenting with urticaria. In this study, the most prevalent gastrointestinal symptoms were diarrhea and abdominal distension, followed by anorexia, vomiting, abdominal pain, dysphagia, weight loss, constipation, epigastric distress, and food intolerance. Additional symptoms included pruritus, arthralgia, headache, fever, myalgia, polyarthritis, generalized hyperemia, rhinorrhea, and dry cough.

To confirm the diagnosis, laboratory tests should be performed. Definitive diagnosis is established by detecting Giardia trophozoites or cysts in stool samples through microscopic examination. Techniques such as direct fecal smear and formalin-ether sedimentation are rapid and cost-effective compared to other diagnostic methods. Given the intermittent excretion of Giardia, increasing the frequency of microscopic examination at different times reduces the likelihood of false-negative results.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and direct fluorescent antibody (DFA) tests are other techniques with high sensitivity and specificity that can identify soluble antigens and intact organisms in stool samples, respectively. These serological tests are useful for clinically suspected cases when the causative organism cannot be detected by conventional methods, as well as for monitoring treatment response and conducting epidemiological research [3, 24]. In antibody assay methods, immunoglobulins may not be detected in immunodeficient patients or in cases of acute urticaria [13, 25]. Additionally, elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), increased C-reactive protein (CRP), and leukocytosis, particularly eosinophilia, are other nonspecific laboratory findings.

In this study, eosinophilia ranged from 0 to 75%, with the highest value of 75% observed in a 10-year-old girl with a history of acute lymphoblastic leukemia and a white blood cell (WBC) count of 30,000/µL [12]. Additionally, the highest reported immunoglobulin E (IgE) level was 190 U/ml in a patient exhibiting only 5% eosinophilia, and the highest ESR was 117 mm/h, corresponding to a patient with an IgE level of 7 IU/ml [9, 15].

Urticaria associated with parasitic infections has been reported with various parasites such as Blastocystis hominis, Toxocara canis, Anisakis simplex, Microsporidia, and Strongyloides stercoralis [26–30]. Although the exact mechanism of pathogenesis linking parasites and urticaria is not fully defined, some studies have explored this potential relationship.

It is widely recognized that the primary processes involved in the development of urticaria include the activation and degranulation of mast cells and basophils. This leads to the release of vasoactive compounds, including histamine, which binds to H1 receptors on cutaneous blood vessels. The resulting interaction causes vasodilation and fluid leakage into the dermis, ultimately leading to edema and urticarial lesion formation. Various stimuli can trigger mast cells (MCs) and basophils, one of which is the interaction of antigen-specific IgE with high-affinity IgE receptors (FcεRI). Since parasitic infections can increase serum IgE levels by up to 100-fold (both specific antiparasitic IgE and polyclonal IgE), it has been hypothesized that specific IgE against parasites may play a significant role in the onset of urticaria in individuals with parasitic infections [31, 32].

In a study conducted by Oteifa et al., children with urticaria who tested positive for Toxocara were found to have significantly higher serum IgE levels compared to the control group. Another study showed a significant correlation between specific anti-Echinococcus IgE and urticaria. Furthermore, research by Mahmoud and colleagues demonstrated that patients with giardiasis and urticaria exhibited notable elevation in specific anti-Giardia IgE when compared to giardiasis patients without urticarial lesions [33–35]. However, the results of this study suggest that urticaria associated with parasitic infections is not always attributable to increased IgE levels, as some patients presented with generalized urticaria affecting various body surfaces, including the face, trunk, and extremities, often with large sizes, despite normal IgE concentrations [14].

Another substance that activates mast cells is T helper 2 (Th2) cytokines. Parasitic infections may induce the release of Th2-initiating cytokines, known as alarmins, including interleukin-25 (IL-25), interleukin-33 (IL-33), and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), which lead to Th2 cytokine production and, ultimately, mast cell activation [36, 37]. Additionally, eosinophilia in parasitic infections has been suggested to play a role in urticaria development, as eosinophils release various substances like major basic protein (MBP) and eosinophil peroxidase (EPO), which can trigger mast cell and basophil degranulation and promote edema [38, 39]. Histologic studies have demonstrated the presence of eosinophils and extracellular eosinophil granule proteins in urticarial lesions [40]. In one study, extracellular eosinophil granule proteins were detected in 43–60% of skin biopsy samples from patients with chronic urticaria [41].

Collectively, these immunologic and pathophysiologic pathways represent proposed mechanisms that may explain the observed association between Giardia infection and urticaria. Although these hypotheses are supported by some clinical and laboratory observations, further research is necessary to define causal relationships and clarify the precise biological mechanisms involved.

It should be noted that in some studies, patients with giardiasis also had other concurrent infections or allergic conditions, which could potentially confound the attribution of urticaria to Giardia infection. However, an important observation across these studies is that urticarial symptoms consistently resolved following successful treatment of giardiasis. This resolution, after targeted anti-Giardia therapy, suggests a stronger and more direct association between giardiasis and urticaria than with other infections or allergic disorders that may coexist. Nevertheless, the presence of co-infections and allergic conditions underscores the necessity for careful clinical and paraclinical evaluation. In addition, more comprehensive and detailed studies are essential to thoroughly investigate these concurrent infections and allergic diseases, with particular emphasis on their pathophysiological and immunological mechanisms, as well as the severity and duration of symptoms.

The results of this study indicate that metronidazole has been widely used and found effective in most patients. In addition, other medications, including tinidazole, quinacrine hydrochloride, furazolidone, nifuratel, and co-trimoxazole, were prescribed either as monotherapy or in combination with metronidazole, yielding beneficial outcomes. In all reviewed cases, urticaria resolved following successful treatment of Giardia infection, highlighting a consistent association between parasite eradication and improvement in urticarial symptoms.

The limitations of this study include potential underdiagnosis of urticaria associated with giardiasis, as some patients with urticaria may not have been evaluated for giardiasis, possibly leading to misclassification of cases as idiopathic or attributed to other causes. Although a comprehensive search was conducted across multiple databases without language restrictions, publication and language biases cannot be fully excluded, as unpublished, non-indexed, or less accessible language articles may have been missed. Furthermore, several included studies lacked detailed clinical and paraclinical data, which may have limited a deeper understanding of the association between urticaria and giardiasis.

Conclusion

In patients presenting with urticaria accompanied by gastrointestinal symptoms such as diarrhea, abdominal distension, anorexia, and vomiting, particularly those residing in areas with a low level of hygiene, using contaminated water, or with recent travel to such regions, parasitic infections, particularly giardiasis, should be considered as one of the differential diagnoses during initial investigations. Moreover, a thorough clinical assessment, including evaluation of exposure risks, allergic history, medication use, travel history, and systemic symptoms such as fever, joint pain, or weight loss, is essential to help differentiate urticaria associated with giardiasis from other causes. A combination of stool microscopy and antigen-based assays is the most effective and practical approach to diagnosing giardiasis in most patients. In specific or complex cases, DNA-based tests may be used.

It is important to note that early diagnosis in these patients is crucial, because it enables timely therapeutic interventions, which may prevent unnecessary and costly diagnostic procedures, inappropriate treatments, and potential complications.
