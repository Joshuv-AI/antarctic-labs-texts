Assessing the Independent and Combined Effects of Hyperosmolarity and Inflammatory Stress on Human Corneal Epithelial Cells
Authors: Deeksha Prasad, Mohammad Gufran Siddiqui, Mahi Al Ahmad Dar, Rohini Sonar, Swati Singh, Pragnya Rao Donthineni, Sayan Basu, Vivek Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeInflammation and hyperosmolarity drive dry eye disease (DED) by inducing oxidative stress, apoptosis, and immune activation, disrupting tear film homeostasis and epithelial integrity. This study aimed to evaluate the independent and combined effects of hyperosmolarity and inflammatory stress on human corneal epithelial (HCE) cells.MethodsHCE cells were exposed to hyperosmolar conditions using sodium chloride, lipopolysaccharide, or a combination of both for 12, 24, and 48 hours. The effects on cell adhesion, cell proliferation, cytokine secretion, and gene expression were analyzed. Expression of genes and inflammatory cytokines was evaluated for stress response pathways and antioxidant marker regulation.ResultsLPS exposure alone significantly increased inflammatory cytokine secretion at 24 hours (e.g. IL-8 = 2481.4 ± 32.5, P < 0.001; IL-17 = 1095 ± 32, P < 0.01) but did not affect adhesion. The combination of LPS and hyperosmolar stress exacerbated cellular damage, with increased levels of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and TLR2 and 4. Gene expression analysis revealed upregulation of stress response genes (HMOX-1 = 13 ± 1.5, P < 0.001; COX2 = 2.2 ± 0.1, P < 0.001; and TonEBP = 4.7 ± 1.7, P < 0.001) and downregulation of antioxidant markers (SOD1 = 0.5 ± 0.0, P < 0.001). Cytokine and oxidative stress marker secretion (IL-2, IL-8, IL-12, IL-17, IL-23, HMOX-1, COX2, and SOD1) peaked at 48 hours under hyperosmolar and combined stress conditions.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates for the first time the combined effect of hyperosmolarity and inflammation that disrupts corneal epithelial homeostasis in vitro, leading to increased oxidative stress by expressing HMOX1, COX2, TonEBP, HIF1, and inflammatory cytokine production. These findings provide further insights into the pathophysiology of DED and support the development of an in vitro model that mitigates hyperosmolar and inflammatory stress-induced epithelial defects.

Dry eye disease (DED) is a multifactorial ocular surface disorder affecting millions of people globally, with a reported prevalence of 5% to 50% according to the Tear Film and Ocular Surface Society (TFOS) Dry Eye Workshop (DEWS)-II report.1 This condition is characterized by tear film instability, hyperosmolarity, and inflammation, leading to symptoms of ocular discomfort, visual disturbance, and a significant reduction in quality of life.2

Tear hyperosmolarity is a hallmark characteristic feature of DED and serves as both a diagnostic parameter and key driver of disease progression.3 It arises from reduced tear secretion, increased evaporation, or a combination of both, leading to osmotic stress in the ocular surface epithelium. This stress induces detrimental cellular responses in corneal epithelial cells, including cell shrinkage, oxidative stress, and activation of inflammatory pathways such as mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) and nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB).4,5 These pathways promote apoptosis and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-αA), and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), which disrupt epithelial homeostasis and exacerbates ocular surface damage.6

Chronic inflammation further amplifies the effects of hyperosmolar stress, creating a self-perpetuating damage cycle. Hyperosmolarity activates toll-like receptors (TLRs), initiating innate immune responses and recruiting inflammatory cells to the ocular surface.7,8 Elevated levels of pro-inflammatory markers in the tears of patients with DED underscore their clinical relevance and potential as therapeutic targets.9,10 In vitro models, particularly human corneal epithelial (HCE) cells, have been pivotal in elucidating the pathophysiology of DED. These models have demonstrated that hyperosmolar or inflammatory stress induces morphological changes, apoptosis, and cytokine secretion, mirroring the clinical manifestations of DED.11,12 Furthermore, HCE cell models have been utilized to evaluate therapeutic interventions, such as osmoprotectants and anti-inflammatory agents, which have shown promise in mitigating hyperosmolarity-induced damage.13,14 Despite the well-established interplay between hyperosmolarity and inflammation in DED, where hyperosmolarity triggers inflammatory responses and inflammation exacerbates tear film instability, the combined impact of these stressors on cellular and molecular mechanisms in an in vitro ocular surface model are less studied. Therefore, this study aimed to mimic the multifactorial stress environment of DED by evaluating the effects of hyperosmolarity and exogenous inflammatory stimuli, both individually and in combination, on HCE cells. LPS was selected to simulate microbial pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) acting through the TLR4 pathway, which is known to be upregulated under osmotic stress. By combining these stimuli, we aimed to develop a more physiologically relevant in vitro model reflective of the complex inflammatory landscape observed in moderate-to-severe DED.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

Human corneal epithelial cell line (HCE-2 [50.B1], American Type Culture Collection [ATCC]) between passages 13 and 20 were cultured at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator with DMEM/F12 (D9785; Sigma, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; S11150H, R&D), 10 ng/mL human epidermal growth factor (EGF; PHG0311L; Thermo Fisher, USA), 5 µg/mL insulin (12585014; Thermo Fisher, USA), and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic solution (15240062; Thermo Fisher, USA). Prior to treatment with sodium chloride (NaCl) or LPS, the cultures were replaced with DMEM/F12 without FBS, and cells with no treatment in DMEM/F12 without FBS were used as the control group throughout the study.

Hyperosmolar Stress in Human Corneal Epithelial Cells

Cells were incubated with DMEM/F12 without FBS prior to treatment. Cells in standard osmolarity (312 mOsm) DMEM/F12 without FBS were subjected to hyperosmolar conditions which were created by dissolving NaCl in sterilized Milli-Q water at room temperature. Osmolarities of 400, 500, and 600 mOsM were attained by adding 20, 25, and 30 mM NaCl, respectively, for 12, 24, and 48 hours, as previously reported by Chen et al., 2008 and Luo et al., 2007. An osmometer (TearLab: model number #200000 REVE, USA) was used to measure the osmolarity of DMEM/F12 without FBS, as depicted in Figure 1.

Experimental design for studying the effects of inflammatory and hyperosmolar stress on HCE. This schematic outlines the workflow for treating HCE cells under different conditions and subsequent characterization assays.

LPS-Induced Inflammation

Sub-confluent HCE cells were transferred to DMEM/F12 without FBS following treatment with LPS, 10 µg/mL (Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany; #L4524) for 12, 24, and 48 hours.

To investigate the combined effect of hyperosmolar conditions and inflammation on HCE cells, the cells were treated with 25 mM NaCl (500 mOsm) and LPS (10 µg/mL), as shown in Figure 1.

Assessment of Cell Viability by MTT

Cell viability was assessed using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. HCE cells were seeded (10,000 cells/cm2) in triplicates on a 96-well plate and incubated overnight 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The cells were treated with LPS, 20, 25, or 30 mM NaCl, or with a combination of 25 mM NaCl and LPS for 12, 24, and 48 hours. Following treatment, the culture medium was carefully aspirated, and 100 µL of 0.5 mg/mL MTT reagent (M6494; Thermo Fisher, USA) prepared in DMEM/F12 without FBS was added to each well and incubated for 1 hour at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Formazan crystals were solubilized in 100 µL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; D2650; Sigma Aldrich, USA) for 5 minutes at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The concentration was determined by measuring the absorbance at 570 nm using a spectrophotometer, against DMSO which is used as a blank.

Cell Detachment Analysis in Response to Inflammatory and Osmotic Stress

To assess cell detachment in response to inflammatory and osmotic stress, HCE cells were seeded in a T-25 flask (100,000 cells/cm2) and incubated under standard culture conditions until complete adherence was achieved. The cells were treated with either LPS or NaCl, or with a combination of LPS and NaCl for 12, 24, and 48 hours. Post-treatment bright-field images were captured using an inverted phase-contrast microscope at 10× magnification to document morphological changes and cell detachment. Image acquisition was performed in randomly selected fields across three biological replicates per condition. For quantification, the acquired images were analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). Cell detachment was determined by measuring the percentage of non-adherent (detached) cells relative to the total area in each field. Thresholding and Particle Analysis tools in ImageJ software were used to segment and quantify the detached cell areas. Data were averaged from three independent experiments and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Assessment of Cell Proliferation Marker Expression and Adherence Junction Proteins

Cells were cultured on coverslips in 12-well culture plates at a density of 20,000 cells/cm2 at 37°C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator for 24 hours. Cells were treated with LPS, NaCl, or a combination of NaCl and LPS for 12, 24, and 48 hours. These cells were assessed for the expression of the nuclear protein Ki67, a cell proliferation marker. The cultured cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 20 minutes, followed by two 10-minute washes with PBS. The cells were permeabilized using 0.03% Triton X-100 in PBS, followed by two 5-minute washes in PBS. The cells were incubated for 45 minutes with 2.5% BSA in PBS to block nonspecific interactions. Indirect immunofluorescence staining was performed, as previously described,15 using primary antibody Ki-67 (1:200 dilution; Invitrogen, MA5-14520), E-cadherin (1:200 dilution; GeneAb, IHC564-100), N-cadherin (1:200 dilution; GeneAb, IHC636-100), and secondary antibody Anti-rabbit Alexa-Fluor 594 (1:400 dilution; A11012, Thermo Fisher, USA) and Anti-mouse Alexa-Fluor 594 (1:400 dilution; A11005, Thermo Fisher, USA). Cells were mounted using Fluorosheild mounting medium with DAPI (ab104139; Abcam, UK) and imaged using a fluorescent microscope (Axio Scope A1 and Zeiss LSM 880; Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) with 40× and 63× objective.

Gene Expression Analysis Using Real‐Time PCR

One million cells from each treatment group at 12, 24, and 48 hours were used to quantify gene expression. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol (15596018; Thermo Fisher, USA), and cDNA was synthesized from one microgram of total RNA using a Superscript IV reverse transcriptase kit (18090010; Thermo Scientific, USA), according to the manufacturer's protocol. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed in triplicate for specific genes (Table 1), using the DyNAmo Color Flash SYBR Green qPCR Kit (F-416L; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and ABI PRISM 7000 Sequence Detection System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Gene expression was normalized to the housekeeping β-actin gene, and the relative fold change was evaluated using the 2ΔΔCT.

Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay

The spent media of the treated cells was collected and stored at −80°C until further processing. Proteins were quantified using a G-Biosciences BCA Protein Assay Kit (cat. no.786-570) according to the manufacturer's protocol. ELISA was performed using Krishgen BioSystems and ELK Biotechnology kits for the following markers: IL-2 (cat. no. KBH12238), IL-8 (cat. no. KB1070), and IL-12 (cat. no. KBH11881), IL-17 (cat. no. KBH0142), IL-23 (cat. no. KB1087), HMOX1 (cat. no ELK1476), SOD1 (cat. no. ELK2709), COX1 (cat. no. ELK5229), and HIF1α (cat. no. ELK1603). Absorbance was measured at a kit-specific wavelength, and the data were analyzed.

Assessment of Cellular Responses in HCE Cells

The cellular stress response was evaluated using a semi-quantitative scoring method in HCE cells exposed to inflammatory and hyperosmotic stress for 24 and 48 hours. Key pathological parameters, including cell detachment, antiproliferative effects, cell death, inflammatory stress, hyperosmotic stress, and oxidative stress, were assessed. Each parameter was assigned a severity score from 1 (least severe) to 4 (most severe). The cumulative stress response was categorized as mild (0–6), moderate (6–12), severe (12–18), or very severe (24) based on the total score. This grading system enables a systematic comparison of stress-induced cellular alterations across different treatment conditions.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate unless otherwise specified. Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 10). Statistical significance was determined using analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Student's t-test, with a P value of ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant and P > 0.05 was considered as nonsignificant. Raw data for all analyses and graphs are provided in Supplementary Data S1a–e. No data were excluded, added, or altered during the course of this study. Correlations among cell viability, adhesion, and proliferation were analyzed via linear regression (Y = mX + b) in GraphPad Prism, with statistical significance determined by P values < 0.05 and variance explained by R².

Results

Effects of Hyperosmolar, Inflammatory, and Combined Stress on Cell Adhesion

To evaluate the effects of hyperosmolarity and inflammatory stress on HCE cells, the cells were treated with LPS, NaCl, or a combination of LPS and NaCl. Representative bright-field images of HCE cells under various treatment conditions are shown in Figures 2a and 2b. The cell adhesion rate was quantified using the ImageJ analysis tool Figure 2c, showing that the control and LPS-treated group cells maintained high adhesion rates (>95%) at all time points, with no significant differences (P > 0.05), indicating that LPS alone did not significantly affect cell adhesion or morphology over time.

In contrast, the treatment group (LPS + NaCl) exhibits visible cell shrinkage, detachment, and increased intercellular spaces (indicated by yellow stars), suggesting cellular stress and impaired adhesion. The second graph illustrates a significant reduction in adhesion at higher osmolarities, particularly at 600 mOsm, with *P < 0.05, ***P < 0.0005, and ###P < 0.0001, indicating statistical significance. The third graph highlights a significant decrease in adhesion in the LPS + NaCl group compared with the control group at 24 and 48 hours. In contrast, hyperosmolar stress induced using NaCl showed a concentration- and time-dependent decline in cell adhesion, that is, loss of cell-to-cell adhesion and increased epithelial instability. At 48 hours, cells treated with 500 and 600 mM NaCl showed a significant reduction in adhesion (83% ± 3.88, P < 0.05 and 79% ± 0.67, P < 0.001) compared with the controls. The most pronounced disruption occurred in cells subjected to the combined LPS and NaCl treatment, resulting in extensive cell detachment and clustering, as highlighted in Figure 2b (yellow-marked areas). This combination significantly reduced adhesion at 24 hours (86% ± 0.61, P < 0.01) and 48 hours (80% ± 3.87, P < 0.001), suggesting that inflammatory stress exacerbates hyperosmolarity-induced cell detachment (see Fig. 2c). NaCl treatment alone (group IV) did not significantly affect adhesion at 24 hours, however, direct comparison between group VI and group IV (see Fig. 2c) revealed a significant reduction in adhesion at both 12 hours (P < 0.05) and 24 hours (P < 0.0005), indicating that LPS and NaCl in combination induce HCE cell detachment at an earlier time point.

To further elucidate the impact of hyperosmolar and inflammatory stress on epithelial cell-cell junctions, we performed immunofluorescence staining for E-cadherin and N-cadherin. As shown in Figure 3, control and LPS-treated HCE cells showed no alteration in the expression of both E and N cadherins. However, treatment with 500 to 600 mOsm NaCl, and more prominently LPS + NaCl, resulted in progressive loss of membrane localization (yellow arrow), disrupted staining patterns, and reduced signal intensity, especially at 24 and 48 hours. These findings suggest that NaCl-induced hyperosmolarity primarily compromises adherens junction integrity, thereby contributing to cell detachment observed in the adhesion assay.

Alterations in Cell Proliferation Under Hyperosmolar and Inflammatory Conditions

Ki-67 immunostaining was performed to assess HCE proliferation under hyperosmolar and inflammatory stress (Figs. 4a, 4b). In the LPS group, a significant difference in cell proliferation was observed at 24 hours (77.8 ± 14.6, P = 0.05) and 48 hours (7.7 ± 2.3, P = 0.0004) compared with the controls. In the NaCl group, at 12 hours, 500 mOsm and 600 mOsm NaCl treatment showed significant difference (86.7 ± 3.4, P = 0.04) and (87.04 ± 2.1, P = 0.006), respectively. At 24 and 48 hours, 400 mOsm (52.5 ± 9.1, P = 0.008) and (42.8 ± 10.8, P = 0.005), 500 mOsm (36.1 ± 8.5, P = 0.003) and (15.1 ± 2.5, P = 0.0002), respectively, and 600 mOsm (8.4 ± 0.1, P = 0.001) and (11.8 ± 0.5, P = 0.001) NaCl exhibited significant differences in cell proliferation, respectively. A significant difference was observed in the combination group from 12 hours (91.3 ± 0.3, P = 0.01) to 48 hours (17.7 ± 1.6, P = 0.0001). These findings suggest that both hyperosmolarity and inflammation significantly influence cell proliferation over time.

Effect of Hyperosmolar, Inflammatory, and Combined Stress on Cell Viability

Cell viability using the MTT assay showed the effect of different treatments on cell viability, where a significant reduction in viability was observed at 24 and 48 hours in the LPS-treated group (81.7 ± 8.6, P = 0.04) and (81.3 ± 10.01, P = 0.001), respectively. However, significant decreases were observed in the 400, 500, and 600 hyperosmolar groups from 12 hours (88.3 ± 4.4, P = 0.003), (81.5 ± 3.2, P = 0.004), and (79.3 ± 3.1, P = 0.0007), respectively, to 48 hours with a gradual and significant decline (80.3 ± 10.01, P = 0.001), (67.5 ± 7.5, P = 0.0001), and (62.9 ± 9.7, P = 0.0001), respectively (Fig. 5a). Pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 5b) revealed a moderate positive correlation between cell viability and proliferation. Cell viability exhibited a significant positive correlation with cell proliferation (r = 0.638, R² = 0.407, P = 0.004), indicating that 40.7% of viability variance was explained by proliferation. Conversely, proliferation showed a stronger correlation (r = 0.756, R² = 0.572, P < 0.001), with 57.2% of its variability attributable to viability, highlighting the strong interdependence between these two cellular processes.

Hyperosmolarity and Inflammatory Stress Regulate Gene Expression in HCE Cells

Gene expression analysis using qPCR showed that LPS and hyperosmotic stress significantly influenced the expression of key inflammatory cytokines, stress response genes, and antioxidant markers in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 6a = bargraph and Fig. 6b = heatmap). The study revealed significant changes in gene expression across various treatment conditions, including LPS, osmotic stress (400 mOsm, 500 mOsm, and 600 mOsm), and the combined treatment of LPS + NaCl evaluated at 12, 24, and 48 hours.

Gene Expression of Inflammatory Markers

The expression of key inflammatory genes, including IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1, was significantly modulated by LPS treatment, osmotic stress, and combined LPS + NaCl conditions (see Figs. 6a–i). LPS treatment induced a robust and time-dependent upregulation of IL-6 expression, with levels peaking at 24 hours (6.40 ± 1.5, P < 0.001) and remaining elevated at 48 hours (4.57 ± 0.3, P < 0.001). Similarly, TNF-α expression was significantly increased at all time points under LPS treatment, with the highest levels observed at 12 hours (2.6 ± 0.6, P < 0.001), whereas IL-1 expression showed a steady increase, peaking at 48 hours (2.3 ± 0.4, P < 0.001). Under osmotic stress, particularly at 500 mOsm, IL-6 expression was significantly elevated at 24 hours (6.3 ± 1.2, P < 0.001), TNF-α at 48 hours (7.5 ± 2.9, P < 0.001), and IL-1 at 24 hours (2.0 ± 0.3, P < 0.001) and 48 hours (2.8 ± 0.1, P < 0.001), indicating that osmotic stress alone can trigger a delayed but potent inflammatory response. The combined LPS + NaCl treatment resulted in a synergistic upregulation of inflammatory genes, with IL-6 levels significantly higher at 24 hours (6.3 ± 1.2, P < 0.001) and 48 hours (5.4 ± 0.3, P < 0.001), TNF-α peaked at 48 hours (7.5 ± 2.9, P < 0.001), and IL-1 reached its highest expression at 48 hours (2.8 ± 0.1, P < 0.001).

Gene Expression of Oxidative and Tonicity Markers

The stress response genes hemeoxygenase-1 (HMOX-1) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX2) were also significantly upregulated under LPS and osmotic stress conditions (Figs. 6j–r). HMOX expression peaked at 48 hours under LPS treatment (2.2 ± 0.1, P < 0.001) and showed a dramatic increase under 500 mOsm osmotic stress at 24 hours (11.0 ± 0.7, P < 0.001) and 48 hours (10.9 ± 1.9, P < 0.001). Similarly, COX2 expression was significantly elevated under osmotic stress, reaching its highest levels at 24 hours (12.7 ± 1.8, P < 0.001) and 48 hours (7.0 ± 2.9, P < 0.001). Combined LPS + NaCl treatment further enhanced the expression of these genes, indicating a synergistic effect. Tonicity-responsive enhancer binding protein (TonEBP), a key regulator of osmotic stress response, under LPS treatment alone, TonEBP expression showed a moderate but significant increase at 48 hours (1.6 ± 0.5, P = 0.002), suggesting that LPS may indirectly influence osmotic stress pathways. However, under osmotic stress conditions, TonEBP expression was markedly upregulated. At 400 mOsm, the TonEBP expression increased significantly at 24 hours (3.2 ± 1.2, P < 0.001) and 48 hours (3.6 ± 0.0, P < 0.001), indicating a strong response to moderate osmotic stress. Similarly, at 500 mOsm, the TonEBP expression was significantly elevated at 24 hours (4.4 ± 0.4, P < 0.001) and 48 hours (5.7 ± 0.0, P < 0.001), demonstrating a dose-dependent response to increasing osmotic stress. At 600 mOsm, the TonEBP expression peaked at 24 hours (7.6 ± 0.7, P < 0.001) and remained high at 48 hours (7.7 ± 0.5, P < 0.001), further confirming its role in mediating cellular adaptation to hypertonic conditions. The combined treatment of LPS + 500 mOsm resulted in synergistic upregulation of the TonEBP expression. At 24 hours, the TonEBP expression significantly increased (7.6 ± 0.7, P < 0.001) and sustained up to 48 hours (7.7 ± 0.5, P < 0.001). This suggests that LPS enhances the osmotic stress response, potentially through inflammatory signalling pathways that amplify TonEBP activity. Notably, the TonEBP expression under combined treatment was comparable to or higher than that observed under 500 mOsm or 600 mOsm osmotic stress alone, highlighting the additive effect of LPS and osmotic stress on TonEBP regulation (Figs. 6s–u).

Gene Expression of Antioxidant and Hypoxia Markers

In contrast, the expression of the antioxidant gene SOD1 was significantly downregulated across all conditions and time points (see Figs. 6m–o), with the lowest expression observed at 48 hours under 500 mOsm osmotic stress (0.4 ± 0.0, P < 0.001). HIF1 expression displayed a distinct pattern, with significant upregulation under LPS treatment at 12 hours (1.3 ± 0.1, P < 0.001) and 24 hours (1.7 ± 0.0, P < 0.001), and a significant decrease at 48 hours (0.7 ± 0.0, P < 0.001). Similarly, under osmotic stress, HIF1 expression was significantly elevated at 12 hours (1.2 ± 0.1, P = 0.002) and 24 hours (1.3 ± 0.2, P < 0.001), but decreased at 48 hours (0.3 ± 0.0, P < 0.001). The combined LPS + NaCl treatment resulted in a similar trend, with HIF1 expression significantly increasing at 12 hours (1.9 ± 0.1, P < 0.001) and 24 hours (1.7 ± 0.0, P < 0.001), but decreasing at 48 hours (0.2 ± 0.0, P < 0.001). These findings demonstrate that both LPS and NaCl independently and synergistically modulate the expression of key inflammatory and stress response genes, while suppressing antioxidant and hypoxia-related markers. This suggests a complex interplay between the inflammatory and osmotic stress pathways, which may have significant implications for understanding cellular responses to infection and osmotic stress in ocular tissues.

Gene Expression of Toll-Like Receptor

Quantitative gene expression analysis of TLR1 to TLR10 expression was performed to investigate innate immune activation in HCE cells under LPS, NaCl, and LPS + NaCl conditions at 12, 24, and 48 hours (Fig. 7). A subset of TLRs, TLR1, TLR2, TLR4, TLR5, TLR8, and TLR10, showed significant upregulation of gene expression at the LPS + NaCl combination group at 24 and 48 hours. TLR1 (2.2 ± 0.2, P < 0.001) and TLR2 (2.7 ± 0.3, P < 0.001) showed a significant increase in gene expression under all stress conditions, with the significant expression observed at 48 hours in the LPS + NaCl group. Discussion

DED is driven by a vicious cycle of tear hyperosmolarity and inflammation that progressively impairs ocular surface homeostasis. Whereas each of these factors independently trigger epithelial damage and immune activation, their combined impact remains less explored in experimental systems. In this study, we established an HCE cell model that mimics both hyperosmolar and inflammatory stress individually and in combination, mimicking the multifactorial environment of moderate-to-severe DED. Our findings provide new insights into how these stressors disrupt cell adhesion, impair proliferation, trigger oxidative and hypoxic stress, and amplify inflammatory signaling pathways, which are central to the pathogenesis of chronic DED. Hyperosmolar stress, a defining feature of DED, was sufficient to induce marked cellular alterations, including cell shrinkage, reduced adhesion, and increased oxidative stress, consistent with prior reports demonstrating activation of MAPK and NF-κB pathways leading to apoptosis and pro-inflammatory cytokine release.16,17 Notably, the transcriptional upregulation of stress response genes, such as HMOX-1 and COX2, as shown in our qPCR data and confirmed at the protein level, supports the activation of antioxidant and inflammatory signaling cascades.18–20 These results align with earlier studies, including those by Yamaguchi et al. and Chen et al., which have established hyperosmolarity as a potent stimulus for inflammatory gene expression and epithelial dysfunction.21,22

In contrast, LPS-induced inflammatory stress primarily enhanced the secretion of cytokines, such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, key mediators of ocular surface inflammation in DED.23–25 Interestingly, LPS alone did not significantly impair cell adhesion in our model, suggesting that its primary impact lies in immunostimulation rather than direct epithelial disruption. This is consistent with the findings of Redfern et al., who showed that TLR activation in HCE cells increases cytokine expression without altering structural integrity.26

A key finding of this study was the synergistic effect observed when hyperosmolar and inflammatory stress were combined. The dual treatment led to the most severe cellular responses, including heightened cytokine secretion (IL-8, IL-17, and IL-23), reduced proliferation, and pronounced cell detachment closely reflecting the pathological features of chronic, advanced DED. This synergism is likely mediated by enhanced TLR signaling, particularly through TLR2 and TLR4, whose expression was markedly elevated under combined stress. Prior studies have shown that hyperosmolarity upregulates TLR4 expression in corneal epithelial cells,26 which may sensitize them to LPS-induced activation, also highlighted by the OCEAN group's emphasis on the hyperosmolar-inflammation axis in DED progression.27 Activation of TLR2, TLR3, and TLR4 on corneal epithelial cells and conjunctival fibroblasts triggers proinflammatory responses at the ocular surface. Erdinest et al. (2014) and Hou et al. (2017) showed that TLR3/4 stimulation induces cytokines like IL-6 and IL-8, whereas Kumar et al. (2006) demonstrated TLR2-mediated β-defensin-2 expression. These studies highlight TLR-driven innate immunity as a key contributor to ocular inflammation in dry eye.28–30 Our results corroborate this mechanism and highlight the importance of TLR-driven innate immune responses in the progression of DED.

The upregulation of TonEBP under combined stress further underscores the interplay between osmotic and inflammatory pathways. As a key transcriptional regulator of osmoprotective genes, TonEBP is activated in response to hyperosmolarity and is further potentiated by inflammatory cues.31,32 This finding is consistent with earlier reports that both MAPK and NF-κB signaling are amplified under combined stress conditions, exacerbating epithelial damage.25

Our model also revealed distinct patterns in oxidative and hypoxic stress responses. Downregulation of SOD1, alongside upregulation of HMOX1, COX1, and HIF-1α, suggests a compensatory cellular adaptation to redox imbalance and metabolic stress hallmarks of chronic DED. These protein-level changes validate our transcriptional findings and provide a deeper understanding of how corneal epithelial cells respond to multifactorial stress.

Importantly, the observed disruption of epithelial adhesion and adherens junction proteins under stress conditions, particularly E-cadherin and N-cadherin supports previous research demonstrating hyperosmolarity-induced barrier dysfunction. The observations on cell damage of cell adhesion are consistent with established research showing that hyperosmotic stress compromises epithelial adhesion by downregulating E-cadherin, disrupting tight/adherent junctions, and altering cytoskeletal organization. For instance, Zhang et al. demonstrated that hyperosmolar stress disrupts ZO‑1, occludin, claudin‑1, and E‑cadherin in HCECs.33 Similarly, Kontoh‑Twumasi et al. reported a dose-dependent loss of barrier integrity and E‑cadherin reduction under hyperosmotic conditions.34 Moreover, Han et al. described that TNF‑α increased under hyperosmolar stress, mediates tight junction disruption via metalloproteinase activation.35 The synergistic impact on cell junctions and viability further strengthens the pathophysiological relevance of this model.

To standardize and objectively evaluate these complex cellular responses, we developed a comprehensive scoring system based on key parameters such as cell detachment, antiproliferative effects, oxidative stress, and inflammatory activation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time such a structured grading approach has been applied to an in vitro DED model. This scoring framework is proposed for future use by other research groups and can serve as a robust tool for benchmarking therapeutic interventions, much like established models for limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD) and other ocular surface disorders.

Collectively, the findings from this study highlight the advantages of using a dual-stress in vitro model to closely recapitulate the complex pathology of DED. This system offers a valuable platform to evaluate potential treatments, such as osmoprotectants (e.g. erythritol and trehalose), anti-inflammatory agents targeting cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α, and novel strategies involving TLR inhibitors or NF-κB antagonists.36–42 The current model has certain limitations, such as the model uses a single immortalized HCE cell line, which may not fully represent patient-specific responses, and in vivo validation is needed to confirm the relevance of the observed cellular and molecular changes. Although our study suggests differences in inflammatory, oxidative stress, and TLR markers among the inflammatory, hyperosmolar, and combined (LPS + NaCl) stress conditions, these were not further validated by hierarchical clustering or additional assays. This represents a limitation of the present study. Future studies are needed to confirm whether pathogen stimulation with barrier disruption amplifies inflammatory responses in DED.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that hyperosmolarity and inflammation independently and synergistically impair corneal epithelial homeostasis, contributing to the core pathologies of DED. The dual-stress model presented here captures the multifaceted nature of the disease and provides a reliable platform for mechanistic studies and therapeutic screening. The application of a quantifiable stress-response scoring system further enhances its translational potential. Future work should focus on validating this model in vivo and using patient-derived primary cells to better capture clinical variability.
