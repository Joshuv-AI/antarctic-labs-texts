The grand piano ratio: an adjunctive intraoperative screening tool for detecting excessive femoral component external rotation in total knee arthroplasty
Authors: Özgür Avci, Alpaslan Öztürk, Burak Olcay Güler, Hünkar Çağdaş Bayrak, Ali Ömer Kaya, Yakup Doğan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate femoral component rotation is essential for optimal outcomes in total knee arthroplasty (TKA), yet reliable intraoperative assessment remains challenging. The “grand piano sign” has been described as a qualitative visual cue, but its quantitative clinical value has not been clearly established. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the grand piano ratio as an intraoperative tool for detecting excessive femoral component external rotation using advanced postoperative imaging as a reference standard.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 170 knees undergoing primary TKA. The intraoperative grand piano ratio was measured after completion of all femoral resections and before trial component implantation using the anterior femoral resection surface. Postoperative femoral component rotation was assessed using MAVRIC-sequence magnetic resonance imaging referenced to the surgical transepicondylar axis (sTEA). Excessive femoral component external rotation was defined as postoperative external rotation greater than 3° relative to the sTEA reference. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate diagnostic performance of the grand piano ratio and determine the optimal cutoff value, while multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent predictors of excessive external rotation.ResultsExcessive femoral component external rotation (> 3°) was identified in 39 of 170 knees (22.9%). The mean grand piano ratio was significantly lower in knees with external rotation compared to those without (0.41 vs. 0.54, p < 0.001). The grand piano ratio demonstrated moderate discriminative ability for detecting femoral component external rotation greater than 3° (AUC = 0.74, 95% CI 0.65–0.83). The optimal cutoff value was 0.44, yielding a sensitivity of 71.8% and a specificity of 74.0%. In multivariable analysis, the grand piano ratio was the only independent predictor of malrotation (p < 0.001). At the identified threshold, the negative predictive value reached 89.9%, suggesting that excessive external rotation was unlikely when the grand piano ratio exceeded the proposed cutoff.ConclusionThe grand piano ratio may serve as a simple and readily available adjunctive intraoperative screening parameter for identifying excessive femoral component external rotation in TKA. A threshold value of 0.44 demonstrated a high negative predictive value and may assist surgeons in intraoperative decision-making. However, the ratio should be considered a complementary assessment tool rather than a definitive method for determining femoral component rotation. The grand piano ratio should be viewed as a complementary intraoperative assessment tool rather than an alternative to navigation or robotic-assisted techniques.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, Femoral component rotation, Intraoperative assessment, Grand piano sign, Rotational alignment

Introduction

In total knee arthroplasty (TKA), the correct rotational alignment of the femoral component is one of the most critical factors in terms of knee flexion stability, patellofemoral tracking and the long-term durability of the prosthesis [1–4]. Incorrect rotational alignment leads to serious complications such as chronic pain, stiffness, patellar instability and revision surgery, and is among the most common causes of patient dissatisfaction following TKA [1, 5, 6]. Surgeons use various anatomical landmarks, such as the surgical transepicondylar axis (sTEA), posterior condylar axis (PCA) and Whiteside line, to ensure correct rotation [1, 7]. However, factors such as severe osteoarthritic deformities, condylar hypoplasia or soft tissue cover can make it difficult to identify these anatomical landmarks accurately during surgery, leading to measurement errors [1]. Femoral component malrotation has also been associated with impaired functional outcomes and increased patellofemoral complications. Consequently, accurate intraoperative assessment of femoral component rotation remains a critical objective in contemporary TKA practice.

To overcome these challenges and verify the intended rotation, an auxiliary visual verification mechanism based on the morphological shape of the anterior femoral resection surface—known as the ‘grand piano sign’—has been described [8, 9]. First described by Insall, this sign refers to the trochlea assuming an asymmetrical bimodal “grand piano” shape following an ideal external rotational cut [8–10]. Studies in the literature focusing on the quantitative analysis of this sign have generally centred on three-dimensional (3D) computed tomography (CT) simulations or cadaver models [8, 10–12]. For example, whilst Cui et al. [8] reported an ideal medial-lateral ratio of 0.66 for an incision parallel to the sTEA in their 3D CT simulation studies, Moyad et al. [11] argued that this ratio should be approximately 0.50 in a cadaver model.

In the current literature, the grand piano sign has been examined primarily through simulation models and morphological analyses; analyses validated using real clinical patient data and advanced imaging techniques are limited [9, 11]. Studies directly correlating intraoperative grand piano ratio measurements with postoperative femoral component rotation assessed using advanced imaging modalities in a clinical patient cohort remain limited. Therefore, the present study was designed to address this gap by evaluating the diagnostic performance of the intraoperative grand piano ratio using postoperative sTEA-referenced MAVRIC MRI as the reference standard.

The aim of this study is to evaluate the relationship between the intraoperative grand piano ratio in patients undergoing primary TKA and the postoperative femoral component rotation measured using sTEA—which is widely accepted as the reference standard—as well as to assess its diagnostic performance in identifying excessive femoral component external rotation greater than 3° [13]. Our hypothesis is that the intraoperative grand piano ratio will demonstrate a significant and strong correlation with femoral component rotation determined by sTEA-referenced advanced imaging, and will be able to predict clinically defined malrotation thresholds to an acceptable degree.

Materials and methods

This study is a retrospective diagnostic accuracy and correlation analysis conducted at a tertiary-level arthroplasty centre. The study was approved by the Institutional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval no. 2024-TBEK 2026/01–20). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients who underwent primary total knee arthroplasty for primary gonarthrosis between January 2018 and December 2022 were retrospectively evaluated. Patients included in the study were those with intraoperative records regarding femoral rotation adjustment, whose postoperative femoral component rotation was assessed using advanced imaging. All included knees had varus-type primary gonarthrosis. Patients with valgus deformity were not included in the study cohort. Patients with inflammatory arthritis, significant femoral deformity (including previous femoral osteotomy, fracture-related deformity, retained hardware affecting distal femoral morphology, or other conditions substantially altering native distal femoral anatomy), poor image quality, or missing data were excluded. Previous arthroscopic procedures without bony alteration were not considered exclusion criteria. A flow diagram illustrating patient selection and exclusion is presented in Fig. 1.

Flow diagram illustrating patient selection and exclusion. Of 502 primary total knee arthroplasties performed between January 2018 and December 2022, 170 knees met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis

All surgeries were performed using a standard medial parapatellar approach under tourniquet control. No robotic-assisted or navigation systems were used. The rotation of the femoral component was determined in accordance with traditional methods. In cases where the measured resection technique was applied, the rotation of the femoral component was set to 3° external rotation relative to the posterior condylar line [14, 15]. In cases where the gap-balancing technique was applied, the rotation of the femoral component was determined to be parallel to the tibial cut within the flexion range, based on soft tissue balance [14, 15]. All surgeries were performed by a single surgeon specialising in arthroplasty.

The grand piano ratio was measured after completion of all femoral resections and before trial component implantation. At this stage, the morphology of the anterior femoral resection surface was clearly visible and could be assessed without interference from implants or soft tissue balancing procedures. The heights of the medial and lateral anterior femoral cortices were measured using a sterile ruler, and the ratio was calculated by dividing the height of the medial anterior cortex by that of the lateral anterior cortex [8–11, 16]. (Fig. 2A–C) Measurements were performed by the primary surgeon and independently verified by a senior orthopaedic resident. In the event of any discrepancy between measurements, the measurement was repeated and the agreed-upon value was recorded for analysis.

Postoperative femoral component rotation was assessed in all patients using Multi-Acquisition Variable Resonance Image Combination (MAVRIC) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) incorporating a metal artefact reduction sequence (General Electric, GE Healthcare brand 1.5 Tesla Signa model). MAVRIC-sequence MRI was selected because it substantially reduces metal artefacts, facilitates reliable visualization of both the femoral component and the sTEA, and avoids additional ionizing radiation exposure associated with CT-based assessments. All measurements were performed using the surgical transepicondylar axis (sTEA) as a reference. The sTEA was defined by identifying the most prominent protrusion of the lateral epicondyle and the sulcus point of the medial epicondyle on axial sections. The posterior condylar line of the femoral component was drawn, and the angle between this line and the sTEA was measured in degrees. (Fig. 2D–F). In the primary analysis, the rotation value was assessed as an absolute value. Values indicating external rotation relative to the sTEA were considered positive, whilst those indicating internal rotation were considered negative. Although a deviation greater than 3° in either direction relative to the sTEA can be considered malrotation, the present study specifically focused on excessive femoral component external rotation (> 3°), because only a very limited number of internally rotated components were identified in the study cohort [17–19].

The femoral component flexion angle was measured as the angle between the anterior surface of the femoral component and the anatomical axis of the femur on lateral knee radiographs. The distal femoral lateral angle (LDFA), on the other hand, was defined as the angle between the anatomical axis of the femur and the distal femoral joint line on full-length lower limb radiographs. All radiographic measurements were performed using Synapse Mobility software (Fujifilm Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan).

Postoperative measurements were performed by two independent orthopaedic surgeons experienced in musculoskeletal radiology, who were unaware of the clinical outcomes and data; inter-observer and intra-observer reliability were assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient. Interobserver reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis based on a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement. ICC values were interpreted as poor (< 0.50), moderate (0.50–0.75), good (0.75–0.90), or excellent (> 0.90) reliability.

The primary endpoint of the study was femoral component external rotation greater than 3° relative to the sTEA reference. However, due to the extremely limited number of cases of internal rotation in the study population, the statistical analyses focused primarily on the development of external rotation exceeding 3°. The secondary endpoints are the relationship between the intraoperative grand piano ratio and postoperative femoral component rotation, and the diagnostic performance of the grand piano ratio in predicting malrotation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. For comparisons performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, effect size (r) was calculated as r = Z / √N. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (0.10–0.29), moderate (0.30–0.49), and large (≥ 0.50).

The primary outcome of the study was femoral component external rotation greater than 3°, as assessed postoperatively. To identify potential predictors of external rotation, univariate logistic regression analyses were initially performed for all demographic, surgical, and radiographic variables.

Variables with a p-value < 0.20 in univariate analysis were subsequently included in the multivariable logistic regression model to control for potential confounding factors. This threshold was selected in accordance with commonly recommended model-building strategies to avoid excluding potentially important predictors during variable selection [20]. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of the grand piano ratio in predicting femoral component external rotation greater than 3°, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed. The area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals was calculated to assess discriminative ability. The optimal cutoff value was determined using the Youden index. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated for the selected threshold. To assess the stability of the cutoff value, bootstrap resampling was performed, and the 95% confidence interval for the optimal cutoff was estimated using bootstrap-derived percentile intervals. Model performance was further evaluated using the Brier score, which provides a measure of overall predictive accuracy. All ROC analyses and bootstrap procedures were conducted using the pROC package in R statistical software. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusted for gender and surgical grade, the grand piano ratio remained significantly associated with femoral component external rotation greater than 3° (OR = 0.003, 95% CI 0.000–0.040, p < 0.001). Gender (OR = 1.946, 95% CI 0.751–5.045, p = 0.171) and grade (OR = 0.642, 95% CI 0.277–1.489, p = 0.302) were not significantly associated with external rotation (Table 4). Because the grand piano ratio ranges between 0 and 1, the odds ratio appears numerically small. Therefore, a decrease in the grand piano ratio was strongly associated with an increased likelihood of femoral component external rotation greater than 3°.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate the ability of the grand piano ratio to discriminate femoral component external rotation greater than 3°. The grand piano ratio demonstrated moderate discriminative ability with an AUC of 0.744 (95% CI: 0.654–0.835, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). The optimal cutoff value determined using the Youden index was 0.44, yielding a sensitivity of 71.8% and a specificity of 74.0%. Bootstrap resampling identified a 95% confidence interval for the cutoff value of 0.40–0.55. At this threshold, the positive predictive value (PPV) was 45.1%, while the negative predictive value (NPV) was 89.9%. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first clinical study to examine the diagnostic performance of the intraoperative grand piano ratio in assessing femoral component rotation using real patient data, and to validate this using postoperative sTEA-referenced advanced imaging. In our study, a significant association was found between the grand piano ratio and femoral component external rotation, and this measurement demonstrated moderate diagnostic performance in distinguishing malrotation (AUC = 0.744). Furthermore, the identification of a threshold value of 0.44 with a high negative predictive value (89.9%) suggests that the grand piano ratio, when measured intraoperatively, may serve as a practical screening tool, particularly for ruling out the risk of excessive external rotation. Although the diagnostic performance of the grand piano ratio was moderate, its clinical value lies in its intraoperative applicability as a real-time screening tool rather than a definitive measurement method. In this context, even a moderate discriminative ability may be clinically meaningful, may help surgeons identify cases at low risk for excessive external rotation during surgery and may contribute to improved surgical safety and intraoperative decision-making. These findings demonstrate that the grand piano sign is not merely a morphological analogy but may function as a quantitative parameter contributing to the assessment of rotational alignment in clinical surgical practice. In this respect, our study provides additional evidence, validated by clinical patient data, to support previous studies on the grand piano sign, which have predominantly been based on cadaveric or simulation models.

The values reported in the literature for the grand piano ratio show marked differences depending on the study’s methodology. Cui et al. [8] and Ohmori et al. [16] reported an ideal ratio in the range of 0.62–0.69 for incisions parallel to the surgical transepicondylar axis (sTEA) in 3D computed tomography (CT) simulations. The value of 0.44 we determined is lower than these simulation data; however, it is more consistent with the ratio of 0.50 reported by Moyad et al. [11] in their cadaver model. The primary reason for this difference, as emphasised by Shon et al. [21], is that, unlike simulations, in actual surgical procedures, the distal femoral osteotomy ‘shortens’ the anatomical peaks and reduces the vertical distance, thereby lowering the ratio. This finding demonstrates that our study reflects the actual anatomical outcomes of surgical practice rather than theoretical models.

The most original clinical contribution of our study is that it has established a moderate diagnostic performance with an AUC value of 0.744 and a quantitative threshold value of 0.44 for the grand piano ratio. However, the confidence interval around the AUC estimate (95% CI, 0.654–0.835) was relatively wide, indicating a degree of uncertainty regarding the precise magnitude of the diagnostic performance. Therefore, the reported discrimination ability should be interpreted with appropriate caution and warrants confirmation in larger independent cohorts. The NPV of 89.9% provided by this threshold value, determined using the Youden index, offers the surgeon critical intraoperative assurance: if the measured ratio is above 0.44, the likelihood of excessive external rotation appears to be low, as reflected by the high negative predictive value observed in our cohort. On the other hand, Kim et al. [22] reported that the frequency of the ‘boot sign’ (low ratio) in cases of malrotation is high in the Asian population, reinforcing the value of the 0.44 threshold we identified as a simple and readily available ‘intraoperative cross-check’ tool that alerts the surgeon to excessive external rotation errors. As there were no patients with internal rotation defects in our study group, it was not possible to predict the upper limit of the cut-off value we determined. Consequently, our findings should be interpreted as applying specifically to excessive external rotation and should not be generalized to internal rotational errors. Furthermore, the fact that the odds ratio appears quite small stems from the grand piano ratio being on a scale ranging from 0 to 1. Consequently, even small decreases in the ratios were found to be associated with a significant increase in the likelihood of excessive external rotation.

Although CT has traditionally been regarded as the reference modality for postoperative rotational assessment, MAVRIC-sequence MRI has emerged as a valuable alternative because it substantially reduces metal artefacts while allowing reliable visualization of both prosthetic components and relevant anatomical landmarks. In addition, MRI avoids the additional ionizing radiation exposure associated with CT-based imaging. For these reasons, MAVRIC MRI was selected as the reference imaging modality in the present study.

Recent advances in CT-based planning, intraoperative balancing strategies, navigation systems, and robotic-assisted TKA have improved the accuracy of component positioning [23, 24]. Nevertheless, the grand piano ratio should not be viewed as an alternative to these technologies. Rather, it may serve as a complementary intraoperative assessment tool that provides an additional layer of verification during rotational alignment [23, 24]. From a practical perspective, the grand piano ratio is not intended to determine femoral component rotation independently. Rather, it may serve as an intraoperative cross-check following rotational alignment established using conventional anatomical landmarks or gap-balancing techniques. A ratio substantially below the proposed threshold may alert the surgeon to re-evaluate rotational alignment before final implantation, whereas a ratio above the threshold may provide additional reassurance that excessive external rotation is unlikely. Given its moderate sensitivity and specificity, the grand piano ratio should not be used as the sole determinant of femoral component rotation. False-positive findings may prompt additional verification of rotational alignment using established anatomical landmarks, whereas false-negative findings underscore the importance of integrating the ratio with other intraoperative assessment methods. Therefore, the proposed threshold should be interpreted as a supplementary screening parameter rather than a definitive decision-making criterion.

Our multivariate analyses confirm that the grand piano ratio is independent of age, BMI, preoperative/postoperative coronal alignment (HKA) or femoral component flexion (all |r| < 0.20), thereby confirming that this parameter is specific to the surgeon’s rotational choice and is not influenced by the patient’s deformity. Although Shah et al. [9] argue that an anatomical ‘single peak’ may be observed in only 21–40% of knees and that this may vary depending on the flexion angle; however, the weak correlation with femoral component flexion in our large series of 170 knees demonstrates that the 0.44 threshold remains a stable guide for validating rotational accuracy despite these variations. Furthermore, consistent with the data from our study, Ohmori et al. [16] reported in their simulation study—conducted using two prosthesis designs with different femoral osteotomy angles (4-degree cutting/7-degree cutting) that the flexion of the femoral component did not affect the grand piano ratio.

It is known that distal femur morphology is more asymmetrical in Asian populations and that the incidence of the ‘boot sign’ may be higher than in Western populations [22]. In our study, determining the rotation of the femoral component using postoperative imaging has provided an objective measure of the effect of these morphological asymmetries on the final postoperative rotational outcome. Although the potential for measurement accuracy to be affected by metal artefacts in MRI is considered a limitation, 3D imaging remains one of the most reliable methods for postoperative rotation assessment [1]. Therefore, the grand piano ratio should not be considered a standalone method for determining femoral component rotation, but rather a complementary parameter supporting intraoperative decision-making. From a clinical perspective, the grand piano ratio may serve as a simple and readily available intraoperative verification tool. Particularly in settings where navigation or robotic systems are not available, a ratio above 0.44 may help surgeons identify a low likelihood of excessive external rotation, thereby improving surgical safety.

This study has certain limitations. Firstly, the study employs a retrospective design and is based on data from a single centre. Whilst the fact that all surgeries were performed by the same surgeon offers an advantage in terms of standardisation of surgical technique, it may limit the generalisability of the results. Another limitation of this study is that the analyses were restricted to imaging-based rotational measurements and diagnostic performance metrics. Clinical outcomes such as pain, functional scores, patient satisfaction, and revision rates were not evaluated. Another limitation is that formal interobserver and intraobserver reliability analyses were not performed for the intraoperative grand piano ratio measurement itself. Although all measurements were obtained using a standardized technique and independently verified by a senior arthroplasty surgeon and an experienced orthopaedic resident during surgery, the reproducibility of the measurement across different observers was not formally assessed. Further studies evaluating the reliability of the intraoperative measurement technique are warranted. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of diagnostic accuracy rather than direct evidence of clinical effectiveness. No cases of internal femoral component malrotation (> 3° relative to the sTEA) were identified in the study cohort. Consequently, the diagnostic performance of the grand piano ratio for detecting internal rotational errors could not be evaluated. This situation can be explained by the tendency of surgeons in clinical practice to specifically avoid internal rotation [2]. Furthermore, all included knees demonstrated varus alignment. Therefore, the findings cannot be directly generalized to valgus knees, in which the morphology of the anterior femoral resection surface may differ. In addition, all patients were derived from a relatively homogeneous population treated at a single institution. Potential variations in distal femoral morphology across different ethnic populations may influence the applicability of the proposed threshold value. Furthermore, the study was conducted using a single femoral component design. Differences in anterior flange geometry among implant systems may influence the appearance of the anterior femoral resection surface and potentially affect the grand piano ratio. Finally, prospective and multicentre studies incorporating clinical outcome measures are required to validate the established cut-off value in different populations and to determine its clinical relevance.

In conclusion, the intraoperative grand piano ratio showed a significant association with external rotation of the femoral component and demonstrated moderate diagnostic performance in distinguishing malrotation. The fact that a threshold value of 0.44 provides a high negative predictive value suggests that this measurement could serve as an adjunctive intraoperative screening parameter for assessing the likelihood of excessive external rotation. However, the grand piano ratio should not be regarded as a standalone method for determining femoral component rotation or as an alternative to established alignment techniques, but rather as an adjunctive intraoperative screening tool that may complement surgical decision-making. Further studies incorporating clinical outcome measures are required to determine whether this translates into improved patient outcomes.
