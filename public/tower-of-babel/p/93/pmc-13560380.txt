Targeting microglial circMBNL1 unlocks a novel nanomedicine therapeutic strategy for major depressive disorder
Authors: Xiaoyu Yu, Yan Lu, Zhiren Tan, Shulei Gao, Long Ye, Xiaoyan Luo, Jian Xie, Yuhao Miao, Bing Wang, Chenrui Liang, Ying Liu, Jilong Wang, Yinchu Ma, Yingli Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent psychological disorder that has a substantial effect on social and psychological functioning, requiring innovative treatments. Circular RNAs (circRNAs) have been shown to play a role in the pathogenesis of MDD and represent promising therapeutic targets. However, the target delivery strategies of interfering circRNAs against depression have not been extensively researched. Here, CircMBNL1 was identified to be significantly upregulated in plasma samples and showed a positive correlation with 24-Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD-24) scores in patients with MDD. Moreover, in the microglia of the brains of mice subjected to the chronic unpredictable stress (CUS) model, circMBNL1 was significantly upregulated. To achieve specific knockdown of circMBNL1 in microglia, PEG/PEI-based nanoparticles were modified with MG1 peptide for the targeted delivery of siRNA (TNP-si-circMBNL1) into activated microglia via intranasal administration. In vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that TNP-si-circMBNL1 efficiently delivered si-circMBNL1 to activated microglia, resulting in a significant reduction in circMBNL1 expression. Notably, intranasal administration of TNP-si-circMBNL1 effectively ameliorated CUS-induced depressive-like behaviors in mice. These results indicate that circMBNL1 is linked to MDD, and the peptide-guided nanoparticle loaded siRNA targeting circMBNL1 (TNP-si-circMBNL1) displays great potential for depression therapy.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04734-2.Keywords: Nanomedicine, Targeted Delivery, MDD, CircRNA, Microglia

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a highly prevalent psychiatric illness characterized by persistent sadness, loss of interest or pleasure, cognitive dysfunction, and diverse somatic symptoms [1, 2]. Epidemiological studies estimate that MDD affects approximately 4.4% of the global population, with a trend toward earlier onset, making it one of the leading causes of disability worldwide [3]. Despite the availability of antidepressant medications and psychotherapies, therapeutic outcomes remain unsatisfactory [4, 5]. More than one-third of patients show inadequate responses to first-line treatments, and remission rates rarely exceed 30% [6, 7]. A key limitation of current therapies is their poor ability to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and effectively reach the central nervous system (CNS), thereby restricting their capacity to restore neuronal and glial function [8]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop innovative therapeutic strategies capable of more effectively targeting the CNS, overcoming the limitations of current treatments, and improving overall therapeutic outcomes.

Significant advancement in nucleic acid production and cell delivery technologies have attracted widespread attention and accelerated the development process of small molecule RNA therapies for the CNS [9]. Compared to traditional drug, small interfering RNA (siRNA) therapies have multiple advantages, including long-lasting effects, lower drug resistance and lower risk of drug tolerance, as well as higher cost-effectiveness in production processes [10]. CircRNAs are covalently closed, noncoding RNAs produced by back-splicing of precursor transcripts [11, 12]. They are particularly abundant in the brain and participate in post-transcriptional regulation by sequestering microRNAs or binding to RNA-binding proteins [13]. Recent research has identified circular RNAs (circRNAs) as key molecular regulators involved in MDD progression [14–17]. For example, brain-enriched circRNAs such as circDYM and circATF7IP modulate microglial activation and oxidative stress; restoring their expression ameliorates depressive-like behaviors in animal models [18–21]. Clinical studies have shown that circMBNL1 expression is significantly elevated in the blood of MDD patients and correlates with disease severity. Mechanistic studies further suggest that circMBNL1 modulates brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) signaling, thereby influencing synaptic plasticity [16]. These findings indicate that circMBNL1 plays a pivotal role in MDD and that its targeted silencing may provide a novel therapeutic approach for MDD.

Microglia, as resident macrophages of the CNS, perform a diverse array of essential functions that are critical for brain development as well as for the maintenance of homeostasis, and the regulation of pathological response [22]. An increasing number of studies have shown that microglia play a significant role in a wide range of CNS diseases by regulating central inflammatory responses [23, 24]. Postmortem analyses of depressed individuals have revealed enhanced microglial activation and increased density in limbic and prefrontal brain regions [25, 26]. Additionally, a large number of chronic stress-induced depression animal models have also indicated that activated microglia can be observed in brain regions such as hippocampus and cortex of mice exhibiting depressive-like behaviors [27–29]. When overactivated, microglia release excessive cytokines, reactive oxygen species, and other mediators, triggering neuroinflammatory responses that in turn lead to depressive-like behavioral manifestations in mice. Collectively, these accumulated evidence demonstrate that microglia-mediated central inflammation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of MDD. Nevertheless, as of now, no effective antidepressant drugs or selective research tools designed to specifically modulate microglial activity have been successfully established.

Delivering nucleic acid therapeutics across the BBB to target microglia is a formidable task. The BBB permits less than 2% of small molecules and almost no macromolecular drugs to enter brain tissue [8, 30]. Although viral vectors and extracellular vesicles (EVs) can achieve gene delivery to the CNS, their toxicity, immunogenicity, and manufacturing complexity limit their therapeutic utility [31, 32]. Alternatively, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) have demonstrated great promise for delivering RNA-based drugs to the brain [33, 34]. For instance, targeted LNPs have been employed for local delivery of Cas9 mRNA [35], and innovative formulations such as perillyl alcohol nanoemulsions (PANEs) and synergistic amine lipid nanoparticles (SALNPs) have facilitated circRNA or siRNA delivery for depression therapy [21, 36]. Despite these advances, efficient, cell-type specific targeting, especially towards microglia, remains a significant challenge for clinical translation [37, 38].

Nanotechnology offers a powerful solution for overcoming these delivery barriers [39, 40]. Rationally designed nanoparticles can encapsulate and protect small interfering RNA (siRNA), enhance its stability, and enable receptor-mediated uptake by specific brain cell populations [41]. Moreover, intranasal administration provides a non-invasive route to bypass the BBB, allowing direct access to the CNS via olfactory and trigeminal pathways while avoiding first-pass metabolism [42, 43]. This delivery route has been shown to produce faster and more potent antidepressant effects than systemic administration, underscoring its potential for CNS-targeted nanomedicine [44–46]. In this study, circMBNL1 was identified to be significantly upregulated in clinical plasma samples and showed a positive correlation with 24-Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD-24) scores of MDD patients. Additionally, circMBNL1 was significantly upregulated in the microglia of the brains of mice exposed to the chronic unpredictable stress (CUS) model. To achieve specific knockdown of circMBNL1 in microglia, we developed an inflammatory microglia-targeted nanomedicine (TNP) by modifying a PEG/PEI polymer with a microglia-homing peptide (MG1) to achieve targeted [47–50] intranasal delivery of siRNA against circMBNL1 (TNP-si-circMBNL1). This nanocomplex efficiently delivered siRNA into activated microglia, resulting in a significant decrease in circMBNL1 expression. Furthermore, animal experiments revealed that circMBNL1 was highly expressed in microglia within the brains of mice subjected to CUS. Treatment with TNP-si-circMBNL1 significantly alleviated depressive-like behaviors in CUS mice by suppressing microglial activation, decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokine production, attenuating astrocyte loss, and preserving BBB integrity. Overall, this work introduces an innovative, non-invasive nanotherapeutic strategy that integrates targeted RNA modulation and precision nanotechnology to treat MDD. By specifically downregulating circMBNL1 in inflammatory microglia, this approach provides a mechanistically grounded framework for the development of next-generation neuropsychiatric therapies (Scheme 1).

Results and discussion

CircMBNL1 is upregulated in MDD patients and correlates with symptom severity

To elucidate the role of circMBNL1 in MDD, its expression level was assessed in the study participants. The study comprised a total of 80 individuals, including 40 MDD patients and 40 healthy controls (HCs). The sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are detailed in Table 1. The copy number of circMBNL1 in each microliter of plasma was calculated based on the Ct values. The results showed that the expression level of circMBNL1 in patients with MDD was significantly higher than that in HCs (Fig. 1A). In addition, the expression level of circMBNL1 was significantly positively correlated with the scores of MDD in the HAMD-24 (r = 0.349, P = 0.028), MADRS (r = 0.395, P = 0.028), as well as SHAPS (r = 0.381, P = 0.034) (Fig. 1B and Table 2). These findings suggest a potential biomarker role for circMBNL1 in MDD, indicating that elevated levels might be associated with the severity of depressive symptoms. To evaluate its diagnostic value, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was constructed to compare the difference in copy numbers of circMBNL1 between patients with MDD and HCs. The results showed that its area under curve (AUC) value in diagnosing MDD was 0.846, with corresponding sensitivity of 74.4%, and a specificity was 82.5% (Fig. 1C).

To evaluate the predictive value of circMBNL1 level dynamics for the clinical prognosis of MDD, plasma samples were collected from patients within two weeks of hospitalization and circMBNL1 levels were quantified. A minimum 50% reduction in HAMD-24 scores indicates response to effective antidepressant therapy, while a reduction below 50% indicates non-response (Table 3). Following two-week antidepressant treatment, plasma circMBNL1 levels decreased in MDD patients who responded, but there was no significant change in circMBNL1 levels in MDD patients who did not respond (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 1A). Additionally, a positive correlation was found between reduced circMBNL1 expression and reductions in HAMD-24 scores in MDD patients (Fig. 1E). However, there was no significant correlation between ∆HAMD-24 scores and ∆circMBNL1 expression for MDD patients in non-response (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Subsequently, an ROC curve was generated using the difference in circMBNL1 levels from admission to two weeks post-treatment to forecast the outcome, resulting in an AUC value of 0.790 (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, the correlation with established depression scales supports the notion that circMBNL1 could be involved in the pathophysiology of MDD, warranting further investigation into its mechanistic pathways and potential therapeutic implications.

To clarify the potential role of circMBNL1 in depression, we first detected and verified its expression level in a depressed mouse model. As shown in Fig. 1G, circMBNL1 was elevated in both plasma and brains of CUS mice, showing a 3- to 4-fold increase compared to controls. This increase was consistent with the findings in MDD patients. Flow cytometry (FCM) was then employed to isolate neurocytes from the brains of mice subjected to CUS (Fig. 1H). The results demonstrated that circMBNL1 was robustly expressed across various neural cell types, with particularly pronounced alterations observed in microglia (Fig. 1I). These findings indicate that circMBNL1 may modulate depression-related pathological processes through regulation of microglial function. Therefore, this study proposes a scientific hypothesis: Downregulating the expression level of circMBNL1 in pro-inflammatory activated microglia may help alleviate depressive-like behaviors, thereby providing a potential intervention strategy for the treatment of MDD.

Formulation and characterization of TNP-siRNA

To enhance the efficiency of downregulating circMBNL1 in pro-inflammatory activated microglia, we developed a targeted nano-delivery system. The rational design of such nanomedicine platforms is a promising strategy to improve the precision and efficacy of therapies for complex diseases. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, our system was constructed by formulating a core nanoparticle from a PEI polymer and siRNA (PEI-siRNA), followed by surface modification with a microglia-targeting peptide. The core nanoparticle was formed by complexing the cationic polymer PEI with anionic siRNA through electrostatic interactions. The efficiency of siRNA encapsulation was confirmed using agarose gel electrophoresis, which showed that siRNA was completely retained within the complex at a PEI: siRNA weight ratio of 25:1 and higher (Fig. 2B). MG1 peptide (CHHSSSARC) was conjugated to DF-PEG-Mal to yield MG1-PEG-CHO, enabling the accurately recognize pro-inflammatory microglia according to a previous report [47] (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Then, MG1-PEG-CHO was added to the PEI/siRNA complexes (PEI-siRNA) in a solution adjusted to pH 7.4. After incubation at 25 °C for 2 h, PEG/PEI based nanoparticles modified with MG1-targeting peptide (TNP-siRNA) were obtained. As control, PEG/PEI based nanoparticles (NP-siRNA) were prepared by reacting DF-PEG with PEI/siRNA complexes using the same procedure described above. Furthermore, the loading amount of the peptides in TNP-siRNA was investigated by the fluorescence intensity changes of MG1 peptide (Cy5 labeled) (Supplementary Fig. 2B-2C). Results of fluorescence intensity before and after TNP-siRNA formulation showed that the conjugation efficiency of MG1 peptide was about 81.04%. The Zettaliter Nano ZS was used to determine the size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of NP-siRNA and TNP-siRNA. NP-siRNA had a diameter of around 108 nm, while TNP-siRNA had a diameter of about 136 nm at a weight ratio of 25 (Fig. 2C). The PDI values indicated that both formulations had a narrow size distribution, which is crucial for consistent delivery performance (Fig. 2D). The zeta potential measurements revealed that NP-siRNA and TNP-siRNA exhibited a low positive charge compared to PEI-siRNA complexes, indicating successful surface modification (Fig. 2E). The features were validated using Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis, revealing that the NP-siRNA and TNP-siRNA had a spherical morphology (Fig. 2F). Moreover, the NP-siRNA and TNP-siRNA nanoparticles remained stable in PBS buffer for 48 h, with only a slight size change (Fig. 2G). The findings suggested that the nanoparticles possess a consistent nanostructure capable of safeguarding the enclosed siRNA for the downregulation of circMBNL1. In the hemolysis test, it was noted that neither NP-siRNA nor TNP-siRNA showed a hemolysis ratio exceeding 3%, indicating favorable blood compatibility of the nanoparticles (Supplementary Fig. 2D). The release profile of siRNA in extracellular and intracellular environments was assessed by measuring the release content of Cy5-siRNA after incubating NP-Cy5-siRNA or TNP-Cy5-siRNA nanoparticles in PBS at 37 °C. Both nanoparticle types showed stability with minimal release at pH 7.4, with less than 25% released after 48 h. However, at pH 5.8, a linear release of Cy5-siRNA was observed, with TNP-Cy5-siRNA nanoparticles releasing 48% within 12 h, which was 10% more than NP-siRNA nanoparticles (Fig. 2H). The results suggested that TNP-siRNA nanoparticles efficiently and promptly delivered siRNA in a simulated intracellular endosomal microenvironment (pH 5.8).

TNP-siRNA successfully downregulated the level of circMBNL1 in vitro

The TNP-siRNA relied on MG1 peptide to identify inflammatory microglia in the CNS, and the exposure of MG1 peptide in TNP-siRNA to the exterior was the key to targeting inflammatory microglia. Therefore, to evaluate the precise recognition effect of TNP-siRNA on pro-inflammatory activated microglia, we activated microglia using lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which is one of the most potent stimuli to induce microglia activation due to its interaction with toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on the surface of microglia. Primary microglia or BV2 cells were treated with LPS at 1 µg/mL for 24 h [47]. Then FCM and laser scanning confocal microscopy (CLSM) were used to evaluate cellular uptake behavior. To further assess whether TNP-siRNA promoted the uptake of pro-inflammatory activated microglia, siRNA was fluorescently labelled by conjugating with Cy5 dye to obtain NP-Cy5-siRNA or TNP-Cy5-siRNA. We incubated nonactivated or activated microglial cells with NP-Cy5-siRNA or TNP-Cy5-siRNA for 4 h. FCM analysis revealed that TNP-Cy5-siRNA increased the uptake efficiency of activated microglia to NP-Cy5-siRNA by 2.0-fold or 1.6-fold (Fig. 3A and B and Supplementary Fig. 3A-3B). This enhanced uptake was corroborated by CLSM, which demonstrated a significantly higher fluorescence intensity in activated microglial cells treated with TNP-Cy5-siRNA compared to those treated with NP-Cy5-siRNA, indicating effective internalization of the nanoparticles (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. 3C). The results demonstrate that TNP-Cy5-siRNA could achieve precise uptake of inflammatory microglia through cascade targeting. The cytotoxicity of NP-siRNA and TNP-siRNA was assessed in microglial cells using CCK8, and no significant cytotoxic effects were seen at concentrations of 50 nM, 100 nM, or 200 nM (Supplementary Fig. 4A-4B). Further studies showed that TNP-si-circMBNL1 treatment induced a significant, dose-dependent reduction in circMBNL1 expression in LPS-activated microglia, with knockdown efficiency reaching 70–80% (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. 3D). NP-si-circMBNL1 treatment indicated an approximately 50% decrease in circMBNL1 levels compared to controls, highlighting the superior efficacy of TNP-si-circMBNL1 in downregulating this specific target (Supplementary Fig. 4C-4D). These findings suggest that TNP-si-circMBNL1 not only enhances the uptake in inflammatory microglia but also significantly improves the silencing efficiency of circMBNL1, potentially paving the way for targeted therapeutic strategies in neuroinflammatory conditions.

To investigate the potential of TNP-si-circMBNL1 treatment in inhibiting the pro-inflammatory activation of microglia, both primary microglia and BV2 cells were initially stimulated with LPS (1 µg/mL) for 24 h to simulate an inflammatory environment. Synthesis of TNP loaded with a scrambled negative control siRNA (TNP-si-NC) and siRNA specific for circMBNL1 (TNP-si-circMBNL1) was conducted for experiments. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to PBS, TNP-si-NC, or TNP-si-circMBNL1 for an additional 24 h. The levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6 were assessed. The results indicated a marked reduction in the secretion of these cytokines in the TNP-si-circMBNL1-treated groups compared to LPS-si-NC groups. As shown in Fig. 3E and F, TNP-si-circMBNL1 led to a decrease of about 56% in TNF-α levels and approximately 50% in IL-6 levels in primary microglia, indicating its effectiveness in modulating the inflammatory response. This effect was also observed in BV2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3E and 3 F). On the other hand, TNP-si-NC did not show a significant effect on cytokine levels, emphasizing the target-specific action of TNP-si-circMBNL1. Further western blot analysis demonstrated that LPS stimulation significantly upregulated the expression of iNOS, a pro-inflammatory marker, in microglia, suggesting polarization toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. The treatment with TNP-si-NC did not affect iNOS expression in LPS-treated microglial cells. In contrast, TNP-si-circMBNL1 treatment markedly reduced iNOS expression levels, suggesting effective suppression of pro-inflammatory activation in primary microglia or BV2 cells (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. 3G). The immunofluorescence detection of iNOS protein expression in primary microglia and BV2 cells also showed decreased effects following TNP-si-circMBNL1 treatment (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Fig. 3H). The western blot and immunofluorescence data showed statistical trends in both primary microglia and BV2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4E-4 H). These findings collectively underscore the therapeutic promise of TNP-si-circMBNL1 in not only in enhancing cellular uptake but also in inhibiting the pro-inflammatory activation of microglia, highlighting its therapeutic potential for the treatment of neuroinflammatory diseases.

Targeting activated microglia of TNP-siRNA after intranasal administration in vivo

The success of the in vitro experiments led to additional research on TNP-si-circMBNL1 as a potential therapeutic treatment in vivo. Adequate CNS accumulation was crucial to effectively downregulate circMBNL1 in inflammatory microglia. Experimental and clinical studies have shown that intranasal (IN) administration allows for direct delivery of therapeutic agents to the brain through the olfactory mucosa or olfactory nerve [51, 52]. This mechanism allowed for prompt and effective therapeutic responses in the treatment of CNS diseases. To assess drug delivery to the brain non-invasively, mice subjected to CUS were intranasally administered NP-Cy5-siRNA or TNP-Cy5-siRNA. The distribution of in vivo fluorescence in the brain was monitored over time using the IVIS live imaging system. TNP-Cy5-siRNA exhibited robust accumulation in the mouse brain, with the strongest fluorescence observed at 12 h post-administration (Fig. 4A). Moreover, its brain accumulation was significantly higher than that of NP-Cy5-siRNA (Supplementary Fig. 5A-5B). After in vivo imaging, the mice were euthanized at each time point, and brain, liver, spleen, kidneys, lungs, and heart tissues were collected for ex vivo organ imaging. Figure 4B confirmed peak fluorescence in the brain at 12 h, with a gradual decrease over time. The quantitative analysis of brain fluorescence indicated brain accumulation of Cy5-siRNA at 12 h post administration (Fig. 4C), suggesting efficient nasal-to-brain delivery of TNP-Cy5-siRNA. The fluorescence accumulation of TNP-Cy5-siRNA increased by 1.4-fold compared to NP-Cy5-siRNA at 12 h post administration (Supplementary Fig. 5C-5D). FCM further demonstrated successful uptake of TNP-siRNA by microglial cells, with an uptake ratio of 35%, whereas the uptake of NP-Cy5-siRNA by microglia was markedly lower (Supplementary Fig. 5E). Additionally, the uptake ratios of TNP-siRNA in neurons and astrocytes were 11% and 12% respectively, which were much lower than the uptake ratio in microglia (Supplementary Fig. 5F). Collectively, this enhancement in delivery underscores the potential of TNP-siRNA as a promising strategy for targeting activated microglia, which play a pivotal role in neuroinflammation and related disorders.

Next, we explored the effectiveness of TNP in delivering siRNA into microglia in the mouse brains at 12 h. The frozen brain was sliced, and the sections were co-stained with markers for microglia (Iba-1+) to investigate the distribution of TNP-Cy5-siRNA. As shown in Fig. 4D, Cy5-siRNA demonstrated significant co-localization with Iba-1⁺ microglia, confirming effective delivery of TNP-Cy5-siRNA to brain microglia. Additionally, to quantitatively the downregulating circMBNL1 in the brain tissues after IN administration, mice were administered TNP-si-circMBNL1 and sacrificed at different time points. Additionally, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that intranasal administration of TNP-si-circMBNL1 significantly reduced the expression of circMBNL1 in the brains of CUS mice, with sustained inhibition observed at both 12 h and 24 h post-dosing (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. 6A). This decrease further supports the hypothesis that TNP-si-circMBNL1 not only reaches its target effectively but also exerts a functional impact on gene expression within microglia.

Furthermore, to exclude potential immune activation induced by TNP-siRNA, FCM was performed to analyze the peripheral blood of normal healthy mice 7 days after TNP-siRNA administration. The frequencies of CD4+ T cells, immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Treg cells), and pro-inflammatory Th17 cells were assessed. As shown in Fig. 4F, the percentage of peripheral immune cell populations did not show a significant change following the injection of TNP-si-NC or TNP-si-circMBNL1, indicating that there was no apparent immune rejection induced in vivo by these treatments. Additionally, the analysis of cytokine levels including IL-6 and TNF-α in the serum revealed no significant elevation of pro-inflammatory markers, further supporting the safety profile of TNP-siRNA administration (Supplementary Fig. 6B-6 C). This lack of immune activation suggestes that TNP-siRNA not only facilitates targeted delivery but also minimizes potential adverse effects associated with systemic immune responses.

TNP-si-circMBNL1 alleviated the CUS-induced depressive-like behaviors

To evaluate the antidepressant effect of TNP-si-circMBNL1, this study established a depression mouse model using the chronic unpredictable stress (CUS) method as reported [20, 53]. After completing the 4-week CUS stimulation, TNP-si-circMBNL1 was administered intranasally to observe its impact on the depressive-like behaviors of the male mice (Fig. 5A). Here, depressive-like behavioral tests demonstrated that TNP-si-circMBNL1-treated mice exhibited increased sucrose preference in comparison to TNP-si-NC-treated mice (Fig. 5B). In both forced swimming test (FST) and tail suspension test (TST), immobility time was significantly extended in CUS mice, which was significantly decreased in TNP-si-circMBNL1-treated mice (Fig. 5C and D). In addition, mice administered with TNP-si-circMBNL1 displayed increased activity and greater tendency to explore the central area compared to the CUS group in the open field test (OFT) (Figs. 5E-G). Moreover, a comparative intranasal administration study was performed using low-dose TNP-si-circMBNL1 and its non-targeted counterpart NP-si-circMBNL1 to rigorously assess the therapeutic advantage conferred by TNP-mediated targeted delivery of si-circMBNL1 (Supplementary Figure S7A). Behavioral analyses revealed CUS mice treated with low-dose TNP-si-circMBNL1 exhibited significant improvements in SPT, FST, TST, and OFT, whereas those administered the same dose of NP-si-circMBNL1 showed no significant alterations in these behavioral parameters (Supplementary Figure S7C-H). Collectively, these findings indicate that TNP-si-circMBNL1-mediated downregulation of circMBNL1 effectively alleviates CUS-induced depressive-like behaviors.

TNP-si-circMBNL1 suppressed of microglia proinflammatory activation and neuroinflammation

TNP-si-circMBNL1 has been demonstrated to be capable of downregulation of circMBNL1 effectively in proinflammatory activated microglia both in vitro and in vivo. To further assess the impact of TNP-si-circMBNL1 on microglial activation in the brains of CUS-exposed mice, we evaluated the proportion of microglia through FCM analysis (Fig. 6A). As shown in Fig. 6B, TNP-si-circMBNL1 effectively decreased the percentage of CD11b+CD45dim microglia in the mouse brain induced by CUS stimulation. Immunofluorescence staining with an Iba-1 antibody was used to specifically label microglia, followed by three-dimensional reconstruction and Sholl analysis, which further verified the inhibitory effect of TNP-si-circMBNL1 on the pro-inflammatory activation of microglia (Fig. 6C). In CUS-induced depressed mice, the immunofluorescent staining results aligned with the behavioral findings. There was a significant statistical difference observed in the suppression of microglial activation between the TNP-si-circMBNL1 and TNP-si-NC groups. The results showed that CUS stimulation could induce the activation of microglia in the mouse brain, manifested as an increase in cell body volume, a higher number of branches, and extended branch length. Notably, TNP-si-circMBNL1 markedly reduced the soma volume, the number of branches, total branches length, and total branch volume of microglia, thereby effectively suppressing microglial activation in the hippocampus of CUS mice (Fig. 6D-G).

Considering that neuroinflammation caused by the activation of microglia towards a pro-inflammatory phenotype has been broadly acknowledged as a central pathological mechanism in depression, we conducted an initial evaluation of the dynamic expression patterns of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) in the brains of mice. As demonstrated in Fig. 6H, TNP-si-circMBNL1 markedly reduced the upregulation of iNOS expression triggered by CUS stimulation. Consistent with this finding, the treatment also significantly attenuated the expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and MCP-1, in the brains of CUS mice (Fig. 6I-L). These findings suggest that TNP-si-circMBNL1 targeted on pro-inflammatory activated microglia in CUS mice, thereby effectively alleviating depressive-like behaviors through suppression of neuroinflammation.

TNP-si-circMBNL1 inhibited astrocyte dysfunction and protected blood-brain barrier integrity

Previous studies have reported a marked decrease in astrocyte density within the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus of individuals diagnosed with depression [54]. In addition, experimental evidence from animal models indicates that prolonged exposure to stress can impair the proliferative capacity of astrocytes, implying their potential involvement in the pathogenesis of depression [20, 55, 56]. Building upon these observations, we investigated the impact of TNP-si-circMBNL1 on astrocytes in the brains of CUS mice. Our results demonstrated that the administration of TNP-si-circMBNL1 effectively reversed the decrease in glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) expression induced by CUS (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, a reduction in the number of ACSA-2+ astrocytes was observed in CUS-induced depressed mice, while an increase in the population of ACSA-2+ astrocytes was evident in CUS-induced mice treated with TNP-si-circMBNL1 (Fig. 7B). In immunofluorescence assays, astrocytes were immunostained using GFAP-specific antibodies, followed by three-dimensional morphological reconstruction and Sholl analysis to assess structural alterations (Fig. 7C). The data demonstrated that TNP-si-circMBNL1 notably enhanced the density of GFAP-positive cells, as well as the complexity of astrocytic arborization, including increased branch count, length, and overall volume in the brains of CUS mice (Fig. 7D-G). Taken together, these results indicate that TNP-mediated downregulating circMBNL1 can effectively alleviate the dysfunction of astrocytes in CUS mice to promote the recovery of depression.

During neuroinflammation, pro-inflammatory molecules initiate inflammatory signaling cascades that decrease tight junction protein expression in endothelial cells, leading to increased permeability of the BBB [57]. The BBB, crucial for maintaining neural homeostasis, serves as a vital interface between the central nervous system and systemic circulation, ensuring structural and functional stability. Disruption of the BBB has increasingly been recognized as a key pathological hallmark in the progression of several CNS diseases. To explore the potential of TNP-si-circMBNL1 in modulating cerebrovascular integrity, we assessed the levels of key tight junction proteins (TJPs)—ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-5. As shown in Supplementary Figure S8A-C, the expression of TJPs significantly decreased in the hippocampus of CUS mice, a decrease that was subsequently restored by TNP-si-circMBNL1 treatment. Additionally, disruption of the integrity of the BBB in neuroinflammation can lead to the migration of peripheral immune cells (such as T cells, monocytes, and neutrophils) into the brain [58–60]. To evaluate how TNP-si-circMBNL1 influences the infiltration of peripheral immune cells into the brains of CUS mice, we isolated CD45+ bone marrow-derived cells from brain tissues and used flow cytometry to measure the proportions of lymphoid and myeloid cell populations (Supplementary Figure S8D). Our study, consistent with prior research, demonstrated an increased presence of T cells, B cells, neutrophils, and monocytes in the brains of CUS mice. Treatment with TNP-si-circMBNL1 notably reduced the infiltration of these peripheral immune cells (Supplementary Figure S8E-H). In summary, TNP-si-circMBNL1 enhanced blood-brain barrier integrity and suppressed the entry of peripheral immune cells, such as lymphocytes and myeloid cells, into the brain parenchyma of CUS mice.

Transcriptome analysis revealing the therapeutic molecular mechanism of TNP-si-circMBNL1 treatment

To deeply explore the molecular mechanism by which TNP-si-circMBNL1 targets microglia to achieve antidepressant effects, the transcriptomic characteristics of brain tissue samples were evaluated through RNA sequencing. As shown in Fig. 8A, the heatmap presents differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the CUS-TNP-si-NC and CON-TNP-si-NC groups. Subsequently, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on the DEGs identified between the CUS-TNP-si-NC group and the CON-TNP-si-NC group. GO pathway analysis highlighted significantly enriched biological processes associated with inflammatory response (Supplementary Fig. 9A). Likewise, KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment in inflammation-related pathways including “Antigen processing and presentation”, “NOD-like receptor signaling pathway”, and “TH17 cell differentiation” (Supplementary Fig. 9B). These findings further support the notion that CUS stimulation induces neuroinflammatory responses in the mouse brains, suggesting that targeting neuroinflammatory may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of depression.

Notably, a total of 573 DEGs were identified between the CUS-TNP-si-circMBNL1 and CUS-TNP-si-NC groups, suggesting that TNP-si-circMBNL1 may exert its antidepressant effects through the modulation of these genes’ expression (Fig. 8B). Further GO enrichment analysis revealed these DEGs were mainly involved in biological processes such as “Immune response”, “Cell adhesion”, and “Brain development” (Fig. 8C). To determine the key molecules downstream of TNP-si-circMBNL1 that exert antidepressant effects, the publicly available database DisGeNET (https://www.disgenet.org/) was utilized to systematically compare the above DEGs with the results previously reported in MDD studies, and found that 28 of these genes were associated with terms related to depression [61]. To further explore the potential interactions among these key DEGs, we constructed a visualized protein-protein interaction (PPI) network using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) [62](Fig. 8D). Additionally, CytoHubba from Cytoscape software was employed to screen top ten hub genes using 12 algorithms (Fig. 8E). Next, the KEGG database was used to examine the biological pathways associated with these key DEGs (Fig. 8F). The enrichment analysis revealed that the MAPK signaling pathway and cAMP signaling pathway were involved in microglial activation [63, 64], while cortisol synthesis and secretion, and renin secretion were thought to regulate astrocyte loss [65, 66]. In addition, MAPK signaling pathway, renin secretion, and cortisol synthesis and secretion, were involved in the disruption of BBB integrity [67–69]. Finally, qPCR was employed to validate the ten hub DEGs identified, and confirmed that there were significant differences in the expression of these hub genes between the CUS-TNP-si-circMBNL1 group and the CUS-TNP-si-NC group. In the CUS-TNP-si-circMBNL1 treatment group, pro-inflammatory genes, including Tacr1 and Rgs4 [70, 71], exhibited significantly reduced expression levels, whereas genes with neuroprotective functions, such as Map2, Adcyap1, and Nrxn1 [72–74], demonstrated significantly elevated expression levels (Fig. 8G). Moreover, these transcriptional changes were corroborated at the protein level by western blot analysis (Supplementary Fig. 10A and 10B). Overall, the aforementioned research findings demonstrate that TNP-si-circMBNL1 effectively modulates the neuroinflammatory response by inhibiting microglial activation, thereby alleviating depressive-like behaviors in mice.

Conclusion

Patients with depression experience severe limitations in their social and psychological abilities. The current lack of RNA-targeted treatments limits the success of clinical interventions for MDD. In this study, circMBNL1 was found to be upregulated in individuals with MDD, with its expression positively correlated with the severity of depressive symptoms. Furthermore, a strong expression of circMBNL1 was observed in microglia. Targeting circMBNL1 may offer a promising approach for modulating microglial activity and improving outcomes in MDD. Here, we propose a novel targeted delivery strategy based on intranasal administration of si-circMBNL1 via a TNP-based nanoplatform. MG1 peptide modification grants specific targeting capabilities to pro-inflammatory microglia. As a result, TNP-si-circMBNL1 successfully decreased circMBNL1 levels and alleviated depressive-like behaviors in the CUS-induced mouse depression model. Moreover, TNP-si-circMBNL1 exhibited a commendable safety profile in vivo. In conclusion, this study presents a novel strategy using nanoplatform to regulate circMBNL1 expression in microglia, highlighting a potential breakthrough in the development of more effective treatments for MDD.

Experimental section

Participants

The patients with major depressive disorders (MDD) were recruited from the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan university between March 2024 and December 2024. A total of 40 MDD patients and 40 Healthy Controls (HC) were recruited to examine differences in the basal expression level of circMBNL1 in plasma. The study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan University (Approval ID: JNU202403RB019). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their authorized representatives prior to their involvement in the study.

The inclusion criteria for participants diagnosed with MDD in this study required adherence to the diagnostic standards specified in the DSM-5, an age range of 18 to 60 years, and a minimum score of 20 on the 24-item Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD-24). Patients with pregnancy or postpartum status, alcohol or drug dependence, neurological or immune system disorders, and taking drugs affecting the central nervous system or immune system within three months were excluded from the MDD patient enrollment in this study.

Clinical evaluation

The study gathered sociodemographic information from every participant, covering aspects such as age, gender, marital status, education level, family history of mental illnesses, body mass index (BMI), and other pertinent details. Additionally, each participant’s level of anhedonia was assessed with the Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS). Moreover, experienced senior psychiatrists assessed the magnitude of anxious and depressive symptoms in individuals diagnosed with Major depressive disorders (MDD) using standardized tools such as the HAMD-24, Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA), and Montgomery Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS).

Collection and treatment of samples

Blood was drawn from participants during the morning hours, with each participant contributing 6 mL of fasting venous blood by using anticoagulant blood vessels. The samples underwent a multi-step processing protocol, beginning with centrifugation at 300 g for 10 min at 4 °C to separate the supernatant from whole blood. This was followed by a second centrifugation at 1000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove platelets. The resulting plasma was then stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Absolute quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

A miRNeasy® Serum/Plasma Kit (QIAGEN, USA) was used to extract total RNA from plasma, platelets, and distinct populations of peripheral immune cells which were then separately reverse transcribed into cDNA using a qPCR Kit (R123-01, Vazyme, China). A 166 bp insert that includes the back-splice junction of the circMBNL1 transcript was integrated into the pUC57 vector (Genscript, Nanjing, China). A standard curve was generated using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q141-02, Vazyme, China) with ten varying plasmid concentrations.

A formula for detecting standard compound copy numbers is as follows: copy number (copy/µL) = 6.02⋅1023 ⋅ plasmid concentration (ng/µL)/[(molecular weight of inserted fragment + molecular weight of vector) ⋅ 660](g/mol). The amplification curve and the standard curve equation were obtained as: Y (the corresponding cycle number) = aX (log value on 10 of the initial amplification copy number) + b. A linear equation of the plasmid standard was used to convert cycle threshold (Ct) values of circMBNL1 to arbitrary units, which allowed conclusions regarding the number of copies of circMBNL1. Each sample has been duplicated. CircMBNL1 primers were synthesized by Invitrogen, and their sequences are provided in the Table 4.

qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (9109, TaKaRa) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To eliminate genomic DNA contamination, 200 ng of each RNA sample was mixed with 2 µL of 4× gDNA Wiper Mix (R123-01, Vazyme) and nuclease-free water to achieve a final reaction volume of 8 µl. The mixture was incubated at 42 °C for 2 min. Reverse transcription was then performed by adding 2 µL of 5× qRT SuperMix II (R123-01, Vazyme) to the reaction, followed by adjustment of the total volume to 10 µl with nuclease-free water. The reaction mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 15 min and subsequently at 85 °C for 2 min to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA). The synthesized cDNA was used as a template for qPCR. The qPCR reaction was carried out in a final volume of 10 µL containing 1 µL of cDNA and SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Q141-02, Vazyme), following the manufacturer’s instructions, on an Applied Biosystems StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Primers for amplification of circRNA and mRNA transcripts were custom-synthesized by Invitrogen, and their sequences are provided in the Table 4.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve

ROC curve was applied to evaluate diagnostic performance of circMBNL1, The optimal sensitivity and specificity of circMBNL1 were calculated in SPSS. Youden (1950) introduced the following index for the ROC curve: J = max {sensitivity(c)+specificity(c)-1}=sensitivity(c0) + specificity(c0)-1, -∞< c <∞. The ROC curve is a graphical tool commonly employed in statistical analysis to assess the performance of binary classification models. It utilizes sensitivity as the vertical axis and 1-specificity as the horizontal axis. The Youden index denotes the identification of an optimal classification threshold by maximizing “sensitivity + specificity − 1” on the ROC curve. A value closer to 1 indicates superior model performance, enabling identification of optimal sensitivity and specificity.

Synthesis of MG1-PEG-CHO and Cy5-MG1-PEG-CHO

DF-PEG2k-Mal (DF-PEG, 0.3 g) and MG1 (10 mg) were dissolved in 2 mL water and stirred at room temperature for 24 h. Then, the mixture was dialyzed by 2000 Da dialysis bag for overnight. The solution was freeze-dried to obtain freeze-dried powder MG1-PEG-CHO. Furthermore, Cy5-NHS (1 mg) was added to the MG1-PEG-CHO solution (10 mg/mL, 10 mL) and reacted for an additional 12 h. The unconjugated Cy5-NHS was removed using a dialysis bag. Finally, Cy5-MG1-PEG-CHO was obtained by lyophilization.

Preparation of characterization of nanoparticles

First, siRNA aqueous solution (6 mg/mL) was mixed with PEI25k aqueous solution in deionized water at various volume ratios and vortexed for 30 s. After 30 min of incubation, the PEI/siRNA complexes were formed. Then, MG1-PEG-CHO was added to the PEI/siRNA complexes with pH 7.4 solution adjusted. After 2 h incubation at 25℃, the TNP-siRNA was obtained. NP-siRNA was obtained with DF-PEG and PEI/siRNA complexes by the same method as above.

To conduct gel electrophoresis, the prepared siRNA-loaded nanoparticles were mixed at different weight ratios with loading buffer (6 × Orange loading buffer, Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) and run on a 2% agarose gel with TAE buffer at 100 V for 15 min. The siRNA bands were observed using a Gel Doc XR+ (BIO-RAD, Malvern, USA) and the images were edited using Image J.

The size and zeta potential of the produced NP-siRNA and TNP-siRNA were determined using a Zettaliter NanoZS (Malvern Instruments). The morphology of NP-siRNA and TNP-siRNA was observed at a concentration of 1 mg/mL with the help of Transmission electron microscope JEM-2100Plus (JEOL, Akijima, Japan).

To investigate the conjugation efficiency of MG1 peptides in TNP-siRNA, Cy5-MG1-PEG-CHO was used instead of MG1-PEG-CHO to prepare Cy5-TNP-siRNA. First, siRNA aqueous solution (6 mg/mL, 100 µL) was mixed with PEI25k aqueous solution (15 mg/mL, 1.0 mL) in deionized water, followed by vortexing for 30 s. After 30 min of incubation, the Cy5-MG1-PEG-CHO (10 mg/mL, 100 µL) was added to the PEI/siRNA complexes with pH 7.4 solution adjusted. After 2 h incubation at 25℃, the Cy5-TNP-siRNA was obtained.

After nanoparticle synthesis, the mixture was centrifuged using an ultrafiltration tube (Mw: 10 kDa) for 30 min to remove unconjugated Cy5-MG1-PEG-CHO from the reaction system (collected in the filtrate). The total input of Cy5-MG1-PEG-CHO (1.0 mg in 1.2 mL) was defined as the 100% baseline. This solution was then serially diluted twofold to obtain a range of concentrations, and the fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer. The amount of unconjugated Cy5-MG1-PEG-CHO was quantified using a standard curve of fluorescence intensity versus concentration. The final conjugation efficiency was then determined by subtracting this unreacted fraction from the initial input.

Hemolysis assay

Mouse red blood cells (RBCs) were collected from healthy mice and diluted with 50X PBS. Mouse RBCs were further centrifuged and washed with PBS buffer twice at pH 7.4. After centrifugation, RBCs were resuspended with PBS at pH 7.4. NP-siRNA and TNP-siRNA were added to resuspended RBCs at siRNA concentrations of 200 nM, and 1% TritonX-100 were added to resuspended RBCs as control. Incubate treated RBCs at 37 °C for 1 h. RBCs were centrifuged and UV absorbance of hemoglobin was measured on the supernatant phase at 541 nm.

The pH-activated release of siRNA in vitro

To analyze the release of the siRNA in nanoparticles, a solution was formulated by blending 5 mg of the particles in 2 mL of the relevant release buffer, either at pH 5.8 or 7.5, and maintained at a temperature of 37 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected at different time points after agitation and filtration through a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cutoff of 14,000 Dalton. Following the process, the supernatant underwent lyophilization. The Cy5 fluorescence signals released into the supernatant was analyzed by HPLC.

Cell culture

Primary mouse microglia were isolated from the brains of C57BL/6J neonatal mice (postnatal days 1–2, P1–P2). Briefly, whole brains were dissected and carefully stripped of meninges and residual vasculature; tissue was then mechanically dissociated and enzymatically digested with trypsin-EDTA (25200056, Gibco) at 37 °C for 5 min. Digestion was halted with DMEM containing 10% FBS, and the cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm nylon mesh. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 1200 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, resuspended in complete culture medium—high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (10100147, Gibco) and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin—and seeded into poly-D-lysine–precoated 25 cm² flasks. After 7 days, astrocytes formed a confluent monolayer; recombinant mouse GM-CSF (CSF2; 0.25 ng/mL) was then added to enrich microglial proliferation. At day 10–12, microglia were selectively detached by orbital shaking (200 rpm, 37 °C, 2 h), and the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 1500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in PBS, and microglia were enumerated using a hemocytometer and plated at 2 × 10⁵ cells/well in 24-well plates for downstream assays.

Mouse BV2 microglial cells were obtained from the Shanghai Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), and maintained in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cell cultures were incubated under standard conditions: 37 °C, 5% CO₂, and > 95% humidity.

Cellular uptake and distribution of nanoparticles

To monitor the precise uptake of pro-inflammatory microglia by MG1 peptide-engineered target nanoparticles, primary microglia or BV2 cells were induced to pro-inflammatory microglia and anti-inflammatory microglia by LPS (1 µg/mL) and IL-4 (20 ng/mL), respectively, and untreated as control. After pretreatment for 24 h, BV2 cells were co-incubated with NP-Cy5-siRNA and TNP-Cy5-siRNA in 24-well plates at a Cy5-siRNA concentration of 100 nM. To facilitate tracking the nanoparticle, the siRNA was labeled with Cy5. After 4 h of incubation, the cells were collected and analyzed by FACS (Celesta™ flow cytometer, BD, America).

For CLSM observation, After LPS-treatment for 24 h, the cells were co-cultured with NP-Cy5-siRNA and TNP-Cy5-siRNA for 4 h. Then, the cells were stained by DAPI according the standard protocol provided by the manufacturer. Subsequently, the samples were visualized under LSM 880 laser scanning confocal (ZEISS, Germany).

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay

To test the cytotoxicity of NP-siRNA and TNP-siRNA during intranasal administration, primary microglia or BV2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates before being treated with NP-siRNA and TNP-siRNA at concentrations of 50 nM, 100 nM, or 200 nM. Cell viability after 48 h was assessed using the CCK8 assay (Beyotime, China).

Knocking down circMBNL1 with NP-si-circMBNL1 and TNP-si-circMBNL1

Primary microglia or BV-2 cells were seeded at 105 cells/well in 24-well plates prior to siRNA transfection. Cells were treated with scramble siRNA (si-NC) or si-circMBNL1 encapsulated by NP or TNP. After LPS-treatment for 24 h, BV-2 cells were transfected with NP-si-circMBNL1 or TNP-si-circMBNL1 at varied siRNA concentrations (50 nM, 100 nM, 200 nM). After transfection, RNA samples were collected from cell lysates using TRizol reagent (Zymo Research, United States) per manufacturer protocol, and gene regulation of circMBNL1 was determined by RT-qPCR.

Evaluation of microglial activation

Primary microglia or BV2 cells were seeded in a confocal culture dish with a density of 1 × 104 cells/well. Cells were induced by LPS (1 µg/mL) for 24 h. Then, the cells were washed with PBS, and the medium containing PBS, TNP-si-NC or TNP-si-circMBNL1 was added into different confocal plates. After 36 h, the cell supernatants of different treatment groups were collected, and the changes of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α were detected by ELISA detection kit. The cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Then the cells were exposed to 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked by Immunol staining blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature. Next, antibodies PE anti-iNOS (Bio legend, 1:100) were incubated with cell samples overnight at 4℃, washed with PBS three times. The cells were stained with DAPI, washed with PBS, and imaged using CLSM (ZEISS, Germany).

In vivo live imaging and biodistribution studies for intranasal delivery of TNP-Cy5-siRNA

Mice were intranasally administered with 36 µg/kg of free Cy5-siRNA loaded TNP or NP. Imaging was conducted at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h post-injection using the In Vivo Imaging System. Ex vivo imaging of mouse brains was also performed at the same time points. The brains were then homogenized for RT-qPCR, immunofluorescence staining, and flow cytometry analysis. Brain sections were fixed, sliced, and stained with FITC anti-Iba-1 antibodies (Wako Pure Chemicals, 1:250). The brains were also stained with DAPI and imaged using CLSM (ZEISS, Germany).

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice aged 6 to 8 weeks were purchased from GemPharmatech. Before the initiation of the experimental procedures, the mice were randomly allocated into different experimental groups and housed in cages measuring 25 × 45 × 15 cm. The mice were maintained under a controlled 12-hour light-dark cycle, with illumination beginning at 7:00 AM. Standard laboratory chow and water were provided ad libitum throughout the study period. Chronic unpredictable stress (CUS) mice were individually housed, while control mice were grouped together.

CUS protocol

Mice were exposed to a series of low-intensity social and environmental stressors, which were randomly applied twice daily for a duration of four weeks. The stress protocol included the following conditions: (1) 24-hour food or water deprivation; (2) continuous illumination throughout the light cycle; (3) exposure to wet bedding for 24 h; (4) absence of bedding material for 24 h; (5) forced swim stress in water at 8 °C for 5 min; (6) tail pinching at a site approximately 1 cm from the distal tip; (7) cage tilting at a 45° angle along a vertical axis for 3 h; and (8) physical restraint for 6 h.

Behavioral tests

Behavioral tests were performed in a controlled environment with minimal sensory stimulation and independently scored by a single researcher. Prior to testing, mice were acclimated to the experimental room for a minimum of 3 h. During this period, their behavior was recorded and analyzed using the Plexon Research Solutions system (Plexon Inc).

Sucrose preference test (SPT)

To assess sugar preference and evaluate anhedonia in mice, the SPT was performed. Briefly, mice were acclimated to a 1% (w/v) sucrose solution provided in a single bottle for 3 consecutive days. Subsequently, two bottles-one containing tap water and the other a 1% sucrose solution-were presented simultaneously for 24 h to determine baseline sucrose preference. Finally, a 24-hour SPT was conducted using identical bottles filled with tap water and sucrose solution. The positions of the bottles were alternated every 6 h to prevent side preference, while simultaneously measuring the intake of both fluids. Sucrose preference was calculated using the following formula: percentage preference = (sucrose intake / total fluid intake) × 100. All experiments were conducted by experimenters who were blinded to the treatment groups to minimize bias.

Forced swim test (FST)

To assess depressive-like behaviors in mice, FST was conducted. Each mouse was placed individually in a transparent acrylic cylinder (diameter: 20 cm; height: 25 cm) filled with water maintained at a temperature of 23–25 °C to a depth of 15 cm. Following an initial period of active swimming, the animals typically exhibited an immobile posture characterized by passive floating, with only minimal movements required to keep their heads above water. After a 2-minute acclimation phase, the total duration of immobility was recorded over a 4-minute testing period. All tests were performed by experimenters who were blinded to the treatment groups to ensure unbiased data collection.

Tail suspension test (TST)

TST was utilized to assess despair-like behaviors and depressive phenotypes in mice. Animals were suspended from the ceiling of a testing chamber using adhesive tape applied approximately 1 cm from the distal end of the tail. Following a 2-minute acclimatization phase, the total duration of immobility was recorded over a 4-minute observation period. All assessments were conducted by an experimenter who remained blinded to the treatment groups to ensure objective data collection.

Open field test (OFT)

OFT was used to evaluate locomotor and exploratory behaviors of mice. The open field apparatus was divided into 16 equal-sized squares, and each mouse was placed in the central area. After a 2-minute habituation period, total distance traveled, time spent in the central area, and the number of entries into the central area were recorded over a 3-minute session. The test was performed by experimenters blinded to the treatment conditions.

Drug treatment

The mice were randomly divided into five group (12 animals in each group): Con+Saline, Con + TNP-si-NC, CUS + TNP-si-NC, CUS + TNP-si-circMBNL1 (12 µg kg− 1), and CUS + TNP-si-circMBNL1 (36 µg kg− 1). Before the treatment began, a successful CUS mouse depression model was established. Subsequently, during the treatment period, each group of mice received nasal administration of saline, TNP-si-NC, or TNP-si-circMBNL1 every day for five days. Finally, the antidepressant effect of TNP-si-circMBNL1 was evaluated through depressive-like behavioral tests.

Flow cytometry (FCM)

After deep anesthesia with 1% phenobarbital sodium in mice, the heart was perfused with ice-cold PBS. The brain was then quickly extracted and stored in ice-cold PBS. For cell analysis, the brain tissue was digested with papain (2 mg/mL, 3119, Worthington) in RPMI 1640 medium at 37 °C. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70-micron nylon mesh to collect single cells by centrifugation. These cells were then resuspended in a density gradient of 30% Percoll (17-001-09, GE Healthcare) and centrifuged at 900 g for 25 min. Finally, cells were obtained by collecting the bottom fraction of the 30% Percoll. Subsequently, cell surface markers of neuron-enriched cells population, microglia, and astrocytes were detected. The following antibodies were used at the manufacturer’s recommended dilutions: FVS780 (AB_2869673, BD Pharmingen), APC anti-mouse NCAM-1 (FAB7820A-100, R&D), PE anti-mouse ACSA-2 (130-116-244, Miltenyi Biotec), FITC anti-mouse/human CD11b (101205, BioLegend), and PerCp-CyTM5.5 anti-mouse CD45 (561869, BD Pharmingen). Samples were gated for NCAM-1/CD56+ (neuron-enriched cell population), ASCA-2+ (astrocytes), and ACSA-2−CD11b+CD45dim (microglia). Moreover, intracellular permeabilization was performed using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor staining buffer set (00-5523-00, eBioscience) to enable NeuN antigen detection. Subsequently, the NeuN antibody (abs156048, Absin) was incubated at the concentration recommended by the manufacturer, followed by immunofluorescence labeling with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (A-11008, Invitrogen). In addition, the flow cytometric gating strategy was as follows: singlets were first identified based on FSC-H versus FSC-A scatter profiles; dead cells were subsequently excluded by negative staining for FVS780; finally, phenotypic characterization of the target populations was performed exclusively within the live, single-cell gate. All samples were sorted using a FACSAria II SORP (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed using FlowJo_V10.

To analyze leukocytes influxes following CUS treatment, cell suspension was obtained from mouse brain as mentioned above. Then cells were stained with FVS780 (AB_2869673, BD Pharmingen), PerCp-Cy™5.5 anti‐mouse CD45 (550994, BD Pharmingen), PE‐Cy7 anti‐mouse Ly6G (127617, Biolegend), PE anti‐mouse Ly6C (128007, Biolegend), PE anti‐mouse CD3e (561824, BD Pharmingen), or APC anti‐human/mouse CD45R/B220 (07131‐80‐25, Biogems). To quantify peripheral myeloid infiltration, samples were gated for CD45+/CD3+(CD3+T cells), CD45+/B220+(B cells), CD45+/Ly6G+(neutrophils), and CD45+/Ly6G–/Ly6Chi(macrophages/monocytes). All samples were measured using a LSRFortessa Cell Analyzer (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed using FlowJo_V10.

Western blot

Cell lysates were prepared using radio-immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor mixture (P0013B, Beyotime). Equivalent amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. Following incubation with blocking buffer, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Occludin (1:5000; 27260-1-AP, Proteintech), Claudin-5 (1:5000; 29767-1-AP; Proteintech), ZO-1 (1:5000; 21773-1-AP, Proteintech), GFAP (1:10000; 16825-1-AP; Proteintech) or iNOS (1:1000; 22226-1-AP, Proteintech), TACR1(1:500, 17942-1-AP, Proteintech), RGS4 (1:500, 14530-1-AP, Proteintech), TRH (1:500, 25332-1-AP, Proteintech), MAP2 (1:5000, 17490-1-AP, Proteintech), NRXN1 (1:500, 55051-1-AP, Proteintech), ADCYAP1 (1:500, 18163-1-AP, Proteintech) as well as GAPDH (1:10000; 60004-1-lg, Proteintech). After three washes with appropriate buffer, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:2000; SA00001-1/SA00001-2, Proteintech). Immunoreactive signals were detected using an automated chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon 5200, Tanon Science & Technology). Densitometric analysis of individual protein bands was carried out using ImageJ software to quantify relative protein expression levels.

Fluorescence imaging

Mice were subjected to transcranial perfusion with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Following perfusion, brain tissues were carefully dissected and sectioned into slices with a thickness of 30 μm. The tissue sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (T109027, Aladdin) for 15 min and subsequently blocked at room temperature with 10% normal goat serum (NGS, ZLI-9056, ZSGB-BIO) for 1 h. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. On the following day, the sections were rinsed thoroughly and incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI prior to mounting. Fluorescent images were captured using an epifluorescence microscope (Imager M2, Zeiss). The primary antibodies used in fluorescence immunohistochemistry are listed below: GFAP (1:400; 60190-1-Ig, Proteintech), and Iba1 (1:400; 019-19741, Wako). Three-dimensional reconstruction and Sholl analysis were conducted using ImageJ software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The sample preparation procedure is as follows: Approximately 100 milligrams of tissue or cultured cells were rinsed and homogenized with 1 mL PBS, followed by overnight storage at -20 °C. Subsequently, two freeze-thaw cycles were conducted to disrupt the cell membrane structure. The supernatant was then collected by centrifuging the homogenate at 5000 g at 4 °C for 5 min. Later, cytokine levels were determined using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The kits for mouse IL-6 (ml06838), mouse IL-1β (mlC50300-1), mouse TNF-α (ml002095), and mouse MCP-1 (ml037840) were acquired from Mlbio Biological Technology Co. Ltd (China).

RNA sequencing analysis

Whole RNA-sequencing analysis was conducted by LC-Bio (Hangzhou, China). Briefly, the mice were divided into the following groups: Con + TNP-si-NC, CUS + TNP-si-NC, and CUS + TNP-si-circMBNL1 (3 animals for each group). The hippocampus from these mice were collected using TRIzol reagent. Unique molecular identifier (UMI) technology was employed to label each sequencing fragment with specific sequence tags, thereby minimizing PCR amplification-induced duplication interference and improving the quantitative accuracy of transcriptome profiling. RNA sequencing reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38/mm10) using the alignment software HISAT2 (v2.0.4). Transcript abundance was calculated as fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped fragments (FPKM).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 25.0, SPSS Inc., USA). Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test, and continuous variables using an independent-sample t-test when normally distributed, otherwise using a Mann-Whitney U test. Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. For comparisons involving multiple groups (three or more), one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Holm-Sidak post hoc test was applied. All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Detailed information regarding the specific statistical tests used for each experiment is provided in the corresponding figure legends. A result was considered statistically significant when P < 0.05 (two-tailed).
