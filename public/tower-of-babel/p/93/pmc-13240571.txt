Evaluating the Impact of an Educational Intervention on Community Pharmacists' Knowledge, Attitudes, and Perceptions Toward Breast Cancer Screening
Authors: Lee Siow Yen, Pravinkumar Vishwanath Ingle, Palanisamy Sivanandy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimBreast cancer remains a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality, and early detection through screening significantly improves survival outcomes. Given their accessibility, community pharmacists are well‐positioned to promote breast cancer screening. However, gaps in knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions (KAP) have limited their effectiveness in this role. Educational interventions may help address these gaps and empower pharmacists to contribute more actively to cancer prevention. This study aimed to evaluate the KAP of community pharmacists in Klang Valley regarding breast cancer screening and to assess the impact of an online educational intervention designed to improve these outcomes.MethodsFully registered community pharmacists in Klang Valley were recruited using convenience and snowball sampling methods. A 4‐day online educational intervention was conducted in small groups via a social media platform. Post‐intervention KAP assessments were carried out within 1 month. Pre‐ and post‐intervention data were compared using the McNemar test and the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Relationships between dependent variables were examined using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient, while binary logistic regression was applied to identify predictors of improved KAP. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0.ResultsA total of 303 respondents were recruited. The median knowledge score increased from 13 (IQR: 11–14) to 16 (IQR: 15–17) out of a maximum of 18 points (p < 0.001). The median attitude score remained at 20, though the IQR widened slightly from 20–24 to 20–25 out of a maximum of 25 points (p = 0.015). The median perception score rose from 22 (IQR: 20–25) to 28 (IQR: 25.5–29) out of a maximum of 35 points (p < 0.001).ConclusionThe online educational intervention significantly enhanced community pharmacists' KAP regarding breast cancer screening. Incorporating pharmacists into national cancer screening frameworks could further increase participation rates and strengthen public health outcomes.

Introduction

Breast cancer has been the most prevalent cancer over the past decade, affecting 1 in 23 women in Malaysia and contributing significantly to years of life lost [1]. Advanced‐stage breast cancer, where the disease has metastasized, has a survival rate of only 23.3%. In contrast, early‐stage breast cancer carries a far more favorable prognosis, with a 5‐year relative survival rate exceeding 80% [2]. Routine mammography and clinical breast examinations remain essential tools in the fight against breast cancer [3, 4]. Since the disease is often asymptomatic in its early stages, regular screening is crucial for early detection [5].

In Malaysia, the National Strategic Plan for Cancer Control Programme 2021–2025 outlines the national screening policy, recommending clinical breast examinations and free opportunistic mammography every 2 years for women aged 50–74 [6]. Despite the availability of screening programs since 2010, nearly half of breast cancer cases continue to present at advanced stages [1, 7]. Screening participation remains suboptimal, with only 29% of Malaysian women reporting ever undergoing a mammogram [8]. Several studies have ruled out lack of awareness as the main barrier to participation [9, 10, 11]. A cross‐sectional study using the Awareness Beliefs about Cancer (ABC) tool revealed substantial knowledge gaps: participants answered only 52.7% of symptom‐related questions and 62.5% of risk factor‐related questions correctly [12]. This underscores the vital role of primary care providers in promoting screening and raising public awareness [13, 14].

With approximately 4000 community pharmacies across Malaysia, pharmacists are well‐positioned to serve as key educators in cancer screening [15]. However, there has been no recent evaluation of their knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions (KAP) regarding breast cancer screening. Previous research reported minimal involvement of community pharmacists in such efforts, coupled with inadequate knowledge. The main barriers identified were a lack of time, educational resources, and training [16]. Consequently, this study seeks to assess community pharmacists' KAP and to implement interventions aimed at strengthening their role, particularly in preparing them for future cancer screening initiatives.

Materials and Methods

Sample Size

This educational intervention study was conducted among community pharmacists working in Klang Valley, Malaysia, from April 2024 to July 2024. A total of 335 community pharmacists were recruited for the study, assuming there is at least one registered pharmacist in every community pharmacy located in the Klang Valley region. There are 1346 community pharmacists who are eligible to participate in the study [15, 16]. By using Raosoft sample size calculator, with a margin of error of 5%, confidence level of 95%, and response distribution of 50%, the calculated sample size was 300 [17]. A 10% non‐response rate was expected. Hence, 335 participants were recruited utilizing convenience and snowball sampling methods. Provisionally registered pharmacists and pharmacist assistants were excluded from this study.

Study Instrument

The study instrument, included in a Google Form, consisted of two sections with 39 questions. The first section gathered data on sociodemographic characteristics (9 questions), and the second section evaluated the KAP of community pharmacists on breast cancer screening.

The first part assessed community pharmacists' knowledge of breast cancer screening (5 questions), while the second part evaluated awareness of signs and symptoms (8 questions) and risk factors (5 questions). Response options were “True”, “False”, and “I do not know”, with correct answers receiving one point. The cumulative scores for the knowledge segments ranged from 0 to 18, based on information from the Clinical Practice Guidelines of Management of Breast Cancer (third edition) [4].

The fourth and fifth parts used Likert‐style responses to assess attitudes (5 questions) and perceptions (7 questions) of community pharmacists towards health promotion initiatives for breast cancer screening. Responses were assigned values like Strongly disagree = 1; Disagree = 2; Neutral = 3; Agree = 4; Strongly agree = 5. Attitude ratings ranged from 5 to 25, while perception ratings ranged from 7 to 35. Questions assessing perception were adapted from Kerrison et al. [18].

Experts reviewed and validated the questionnaire. Before the actual survey, a pilot study was carried out with 30 community pharmacists to evaluate the internal consistency. Reliability tests were performed separately for all sections, with Cronbach's alpha values of 0.619 for knowledge, 0.960 for attitudes, and 0.853 for perceptions, averaging 0.811 for the entire questionnaire.

Intervention and Follow‐Up Activities

After conducting the pre‐intervention KAP assessment, participants were divided into groups of 10–20 using a social media application. Educational materials were sent online to the small groups created. A video and educational intervention materials emphasizing breast cancer screening guidelines, signs and symptoms, risk factors, and breast cancer screening modalities [4, 19, 20, 21] were prepared and validated by experts in the institution and selected community pharmacists. The intervention was provided for 4 consecutive days, and on the last day, the same questionnaire was redistributed to assess the post‐intervention KAP of the participants.

Throughout the intervention period, the research team actively engaged with the participants in the small groups created in social media applications using polls and quizzes, and also addressed any inquiries that the participants posted. Those who did not complete the post‐intervention questionnaire or voluntarily left the intervention group were classified as dropouts. Their responses were not included in the statistical analysis. Participants who completed the post‐intervention questionnaire were labeled as “respondents”.

Ethical Considerations

We have received ethics approval from the IMU University's Joint Committee on Research and Ethics (IMU JC), with approval number MPP 1‐2024(02). We also obtained informed consent from all the participants before enrolling them in the research. The respondents were awarded seven continuing professional development (CPD) points from the Malaysian Pharmaceutical Society (MPS). During the data‐gathering phase, absolute confidentiality was maintained for all obtained information.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29 software. Based on the previous study by Alshammari et al., the scores were converted into percentages. Using Bloom's cut‐off point, community pharmacists who scored ≥ 80% were categorized as having “good” knowledge, those who scored between 60% and 79% were considered to have “satisfactory” knowledge, and those with scores less than 60% were categorized as having “poor” knowledge [22]. The same score categorization was applied for both attitudes and perceptions.

Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the goodness‐of‐fit of the distribution for all variables. The test indicated a p value of less than 0.001 in all variables, indicating non‐parametric data. The association between the dependent variables and the independent variables was assessed using Mann–Whitney U (two categorical variables) or Kruskal–Wallis tests (more than two categorical variables), with Dunn's Bonferroni post hoc multiple comparison tests performed to sort out which variables differed. The proportional difference of correct answers or positive answers pre‐ and post‐intervention was assessed using a McNemar test [23].

Wilcoxon‐signed rank tests were performed to examine the difference in the KAP scores pre‐ and post‐intervention. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was used to investigate the relationship between the dependent variables [24]. The relationship between KAPs (with improvement and without improvement) and variables was investigated using binary logistic regression. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Comparison of Pre‐ and Post‐Intervention Attitudes Regarding Breast Cancer

Most respondents were initially willing to provide counseling, promote cancer health, educate on breast self‐examination, encourage screening, and refer customers with symptoms. Post‐intervention, positive responses increased by 2%–4%, but McNemar's test found no statistically significant changes (p values: 0.054–0.470). Median scores remained consistent at 4 (IQR: 4–5) for all questions. However, the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test revealed statistically significant positive shifts from less favorable to more favorable attitudes in providing counseling, educating on self‐examination, and encouraging mammography screening. The details are presented in Supporting Information S1: Table S2.

Comparison of Pre‐ and Post‐Intervention Perceptions Regarding Breast Cancer

Respondents showed significantly improved perceptions following the intervention, as evidenced by the shift from more negative to more positive answers (p < 0.01). Additionally, there were also statistically significantly higher proportions of positive responses for all questions (p < 0.01). The details are presented in Supporting Information S1: Table S3.

Effectiveness of the Educational Intervention

There were significant improvements in median scores for knowledge, attitude, and perception following the intervention. Initially, most respondents had “satisfactory” knowledge levels, with only 21.12% (n = 64) achieving a “good” rating. Post‐intervention, this figure rose to 76.90% (n = 233). In contrast, attitude scores remained constant at a median of 20 (80.00%) pre‐ and post‐intervention, indicating “good” attitudes. While the interquartile range (IQR) showed a slight positive shift due to more respondents expressing favorable attitudes, there was no statistically significant change in the proportion of those with a “good” attitude. On the other hand, perception scores improved significantly, moving from “satisfactory” pre‐intervention to “good” post‐intervention. This shift was accompanied by a notable increase in the proportion of respondents with “good” perceptions and a marked decrease in those with “poor” perceptions. The details are presented in Supporting Information S1: Table S4.

Correlation Between Dependent Variables

Spearman's rank correlation coefficients revealed positive correlations between knowledge, attitude, and perception scores after the intervention. Prior to the intervention, the correlation between knowledge and attitude was minimal; however, a weak positive correlation was observed post‐intervention. The correlation between knowledge and perception remained weak both before and after the intervention. Remarkably, the correlation between attitude and perception significantly improved, reaching a “moderate” level post‐intervention. Further details can be found in Table 3.

Discussion

The demographic characteristics of pharmacists in this study were comparable to those reported in earlier research. The median age of participants in this study aligned with the mean age in the previous study, suggesting that community pharmacists generally represent a younger workforce. Similarly, females outnumbered males, reflecting the gender distribution observed among pharmacy students in Malaysia [16]. The study also revealed that most community pharmacists were Chinese, consistent with two other cross‐sectional studies conducted in Klang Valley, which reported that approximately 80% of pharmacists were Chinese [25, 26]. This overrepresentation may be attributed to the composition of the Malaysian workforce, where Bumiputera individuals are more likely to work in government positions, while Chinese individuals more commonly enter the private sector, including community pharmacies [27].

Academic qualification was the only sociodemographic variable showing a statistically significant difference in baseline knowledge levels. Respondents with a Master's degree demonstrated higher knowledge compared with those holding only a Bachelor's degree. This finding aligns with an earlier study in which participants with a Master's degree achieved higher median knowledge scores [28]. Gender differences were also observed in attitudes: female respondents displayed more favorable attitudes, possibly influenced by breast cancer's greater visibility, its lower incidence in men, and its significant impact on women's health [29, 30].

The results of this study support previous research indicating insufficient knowledge among community pharmacists regarding breast cancer screening [16, 31, 32, 33]. Notably, only a small proportion of survey participants reported receiving adequate training prior to the intervention. This mirrors findings from a study in Istanbul, where 90% of pharmacists lacked cancer screening training [34]. Such evidence highlights the urgent need for continuing professional development (CPD) programs to equip pharmacists with the knowledge and skills required to effectively promote cancer screening.

A key finding of this study is that fewer than half of respondents recognized that ultrasound should not be used as the sole screening tool for breast cancer. While ultrasound serves as a valuable complement to mammography, it is often mistakenly perceived as a substitute due to its lower cost and accessibility [35]. However, research demonstrates that although ultrasound can improve detection rates when combined with mammography, it is ineffective as a primary screening method. Encouragingly, following the intervention, correct responses on this topic rose to more than 80% [36, 37].

Previous studies at the Science University of Malaysia have shown that healthcare professionals' knowledge and recommendations significantly influence mammogram uptake. Addressing misconceptions among community pharmacists can therefore empower them to provide accurate information and encourage appropriate screening practices. Furthermore, other research underscores the vital role of pharmacists in raising awareness, advocating for mammogram screening, and ultimately fostering informed decision‐making and greater participation in preventive health behaviors [38].

Additionally, a substantial gap was observed among respondents in identifying breast cancer risk factors. Before the intervention, fewer than 70% correctly recognized key factors such as early menarche, low parity, lack of breastfeeding, overweight, oral contraceptive use, and late menopause. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Jordan, where response rates were similarly low. However, Jordanian pharmacists demonstrated greater awareness of the risks associated with oral contraceptive use (87% vs. 69% in the Malaysian cohort) [39]. This gap is concerning, as inaccurate information from pharmacists could lead to misinformation and potential harm to patients. Another study also highlighted how industry standards and priorities can influence the content and focus of CPD programs [40]. Therefore, comprehensive and independently designed educational interventions, such as the one in this study, may better support community pharmacists by avoiding bias.

Despite a significant improvement in correct responses post‐intervention, many respondents mistakenly identified “having many children” and “breastfeeding” as factors that increase breast cancer risk. This likely reflects the complexity of these items, as both reduce risk. Correct responses regarding “family history of breast cancer at a young age” also decreased by 8.9%, possibly due to its omission from the educational materials. While the overall knowledge gain suggests the intervention was effective, these gaps highlight the importance of ensuring content is both comprehensive and accurate. The findings also suggest a need for more targeted education on complex or commonly misunderstood risk factors. Interestingly, respondents with 21–25 years of experience were less likely to show improvement compared to those with fewer years of practice, possibly because newer pharmacists had more recent exposure to cancer screening during their training. This underscores the importance of a well‐structured undergraduate curriculum that emphasizes the practical application of breast cancer screening to prepare work‐ready pharmacists.

Accurate identification of breast cancer signs and symptoms is crucial for early detection and effective patient care [41]. The study revealed strong baseline knowledge among community pharmacists, with more than 80% correctly identifying symptoms such as lumps, nipple discharge, and breast pain. This may reflect frequent patient inquiries, as also reported in a UAE study where 62% of pharmacists had responded to questions about breast cancer signs and symptoms [42]. However, only 62.05% of respondents recognized rashes on the breast as a potential warning sign. Given pharmacists' role as first‐line consultants for dermatological issues, this represents a concerning oversight. Misidentification could result in inappropriate recommendations, such as treating skin changes with topical creams, potentially delaying diagnosis [43].

Although the study showed statistically significant improvements in overall attitude scores, the magnitude of change was minimal due to already high baseline levels. Nevertheless, limitations in resources and confidence to perform screenings remain, as reflected in moderate perception scores before the intervention. Similar findings were reported in another study, where 92.7% of community pharmacists expressed positive attitudes toward breast cancer screening [31]. Compared to another study, where only 68.6% of pharmacists expressed interest in breast cancer health promotion, this study reported a significantly higher rate of interest (around 85%) [16]. The Wilcoxon signed‐rank test further revealed notable improvements in respondents' willingness to educate customers about breast cancer screening, perform breast self‐examinations, and encourage eligible customers to undergo screening. These results suggest that the educational intervention was effective in enhancing pharmacists' attitudes at an individual level.

Following the intervention, overall perception scores improved, shifting from “satisfactory” to “good.” A growing percentage of respondents reported confidence in making referrals and recognizing signs and symptoms, suggesting that enhanced training and knowledge significantly boosted their confidence [44]. In addition, the intervention reduced time‐related barriers to cancer screening and created more opportunities for community pharmacists to conduct screenings. This was reflected in the increased proportion of respondents who felt they had sufficient time to discuss breast cancer screening with their customers [18, 44]. The 13% rise in positive responses to the last two perception questions indicated that community pharmacists were generally more motivated to offer breast cancer screening services.

This study demonstrated significant improvements in the knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions (KAP) of community pharmacists following a 4‐day online educational intervention. The findings align with those of a previous study conducted in Kedah, Malaysia, where the distribution of pamphlets to female university students improved knowledge and breast self‐examination practices [45]. However, unlike that study, the current research did not assess practical application, as data collection was limited to a 2‐month period. Furthermore, sustained organizational training is essential to foster new practices and ensure long‐term adoption [46]. Future studies should therefore focus on measuring community pharmacists' actual cancer screening practices to address this gap.

Online education provides a cost‐effective and flexible learning method, enabling participants to study at their own pace and revisit materials anytime, provided they have access to a mobile phone. This approach is particularly beneficial for working adults, offering them greater flexibility to balance professional responsibilities with learning opportunities [47]. Nevertheless, challenges such as varying levels of technological literacy may arise [48]. Ensuring both accessibility and effective use of digital platforms is therefore critical to maximizing the impact of online interventions [49]. Prior research has also highlighted the effectiveness of using social media for education through short, focused modules, such as a 30‐min voice‐over PowerPoint presentation, due to its broad reach [50]. In line with this, our study utilized WhatsApp, a widely used application, to address accessibility concerns and effectively engage participants [51].

The bivariate correlation analysis revealed weak positive relationships between knowledge and attitudes, and between knowledge and perceptions. Similarly, a previous study found only a weak correlation between knowledge and attitudes among university students, emphasizing that knowledge alone may not determine attitudes; other factors likely contribute [52]. This study also observed a significant but weak correlation between attitudes and perceptions before the intervention, which strengthened to a moderate level afterward. This finding echoes results from an earlier study reporting a stronger correlation between attitudes and perceptions (rs = 0.56) than between knowledge and attitudes (rs = 0.24) [52]. In Australia, community pharmacists with higher knowledge demonstrated greater confidence in carrying out cancer screenings [53]. These findings underscore that psychological variables, such as attitudes and perceptions, influence pharmacists' behaviors in promoting cancer screening. Consequently, enhancing knowledge through targeted educational interventions may positively influence their professional practices [18].

Community pharmacists, as primary care providers, can play a crucial role in delivering cancer screening education and providing early detection resources, such as brochures, videos, and personalized counseling. They are also well‐positioned to correct public misconceptions about cancer [41]. The educational materials developed in this study could be adapted to enhance public awareness and understanding of cancer screening. However, Malaysia currently lacks a structured framework to involve pharmacists in breast cancer screening programs [4, 6]. Future initiatives should aim to integrate pharmacists into these efforts, supported by studies evaluating the effectiveness of pharmacist‐led interventions on public health outcomes.

This study had several limitations. First, it did not include a control group, a decision informed by existing literature that highlights inadequate breast cancer screening knowledge among community pharmacists, making it necessary to provide the intervention to all participants [16]. The absence of a control group made it difficult to determine whether changes in knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) were solely attributable to the educational intervention, as other factors, such as personal experiences or external influences, might have contributed [54]. It is therefore crucial that future interventions develop comprehensive, well‐validated educational content to avoid such limitations and ensure that no essential information is overlooked.

Additionally, there was no fixed deadline for completing the post‐intervention questionnaire, and the impact of time was not analyzed. A 1‐month response window was provided to accommodate pharmacists' busy schedules. Self‐reporting bias may also have influenced the results, as participants answered based on their own perceptions. Furthermore, 80% of the data was collected through convenience sampling, which may have introduced selection bias, though this approach was necessary to complete data collection within 2 months [55]. Finally, the study was conducted exclusively in the Klang Valley, excluding rural community pharmacies, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should broaden recruitment to include other regions to improve representativeness.

Conclusion

The study found that community pharmacists in Malaysia possessed satisfactory baseline knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions regarding breast cancer screening. An online educational intervention significantly enhanced these areas, raising all three KAP variables to “good” levels. Notably, most pharmacists expressed willingness to engage in breast cancer screening health promotion activities. Looking ahead, integrating community pharmacists into the national cancer screening framework could boost participation rates and further strengthen public health outcomes.
