Mediating Roles of Self-Control and Social Support in the Relationship Between Problematic Internet Use and Depression Among Korean Children and Adolescents
Authors: Cheongwoo Lee, Subin Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study examined whether self-control and perceived social support mediated the relationship between problematic Internet use (PIU) and depression in Korean adolescents and whether these pathways differed by PIU risk level based on the Korean version of the Young Internet Addiction Test (YIAT; cutoff=40).MethodsThis study analyzed data from 2294 adolescents in a nationally representative cohort. The participants completed the YIAT, Children’s Depression Inventory, Gottfredson Self-Control Scale, and Social Support Appraisal Scale. A hierarchical regression based on Baron and Kenny’s mediation framework was conducted, and serial multiple mediation was tested with subgroup analyses by risk level.ResultsPIU was significantly associated with lower self-control, lower perceived social support, and higher levels of depressive symptoms. Mediation analyses in the total sample revealed that self-control and social support partially mediated the association between PIU and depressive symptoms. The strongest indirect effect was found in the serial pathway (PIU → self-control → social support → depression; effect=0.050, 95% confidence interval, CI [0.037, 0.065]). Subgroup analyses demonstrated that self-control fully mediated this relationship in the low-risk group, whereas only partial mediation was observed in the high-risk group. In the low-risk group, the self-control-only pathway (PIU → self-control → depression) was the strongest (effect=0.071, 95% CI [0.049, 0.094]), whereas in the high-risk group, the serial path had the largest effect (effect=0.040, 95% CI [0.030, 0.051]).ConclusionSelf-control and social support mediated the relationship between PIU and depressive symptoms, with different patterns by risk group. Therefore, targeted interventions should consider these distinct mechanisms.Keywords: Problematic Internet use, Self-control, Social support, Depression, Adolescents, Korea

INTRODUCTION

The Internet has become an essential component of the modern lives of young adolescents in South Korea and has both advantages and disadvantages. According to Young [1], the term problematic Internet use (PIU) refers to a condition in which individuals experience a loss of control over their Internet usage, leading to significant distress or impairment in their daily functioning [2,3]. Studies conducted by Ha et al. [4], Shapira et al. [5], Tsai and Lin [6], and Bell et al. [7] have demonstrated that excessive or PIU is associated with depressive symptoms and a range of other mental health disorders. These findings suggest that PIU is not merely a behavioral concern but is also closely associated with emotional disturbances such as depression. Therefore, it is critical to examine how and through which mechanisms PIU leads to negative psychological outcomes among adolescents. One core construct that has drawn increasing attention in this context is self-control, which may help explain individual differences in vulnerability to such outcomes.

According to DeLisi [8], self-control refers to an individual’s ability to manage their thoughts, emotions, and behaviors, particularly when facing external pressures. Drawing on the impulsivity theory by Ainslie [9], Peluso et al. [10] stated that students who demonstrated stronger emotional and behavioral regulation tended to exhibit lower impulsivity and were therefore less likely to engage in short-term, maladaptive behaviors such as compulsive Internet use. Empirical findings by Li et al. [11] support this view, with self-control shown to be negatively associated with PIU.

In addition to its relevance to behavioral outcomes, such as PIU, self-control plays a crucial role in emotional functioning. Previous research by DeWall et al. [12] showed that individuals with higher levels of self-control were less likely to experience symptoms of depression. These studies suggest that self-regulatory capacity may serve not only as a buffer against behavioral dysregulation but also as a critical determinant of emotional well-being.

While self-control may reflect an individual’s internal regulatory mechanism, another important factor influencing adolescent adjustment is social support, which captures the quality of interpersonal relationships and the perceived emotional availability of others. Previous studies have established a relationship between perceived social support and severity of PIU. For instance, Esen [13] found that adolescents with greater support from their parents and teachers reported lower levels of Internet use difficulties. Maslow suggested that social support, often conceptualized as an emotional and instrumental resource available during times of adversity, might help fulfill core psychological needs such as belongingness, affection, and esteem [14]. In the absence of sufficient support in offline settings, individuals may turn to online communities to fulfill these needs. However, Chou et al. [15] and Chou and Hsiao [16] have found that reliance on digital platforms to satisfy unmet social needs may reinforce habitual or excessive Internet use, contributing to dependency over time.

Importantly, social support is not only associated with PIU but is also with internalizing symptoms, such as depression. Diverse studies by Stice et al. [17] and Dingfelder et al. [18] have indicated that higher levels of social support are associated with a lower risk of depression. Previous studies on the social causation model by Kaniasty and Norris [19] posited that social support was a foundational determinant of psychological well-being and that insufficient support was likely to lead to emotional distress and poor mental health outcomes.

Studies on PIU often classify users into low- and high-risk groups based on validated scales. For example, Young’s Internet Addiction Test (YIAT) originally developed by Young categorizes scores 0–39 as “normal users” (low-risk), 40–69 as “high-risk,” and 70–100 as “Internet addiction” [20]. In adolescent samples, Kormas et al. [21] and Jang et al. [22] suggested that the high-risk group was usually much larger than the fully addicted group. According to Young and Rogers [23], these high-risk youths show significant psychosocial differences, such as higher scores for depression. Kormas et al. [21] found higher odds of hyperactivity/inattention problems, whereas Naseri et al. [24] suggested that high-risk users might experience poorer social support than low-risk users.

Taken together, the literature suggests that self-control and social support are essential psychological and interpersonal resources that mediate the effects of PIU on adolescent mental health. This study examined the mediating roles of self-control and social support in the relationship between PIU and depressive symptoms among Korean adolescents. Based on previous studies by Kormas et al. [21], Young and Rogers [23], and Naseri et al. [24], this study hypothesized that these mediating effects differed in the high-risk and normal user groups.

METHODS

Participants

This study used secondary data from the “School-based Internet, Game, and Smartphone User Cohort,” a nationally representative longitudinal panel study conducted by the National Center for Mental Health (NCMH; CSIG-9) in South Korea [25]. The cohort was designed to monitor digital media use and psychological development among Korean youth. Data were collected in three waves: baseline (2015-2016), 12-month follow-up (2016-2017), and 24-month follow-up (2017-2018). Baseline data were used. Detailed information regarding the cohort design and data collection procedures is available in the official user manual published by the NCMH (2023, Ver. 1.1; Publication No. 11-1352629-000130-01) [25].

The participants included students in grades 3 and 4 of elementary school and grade 7 of middle school, as well as their parents. A total of 2319 adolescents were initially enrolled in the study; after de-identification, data from 2294 participants were made publicly available by the NCMH. The participants were recruited using stratified random sampling to ensure national representation across geographic regions and school types.

The survey administration used a mixed-methods data collection strategy. Elementary school students completed structured interviews conducted by trained research assistants using tablet PCs in a one-on-one format. Middle school students completed self-report surveys on desktop computers in school computer laboratories. Parental surveys were conducted through home visits by trained surveyors or in designated school spaces. This study was approved by the Human Subjects Institutional Review Board of the National Centre for Mental Health (IRB No. 116271-2025-20). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the board, as the study analyzed de-identified secondary data.

Measures

Participants’ levels of PIU were assessed using the Korean adaptation of the YIAT originally developed by Young [20] and later validated in Korean by Kim et al. [26] for Korean adolescents. This measure consists of 20 items. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (always), producing total scores ranging from 20 to 100. Higher scores indicated greater levels of PIU. For group classification, the scoring rubric used by Young [20] was adopted: a score of ≤39 was classified as the low-risk group, whereas a score of ≥40 indicated having experienced occasional PIU (high-risk group).

Depressive symptoms were measured using the Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI), a self-report instrument developed by Kovacs [27] to assess the cognitive, affective, and behavioral symptoms of depression in children and adolescents. The Korean version of the CDI has been adapted and validated by Cho and Lee [28]. It includes 27 items, each with three response options scored from 0 to 2, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 54. Higher scores indicated more severe depressive symptoms. The CDI assesses symptoms such as anhedonia, negative mood, interpersonal difficulties, and low self-esteem.

The Gottfredson Self-Control Scale (SS) developed by Gottfredson and Hirschi [29] is a self-report instrument designed to assess adolescents’ ability to resist impulsive behaviors that offer immediate gratification but may lead to negative consequences. The Korean version of the SS used in this study was translated and validated by Nam and Ok [30] and included 20 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree), with a total score ranging from 20 to 80. Higher scores indicated higher levels of self-control.

The Social Support Appraisal Scale (SSAS) assesses children’s perceived emotional and instrumental support from their families, peers, and teachers. The scale consists of 24 self-report items scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 24 to 120. Higher scores indicated a higher perception of available social support. The original version was developed by Dubow and Ullman [31], and the Korean adaptation was introduced by Kang and Lee [32].

All four major instruments used in this study—the YIAT, CDI, SS, and SSAS—were administered as self-report measures. For the third- and fourth-grade elementary school students, trained research assistants conducted structured oneon-one interviews using tablet PCs to record responses, whereas seventh-grade middle school students completed the questionnaires directly as self-administered surveys on desktop computers in school computer laboratories [25].

Sociodemographic data were collected from the elementary school students, their parents, and middle school students. The variables included gender, birth year, household composition (two-parent vs. single-parent), and perceived socioeconomic status (SES). SES was rated on a 5-point scale and categorized into low (1–2), middle (3), and high (4–5). Household composition was derived from responses to cohabiting family members and parental status (e.g., divorce, bereavement, separation, and single parenthood).

Statistical analyses

The participants’ sociodemographic and psychological factors were assessed. Independent-sample t-tests were conducted to examine group differences in the major study variables (YIAT, SS, SSAS, and CDI). The hypothesized mediational model, wherein self-control or social support was included as a mediator in the relationship between PIU and depressive symptoms, was tested using Baron and Kenny’s method [33], which comprised three steps: 1) determining the relationship between the independent and outcome variables, 2) determining the relationship between the independent variable and mediator, and 3) determining the relationship between the mediator and dependent variable and examining how controlling for the mediator changed the relationship in step 1. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 29.0.1.0; IBM Corp.), with statistical significance defined as an alpha level <0.05.

To further evaluate the practical significance of the mediating pathways, this study examined standardized indirect effects with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and also conducted serial mediation analyses (PIU → social support → self-control → depression / PIU → self-control → social support → depression) using additional PROCESS macro for SPSS (Model 6; Hayes, 2017; available at http://processmacro.org), with subgroup analyses by risk level.

The missing data were assessed and found to be missing completely at random. Given the low rate of missing responses (<2%) and large sample size, listwise deletion was used in the mediation analyses.

RESULTS

A total of 2294 adolescents participated in this study. Men accounted for 56.97% (n=1307) of the sample, whereas women accounted for 43.03% (n=987). Most participants lived in two-parent households (92.46%), and nearly half of them reported a middle SES (48.69%). The mean YIAT score for the overall sample was 40.82 (standard deviation [SD]=14.52), the mean self-control score was 60.98 (SD=8.19), the mean social support score was 96.85 (SD=16.25), and the mean CDI score was 8.24 (SD=7.09) (Table 1).

Independent-samples t-tests revealed that the low-risk group reported significantly lower scores on the YIAT (mean [M]=30.19, SD=5.25 vs. M=53.35, SD=11.62, p<0.001) and CDI (M=5.91, SD=5.51 vs. M=10.98, SD=7.72, p<0.001) and significantly higher scores on the SS (M=64.17, SD=7.84 vs. M=57.24, SD=6.91, p<0.001) and SSAS (M=100.91, SD=15.42 vs. M=92.08, SD=15.90, p<0.001) than the high-risk group.

Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the relationships among PIU, self-control, social support, and depressive symptoms. Results showed that PIU was significantly negatively correlated with self-control (r=-0.502, p<0.001) and social support (r=-0.326, p<0.001) and positively correlated with depressive symptoms (r=0.448, p<0.001). Self-control was positively correlated with social support (r=0.585, p<0.001) and negatively correlated with depressive symptoms (r=-0.460, p<0.001). Social support was also negatively correlated with depressive symptoms (r=-0.516, p<0.001). These results indicated that higher levels of self-control and perceived social support were associated with lower levels of PIU and depressive symptoms in adolescents.

Mediation analyses were conducted to assess whether self-control and social support mediated the relationship between PIU and depressive symptoms. In the total sample, self-control and social support partially mediated this relationship. Higher PIU was associated with lower self-control and social support, which, in turn, were associated with increased depressive symptoms. However, even after accounting for these mediators, the direct relationship between PIU and depression remained significant, indicating a partial mediation (Table 2).

In addition, the hypothesized mediation model was tested separately for low- and high-risk groups. Hierarchical regression analyses indicated that self-control fully mediated the relationship between PIU and depressive symptoms among the students in the low-risk group. When self-control was included as a mediator, the direct effect of Internet use on depression became statistically non-significant, suggesting complete mediation of the psychological pathway. By contrast, in the high-risk group, self-control partially mediated this relationship. Although self-control significantly predicted depressive symptoms, the direct association between PIU and depressive symptoms remained significant even after controlling for self-control. Regarding social support, results showed a partial mediation in both the low- and high-risk groups, similar to the results observed in the total group. In the low-risk group, perceived social support partially explained the relationship between PIU and depressive symptoms, whereas the direct effect of Internet use remained significant. Serial mediation analyses in the total sample showed that the serial mediation pathway (PIU → self-control → social support → depression) had the strongest effect (effect=0.050, 95% CI [0.037, 0.065]), followed by the self-control-only (effect=0.027, 95% CI [0.015, 0.039]) and social support-only paths (effect=0.008, 95% CI [-0.001, 0.016]). In the low-risk group, the indirect effect via self-control alone was the strongest (effect=0.071, 95% CI [0.049, 0.094]), whereas the same serial path as above also had a significant effect (effect=0.066, 95% CI [0.047, 0.088]), and the social support-only path was minimal (effect=0.001, 95% CI [-0.015, 0.018]). In the high-risk group, the same serial path again had the largest effect (effect=0.040, 95% CI [0.030, 0.051]), with smaller but significant effects via self-control (effect=0.015, 95% CI [0.001, 0.031]) and social support (effect=0.015, 95% CI [-0.004, 0.034]).

DISCUSSION

Our study found that adolescents with higher levels of PIU exhibited significantly lower levels of self-control and perceived social support, along with higher levels of depressive symptoms. Mediation analyses in the total sample demonstrated that self-control and social support partially mediated the relationship between PIU and depression. These results are consistent with previous findings associating PIU with increased depressive symptoms and reduced psychosocial resources. Previous studies by Ha et al. [4] confirmed the association between PIU and depression in adolescents, whereas Li et al. [11] and Dewall et al. [12] found that self-control negatively predicted PIU and depressive symptoms. Esen [13] and Rueger et al. [34] have also indicated that social support is negatively associated with PIU and depression in adolescents.

When participants were divided into low- and high-risk groups based on their YIAT scores, different patterns emerged. In the low-risk group, self-control fully mediated the relationship between PIU and depressive symptoms, indicating that a higher self-regulatory capacity might prevent the development of depressive symptoms, even when PIU was present. Conversely, in the high-risk group, self-control only partially mediated this relationship, suggesting that among adolescents with higher levels of PIU, self-regulation alone might not be sufficient to fully buffer depressive outcomes. Perceived social support was a partial mediator in both groups, reinforcing its role as a consistent but not all-encompassing protective factor.

Serial mediation analyses provided additional insights into the mechanisms associating PIU with depressive symptoms. In the high-risk and total samples, the pathway through both self-control and social support sequentially exerted the strongest indirect effect, suggesting that reduced self-control might weaken social support, which in turn contributed to heightened depressive symptoms. This pattern highlights the interdependence of self-regulatory capacity and social connectedness in shaping emotional outcomes. In the low-risk group, self-control alone was the most influential mediator with the strongest indirect effect. Nevertheless, the serial pathway contributed meaningfully to this group, albeit to a lesser extent.

The observed full mediation effect of self-control in the low-risk group suggests that even when adolescents experience levels of PIU, those with higher self-regulatory capacity are less likely to develop depressive symptoms. This aligns with the existing theoretical models stated by Ainslie [9] and Peluso et al. [10] and the empirical evidence suggested by Nie et al. [35] and Tangney et al. [36] which emphasize the protective function of self-control in emotional and behavioral regulation. Conversely, the partial mediation observed in the high-risk group might be due to diminished regulatory capacity resulting from chronic Internet engagement, as neurocognitive studies by Ding et al. [37] showed structural and functional brain changes responsible for impulse control and emotion regulation in heavy digital media users. In this study, adolescents in the low-risk group reported significantly higher levels of self-control than their high-risk peers, who exhibited markedly reduced self-control. This findings aligned with those of previous empirical studies by Mei et al. [38], which showed that adolescents in low-risk groups demonstrated higher self-regulatory capacity (i.e., greater self-control) than those in high-risk groups.

PIU, self-control, and depression may share overlapping neurobiological mechanisms, including dysfunction of the stress-response system and weakened activity in the prefrontal cortex. Previous research by Tereshchenko [39] indicated that PIU was associated with stress-related physiological dysregulation (e.g., imbalance of the autonomic nervous system and cortisol alterations because of chronic stress). At the same time, another study conducted by Cui et al. [40] suggested that major depression could involve hyperactivation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) stress-response axis, and Pizzagalli and Roberts [41] found functional impairments in prefrontal cortical regions responsible for self-control and executive function. These shared patterns suggest common neurobiological vulnerabilities in both conditions. Adolescence, in particular, is a developmental period during which the prefrontal cortex is still maturing, and neural activity tends to favor motivational (reward) regions over control regions, resulting in lower levels of self-control [37,42]. Therefore, in adolescence, the degree of maturation of the prefrontal cortex and subsequent development of self-regulatory capacity may have a critical impact on psychopathology such as addiction and depression.

Consistent with our results, social support emerged as a significant but partial mediator between PIU and depression in both the total group and subgroups, consistent with the findings of Jiang et al. [43]. According to Aneshensel and Stone [44], social support is widely recognized as a buffer between stress and depressive symptoms. Esen [13] stated that in the general adolescent population, higher social support predicted a lower risk of PIU, and Li et al. [45] found that unsatisfactory social support led to a more compensatory Internet use. Previous studies by van Harmelen et al. [46], Chang et al. [47], and Pössel et al. [48] also found that more social support indicated fewer depressive symptoms in adolescents. However, perceived social support seemed to help but did not suffice in completely protecting adolescents from the emotional harm of PIU, which aligned with previous findings by Rueger et al. [34].

This study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design restricted its ability to draw causal inferences between variables. Reliance on self-reported data could introduce social desirability and recall biases, and future studies should incorporate behavioral or observer-based assessments. Additionally, other contextual factors, such as participants’ exact age, academic stress, parenting style, or sleep disturbances, which were not included in the current model, might influence the relationships among Internet use, self-control, social support, and depression. Thus, future studies using richer datasets may incorporate these important variables to build more comprehensive explanatory models.

From an intervention standpoint, our findings suggest that enhancing self-regulatory capacity in adolescents who are not yet addicted but show emerging patterns of PIU may be a potential preventive strategy—not only against PIU but also against the development of depressive symptoms. Wang et al. [49] also showed that youths with greater self-regulatory ability were associated with a lower risk of developing PIU and were found to be more resilient against stress, anxiety, and depression. To achieve this, several intervention strategies should be considered, such as increasing self-regulation and social-emotional learning. This aligned with previous studies by Ayub et al. [50], which indicated that interventions focusing on self-regulation were effective in reducing the symptoms of PIU in youths. Additionally, school-based social and emotional learning programs could strengthen coping skills and resilience, which might help reduce depressive symptoms. For high-risk individuals, multifaceted interventions that incorporate cognitive restructuring, parental involvement, and structured offline activities may be more appropriate. Programs that promote offline bonding and reduce reliance on digital substitutes for social connections may also help mitigate depressive symptoms associated with PIU.

CONCLUSION

This study emphasizes the mediating roles of self-control and social support in the relationship between PIU and depressive symptoms among Korean adolescents. These findings call for stratified mental health strategies: prevention efforts that reinforce self-control among the general youth population and comprehensive interventions for adolescents already experiencing PIU.
