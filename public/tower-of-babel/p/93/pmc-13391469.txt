Analysis of Infection Types, Pathogens, Antimicrobial Treatment, and Clinical Outcomes Data in Hospitalised Patients: Comprehensive Online Database for Antimicrobial Resistance (CODAR) Retrospective Pilot Study
Authors: Andy Townsend, Naglaa Mohamed, Alona Kuraieva, Kristi Kuper, Alexandra Passarelli, Lili Jiang, Katherine Perez, Russell Outlaw, Vaughan Reed, Neera Hobson, Wendy Wilkinson, Stuart Bond, Jade Lee-Milner, Kuldeep Kumar, Anu Gupta, Ravneet Kaur, Yashesh Paliwal, Rafael Franco-Cendejas, Alexandra Martin-Onraët, Juan Luis Mosqueda-Gómez, Vicente Estrada, Ali Somily, Anthony Wilson, Tim Felton, Mahableshwar Albur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective surveillance of prevalent infections, pathogen distribution, and treatment patterns help inform patient management and enhance outcomes. The Comprehensive Online Database for Antimicrobial Resistance (CODAR) pilot study aimed to provide insights into epidemiology, existing treatment strategies, and outcomes.MethodsThis surveillance study (February 2023–2024) across 21 hospitals in India, Mexico, Saudi Arabia, Spain, and the United Kingdom (UK) evaluated pathogen distribution, antimicrobial treatments, and survival rates in hospitalised patients using electronic health records and hospital reports.ResultsAmong 8060 eligible patient records, 7434 (92.2%) with complete data were included in this analysis. Urinary tract infections (UTI), predominantly occurring in female patients (59.0%), were the most prevalent across countries (29.0%). Comorbidities such as vascular and metabolic and nutritional disorders were most commonly observed across countries included in this study. Meropenem and amoxicillin-clavulanate were most commonly used antimicrobials in India and the UK, respectively, across most infection types. Patients with skin and skin structure infections had the highest survival rates (94.4%) and shortest hospital stay (mean [standard deviation], 20.6 [33.53] days), whereas those with bloodstream infections (BSI, 81.7%), mixed infections (82.1%), and lower respiratory tract infections (LRTI, 83.3%) had relatively lower survival rates. Low proportion of patients alive (≤55.0%) with both monotherapy and combination therapy were observed with Acinetobacter baumannii (LRTI), Klebsiella pneumoniae (LRTI and BSI), and Escherichia coli (UTI) in India and Staphylococcus aureus (SSSI) and Enterococcus faecium (UTI) in the UK.ConclusionLow survival rates associated with infections caused by specific pathogens highlight challenges in managing these infections, necessitating ongoing surveillance for effective infection control with suitable antimicrobial therapies to enhance patient care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44197-026-00578-z.Keywords: Infectious diseases, Microbiological surveillance, Electronic health records, Antimicrobial treatment, Laboratory information management system, Microbiology laboratory data, Hospitalised patients

Background

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study estimated that 13.7 million infection-related deaths occurred globally in 2019, with lower respiratory tract infections (LRTI) causing 4.0 million (95% uncertainty interval [UI], 3.33–4.89) deaths and bloodstream infections (BSI), 2.9 million (95% UI, 1.74–4.53) deaths [1]. Peritoneal and intra-abdominal infections (IAI) accounted for 1.3 million (95% UI, 0.83–1.86) deaths [1]. Globally, Staphylococcus aureus was the leading cause of death in 135 countries, followed by Escherichia coli in 37 countries, Streptococcus pneumoniae in 24 countries, and Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii in 4 countries each. S. pneumoniae and S. aureus were leading causes of LRTI and BSI, respectively, while E. coli was the most common causative pathogen for IAI [1]. Another analysis of GBD 2019 estimated that over 404.6 million individuals worldwide experienced urinary tract infections (UTI), resulting in 236,786 deaths [2]. Of 4,859.3 million new cases of skin and skin structure infections (SSSI) included in GBD 2019, the majority were fungal (34.0%) and bacterial (23.0%) skin diseases, leading to 98,522 deaths [3]. Reducing the burden of infectious diseases and infection-related mortality remains a critical global public health priority. Given the high mortality rates associated with infectious diseases and limited availability of effective treatment options, ongoing surveillance through comprehensive surveillance programmes, large multi-centre studies, and institutional studies is essential to identify the most significant public health threats and inform treatment strategies and prevention programmes effectively [1, 4–10]. Despite efforts to improve surveillance study designs, most programmes face challenges such as operational complexity and the lack of adequate sample metadata to contextualise isolates epidemiologically, risking bias in the study. Hence, they do not replace institutional or hospital-based epidemiological studies, which monitor pathogen distribution, prevalence of resistant pathogens, and patient outcomes to help evaluate treatment protocols and implement targeted interventions based on their unique circumstances.

The pilot for the Comprehensive Online Database for Antimicrobial Resistance (CODAR), a retrospective, observational, descriptive, surveillance study, aimed to integrate microbiology laboratory data (including antimicrobial treatment information) and longitudinal clinical data from hospitalised patients, primarily sourced from hospital electronic health records (EHRs) and laboratory information management systems (LIMS). The CODAR pilot further aimed to harmonise data from multiple hospital sites into a coherent database to facilitate analysis and reporting, thereby assessing the feasibility for a similar, larger-scale endeavour that incorporates data from additional countries and regions. The objective of the current analysis is exploration of epidemiological and clinical data generated as part of CODAR to gain preliminary insights into patient management and outcomes. Data was collected retrospectively from patients hospitalised across sites in India, Mexico, Saudi Arabia, Spain, and the United Kingdom (UK) for any period between January 2020 and February 2024.

Methods

Study Design

The study period for this retrospective, observational, surveillance pilot study was February 2023–2024, with data collected retrospectively from patients hospitalised for any period between January 2020 and February 2024. The data was collected from a total of 21 hospital sites spread across India, Mexico, Saudi Arabia, Spain, and the United Kingdom (UK) (Table 1). Characteristics of the hospitals/sites included in this study have been described in Table S1. Data collection was managed by the study coordinator at each site, with data transferred via an electronic case report form (eCRF) or Excel spreadsheets, followed by batch uploads of mapped CSV files onto the OpenClinica (Needham, MA) electronic data capture (EDC) database. Sources included EHRs, paper-based medical records, and LIMS reports. Continuous data cleaning was performed within the EDC system, with key parameters including therapy assessment and clinical outcomes necessitating both computer-based and manual supervision. Study coordinators and relevant staff had full access to EHR and EDC systems to address queries and make changes. Clean data were intended for export to the Microbiological Analysis and Reporting System (MARS; Micron Research Ltd, Ely, UK) for regular analysis and reporting. However, due to data heterogeneity, simultaneous cleaning and upload to MARS were not feasible as planned (Fig. 1) and this was instead completed after the study ended.

Study Patients

The number of patients at each site was based on the institution’s size and the number of patients treated for infectious diseases. The inclusion in the current analysis was based on voluntary site participation and data availability, and for inclusion in the current analysis, all patient records at these sites were required to have documented relevant demographic information, including age and sex, identification of at least one target pathogen, at least one type of infection as outlined in the protocol during screening, and at least one night of hospitalisation. Records from adult patients (≥ 18 years) with confirmed infections from pre-specified hospital and community bacterial and fungal pathogens of interest over an 8–14-month period (Table S2) were eligible. These patients included those with IAI, UTI, SSSI, LRTI (including community-associated pneumonia [CAP], hospital-associated pneumonia [HAP], and ventilator-associated pneumonia [VAP]), BSI, and central nervous system (CNS) infections, occurring alone or together (mixed infections, defined as a patient with multiple [> 1] infection). Data from patients admitted before January 2020, those participating in clinical trials of new antimicrobial agents, or those with duplicate isolates (same species) were excluded, as were isolates from surveillance swabs or in-situ drains.

Outcomes Assessed

Observational data collected included baseline patient information such as demographics, medical history, comorbidities, and antimicrobial treatment over the past six months. Modes of antimicrobial treatment included in this analysis were monotherapy and combination therapy, and the treatment strategies included were empiric therapy and modified therapy approaches. Empiric therapy is the usual standard of care for a patient presenting with a particular type of infection, prior to identification of the infectious organism. Modified therapy refers to the addition of a new antimicrobial to the treatment regimen within four days of initiation of empiric therapy. An overview of country-specific clinical guidelines used for prescribing empiric therapy is provided in Table S3. Survival was analysed and expressed as the proportion of patients alive. Data collected from hospital records did not consistently distinguish between empiric and targeted therapy nor systematically capture empiric therapy data across regions. Microbiological data included the type of specimen and date of collection, causative pathogens, and details of antimicrobial treatment during hospitalisation, along with therapy for comorbidities. Clinical outcomes, determined by the treating physician as the patient’s life status (alive, deceased, or unknown) at discharge, were also documented, as were the length of hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) stays.

Ethics

This non-interventional, retrospective, observational analysis ensured the protection of patients’ identity through the pseudonymisation of data within the EDC system. The study analysis protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board and/or Independent Ethics Committee and local data protection agencies. Adherence to legal, regulatory, and other applicable guidelines including Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practices, Good Practices for Outcomes Research, Good Practices for Real-World Data Studies of Treatment and/or Comparative Effectiveness, International Ethical Guidelines for Epidemiological Studies, European Medicines Agency (EMA) and European Network of Centres for Pharmacoepidemiology and Pharmacovigilance (ENCePP) Guide on Methodological Standards in Pharmacoepidemiology, ENCePP Code of Conduct for scientific independence and transparency in the conduct of pharmacoepidemiological and pharmacovigilance studies, and Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Guidance for Industry and FDA staff ensured scientific integrity.

Statistical Analyses

The study’s primary aim was descriptive, focusing on characterising infection and antimicrobial use patterns and outcomes within countries rather than hypothesis testing between groups. Categorical variables were summarised using frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables using descriptive statistics including the number of patients with an observation (n) and the mean, standard deviation (SD), median, minimum, and maximum values. The number and percentage of patients for whom all the above variables were correctly recorded and linked were calculated by country and in total. All tables and listings were produced using SAS® Version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). The study size was based on patients with ‘complete’ data: patient records which fulfilled the inclusion criteria based on demographics, infection, and hospitalisation (as defined in “Sect. 2.2 Study Patients”) were included in the analysis and missing data were not imputed.

Use of Artificial Intelligence Tool

During the preparation of this work, the author(s) used Pfizer’s generative artificial intelligence (AI) tool ‘Medical AI Assistant’ (MAIA) to assist with the production of sections of the first draft of the manuscript. Thereafter, the authors reviewed and edited the content as needed and take full responsibility for the content of the publication. No AI tool(s) were used for data collection processes or data analysis.

Results

Demographics and Baseline Characteristics

Of 8060 patient records screened in the CODAR pilot study from India, Mexico, Saudi Arabia, Spain, and the UK, 7434 (92.2%) were analysed. The demographic characteristics of those patients included in the analysis are summarised by country and infection type in Table 1. Most patients were from the UK (n = 5377, 93.5% of records screened), followed by India (n = 1187, 87.3%), Mexico (n = 539, 89.5%), Spain (n = 231, 93.9%), and Saudi Arabia (n = 100, 100%). Across all infection types, patients were predominantly male, except for UTI (59.0% female). The overall mean (SD) patient age was 64.9 (18.89) years. Over 40.0% of patients in Spain and the UK were aged ≥ 75 years, as were ≥ 44.2% of patients with UTI or mixed infections. A large proportion of patients (≥ 65.7%) with IAI or CNS infections were in the two youngest age groups (Table 1). Hospital-related characteristics at admission are summarised by country in Table S4. General medicine (22.2%) and emergency ward (20.6%) admissions were highest in India. Countries reporting the highest number of unspecified ward type at the time of admission were Saudi Arabia (78.0%), the UK (44.0%), and Mexico (34.1%). For patients with available ward information in these countries, ICU (19.0%), acute care (28.8%), and oncology (12.6%) wards, respectively, were the most common admission wards. Except for Spain, community-associated infections were more prevalent in each country than hospital-associated infections (overall, 59.3%; with the highest rates in Saudi Arabia [68.0%] and the UK [61.6%]).

Distribution of Infection Types by Country

Overall, the most frequent infection type was UTI (29.0%; although there were no patients with UTI in Saudi Arabia), followed by BSI (26.4%) (Table S5). The most common infection type was UTI in India (30.9%) and the UK (31.5%); BSI in Mexico (33.0%) and Saudi Arabia (75.0%); and LRTI in Spain (28.1%).

Pathogen Distribution

The distribution of pathogens based on infection type and country is summarised in Table S6. Overall, E. coli was the leading cause of IAI and UTI across countries included in the study. Haemophilus influenzae was the most common LRTI pathogen, despite being isolated from only two countries (Spain [12.3%] and the UK [26.0%]). SSSI isolates were primarily S. aureus, with highest rates observed in the UK (62.1%), followed by Mexico (27.2%) and Spain (25.4%). BSI were mostly caused by E. coli in Mexico (42.1%), Saudi Arabia (30.7%), and India (24.7%). Mixed infections mostly implicated E. coli, with the highest rates in Mexico (56.8%) and the UK (47.4%). Only India and the UK had ≥ 10 isolates from CNS infections, most commonly S. aureus (n = 6), E. coli (n = 5), and S. pneumoniae (n = 5).

Antimicrobial Treatment for Infections

Overall, 83.3% of patients and by country, ≥ 90.0% of patients in Mexico, Saudi Arabia, Spain, and the UK were treated with an antimicrobial. In India, the use of antimicrobial agents was not recorded in 54.8% of patients. Overall, the most common antimicrobial classes used were the β-lactam combination agents (51.5%); however, variations were observed by country. In India, carbapenems (mostly meropenem) were the most frequently used antimicrobial class (21.2%; Table S7) across all infections (except mixed infections, Table S8). In Mexico, cephems (55.3%; mostly ceftriaxone) and carbapenems (54.7%; mostly meropenem) were the most commonly used antimicrobial classes (Tables S7 and S8). In Saudi Arabia, the most frequently used antimicrobial classes were the β-lactam combination agents (73.0%; mostly piperacillin-tazobactam) and glycopeptides (60.0%; vancomycin), (Tables S7 and S8). In Spain, β-lactam combination agents (53.2%; mostly amoxicillin-clavulanate and piperacillin-tazobactam), followed by carbapenems (38.1%; mostly meropenem) and fluoroquinolones (38.1%; mostly ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin), were the most frequently used (Tables S7 and S8). In the UK, β-lactam combination agents (60.4%; mostly amoxicillin-clavulanate and piperacillin-tazobactam) followed by aminoglycosides (28.4%; mostly gentamicin) were most frequently used (Tables S7 and S8).

Clinical Outcomes and Comorbidities

Comorbidities were recorded based on the system organ classes affected and stratified by country (Table S9). The presence of comorbidities was documented from patient medical history records, with most patients having at least one recorded comorbidity (79.9%) and 66.6% of patients having two or more comorbidities. The most commonly implicated system organ class varied by country and were vascular disorders in India (35.6%) and the UK (44.3%), neoplasms in Mexico (57.9%), and metabolism and nutrition disorders in Saudi Arabia (51.0%) and Spain (51.1%).

No clear pattern was observed between survival rates and number of comorbidities (0, 1, and > 1 comorbidities) in most cases (a combination of a pathogen and country associated with an infection type was defined as a single case), as the number of patients were mostly too low to make a determination. A minimum of 10 patients in each of the three comorbidity categories was required for this determination and only 15/68 cases qualified for inclusion. Of these 15 cases, survival rates decreased with increase in number of comorbidities in 4 cases. However, survival rates were similar across categories in 2 cases, and only in the remaining 2 cases was a downward trend in survival with increasing number of comorbidities observed i.e., LRTI in India caused by K. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa. In 1 case (LRTI in UK caused by H. influenzae), this pattern was reversed. In another case, survival rates remained the same across all three comorbidity categories (SSSI in India caused by S. aureus) (Table 2).

Survival Rates and Duration of Hospital Stay

Of 7434 patients, 87.1% were discharged alive, with the UK having the highest survival rate (87.8%, 4720/5377) and Saudi Arabia the lowest (72.0%, 72/100). Overall, patients with SSSI had the highest survival rate (94.4%) and the shortest hospital stay (mean [11], 20.6 [33.53] days). Patients with UTI had a 92.0% survival rate and a mean (SD) stay of 22.5 (26.78) days. Patients with mixed infections and IAI had the longest (mean [SD]) stays (25.0 [26.46] and 24.7 [27.64] days, respectively), with survival rates of 82.1% and 89.7%, respectively. Patients with BSI had an 81.7% survival rate, and a mean (SD) stay of 21.2 (27.64) days. Similarly, patients with LRTI and CNS infections had lower survival rates (83.3% and 84.4%, respectively) with mean (SD) stays of 23.0 (26.27) and 23.2 (22.02) days, respectively (Table 3).

Discussion

This was a non-interventional, retrospective, observational, multi-centre pilot study conducted across 21 hospitals in 5 countries spanning 4 geographical regions, for collection of epidemiological, clinical, and microbiological data.

Distribution of Pathogens and Infection Types

In line with prior studies [1, 12–14], the distribution of pathogens varied by country and infection type. E. coli was the predominant pathogen for IAI, UTI, BSI, and mixed infections, H. influenzae for LRTI, and S. aureus for SSSI. Also, S. aureus isolates from SSSI had the highest rates in the UK (62.1%) compared with other countries included in this study. The disproportionately high number of SSSI isolates from the UK may reflect a combination of factors and it is important to interpret the surveillance data in light of local clinical practices, population health factors, and methodological heterogeneity. We also report that overall and in India and the UK, UTI were the most prevalent infection type. This is in accordance with a previous analysis of the GBD 2019 study where a large number of UTI cases were observed globally (404.6 million [95% UI, 359.4–446.5]) with the highest incidence of UTI reported in India (100.8 million [88.1–112.6]) [2]. Similarly, in the UK, over 1.8 million hospital admissions involving UTIs were reported during 2018–19 and 2022–23, with UTI considered as most common causes of emergency hospital admissions [15, 16]. Additionally, per the recent findings from the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA), 189,756 hospital admissions related to UTI occurred in UK during 2023–2024, an increase of 9% from the previous year [17].

Except for Spain, all participating countries exhibited higher rates of community-associated infections than hospital-associated infections. This finding warrants contextualization within each country’s healthcare system and community health landscape. In the UK, a high proportion of community-associated infections (61.6%) was observed, which aligns with national surveillance data on increased antimicrobial use for these infections [18]. The presence of robust primary care networks and comprehensive electronic health record systems in the UK may have facilitated early detection and reporting of community-onset infections [19, 20]. Similarly, in Saudi Arabia, a predominance of community-acquired infections was observed in this study (68.0%) similar to that documented in a recent point prevalence survey conducted across Middle East, including Saudi Arabia [21]. In contrast, Spain reported a higher proportion of hospital-associated infections (55.8%), which may be influenced by patient demographics (notably, a higher proportion of elderly patients). These findings are consistent with earlier Spanish epidemiological studies that highlighted the burden of nosocomial infections in older populations and tertiary and long-term care settings [22, 23].

The observed inter-country differences may also reflect variations in healthcare infrastructure, antimicrobial stewardship implementation, and public health interventions such as vaccination coverage and community-based infection prevention programs. For example, the UK’s longstanding national antimicrobial stewardship initiatives and vaccination campaigns may have contributed to shifting infection patterns toward the community setting [24, 25]. It is important to note that the pilot nature of the CODAR study, including voluntary site participation and inconsistent data completeness, may introduce selection bias. Therefore, while our findings provide valuable real-world insights, they should be interpreted in the context of these limitations and may not be fully generalizable to all healthcare settings within each country. Future studies, with expanded site participation and standardized data collection, will enable more granular analyses of the interplay between healthcare system characteristics, community health status, and infection epidemiology.

Antimicrobial Treatment

Most patients received an antimicrobial (83.3%) for treatment of infections during the study, with ≥ 90.0% of patients receiving antimicrobial treatment in most countries included in this study. However, in India, use of antimicrobial agents was not recorded in 54.8% of patients in this study. A cross-sectional analysis noted the lack of a formal system to monitor antibiotic use in India, and reported a relatively lower per capita antibiotic consumption in India compared to many countries [26].

In this study, across infection types, β-lactam combination agents (51.5%) were the most commonly used antimicrobial class for treatment, mirroring the most prevalent antimicrobials reported in the global point prevalence survey from 53 countries and reflecting the broad-spectrum activity and effectiveness of β-lactam combination agents against a wide range of bacterial pathogens, making them a cornerstone in the treatment of serious infections [27, 28]. In accordance with this, amoxicillin-clavulanate was among the most commonly used antimicrobials in the UK (30.2%–71.5%) in this study. Although amoxicillin-clavulanate is present in the Watch category in the UK’s AWaRe classification system with experts determining its use as prone to fostering bacterial resistance, it is recommended as first-line treatment for respiratory tract infections in European guidelines (including in the UK) and by the World Health Organization (WHO) as first choice of drug for most primary care infections [29, 30].

Consistent with national multi-centre point prevalence surveys, meropenem was the most frequently used antimicrobial for most infection types (9.9%–44.4%) in India in this study [31, 32]. Other studies from India have also indicated that meropenem was the preferred treatment for managing challenging infections caused by multidrug- and extensively drug-resistant pathogens [33, 34]. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of tailoring antimicrobial prescribing to local resistant pathogens and the need for continuous surveillance to guide effective treatment strategies.

Patients With Comorbidities

In this study, 66.6% of patients had two or more comorbidities. The presence of multiple comorbidities may be indicative of complex medical histories among the patient population. Notably, comorbidities such as vascular and metabolic and nutritional disorders were prevalent across countries included in this study. Similarly, two systematic literature reviews and a cross-sectional study on global and multi-country multimorbidity patterns, identified diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular and metabolic disorders as the most prevalent concomitant comorbidities [35–37]. Extensive research indicates that patients with these comorbidities are at higher risk of developing serious infections resulting in significant health complications necessitating hospitalisation [38–40]. No clear pattern was observed between survival rates and number of comorbidities in most cases in this study. Overall, these findings underscore the similarity in comorbidity patterns observed globally and, in this study, and highlight that the patients assessed here reflect the risk observed in global population for serious infections and hospitalisations associated with concomitant comorbidities.

Survival Rates by Infection Types

Overall, the lowest survival rates were observed for patients with BSI (81.7%), mixed infections (82.1%), and LRTI (83.3%). Mortality estimates from the GBD 2019 study also showed that both BSI and LRTI were associated with the highest number of deaths (over two million each) across regions [1]. Projections from GBD data for 1990–2016 forecasted that LRTI would remain one of the leading causes of years of life lost in 2040 [41]. In the current study, SSSI, UTI, and IAI were associated with comparatively higher survival rates (89.7%–94.4%). SSSI were also associated with the shortest hospital stays, similar to a retrospective, multi-centre study from Spain (2011–2017) with short duration of hospitalisation noted for patients with SSSI [42]. In this study, mixed infections followed by IAI had the longest hospital stays, which may have been due to their complexity and the need for surgical intervention or prolonged antimicrobial therapy [43–45].

Proportion of Patients Alive on Monotherapy and Combination Therapy

Several pathogens and infection types were associated with low proportion of patients alive (≤ 55.0%) with both monotherapy and combination therapy, particularly in India and the UK. In India, these included A. baumannii (LRTI), K. pneumoniae (LRTI and BSI), and E. coli (UTI). In the UK, those included were S. aureus (SSSI) and E. faecium (UTI). Several of these pathogens are categorised as critical or high priority in the WHO priority list of pathogens, due to associations with severe, difficult-to-treat infections and significant contribution to morbidity and mortality [1, 10, 46]. The low proportion of patients alive with combination therapy may have been due to the severity of illness, delayed or inappropriate therapy, and patient characteristics. Patients requiring combination therapy may often have underlying comorbidities, compromised immune systems, or hospital-acquired infections, all of which can increase mortality risk [47–49]. Moreover, combination therapy may be used to target particularly resistant pathogens, which are inherently more challenging to treat and associated with higher mortality [50–52].

Limitations

Our pilot study has a number of limitations for consideration. There was some risk of introducing bias due to the retrospective and observational nature of the study. Patient data available for analyses at each site varied in terms of infection type and causative pathogen. Data from medical records may have been incomplete, inaccurate, or inconsistently assessed across patients, introducing a potential risk of selection bias. The retrospective hospital records did not distinguish the pathogens causing the infections in terms of Gram types and accordingly, this information was not available. Data collected for UTI was not exclusively attributed to pre-operative or surgical infections. Also, low patient numbers for some infection types and countries in this study should be taken into consideration while interpreting the findings. Variations in hospital protocols and procedures, including antimicrobial stewardship, medicines administered to patients (including antimicrobial agents), and site-specific diagnostic algorithms may also act as potential confounders. Lack of multivariate analysis may limit the extrapolation of data from this study. Finally, achieving automated, real-time data cleaning and reporting at regular intervals was challenging due to the study’s large, multi-centre design and site variability. This study revealed that hospitals may find it difficult to implement the required level of automation for data extraction and linking from LIMS or EHRs and existing processes may not support the scale-up of this pilot study. Additionally, process and health-system related infrastructure may vary across different institutions/countries, with many hospital centres facing challenges with continuous data collection and reporting. However, modernisation of Healthcare IT systems and emergence of standardised EHRs combined with machine learning technology (AI) may offer a solution in the future for continuous data collection, thereby resolving the challenge of near real-time monitoring of antibiotic utilisation patterns and resistance trends across different geographies and health care systems. Despite these limitations, this analysis presents real-world findings from multiple countries and demonstrates the value of datasets of this type.

In conclusion, linking microbiological and clinical data in this large multi-centre study offers important observations and trends in infection types, pathogen distribution, antimicrobial treatment, and clinical outcomes across diverse patient populations in different countries. Low survival rates were noted for BSI, LRTI, and mixed infections caused by pathogens such as A. baumannii, S. aureus, K. pneumoniae underscoring the difficulties in managing these infections. Given the limited treatment options and the increasing spread of resistant infections, ongoing surveillance is crucial to establish effective infection control measures and select appropriate antimicrobial therapies. Ultimately, these data-driven approaches are essential for managing and preventing the rapid spread of resistant infections, safeguarding public health, and optimising patient care.
