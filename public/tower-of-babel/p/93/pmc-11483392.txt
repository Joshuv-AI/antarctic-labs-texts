Exploring Electrolyte Adsorption on the Different Types of Layered Cathode Surfaces in Lithium-Ion Batteries via a Universal Neural Network Potential Method
Authors: Attila Taborosi, Hiromasa Shiiba, Michihisa Koyama, Nobuyuki Zettsu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
LiNixMnyCozO2 (NCM) is a promising cathode material for lithium-ion batteries. Their long-term stability and impedance growth as cycles are strongly influenced by the properties of the cathode electrolyte interface (CEI), formed by the reaction between the electrolyte and electrode surface. Understanding of these interactions at the atomic level of the NCM electrode surface and electrolyte will provide a new strategic approach for the design of a highly functional CEI layer. In this study, we explored the influence of Ni content in transition metal layers on surface energies under different synthetic conditions and terminations using a density functional theory (DFT) validated universal neural network potential (UNNP) method. Furthermore, we investigated the adsorption of ethylene carbonate (EC) and dimethyl carbonate (DMC) on the most favorable NCM surfaces. EC and DMC displayed similar adsorption energy trends; however, differences were observed in the preferred configurations, which can affect the formation of the CEI.

Introduction

Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are widely used as power sources in portable electronics, electric vehicles, and stationary storage systems. One of the most promising cathode materials for LIBs is LiNixMnyCozO2 (NCM) with different compositions of Ni, Co, and Mn in the transition metal (TM) layer.1,2 However, phase degradation, side reactions, and crack formation in cathode materials can lead to battery damage and poor cycling ability. The performance of LIB cathodes is strongly influenced by the properties of the cathode electrolyte interface (CEI), formed by the reaction between the electrolyte and electrode surface.3,4 The formation of a stable CEI is essential to achieve high cyclability and long life cycles of LIBs and to avoid the continuous growth of the CEI, which consumes the active Li+ ions and impacts the performance of the battery.5,6 During the initial charge and discharge cycles of the LIB, electrolyte components can be adsorbed onto the electrode surfaces, contributing to the formation of CEI. Furthermore, the adsorption of different electrolyte components can affect the mechanical and electrochemical properties of the CEI. Therefore, understanding the first step of CEI formation, and thus the adsorption of different electrolyte molecules on the cathode or anode surface, is crucial for the subsequent examination of electrolyte decomposition and CEI formation.

Several previous theoretical simulations have been conducted on the surface formation, adsorption, and decomposition of electrolytes at different NCM cathode sites. Kramer et al. investigated the surface energies of LiCoO2 as a function of the external lithium and oxygen chemical potentials.7 They found that the (001) and (104) surfaces are present regardless of Li and O chemical potentials, while the (012) surface is stable only under oxidizing conditions. Hong et al. found similar results during the investigation of surface termination of LiCoO2.8 Garcia et al. studied the stability of several surfaces of NCM333 as a function of Li and O chemical potentials and found that nonpolar (104), polar (001) or (012), and reconstructed polar (110) surfaces were the most stable surfaces.9 Liang et al. found that the NCM811 particles were terminated mostly by the (012) and (001) surfaces under oxygen-poor conditions, whereas the (104) and (001) surfaces were terminated under oxygen-rich conditions.10 Furthermore, the observed elastic anisotropy revealed that uneven deformation was more likely to occur in particles synthesized under poor oxygen conditions. Jiao et al. performed first-principles calculations combined with the Wulff shape construction and crystal orbital Hamilton population analysis for eight types of single-crystal cathodes.11 Surface structural units with different Miller indices were identified, and a linear relationship was established between the surface energy and density of the cleaved bonds, indicating that the NCM surface morphology can be effectively tuned by modulating the TM–O bond strength. Xu et al. studied the decomposition of ethylene carbonate (EC) on the (104) surface of the NCM523 cathode and predicted a very fast chemical reaction involving EC ring opening, independent of the operating voltage of the battery.12 Furthermore, only two initial adsorption structures were considered before the decomposition of EC; thus, a single EC molecule and Li+ ions were coordinated with four EC molecules. Qin et al. examined the oxidative decomposition of EC on the (110) surface of LiCoO2 (LCO) and NCM333.13 They identified two steps in the decomposition reactions; thus, the EC decomposition was initiated by the ring-opening reaction, followed by a proton transfer contributing to hydroxyl group (–OH) formation. Furthermore, they studied the adsorption of EC on the (110) surface of LCO and NCM cathodes with three types of EC orientations, resulting in 26 possible adsorption structures. Shi et al. explored the adsorption energies of Li2S, acetonitrile (AN), EC, and dimethyl carbonate (DMC) on the (003) O termination of NCM523, where the adsorption of the four molecules on the surface followed the order DMC > EC > AN > Li2S.14 However, only one initial adsorption configuration was examined for each electrolyte molecule. Additionally, Pan et al. investigated the adsorption of EC and propane sultone on the (111) surface of NCM333 and found varying adsorption energies that depended on the type of the TM–O bond between the surface and electrolyte molecule.15 However, only two adsorption structures, namely, monodentate adsorption via an ethereal oxygen atoms and bidentate adsorption via the carbonyl and one of the ethereal oxygen atoms, were considered. Leggesse et al. carried out first-principles calculations to study the adsorption and decomposition reaction mechanisms of EC on the (100) surface of charge (Li0.6Mn2O4) and fully discharge LiMn2O4.16 The results showed that adsorption and decomposition can occur on the surfaces; however, decomposition of EC is more likely on the charged surface despite having higher adsorption energy. Ebadi et al. studied the reduction of EC, PC, and DMC on the Li metal anode (100) surface and had found that the CO-producing route is energetically more favorable for EC and PC decomposition.17 Furthermore, EC and PC showed higher adsorption energies than DMC. Huai et al. investigated the degradation mechanism of DMC on the (010) surface of LiCoO2 and delithiated Li1/3CoO2.18 The delithiated surface showed stronger attraction to DMC than the LiCoO2. Okuno et al. investigated the distribution of the adsorption modes of EC and decomposition reaction on the (100) surface of LNMO.19 The most stable adsorption mode was the O atom of EC bonds to the Li atom of the surface, while the methylene group of EC orientation toward the LNMO surface mode was also found. Fuhst et al. studied the decomposition of EC on the (104) surface of LiCoO2 considering the magnetic ordering.20 The magnetic ordering affected the adsorption energy of EC, where the antiferromagnetic surface exhibited the lowest surface energy compared to nonmagnetic or ferromagnetic.

Thus, previous studies have reported energetically preferable surfaces (104), (003), (012), and (110) under different synthetic conditions (oxidative or reductive) for different NCM types (333, 523, and 811). However, these examinations did not compare and analyze the effect of changing the Ni content in the TM layer of NCM from low to high (523, 622, and 811) on the surface energies. Furthermore, different electrolyte (EC, DMC, etc.) adsorption and decomposition studies have provided insights into the possible formation of the CEI; however, the possible adsorption structure of the electrolyte molecules on the cathode surface is limited. Furthermore, the density functional theory (DFT) method imposed the above-mentioned limitations of the extended exploration of surface and electrolyte adsorption and was used in all previously mentioned studies. These limitations can be addressed using neural network potentials (NNPs), especially with emerging and promising universal neural network potentials (UNNPs), where the recreation of NNP data sets for each structure of interest is not necessary. Therefore, in this study, we first explored the effect of the Ni content in the TM layer on the energy of different surface terminations and synthetic conditions (oxidative or reductive) and thus on stoichiometric (ST) and nonstoichiometric (NST) surfaces. Furthermore, we validated the UNNP based on DFT results such as the surface energies of the different surface planes, TM/Li–O distances in the surface layers, and the effect of Ni content in the TM layer. Additionally, we extended the investigation space and studied several initial adsorption structures for the most energetically preferable surface and the effects of Ni content and delithiation on these adsorption configurations.

Computational Methods

The DFT calculations were performed based on the plane-wave basis set and projector-augmented wave method,21 as implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package.22−24 The GGA functional of PBE25 was used to describe the exchange and correlation interactions. A Hubbard U26 term was included for the TM (Ni: 6.20 eV, Co: 3.32 eV, Mn: 3.90 eV) to correct the self-interaction of the TM d orbitals, and the cutoff energy was 520 eV. The calculations of the bulk structure were conducted using 3 × 3 × 3 k-points, while the different surface models used 1 × 1 × 1 k-points. The surface models were constructed as 5 × 2 × 1 supercells of the bulk unit cell with 10 layers for surface energy simulations and 6 layers for the adsorption simulations. During the DFT geometry optimization, the top three TM/Li–O layers were relaxed while the rest of the model was fixed (Figures 1 and S1). An UNNP, called PreFerred Potential27 (PFP) version 3.0 was adopted in this study, and the same geometry optimization conditions were applied as with the DFT calculations to compare the obtained surface energy results.

Most stable bulk structure for NCM523/622/811 created from the LiCoO2 (R3̅m) primitive cell after the determinations of the most preferable TM arrangements using the cluster expansion method and considered surface planes (104), (003), (012), and (101) of various NCM types. The highlighted areas of the surfaces show the relaxed regions during the geometry optimization with DFT and UNNP methods.

The primitive cell of the LCO (R3̅m) crystal structure was used to create the bulk structure of NCM523/622/811 (Figure 1). The TM arrangements for each NCM bulk structure were determined by the cluster expansion method, whereas 5940, 3256, and 325 TM arrangements were considered for NCM523, NCM622, and NCM811, respectively. Then, different surfaces were constructed from the energetically most stable bulk structures using the Pymatgen python package.28 Based on the previous XRD measurements,1 the (104), (003), (012), and (101) surface planes, each with different ST and NST terminations (Figures 1 and S1), were considered. In total, 36 different surface combinations were created (12 for each NCM type), and their surface energies were determined and compared using the two methods (DFT and UNNP) and different NCM types. Further information regarding the procedure for surface energy determination can be found in the Supporting Information. The average TM–O and Li–O distances for the top three optimized layers of each surface were compared. The EC and DMC adsorption studies were carried out on the previously determined most preferable surfaces with Li-1.0 and Li-0.9 compositions (Figure S4). Regarding the determination of the adsorption energy, please refer to the Supporting Information (Figure S5). The 21 initial adsorption configurations for both EC and DMC were examined for each NCM type (523, 622, and 811) and on the four different surface types with Li-1.0/0.9 compositions. Thus, 1008 different adsorption simulations were conducted by using the UNNP method. The theoretical infrared (IR) spectra were determined using the UNNP method along the charges of different atoms (C, H, and O) in the EC and DMC molecules, to determine their dipole moments and calculate the vibrational modes and infrared intensities implemented in the atomic simulation environment (ASE).29 Theoretical IR spectra were obtained for the single molecules of EC and DMC. Furthermore, the average IR spectra were determined from the seven adsorption configurations of EC and DMC on the most preferable (104) and (003) atoms for the different NCM types.

Results and Discussion

Determination of the Most Preferable Surfaces for NCM

First, we identified the thermodynamically favorable surfaces based on their surface energies among four distinct planes, namely, the (104), (003), (012), and (101) planes with their ST and NST terminations determined by the DFT method. Our analysis revealed that regardless of the NCM type, the ST (104) and (003) planes exhibited lower surface energies (<1.0 J/m2) than the ST (012) and (101) planes (Tables S4). To understand the difference in the surface energy, we investigated the distances between the TM-O and Li–O atoms in these planes (Table S6A). The TM-O distances remained similar across all investigated planes. Thus, Ni–O showed longer (2.06 ± 0.07 Å) distance compared to Co–O (1.94 ± 0.06 Å) or Mn–O (1.94 ± 0.06 Å), which showed similar distances. However, the Li–O showed dependency in the function of planes, with the shortest distance (1.89 ± 0.06 Å) for (003), intermediate distances for (012) (2.07 ± 0.09 Å) and (101) (2.05 ± 0.13 Å), and the longest distance (2.13 ± 0.13 Å) for (104). Additionally, the differences observed on the surface of the TM or Li coordination numbers depended on the plane. The coordination numbers were five or six for the (104) and (003) planes (which showed lower surface energies) and three or four for the (012) and (101) planes (which showed higher surface energies).

We then compared the NST (003), (012), and (101) surfaces, while (104) was excluded due to the lack of an NST (Table S5). Specifically, the NST (003) Li–O in reductive and TM termination in both synthetic conditions exhibited high surface energies ranging from 2.5 to 5.2 J/m2 (Table S5). However, the Li–O oxidative condition resulted in a lower surface energy of 1.2 J/m2. The increased surface energies can be attributed to the formation of oxygen molecules on the Li–O termination, low coordination number of Li (three or four), and intermediate distances for Li–O (2.06 ± 0.10 Å). Furthermore, the low coordination number of the TM on the NST (003) TM termination resulted in an increased surface energy. However, the NST (003) Li in reductive and TM–O in oxidative conditions showed lower surface energies ranging from 0.6 to 1.1 J/m2 (Table S5). The Li termination showed shorter Li–O distances (1.91 ± 0.15 Å), but with a coordination number of three. However, the TM–O termination showed longer Li–O distances (2.15 ± 0.07 Å), but a high coordination number of six. Two terminations were considered for the NST (012) surface, namely, the Li–TM and O terminations (Figure S1). The Li–TM termination exhibited high surface energies (3.0 J/m2) across all synthetic conditions, while the O termination exhibited lower surface energies (1.5 and 2.5 J/m2), which can be attributed to the low coordination number of TM and Li. The Li–O distance was short (1.96 ± 0.13 Å) and intermediate (2.07 ± 0.11 Å) for the Li–TM and O terminations. We further examined two terminations on the NST (101) surface, thus, the Li and TM terminations. The Li termination exhibited high surface energy under both oxidative (1.5 J/m2) and reductive (2.5 J/m2) synthetic conditions. The TM termination showed surface energies similar to Li, with 1.2 and 2.0 J/m2 exhibited for oxidative and reductive conditions, respectively. Both NST (101) TM and Li terminations showed coordination numbers of five, however intermediate (2.09 ± 0.09 Å) Li–O distances. In summary, our analysis of the surface energies suggests that the (104) and (003) planes of ST are preferable to the (012) and (101) planes, which trend had been confirmed previously for NCM,9−11 LiCO2,7,8,11 and LiNiO2.10,11 Additionally, under oxidative conditions, NST (003) TM–O was favorable, whereas under reactive conditions, NST (003) Li.

Comparison of the Most Preferable Surfaces between NCM Types

The (104) surface exhibited a decrease in surface energy from 0.86 J/m2 (NCM523) to 0.84 J/m2 (NCM622) and 0.77 J/m2 (NCM811), indicating an increased stability as the number of Ni ions in the TM layer increased (Figure 2). Furthermore, the (104) surface, with one of the lowest surface energies, was one of the predominant surfaces across all NCM types. The value of the surface energy of (104) is similar to the previous studies’ surface energy values of NCM (0.769, 0.5310, 0.8111 J/m2), LiCoO2 (1.057, 0.798 J/m2), LiNiO2 (0.5310, 0.6711 J/m2), and LiMnO2 (0.7111 J/m2) layered materials. The Ni–O distance decreased from 2.06 ± 0.04 Å (NCM523) to 2.03 ± 0.06 Å (NCM622) and 1.99 ± 0.07 Å (NCM811) (Figure 2). Additionally, regarding the Co–O and Mn–O bonds, NCM811 exhibited the shortest distances (1.90 ± 0.03 and 1.92 ± 0.04 Å), while NCM523 and NCM622 displayed longer distances. The Li–O distance remained at 2.13 ± 0.08 Å regardless of the changes in the TM content within the layers. Consequently, only the Ni–O distances changed with an increase in Ni content in the TM layer.

(a) Comparison of the most preferable surfaces, including (104), (003), NST (003) TM-O, and NST (003) Li based on their surface energies. (b) Ni–O, (c) Co–O, (d) Mn–O, and (e) Li–O of the first three layered average distances between the different NCM types (blue circle: NCM523, red diamond: NCM622, and green triangle: NCM811).

The ST (003) type showed an increase in surface energy with increasing Ni content in the TM layer, from 0.89 J/m2 (NCM523) to 0.97 J/m2 (NCM622) and 0.98 J/m2 (NCM811). Furthermore, the (003) type exceeds the second lowest surface energy after (104), which had been confirmed by several previous studies with similar values in NCM (1.049, 0.7810, 0.6211 J/m2), LiCO2 (1.007, 1.048 J/m2), LiNO2 (0.7110, 0.7311 J/m2), and LiMnO2 (0.8711 J/m2) as in this study. A decrease in the Ni–O distance from 2.07 ± 0.04 Å (NCM523) to 2.04 ± 0.07 Å (NCM622) and 2.01 ± 0.09 Å (NCM811), which followed a similar trend as seen for the (104) surface, was observed. Additionally, the Co–O showed a longer distance (2.00 ± 0.08 Å) for NCM811 compared to NCM523 (1.93 ± 0.06 Å) and NCM622 (1.93 ± 0.06 Å), while the Mn–O distance remained constant (1.94 ± 0.03 Å) across the NCM types. The Li–O distance on the ST (003) surface was unaffected by the TM ratio. However, it was shorter (1.88 ± 0.05 Å) compared to the Li–O distance on the (104) surface (2.13 ± 0.08 Å), likely due to the lower coordination number of the Li ions on the ST (003) surface (Figure 1). The NST (003) Li and TM-O terminations exhibited stable surfaces compared to those of NST (003) Li–O and TM, particularly for Li under reductive conditions and TM-O under oxidative conditions. The NST (003) Li surface displayed the highest energy for NCM523 (1.12 J/m2) followed by an intermediate energy for NCM622 (0.96 J/m2) and the lowest for NCM811 (0.86 J/m2). The Ni–O and Mn–O distances showed no variation among the NCM types. However, the Co–O distance decreased as a function of surface energy, and the Ni content in the TM layer increased (Figure 2). The NST (003) TM-O termination presented a stable surface under oxidative conditions for all NCM types, with the lowest surface energy observed for NCM523 (0.62 J/m2), followed by an intermediate value for NCM622 (0.69 J/m2) and the highest value for NCM811 (1.08 J/m2). A longer Ni–O distance, shorter Co–O distance, and fewer Ni ions in the TM layer (NCM523) are preferable for the NST (003) TM-O surface type (Figure 2).

Validation of the PFP Method Based on the NCM Surfaces

After evaluating and comparing the various surfaces of the NCM types using the DFT method, we calculated the surface energies using the PFP method. We compared the energies and TM/Li–O bond distances obtained using the two methods (Figure 3). The overall correlation between the surface energies obtained by using DFT and PFP was 0.93. Breaking down the comparison for the specific NCM types (523, 622, and 811), the NCM622 surface energies showed the highest correlation (R2 = 0.95), while the NCM523 (R2 = 0.93) and NCM811 (R2 = 0.92) exhibited lower but still strong correlations above 0.90. Further comparisons were conducted for the TM–O (Ni–O, Co–O, Mn–O) and Li–O distances. Overall, the Ni–O (R2 = 0.93) and Li–O (R2 = 0.98) distances exhibited the best correlation, while the Co–O (R2 = 0.75) and Mn–O (R2 = 0.82) distances had smaller correlations. Considering the specific NCM types, TM–O distances for NCM523 and NCM622 were well reproduced by the PPF method (R2 > 0.85), whereas the TM-O distances for NCM811, especially Co–O (R2 = 0.49) and Mn–O (R2 = 0.80), showed the least agreement. Thus, the PFP method cannot accurately describe surfaces with low Co/Mn and high Ni contents in the TM layer. The differences in the surface energy can be attributed to two main factors. The determined μ(O) – 1/2E(O2) potential differed from the DFT-determined potential, while the μ(Li) – E(Li) potential was reproduced by the PFP method (Table S3). Surfaces with high dipole moments posed challenges for the PFP method in accurately reproducing the surface energies. The differences in the TM-O distances can be attributed to the different arrangements of the TM cations in the layers, which were exposed differently on each surface of the NCM types, making it challenging for the PFP method to accurately reproduce them.

The most preferable nanoparticle configurations were constructed using the Wulff theorem, based on the previously determined surface energies of different NCM types under various synthetic conditions obtained using the DFT and PFP methods (Figure S3). Under the oxidative synthetic conditions, the (104) and NST (003) TM–O surface types were the most preferable for all NCM types, except for NCM811 (according to the DFT method), where the ST (003) surface types were preferable to NST (003) TM–O. The PFP method mostly reproduced some of the trends in the surface appearance under oxidative conditions; however, some differences were noted. For example, for NCM523, an NST (101) TM appeared on the surface; for NCM622, an NST (003) Li–O termination was observed instead of TM–O; and for NCM811, another NST (101) TM appeared, whereas a NST (003) Li–O termination was observed instead of ST (003) (Figure S3). Under reductive synthetic conditions, the (104) area increased (except for NCM811), while the NST (003) TM–O changed to ST (003) and NCM811 changed from ST (003) to NST (003) Li. The PFP method reproduced these findings better than the oxidative condition. Only area differences were found between DFT and PFP, and the terminations were reproduced (Figure S3).

Adsorption of EC and DMC

The most preferable surface of NCM, as determined previously (Figure S4), included the (104), ST (003), and NST (003) TM–O and Li terminations. We employed these surfaces to investigate the adsorption of EC and DMC to gain further insights into CEI formation. Furthermore, the influence of various combinations of NCM on the TM layer and delithiation process were investigated. Prior to conducting the EC and DMC adsorption, we investigated the impact of the asymmetric (ASYM) and symmetric (SYM) arrangements of the NCM523 surface, including the previously mentioned planes and terminations (Figure S4), on the adsorption energy of EC and DMC. Our results revealed that the arrangement (ASYM or SYM) did not affect the adsorption energies of EC or DMC (Figure S9).

We compared the average adsorption energies of 21 adsorption structures of EC and DMC for the NCM (104) and (003) surface orientations with those of the Li-1.0 compositions (Figure 4 and Table S8). The results demonstrated differences in the average adsorption energies of EC and DMC depending on the surface type. The Li termination types, specifically the ST (003) and NST (003) Li surfaces, exhibited the highest adsorption energies of −0.95 ± 0.02, −1.05 ± 0.02, and −0.90 ± 0.02 eV for NCM523, NCM622, and NCM811, respectively (Figure 4). Furthermore, two distinct adsorption configurations were observed for EC and DMC on the ST (003) surface (Figure S7A). In one configuration (2OEC/DMC···Li), both carbonate O atoms of EC and DMC interact with the surface-exposed Li, while in the other configuration (1OEC/DMC···Li), only one carbonate O atom interacts. Moreover, both configurations exhibit an adsorption energy difference of approximately 0.2 eV (Figure 4). The NST (003) TM–O showed significantly lower adsorption energy for both EC (−0.5 eV) and DMC (−0.3 eV), because of the change in the configuration from OEC/DMC···Li to HEC/DMC···O. Previous study14 on the (003) TM–O surface of NCM333 using the DFT method showed slightly different adsorption energies (EC = −0.3 eV, DMC = −0.1 eV); however, similar trends, thus, showed stronger adsorption for EC than DMC. Another study13 showed high adsorption energies (−1.3 eV) for OEC···Li or OEC···TM modes on the (110) surface of LCO or NCM333 and lower adsorption energy (−0.5 eV) for HEC···O modes. Finally, studies on the (100) surface of LiMnO2,16 Li metal,17 and LNMO19 with OEC···Li or OEC···TM modes of adsorption energy ranged between −0.6 and −1.3 eV.

Average adsorption energies of EC (a,b) and DMC (c,d) (each surface with 21 possible configurations) for various NCM types (blue circle: NCM523, red diamond: NCM622, green triangle: NCM811) as functions of the most preferable surface types [(104) and (003)] and different Li compositions (Li1.0 and Li0.9).

The (104) surface plays a crucial role as the Li ion channel between the cathode and electrolyte. The adsorption energies of EC and DMC on this surface are lower (around −0.4 eV) compared with the (003) surface types (Figure 4). This difference can be attributed to the changes in the adsorption configurations (Figure S7A). Both EC and DMC prefer either an OEC···TM/Li interaction with the surface or a hydrogen bond-type interaction (HEC···O) between the hydrogen of the electrolyte and oxygen of the surface (Figures 4 and S7A). The (104) OEC···TM/Li configuration shares similarities with the ST (003) 2OEC···Li. However, ST (003) 2OEC···Li exhibits higher adsorption energy than the (104) configuration due to the differences in the coordination number of Li or TM at the (003) and (104) surfaces (Figure S7A). The (104) OEC···TM/Li has a higher adsorption energy (−0.4 eV) compared to the (104) HEC···O configuration (−0.2 eV), with no differences between EC and DMC (Figure 4). The previous study20 on the nonmagnetic (104) surface of LiCoO2 of OEC···TM/Li configuration showed −0.7 eV adsorption energy, slightly higher value than our results on the (104) of NCM.

Previous studies on the adsorption and reaction mechanism of EC focused on the ring-opening mechanism that occurs after adsorption on the surface,12,13 when adsorption occurred through the carbonate group of EC on the surface. Therefore, we compared the key carbonyl group (C=O) and alkoxy group (O–C) distance changes in EC and DMC upon surface adsorption. The single molecules of EC and DMC exhibited the typical distances of the carbonate group with a short C=O distance (1.20 and 1.21 Å) and longer O–C distances (1.37 and 1.36 Å) (Figure 5). Upon EC and DMC adsorption on the surfaces, these distances were altered, especially for the OEC···TM/Li and 2OEC···Li configurations. Thus, the C=O distance elongated (1.22 and 1.24 Å), indicating the weakening of the C=O bond, and parallel to this the O–C bonds contracted (1.35 and 1.33 Å), strengthening the O–C bonds. However, when EC or DMC interacts with the surface through HEC···O, the carbonate group does not undergo any change. Therefore, the alteration of the carbonate group is a key first step in the decomposition mechanism and formation of CEI, which can occur only through the interaction of an OEC···TM/Li or 2OEC···Li interactions.

Effect of NCM Types on the Adsorption Energies of EC and DMC

We further compared the different NCM types and their impact on the adsorption energies of EC and DMC on the (104) and (003) surface planes. For the (104) OEC···TM/Li configuration, the adsorption energy (−0.4 eV) was not affected by the NCM types. However, for the HEC···O configuration, some variations were observed, with NCM523 showing the highest (−0.23 ± 0.04 eV) and NCM622 the lowest (−0.16 ± 0.03 eV), while NCM811 exhibited an intermediate value (−0.19 ± 0.02 eV) (Figure 4). Furthermore, we calculated the percentage probability between the two adsorption configurations of the (104) and (003) surface planes based on their appearance within the 21 adsorption structures. Thus, for the (104) surface plane, the OEC···TM/Li configuration NCM622 showed the highest probability (43%), while NCM523 and NCM811 had lower probabilities (33% and 29%), while vice versa probability for HEC···O configuration (Figure S8A). The DMC on (104) shows several differences compared with the EC on (104). The ODMC···TM/Li configuration exhibited the highest adsorption energy for NCM811 (−0.53 ± 0.00 eV), followed by NCM523 (−0.42 ± 0.13 eV) and NCM622 (−0.37 ± 0.14 eV) (Figure 4). Additionally, the HDMC···O configuration showed higher adsorption energy for NCM523 and NCM811 (−0.18 ± 0.01 and −0.16 ± 0.01 eV) than for NCM622 (−0.12 ± 0.01 eV). When comparing the probability between the two configurations, we concluded that NCM523 and NCM622 had a higher probability (24%) for the ODMC···TM/Li configuration than NCM811 (5%) and vice versa for the HDMC···O configuration (Figure S8A). Overall, EC and DMC exhibited similar adsorption energies for the OEC···TM/Li configuration (−0.4 eV) and HEC···O (−0.2 eV) (Figure 4). However, the probability of these two configurations differed, with DMC preferring to adsorb with the HDMC···O configuration, while EC preferred to adsorb with the OEC···TM/Li configuration on the (104) surface. Furthermore, NCM811 showed a higher probability for the HEC···O configuration for both EC and DMC, while NCM523 and NCM622 showed a higher probability for the OEC···TM/Li configuration.

When comparing the ST (003) surface among the NCM types, we found that regardless of the 2OEC···Li or 1OEC···Li configurations and EC or DMC types, the sequence of adsorption energies was the lowest for NCM811 (−0.90 ± 0.02 and −0.70 ± 0.07 eV), slightly higher for NCM523 (−0.95 ± 0.02 and −0.75 ± 0.04 eV), and the highest for NCM622 (−1.05 ± 0.02 and −0.80 ± 0.04 eV) (Figure 4). However, there were differences in the probability percentage between the NCM types of the adsorption configurations (2OEC···Li or 1OEC···Li) (Figure S8B). NCM622 showed the lowest probability for the 2OEC···Li configuration (38%), while NCM811 (52%) and NCM523 (57%) had higher probabilities. No differences were found between the NCM types and DMC configurations, as all showed a lower probability for 2ODMC···Li (43%) and higher probability for 1OEC···Li (57%). Additionally, for NST (003) TM-O termination, the same adsorption energy sequence was observed among the different NCM types for both EC and DMC. NCM523 had the lowest adsorption energy (−0.49 ± 0.01 and −0.33 ± 0.02 eV), while NCM622 (−0.60 ± 0.01 and −0.47 ± 0.02 eV) and NCM811 (−0.58 ± 0.02 and −0.45 ± 0.02 eV) had higher. Thus, the increased Ni content in the TM layer allows for stronger interactions between the hydrogen of the ethyl or methyl groups and the surface oxygen. For most previously reported surfaces, such as ST (003) and NST (003) Li, for both EC and DMC, NCM811 exhibited the lowest adsorption energy, whereas for NST (003) TM-O, NCM523 showed the lowest.

Effect of Delithation on the Adsorption Energies of EC and DMC

The Li-0.9 composition increased the adsorption energy by 0.2 eV for both EC and DMC. However, the adsorption configuration remained unchanged, as in the previously observed OEC···TM/Li and HEC···O configuration for the (104) surface plane (Figure S7B). The probability of the (104) OEC···TM/Li configuration decreased, especially for NCM523 (29%) and NCM811 (10%), while it slightly increased for the ODMC···TM/Li configuration for all NCM types. As there was no Li exposed on the surface of NST (003) TM–O, the Li-0.9 composition was not investigated. Delithiation affected the EC and DMC adsorption energies and probability distributions of the two configurations (2OEC···Li and 1OEC···Li) on the ST (003) surface. Specifically, the adsorption energy of the ST (003) 2OEC···Li configuration increases by 0.5 eV for both EC and DMC. Furthermore, the adsorption energies of NCM811 and NCM622 were higher than that of NCM523. However, the adsorption energy of the ST (003) 1OEC···Li configuration decreased by 0.3 eV. This significant change in the adsorption energy (Figure S7B) can be attributed to the change in the configuration from 1OEC···Li to HEC···O. The probability distribution also shifted upon Li removal, with the probability of the 2OEC···Li configuration increasing compared to the HEC···O configuration (Figure S8B). However, no differences were observed in the probability of adsorption configurations between the NCM types. The EC and DMC adsorption energies on the NST (003) Li surfaces remained unchanged. Furthermore, the sequence between the NCM types was altered, with the NCM622 adsorption energy decreasing for both EC and DMC, resulting in a lower adsorption energy compared to that of NCM523.

Theoretical IR Spectra of Adsorbed EC and DMC

We also conducted a theoretical analysis and compared the IR spectra during the adsorption of EC and DMC on the two main surface planes [(104) and (003) TM-O] for different NCM types. Initially, we compared our theoretical and experimental30−33 IR spectra, focusing on the isolated states of the EC and DMC molecules in the theoretical simulations. The experimental measurements were performed under vapor conditions. The primary IR active modes for EC were the stretching C–H vibrations (νas and νs) at 3044 and 2988 cm–1, stretching of the C=O bond at 1840 cm–1, and stretching of O–C bonds at 1084 cm–1. Our analysis of the main IR modes and their associated wavenumber in the theoretical EC spectra demonstrated good agreement with the experimental data, with deviations typically falling within the range of 30 and 50 cm–1 (Figure S10 and Table S9). Similarly, for DMC, the primary IR active modes comprised the stretching C–H vibrations (νas and νs) at 3064 and 2976 cm–1, stretching C=O mode at 1772 cm–1, various bending C–H vibrations at 1432 and 1136 cm–1, and stretching of the O–C bonds at 1232 cm–1. Our comparison of the theoretical DMC spectra with the experimental data yielded results like those observed for EC, with discrepancies typically falling within the ranges of 30 and 50 cm–1 (Figure S11 and Table S10). Although, the theoretical IR using the PFP method reproduced the peak positions and their sequences for both EC and DMC, while the peak intensity ratio and width were not reproduced accurately. Achieving this level of reproducibility remains challenging, even when DFT or post-HF method is used.

We compared the differences between the number of adsorbed configurations in the previously discussed configurations (OEC···TM/Li and HEC···O) for both EC and DMC on the (104) surface. For EC, the number of configurations between these two options was identical across the various NCM types, when only seven optimized structures were compared, resulting in three OEC···TM/Li and four HEC···O configurations. However, we found differences between the ν O–C and ν C=O modes across the NCM types. The primary distinction we observed was the formation of an OEC···TM/Li configuration. Specifically, NCM523 tended to form with one OEC···TM configuration and one OEC···Li configuration, causing an appearance of an additional ν O–C at 1136 cm–1 (Figure 6 and Table S11). Furthermore, the NCM622 exhibited the formation of two OEC···TM configurations (1076 cm–1), while the ν O–C shifted to 1030 cm–1 in the OEC···Li configuration. The shoulder appearance of ν C=O for NCM523 is further proof of these configuration differences. Furthermore, we can observe a difference in the peak positions of ν C=O when the O of the carbonyl group interacts with the TM (1828 cm–1) for NCM523 and NCM622 or interacts with Li (1820 cm–1) for NCM811. In contrast, the number of configurations for DMC differed across the NCM types. The following distinction was observed when seven optimized configurations were compared for DMC: three ODMC···TM/Li for NCM523, one for NCM622, and zero for NCM811. Conversely, the pattern reversed for the HDMC···O configurations. Consequently, the formation of ODMC···TM/Li was more probable for NCM523 and less likely for NCM622 and NCM811. This difference between NCM523 and NCM622 or NCM811 can be observed again in the ν O–C and ν C=O of DMC (Figure 6 and Table S12). Thus, for NCM523, an additional peak appears (1292 cm–1) beside the 1220 cm–1 peak for ν O–C. Additionally, the ν C=O shifts to 1752 cm–1 from 1764 cm–1, because of the ODMC···TM/Li configuration.

Conclusions

In this study, we explored the most energetically favorable surface planes and terminations of different types of NCM cathodes (523, 622, and 811) using DFT and UNNP called PFP methods. Based on the previous XRD, we investigated four distinct planes, (104), (003), (012), and (101), considering both their ST and NST terminations. The analysis revealed that irrespective of the NCM type, the ST (104) and (003) planes exhibited lower surface energies (<1.0 J/m2) than the (012) and (101) planes. Furthermore, the oxidative synthetic conditions favored NST (003) TM-O termination, whereas the reductive conditions favored NST (003) Li termination. To understand the differences in the surface energies, we examined the TM–O and Li–O distances in the surface layers. While the TM–O distances remained similar across the different surface planes, variations were observed in the Li–O distances. The (003) surface planes exhibited smaller Li–O distances (1.90 Å), while the (104) surface planes had longer Li–O distances (2.12 Å). Moreover, the (012) and (101) surface planes exhibited intermediate Li–O distances (2.06 Å). Furthermore, the coordination environment of the TM on these surfaces also played a significant role, with the (104) and (003) planes exhibiting five or six coordinated structures, while the (012) and (101) planes featured three or four coordinated arrangements. Furthermore, the effect of the different NCM types (523, 622, and 811) on the surface energies and distances in the surface layers were examined. The surface energy of (104) decreased with an increase in the number of Ni ions in the TM layer. Additionally, the Ni–O distances were shortened, while the Co–O and Mn–O distances varied between the NCM types. The Li–O distance on the ST (003) surface remained the same but was shorter than that observed on the (104) surface.

The adsorption of EC and DMC onto the most favorable NCM surfaces (104) and (003) was investigated using the PFP method. Notably, the surface type influenced the adsorption energies and configurations of EC and DMC while displaying similar adsorption energy trends. However, their preferred configurations differed. Thus, DMC favored the HDMC···O interaction, while EC preferred the OEC···TM/Li interaction. As the decomposition reaction can lead from the OEC···TM/Li configuration rather than from the HDMC···O one, this adsorption behavior difference between the two electrolytes can affect the formation of CEI. We explored the influence of the NCM type on the adsorption energies and configurations of EC and DMC. For instance, NCM622 exhibits the highest adsorption energies on most surfaces, whereas NCM523 and NCM811 show lower adsorption energies. Furthermore, it had been found that for both EC and DMC, the HEC···O configuration is preferable for NCM811 over NCM523 and NCM622. Thus, the decomposition and formation of the CEI should be different for the NCM types. Delithiation of the surfaces led to a slight increase in the adsorption energy for both EC and DMC; however, the adsorption configurations remained unchanged. Finally, we conducted a theoretical IR spectral analysis during EC and DMC adsorption on the (104) and NST (003) TM–O surfaces for different NCM types, and the variations in the number of adsorbed configurations explained the differences in the theoretical IR spectra between the NCM types and surfaces.
