Gut Microbiota Composition Correlates to Trunk Fat Percentage in Male Cynomolgus Macaque Monkeys
Authors: Ditte Olsen Lützhøft, Rasmus Riemer Jacobsen, Denitsa Vladimirova Stefanova, Karen‐Margrethe Pedersen, Dennis Sandris Nielsen, Berit Ø Christoffersen, Lindsay Bader, Melissa Kirigiti, Ellen Marie Straarup, Paul Kievit, Axel Kornerup Hansen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObesity and its co‐morbidities are increasing as public health challenges worldwide. Non‐human primates with their similarity to human physiology and susceptibility to metabolic diseases represent a valuable, clinically translational animal model in metabolic disease research. We aimed to characterize the impact of long‐term high fat, high carbohydrate and high cholesterol diet (FFC) feeding on the host metabolism and gut microbiota in male Cynomolgus Macaque monkeys. Twenty‐three male Cynomolgus Macaque ( Macaca fascicularis) monkeys were housed indoors from the age of approximately 2 years and fed a FFC diet for 4.5 years. Diabetes and obesity‐related parameters, gut microbiota composition, circulating bile acids and short chain fatty acids were characterized. The FFC diet induced obesity with elevated levels of trunk fat (TF%) of 39.7% ± 14.9%. There was a significant association between TF% and fasting insulin, HbA1c%, triglyceride levels, and the ratio of glycine‐ to taurine‐conjugated bile acids. TF% was associated with changes in the fecal microbiota composition, predominantly driven by Coprococcus spp. and members of the families Enterobacteriaceae and Lactobacillaceae, similar to observations in humans with obesity. It is concluded that the FFC diet induced obesity, hypertriglyceridemia, impaired insulin sensitivity and dysregulated glucose metabolism. Microbial diversity did not differ between obesity states described as four TF% quartiles, but the fecal microbiota composition was significantly associated with the TF% driven by Coprococcus spp. and members of the families Enterobacteriaceae and Lactobacillaceae. Whether the gut microbiota changes were a cause or consequence of the obese state and impaired glucose metabolism remains to be studied.Keywords: bile acids, gut microbiota, insulin resistance, Macaca Fascicularis , obesity, western‐style diet

Introduction

Gut microbiota imbalances contribute to obesity, and five mechanisms have been proposed: (1) increasing dietary energy harvest, (2) promoting fat deposition, (3) possibly modifying locomotor activity, (4) having central effects on satiety, (5) and triggering systemic inflammation [1]. In diet‐induced obese mice [2] and in humans [3], gut microbiota composition strongly correlates to diabetes/obesity parameters. This is facilitated through the production of signaling molecules by microbiota, such as secondary bile acids, short‐chain fatty acids (SCFA) or Gram‐negative bacterial lipopolysaccharides [4, 5]. Primary bile acids produced from liver cholesterol are secreted in both taurine unconjugated and conjugated forms into the duodenum [6] and are essential for emulsifying and solubilizing luminal lipids, cholesterol and fat‐soluble vitamins, in addition to signaling in glucose and lipid pathways [7]. In obese and diabetic patients taurine synthesis is decreased and intestinal absorption and urinary excretion of taurine are increased [8], which may alter the gut bacterial composition [9]. In return, the bacterial composition can influence deconjugation and biotransformation of bile acid [9], adding to a complex relationship in host–gut microbiota interactions. Bile acid actions are mediated through activation of the nuclear farnesoid X receptor (FXR), and the Takeda G protein‐coupled receptor 5 (TGR5) [10]. FXR exhibits potent activation by the bile acid chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), as well as lithocholic acid (LCA) and deoxycholic acid (DCA) [11]. As reviewed by Wang et al. [12] activation of FXR increases secretion of the hormone Fibroblast Growth Factor 19 (FGF19) with eventual subsequent modulation of bile acid levels, repressed gluconeogenesis and lipogenesis, and promoted glycogen synthesis, and FGF19 is implicated in the regulation of enterohepatic circulation, glucose, lipid, energy metabolism, and tumorigenesis. FGF19 increases energy expenditure, decreases weight and improves glucose tolerance [7]. Anaerobic microorganisms produce gut SCFAs [13], which can cross the gut barrier to exert a direct metabolic impact, such as their role as ligands for G‐protein coupled receptors influencing enteroendocrine signalling [14]. SCFAs such as acetate, propionate, butyrate as well as the isobutyric, isovaleric and succinic acids correspond to metabolic pathways [15]. Colon‐produced imidazole propionate causes impaired insulin signaling in mice and humans [16] and is associated with non‐alcoholic steatohepatitis in pigs [17].

Non‐human primates are frequently used preclinical models to study obesity, glucose homeostasis and insulin resistance [18]. Cynomolgus monkeys fed a high‐sugar high‐fat diet significantly increase their trunk fat mass and display obesity‐related human similar characteristics [19]. The healthy cynomolgus monkey gut microbiome enterotypes consists predominantly of Lactobacillus, Prevotella, and Ruminococcus, while in healthy humans the microbiome is driven by Prevotella, Ruminococcus, and Bacteroides, rather than Lactobacilli [20]. In humans, Bifidobacterium [21], Alistipes [22], and Faecalibacterium [22] are also important gut microbiota members. Gut microbial compositions and functions are closely related to dietary habits [23] and applying a humanized dietary pattern in captive non‐human primates is reported to cause human analogue gut microbiome changes [24]. However, the effect of long‐term high‐fat diets on the gut microbiota and related metabolic consequences in cynomolgus monkeys are not well described. In this study, we characterized obesity, glucose and lipid metabolism, and fecal gut microbiota composition in a colony of long‐term diet‐induced obese male cynomolgus monkeys. We anticipated that not all monkeys would develop obesity in the same degree despite being on the same diet, and based upon previous observations in mice [2] and humans [3], we hypothesized that there would be a significant positive correlation between fecal microbiota composition and trunk fat, independent of diet.

Material and Methods

Ethics

The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the Oregon National Primate Research Center (ONPRC) at Oregon Health and Science University approved the study. The study is reported according to the ARRIVE guidelines [25].

Animal Study

Twenty‐three male cynomolgus macaques (Mauritian Macaca fascicularis ) were housed in modular pens in groups of 2–9 animals in a 12/12 day/night light cycle at the ONPRC in custom‐designed pens (Carter2 Systems Inc., Hillsboro, United States). Each pen unit was 2 m wide, 1.3 m deep and 2.6 m high (floor to ceiling) with a maximum of 3 animals per pen unit. Larger groups of up to 9 animals had access to 4 connected pens (8 m × 1.3 m × 2.6 m). Pens were furnished with shelves, climbing structures, verandas, perches with thermo‐neutral solid surfaces, and solid flooring. Lights were gradually turned on or off over a 30‐min period to simulate sunrise and sunset. Bedding stimulated natural foraging and provided sound dampening while pools were used for enrichment.

Feeding

Obesity was induced by twice‐daily ad libitum feeding for approximately 4.5 years, from approximately 1.5–6 years of age, of a western diet high in sucrose, fructose, fat and cholesterol (LabDiet, Richmond, IN; 5L0P, 36.4% of calories from fat, 45.3% calories from carbohydrates, 18.3% from protein; cholesterol 612 ppm) supplemented with a caloric dense peanut butter treat as well as additional daily vegetables, fruit or popcorn.

Body Weight, Body Composition, Intravenous Glucose Tolerance Test and Fecal Sampling

Average body weight of the animals was 10.0 ± 0.5 kg. For weighing, the monkeys were voluntarily guided from the pen into a tunnel with an integrated, calibrated scale. To monitor body conditions, animals were sedated with 3–5 mg/kg Telazol (3–5 mg/kg, Tiletamine HCl/Zolazepam HCl, Zoetis, Parsippany, NJ) after an overnight fast. To maintain sedation, a small dose of Ketamine (5–10 mg/kg, Abbott Laboratories, North Chicago, IL) was provided intravenously if needed. Animals were positioned supine on the scanner bed to measure total body fat, trunk fat and non‐fat mass by dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DEXA, Hologic QDR Discovery A, Hologic Inc., Bedford, MA). Data were analyzed using QDR software (Hologic).

Immediately after the DEXA scan, an intravenous glucose tolerance test (IVGTT) was performed. A baseline fasting blood sample was obtained from each animal in EDTA vacutainers (K3‐EDTA 8 mM, BD BioScience, San Jose, CA) with HALT protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher, Scientific, Waltham, MA) and DPP4 inhibitor (EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA). Then, a glucose bolus of 0.6 g/kg (50% dextrose solution) was administered. Blood samples were collected 1, 3, 5, 10, 20, 40, 50 and 60 min later via the contralateral saphenous vein. Areas under the curve for glucose (GLUAUC), insulin (INSAUC), and C‐peptide (C‐PEPAUC) were calculated using the trapezoidal rule with T = 0 as baseline. The glucose disappearance rate (K G) was determined using the data from 10 to 60 min after glucose administration with the equation: (Ln(GLU10 min) – Ln(GLU60 min)/50)*100, and ΔINSAUC 0–60 min as incremental AUC. The insulin sensitivity index was calculated as K G/ΔINSAUC as described by Galvin and colleagues [26].

During the same anesthesia, a rectal ZymoBiomics DNA/RNA hydraflock swab (DNA/RNA Shield Collection Tube w/Swab, Zymo Research, CA) was taken from each monkey by gentle insertion and three times rotation before being transferred to the shield collection tube and subsequently stored at −80°C until further processing.

For comparison, trunk fat data were collected from a cohort of 32 healthy cynomolgus monkeys aged 5–7 years.

Analysis of Blood Samples

Blood glucose was monitored with a hand‐held glucometer (OneTouch Ultra2 Blood Glucose Monitor; LifeScan, Milpitas, CA) and a glucose analyzer (Biosen, EKF Diagnostics, Borne, TX). HbA1c% was monitored with a DCA Vantage analyzer (Siemens Health, Deerfield, IL). Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 2400 g at 4°C for 20 min and analyzed for insulin and c‐peptide (Cobas 411, Roche Diagnostics), triglycerides, total cholesterol, LDL‐cholesterol and HDL‐cholesterol (ABX Pentra 400 chemistry analyzer, Horiba Diagnostics) and bile acids (LC–MS/MS, Agilent 1290 UHPLC/Sciex QTrap 6500 or 6500+, Metabolon, Morrisville, NC). Circulating FGF19 was assayed by commercial ELISA according to the manufacturers' specifications (Cat# DF1900, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN).

Blood biochemistry for Albumin (ALB), Alkaline Phosphatase (ALKP), Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT), Aspartate Transaminase (AST), Total Bilirubin (TBIL), Creatinine (CREA), Gamma Glutamyltransaminase (GGT), Inorganic Phosphorus (Phos), Total Protein (TP), Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN), Chloride (Cl), Potassium (K), Sodium (Na), and Magnesium (Mg) was monitored by the Clinical Pathology Unit at ONPRC using an ABX Pentra 60 C+ Hematology Analyzer (Horiba Diagnostics, Piscataway, NJ).

Circulating amino acids, short‐chain fatty acids and indole‐3‐propionic acid were measured by Metabolon Inc. (Morrisville, NC) with standard methods by LC–MS/MS (Agilent 1290 UHPLC/Sciex QTrap5500, Santa Clara CA).

DNA Extraction, Sequencing and Pre‐Processing of Raw Data

After room temperature thawing and vortexing, the fecal swabs were removed from the DNA/RNA hydraflock tubes with a sterile tweezer and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 3 min before supernatant removal. The pellet was washed with 500 μL sterile water and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 3 min before supernatant removal. The pellets with the bacterial cells were purified using the Genomics Micro AX Bacteria Gravity kit (A&A Biotechnologies, Gdynia, Poland) following the manufacturer's instructions.

A 16S rRNA gene amplicon library was constructed by amplifying the 16S rRNA gene containing multiple forward and reverse primers targeting the 16S V1–V9 regions. PCR conditions for the amplification were 95°C for 5 min, 2 cycles of 95°C for 20 s, 48°C for 30 s, 65°C for 10 s, 72°C for 45 s, and a final extension at 72°C for 4 min. A second PCR step was then performed to barcode PCR amplicons with 95°C for 2 min followed by 33 cycles of 95°C for 20 s, 55°C for 20 s, 72°C for 40 s, and a final extension at 72°C for 4 min. After each PCR reaction, PCR amplicons were cleaned up using SpeedBeads magnetic carboxylate (Sigma Aldrich, Søborg, Denmark). The size of barcoded PCR products (approximately 1500 bp) was checked by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. The entire amplified 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) library was sequenced using the Oxford Nanopore sequencing platform, MinION and Flow cell R9.4.1, following the “Amplicons by ligation” protocol (SQK‐LSK109) provided by Oxford Nanopore Technologies. Raw pair‐ended reads were merged and trimmed using fastq_mergepairs and fastq_filter scripts implemented in the USEARCH pipeline [27]. The chimeric reads were removed and the zero radius Operational Taxonomic Units (zOTUs) were identified using the UNOISE3 algorithm [28]. For taxonomy assignment the Greengenes (13.8) 16S rRNA gene collection was used as a reference database [29]. Data generated by MinION (Nanopore, Oxford, UK) were collected using MinKnow software v19.06.8 (https://nanoporetech.com). The Guppy v3.2.2 base calling toolkit was used to base call raw fast5 to fastq (https://nanoporetech.com). Porechop v0.2.2 was used for adapter trimming and sample demultiplexing (https://github.com/rrwick/Porechop). Sequences containing quality scores (fastq files) were quality corrected using NanoFilt (q ≥ 10; read length > 1 Kb). Taxonomy assignment of quality corrected reads against the Greengenes (13.8) database was conducted using the uclast method implemented in parallel_assign_taxonomy_uclust.py (QIIME v1.9.1). The uclust settings were tuned on mock communities (—similarity 0.8; min_consensus_fraction 0.51), assuring annotations to the lowest taxonomic level with no false positive annotations. The settings allowed it to treat individual amplicon sequence variants as individual “seeds”. Reads classified to at least the phylum level were subjected to further analysis.

Statistics, Calculations, and Bioinformatic Analysis

The monkeys were divided into trunk fat percentage (TF%) quartiles: 1st quartile TF % below the 25th percentile (TF% < 25%) ‘slightly overweight’, the 2nd quartile between 25th and 50th percentile (TF%: 26%–42%) ‘overweight’, the 3rd quartile between the 50th and 75th percentile (TF%: 43%–52%) ‘obese’, and the 4th quartile above the 75th percentile (TF% > 53%) ‘very obese’. To evaluate interquartile differences in the metabolic parameters, one‐way ANOVA was applied adjusting for multiple comparison using false discovery rate (FDR) (q values) and determined statistically significant if q < 0.05. Shapiro–Wilk test and QQ plots were used to evaluate normality and the F‐test was used to evaluate variance equality (PRISM v9.3.1, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Pearson correlation matrix was used to evaluate associations between variables (log transformed data), while adjusting for the number of correlations using FDR (R software v4.1.1 and package psych v2.1.9; Minitab v22.2.1. (Coventry, UK), PRISM). For conducting bioinformatic analysis of the fecal microbiota, R software v4.1.1 with packages R base, phyloseq, vegan, ggpubr v0.4.0 [30], and ggplot2 v3.5.5 was used. Permutational ANOVA (PERMANOVA) was used to evaluate group comparisons of bacterial community composition, using the Bonferroni–Holm method for multiple testing correction. DeSeq2 was used to determine bacterial differences in the colon microbiota between TF% quartiles. Statistical significance was p < 0.05. For data visualization PRISM v9.3.1, R software V4.1.1 with corrplot package v0.9, ggplot2 v3.5.5 and ggprism v1.0.3 was used.

Insulin and Triglyceride Parameters Were Associated With Trunk Fat Percentage

The whole group's mean TF% (±SD) was 39.7% ± 14.9%, and although all monkeys were fed FFC, they could be divided into four quartiles based on TF%, that is, Q1 ‘Slightly overweight’, Q2 ‘Overweight’, Q3 ‘Obese’ and Q4 ‘Very obese’ (Figure 1A). The TF% of monkeys in the 1st quartile was slightly elevated compared to the monkeys in the healthy cohort (Figure S1). Fasting plasma concentration of triglycerides in the 4th quartile ‘very obese’ was significantly elevated compared to the overweight animals of the 1st and 2nd quartiles (Figure 1B). Serum total cholesterol, LDL‐cholesterol, fasting plasma glucose levels and HDL‐cholesterol were similar across the quartiles (Figure 1C–E), whereas fasting plasma insulin (Figure 1F) was significantly higher for the 4th quartile ‘very obese’ compared to both the 1st, 2nd and 3rd quartiles. HbA1c% level was significantly higher in the 4th ‘very obese’ compared to the 1st quartile ‘slightly overweight’ (Figure 1G). No significant difference in calculated IVGTT insulin sensitivity was observed across quartiles, although it trended higher for the 1st quartile ‘slightly overweight’ (Figure 1H). The HOMA‐IR index (Figure 1I) was significantly higher for the 4th quartile ‘very obese’ compared to both the 1st, 2nd and 3rd quartiles. To identify associations between TF% and metabolic parameters a Pearson correlation matrix was determined and significant positive correlations between TF% and body fat, HOMA‐IR index, HbA1c%, and triglyceride level were found (Figure 2A–D). Other clinical chemistry parameters did not differ between the quartiles (Table S1).

Fecal Microbiota Shifted According to Trunk Fat Percentage

We used 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing to investigate the fecal microbiota (Figure 3A). At the genus level the fecal microbiota was dominated by Prevotella, lactobacilli, as well as members of Clostridiales and Bacteroidales orders (Taxa summary, Figure 3A). There were no differences in the number of Observed Species and Shannon diversity indexes across trunk fat quartiles. There was a significant correlation between fecal microbiota composition and TF% in the cynomolgus monkeys as determined by Bray–Curtis dissimilarity metrics (Adonis: p = 0.033, Figure 3B). The relative abundance of Coprococcus in the 1st TF% quartile ‘slightly overweight’ (4.1%) was significantly higher compared to the 4th TF% quartile ‘very obese’ (1%) (Figure 3C). In addition, species from the Enterobacteriaceae family were found to be higher in the 1st TF% quartile ‘slightly overweight’ compared to the other quartiles (2nd–4th) (Figure 3C). There was a minor but significant increase in Ligilactobacillus agilis in the 4th TF% quartile ‘very obese’ compared to the other quartiles (1st–3rd) (Figure 3C). The relative abundance of Succinogenes (Fibrobacteraceae family) was increased in the 2nd and 3rd TF% quartiles compared to the 1st and 4th quartiles, while Facklamia spp. (Aerococcaceae family) were increased in the 2nd quartile ‘obese’ compared to the other quartiles (Figure 3C).

There Were no Relationships Between Circulating Gut‐Related Metabolites and Obesity Related Factors

Selected gut‐related metabolites in circulation (amino acids, SCFA species and imidazole propionate) were comprehensively assessed. In an initial Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to explore potential clustering patterns based on TF%, no significant associations were observed between plasma metabolites and TF% (Figure S2A), and Pearson correlations of PC1 and PC2 with TF% were all insignificant. Similarly, no correlation between measured metabolites and HOMA‐IR was observed (data in repository). After FDR correction there was only a borderline significant correlation between imidazole propionate and HOMA‐IR (Figure S2B, q = 0.064), while all other correlations were insignificant (Figure S2B,C).

The Ratio of Glycine‐To Taurine‐Conjugated Cholic Acid Correlated Negatively To Trunk Fat Percentage and HOMA‐IR

Metabolic influence of the entero‐hepatic system was evaluated through the circulating bile acid profile. The total plasma bile acid and total conjugated bile acid levels were comparable across TF% quartiles (Figure 4A). The ratio of glycine‐ to taurine‐conjugated bile acid was significantly elevated in the 1st TF% quartile ‘slightly obese’ compared to the other quartiles and exhibited a significant negative correlation to TF% (Figure 4B). This association was primarily driven by the negative correlations between TF% and the ratio of glycine‐ to taurine‐conjugated cholic acid (GCA/TCA) in addition to glycine‐ to taurine‐conjugated deoxycholic acid (GDCA/TDCA) (Figure 4C). We observed a significant positive correlation between insulin sensitivity and the ratio of glycine‐ to taurine‐conjugated bile acid (Pearson correlation: r‐sq = 0.25, p = 0.014, data in repository), suggesting a potential link between insulin sensitivity and bile acid conjugation profile. Circulating levels of FGF19, which is primarily produced by the ileal epithelium and is known to play a crucial role in the regulation of bile acid synthesis [31] were not correlated to either primary or secondary bile acids (Figure 4D,E), HOMA‐IR or TF% (Figure 4D). However, we found a significant positive correlation between circulating levels of FGF19 and both lithocholic acid (LCA) and deoxycholic acid (DCA) (Figure 4E). There was a significant correlation between the total primary bile acids and the microbiota composition (PCoA axis 1), which was driven by a significant correlation to chenodeoxycholic acid (Table S1).

Discussion

Obesity, glucose‐ and lipid‐metabolism and fecal gut microbiota composition were characterized in long‐term FFC diet‐fed male cynomolgus monkeys of the same age, in the same environment on the same diet, which responded to the FFC diet with a high inter‐individual variation in TF%.

As hypothesized, fecal microbiota composition correlated significantly to TF%. The highest TF% 4th quartile ‘very obese’ animals had elevations in plasma insulin levels, HOMA‐IR index, HbA1c%, and plasma triglycerides. When analyzed as a continuum, TF% correlated positively to body fat %, HOMA‐IR index, HbA1c%, and plasma triglyceride level. The ratio of glycine‐ to taurine‐conjugated cholic acid correlated negatively to TF% and HOMA‐IR. As expected [32], in the monkeys with high TF%, HbA1c% [33] and fasting plasma insulin concentration were increased compared to the animals in the lower TF% quartiles, with the latter resulting in elevated HOMA‐IR index despite similar plasma glucose levels. This indicates that high TF% monkeys were insulin resistant and prediabetic [33, 34] as previously shown [35].

Overall, Clostridiales and Bacteroidales followed by Lactobacillales, Erysipelotrichales, and Campylobacterales members dominated the fecal microbiota. Species richness and diversity varied independent of obesity. Also, previously no alpha diversity differences between lean and obese African green [23] or cynomolgous [36] monkeys were observed. Therefore, in contrast to humans [37, 38], obesity per se does not seem to correlate with alpha diversity in monkeys. However, the larger variation in diet and environmental factors in human studies may greatly influence the gut microbiota, which may indicate that previously reported obesity‐related microbial changes in humans are associated with diet and environment rather than obesity. Interestingly, we observed a not previously reported distinct microbial clustering according to TF% [24, 36]. In accordance with humans [37], a main driver of the TF% correlated shift in fecal microbiota was a reduced abundance of the Coprococcus genus of the Lachnospiraceae family. Members of Lachnospiraceae produce SCFAs [37], which are important regulators of glucose, lipid and cholesterol metabolism [39]. However, we found no correlation between TF% and circulating SCFAs. Lactobacillaceae, which slightly expanded in relative abundance with increasing TF%, are known as members of the obesity‐related gut microbiota in mice and humans [37]. Enterobacteriaceae, which are among the first colonizers in neonates, and, therefore, a natural member of the healthy human gut [40], were slightly decreased in the fecal microbiota of the monkeys with the highest TF%. Overabundance of Enterobacteriaceae, is known to promote a persistent low‐grade inflammation through a high lipopolysaccharide production [37], another hallmark of obesity [41]. Enterobacteriaceae abundance was inversely associated with secondary bile acids across quartiles, suggesting that the bile acid environment in higher‐adiposity animals may suppress Enterobacteriaceae, whereas lower secondary bile acid levels in Q1 permit occasional expansions. However, this is purely speculative.

TF% correlated positively with circulating triglycerides and negatively to glycine–taurine‐conjugated bile acid ratios as in humans [35]. The latter was mainly driven by the ratio of the glycine–taurine‐conjugated cholic acid (primary bile acid) and DCA (secondary bile acid). Previously, a decreased level of taurine‐conjugated bile acid has been associated with human obesity and high‐fat diet‐induced animal models [42]. We can, therefore, conclude that primates seem to be comparative as models for humans in this aspect. 7α‐dehydroxylation is carried out by certain bacteria, such as the gut bacterium Clostridium scindens [43]. However, we did not detect classical 7α‐dehydroxylating species at the species level resolution. Confirming causality will require metagenomic or functional assays to detect the bile‐acid‐induced operon (termed bai) and bile salt hydrolase gene activity and to distinguish microbial deconjugation from host‐driven changes in hepatic conjugation. Understanding these interactions could reveal pathways linked to metabolic disorders and identify potential therapeutic strategies to address the adverse effects of weight gain. Further, obese mice have reduced adipose taurine synthesis [8]. We revealed a significant positive correlation between circulating FGF19 and LCA, as well as DCA, indicating a speculative association between FXR‐activating bile acids and the circulating FGF19, as previously described [11]. Bile acids can activate hepatic FGF19 signaling, inhibiting the production of primary bile acids through cholesterol 7α‐hydroxylase employing an autocrine/paracrine mechanism [44]. There was no correlation between circulating primary bile acids and FGF19. Neither were correlations observed between plasma levels of FGF19 and TF% or HOMA‐IR, although current literature suggests that FGF19 promotes weight loss and aids in the remission of type‐2‐diabetes [45].

The lower weight gain in some of the animals may be considered a reflection of social behavior, genetic variation and host–gut microbiota interactions. In diet‐induced obese inbred mice fighting has been shown to be a key weight gain reducing factor [46]. The observed differences in TF% could also partly arise from the behavioral hierarchy in our group of socially housed primates, in which the alpha‐male may stress lower ranking beta‐males with limited resource access [47]. Although Mauritian cynomolgus monkeys exhibit a lower genetic diversity than other primate species [48], gut microbial variation in outbred monkeys may be influenced by host epigenome‐microbiota relations [49]. Furthermore, the relatively small number of animals included, especially when compared to larger human epidemiological studies, represents a limitation.

In conclusion, in cynomolgus monkeys a high‐fat diet can induce obesity, although to a varying extent, with an association between TF% and fasting insulin, HbA1c%, triglyceride levels, and the ratio of glycine‐ to taurine‐conjugated bile acid. The fecal microbiota composition was significantly associated with the TF%, which was associated with Coprococcus spp., Enterobacteriaceae and Lactobacillaceae members' relative abundance, but in contrast to humans, alpha diversity did not differ between TF% quartiles. Whether the gut microbiota changes were the cause or consequence of the obese state and impaired glucose metabolism remains to be further studied.
