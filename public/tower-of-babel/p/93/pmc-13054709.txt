Efficacy and safety of danazol for pulmonary fibrosis or bone-marrow failure associated with telomere-related gene mutation
Authors: Flore Sicre de Fontbrune, Stéphane Jouneau, Vincent Cottin, Grégoire Prevot, Hilario Nunes, Louis Terriou, Sylvain Marchand-Adam, Bruno Crestani, Ambroise Marcais, Anne Bergeron, Marie-Pierre Debray, Aurélie Plessier, Elodie Lainey, Ibrahima Ba, Caroline Kannengiesser, Kristell Desseaux, Matthieu Resche-Rigon, Lucie Biard, Raphael Borie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTelomere-related gene (TRG) pathogenic variants are detected in ∼30% of familial pulmonary fibrosis (PF) cases. Danazol, a synthetic sex hormone with androgenic properties, has been found associated with telomere elongation and haematological response in patients with short telomeres. The objective of the ANDROTELO multicentre, prospective, open-label single-arm phase II clinical trial (NCT03710356) was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of danazol in carriers of TRG mutations with PF or bone-marrow failure (BMF).MethodsIncluded patients were carriers of a pathogenic or likely pathogenic TRG variant and presented a PF lung involving ≥10% parenchymal involvement on chest computed tomography (PF group) and/or severe BMF (BMF group). Treatment was danazol 400 mg twice a day for 12 months. We included 25 patients with PF (16 males) and five with BMF (two males). One PF patient withdrew consent.ResultsAt inclusion, the median age in the PF group was 62.5 years, forced vital capacity (FVC) was 69% (interquartile range 40–120) and diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) was 44% (29;84). Ten of 24 PF patients (42%) completed the 12-month treatment. Causes of premature treatment discontinuation were side effects (n=9), death (n=3), lung transplantation (n=1) and disease progression (n=1). 14 PF patients were evaluated at month 12 and eight showed a relative decline in FVC of <5%. The median relative decrease in FVC and DLCO was −10% (interquartile range −14– −2) and −10.3% (−22.7–4.2). The five patients in the BMF group completed the 12-month treatment and exhibited at least a partial response.ConclusionDanazol was poorly tolerated in patients with TRG-related PF in this study, thus precluding efficacy assessment.

Introduction

Pathogenic variants in telomere-related genes (TRGs) were initially reported in individuals with dyskeratosis congenita, a rare multisystemic syndrome, including a specific cutaneous triad, bone-marrow failure (BMF) and short telomeres. Fibrotic interstitial lung disease (ILD), liver cirrhosis and some other rare specific manifestations have been found associated with TRG pathogenic variants. Phenotypes associated with these variants have been collectively called short telomere syndrome or telomere biology disorder (TBD) [1]. To date, more than 10 different TRG variants have been found associated with pulmonary fibrosis (PF); TERT, TERC, RTEL1 and PARN are the most frequent and found in about 30% of familial PF [2–4]. The mechanisms leading to PF and BMF in TRG mutation carriers are unknown, but loss of function TRG variants are for most of them associated with reduced telomerase activity and short telomere length (TL).

Danazol is a synthetic sex hormone with androgenic properties. In a 2-year trial of patients with TBD, involving 10 patients with “overt” PF and 15 with “subclinical” PF, danazol was associated with significant telomere elongation and haematological response in 19 of 24 (79%) [5]. They also showed stabilisation of diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO), forced vital capacity (FVC) and computed tomography (CT) scan involvement. In addition, in another 2-year prospective trial of patients with short telomere syndrome, three of seven patients with ILD showed pulmonary improvement with nandrolone decanoate, another synthetic androgenic [6].

The objective of the ANDROTELO phase II clinical trial (NCT03710356) was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of danazol in TRG variant carriers with PF or BMF. Efficacy was assessed on clinically relevant end-points. We also assessed the association between clinical efficacy and changes in TL between month 0 and month 12.

Methods

Trial design

This was a multicentre, prospective, open-label, single-arm, phase II clinical trial conducted in France from December 2018 and April 2022 and sponsored by the Assistance Publique - Hôpitaux de Paris (AP-HP). All trial aspects were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki, in compliance with French regulations on clinical research. The study was approved by the French Est-III Ethics Committee (approval no. 18.07.07) and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03710356). The study was funded by a grant from Programme Hospitalier de Recherche Clinique Inter-Régional Ile-De-France 2017 (Ministère de la Santé, no. AOR17102, P170925J, EUD2018-001686-17).

Participants

Inclusion criteria were 1) age ≥15 years; 2) carriage of a pathogenic or likely pathogenic TRG variant [7]; and 3) presenting PF with parenchymal involvement >10% on CT scan (PF group) or severe haematological involvement (platelet count<30 g·L−1, neutrophil count <0.5 g·L−1, haemoglobin level <9 g·dL−1 or transfusion needs) without proven myeloid malignancy (BMF group). We excluded patients if they had a contraindication to danazol: a history of solid malignancy and/or thrombosis, severe hepatic disease, ongoing pregnancy or absence of an efficient birth control strategy for women.

Treatment

After inclusion, patients received danazol 400 mg twice a day, as reported in the phase I–II study, for a total scheduled treatment period of 12 months [5]. Patients with transaminase level >2N and <5N at inclusion were given danazol 200 mg×2·day−1, increased to 400 mg twice a day after 4 weeks. In patients with normal transaminase level at inclusion and increased transaminase level >2N and <5N during follow-up, the dosage was reduced to 200 mg twice a day and could be increased again to 400 mg twice a day after a 4-week plateau in case of normalisation of transaminase level.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was a clinical response defined as a relative decline in FVC of <5% predicted at 12 months in the PF group and a haematological response in the BMF group, defined at 12 months as a ≥1.5 g·dL−1 increase in haemoglobin level, ≥0.5 g·L−1 increase in neutrophil count or ≥20 g·L−1 in platelet count according to the failing lineage at baseline.

Secondary outcomes were 1) hepatic and extrahepatic tolerance of danazol up to 12 months; 2) change in TL between inclusion and 12 months; 3) appearance of bone-marrow cytological and cytogenetic abnormalities under treatment; 4) quality of life evaluated with the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) QLQ-C30 v.3.0 questionnaire at inclusion and 3, 6 and 12 months [8]; and 5) lung transplant free-survival.

A liver tolerance assessment included a biological liver test evaluation at inclusion and at 1, 2, 3, 6, 9 and 12 months; ultrasound imaging at inclusion and 6 months; and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at inclusion and 12 months. Systemic tolerance included endocrinology, bone evaluation and prostate-specific antigen evaluation at inclusion and 3, 6 and 12 months for men. A CT scan was performed at inclusion and 6 and 12 months and was centrally reviewed by an expert radiologist. Pulmonary function tests (FVC and DLCO) were performed at inclusion and 3, 6, 9 and 12 months. TL was assessed at inclusion and 12 months by flow cytometry combined with fluorescent in situ hybridisation (Flow-FISH) on peripheral blood mononuclear cells with the same method for all patients [9]. When available, the months 0 and 12 samples were included in the same batch. According to the Flow-FISH analysis in line 1301, an increase of 0.1 corresponds to a gain of 23.5 bp [10]. Blood count was monitored at inclusion and 3, 6 and 12 months; bone-marrow aspiration with cytological and cytogenetic examinations was planned at inclusion and 12 months. In BMF patients, transfusion independence and complete haematological response (haemoglobin level >10 g·dL−1, neutrophil count >1 g·L−1and platelet count >100 g·L−1) were assessed at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarised as median (interquartile range (IQR)) and categorical variables as number (percentage). The primary end-point was analysed separately in each group (PF and BMF) by using a Bayesian β-binomial model with a weakly informative prior distribution β(1,1) for the clinical response rate [11]. We also used a prior distribution β(0.5, 0.5) in a post hoc sensitivity analysis to obtain a reduced prior effective sample size (1 versus 2) given the limited sample sizes in the PF and BMF groups. The main analysis was performed on the intent-to-treat sample, including all included patients with the exception of those with consent withdrawal before treatment initiation; incomplete observations on the primary end-point due to treatment discontinuation or premature end of follow-up were considered failures. On the same intent-to-treat sample, descriptive analyses were performed for the secondary end-points, overall and according to the group, as appropriate (PF and BMF). Time to event end-points were analysed by using the Kaplan–Meier estimator.

For this academic one-stage, single-arm, phase II clinical trial of a rare disease (50–60 annual incident cases in France), the planned sample size was initially 40 patients, 20 patients in the PF group and 20 in the BMF group, allowing for about a 21% half-width for 95% exact confidence intervals (CIs) given an expected 70% response rate. In the context of the COVID-19 pandemic and its impact on healthcare providers in France, notably during the first waves of the pandemic, the sample size was revised in December 2020 to 25 and 15 in the PF and BMF groups, respectively, to account for the observed accrual distribution between the groups thus far (20 and five at the time). These subsamples would allow for acceptable precision in estimating the response rate: as indicative of precision, we observed about 20% and 25% half-widths for 95% exact CIs given an expected 70% response rate in the PF and BMF groups. p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

From December 2018 and April 2022, 30 patients fulfilled inclusion and exclusion criteria. At the end of the planned recruitment period (December 2020), 20 patients had been included in the PF group and five in the BMF group. Two protocol amendments allowed for prolonging the recruitment period by 12 months, then 6 months and including five more patients in the PF group (ethics approvals 1 December 2020 and 2 March 2022). Eventually, the sponsor decided against further prolonging the study and recruitment was terminated in April 2022. One patient withdrew consent before treatment initiation; 29 patients (18 males; median age 58 years) received at least one dose of danazol: 24 in the PF group (16 males; median age 62.5 years) and five in the BMF group (two males; median age 30 years) (figure 1 and supplementary figure S1).

Clinical response was achieved in eight of 24 patients (33%). The median change in FVC from baseline to month 12 was −170 mL (IQR −440–20) in the whole population, corresponding to −220 mL (−470–0) in the PF group. The absolute decline in FVC was −9% (−12– −1) in the whole cohort and −10% (−14– −2) in the PF group. The relative change in DLCO was −3.7% (−20.4–9.6) in the whole cohort and −10.3% (−22.7–4.2) in the PF group (figure 2).

Only 16 patients had a CT scan available at month 12; 10 of 16 (62%) showed radiological progression according to the American Thoracic Society (ATS)/European Respiratory Society (ERS)/Japanese Respiratory Society (JRS)/Latin American Thoracic Association (ALAT) guidelines [12]. Six had both radiological progression and >10% decline in FVC and four had radiological progression but <10% decline in FVC.

The median change in haemoglobin level from baseline at 12 months was +0.6 (IQR −0.4–1.0). The median change in platelet count was +53 g·L−1 (1–72) (figure 2) and median change in neutrophil count was +0.7 g·L−1 (0.26–1.21). Only two patients required a transfusion of blood products: one received platelets only and one received only red blood cells. 10 patients had a bone-marrow examination at month 12; one showed signs of dysplasia, not clinically significant and without associated cytogenetic abnormality.

The median absolute difference in TL at month 12 was +0.2 (IQR 0.0–0.3) (p=0.1) (supplementary figure S2). The Bayesian posterior probability of increased length at month 12 was 73% overall. Of the eight patients with TL available at baseline and <10th percentile, one had TL >10th percentile at month 12. The change in TL did not differ between patients who met and did not meet the primary pneumology outcome criteria (0.1, IQR 0.0–0.2 versus 0.2, 0.05–0.45).

We found no significant improvement in EORTC QLQ30 score (supplementary table S3 and supplementary figure S3), and treatment could, at best, stabilise quality of life.

BMF group

All patients from the BMF group received the treatment for 12 months.

Clinical response was achieved in all five (100%) patients. According to Bayesian estimation, the posterior mean response rate was 85.7% (95% CrI 54.1–99.6) (figure 2). In a post hoc sensitivity analysis, the posterior mean response rate was 91.7% (95% CrI 62.1–100.0).

Three patients (60%) experienced hepatic side effects within the 12-month follow-up and three had an increase in liver enzyme levels that required reducing danazol. Patients reported a median of eight (IQR 4–15) side effects (table 4 and supplementary table S2).

The median change in FVC from baseline was +285 mL (IQR −218–838). The absolute decrease in FVC was −2.5% (−5.5– −0.5). The median relative change in DLCO was 21% (9.6–35.5) (figure 2).

At 12 months, the median change in haemoglobin level from baseline was +3.2 g·dL−1 (IQR 3.1–3.7) (figure 2). The median change in platelet count was +15 g·L−1 (10–21) and the median change in neutrophil count was +0.02 g·L−1 (0.0–0.3). Only one patient required a transfusion of blood products of both platelets and red blood cells. Five patients had a bone-marrow examination at month 12; one showed signs of dysplasia, which was not clinically significant and was without associated cytogenetic abnormality. One patient had a persistent cytogenetic abnormality at month 12 that was already present at inclusion but without dysplastic features.

The median absolute difference in TL at month 12 was +0.2 (IQR 0.1–0.5) (p=0.73). The Bayesian posterior probability of increased TL at month 12 was 73% overall. Of the five patients with TL available at baseline and <10th percentile, none had TL >10th percentile at month 12.

We found no significant improvement in EORTC QLQ30 score (supplementary table S3 and supplementary figure S3) with the limitation of the number of patients analysed.

Discussion

The ANDROTELO study is the first prospective study of danazol in patients with ILD and a TRG pathogenic variant. The tolerance profile of danazol was poor in the PF group: only 10 of the 24 included and treated patients completed the planned 12 months of treatment. The Bayesian probability of efficacy of danazol for PF was only 38%. However, danazol was well tolerated in the 5 BMF patients and might be a possible option for haematological involvement because all five patients completed the 12 months of treatment and achieved haematological response (Bayesian posterior probability of efficacy 85%).

Cohort studies showed that the pulmonary phenotype associated with TRG mutation was not limited to idiopathic PF but included a spectrum of potentially progressive PF [13–18]. They suggested a more rapid decline of FVC of up to 300 mL per year and a more severe prognosis in TRG mutation carriers with ILD compared with those with sporadic idiopathic PF, regardless of the TRG involved and the underlying ILD entity diagnosis [16, 18, 19]. Antifibrotic drugs (nintedanib and pirfenidone) demonstrated a reduced decline of FVC in idiopathic PF and progressive PF cases [20]. One retrospective analysis of patients with a TRG mutation who received antifibrotics showed a reduction in FVC decline after treatment initiation and a safety profile comparable to previous idiopathic PF trial observations [21]. Moreover, a post hoc analysis of trial data suggested that pirfenidone was beneficial regardless of the presence of a TRG mutation. New antifibrotic strategies are needed for idiopathic PF and progressive PF, particularly for TRG variant carriers.

Danazol and androgen agonists seemed promising for patients with telomere biology disorders; however, data concerning ILD are limited. Only 12 of 27 patients in the study by Townsley et al. [5] achieved the 24-month treatment and only eight had DLCO results available at 24 months and four at 36 months. Because corrected DLCO is related to haemoglobin level, DLCO can also be considered a haematological end-point. Only 10 of 20 patients in the study by Clé et al. [6] achieved the 24-month treatment, only five with ILD and three of five showed improvement. Finally, a Dutch prospective observational cohort study failed to show a change in FVC decline in 50 patients with idiopathic PF [22]. Although data from the ANDROTELO study do not support treatment efficacy in TRG-related ILD, results are pending from the TELO-SCOPE study (a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase II trial of danazol for short-telomere-related PF) [23].

The question of a particular toxicity of danazol rather than a lack of efficacy in patients with ILD may be raised. In other prospective clinical trials, most patients had ILD progression or hepatic side effects [5, 6, 22]. Tolerance may indeed be related to the severity of the ILD, and patients with less severe disease could receive treatment with fewer adverse events, less frequent treatment discontinuation or less disease progression. This could explain why the three patients in our haematological group who had interstitial lung abnormalities or ILD and less severe disease at inclusion (FVC 81%, IQR 75–88) continued the 12-month treatment. We found no association between the presence of baseline liver disease and treatment discontinuation in the protocol, which did not allow for identification of an at-risk population in particular; in univariate analysis, pretreatment transaminases and bilirubin were not predictive of discontinuation regardless of the cause. Considering all side effects, danazol cannot likely be considered a long-term therapeutic option, including for asymptomatic relatives.

Danazol increased TL in our study. TL was increased in the study by Townsley et al. [5], with discrepancies between Flow-FISH and PCR analyses and in 10 of 13 patients assessed by Flow-FISH in the study by Clé et al. [6]. In vitro studies support an effect of androgen on TL and telomerase activity [24]. TL measured by Flow-FISH are mean data. Other factors around average TL (e.g. lower value, different type of cells and DNA damage) are likely involved in the pathophysiological process. Therefore, the apparent increase in TL seen on Flow-FISH may be related to other factors such as the selection of cells with the longest telomere rather than an increase in telomerase activity. Of note, in our analysis, four patients aged >70 years were pooled with patients aged 61–69 years. Although the study showed a probability of improvement of TL by Flow-FISH >50% between months 0 and 12, the change could be considered nonclinically significant; moreover, the change in TL was similar in responders and nonresponders in the PF arm, which suggests that this factor may not a good predictor of clinical response.

Although the number of expected inclusions was not reached in the haematological stratum, our results suggest the efficacy of danazol in patients with severe cytopenia and its potential as a first-line treatment. The tolerance of danazol in this group was generally acceptable, with mainly endocrinological and mood effects. The impact on TL was not evident in this subgroup, and no comparison was possible in the absence of nonresponders. Long-term follow-up of these patients are needed to evaluate the impact on extra-haematological manifestations.

This study has several limitations. The single-arm uncontrolled design precludes definitive assessment of the treatment effect. The study was underpowered, especially in the BMF arm. Variants were classified without functional confirmation and not all patients had TL <10th percentile; however, post hoc review of all variants revealed only one class 3 variant, whereas all others were classified as pathogenic. The haematological and pneumological groups were not well balanced, but TRG variants were more frequently found in PF than BMF patients, and several patients received danazol for BMF during the inclusion period out of the clinical trial, thus reducing the number of patients in the BMF group.

Androgens in general and danazol in particular can still be considered for haematological involvement, but regarding side effects, this drug would not likely be offered for treating the pulmonary involvement. The pathophysiology of lung fibrosis in TRG variant carriers remains unknown, and several hypotheses may coexist. Several cohorts have demonstrated the monocyte count as a prognostic biomarker in patients with IPF [25]. We hoped with the ANDROTELO study that the danazol effect on haematological cells may impact the natural history of PF in TRG variant carriers [26]. The ANDROTELO study also showed that a prospective clinical study of a very rare condition could be conducted. This study should encourage us to continue exploring new therapeutic avenues [27].
