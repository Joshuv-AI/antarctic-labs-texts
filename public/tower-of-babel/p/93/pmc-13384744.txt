Atypical Behavior in a Typical Carcinoid: When Minimal Proliferation Meets Lympho‐Vascular Invasion—A Case Report
Authors: Qilian Wang, Xi Chen, Sicheng Zhou, Guowei Che
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTypical carcinoid tumors (TC) are a low‐grade neuroendocrine tumor characterized by low proliferation activity and an overall favorable prognosis. Lympho‐vascular invasion is uncommon in TC and is more frequently associated with atypical carcinoid. We reported a 46‐year‐old non‐smoking woman who underwent right lower lobectomy for a right pulmonary hilar mass after multidisciplinary team (MDT) evaluation (cT1cN0M0, AJCC 9th edition, IA3). Postoperative histopathological examination confirmed typical carcinoid tumor with a low Ki‐67 proliferation index (< 2%), and no tumor necrosis was identified, fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for typical carcinoid according to the current WHO classification of thoracic tumors. Unexpectedly, focal lympho‐vascular invasion in the form of tumor emboli was identified. Additionally, the resected lung showed caseating granulomatous inflammation with acid‐fast bacilli, suggestive of a non‐tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) infection; however, species‐level identification was not performed. No lymph node metastasis was detected (pT1cN0M0, AJCC 9th edition, IA3). The patient had minimal pleural effusion on the surgical side postoperatively, and no signs of recurrence or metastasis have been detected during 20 months of follow‐up to date. This report highlights a pathological discordance between the low proliferation index and the presence of lympho‐vascular invasion in TC. The concomitant granulomatous inflammation may have contributed to diagnostic complexity, although a causal relationship cannot be established. This case underscores the importance of careful histopathological evaluation for invasive features in typical carcinoid tumors, even in the context of low proliferative activity. Surgical treatment alone might be safe in this specific scenario.

Introduction

Primary Pulmonary Carcinoid (PPC) is a rare neuroendocrine tumor (NET) that accounts for 1%–2% of all lung malignancies and occurs in close association with Kulchitsky cells in central bronchial mucosa [1, 2]. The classification of PPC is primarily determined by its biological behavior and immunohistochemical features. The most prevalent classification is between typical carcinoid (TC) and atypical carcinoid (AC), with TC exhibiting a lower proliferative rate, less aggressive biological behavior, and a more favorable prognosis [3].

Histopathological features associated with tumor aggressiveness, including lympho‐vascular invasion and nodal metastasis, are uncommon in TC and are more frequently observed in AC. Consequently, a low proliferative index, particularly a low Ki‐67 labeling index, is often regarded as a marker of indolent behavior in pulmonary carcinoid tumors. However, occasional cases may demonstrate discordance between proliferative activity and invasive pathological features, raising challenges in risk stratification and clinical management.

We report a patient with TC exhibiting a very low Ki‐67 proliferation index (< 2%) but a rare feature of lympho‐vascular invasion identified on postoperative pathological examination. In addition, concomitant caseating granulomatous inflammation with acid‐fast bacilli was observed in the surrounding lung parenchyma, although species‐level identification was not available. This case highlights the potential discrepancy between proliferative activity and invasive behavior in TC and underscores the importance of comprehensive MDT evaluation, even in tumors that would otherwise be considered low risk. The rare feature may also suggest the potential for an underlying biological or behavioral abnormality, a molecular mechanism variant, and a specific tumor microenvironment due to the potential NTM infection [4, 5]. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist.

Case Presentation

A 46‐year‐old non‐smoking woman was incidentally found to have a 2.4 × 2.1 cm right lower lobe hilar nodule on chest computed tomography (CT) IN Dec, 2021 (Figure 1A). Follow‐up CT examined 6 months later showed no significant change in lesion size (Figure 1B). Endobronchial ultrasound guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS‐TBNA) was performed at the same time, which revealed a smooth bronchial surface without evidence of endobronchial obstruction or mucosal abnormality (Figure 1C). Cytopathological examination suggested a neuroendocrine tumor (NET; cT1cN0M0, Stage IA3).

Stepwise diagnostic workup and initial multidisciplinary decision. (A) Contrast‐enhanced chest CT at initial diagnosis showing a 2.4 × 2.1 cm right lower lobe hilar nodule. (B) Follow‐up contrast‐enhanced CT 6 months later showing no significant change. (C) Bronchoscopy revealing a smooth bronchial lumen with no endobronchial lesion. (D) Preoperative CT showing mild to moderate enhancement. Following multidisciplinary discussion (1st MDT), the patient was recommended for surgical resection.

Following MDT discussion, surgical resection was recommended; however, the patient initially declined intervention. After 1 year of radiographic stability, she was admitted for surgical evaluation in May 2023. She had no significant past medical history and physical examination was unremarkable. Contrast‐enhanced CT of the chest demonstrated a stable hilar mass with mild to moderate enhancement without hilar or mediastinal lymphadenopathy (Figure 1D). Laboratory tests revealed elevated blood pro‐gastrin releasing peptide (Pro‐GnRP: 92.37 pg/mL; reference: 0.00–65.7 pg/mL) and a positive interferon‐gamma release assay (TB‐IGRA: 160.59 pg/mL; reference: 0.00–14.00 pg/mL). Meanwhile, systemic staging, including contrast‐enhanced CT of the head and abdomen, revealed no evidence of distant metastasis.

The patient underwent video‐assisted thoracoscopy (VATS) right lower lobectomy with systematic mediastinal lymph node dissection. Intraoperative and gross pathological findings were consistent with a typical pulmonary carcinoid tumor (TC). Unexpectedly, microscopic examination revealed focal lympho‐vascular invasion in the form of tumor emboli, as well as caseating granulomatous inflammation within the surrounding lung parenchyma (Figure 2A,B). Acid‐fast bacilli (AFB) were identified on histochemical staining; however, Mycobacterium tuberculosis –specific DNA was not detected by quantitative PCR (qPCR). These findings were suggestive of mycobacterial infection, but species‐level identification was not performed, and therefore a definitive diagnosis of nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infection could not be established. All 12 resected lymph nodes from 7 stations (Station 2, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10, and 11) were negative for metastasis (pN0). Immunohistochemistry confirmed the diagnosis of typical carcinoid. Tumor cells were positive for synaptophysin (Figure 2C), chromogranin A (CgA), and somatostatin receptor 2 (SSTR2), with weak CK expression and partial SATB2 positivity. The Ki‐67 labeling index was low (< 2%) (Figure 2D), and p53 expression was detected in approximately 2% of tumor cells. CK7, TTF‐1, and GATA3 were negative.

Pathological paradox and multidisciplinary risk reassessment. (A and B) Histology showing typical carcinoid morphology (H&E, ×100, ×200). (C) IHC staining of Syn confirming typical carcinoid (×200). (D) Ki‐67 immunohistochemistry showing minimal proliferation index (< 2%, only 2 positive cells per high‐power field). After 2nd MDT, enhanced surveillance was recommended despite the low Ki‐67 index.

The patient recovered uneventfully after surgery. Following MDT discussion, no adjuvant chemotherapy or anti‐mycobacterial therapy was administered, and active surveillance was recommended. A follow‐up chest CT at 18 months postoperatively demonstrated minimal loculated pleural effusion in the right hemithorax without evidence of tumor recurrence or metastasis (Figure 3A,B).

Multidisciplinary long‐term surveillance. (A) Follow‐up chest CT at 18 months showing minimal loculated pleural effusion without recurrence. (B) Clinical timeline from diagnosis through surgery to 20‐month follow‐up. 3rd MDT decided against anti‐NTM therapy and scheduled regular imaging surveillance.

Discussion

This case highlights an unusual pathological discordance in a typical pulmonary carcinoid (TC), characterized by a very low Ki‐67 proliferation index (< 2%) but the presence of definite lympho‐vascular invasion. In general, TC is regarded as a low‐grade neuroendocrine tumor with an indolent clinical course, and vascular invasion is more commonly associated with atypical carcinoid [2, 3, 4]. Therefore, the coexistence of these features in a tumor with such a low proliferative index represents a notable diagnostic and pathological challenge.

The additional finding of caseating granulomatous inflammation with acid‐fast bacilli in the surrounding lung parenchyma further complicated the pathological interpretation [6]. However, species‐level identification was not performed; thus, a definitive diagnosis of nontuberculous mycobacterial infection cannot be established. The inflammatory background in this case should therefore be interpreted as a concomitant granulomatous process of uncertain etiology.

Although the biological relationship between chronic inflammation and tumor behavior has been suggested in previous studies, no causal inference can be drawn from a single case. The spatial coexistence of lympho‐vascular invasion and granulomatous inflammation in this case may reflect a complex local microenvironment; however, this remains speculative [7, 8, 9].

From a clinical perspective, this case underscores several important points. First, a low Ki‐67 proliferation index should not be assumed to exclude the possibility of lympho‐vascular invasion in TC [2, 10]. Second, careful histopathological evaluation of vascular invasion remains essential in pulmonary neuroendocrine tumors, particularly when biopsy samples may underestimate tumor heterogeneity [2, 11]. Third, the incidental identification of granulomatous inflammation or acid‐fast bacilli in resected specimens should be interpreted cautiously, and management decisions should be guided by clinical context rather than histological findings alone [3].

Finally, this case reinforces the value of complete surgical resection for accurate pathological assessment in pulmonary neuroendocrine tumors, as preoperative sampling may not fully capture invasive features such as lympho‐vascular invasion [12]. However, further studies are required to clarify the prognostic significance of vascular invasion in typical carcinoid tumors with low proliferative activity [11].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a single case report, the observations described herein require validation in larger cohorts to determine their generalizability. Second, although acid‐fast bacilli were identified histologically in the resected lung tissue, species‐level identification and susceptibility testing were not performed; therefore, the granulomatous inflammation cannot be definitively classified as non‐tuberculous mycobacterial infection and should be interpreted as a mycobacterial or mycobacterium‐like infectious process of uncertain etiology. Third, due to the observational nature of this report, no causal relationship can be established between the inflammatory background and the observed lympho‐vascular invasion. Further studies are required to clarify the pathological and clinical significance of lympho‐vascular invasion in typical pulmonary carcinoid tumors.

Conclusions

In this case, a typical pulmonary carcinoid tumor with a very low Ki‐67 proliferation index (< 2%) demonstrated unexpected lympho‐vascular invasion, indicating a discordance between proliferative activity and invasive behavior. These findings suggest that a low Ki‐67 index alone may not fully reflect the biological behavior of typical carcinoid tumors. Careful histopathological assessment, including evaluation for vascular invasion, remains essential even in tumors with low proliferative activity. The presence of concomitant granulomatous inflammation may further complicate pathological interpretation, although its clinical significance remains uncertain in this case.
