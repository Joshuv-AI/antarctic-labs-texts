Association of knee osseous morphometric parameters with degeneration and tear of the discoid lateral meniscus: a cross-sectional quantitative MRI study
Authors: Sadaqat Ali, Wei Zeng, Hongyu Li, Xu Chen, Ziyi Tang, Qiao Liu, Yongliang Pu, Yao Jin, Wenjing Fang, Haitao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiscoid lateral meniscus (DLM) is a congenital knee variant prone to degeneration and tears owing to its unique morphology and altered biomechanics. While magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the gold standard for DLM evaluation, the anatomical contributors to its pathological progression remain unclear. This study aimed to explore MRI-based morphological factors associated with DLM degeneration and tears using quantitative analysis.MethodsA total of 292 patients with DLM were retrospectively divided into three groups based on MRI findings: normal, degenerative, and tear DLM. Quantitative morphometric measurements of knee bony structures were acquired, including fibular head height, tibial spine height, anteroposterior (AP) diameter, width, and height of the medial and lateral femoral condyles, as well as the AP diameter and width of the medial and lateral tibial plateaus. Statistical analyses were performed to explore the associations between these morphological parameters and DLM pathological grading.ResultsThere were significant differences in osseous morphometric MRI parameters between meniscus grading groups. Significant differences were found in fibular head height, medial tibial spine height, width of lateral femoral condyle, medial and lateral femoral condyle heights, AP diameters of the medial and lateral tibial plateau, and medial tibial plateau width between groups (P<0.05). The medial tibial plateau AP diameter [odds ratio (OR) 1.37, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.22–1.55, P<0.001] and fibular head height (OR 1.31, 95% CI: 1.12–1.53, P<0.001) were independent predictors of DLM tears. The medial tibial plateau AP diameter showed superior diagnostic ability [area under the curve (AUC) 0.856, 95% CI: 0.807–0.906], followed by fibular head height (AUC 0.846, 95% CI: 0.792–0.900). The combined model yielded a higher diagnostic value (AUC 0.973, 95% CI: 0.954–0.991).ConclusionsSeveral bony morphological features of the knee, especially the AP diameter of the medial tibial plateau, are associated with an elevated risk of DLM degeneration and tears. These findings may facilitate early risk stratification and clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Discoid lateral meniscus (DLM) is a congenital or developmental abnormality of the knee characterized by central hypertrophy and a thickened, disc-like shape rather than the normal semilunar form (1). It is clinically notable for high tear susceptibility, frequent misdiagnosis, and association with early degenerative joint disease. Medial DLM is extremely rare (0.06–0.3%), while lateral DLM is prevalent (0.4–17%), with Asians (10–15%) more affected than Westerners (3–5%) (2,3). Atypical morphology leads to 35–42% misdiagnosis of DLM tears (4), and delayed diagnosis accelerates knee osteoarthritis (OA) via meniscal instability-induced joint malalignment (5-7).

Due to the nonspecific clinical signs, imaging is critical for diagnosing DLM. Plain radiography, typically as the initial imaging modality, shows indirect signs (e.g., widened lateral joint space, squaring of the lateral femoral condyle) (8,9). Definitive diagnosis depends on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), using criteria including meniscal tibial plateau width ratio ≥20% (coronal), combined anterior/posterior horn width-meniscal diameter ratio ≥75%, coronal width >15 mm, and continuity across three or more 5 mm sagittal slices showing the anterior and posterior horns of the lateral meniscus (10). For the Wrisberg variant, MRI indicates instability/tearing via posterior horn displacement and high T2 signal (3).

From a biomechanical and histological perspective, the discoid meniscus predisposes to abnormal load transmission and structural failure. Degeneration and tearing of the discoid meniscus occur from disruption of the circumferential collagen fiber network, impairing hoop stress distribution (11). Symptomatic DLMs exhibit abnormal collagen fiber morphology and fewer fibers, rendering them more prone to tearing or displacement during normal activity (12,13). MRI studies of asymptomatic DLMs show increased meniscal width and altered ratios, indicating that inherent anatomy, not just tears, may influence symptoms (13). While morphological abnormalities of the DLM relative to normal menisci, as well as associated clinical symptoms, have been documented in previous studies (14-16), few have systematically evaluated the anatomical factors correlated with DLM tear risk. Furthermore, quantitative morphometric assessment of knee anatomical structures has not been fully exploited to predict the occurrence of DLM tears.

Therefore, the present study aimed to identify anatomical factors associated with tear development in the DLM using quantitative MRI. We hypothesized that specific knee morphometric variations are significantly associated with an elevated risk of DLM tearing. Such anatomical configurations may alter load transmission and joint congruity, thereby predisposing the DLM to increased mechanical stress and subsequent structural failure. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0398/rc).

Methods

Subjects

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This retrospective comparative study was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Review Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (No. KX2026-KYC122) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. We retrospectively reviewed patients who underwent knee MRI at our department between January 2019 and June 2025 using the picture archiving and communication system (PACS). Subjects aged 18–60 years with MRI-diagnosed complete DLM, as defined by established criteria (17), were enrolled in the study. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) patients with DLM bucket-handle tears, which involve complex and heterogeneous pathogenic mechanisms, were excluded from the present study; (II) concurrent acute ligamentous injury; (III) a history of prior knee fracture or surgery, which would interfere with morphometric assessment; (IV) inflammatory arthritis, advanced OA, or inadequate MRI quality. Subjects under 18 and over 60 were excluded, as incomplete maturation of the bony structures of the knee in the young and degenerative changes associated with advanced age may compromise measurement accuracy.

A total of 306 consecutive subjects with complete DLM identified on knee MRI initially met the inclusion criteria. Fourteen patients with bucket-handle meniscal tears were subsequently excluded. The final study cohort thus consisted of 292 subjects. Based on MRI morphologic features and signal characteristics, subjects were classified into three groups: (I) normal DLM, without evidence of degeneration or tear (n=108); (II) degenerative DLM, characterized by intrameniscal signal changes without definite articular surface disruption (n=91); and (III) tear DLM, defined by abnormal meniscal morphology and signal intensity extending to at least one articular surface (n=93). MRI protocol

Knee MRI examinations were conducted using 1.5-T or 3.0-T scanners (Magnetom Essenza/Skyra, Siemens Healthcare, Munich, Germany) with a dedicated extremity matrix knee coil. Standardized scanning sequences with consistent 16 cm field of view (FOV) and 4 mm slice thickness were applied, including sagittal and coronal T1-weighted turbo spin echo (TSE) [repetition time (TR), 520 ms; echo time (TE) 11–13 ms; 320×320 matrix], sagittal T2-weighted TSE (TR 3,100 ms; TE 77–116 ms; 320×320 matrix), proton density fat-suppressed sequences (PD-FS) (TR 2,360 ms; TE 39–48 ms; 256×256 matrix), and axial T2-weighted TSE (TR 2,790 ms; TE 26–67 ms; 256×256 matrix). Unified scanning parameters were used on both magnetic field strength devices to guarantee consistent image quality.

All MRI parameters were quantitatively measured by two trained musculoskeletal radiologists (S.A. and X.C., with 2 and 6 years of clinical experience, respectively), and the interval between the first and second readings was 4 weeks (S.A.). Measurements were performed on a randomly selected subset of 70 patients. Before quantitative MRI measurements, two assessors underwent systematic training and practice to understand the MRI features of each lesion and method, and had to pass a test administered by a senior musculoskeletal radiologist (H.Y.).

Image analysis

Thirteen morphometric MRI measurements were performed on the PACS workstation using standardized coronal, sagittal, and axial knee imaging sequences. (I) Fibular head height was defined as the perpendicular distance between a horizontal line across the tibial plateau and a second line through the highest point of the fibular head, in accordance with previous studies (18,19) (Figure 2A). (II) The lateral and medial tibial spine heights were defined as the vertical distance from the apex of the tibial spine to a reference line along the tibial plateau, as described by prior studies (20) (Figure 2B). (III) The anteroposterior (AP) diameter of the lateral and medial femoral condyle was measured on the axial slice level of the intercondylar fossa with respect to previous studies (21) (Figure 2C). (IV) The femoral condyle width was measured using a combined coronal-axial approach designed for this study. This method ensures accurate assessment of the distal femoral shape by first selecting the correct axial slice, guided by a coronal reference line above the intercondylar fossa. The measurement involves determining the maximal horizontal width from the lateral cortical margin to a vertical line drawn from the deepest part of the anterior cortex (22) (Figure 2D). (V) The lateral and medial femoral condyle heights were determined as perpendicular distances from the most distal point of each condyle to the respective femoral reference line on coronal MRI images; similar condylar height measurements have been used in distal femoral morphometric studies (21) (Figure 2E). (VI) The AP diameters of the lateral and medial tibial plateaus were measured on the central sagittal slice as the distance between the anterior and posterior articular margins, consistent with AP tibial plateau measurements described by previous studies (23) (Figure 2F). (VII) The width of the lateral and medial tibial plateau was measured as the maximal transverse diameter on coronal MRI, in accordance with tibial plateau morphometric methods reported by prior studies (24) (Figure 2G).

Quantitative MRI measurements. (A) Fibular head height: perpendicular distance between the tibial plateau and the highest point of the fibular head on coronal images. (B) Tibial spine height: vertical distance from the medial tibial spine apex to the tibial plateau. (C) AP femoral condyle diameter: measured parallel to the reference line on axial images at the intercondylar fossa, spanning the anterior and posterior condylar margins. (D) Femoral condyle width: maximum horizontal width of the condyle on axial slices referenced to the coronal plane above the intercondylar fossa. (E) Femoral condyle height: perpendicular distance between lines along the intercondylar notch and distal condylar surface. (F) AP tibial plateau diameter: AP span of the anterior and posterior articular borders on the central sagittal slice. (G) Tibial plateau width: maximum mediolateral width of the medial tibial plateau on coronal articular-surface images. AP, anteroposterior; MRI, magnetic resonance imaging.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was first applied to evaluate the normality of all knee morphometric measurements. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed variables to compare differences across the three groups. In contrast, the Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA test was used for non-normally distributed variables. Once a significant overall intergroup difference was identified, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed to control for multiple testing. Univariate and multivariable multinomial logistic regression analyses were used to examine independent associations between anatomical MRI indices and DLM status (categorized as normal, degenerative, and tear). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic efficacy of significant quantitative MRI parameters. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to evaluate intra- and inter-observer measurement agreement, with agreement graded as good (ICC ≥ 0.75), fair (0.50 ≤ ICC < 0.75), and poor (ICC < 0.50). A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, with the stricter Bonferroni correction applied for multiple pairwise comparisons where appropriate.

Demographic characteristics of the study cohort

Among 292 subjects with DLM, 108 cases (men 47, women 61, mean age 41.23±9.62) were normal DLM, 91 cases (men 42, women 49, mean age 42.07±10.66) were degenerative DLM, and 93 cases (men 35, women 58, mean age 44.20±11.28) were tear DLM. There were no statistically significant differences in age, sex distribution, or body mass index (BMI) among the three groups (all P>0.05) (Table 1 and Table S1).

Analysis identified anatomical factors associated with DLM degeneration and tear

Univariate analysis identified anatomical factors associated with DLM degeneration and tears: higher fibular head height correlated with both conditions, medial tibial plateau AP diameter was the strongest factor for meniscal tears [odds ratio (OR) 1.26, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.16–1.36, P<0.001], and lateral tibial plateau AP diameter was significantly associated with tears (Table 3). Multivariable multinomial logistic regression showed that medial tibial plateau AP diameter remained the strongest independent predictor of tears (OR 1.37, 95% CI: 1.22–1.55, P<0.001); fibular head height independently increased the odds of degeneration/tear with a modest effect (OR 1.31, 95% CI: 1.12–1.53, P<0.001); width of the lateral femoral condyle was independently associated with DLM tears compared with the normal group (OR 0.80, 95% CI: 0.70–0.90, P<0.001); medial femoral condyle height was also independently associated with tears (OR 0.85, 95% CI: 0.72–0.99, P=0.044). Other univariate significant variables lost significance after adjustment (Table 4, Figure 4).

Association between quantitative MRI metrics and the DLM degeneration and tears

ROC analyses were performed to explore the anatomical correlates and pathological basis of DLM lesions spanning normal status, degeneration, and tear, rather than for direct clinical diagnosis. Multiple quantitative MRI morphological parameters demonstrated good discriminative power for DLM pathological changes (Figure 5). The medial tibial plateau AP diameter had the best individual performance [area under the curve (AUC) =0.856, 95% CI: 0.807–0.906; sensitivity: 90%, specificity: 51%]. Fibular head height also exhibited strong discrimination (AUC =0.846, 95% CI: 0.792–0.900; sensitivity 86%, specificity 47%). Medial and lateral femoral condyle heights showed moderate efficacy, with AUCs of 0.801 (95% CI: 0.738–0.864) for both. Lateral femoral condyle and tibial plateau diameters showed acceptable predictive performance, with AUCs ranging from 0.751 to 0.782. By comparison, medial tibial spine height had no significant correlation with DLM lesions (P=0.16, P=0.19). The combined logistic regression model of multiple MRI anatomical parameters achieved optimal discriminative efficiency (AUC =0.973, 95% CI: 0.954–0.991; sensitivity 95%, specificity 81%), supporting the notion that anatomical morphological variations underlie the development and progression of DLM degeneration and injury.

Receiver operating characteristic curves of each morphological factor associated with DLM. AP, anteroposterior; DLM, discoid lateral meniscus; ROC, receiver operating characteristic.

Reproducibility of quantitative MRI measurements (intra/inter-observer reliability)

The intra-observer reliability of anatomical MRI measurements was good, with ICCs ranging from 0.780 to 0.998. Inter-observer reliability was also fair to good, with ICCs ranging from 0.719 to 0.945, indicating good agreement among observers and high measurement consistency (Tables S2,S3).

Discussion

This present study included a large sample of DLM cases for quantitative MRI analysis to explore anatomical factors associated with DLM degeneration and tears. Our study confirmed the presence of several bony geometric variations across DLM grades, involving the medial and lateral tibial plateaus, the fibular head height, and the femoral condyles. Among these factors, increased AP diameter of the medial tibial plateau and increased fibular head height were independently associated with DLM tears.

Among all evaluated morphometric parameters, AP diameter of the medial tibial plateau demonstrated the strongest association with DLM tear. This observation is biomechanically plausible and supported by prior MRI-based morphologic studies showing that increased AP diameter facilitates greater femoral rollback and tibiofemoral translation during knee flexion, thereby amplifying shear and compressive forces acting on the meniscus (17,25). In the context of a discoid meniscus, which already occupies a disproportionately large portion of the lateral compartment, such altered kinematics may accelerate intra-meniscal degeneration and precipitate surface-disrupting tears. Similar associations between tibial plateau geometry and meniscal injury have also been reported in non-discoid populations, reinforcing the concept that bony architecture constitutes a fundamental associated factor for meniscal pathology (26). An increased AP size of the medial tibial plateau may lead to DLM injury by affecting tibiofemoral movement and load distribution across the knee (27). A relatively elongated medial tibial plateau can alter the tibia’s sagittal alignment, potentially leading to greater anterior tibial movement and rotational instability during weight-bearing (28). These biomechanical shifts can shift contact forces toward the lateral side, increasing shear stress and repetitive loading on the lateral meniscus. Since a discoid meniscus is already thicker and less flexible, this uneven load may promote degeneration and tears (29). Thus, variations in the AP diameter of the medial tibial plateau can affect lateral meniscal integrity by altering overall knee biomechanics, rather than acting as a purely medial factor.

Building on previous research, recent studies highlight tibial morphology as a key factor in meniscal injury, linking changes in tibial spine height, plateau shape, and intercondylar notch morphology to the incidence and severity of tears (30). Our study extends these findings by focusing on the DLM group. It shows that the medial tibial plateau widens prematurely, at the degenerative stage, before surface tears, indicating that skeletal morphology contributes to early meniscal pathology. This aligns with MRI studies of discoid menisci, such as those by Dai et al. (25), who found sagittal AP differences between intact and discoid menisci with tears, emphasizing the role of AP geometry in DLM pathology. By shifting the focus from meniscal size to bone structure, our research provides a structural basis for the diverse clinical symptoms observed. In addition to tibial plateau geometry, femoral condylar height also independently correlates with DLM tears, affecting joint congruency and contact mechanics and potentially increasing stress on the lateral meniscus during weight-bearing, similar to findings in knees with ligament pathology (31,32). Other parameters, such as tibial spine height and tibial plateau width, showed no significant differences among groups (33), indicating that tibial enlargement or vertical prominence alone doesn't significantly influence injury risk. Instead, sagittal-plane geometry, especially the AP diameter of the medial tibial plateau, affects tibiofemoral translation and rotation, potentially increasing shear and compressive stresses that lead to early degeneration and tears of the discoid meniscus (34).

The height of the fibular head was also a significant factor associated with DLM injury in the multivariable analysis. Although it is often overlooked in meniscal studies, fibular head enlargement has long been recognized as a characteristic imaging feature in knees with a discoid meniscus (35,36). Our findings indicate that fibular head height may serve as an indicator of the structural configuration of the lateral compartment and of changes in load transmission, reflecting the combined effects of congenital shape and adaptive joint mechanics. Because it is easily identified on routine MRI, fibular head height could act as a useful additional parameter for imaging-based risk assessment. The height of the fibular head can significantly influence the development of DLM injuries by affecting lateral knee stability and load sharing (37). A higher or more proximal fibular head height can change the tension and alignment of key stabilizing structures in the posterolateral corner (PLC), such as the lateral collateral ligament (LCL) and biceps femoris tendon. These changes may weaken the lateral compartment’s ability to resist varus and rotational forces during movement, thereby increasing shear stress on the lateral tibiofemoral joint. In knees with a DLM, which is generally thicker and less adaptable, such biomechanical shifts may accelerate meniscal overload and increase the risk of meniscal tears (38). Consequently, the height of the fibular head should be considered a factor influencing lateral compartment biomechanics and may contribute to DLM injury.

Medial tibial plateau AP diameter (OR 1.26, 95% CI: 1.16–1.36) and fibular head height (OR 1.24, 95% CI: 1.09–1.42) were significantly but weakly to moderately associated with DLM injury, reflecting the multifactorial pathogenesis of DLM injury. An enlarged medial tibial plateau AP diameter amplifies anterior tibial translation and axial rotational torque during weight-bearing, concentrating pathological hoop stress and shear forces on the lateral compartment (39,40). Similarly, a higher fibular head impairs PLC stability, thereby weakening the lateral compartment’s resistance to varus and rotational loading (41). Such mechanical abnormalities promote progressive meniscal degeneration and tearing, further predisposing to early OA that may necessitate arthroscopic repair (42,43). Beyond their biomechanical implications, these two morphological parameters are easily and reproducibly quantified on routine MRI to identify anatomically high-risk DLM patients and to guide early lifestyle adjustments, follow-up scheduling, and orthopedic referral prior to irreversible meniscal and cartilage injury. These markers may serve as auxiliary imaging indicators and, combined with meniscal morphological assessment and clinical evaluation, facilitate risk stratification and clinical decision-making.

This study has several limitations. First, DLM diagnosis and pathological grading relied solely on MRI findings without arthroscopic or histopathological verification, which may introduce potential misclassification bias. Nonetheless, the MRI criteria for differentiating DLM degeneration from tear, based on meniscal signal features and articular surface integrity, are well established and widely validated in the literature (44). Second, some clinical and biomechanical factors, including BMI, lower-limb alignment, activity levels, and history of microtrauma, were not evaluated, potentially confounding the observed associations. Third, the absence of a non-DLM control group limited the evaluation of the specificity of anatomical features for DLM pathology. Nevertheless, previous case-control studies between DLM and non-DLM populations have already verified consistent osseous morphological alterations associated with DLM, supporting the reliability of the morphological parameters analyzed in our work (45,46). Future prospective longitudinal studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate our results, adjust for clinical and biomechanical confounders, and clarify the interactive impacts of anatomical features on DLM onset and progression.

Conclusions

Quantitative MRI analysis revealed that a larger AP diameter of the medial tibial plateau, a higher fibular head height, and femoral condylar height are independently associated with degeneration and tears in the DLM. Of these, the AP diameter of the medial tibial plateau was the strongest associated factor of tears, highlighting the importance of tibial sagittal geometry in discoid meniscus biomechanics. These MRI-based morphometric markers provide objective imaging features that could support early risk assessment and personalized treatment. Nonetheless, longitudinal prospective studies examining biomechanical and clinical outcomes are needed to validate their association-based risk stratification.
