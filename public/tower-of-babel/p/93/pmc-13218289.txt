Mapping antibiotic treatment failure definitions and criteria in ambulatory care: a systematic literature review
Authors: Fade Alkhlaileh, Annelien Dankaerts, Tareq Haddad, Jan Y Verbakel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost antibiotic prescriptions occur in ambulatory care, making it a key setting for evaluating treatment outcomes. However, there is no consensus on how antibiotic treatment failure (ATF) is defined.ObjectivesTo systematically map definitions and criteria used to define ATF among patients with common bacterial infections in ambulatory care.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, Scopus, and Web of Science Core Collection for studies published from 1 January 1996 to 7 February 2025, and reported following PRISMA guidelines. Eligible studies included patients of any age with common infections treated in ambulatory care. ATF definitions and criteria were extracted, coded, and analysed using inductive and deductive content analysis. Risk of bias was assessed with the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, AXIS, and Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool 2.0 (PROSPERO: CRD42023484991).ResultsOf 8979 records, 194 studies were included, mostly purely outpatient (56%), retrospective cohort (53%), and from high-income countries (84%); 65% had low-risk of bias. Terminology for ATF varied. Four main criteria were identified: ‘Prescription Change’ (78%), ‘Clinical Condition’ (54%), ‘Escalation of Care’ (41%), and ‘Mortality’ (9%), encompassing 25 unique sub-criteria. One complementary criterion, ‘Diagnostic Tests’ also emerged. ATF follow-up time windows differed by indication, shorter for skin, ear, and respiratory infections and longer for genitourinary or mixed infections. Limitations include coding subjectivity and ambiguous definitions.ConclusionATF is inconsistently defined, timed, and operationalized. Our review maps common criteria and sub-criteria to support future consensus-building efforts aiming for standardization and improved study comparability.

Introduction

Antibioticprescription in ambulatory care settings represents a significant proportion (around 80%) of overall antibiotic use,1,2 making this context critically important for understanding and addressing antibiotic treatment failure (ATF). Given that ambulatory care is the frontline for managing common infections, understanding and defining ATF in this setting is crucial for research, clinical practice, and public health.

ATF is influenced not only by antimicrobial resistance (AMR), but also by patient-related factors (e.g. immunosuppression, non-adherence) and clinician-related factors (e.g. diagnostic uncertainty, inappropriate prescribing).3–6 Understanding ATF therefore requires a broader lens that captures the interplay between clinical response, prescribing decisions, and healthcare escalation.

Despite its importance, there's still no widely accepted definition of ATF, which poses a substantial challenge in both clinical and research contexts.7–9 Achieving clarity and standardization of ATF would improve the ability to compare studies and draw reliable conclusions, which facilitates scientific progress. Moreover, it can be used to assist clinicians in determining when and how to assess antibiotic effectiveness, steering subsequent management steps in line with a more evidence-based approach. Furthermore, a well-defined ATF metric could serve as a valuable measure for antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) programme outcomes, and serve as a reliable point of data to support policy and decision makers.

Neill et al.8 conducted a systematic review of randomized trials and observational studies on antibiotic prescribing strategies, specifically focusing on variations in ATF definitions. Their study identified substantial variability in ATF components and timeframes, even among studies examining the same infection types. Key criteria commonly used in ATF definitions included prescription changes, escalation of care, and changes in clinical condition. The variability even extended to the terminologies used, where ‘ATF’, ‘clinical failure’, and ‘therapy failure’ were used interchangeably.

Our systematic review seeks to contribute to the ongoing effort to establish a more consistent definition of ATF by answering the question; What are the definitions and criteria used by researchers to define ATF in patients presenting with commonly encountered bacterial infections in ambulatory care across clinical studies? To this end, the review takes a deliberately broad approach, incorporating diverse infection types, timeframes, study designs, and ambulatory settings. Rather than proposing or imposing a standardized definition, this review aims to comprehensively map how ATF has been operationalized in the existing literature. By systematically analysing and organizing reported criteria, sub-criteria, and time windows, we develop a descriptive framework that reflects current research practice. This taxonomy is intended as a map of existing approaches rather than a prescriptive or definitive classification, and is designed to inform and facilitate future consensus-building efforts, leading to clearer comparisons across studies, more reliable guidance for clinical decision-making, and enhancing the usefulness of ATF as an outcome measure in AMS.

Methods

Study design and registration

This systematic review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.10 The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42023484991).11

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using the following databases: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, Scopus, and Web-of-Science Core Collection. The search strategy was designed to capture studies published from 1 January 1996 onwards, which is around the time antibiotic stewardship programs were beginning to be adopted.12,13 Key terms included ‘antibiotic’, ‘treatment failure’, ‘ATF’, and a list of commonly encountered infectious disease in ambulatory care [see Appendix S1 (available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online) for full search strings].14–16 The main search was done on 13 October 2023, and an updated search was reran on 7 February 2025 to identify newly published studies.

Study selection

We included studies involving patients of any age who were managed for commonly encountered infections in ambulatory care settings, including general practice, outpatient clinics, and emergency or urgent-care departments. This focus aligns with healthcare settings where the majority of antibiotics are prescribed.1,17 Eligible interventions were oral or topical antibiotic monotherapy or fixed-dose combinations commonly used in primary care such as amoxicillin/clavulanic acid. Additionally, intramuscular formulations of benzathine-penicillin G (for syphilis) and ceftriaxone (for gonorrhoea) were included, as they are recommended as first-line treatments in clinical guidelines.18–21 These criteria reflect real-world prescribing practices.

The primary outcome of interest was the definition of ATF as reported by study authors, including the criteria used to operationalize the term. While numerical data such as failure rates or risk factors were extracted, they were not analysed in this review, and will be reported in a future publication.

We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), non-randomized interventional studies, cohort studies, case-control studies, and cross-sectional studies to capture the range of ATF definitions currently being used in real-world research settings.

We excluded studies conducted exclusively in laboratory or microbiological contexts, those involving dental settings, hospital in-patients or ICU populations, and special patient populations such as individuals with cancer, those undergoing dialysis, or those who were immunocompromised. We also excluded case reports, guidelines, conference abstracts, study protocols, economic or modelling studies, and systematic reviews. However, reference lists of relevant reviews were screened for eligible primary studies.

No language or publication-status limits were applied; non-English articles were translated with DeepL® Translate.22 No published search filters were used. Duplicate studies were removed using Endnote® and Covidence®.23,24 Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts against eligibility criteria; disagreements were resolved by discussion or a third reviewer. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction and Risk of Bias assessment were performed independently and in duplicate using the Covidence® systematic review software. To ensure the reliability of the qualitative categorization of ATF definitions, any discrepancies between the two primary reviewers were resolved through a formal consensus procedure involving a third senior reviewer [JV]. The researchers collected data on study characteristics (authors, year, time-frame, country, setting), methods (study design, antibiotics used, data collection methods and sources, inclusion and exclusion criteria, sample size, withdrawals), patient characteristics (age, gender, ethnicity, comorbidities, smoking, BMI, socioeconomic status), as well as ATF definitions and criteria (as reported by the authors) and the time frames indicated for the ATF definitions (time window and starting point). Two secondary outcomes were also extracted but will be reported in a separate paper which are; ATF rates, and ATF risk factors.

Risk of bias assessment

Quality assessment was performed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for cohort, cross-sectional, and case-control studies,25 the Appraisal tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS),26 and the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool 2.0 for RCTs.27 Two independent reviewers assessed the risk of bias, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Data synthesis and analysis

We used a combined quantitative and qualitative content analysis approach, applying a mixed inductive-deductive method.28,29 Three criteria were defined in advance based on the framework by Neill et al.8 namely ‘Clinical Condition’, ‘Prescription Change’, and ‘Escalation of Care’. In parallel, we manually conducted an inductive analysis by systematically categorizing ATF definitions from the included studies, allowing additional criteria to emerge naturally from the content and context of each definition.

For each extracted definition of ATF, we first recorded whether the definition was explicitly stated by the authors or inferred from the text. We then assessed whether multiple definitions were reported within the same study and documented each separately when applicable. Each definition was then carefully examined to determine whether the conditions it specified matched any of our pre-defined criteria. If a definition did not align with existing criteria, a new criterion was created to accommodate it. Within each criterion, we further identified and classified sub-criteria based on the specific components described in the extracted definitions. It is important to note that sub-criteria were treated as individually sufficient; the presence of any single sub-criterion was enough to consider the broader criterion fulfilled.

For every definition, we also extracted details about the timeframe, including both the duration and the starting point of the time window used to define ATF. At the end of the coding process, we quantified the number of identified criteria and sub-criteria, and the time window durations and their starting points across all included studies. Discrepancies in the categorization of ATF definitions between reviewers were resolved via a dedicated formal meeting with a third senior reviewer [JV], to ensure reliability of the final taxonomy.

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics to summarize the distribution of the upper-limit values of time windows used to define ATF across studies. Specifically, we generated boxplots to display the median, interquartile range (IQR), and range of time window durations for each condition. Outliers were defined as values falling below Q1 − (1.5×IQR), or above Q3 + (1.5×IQR), and were plotted as individual points.

Given the qualitative nature of our outcome (definitions and criteria used to describe ATF), no meta-analysis was conducted, and statistical measures such as effect estimates, heterogeneity statistics, or sensitivity analyses were not applicable. Our synthesis relied on a combined inductive-deductive content analysis approach, focused on thematic and frequency-based categorization of qualitative data.

Results

Identification of studies

From the database search, a total of 18 491 records were identified across Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, MEDLINE, and CENTRAL. After removing 9512 duplicate records, 8979 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 465 full-text reports were sought for retrieval, with 434 retrieved and assessed for eligibility. Ultimately, 194 studies were included in the review (Figure 1).10

Overview of extracted definitions

We extracted the definitions and criteria used to describe ATF from the 194 included studies. A comprehensive list of all extracted definitions is presented in Table S1. In 14 studies (7.2%), the authors did not explicitly report an ATF definition. Terminology used to describe ATF was highly variable. While ‘treatment failure’ was the most common term (43.8%), alternatives such as ‘clinical failure’ (11.9%), ‘non-response’ (7.7%), and ‘treatment switch’ (4.6%) were also used. Notably, 25.3% of studies described failure outcomes without using any explicit failure-related terminology (Table S2). In these cases, the definition was derived from the criteria used to measure and report ATF rates. Nineteen studies (9.8%) employed multiple definitions of ATF. This was often observed when different definitions were applied for early versus late ATF, or when both a specific and a cumulative treatment failure definition were used, or when a stricter definition was utilized for sensitivity analysis. In some studies, ATF was closely linked with relapse, re-infection, or recurrence, either by incorporating these events into the ATF definition or by reporting them together as a composite failure outcome. Notably, in several cases, the definitions and criteria used for relapse were identical to those for ATF, differing only in the application of a longer time window. A list of the extracted definitions for relapse, re-infection, and recurrence is provided in Table S3.

Criteria and sub-criteria

Through analysing the extracted definitions, we identified recurring themes that were subsequently organized into criteria and sub-criteria. Three criteria were pre-defined based on previous work by Neill et al.8 and those were ‘Clinical Condition’, ‘Prescription Change’, and ‘Escalation of Care’. Through our analysis we identified a fourth criterion ‘Mortality’, as well as one complementary criterion less frequently used ‘Diagnostic Tests’. Finally, we created a ‘Residual Category’ for sub-criteria not aligning with the main taxonomy found in Supplementary materials section S10. A breakdown of the coding for criteria and sub-criteria using examples is shown in Table S4. The following criteria and sub-criteria emerged from this analysis, starting with the four main criteria:

1) Clinical Condition

1) Clinical Condition

This criterion reflects the patient's clinical condition throughout the course of antibiotic treatment. It primarily captures the presence, persistence, worsening, or recurrence of signs and symptoms, the necessity for medical interventions or procedures, the occurrence of infectious complications, or any adverse effects (AEs) related to antibiotic use.

The utilization of Clinical Condition and its sub-criteria is illustrated in Figure 2a. AEs specifically attributed to antibiotic use were infrequently reported, with severe AEs noted in 3 studies (2.9%),32–34 and any AEs in another 3 studies (2.9%).35–37 Finally, the least reported sub-criteria was Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) (1 study).36

Diagram detailing the identified sub-criteria within (a) Clinical Condition, (b) Prescription Change, (c) Escalation of Care, and (d) Mortality, while also showing the number of studies that used each sub-criterion. IV, intravenous; AE, Adverse Effects; CDI, Clostridioides difficile infection; ER, Emergency Room.

Murray et al.38 explicitly stated that antibiotic changes due to side effects alone were not considered treatment failure, classifying such patients as clinical responders if no other failure criteria were met. Nine other authors also excluded side effects as an indicator of ATF,39–47 with one specifically excluding only non-severe side effects from their definition.32

2) Prescription Change

2) Prescription Change

The criterion Prescription Change describes any modifications made to the antibiotic regimen during treatment. This includes switching from one antibiotic to another (e.g. antibiotic A to antibiotic B), adding a second agent to the existing therapy (e.g. A to A + B), prolonging the duration of the current antibiotic (e.g. A to extended A), upgrading the route of administration (e.g. from oral to intravenous), or increasing the antibiotic dose.

The criterion Prescription Change was the most widely used and was employed in 151 out of the 194 included studies (77.8%) (Figure 2b). The most commonly reported sub-criterion was ‘different prescription’ (which could indicate either a switch or addition), appearing in 79 studies (52.3%). This was followed by the use of a ‘subsequent prescription’ (which does not specify if the change was either a switch, addition, or prolongation) in 48 studies (31.8%). Less frequently reported sub-criteria included ‘prolongation’ of the original antibiotic course (19 studies, 12.6%), ‘switch’ to a different antibiotic class (18 studies, 11.9%), and ‘upgrade to intravenous therapy’ (16 studies, 10.6%). Furthermore, ‘addition’ of a second agent was reported in 5 studies (3.3%),34,48–51 while ‘increased antibiotic dose’ appeared in just 1 study.50

Notably, only Madaras-Kelly et al.50 used the sub-criterion ‘increased dose’ and were the only authors to explicitly differentiate between addition, switch, and prolongation of antibiotic therapy, rather than using broader, less specific sub-criteria such as ‘different’ or ‘subsequent’ prescription. Meanwhile, Wolterink et al.52 explicitly excluded ‘prolongation’ from their ATF definition, considering it a continuation, prophylaxis, or new episode rather than failure. They also defined ATF based on changes to a pre-specified antibiotic list, explicitly excluding agents like amoxicillin or doxycyclines, which were considered likely prescribed for non-UTI reasons. Furthermore, Vogler et al.53 considered the need to change antibiotics an indication of ATF only if it was based on culture and sensitivity results. Reilev et al.54 introduced a nuanced approach, considering prescription switch later on in the treatment as treatment failure, while switches within the first 3 days were not considered as ATF, and were attributed to non-adherence or side effects.

3) Escalation of Care

3) Escalation of Care

This criterion refers to the need to intensify medical management due to inadequate response to initial antibiotic therapy. This may involve a visit to the emergency department, hospitalization, transfer to a higher level of care, or referral to a specialist service such as a microbiology lab or a specialist physician clinic.

The criterion Escalation of Care was used in 79 out of the 194 included studies (40.7%) (Figure 2c). Less frequently reported sub-criteria included ‘return visits’ to the healthcare provider, recorded in 16 studies (20.3%), and ‘referral to a specialist service’, which appeared in 5 studies (6.3%).38,55–58 The conditional reasons for ‘hospital admission’, as described in the included studies, varied and were often linked either to the underlying diagnosis itself, complications arising from the diagnosis, or the need for intravenous (IV) antibiotic administration. It is worth noting that some studies counted hospitalizations for any reason (all-cause hospitalizations), without specifying whether they were related to the infection under treatment.

4) Mortality

4) Mortality

Mortality describes instances where a patient undergoing antibiotic treatment dies. Some studies specifically require attributing mortality to an infectious disease, while others report all-cause mortality or exclude accident-related deaths. However, the majority of studies employing this criterion do not specify the cause of death.

As demonstrated in Figure 2d, Mortality was utilized in 18 out of the 194 included studies (9.3%). Of which, the most commonly reported sub-criterion was unspecified mortality, appearing in 11 studies (61.1%). Fewer studies chose more specific mortality data: death with an infection-related diagnosis was reported in 4 studies (22.2%),55–57,59 all-cause mortality was used as a criterion in 2 studies (11.1%), and non-accident-related mortality was reported in just 1 study (5.6%).60

Diagnostic tests; a complementary criterion

Unlike the main criteria, this complementary criterion usually does not independently reflect treatment failure and was mostly used as secondary or adjunct measures rather than a stand-alone criterion.

It describes any laboratory tests or cultures used as part of the assessment of ATF, and it was applied in 12 of the 194 included studies (6.2%). Nine distinct sub-criteria were identified. The four most commonly reported sub-criteria, each appearing in 2 studies, were ‘positive test-of-cure (TOC) results’ for Mycoplasma genitalium urethritis,61,62 ‘≥5 polymorphonuclear leukocytes per high-power field (PMNs/HPF) on microscopic examination’ for non-gonococcal urethritis,63,64 ‘positive rapid antigen test or throat culture for group A Streptococcus (GAS)’,65,66 and ‘pyuria or positive urine culture’ for UTIs.67,68 The remaining sub-criteria were each reported in a single study and included ‘positive blood culture isolating the same pathogen as the index infection’ for catheter-associated UTI,69 ‘clue cells plus ≥2 positive Amsel criteria’ indicating bacterial vaginosis,70 ‘positive culture for methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) from any site’ for skin infections,71 and ‘detection of AMR to the empiric regimen’ in UTI.72

All studies using this criterion also incorporated at least one of the main criteria. Most of the studies applying this criterion focused on UTIs, particularly urethritis. This suggests that while certain diagnostic tests may be useful as independent measures in specific conditions, the overall criterion may lack applicability across all infection types.

Interestingly, some studies examined microbiological or bacteriological treatment failure alongside ATF, either to explore potential correlations or as an additional measure of antibiotic efficacy. The extracted definitions for these studies are provided in Table S5.

Criteria use across all studies

Number of criteria used

The maximum number of criteria used in a single study was four. Most studies used two of the six criteria in their definition of ATF (88 studies, 45%), followed by one criterion (63 studies, 32%), three criteria (38 studies, 20%), and four criteria (5 studies, 3%) (Figure 3a). Nearly seven out of ten studies (68%) employed composite definitions spanning multiple criteria. The pattern varied for some indication groups. For example, studies on lower respiratory tract infections (LRTI) and skin and soft tissue infections (SSTI) more often used three or more criteria (37% and 44%, respectively). Notably, in a subset of paediatrics only studies (n = 67) overall patterns of the number of criteria used were still highly consistent with the entire 194 included studies.

Type of criteria used

As described in Figure 3b, Prescription Change was the most frequently used criterion overall, appearing in 151 studies (78%). Clinical Condition was the second most used criterion, reported in 104 studies (54%). Escalation of Care was used in 79 studies (41%), followed by Mortality (18 studies, 9%). The complementary criterion Diagnostic Tests (12 studies, 6%) was used less frequently and was limited to a few specific indications. Overall, the pattern of criteria used in paediatric studies was consistent with the overall analysis, with the exception of Mortality, which was used more frequently (17.9% versus 9%). All paediatric studies using Mortality were on LRTIs, however not all paediatric LRTI studies applied the Mortality criterion.

Most indication groups followed the same general pattern of using two criteria: primarily Prescription Change, followed by Clinical Condition. However, there were some variations, as acute otitis media (AOM) studies relied heavily on Clinical Condition, which was used in 76% of studies in this group, a proportion which is higher than in any other indication. UTI studies reported the highest use of Diagnostic Tests, accounting for 7 of the 12 studies that applied this criterion across all indications.

LRTI studies demonstrated a broader and more diverse application of criteria. This group had the highest use of the Mortality criterion (13 studies, 30%), and notably, Escalation of Care (24 studies, 55%) was used even more often than Clinical Condition (18 studies, 41%).

Finally, among the 31 studies conducted in low and middle income countries (LMICs), Clinical Condition was the most frequently used ATF criterion (26/31, 83.9% versus 104/194, 54% in the full dataset), followed by Prescription Change (17/31, 54.8% versus 151/194, 78%), Mortality (11/31, 35.5% versus 18/194, 9%), Escalation of Care (7/31, 22.6% versus 79/194, 41%), and Diagnostic Tests (1/31, 3.2% versus 12/194, 6%).

Time window analysis

Duration

The upper bounds of follow-up time windows used to assess ATF varied by indication group, following broadly expected patterns based on infection type (Figure 4a). Respiratory and otologic infections generally employed shorter follow-up periods, with most studies centring around a 14-day window and relatively narrow dispersion. These trends were mirrored in the paediatric-only subset, which is driven by a high proportion of AOM and LRTI cases, reporting a median follow-up of 14 days (mean 15.15 ± 9.52; range 2–45 days), with five outliers exceeding 23.75 days.

Each boxplot presents the median, IQR, and whiskers extending to 1.5 times the IQR. (b) Stacked bar graph representing the starting time-points across the included studies. Conversely, UTI and LRTI studies demonstrated significantly more dispersion. Despite a LRTI median of 14 days, both indications featured broad interquartile ranges and notable outliers reaching up to 56 and 90 days, respectively. Studies including multiple indications also demonstrated broader ranges, reflecting heterogeneous clinical trajectories. SSTI studies, shown separately (Figure S1), were notable for extreme outliers extending to 90 and 180 days despite most studies using short follow-up windows.

Overall, while follow-up durations for skin, respiratory, and ear infections typically clustered around 2 weeks, genitourinary and lower respiratory infections exhibited the widest dispersion in follow-up timing. These findings highlight infection types—particularly UTI, LRTI, and SSTI—where greater consistency in follow-up and response assessment timing may be most warranted. Further stratification by design or setting was not feasible due to small numbers per stratum.

Starting point

The most common follow-up anchor was the index antibiotic start date, used in 55 studies (28%). Other frequent anchors included the index clinical encounter (28 studies, 14%) and the antibiotic prescription date (25 studies, 13%). Less common choices were the index diagnosis date (22 studies, 11%) and completion of the antibiotic course (18 studies, 9%). Fewer studies used the study enrolment date (16 studies, 8%), multiple starting points (15 studies, 8%), or did not specify an anchor (8 studies, 4%). Anchoring to the antibiotic dispensing date (4 studies, 2%) or hospital discharge (3 studies, 2%) was rare (Figure 4b).

Stratification by study design

Use of antibiotic ATF criteria varied by study design (Table S6). RCTs mostly used Clinical Condition (87.9%), followed by Prescription Change (56.9%). RCTs were also the most study design to utilize Mortality as a criterion at 17.2%. In contrast, retrospective cohort studies relied heavily on Prescription Change (89.3%) and Escalation of Care (56.3%), while use of Clinical Condition was notably lower (29.1%). Prospective cohort studies favoured Clinical Condition (75.0%) and Prescription Change (71.4%), with use of Escalation of Care at 32.1%.

Stratification by risk of bias

The number of ATF criteria used showed modest variation by risk of bias (Table S7). Two criteria were the most common approach, used in 42.1% of low-risk, 50.0% of unclear-risk, and 54.5% of high-risk studies. High-risk studies more often used a single criterion (40.9%) than low-risk (31.7%) or unclear-risk studies (30.4%). Low-risk studies were more likely to use three or more criteria (26.2%) than unclear-risk (19.6%) or high-risk studies (4.5%).

Other outcome definitions

In addition to ATF, several studies included in this review reported outcome definitions describing treatment success, improvement, cure, or recovery. These outcomes were variably defined and, in some cases, conceptualized as binary (e.g. success versus failure), while in others they were presented along a multi-level classification (e.g. success, partial improvement, failure). All extracted definitions of these outcomes are detailed in the Table S8.

Discussion

This systematic review is the first to catalogue, dissect and compare every published definition of ATF used in common infections in ambulatory care research since 1996. Building on the work of Neill et al.8 this review expands the criteria of ATF from three core components to a comprehensive six-part taxonomy. In addition to the established criteria of Clinical Condition, Prescription Change, and Escalation of Care, our analysis introduces Mortality as a main criterion.8 It also identifies a complementary but potentially relevant criterion, Diagnostic Tests, and a catch-all Residual Category, providing a more complete representation of how ATF is operationalized in the literature.

Through detailed content analysis, this review offers greater granularity by identifying 25 distinct sub-criteria, enabling more precise characterization of failure definitions than previously possible. By mapping these criteria across infection types, study designs, and risk-of-bias levels, we provide a more interpretable cross-sectional view.

With respect to timing and duration, this review advances prior work through a more systematic and analytical examination of follow-up windows. Whereas Neill et al.8 primarily described variability qualitatively, we provide statistical mapping and visualization of time windows, including medians, interquartile ranges, and outliers across indications. Indication-specific patterns were observed, with shorter follow-up windows commonly applied to skin, ear, and respiratory infections, and longer windows used for genitourinary and mixed indications. We further extend temporal analysis by explicitly distinguishing between the duration of follow-up windows and their starting points, revealing important operational nuances.

Our analysis shows that even within highly comparable studies where the same criteria were applied, investigators diverged in the sub-criteria chosen, and in the time-window start-points and durations, hampering direct comparison of outcomes, underscoring the absence of consensus. These observations substantiate long-standing calls for standardization of ATF definitions in order to enable cross-study comparability.7–9

Clarifying what ATF is and is not

We believe it is important to have clarity on what ATF stands for, and to keep it as distinct as possible from other types of treatment failure. A possible way forward may be to distinguish between several related but conceptually distinct outcomes: ATF, microbiological failure, serological failure, relapse, recurrence, and re-infection. ATF can be understood as the inability of an antibiotic to improve the patient’s condition during the course of treatment, reflecting the clinical trajectory most relevant to patients and providers. Microbiological failure reflects persistence of the causative pathogen despite therapy, which may or may not align with clinical status, as patients can feel well despite ongoing positive culture results or, conversely, feel unwell despite microbiological eradication. Similarly, in infections where serology is used, serological failure refers to titres that do not decline as expected after treatment, which like microbiological failure, may diverge from the clinical picture. Additionally, both microbiological and serological data are often lacking in ambulatory settings. Furthermore, according to our findings, whether relapse, recurrence and re-infection constitute distinct endpoints of ATF or fall under a broader composite outcome with ATF remains unresolved. At minimum, researchers should report these events separately, creating more room for clear interpretation of results. Each of these terms generally presumes that the patient initially experiences improvement or resolution of their condition, followed by a subsequent infectious episode with reappearance of symptoms or clinical signs. This distinction is particularly relevant in the context of ATF, where treatment effectiveness is typically evaluated against a specific pathogen that is currently causing an infectious episode. It is important not to conflate that with subsequent infectious episodes, as this might be caused by a change in the causative organism, or even a change in susceptibility within the same organism or strain. Coming to an expert consensus that clearly defines and separates these outcomes could reduce misclassification, support accurate interpretation of study results, and provide a more nuanced framework for future research and clinical guideline development. Heterogeneity

Our analysis confirmed earlier work by Neill et al.8 in demonstrating wide variation in ATF definitions and criteria.8 Notably, even among a small subset of highly comparable studies with a similar indication, setting, patient population, and risk of bias, substantial variation persisted in the choice of sub-criteria and follow-up time windows. In some cases, a single study can use multiple definitions, often distinguishing between ‘early’ and ‘late’ failure. Time windows generally reflected the natural history of the disease but were also variable. This has direct implications for outcome classification. Shorter windows may underestimate ATF by missing delayed cases, whereas longer windows risk overestimating failure by capturing new or unrelated infectious episodes. This inconsistency introduces differential misclassification across studies, limiting the validity of comparisons and pooled estimates. The divergent starting points used add yet another layer of complexity and risk overlooking early failure if the clock starts after antibiotic completion.

The taxonomy we produced also exposed areas of disagreement. Some authors included adverse drug reactions and C. difficile infections as indicators of ATF because they can prompt antibiotic discontinuation or change, while others excluded them or included only severe adverse reactions. Guidance from expert panels could help determine whether such events should be considered integral to ATF or classified as related but distinct outcomes.

Precision in language matters

Some commonly used terms such as ‘lack of improvement’ or ‘not resolved’ describe the absence of success without explicitly indicating treatment failure, making them difficult to interpret consistently. In contrast, terms like ‘worsening’ and ‘persistent’ more clearly describe negative clinical trajectories. Failure specific terms could also convey different clinical realities: ‘worsening’ often signals a more severe or deteriorating condition, whereas ‘persistent’ may indicate a milder but prolonged course. Standardizing the use of such clear and differentiated terms could improve clarity in both clinical practice and research reporting.

For the criterion Prescription Change, terminology was often imprecise, using labels such as ‘different’, ‘other’, or ‘second antibiotic’. These terms obscure whether the change reflected an addition, a switch, or a prolongation. This ambiguity reduces comparability between studies, as illustrated by Wolterink et al.52, who explicitly excluded prolongation from their ATF definition.52 Distinguishing between these terms could also provide a clearer view of the likely reason for the change. An addition might reflect the need for broader coverage; a switch could indicate intolerance, AEs, or a shift to target a different pathogen; and a prolongation may suggest slow and careful escalation. These differences could be associated with different clinical outcomes. For example, an addition or switch might be correlated with a more severe trajectory, whereas a prolongation could indicate a milder course. Recognizing these patterns can inform the refinement of treatment guidelines.

Validity of criteria and sub-criteria as indicators of ATF

Only one study counted ‘Increased dose’ as failure.50 Nevertheless, dose escalation is endorsed by the European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Disease (ESCMID) as part of stewardship-aligned optimization and warrants consideration as a formal sub-criterion.73

Moreover, not every prescription change signals genuine failure. Changing antibiotics based on culture-sensitivity results, or due to drug-interactions, or patient adherence preferences may illustrate sound clinical judgment rather than failed care. Future frameworks could recommend documenting the reason for antibiotic change in addition to the fact of change. In a similar fashion, Escalation of Care may not exclusively reflect ATF, as it can also be influenced by baseline patient complexity, comorbidities, social circumstances, and precautionary clinical decision-making.

Mortality is a clinically important outcome; however, its use as an indicator of ATF might be limited by substantial heterogeneity in definition and analytic approach. Most studies relied on unspecified rather than infection-attributable mortality, which is strongly influenced by baseline patient complexity, comorbidities, and competing risks. These factors increase the risk of misclassification when mortality is used as a proxy for ATF.

Similarly, Diagnostic Tests are highly context–and indication–dependent, and their inconsistent reporting limits their generalisability as indicators of ATF. Most included studies using diagnostic criteria focused on UTIs, and even within these, the application of sub-criteria varied across study designs. For non-UTI infections, stratifying by infection type or study design might clarify discrepancies, but the scarcity of data prevented meaningful analysis.

The complexity of the link between some criteria and sub-criteria with treatment failure underscores the need for expert consensus-based guidance to distinguish between true indicators of ATF and outcomes driven by study conduct.

Indication, context, and design-specific patterns

Escalation of Care and Mortality were most frequently reported in studies of LRTIs, reflecting the inherently greater severity of these conditions. As such, these two criteria may also serve as indicators of the severity of ATF. Diagnostic Tests were common in STI studies, where test-of-cure is more routine, and in UTI studies, where assessments such as pyuria were frequently reported. RCTs and prospective cohort studies tended to use Clinical Condition as an ATF criterion, likely because clinicians collecting data for these studies can document detailed clinical findings. In contrast, retrospective studies relied more often on Prescription Change, since prescription records are readily available in most electronic medical records, whereas clinical details are often not reported as extensively. Low-risk-of-bias studies more frequently combined three or more criteria, suggesting that the use of multiple criteria might correlate with higher methodological rigour.

The predominance of studies from high-income countries limits the transferability of this taxonomy to LMICs and other resource-limited settings. In an exploratory review of the LMIC subset, ATF definitions appeared to rely more often on clinically observable deterioration and mortality, while diagnostic-test-based criteria were uncommon and escalation-of-care criteria were used less frequently than in the full dataset. This may reflect more limited access to diagnostics, referral systems, and higher levels of care, as well as differences in documentation and prescribing pathways. In such contexts, prescription change may also be influenced by non-clinical factors, including drug availability, cost, or access to follow-up care, and may not automatically be interpreted as evidence of treatment failure. Similarly, treatment discontinuation or presentation to care after self-medication or pharmacist-supplied antibiotics may reflect delayed access or fragmented care pathways rather than ATF per se. The LMIC subset was relatively small and heterogeneous; however, these considerations highlight the need for future consensus-building efforts to include experts from LMIC settings so that ATF definitions remain clinically meaningful and applicable across diverse health-system contexts.

Implications for research and practice

The documented variability in ATF definitions undermines evidence synthesis, scientific progress, and AMS initiatives. Through this review, we identified substantial variation in both the criteria and timing used to define and measure ATF, even among otherwise comparable studies. A key driver of this variability appears to be the lack of agreement on the scope of ATF itself. This includes uncertainty around the infection episode (e.g. whether ATF refers only to the index episode or also includes relapse, recurrence, or re-infection) and the nature of failure. Failure may reflect ineffectiveness—arising from pathogen, patient, or clinician factors—but may also include intolerance, such as AEs, toxicity, or CDI.

Clarifying the intended outcome is equally important. ATF could be defined as the inability of antibiotic therapy to achieve the intended clinical improvement or resolution of infection, due to either ineffectiveness or intolerability, leading to treatment modification, discontinuation, or escalation. Emphasizing clinical response, rather than laboratory or microbiological outcomes, helps distinguish ATF from serological or microbiological failure, which may not reflect the patient’s clinical condition.

By structuring this variability into a coherent taxonomy and dismantling ATF definitions into discrete components—such as scope, terminology, criteria and sub-criteria, timing, and interpretation—our synthesis provides a practical foundation for standardization and future consensus development. Rather than mandating a single definition, we advocate for the development of a consensus-driven checklist or decision-tree to guide consistent reporting while allowing flexibility across contexts (Table 2).

A multidisciplinary consensus process, such as a modified Delphi involving clinicians, microbiologists, pharmacologists, epidemiologists, and patient representatives, represents a logical next step.74,75 Establishing the scope of ATF is foundational, and would facilitate consensus on the criteria and timing. Through this expert-driven agreement, the identified criteria and sub-criteria can be evaluated and prioritized based on the scope, enabling the development of harmonized and transparent recommendations. Given that follow-up timing is likely to depend on factors such as indication, setting, study design, antibiotic choice, and patient population, consensus efforts should also focus on identifying the key determinants that guide appropriate timing and duration of ATF assessment. Together, such practical recommendations would enhance comparability across studies, facilitate meta-analysis of ATF outcomes, strengthen its utility as a metric in AMS, and provide additional guidance for clinicians on how and when to determine when an antibiotic treatment has failed.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this review include an extensive protocol-driven search across five databases, over a broad range of common indications, since 1996, using duplicate screening, including non-English studies and employing a mixed inductive-deductive coding strategy. Beyond establishing a new taxonomy, this review highlights several important conceptual ambiguities in the field. First, we distinguish clinical ATF from microbiological and serological failure, emphasizing that laboratory markers do not necessarily reflect patients’ clinical outcomes. Second, we underscore the need to differentiate true treatment failure from relapse, re-infection, and recurrence, which may reflect distinct clinical processes rather than failure of the initial therapy. Finally, we show that reported treatment outcomes span a spectrum from clinical success and partial improvement to failure, a distinction that is critical when interpreting and defining ATF.

Limitations stem from subjectivity in manual coding, as even with a structured codebook some definitions were inferred. Also, while we observed differences in criteria usage by study design and indication, we did not statistically test their significance (e.g. via Chi-square analysis or meta-regression) due to heterogeneity, which limits the strength of these comparisons. Because this review focused on the extraction and synthesis of qualitative definitions and criteria (rather than quantitative treatment outcomes or effect sizes), traditional concerns related to reporting bias, assessing certainty, and missing results were not directly applicable. However, we acknowledge that 14 out of the 194 included studies used implicit or poorly reported definitions of ATF, which may have limited our ability to capture all their relevant criteria. To mitigate this, we extracted definitions inferred from study design and outcome measures. Notably those studies did not introduce any novel criteria or sub-criteria that were not already present in the broader dataset.

Conclusion

We found substantial inconsistency in how studies define, apply, and time ATF in ambulatory care. This variability hampers meaningful comparison across studies and limits our ability to fully understand the real-world impact of antibiotic failure. Our synthesis may provide a useful starting point for a multidisciplinary expert panel to develop a practical, consensus-driven, and adaptable tool for future research. Moving toward standardized and transparent ATF definitions could strengthen comparative effectiveness research, inform clinical guidelines, support stewardship initiatives, and ultimately contribute to improving patient outcomes.
