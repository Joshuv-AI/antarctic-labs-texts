Barriers to the Development of Health Literacy in Iran's Health System: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Fateme Darabi, Arash Ziapour, Fatemeh Mohammadkhah, Murat Yıldırım, Nazila Nejhaddadgar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Despite the significance of health literacy, studies show the inadequacy of health literacy in the health system of Iran. Inadequate health literacy is considered a warning for service providers. It needs to be integrated into educational programs that are administered within health and medical centers.Objective:Therefore, the present study was conducted to explore the barriers to health literacy in Iran's health system.Methods:The present qualitative study was conducted in 2023 using a conventional content analysis. To this aim, 18 participants were selected among urban health center staff, 21 from rural health centers, 10 health volunteers, and 8 trainees (people participating in training classes) for interviews and group discussions. After the first interview, continuous data analysis began and continued to saturation level. The data were collected through semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions and were simultaneously analysed using a conventional qualitative analysis.Key Results:Two main categories of internal and external barriers were detected. The data analysis led to the extraction of four main subcategories: (1) personal barriers (trainee's inadequate competence, lack of physical or mental health, low motivation and commitment of health staff, beliefs, contextual barriers); (2) interpersonal barriers (health care providers' misunderstanding of correct training, malcommunication or miscommunication in training sessions, the trainer's limited skill, underestimated role of education in behavior change); (3) organizational barriers (high workload of health care staff, inadequate human resources, poor management and distrust in the health system, low organizational motivation); and (4) social barriers (uninterest in learning about health issues, issues related to the internet and cyberspace, lack of interpectoral cooperation).Conclusion:Considering the multidimensional nature of barriers to health literacy, it is recommended to make and implement a comprehensive plan to integrate efforts by health authorities and institutions, mass media, experts, decision-makers, policymakers, and service providers to remove barriers.

Health literacy is defined as a wide range of individuals' motivation, knowledge, and skills to access, understand, evaluate, and apply health information for making decisions about healthcare, prevention, and health promotion to improve quality of life (Sørensen et al., 2012). Health literacy is a major means of improving the state of health in society (World Health Organization, 2022). Health literacy, as a social and cognitive skill, has multiple dimensions such as accessing, appraising, understanding, and applying the acquired health knowledge and information (Adriaenssens et al., 2022).

In most educational programs, the focus is on increasing health-related knowledge and not necessarily on health literacy (Okan et al., 2023). Yet, these two are different. For instance, there is a difference between memorizing derivation formulas in mathematics and solving derivative problems (Schaeffer et al., 2023). Benjamin Bloom believed whenever we teach new material, learning begins at the lowest level of cognition (Walter & Ezra, 2015). The more we try to focus on material and engage learners (through Q &As), we reach the more advanced levels of education, namely understanding and applying the skills. There will be also more chances for learners to fully understand and show the desired behavior (Walter & Ezra, 2015).

Health knowledge is the ability to retrieve, recognize, and remember information that has been memorized, which in most cases does not lead to stable behavior, most training provided in the health system stops at this stage while health literacy is a cognitive skill that determines people's use information to improve health (Rudd et al., 2023). Based on the results of studies, people with higher health literacy have more knowledge of health, preventive measures, and screening (Fernandez et al., 2016) and, thus, have more medication adherence and better general health (Lee et al., 2017). In fact, health literacy acts as social vaccination, which can increase the massive safety of society and prevent the exacerbation of health problems (Okan et al., 2023).

Most training and information in the health system are provided in written form such as pamphlets, posters, and educational booklets, which require sufficient skill in reading, user evaluation, and design. However, the majority of the middle class does not have enough skills, which can lead to the ineffectiveness of educational programs (Choukou et al., 2022). A study conducted by Nasiri et al. (2021) showed that the most important barriers to health literacy are gender and lack of access to the necessary information. Also, Andrade et al. in their study developed a health literacy promotion index for the appropriateness of materials for training (Andrade et al., 2017). It includes simple language, clear purpose, appropriate graphics, active participation of the target population, skill-based learning, appropriateness with the target population, how to use a tool or perform a skill, behavior details, evaluation, and observation methods. (Andrade et al., 2017).

Considering the above-mentioned cases and the importance of implementing health behaviors, it is necessary to identify barriers to health literacy education in the health system. Because inadequate health literacy is considered a warning for service providers, it is necessary to pay more attention to it in educational programs provided in health centers. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to explore barriers to health literacy in Iran's health system.

Methods

Study Design and Data Collection

In this qualitative study, a conventional content analysis was used to cover the period of June 2023 to August 2023 in the Ardabil province of Iran (Ardabil is an ancient city in northwestern Iran and the capital of Ardabil Province). Among the participants, 18 were employees of urban health centers (as a trainer); 21 from rural health centers (as a trainer); 10 were health volunteers (as a trainee), and 8 were participants in training sessions (as a trainee), Interviews were conducted in health centers. They were selected to participate in an interview and group discussion. The design and findings of this study were reported based on the Consolidated Criteria for Qualitative Research Reporting checklist. The inclusion criteria were at least 6 months of work experience as a caregiver and health volunteer, willingness to participate in the interviews and group discussions, and ability to express experiences, opinions, and views. The exclusion criteria were unwillingness to continue participation in the study and no consent to record interviews and group discussions. To select the participants, a purposive sampling method was used with maximum variety. Different interviewees were selected from all levels of the health system and different were included in different areas receiving health care services from the university.

Data Collection and Procedure

To collect data, semi-structured interviews and focused group discussions with open-ended questions were used according to the main research question. Focal group discussion sessions and interviews were conducted by the corresponding author (the third author). At the beginning of each session, the researcher (N.N.) was introduced and explained the objectives of the study. In this phase of the study, to ensure accurate and complete recording of sessions, interviews were recorded with the participant's consent. The duration of each interview and group discussion ranged between 20 and 46 minutes. The sessions continued until data saturation. (After 5 group discussion sessions, the data were saturated.)

As for group discussions, at the beginning of each session, the participants introduced themselves. As all participants agreed, there was a division of labor. Next, the necessary explanations were provided about what every participant was supposed to do. The researcher (N.N.) was present in the sessions as facilitators and raised general questions to enter the discussion. Also, before the interview, the participants were told about the content and purpose of the study. They were also assured of the confidentiality of the information they provided. If they agreed to take part in the study, they signed an informed consent form and allowed the recording of content. No one was present in the interview except for the main participants of the study. No interview was repeated. The content of the interview was demographic information about participants (e.g., sex, age, education level, and work experience). A guideline was developed before conducting the interview. To develop the guideline for interview questions, all authors attended two discussion sessions, which began with a general and open-ended question, “What prevents the improvement of trainees' health literacy?” Then, based on the content, some further probes were asked to clarify previous questions and guide the interview process to achieve the purpose of the study. If the content was unclear, the participants were asked to explain more and provide examples. In the interview sessions, after asking about demographic information, the type of questions and the method of asking questions were similar to the focal group discussion (Table 1).

Data Analysis

The data categorization and analysis were done in MAXQDA-2018. For analysis, Graneheim and Lundman's approach (Graneheim, 2017) was followed by the corresponding author (N.N.). In the first phase, the researcher (N.N.) typed the interviews in Microsoft Word immediately after the interview on the same day the interview was held with the help of the coauthor. In the second phase, the transcript of each interview was carefully read three times by the researchers (N.N.) to get a general idea of the content. In the third phase, all transcripts were read line by line and word by word with great care and patience to extract the primary codes. In the fourth phase, codes that were similar in meaning and concept were placed in one category and their relationship was explored. Then the codes and categories were grouped as main categories, which were conceptually more comprehensive and summarized, and the themes were extracted. Finally, in a joint meeting, the entire process of data analysis was reviewed and the opinions of all authors were used.

Rigor

The pretest of a semi-structured interview guide was provided at Ardabil University of Medical Sciences. The pretest ensured that ambiguous questions were modified to ensure readability and clarity to respondents. To ensure a deep understanding of the findings, the researchers (F.D., N.N.) had prolonged interactions with health care workers and recipients of health services. Member check was used to validate data. Transcription and coding of data were done by the research team (F.D., N.N.) to ensure that challenges and expectations were reported correctly. The researchers (F.D., N.N.) met health care providers to find out if the provided themes adequately represented their opinions or not. The authors' background as qualitative researchers further contributed to the increased rigor of qualitative research.

Trustworthiness

Guba and Lincoln's criteria (8) were used to increase the trustworthiness of the findings. To increase credibility, the researchers selected participants of a maximum variety of demographic characteristics to comply with the principle of maximum variety and representativeness in sampling. The data analysis was explained to a panel of experts in qualitative research and corrections were made accordingly. Also, to ensure dependability, parts of the findings were provided to participants to approve. To increase confirmability, all authors participated in the analysis and coding process and expressed their opinions freely by attending group meetings. In addition, the research findings were sent to three prominent researchers (A.Z., F.M., M.Y.) in qualitative research to approve the steps of conducting research, analyzing data, and coming up with findings. For transferability, while describing the complete research procedure, different methods (face-to-face or visual) were used for data collection. Direct quotes from participants were used in large sizes. Also, the findings were provided to 5 individuals relatively similar to the main participants, but they were excluded from the main study. They also confirmed that they had similar experiences to the present participants. The present research was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee of Ardabil University of Medical Sciences.

Ethical Considerations

Before the study was conducted, a code of ethics (IR. ARUMS.REC 1395.402.269) was obtained from the Ardabil University of Medical Sciences. To adhere to research ethics, the interview was conducted with the participants' full consent and knowledge of the objectives of the study. The participants were assured of the confidentiality of the information they provided. Attempts were made not to distort the participants' utterances and use the same accounts in writing research findings. Also, the interview was conducted one-by-one, so that the participants could express their lived experiences in peace and confidentiality. To preserve confidentiality, a code was used to introduce each participant. After the interview, the transcript was returned to the interviewee to confirm the accuracy of the content and express their satisfaction with publishing the results.

Categories, Subcategories, and Codes

Internal Barriers

Personal barriers. This theme included the category trainee's inadequate competence, lack of physical or mental health, low motivation and commitment of health staff, beliefs, and contextual barriers.

Trainee's inadequate competence. From the participants' point of view, one barrier to the formation of health literacy is the trainee's inadequate competence. Issues such as low education, lacking skills to participate in face-to-face or group training sessions, low self-efficacy, absence in training sessions, and asking questions cause them not to have enough competence to understand and analyze the content.

Participant 2: “Our health care provider uses technical words in the training that I hardly understand. Also, I'm too embarrassed to ask questions, which prevents me from understanding most of the training content” (19-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 17: “In training sessions, the recipients are not interested in listening to educational materials. It seems that they go to health centers for fun or just as a get-together rather than really wanting to learn something” (47-year-old male service recipient).

Participant 31: “When I attend classes, I feel that I will not learn anything” (57-year-old female service recipient).

Lack of physical or mental health. From the participants' point of view, lack of physical and mental health hinders concentration in training sessions and consequently, the content presented is not well understood. The participants mentioned such issues as age conditions, family concerns, stress and depression, and physical problems.

Participant 20: “We cannot apply the training we receive from the health care workers because we are not physically in a condition where these issues are a priority in our lives. Most of us are sick or not in the mood when we go to health centers. We don't have the time and the education to take care of ourselves” (32-year-old female service recipient).

Low motivation and commitment of health staff. From the participants' point of view, low motivational factor is a great barrier that prevents people from receiving and acting on educational materials.

Participant 1: “Learning health-related topics does not involve any prestige. For example, if we learn about the economy or the market and talk to people or friends, people will consider us more important and pay attention to us more than health topics” (32-year-old male service recipient).

Participant 6: “Training sessions have no attraction except hearing the redundant content on television, radio, and cyberspace. I have diabetes, I just want to take my medicine and go back” (37-year-old male service recipient).

Participant 14: “In these sessions, long speeches are made regardless of the fact that we are bored. At the end of the session, it is not clear what we should do. I think the organizers of sessions themselves do not value these meetings” (41-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 11: “We attend these classes out of pure interest and because we really hope to learn something and be able to use it in life and teach others too. Yet, I don't think anyone cares that we come and participate” (59-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 47: “We seek for a social identity by attending these classes, but what we want just falls on deaf ears” (22-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 28: “We would like to have at least a celebration or entertainment program and a tour. We would like to be honored for our voluntary work so that our words will influence our family and friends” (59-year-old male service recipient).

Beliefs. From the participants' point of view, some beliefs are a big barrier to health literacy, such as disbelief in the role of education in improving quality of life. In other words, people did not believe in the significant role of education in life, and their traditional views were better. They knew about the training of health care workers.

Participant 8: “I don't believe in what health care workers often say. I think what our grandparents advised was more effective and affordable” (36-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 24: “My grandmother used to say if a child has diarrhea, he should eat turmeric, but now they say he is advised to take medicine. Turmeric is better than medicine. It is cheaper, and it works faster. The health care workers don't ask us whether we believe in it or not! We really want to be accepted” (28-year-old female service recipient).

Contextual barriers. From the participants' point of view, some contextual barriers in personal life or personality traits act as barriers to health literacy. Factors such as family problems, morals, personality traits, educational model, intelligence, and talent are among them.

Participant 15: “I don't believe in education and attending classes. I know everything myself. Anything I need to know about can be found on the internet” (21-year-old male service recipient).

Participant 16: “During the pandemic, health care workers kept advising to get the vaccine. I read on the internet that these vaccines were not approved and they only sought to have them tested on us. I myself did not get the shot, and now I am healthy. People themselves are adequately aware” (59-year-old male service recipient).

Participant 42: “I think traditional or herbal medicine is much better than the training they provide us with. At least in traditional medicine, they tell us exactly what to do or what to eat” (34-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 9: “Most training classes are held before noon and people are either at work before noon or have another job to do and don't have time to attend classes. We can't force people to attend these classes” (36-year-old male service recipient).

Participant 19: “In most cases, people don't want to attend these sessions because of the cost and the distance between their home and health centers” (19-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 56: “Most people prefer to get information from informal sources such as family, friends, cyberspace, and social media” (37-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 45: “People don't trust what the officials say, and they mostly acquire the information they need in parks and get-togethers” (26-year-old male service recipient).

External Barriers

Interpersonal barriers. Interpersonal barriers are those that occur in the interaction of trainees, trainers, and other people. This theme included the categories of health care providers' misunderstanding of correct training, malcommunication or miscommunication in training sessions, the trainer's limited skill, underestimated role of education in behavior change

Health care providers' misunderstanding of correct training. Health system officials do not have a proper understanding of the role of education in improving health literacy and improving people's quality of life. From the point of view of health system officials, education is equal to health literacy and behavior improvement, while this view is misleading because from the perspective of most people, health and prevention are not a priority.

Participant 30: “Authorities do not have a correct understanding of the role of training, they only emphasize the number of training sessions” (26-year-old male health worker).

Participant 29: “In the health system, training is repetitive and we are forced to proceed with the Ministry of Health's priority, not the needs of the audience” (49-year-old male health worker).

Malcommunication or miscommunication in training sessions. From the trainees' point of view, problems in communication are a barrier to the development of health literacy. There were cases such as lack of communication between health care providers and trainees, and lack of communication between trainees.

Participant 35: “I'm not interested in training and meeting where I can't talk. In most training sessions, we, the audience, do not talk at all and just listen” (31-year-old male health worker).

Trainer's limited skill. The participants believed that the health staff provided the training as their duty and did not know the needs of the audience.

Participant 40: “When we participate in class, the teacher chooses and teaches a topic as she/he likes. We may be uninterested in most topics” (44-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 50: “The quantity of material covered is large. For example, sometimes they give a book and tell you to read it and talk about it next session” (51-year-old male service recipient).

Participant 27: “In most training, most concepts are not familiar to us and we cannot understand them. Since there is no possibility of talking and interaction, we cannot analyze the content. Finally, we cannot use them in everyday life” (42-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 10: “When training is given, in my opinion, understandable and accessible teaching aids should be used” (29-year-old male health worker).

Participant 48: “Sometimes, the health staff tells us to prepare and install a special application on mobile phone, yet this application requires money, and we have to get help from other people to prepare and install it” (36-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 34: “In the training session, the health staff told us to install the weight loss diet application but did not teach us how to use it at all” (27-year-old female service recipient).

Underestimated role of education in behavior change. The low perceived importance of the role of education in comprehensive health centers is an important factor for the lack of health literacy.

Participant 5: “When the doctor does not follow the training, usually the health staff do not follow the issue” (30-year-old male health worker).

Participant 33: “Basically, education is either the last option or an option only for homework, and even if it is done, it is never in line with improving the trainees' health literacy” (35-year-old female health worker).

Participant 39: “If education and its role in monitoring and supervising officials were prioritized, at least they would be monitored closely” (31-year-old female health worker).

Participant 43: “Supervisors do not even ask the teacher for a lesson plan. The only question is; do you teach? How many people did you teach at last?” (45-year-old male health worker).

Participant 23: “If the officials cared about education, I would also care about the teacher and it would be important to me” (25-year-old male health worker).

Participant 44: “If someone cared about training, the trainer would teach me the necessary skills. Throughout the year, they hold various workshops, yet most of them are repetitive” (55-year-old female health worker).

Participant 52: “During my service, I did not see a workshop on how to train and change people's behavior. This shows no one cares about training. It's just a pretension” (21-year-old female health worker).

Organizational barriers. Organizational theme refers to the barriers the health system creates to improve health literacy. This theme includes the high workload of health care staff, inadequate human resources, poor management and distrust in the health system, and low organizational motivation.

High workload of health care staff. As the participants perceived, the health staff do not care about educational programs and an important reason for this is their high and varied workload, which reduces the motivation of caregivers in education.

Participant 36: “The high workload in the health system and the large number of patients take away the possibility of effective training. We have to visit patients. The number of patients in each work shift is really high” (35-year-old female health worker).

Participant 53: “It is impossible to visit and teach both. What the system expects us to do is practically unfulfilled” (39-year-old male health worker).

Inadequate human resources. As perceived by the participants, the health care empowerment program was not desirable in terms of training the people, and the empowerment training programs were mostly theoretical and lacked any training on skills.

Participant 37: “The trainer of the workshop should hold this class to motivate the promotion of health literacy. Most of the time, the teachers themselves do not have mastery or belief in what they teach” (29-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 21: “Health care workers don't speak clearly because they don't have control over what they teach, maybe they don't feel committed to people's health” (56-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 13: “I think the health staff do not have a good command of what they teach. They do not allow us to ask questions. I think these classes should be practical or they should ask us to do something similar to a show so that people feel in control of the situation and have the right to choose and decide” (23-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 57: “It is easy to talk. The content is not upto-date and it's not interesting to us” (35-year-old male health worker).

Participant 54: “We like to continue communication with the health staff so as not to be embarrassed, but most of these caregivers change quickly. Every session, one person comes, says something, and leaves. We have turned into a machine!” (50-year-old female service recipient).

Poor management and distrust in the health system. From the participants' point of view, the poor and inefficient health system is a barrier to the promotion of health literacy. In the present study, participants considered issues such as the inappropriateness of training programs and mismatch between trainees' needs and the quality of the course, the lack of prioritization of health literacy, and prevention as the main factors.”

Participant 41: “When most of the expenses of the Ministry of Health are spent on building hospitals and providing medicine, it shows no one cares about prevention and health literacy” (29-year-old male health worker).

Participant 51: “Every money in the system is spent on treatment and medicine. Until now, they have provided the money and asked to provide a training course. When the officials go to the health centers to monitor the work, training is often the last priority” (55-year-old male health worker).

Participant 12: “Because the dominant point of view is treatment, perhaps attention is paid to education in conversations, but in reality, there is no specific and documented plan for this issue” (29-year-old female health worker).

Low organizational motivation. The system's lack of financial support for health staff, the furtherance of education, sharp differences in health and treatment workers in work conditions, and officials' inadequate attention to educational problems and requirements were among the reasons raised in this category.

Participant 4: “In most cases, due to ministerial policies, educational units are considered minor units, and there is no positive view of this unit and its performance. Its presence or absence in the system does not matter” (33-year-old female health worker).

Participant 38: “Most officials believe that there is no teaching. People should seek learning by themselves. There is no meaning in the health system as part of education” (27-year-old male health worker).

Social barriers. Social barriers are those created by society for the trainer and trainees. The categories are uninterested in learning about health issues, issues related to the internet and cyberspace, and lack of interpectoral cooperation.

Uninterested in learning about health issues. The participants believed that lacking interest in education and learning is an issue that hinders attendance and participation in educational matters.

Participant 32: “People are not patient enough to attend the training sessions. Probably, an important reason is time pressure and financial and family concerns” (46-year-old female health worker).

Participant 25: “For people, health is not a priority in life, and they don't take time to attend the training sessions. People are more interested in having fun or working, and they don't have time for education. Maybe education is not attractive to them” (51-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 26: “Economic issues have a great impact on training caregivers. They tell us to eat certain things or behave in a certain way. All these behaviors need money. They never suggest a good alternative. We are tired of the instructions that cannot be followed” (41-year-old female service recipient).

Participant 3: “Many social problems originate from culture. Many of the instructions that are provided to people are not followed because most of the behaviors are rooted in people's culture” (44-year-old female health worker).

Participant 49: “Change is hard and time-consuming. For example, in the training, we tell mothers that no foreign object should be placed in the navel of a newborn baby as it gradually dries up and falls off. Yet, they often think otherwise and believe that, for example, turmeric can help” (47-year-old female health worker).

Participant 7: “Cultural factors prevent people from taking our advice. These factors are ingrained in people's beliefs for years and it is not possible to change them overnight. We should give people time and explain hygienic behavior to them clearly and simply” (44-year-old male health worker).

Issues related to the internet and cyberspace. The participants stated that easy access to the cyberspace on smart-phones and computers is one reason why people do not actively attend the training sessions held in health centers.

Participant 18: “Easy access to published content, which often contains wrong information and misleads people, prevents people from participating and receiving information in health center meetings” (32-year-old male health worker).

Lack of intersectoral cooperation. The participants believed that organizations outside the Ministry of Health do not play a major role in increasing public health literacy, which is directly related to the lack of commitment of organizations to the health issue.

Participant 29: “To arrange for a main course needed by the general population on TV, we need a long and costly administrative process; otherwise, the TV organization will not cooperate” (48-year-old male health worker).

Participant 55: “A reliable source for public information is radio and television; otherwise, people get information either from online sources or friends and acquaintances. They can never be sure of the accuracy of information” (31-year-old female health worker).

Participant 46: “We suggested the health department hold training programs in a suitable place or a park because we were interested in learning and having fun together. We could also bring our children with us, but the health care provider said this is hard to do and requires pre-arrangement” (29-year-old female health worker).

Discussion

This study was conducted to examine the barriers to health literacy in the health care system of Iran. In this study, participants identified barriers such as individual, interpersonal, organizational, and social. Health literacy can be the first step in improving individuals' health and well-being (Nasiri et al., 2021). Generally, health literacy is defined as a broad range of knowledge and skills related to acquiring, processing, understanding, and using health information (Shum et al., 2018). The importance of health literacy in influencing health outcomes is well recognized and plays a significant role in individuals' decision-making regarding their health needs (Shum et al., 2018). Improving individuals' health literacy leads to outcomes such as increased potential for making informed decisions, reduced health-threatening risks, increased disease prevention, enhanced security, improved quality of life, and improved quality of care for individuals (Andrade et al., 2017; Kaphingst et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2017). Health literacy has gained the attention of policymakers due to its significant role in how individuals make decisions regarding community health-related issues and enhancing the quality of health and medical services (Paasche-Orlow et al., 2005).

Some barriers to health literacy can arise from within the individual, including inadequate competency of trainees, lack of physical or mental health, low motivation and commitment of health care workers, misconceptions, and contextual barriers. In the study by Vallis (2009) emotional, motivational, and behavioral factors were identified as individual barriers. Many of us are ready to engage in health-promoting behaviors in the short to medium term, but struggle to maintain these behaviors in the long run. Based on evidence, even small amounts of healthy behaviors, if sustained over the long term, can have significant positive effects on health. However, motivation is a complex issue, and health care providers can play an important role in helping individuals with chronic illnesses find meaningful motivation. The health care provider can assist the individual in finding appropriate motivation through questions. In the study by Muvuka et al. (2020), factors such as limited educational opportunities, racism, distrust in the health care system, and lack of suitable cultural information and health care services were shown to be barriers to health literacy (Muvuka et al., 2020).

In the study by Fort et al. (2013) financial and economic problems in patients with type 2 diabetes and hypertension were identified as barriers to self-management. In the study by McKenna et al. (2020), the need for better walking facilities and the need to pay attention to mental health were reported as barriers to health literacy. In the study by Mulligan et al. (2017), poor mental health due to cognitive issues and low mood was identified as a key barrier to diabetes management. Misconceptions were another individual barrier facing health literacy in participants. In one study, beliefs and convictions were identified as one of the barriers to accessing health care services (Sheybany Sadr et al., 2023). The results of the study by Hakari et al. (2011) showed that perceptions, beliefs, misconceptions, individual barriers, and cultural and religious factors are common reasons why some may not seek prenatal care services (Hakari et al., 2011). Some individuals, due to their belief limitations and convictions, lack the decision-making power for their health and the ability to overcome barriers to receiving care (Sheybany Sadr et al., 2023). It seems that educational programs aimed at changing individuals' beliefs and convictions and promoting cultural awareness could bring about appropriate changes for individuals. Considering that the present study gathered information from the perspectives of experts, health caregivers, and health system workers, it seems to address broader and deeper aspects of the topic. Nowadays, understanding and implementing empowerment strategies is a clear goal for improving community health. Health empowerment can be examined from both individual and social perspectives. Personal empowerment is a process through which individuals exert greater control over the decisions, lifestyles, and activities that affect their health, requiring that the facilities and necessities for this empowerment be provided at home, school, work, and in the community (McKenna et al., 2020). On the other hand, health literacy is an important empowerment strategy to increase individuals' quest for health information (Walter & Ezra, 2015). Enhancing the health literacy of the population is an essential need of society, requiring comprehensive education, awareness-raising, cultural and health development, and scientific planning to prevent significant human and financial losses for people and high costs for the national economy. Therefore, given the importance of individual barriers, it is suggested that educational interventions be designed and implemented to address these personal barriers and enhance the empowerment of experts, health caregivers, and health workers. Additionally, policymakers, planners, and health care staff need to be aware of this issue and consider it when designing and developing related programs and interventions.

Based on the results of the present study, interpersonal barriers were identified as one of the obstacles to health literacy, aligning with various studies on health literacy, including the studies by Aguirre Velasco et al. (2020), Nutbeam and Lloyd (2021), Moudatsou et al. (2020), and Freibott et al. (2022). Although not all interpersonal barriers were mentioned in different studies, referencing many of these barriers indicates the importance of the topic of interpersonal barriers. Therefore, it is recommended that all individuals in direct contact with health workers, caregivers, and health counselors be trained on factors such as motivation, quality improvement, and methods of monitoring and oversight, to adjust or eliminate interpersonal barriers. On the other hand, the health care system and the valuable role of health caregivers should be introduced to the community through various means, including mass media, social networks, and so on, and by addressing interpersonal barriers, an important step can be taken to enhance health education. Additionally, training community health workers to conduct a comprehensive assessment of social needs and how to screen for risk factors during the initial encounters with clients can help identify related issues and contribute to providing more comprehensive services as part of the care team (Freibott et al., 2022).

We identified significant interpersonal barriers that need to be addressed to help improve health literacy. Because similar cases to the current study have been limited in the world and especially in Iran, finding similar articles to compare the results was difficult. The research team searched for related articles as much as possible and used them in the discussion of the article, but there were instances in the research findings where no similar articles were found. The results of our study with the study by Nutbeam and Lloyd (2021) and Wang and Wang (2020) were consistent with the findings of the present study. In the study by Svendsen et al. (2020), low organizational motivation was identified as a barrier to health literacy. Although some of these studies did not specifically use the term organizational factors, the type of organizational barriers in them did not align with the present study, which seems to stem from the limited scope of studies focused on the service delivery level, and the other hand, due to cultural differences between countries and the structures of health service systems. Since many organizational problems in these studies are interconnected and reinforce each other, it seems necessary to address these root causes and holistically break the vicious cycles to solve organizational problems and barriers. For instance, issues like weak human resources and management, as well as low organizational motivation, create problems. Therefore, it is suggested that officials from the Ministry of Health, university leaders, and representatives of service providers convene meetings to discuss these issues and devise comprehensive interventions to mitigate and eliminate these barriers. On the other hand, it is essential for the public to feel the need for awareness and the ability to prevent risks and to have access to training from centers approved by the Ministry of Health, such as health houses and bases, as well as related media. Thus, to achieve effective educational centers for enhancing people's health literacy, overcoming these organizational barriers is essential (Nejhaddadgar & Darabi, 2023). Additionally, formulating policies or regulations that integrate organizational health literacy into the planning of health institutions is crucial to increasing health care personnel's awareness of health literacy and strengthening health literacy within health care institutions.

The findings of the study by Tzenios et al. (2019) and the study by Svendsen et al. (2020) identified people's lack of interest in learning about health topics as social barriers to health literacy. Additionally, in the study by Oh and Lee (2019) and the study by Bin Naeem and Kamel Boulos (2021), issues related to the internet and the virtual space were reported as social barriers to health literacy, which aligned with the results of our study. Enhancing health literacy requires the development of media and cultural literacy; however, these days, we see a lack of this prerequisite because many people do not have the necessary skills to make use of online resources and social networks, and no one is there to answer their questions. Due to low health literacy, individuals lack the skills needed to identify credible sources, information analysis, search for and obtain accurate content, and make appropriate decisions to solve problems and adopt suitable behaviors. This issue can be attributed to confusion in selecting the right messages and even having the correct understanding of those messages, along with a misconception that health literacy means obtaining and comprehending all complex information, which leads to a misjudgment of their knowledge in this area. Public health officials must create conditions that allow individuals and various groups in society, especially low-income sectors, to access the internet and work to overcome its barriers. They should assist people by teaching them the correct way to use internet resources and acquire health information from these sources. The results of the study by Mahmoudi and Taheri (2015) and the study by Saied Moallemi and Haghighi (2016) align with the present study. As the effectiveness of artificial intelligence (AI) in improving aspects of health care delivery becomes increasingly evident, there is a likelihood that AI will be integrated into routine clinical care in the near future. This promise has led to increasing focus and investment in AI medical programs by both government organizations and tech companies (Reddy et al., 2020).

Health literacy is not just a personal issue; a higher level of health literacy in communities brings social benefits that are not solely the responsibility of individuals. Governments and health systems need to provide transparent, accurate, appropriate, and accessible information to boost health literacy for various audiences. Those with social responsibilities in health must align their roles with the relevant authorities to enhance health literacy. Improving health literacy requires both individual and social investment and focusing on both areas will help elevate health literacy in society. Additionally, because many health literacy interventions are centered around the health system, more research is needed on community-based health literacy interventions (Logan et al., 2015). On the other hand, promoting health literacy requires participation at all levels of the community. It shouldn't just be seen as the responsibility of individuals, policymakers, or health care professionals. Instead, key stakeholders in this effort—including government organizations, health care providers, educational institutions, workplaces, and community groups—must collaborate with one another. This team approach is essential for improving health literacy (Koh et al., 2013; Rosenbaum et al., 2016).

Study Limitations

Several limitations for this study should be considered, including the nonparticipation of some participants in the focus group discussions, which was attempted to be addressed through proper scheduling and coordination with participants. The relatively small sample size of participants in the study is a significant limitation, and the results should be cautiously generalized. Therefore, practical studies involving larger populations and other ethnicities and communities regarding recommendations to overcome health literacy barriers are essential. Time constraints in accessing certain experts, caregivers, and health workers should also be considered.

Conclusion

This study provides an analytical breakdown of the perceptions of the study samples regarding the health literacy barriers present in Iran. The results indicate that the obstacles and issues related to health literacy are multifaceted at various levels (individual, interpersonal, organizational, societal). In other words, health literacy barriers are not merely general obstacles that prevent any significant efforts to improve quality. Health policymakers must work and plan to eliminate health literacy barriers in the community and intervene in the areas of individual, interpersonal, organizational, and social obstacles, providing the necessary facilities. Therefore, the development and implementation of a comprehensive health literacy document based on the specific conditions, context, and culture of Iran with the participation of officials, experts, and service providers is recommended.
