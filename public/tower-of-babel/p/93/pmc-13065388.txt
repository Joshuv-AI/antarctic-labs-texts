Daily turmeric and ginger beverage consumption attenuates physical menstrual cycle symptoms in sub-elite female footballers: a pilot study
Authors: Jasper van der Wolf-Ong, Connor Parker, Eleanor L Procter, Samantha J Abbott, Sophie Harries, Chloe Everton, John Hough, Ross Burbeary, Matthew Mayer, Daniel Martin, Loris A Juett, David J Clayton, Ian Varley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract[Purpose] Female footballers frequently experience menstrual cycle-related symptoms, often most severe during menstruation, compounding exercise-induced muscle soreness, and impairing performance and wellbeing. Turmeric and ginger have anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties and may alleviate menstrual symptoms and reduce exercise-induced muscle soreness, but have not been studied as a whole-food combined beverage. This study investigated the effects of daily consumption of a turmeric and ginger containing beverage (TGB) on menstrual cycle related symptoms, post-match soreness, and markers of inflammation.[Methods] Six sub-elite female footballers completed a six-month protocol involving a 99-day control period without TGB consumption (CON), followed by an 89-day intervention period with self-administered daily TGB consumption (TGB). Each TGB contained juice from 35 g turmeric root and 6 g ginger root, with a black pepper mix (BioMax Uptake Blend®). Menstrual cycle-related symptoms were self-reported daily during menstruation, while muscle soreness (leg-specific and whole-body) and plasma concentrations of creatine kinase (CK), C-reactive protein (CRP), and intestinal fatty acid-binding protein (I-FABP) were assessed immediately after five competitive matches during each period.[Results] Physical menstrual symptoms were reduced 10% during TGB compared with CON (P = 0.013). No differences between trials were observed for psychological menstrual symptoms (P = 0.209), post-match soreness (leg-specific: P = 0.388; whole-body: P = 0.633), CK (P = 0.303), CRP (P = 0.917), or I-FABP (P = 0.322).[Conclusion] This study provides preliminary evidence that a daily turmeric and ginger-containing beverage can reduce physical menstrual cycle-related symptoms in female footballers, providing scope for larger placebo-controlled trials to confirm efficacy.Keywords: curcumin, inflammation, recovery, female health, athletes, supplementation, whole foods, nutraceuticals, gut health

INTRODUCTION

Menstrual cycle-related symptoms are reported by up to 97% of female footballers, with severity often peaking during menstruation [1,2]. Around 40% of players report that the menstrual cycle disrupts training and competition performance, as well as the ability to work and study [1-5]. Despite this high prevalence, effective management strategies are limited. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as ibuprofen and naproxen, are commonly used [3,6], but regular use carries risks to gastrointestinal and renal health [7]. It is important to identify treatments, with a superior risk to benefit profile, that could support health and performance goals of female athletes.

Turmeric and ginger are two food sources that are rich in bioactive compounds—curcuminoids and gingerols, respectively—with anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties [8]. Experimental and clinical evidence supports their role in reducing menstrual symptoms in non-athletic populations. Supplementation with 100 mg of curcumin twice daily has been shown to reduce symptom severity in female university students [9], while 750 mg/day of ginger powder alleviated dysmenorrhea in adolescent girls [10]. These findings suggest potential utility, but whether female athletes could achieve similar benefits remains unknown. Exercise elicits transient increases in pro-inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species, followed by anti-inflammatory and adaptive responses that could alter the metabolism of curcumin and gingerol [11]. In addition, fluctuations in ovarian hormones across the menstrual cycle influence inflammatory activity and pain sensitivity, potentially modifying the response to these bioactive compounds [12].

There is also evidence that curcumin and gingerol can accelerate recovery from exercise-induced stressors [13-16]. Team sports such as football involve repeated high-intensity efforts, eccentric muscle contractions, and substantial metabolic and cardiovascular demands [17]. This causes considerable muscle damage, leading to muscle soreness, and systemic inflammation [18,19]. High-intensity, intermittent exercise also reduces gastrointestinal blood flow during exertion, which can affect gut permeability and gut health [19-21]. These stressors can impair subsequent performance, particularly during congested fixture schedules when recovery time is restricted. A growing body of evidence supports the potential of curcumin and gingerol supplementation to mitigate these effects. Studies in male and female footballers have shown that curcumin can reduce post-match soreness and/ or markers of inflammation, potentially accelerating muscle function recovery [14,22], and gingerol-containing preparations have also been associated with reductions in proinflammatory markers and subjective soreness [15,16]. These data suggest that such compounds could address two overlapping challenges for female athletes: managing menstrual cycle-related symptoms and musculoskeletal pain associated with football.

Much of the existing research has focused on isolated bioactive extracts, particularly curcumin. Extracts deliver a narrow spectrum of compounds, whereas whole turmeric contains numerous constituents beyond curcumin, including curcuminoid analogues, essential oils, diarylheptanoids, polysaccharides, proteins, fibres, and minerals, that may interact synergistically to influence health and performance outcomes [23]. A small number of studies have examined the effects of turmeric consumption in elite male footballers. One study found that footballers consuming two turmeric-containing beverages (each 60mL, contained juice from 35 g turmeric root) per day experienced reduced muscle soreness and lower concentrations of c-reactive protein (a marker of systemic inflammation), compared to non-turmeric consuming footballers [24]. Furthermore, a once-per-day turmeric, vitamin C and vitamin D containing beverage, reduced upper-respiratory tract illness symptoms, gastrointestinal distress, and a marker of gut damage (intestinal fatty acid binding protein) [25]. These findings suggest that turmeric in whole-food form may provide functional benefits for exercise recovery, though whether these effects extend to female athletes remains unknown.

Surveys consistently report a consumer preference for natural remedies, with herbal products often perceived as safer and more acceptable than pharmaceutical medications [26]. This preference is particularly relevant for athletes, who may be cautious about medicines or compounds in pill or powder form, due to concerns over side effects and anti-doping regulations [27]. Consequently, minimally processed, widely recognised whole foods, with established analgesic and anti-inflammatory properties, may represent an attractive alternative for managing common symptoms affecting female footballers.

Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to investigate the effects of consuming a turmeric and ginger containing beverage once per day on menstrual cycle-related symptoms during menstruation in female footballers. Additionally, secondary aims were to explore effects on subjective muscle soreness, markers of inflammation, and gut damage. It was hypothesised that daily consumption of a beverage containing turmeric and ginger would attenuate menstrual cycle-related symptoms, muscle soreness, inflammatory markers, and gut damage, compared to the control period when this beverage was not consumed. This was the first practice-based pilot trial examining a turmeric and ginger containing beverage consumption in female footballers, providing novel data on the practical application of this strategy for alleviating menstrual-cycle related symptoms.

METHODS

Participant Characteristics

Fourteen sub-elite female footballers, who competed in the third tier of English football during the 2023/24 season, participated in this study. Eight participants were withdrawn from the study due to reasons such as transfer from the club and lack of engagement with the menstrual cycle questionnaire, leaving 6 participants who completed the study (age: 26 ± 4 years, body mass: 71.4 ± 8.8 kg, height: 1.69 ± 0.05 m). To qualify for enrolment at the start of the study, participants needed to be regularly menstruating, not using hormonal contraception, outfield players, have no cardiovascular or gastrointestinal disease, and not regular consumers of turmeric supplements. All participants provided written consent. The Nottingham Trent University Human Invasive Ethics Committee (REF: 716) approved the study.

Study Design

The study was a non-randomised within-group intervention, conducted during the competitive season, starting with a control period (CON) of 99 days, during which participants were instructed to avoid consuming any products containing turmeric and ginger. This was followed by an intervention period (TGB) of 89 days, during which players consumed a commercially available raw turmeric and ginger 60 mL “shot” daily (Raw turmeric and ginger shot, The Turmeric Co., Cambridgeshire, UK). The duration of each study period was based on players competition schedule and enabled the capture of five competitive matches during each period. Each shot contained watermelon, pomegranate, pineapple, and lemon juice, as well as the juice from 35 g organic turmeric root and 6 g ginger. The shots also included the trademarked BioMax Uptake Blend® (black pepper mix; designed to increase the bioavailability of curcumin). Beverages were provided to participants in boxes of 28 by the club staff every 28 days and adherence with the previous 28 days were confirmed on collection. Participants self-administered the beverage at home and were asked to ideally consume it in the morning (although were permitted to consume it later in the day if they forgot). Prior to each match, participants verbally confirmed compliance over the previous 7-days. Additionally, participants reported their psychological and physical menstrual cycle-related symptoms via a daily questionnaire.

The CON period took place from September 2023 to December 2023 and included five competitive league matches. The TGB period occurred from January 2024 to April 2024, also including five competitive league matches. Subjective muscle soreness was assessed, and a capillary blood sample was collected within 30 minutes after each match.

Menstrual Cycle Measurements

Participants were asked to record which day of their menstrual cycle they were in on a daily basis and whether or not they were bleeding, so that their menstrual phase (menstruation) could be determined each cycle. Average menstrual cycle length was 29 ± 3 days, with an average of 2.6 menstrual cycles captured during CON and 2.9 captured during TGB. First menstruation occurred between 9-18 days after beginning TGB consumption.

To assess menstrual cycle-related symptoms, participants completed a daily menstrual cycle symptom questionnaire (adapted from Bruinvels et al. [28]). Participants rated the presence of 14 physical symptoms (Changes to/difficulty breathing, Nausea/Sickness/Vomiting, Constipation, Joint Pain/Muscle Cramps, Temperature Fluctuation (hot flashes), Disrupted Sleep, Diarrhea, Lower Back Pain, Water Retention, Bloating/Increased Gas, Stomach Cramps, Breast Pain/Tenderness, Muscle Soreness, Sleep Quality) and 6 psychological symptoms (Reduced co-ordination, Poor Concentration/Memory, Headaches/Migraines, Tiredness/Fatigue, Cravings/Increased Appetite, Mood Swings/Anxiety/Stress) over the previous 24 h on a scale of 0 to 3 (0—not present, 1—low, 2—moderate, 3—strong). Compliance with the questionnaire was 98% ± 2%.

Blood Collection and Analysis

After selected matches completed during the CON and TGB study periods, a fingertip blood sample was collected. Fingertip blood samples were collected into 300 μL EDTA collection tubes (Microvette, Sarstedt, Germany) within 30 minutes post-match. After collection, samples were stored on ice, before being centrifuged (13,000×g, 10 min, 4°C), the plasma aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C. The concentrations of creatine kinase (CK) and C-reactive protein (CRP) in plasma samples were analysed using an ABX Pentra C400 (Horiba Medical, Kyoto, Japan; [CK] CV: 1.7%; [CRP] CV: 0.6%). The concentrations of intestinal fatty acid binding protein (I-FABP) in plasma samples was determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Hycult Biotechnology, Uden, The Netherlands; CV: 1.7%).

Subjective Soreness Measurements

Paper-based, 100 mm visual analogue scales (VAS), which are valid and reproducible [29], were used to assess subjective whole-body and leg-specific soreness. These scales were anchored at 0 mm for “no pain” and at 100 mm for “as much pain as it could be.” Participants marked their level of soreness on the line, which was then measured with a ruler for quantification. Subjective soreness measurements were taken immediately after each match.

Global Positioning System (GPS)

The physical demands during both match play and training were tracked using a 10 Hz GPS system (S7, Vector, Catapult, Victoria, Australia), a tool validated and reliable for evaluating athlete movements [30]. Each athlete placed the GPS device between their shoulder blades, within a specially designed garment. Data from each GPS device was analysed after each training session or match, using commercial software (Open Field, Catapult, Victoria, Australia). The monitored performance metrics included total distance covered (m), distance covered at high speed ( > 5.5 m/s), distance at very high speed ( > 7.0 m/s), and the count of accelerations exceeding 0.5 m/s2 and decelerations below −0.5 m/s2 sustained for more than 0.5 seconds.

Statistics

Data were assessed for normality of distribution by performing a Shapiro-Wilk test. A paired t-test was conducted to assess differences between CON and TGB for parametric data, whereas a Wilcoxon signed-rank test was conducted to assess differences between CON and TGB for non-parametric data. Whole body and leg-specific soreness, as well as concentrations of CK, CRP and I-FABP were determined for participants who competed in at least one match in both CON and TGB (n = 6). For players that played in more than one match during CON or TGB, an average value for CON and an average value for TGB were calculated. Data between CON and TGB were deemed to be significantly different when P < 0.05. Unless stated otherwise, data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

DISCUSSION

The primary aim of this study was to examine the effects of consuming a once-daily beverage containing turmeric and ginger on menstrual cycle-related symptoms during menstruation, with secondary aims to assess effects on muscle soreness, markers of inflammation (CRP and CK), and a marker of gut damage (I-FABP). The main finding was that daily turmeric and ginger beverage consumption reduced physical menstrual cycle-related symptoms by 10%, but had no effect on psychological symptoms, post-match soreness, or markers of inflammation and gut damage. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effects of combined turmeric and ginger ingestion in female footballers, providing preliminary evidence suggesting this could be a viable nutritional strategy to alleviate physical menstrual cycle-related symptoms.

These findings align with previous studies in non-athlete populations, where both turmeric and ginger supplementation reduced the severity of physical menstrual cycle-related symptoms in female students [9,10]. Although the exact mechanisms are not fully understood, several biological pathways may contribute. Prostaglandins rise during menstruation, causing uterine contractions and vasoconstriction to help shed the uterine lining, which produces the cramping associated with primary dysmenorrhea [31,32]. Curcumin (found in turmeric), gingerols and shogaols (found in ginger) inhibit the enzyme cyclooxygenase (COX), limiting prostaglandin release and potentially reducing physical pain [33]. In addition, inflammation is implicated in the aetiology of menstrual symptoms [34]. Curcumin has been shown to inhibit NF-κB, a transcription factor regulating the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [35]. Curcumin, shogaols, and gingerols act as antioxidants that can neutralise reactive oxygen species, thereby reducing inflammation [36,37]. Evidence from cell models also suggests a synergistic anti-inflammatory effect when turmeric and ginger are combined [8], highlighting the value of testing these foods together in applied settings.

No effects were observed on psychological menstrual cycle-related symptoms, which contrasts with prior research reporting improvements in mood and behavioural symptoms with curcumin supplementation, in women with premenstrual syndrome. Discrepancies may reflect differences in participant characteristics, with the footballers in this study reporting relatively minor symptoms, whereas Fanaei et al. [9] recruited females with diagnosed premenstrual syndrome, who may be more responsive to the intervention.

No differences were observed for any individual menstrual cycle-related symptoms, indicating that the overall reduction in physical symptoms was driven by small, consistent improvements across several questionnaire items. The magnitude of this change (~10%) approaches commonly used thresholds for a meaningful change in symptom-based scales [38], suggesting that our observed reduction in physical symptom severity is small, but could be perceptible and beneficial to athletes. The absence of significant effects for individual symptoms likely reflects limited statistical power to detect small, item-level differences, which may also apply to overall psychological symptoms. Small sample sizes are a common limitation in athlete research, particularly in chronic nutritional intervention studies, due to challenges in recruitment and participant retention within high-performance environments. Whilst definitive conclusions cannot be drawn from small open-label studies, the applied context provides valuable insights for practitioners working with female athletes.

No effects of turmeric and ginger consumption were found on markers of soreness or inflammation. A previous study in elite male footballers found a similar turmeric-based beverage reduced post-match soreness and CRP [24], however, these differing results are likely due to when measurements were collected. Statistically lower CRP concentrations only emerged 64 h post-match in the previous study [24]. Participants in the current study (part-time athletes) were not required to attend the training facility every morning, which restricted our sample collection to immediately post-match in the current study. Delayed onset of muscle soreness increases during the first 24 h after exercise and typically peaks 24-72 h post-exercise [39], and as such, it is possible we missed any differences emerging later in the recovery period. Supporting this, Tanabe et al. [13], found curcumin supplementation (180 mg/day) was ineffective when consumed before exercise but improved recovery when consumed post-exercise, with positive effects on soreness and range of motion evident on 3- and 4-days post-exercise. Another study in female footballers found that curcumin supplementation (270 mg/day) for 2-weeks reduced fasted concentrations of interleukin-6, during a period of heavy training [22]. Together, these findings suggest that the benefits of turmeric may emerge later in the recovery period, which could not be captured in the current study. Nevertheless, this study is the first to explore turmeric and ginger in sub-elite female footballers, a population facing the dual stressors of exercise-induced muscle damage and menstrual symptoms, and therefore contributes novel applied evidence. Future studies should aim to recruit full-time female athletes and to take measurements for several days following match play.

Increased gut permeability is common after high intensity exercise, but can increase the risk of endotoxemia and impair recovery via decreased nutrient absorption [20]. A previous study found that a formulation of turmeric, vitamin C (1000 mg), and vitamin D (75 μg), reduced post-match I-FABP (a marker of gut permeability) concentrations [25], whereas the turmeric and ginger beverage in the current study had no effect. This discrepancy may relate to vitamin D, included in the previous formulation, which plays a key role in maintaining gut barrier integrity [40]. There is evidence that high-dose curcumin (500 mg/day) supplementation for 3-days can reduce I-FAPB after exercise [41], but the results of the current study imply that a higher dose of turmeric may be required to reach a curcumin intake capable of improving markers of gut permeability. Gingerol-enhanced ginger has been shown to improve intestinal barrier function in rodents [42], but evidence in humans is limited. Further research is required to determine whether turmeric and ginger, alone or in combination, can support gut integrity in athletes.

Dosing recommendations for turmeric remain a challenge, as the growing conditions and origin of turmeric inevitably influence curcumin concentration [43]. However, whole-food turmeric and ginger products may offer distinct advantages over isolated extracts. Whole-foods typically contain a broader spectrum of potentially synergistic bioactive compounds, and are generally perceived as safer and more acceptable than extracts and pharmaceuticals [23]. This is particularly relevant for athletes, who may avoid supplements due to concerns about side effects and inadvertent doping violations [27]. Minimally processed, widely recognised foods with established anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties may therefore represent a practical alternative for managing common health challenges, such as menstrual symptoms, in female footballers. By examining turmeric and ginger in their food form, this study provides novel, contextually relevant evidence that such products could be a practical nutritional strategy for female athletes.

This study has several limitations. The open-label, non-randomised design introduces potential bias, as participants were aware of the intervention. However, the three-month duration of both phases meant athletes completed multiple menstrual cycles per condition, reducing the likelihood of consistent reporting bias. Moreover, no placebo effect was evident for subjective reports for psychological menstrual cycle-related symptoms, or muscle soreness. While blinding with a suitable placebo would have strengthened the design, this is often impractical in professional athletes, who are cautious about supplement use due to concerns over side effects, doping regulations, and performance incentives. In this respect, the open-label design may provide greater ecological validity, reflecting how such products are typically consumed in real-world sport settings.

The duration of CON and TGB was uneven (99 vs. 89 days), which was due to capturing five competitive matches in each study period. Consequently, participants may have completed a different number of menstrual cycles during each condition, potentially reducing our ability to detect differences in specific physical and mental outcomes from the questionnaire. In addition, the non-randomised study design meant that the CON and TGB occurred at different times of the year, September to December for CON (primarily UK autumn) and January to April for TGB (primarily UK winter). Seasonal factors such as daily sunlight exposure, weather, and ambient temperature, alongside associated behavioural changes related to dietary and physical activity patterns, could have influenced results.

A further limitation was the small sample size, which reduced statistical power and generalisability. This is a common constraint in applied athlete studies, particularly in women’s football, where player access can be restricted by part-time status and highly congested schedules. These same constraints limited data collection to the immediate post-match period, preventing assessment of delayed recovery responses. Despite these limitations, the study provides rare data on an understudied population and offers valuable foundational evidence to support rigorous, placebo-controlled trials in the future. A key strength is that this is one of the first nutrition intervention studies conducted in sub-elite female footballers, providing novel, applied insight into an underrepresented group.

CONCLUSIONS

The findings from the present study suggest that daily consumption of a 60 mL turmeric and ginger containing beverage reduced subjective physical menstrual cycle-related symptoms during menstruation by 10% in sub-elite female footballers. However, there was no effect on psychological menstrual cycle symptoms, muscle soreness, markers of inflammation or a marker of gut damage. These findings should be considered preliminary given the open-label, non-randomised design and small sample size. Placebo-controlled randomised trials with larger cohorts are needed to confirm these effects and to causally determine whether turmeric and ginger can also support recovery and gut health in female athletes.
