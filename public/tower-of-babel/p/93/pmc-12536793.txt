Aza-peptide aldehydes and ketones: synthesis and evaluation as human 20S proteasome inhibitors
Authors: Thomas S Corrigan, Sarah E Border, Leilani M Lotti Diaz, Kayla Q Kasper, Abigail M Noonchester, Rasmiah Amer, Kayla S Kucway, Michael Fleisher, Joseph P Fernandez, Alex R Lovins, Ana K Serrano, Conor R Caffrey, Anthony J O’Donoghue, Don M Benson, Christopher M Hadad, Özlem Doğan Ekici
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsAza-peptide aldehydes and ketones were developed as a new class of peptidyl analogues to inhibit the human constitutive (c)20S proteasome as alternative therapeutics to treat multiple myeloma (MM).Material and methodsEleven new aza-peptide aldehydes and ketones were designed based on their preference to bind at the ß5 catalytic subunit of c20S proteasome with benzyloxycarbonyl(Cbz)-Leu-Leu-Leu (MG132-like) and morpholinyl(Mp)-Homophenylalanyl(HPh)-Leu-Phe-Leu (Carfilzomib-like) sequences, synthesized, structurally characterized and evaluated for their inhibitory potency in competitive kinetic assays in vitro. Additionally, cell viability assays and molecular modeling experiments were designed and performed in support.ResultsAza-peptide aldehydes and ketones generated inhibitory activity with IC50 values in the µM range when tested at the ß5 catalytic subunit of the human c20S proteasome. Compound 1 was the most potent compound with an IC50 value of 2.3 ± 1.5 µM. When tested for concentration-dependent killing of three multiple myeloma, one leukemic and two normal natural killer (NK) cell lines, two compounds generated mid-µM EC50 values only for the cancer cells after 48 h.ConclusionsOverall, aza-peptide aldehydes and ketones are a new class of selective human c20S proteasome inhibitors with the potential for further development as alternative therapeutics for multiple myeloma.KEYWORDS: Proteasome inhibitor, aza-peptide aldehydes, aza-peptide ketones, anticancer, multiple myeloma

Introduction

The ubiquitin-proteasome system is responsible for the homeostasis in eukaryotic cells. The 26S proteasome plays a central role in this system whereby it degrades the ubiquitinated proteins to recyclable oligopeptides [1]. The constitutive 26S proteasome (c26S) consists of a c20S core particle that is capped with two 19S regulatory domains. The c20S core particle is a 750 kDa threonine protease that comprises four stacked rings (α,ß,ß,α) in a C2 symmetrical barrel shape. Each α and ß ring is composed of seven distinct subunits of α (α1–7) and ß (ß1–7), respectively. The c20S catalytic core contains the three catalytic activities designated as caspase-like (C-L or ß1), trypsin-like (T-L or ß2), and chymotrypsin-like (CT-L or ß5) [2].

The c20S proteasome is overexpressed in tumor cells [3], particularly in certain cancers such as multiple myeloma and mantle cell lymphoma [4]. Inhibition of c20S causes apoptosis and is a validated strategy for the design of effective drugs to treat multiple myeloma [5]. Specifically, proteasome inhibitor blockbuster drugs, such as bortezomib, carfilzomib [6,7] and ixazomib, which were FDA-approved in 2003, 2012 and 2015, respectively, are the standard therapies. However, there are severe side effects with these drugs. For example, bortezomib causes peripheral neuropathy in up to 64% of newly diagnosed multiple-myeloma patients [8,9]. Carfilzomib is associated with adverse cardiovascular effects in up to 30% of the treated patients [10]. In addition, multi-drug resistance in various cancer cells has decreased the efficacy of both drugs [4]. Thus, the development of new c20S inhibitors to treat multiple myeloma is highly desirable.

Our group has previously reported aza-peptide aldehydes and ketones as a novel class of protease inhibitors [11]. These compounds inhibit the human c20S proteasome, caspases−3 and −6, as well as the asparaginyl endopeptidases (legumains) from the bloodfluke (Schistosoma mansoni) and the tick (Ixodes ricinus) with inhibition potencies in the mid µM IC50 range. Herein, we report the investigation of the various aspects of inhibition of the human c20S proteasome by aza-peptide aldehydes and ketones in more detail. To this end, we synthesized and evaluated 11 new proteasome-specific aza-peptide aldehydes and ketones with various aza-P1 residues. We show that they are low to mid-µM inhibitors of the c20S ß5 catalytic subunit and demonstrate concentration-dependent killing of various transformed and non-transformed cell lines in vitro.

Aza-peptide aldehydes and ketones are designed with the goal of developing selective, reversible inhibitors that can be tuned for the inhibition of specific proteases [11]. There are three main design features incorporated into this class of inhibitors. First, the amino acid side chains R1, R2 and R3 (Figure 1) are to be used to create selectivity by mimicking the “P” amino acid residues of the natural substrate for the targeted protease. The second design feature is the incorporation of an aza-peptide residue at the P1 position; wherein the typical α-CH found in a peptide chain is replaced with a nitrogen atom. This change in the design is aimed to deviate the resemblance of the inhibitors from natural peptides, thereby increasing metabolic stability and selectivity of the drugs (Figure 1, blue) [12]. Aza-peptides, such as aza-peptide epoxides, aza-peptide Michael acceptors, and aza-peptide O-acyl hydroxamates have been successfully implemented into irreversible protease inhibitors showing high selectivity for clan CD cysteine proteases such as caspases, legumain and gingipains [9–11,13–16]. Aza-peptide nitriles are members of another aza-peptide inhibitor class that was developed for proteases by Gütschow and colleagues [17,18]. They are effective and stable inhibitors for clan CA proteases, such as cathepsins L, S, and K.

The third design feature of the aza-peptide carbonyl inhibitors is the electrophilic carbonyl (aldehyde or ketone) reactive group or “warhead.” In our design of proteasome inhibitors, the warhead is a group strategically placed where the scissile bond of the natural substrate would reside and is intended to react with the enzyme’s catalytic-threonine (1Thr) residue. The electrophilic carbonyl warhead for the aza-peptide ketones and aldehydes is proposed to inhibit the proteasome by the formation of a reversible covalent bond with the active site nucleophilic 1Thr-O, thus competitively interrupting the natural catalytic cycle of the proteasome [11]. A similar interruption is shown to occur by means of the formation of a tetrahedral intermediate (Figure 1). We previously have determined the formation of a tetrahedral intermediate between our caspase-specific aza-peptide ketone inhibitor and caspase-3 by X-ray crystallography [11]. Here, we propose a similar mechanism of inhibition based on computational modeling study, with which we observe the proximity of the ß5 1Thr – O to the carbonyl carbon of the aza-peptide aldehyde or ketone warhead of our most potent compound, Compound 1.

For the human c20S proteasome, the aza-peptide carbonyl inhibitor design incorporates the tripeptide P3-P2-P1 backbone as the recognition element. The ideal tripeptide P3-P2-P1 is used to increase binding by the formation of favorable interactions with the corresponding S3-S2-S1 pockets within the ß5 active site of the human c20S proteasome. The S1 pocket of the proteasome β5 subunit is composed of aliphatic residues such as alanine, valine, and methionine amino acid residues which form favorable interactions with hydrophobic P1 residues [17,18]. The S2 pocket is a spacious, open cavity that can accommodate larger P2 residues, such as leucine or phenylalanine.19 This accommodation of larger groups in the S2 pocket can be used to induce selectivity of inhibitors for the proteasome over other proteases where the larger size of the P2 residue may prevent the inhibitor from fitting close to the active site [19]. The S3 pocket, like the S1 pocket, also forms favorable interactions with aliphatic P3 residues [18]. Based on these binding preferences of the β5 catalytic unit of the proteasome, a variety of aliphatic P1 aza-amino acid residues (AGly, AAla, ALeu and ANle) were included as the first choices for inhibitor synthesis (Figure 2, R1). The P2 amino acid (Figure 2, R2) was proposed to be a bulky Leu, or Phe to take advantage of the spacious S2 pocket. Finally, the P3 amino acid (Figure 2, R3) was chosen as a Leu residue. The P3 amino acid terminal nitrogen of the inhibitor was capped with a carboxybenzyl group to prevent undesired reactions of the free amine and to further interact on the N-terminal side. For tetrapeptidyl inhibitors, the N-terminal cap (N-cap) group was chosen as a carboxybenzyl or morpholino group, based on the carfilzomib recognition sequence [20].

Herein, we show that aza-peptide aldehydes and ketones are selective inhibitors with considerable activity against the human c20S proteasome.

Experimental

Chemistry

All chemical reagents were purchased from reputable suppliers (Sigma Aldrich, Fisher Scientific, Matrix Scientific, Acros Organics). Unless otherwise noted, all reagents were used without further purification or purified in accordance with procedures described in Purification of Common Laboratory Chemicals. Unless otherwise noted, all reactions were carried out in flame/oven-dried glassware under a nitrogen or argon atmosphere. Product purification by flash chromatography was carried out using normal phase 40–64 μm 60 Å silica gel from Sigma Aldrich or using the auto column system Isco CombiFlash RF+UV by Teledyne. Dichloromethane, tetrahydrofuran, N,N-dimethylformamide, diethyl ether, and pentane solvents were obtained from a solvent purification system (with activated alumina columns). Triethylamine and diisopropylethylamine were freshly distilled over CaH2 prior to use and stored under an argon atmosphere. NMR experiments were performed on Bruker 400, 700 or 850 MHz spectrometers. Chemical shifts are expressed in parts per million (δ, ppm) while coupling constant values (J) are given in Hertz (Hz). Residual solvent protons were used as internal standards: for 1H NMR spectra CDCl3 = 7.26 ppm, dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 (DMSO-d6) = 2.50 ppm and D2O = 4.79 ppm; CDCl3 and DMSO-d6 were purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories. High-resolution mass spectra were recorded on a Bruker MicroTOF II instrument with internal sodium formate calibrant under electrospray ionization (ESI) conditions. Hydrogenations were carried out using a Parr hydrogenation apparatus. Kinetic assays were performed on Molecular Devices SpectraMax i3 and Molecular Devices SpectraMax id5 instruments. MTS cell viability assay kit (CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay) was purchased from Promega, Madison WI. The cell lines MM1.S, RPMI-8226, U266 and NK92 were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) (Manassas, Virginia) with the following ATCC numbers: MM1.S (CRL-2974), RPMI-8226 (CCL-155), U266 (TIB-196), NK92 (CRL-2407). Blood samples were obtained from leukopacks of healthy donors from the American Red Cross (Columbus, Ohio) via the signed agreement between The Ohio State University (where Don Benson is the PI) and the American Red Cross for non-transfusable blood products. The agreement outlines that the distribution of research materials by the American Red Cross Biomedical Services (ARCBS) is governed by the ARCBS Institutional Review Board (IRB) in accordance with Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS) regulations at Title 45 CFR Part 46 and by Office of Human Research Protection (OHRP) guidance.

All general procedures are performed similar to previously described by our group and others, and presented in Supplemental Information [11,13,14].

Kinetic assays

Human c20S proteasome β5 catalytic subunit, human liver cathepsin B and bovine pancreas α-chymotrypsin kinetic assays were performed similar to previously described by our group and presented in Supplemental Information [11].

MTS cell viability assays

All 10 compounds were tested against three multiple myeloma cell lines (MM1.S, RPMI-8226, U266), one natural killer cancer cell line (NK92), and two primary, healthy donor natural killer cell-enriched samples. As described previously [21], 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(sulfophenyl)-2 H-tetrazolium (MTS) assays were conducted according to manufacturer’s instructions to assess cell viability at 48 h over a 1–200 µM range of inhibitor concentrations. In brief, cells were seeded into 96-well, flat-bottomed plates in 1 mL serum-free medium. After 48 h treatment, 20 µL of MTS was added. After 2 h, the optical density of cells at 495 nm was recorded.

Molecular docking – preparation of the receptor Model

Preparation of the receptor for subsequent molecular docking simulations was performed using the Schrödinger [22–24] suite of computational tools and the three-dimensional structure of the protein that was obtained from the crystal structure PDB ID: 5LEY [25], following the protocol as previously described [26]. The oprozomib ligand was severed from the catalytic residue and the ligand’s centroid was used to place the box in which ligands were docked. Further constraints were added, such that the reactive “warhead” (electrophilic carbonyl) was docked within 4 Å of the catalytic 1Thr residue. Ligands were prepared from SMILES strings using LigPrep [27] to ensure proper geometry and ionization states at physiological pH.

For each compound, multiple docking runs were performed using Schrodinger’s Glide to explore different binding poses. The top-ranked pose was selected based on the docking score and visual inspection of key interactions. The docking score (glide score) and other relevant energy terms, such as van der Waals interactions, electrostatic energies, and hydrogen bonding contributions, were recorded for further analysis. Additional modeling work was also performed with the Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) suite of programs [28] and Autodock Vina [29].

Results and discussion

Chemical synthesis

The general preparation of aza-peptide aldehydes and ketones is previously described [11]. For both aldehydes and ketones, this approach first involves the synthesis of the substituted hydrazide precursor from the peptide methyl ester [14]. To obtain aldehydes, the hydrazide precursor is coupled to oxoacetyl chloride (prepared in situ with thionyl chloride; Path A, Figure S1) or monoethyl fumarate followed with subsequent ozonolysis (Path B, Figure S1). For ketones, the coupling is performed with either methyl or benzyl pyruvic acid chlorides, respectively (Path C, Figure S1).

We have synthesized seven carboxybenzyl protected tripeptidyl analogues with various aza-P1 residues. For all seven analogues, this synthesis begins with an isobutyl chloroformate mediated coupling between the methyl ester of the P2 amino acid and the carboxybenzyl protected P3 amino acid as previously reported (Figure S1) [13].

From the P3-P2 dipeptide, the furnishing of the P1 aza-amino acids of the ALeu, ANle and AGly in quantitative yield proceeded by the addition of excess hydrazine to the methyl ester to furnish the corresponding substituted hydrazides (Figure S2). For AGly analogues, the hydrazide is directly taken to the final coupling step. For the ALeu and ANle analogues, isobutyraldehyde and butyraldehyde are used in the first step of a two-step reductive amination reaction to furnish the corresponding P1 side chains of ALeu and ANle, respectively. In the second step, subsequent reduction with sodium cyanoborohydride effectively installed the isobutyl side chain on the hydrazide to form the ALeu intermediate and the butyl side chain on the hydrazine for ANle in good yields (Figure S2).

The first attempt to synthesize the AAla analogue, which proceeded via reductive amination with formaldehyde resulted in a mixture of products. In an alternate synthetic route, methylhydrazine was first protected at the alkylated nitrogen to prevent the competing nucleophilic addition between the differentiated nitrogens and the carbonyl of the inhibitor P2 amino acid. The selective protection for more nucleophilic methylated nitrogen was carried out with di-tert-butyl dicarbonate to afford the desired 1-Boc-1-methylhydrazine with high selectivity and an isolated yield of 98% (Figure S3, Boc = t-butoxycarbonyl). Following the hydrolysis of methyl ester of Cbz-Leu-Leu-OMe, the resulting carboxylic acid Cbz-Leu-Leu-OH was coupled via EDC and HOBt to afford the Boc-protected AAla peptide (Figure S3). Finally, the deprotection of the Boc group with trifluoroacetic acid liberated the substituted hydrazide intermediate for the AAla final compounds.

The tetrapeptidyl Mp-HPh-Leu-Phe-ALeu-CHO inhibitor was prepared by coupling the aza-peptidyl intermediate to monoethyl fumarate, followed by ozonolysis. For the ketone series, we synthesized six methyl and one benzyl ketone analogues. For the final coupling step to the warheads, first pyruvic acid or phenylpyruvic acid were converted to the corresponding acid chloride intermediates via reaction with oxalyl chloride. Then, acyl substitution of the acid chlorides with the aza-peptide hydrazide intermediates yielded the final aza-peptide ketones.

In vitro inhibition

We synthesized a total of 11 new aza-peptide aldehydes and ketones targeting the ß5 active site of the c20S proteasome (Table 1). It has been shown that the ß5 active site prefers amino acids with small aliphatic side chains at the P1 and P3 positions [17,18]. To our knowledge, our group is the first to report this warhead being specifically designed for the human c20S proteasome [11]. In this study, we focused on making a series of MG132-like tripeptidyl compounds while we varied the aza-P1 residue as ALeu, ANle, AAla or AGly to understand the unique fitting of the aza-peptide carbonyl warhead in the active site. We also synthesized three carfilzomib-like tetrapeptidyl analogues (all with ALeu at their P1 position) to improve potency (Table 1). To better understand the binding and the inhibition of these compounds, we also computationally modeled the ß5 active site and ran a docking study, which will be detailed below in the molecular modeling section.

Previously, we reported three aza-peptide carbonyls with the sequence of Cbz-Leu-Leu-ALeu that generated IC50 values of 9–14 µM against c20S proteasome β5 [11]. Here, the three tetrapeptidyl compounds, 7, 8, and 9, were the most potent with IC50 values of 5.4 μM, 2.6 μM and 9.9 μM, respectively against c20S β5.

The compounds in the tripeptidyl Cbz-Leu-Leu-AAla series performed relatively poorly compared to the others. Compound 4 is a combination of aromatic groups at the P2 and P1´positions and was the weakest in the Cbz-Leu-Phe-ALeu series (IC50 = 91.7 µM). We designed one inhibitor, compound 10, with the ANle at the P1 position as a potential human immunoproteasome β5(i) active site-directed inhibitor. When tested with c20S β5, 10 performed equally well as ALeu (IC50 = 15.1 µM), not showing any significant selectivity for a potential future i20S proteasome inhibitor development with this class. Our best compound was Compound 1, the analogue with the AGly residue at the P1 position with an IC50 value of 2.3 µM. This observation was surprising due to the lack of a P1 side chain and the absence of interactions in the S1 pocket to increase potency. It enabled, however, the carbonyl carbon of the warhead to position itself in close proximity to the nucleophilic oxygen of the 1Thr residue at the active site, as observed in our molecular docking results (below).

When tested with the other catalytic ß subunits at 100 µM, we observed slight inhibition of ß2 and none of ß1 (Figure 3). This observation is consistent with previous studies showing the effectiveness of the Leu-Leu-Leu tripeptidyl motif for ß5 [30]. This observation also emphasizes the relative strength of the aza-peptide carbonyl warhead when paired with the ideal recognition Leu-Leu-Leu sequence that together yield low µM inhibition values. Our bortezomib-like compound, 11, is a weak inhibitor due to the short dipeptidyl recognition sequence Pz-Phe-ALeu that is insufficient for binding to the S1 and S2 pockets.

Inhibition of ß1 and ß2 catalytic subunits of the human c20S proteasome.

A number of our compounds were also tested for inhibition of the cysteine protease human cathepsin B, and the serine protease chymotrypsin, due to some of their peptide recognition elements being in common with the c20S proteasome [31]. No inhibition was recorded at 50 µM for either enzyme (Table 2). One exception was Compound 7 which generated an IC50 value of 31.5 µM with human cathepsin B. The N-capping group serves as the major determinant for selectivity as its replacement with a morpholino group (Compound 9), leads to no inhibition of cathepsin B. The morpholino group found in carfilzomib not only gives such analogues more drug-like properties overall, but also serves to diminish the cross-reactivity between various proteases [32].

Molecular docking

In our previous study, we showed that caspase-3 forms a covalent bond between its active site 163Cys-S atom and the carbonyl carbon of the caspase-3 inhibitor Cbz-Asp-Glu-Val-AAsp-COMe. In the same study, we also hypothesized that the proteasome would be inhibited by the active site 1Thr-O nucleophilic addition to the carbonyl carbon of the warhead, thereby forming a reversible covalent bond. Here, to investigate the proximity between these two atoms at the active site, we have performed a docking study of all of the aza-peptide aldehydes and ketones in the ß5 active site using Autodock Vina [29].

To validate the docking protocol, oprozomib was re-docked into the human c20S proteasome crystal structure (PDB 5LEY) [25]. The full complex was loaded into MOE, the ß1 and ß5 subunits (chain L and chain K) were isolated, and oprozomib was docked back into the crystallographic binding site. The pose that best aligned with the experimental conformation was selected, and the procedure was carried out three times.

The selected pose from the re-docking was the one with the shortest reactive distance between the carbonyl warhead of oprozomib and the nucleophilic active site atom (1Thr-O). All three replicates showed the same trend, yielding an average reactive distance of 3.14 Å (Figure S4).

The percentage of selected poses for each compound was determined based on the number of poses with a reactive distance of ≤3.5 Å, a distance consistent with hydrogen bonding interactions [33]. This fraction may reflect the compound’s propensity to adopt a catalytically favorable conformation [34]. From the re-docking runs, the pose with the shortest reactive distance was chosen to represent each compound (Table 4).

The top-performing analogue, Compound 1, produced the largest percentage of poses with reactive distances less than 3.5 Å. Furthermore, across the three docking trials, this compound displayed the shortest average distance of 3.28 Å for the best pose across the three docking trials. This compound demonstrates favorable spatial alignment within the binding pocket, closely matching the cavity’s contour. In addition, a violin plot analysis was performed to further analyze the binding mode distribution of Compound 1 and oprozomib. For both compounds, the carbonyl-to-1Thr-O reactive distances were calculated for every docking pose. These distances were plotted as violin plots, and each pose was weighted by a Boltzmann factor calculated from its predicting binding score (Figure S5). In these violin plots, the width at each distance represents the pose probability density, and the overall shape reveals preferred binding modes.

Both oprozomib and Compound 1 display a dominant unimodal distribution, suggesting a distinct, stable active site conformation at a long reactive distance (~10–11 Å). However, both compounds also exhibit a minor, less-frequent peak at a shorter reactive distance (~3 Å), corresponding to a conformationfavorable for protein inhibition. These results indicate that the compounds predominantly occupy a single binding mode but can transition into an alternative and less populated, yet still favorable, pose. This dual behavior appears essential for achieving high inhibitory potency.

Conclusions

The current study validates the aza-peptide aldehydes and ketones as a new and selective class of low-µM inhibitors of the human c20S proteasome. Aza-peptide aldehydes and ketones inhibit the ß5 active site and the SAR study of the P1 position revealed that AGly at this position provided the most potent compound of this class of inhibitors. As expected, tetrapeptidyl Carfilzomib-like analogues displayed more potency, and the morpholino N-capping group displayed more selectivity. When tested against three multiple myeloma, one NK leukemic and two healthy donor NK cell lines, two inhibitors displayed a clear selectivity for inhibition of cancer cell lines relative to primary, non-cancerous NK cell lines. This observation is in line with the c20S proteasome being a validated drug target due to its overexpression in malignant cells. Future studies will focus on synthesizing and testing tetrapeptidyl AGly inhibitors at the P1 position as well as exploring the effectiveness of the P1 AGly motif with various other electrophilic warheads such as Michael acceptors, epoxides and halomethyl ketones.
