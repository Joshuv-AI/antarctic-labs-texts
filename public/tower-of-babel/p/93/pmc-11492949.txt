Interferon-induced factor 16 is essential in metastatic melanoma to maintain STING levels and the immune responses upon IFN-γ response pathway activation
Authors: Yuta Kobayashi, Matias A Bustos, Yoshinori Hayashi, Qiang Yu, Dave Hoon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitor (ICIs)-based therapies are the standard of care treatment for patients with metastatic melanoma (MM). The stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling pathway is critical in controlling immune responses to ICIs. Interferon (IFN)-γ-inducible protein 16 (IFI16) is a cytosolic DNA sensor that activates the STING signaling pathway. The link between IFI16 and STING signaling pathway on IFN-γ stimulation and the connection to ICIs response remains not completely understood.MethodsDeconvolution analyses were performed using the TCGA-SKCM, GSE91061, and PRJEB23709 public RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data sets that contained RNA-seq for patients with MM. Functional assays combined with cytokine arrays were performed using MM cell lines to validate in silico data. Multiplex immunofluorescence was performed on untreated or pretreatment tumor samples from patients with MM.ResultsDeconvolution analysis showed that high-IFI16 levels in melanoma cells were associated with a good prognosis in patients with MM and positively correlated with M1-macrophage infiltration. Functional assays using MM cell lines demonstrated that IFI16 is a key molecule to sense cytosolic DNA and activate STING and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathways, independent of cyclic GMP-AMP synthase or absent in melanoma 2, on IFN-γ stimulation. IFI16 knockdown significantly decreased CXCL10 and ICAM1 secretion. EZH2 inhibitor reversed the repressive epigenetic control on IFI16 to promote STING and NF-κB signaling pathways on IFN-γ stimulation. Increased IFI16, ICAM1, and CXCL10 levels in tumor samples from patients with MM were positively correlated with M1-macrophage infiltration and a significantly better response to ICIs.ConclusionsThis study identifies IFI16 as a key sensor during IFN-γ stimulation associated with ICI response, and it proposes the epigenetic EZH2 inhibitor as an alternative treatment strategy to overcome ICI resistance in patients with MM.Keywords: Immune checkpoint inhibitor, Skin cancer, Gene expression profiling - GEP

Background

The outcomes for patients with metastatic melanoma (MM) have significantly improved with immune checkpoint blockade (ICI) therapies.1 However, the response rate remains low, ranging between 10% and 44%, and ICI therapies are effective in the long-term for a small population of patients with MM.2 The use of biomarkers, including interferon gamma (IFN-γ) signature score, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, and tumor mutation burden, has also limited value in predicting the efficacy of ICI in MM due to the complicated interactions between tumor cells and the heterogenous tumor immune microenvironment (TIME).3 4 Therefore, there is a need for improved biomarkers to better assess patients who already have primary resistant tumors and/or tumors that will acquire resistance to ICI therapies.

The stimulator of interferon genes (STING) has been identified as a major factor regulating a spontaneous immune response in various types of tumors.5,7 STING regulates the activation of multiple pathways, whereby the majority of studies focused on the secretion of type-I IFNs through the recruitment and activation of IFN regulatory factor 3 (IRF3).8 However, STING concomitantly induces a pro-inflammatory cytokine response through the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway activation.9 The secretion of several chemokines and cytokines by melanoma cells modulates the TIME and induces the polarization of macrophages towards an M1-like macrophage state.10 11

The cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) and IFN-γ inducible protein 16 (IFI16) are cytosolic DNA sensors that activate the STING pathway.12 Most of the studies have focused on cGAS,13 but little is known about the role of IFI16 in cancer cells. The function of IFI16 in immune cells has been well studied in the context of inflammation and virus infection.12 14 Recently, our group has shown that downregulation of IFI16-dependent STING signaling drives a poor anti-HER2 immune response in HER2+ breast cancer.15 While accumulating evidence has demonstrated the importance of the STING signaling pathway in MM,13 there is no evidence showing the role of IFI16 in relation to STING signaling pathways in patients with MM and/or how IFI16 levels modulate the TIME and the sensitivity to ICI therapies.

The current study aims to understand the function of IFI16 in melanoma cells in controlling STING and NF-κB signaling pathways; (1) the downstream effects of IFI16 on the melanoma cells secretory cytokines/chemokines to modulate the immune responses in patients with MM receiving ICIs; and (2) the role of IFI16 as a prognostic factor in patients with MM receiving ICI. Briefly, IFI16 upregulation mediated by IFN-γ stimulation activates both STING and NF-κB signaling pathways and the secretion of CXCL10 and ICAM1 by melanoma cells. IFI16 has an active regulatory site in the promoter region for EZH215 which leads to IFI16 repression. The treatment with EZH2 inhibitor (EZH2i) primed the response to IFN-γ of melanoma cells through the upregulation of IFI16. Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) using clinical samples from patients with MM revealed that the IFI16 levels were positively associated with STING levels in melanoma cells and the upregulation of M1 macrophage infiltration in the TIME; both of which are associated with the improved efficacy in patients to adjuvant ICI therapies.

Methods

Human tissue samples

This study followed the principles in the Declaration of Helsinki. All human specimens and clinical information, including informed consent, were obtained according to the protocol guidelines approved by the Saint John’s Health Center (SJHC)/Saint John’s Cancer Institute (SJCI) and Western Institutional Human Subjects Review Board.

The study involved formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) with clinical, and pathology annotated melanoma tumors (n=14) obtained from 14 patients with melanoma. All patients underwent surgeries at SJHC, and specimens were identified by experienced melanoma surgical pathologists at SJHC Surgical Pathology Department. Detailed characteristics of patients are shown in online supplemental table 1.

FFPE tissue section slides from a well clinically annotated tissue microarray (TMA) containing 320 cores from 160 patients diagnosed with stage III and IV cutaneous melanoma (American Joint Commitee on Cancer eighth edition) developed at the SJCI (formerly John Wayne Cancer Institute) were assessed for the protein levels using Opal staining Opal 7-Color Manual IHC Kit (NEL 811001KT, Akoya Biosciences). All the tissue samples were collected at SJHC Surgical Pathology Dept and the clinical outcomes and follow-up data for the patients were collected using the SJCI database. Patients (n=34) who had a pretreatment history (previous melanoma treatment or neoadjuvant chemotherapy) were excluded from the analysis. In addition, a total of 16 tissue core samples were removed from the analysis because the cores detached from the slide or were depleted from the TMA. Detailed characteristics of patients are shown in online supplemental table 2. Detailed information about primary antibodies, the panels, and the corresponding fluorophores are indicated in online supplemental tables 3 and 4.

Analysis of public data sets

The detailed information of the public data set used in this study is described in online supplemental material and listed in online supplemental table 3.

Deconvolution analysis of the bulk RNA sequencing data sets

BayesPrism16 and CODEFACS17 deconvolution analyses were applied to the bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data sets of TCGA-SKCM,18 PRJEB23709,19 and GSE9106120 to get the melanoma cell-specific expression profiles. For BayesPrism, the count matrixes of messenger RNA (mRNA) expression profiles were provided as input and the raw count matrix of the single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data set from GSE15499621 was used as the reference gene expression profile. For CODEFACS, the mRNA expression profiles of TPM format were provided as input and the same gene expression profile generated in the previous study was used as reference.17 CODEFACS, CIBERSORT,22 quanTIseq,23 EPIC,24 and MCP-counter25 deconvolution analyses were used to get the immune cell fractions. For CODEFACS, the LM22 signature matrix22 was used as the reference gene expression profiles of immune cells. LM22 was downloaded from the CIBERSORTx web portal (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/).22 For CIBERSORT, quanTIseq, EPIC, and MCP-counter, the web portal of TIMER2.026 (http://timer.cistrome.org/) was used for the analysis of immune cell fractions imputation.

Metastatic melanoma cell lines

Established melanoma cell lines from SJCI/JWCI were obtained from patients with melanoma who received elective surgery (MH-0331, SG-0035, M16, and FD-0836). All cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (complete medium). All the above cell lines have been authenticated using short tandem repeat profiling and were Mycoplasma-free.

Establishment of stable melanoma cell lines

MH-0331 and SG-0035 cell lines (1×105 cells/well) were seeded in 24-well plates and incubated for 24 hours. Cell lines were transfected with the IFI16-shRNA vector (shIFI16, HSH059925-nU6, GeneCopoeia, Rockville) or the non-targeting scrambled control vector as a control (shCTRL, GeneCopoeia, Rockville) using jetPRIME (Polypus-transfection, Illkirch, France). The MH-0331 and SG-0035 shIFI16 or shCTRL of cell lines were selected with 0.5 µg/mL Puromycin (Life Technologies, Grand Island, New York, USA). The IFI16 levels were confirmed by both quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) and western blot in all the cell lines. All experiments using these cell lines were performed within 10 passages after establishment.

Drug treatment

EZH2i (tazemetostat; EPZ-6438, Selleck Chemicals, Houston) and HDAC inhibitor (HDACi) (Belinostat; PXD101, Selleck Chemicals, Houston) were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at a concentration of 1 mM and 10 mM, respectively. Recombinant human IFN-γ protein (Novus Biologicals, Littleton, Colorado, USA) was dissolved in distilled water at a concentration of 1,000 IU/µL. Detailed information for the use of the drug for each experiment is described in online supplemental material. For specific experiments, cell lines were treated with optimized dose at 500 nM of EZH2i for 5 days and, then exposed to 250 IU/mL of IFN-γ for 2 hours.

Multiplex immunofluorescence

All mIF were performed as previously described27 using Opal 7-Color Manual IHC Kit (NEL 811001KT, Akoya Biosciences). The detailed methods are described in online supplemental material. In brief, the images of multiplex-stained slides were acquired by the Mantra Multispectral Imaging System (V.1.0, Akoya Biosciences). The normalization of the signal intensities was performed for each marker and spectral unmixing was conducted by inForm analysis software (V.2.6.0, Akoya Biosciences). As for IFI16 scores and STING scores, double-positive cells (IFI16+, MART1+ or STING+, MART1+) were first counted for each image. Then, both scores were calculated by the number of double-positive cells divided by the total number of MART1+ cells as described in online supplemental figure 1. Macrophage scores were obtained by counting the cells (CD68+, CD80+, CD163−) for M1 macrophage and the cells (CD68+, CD80−, CD163+) for M2 macrophage, respectively. All the scores obtained by mIF are shown in online supplemental tables 5 and 6.

Proteome profiler human cytokine array

The Proteome Profiler Human Cytokine Array Kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA) was used following manufacturer’s recommendations. MH-0331 cell lines with stable IFI16 knockdown (shIFI16) and respective control (shCTRL) were treated with 500 nM of EZH2i or DMSO for 5 days. After treatment, the medium was changed and 250 IU/mL of IFN-γ was added. The conditioned medium (CMed) was collected 24 hours after IFN-γ stimulation.

Biostatistics analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using the R V.4.3.0 in a two-tailed way. Two groups were analyzed by the Mann-Whitney U test. Multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance followed by a post hoc Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. The correlation was determined by Pearson’s correlation test. Patients from the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) study were divided into high-IFI16 and low-IFI16 mRNA levels groups using the minimum p value approach.28 Overall survival was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test. All figures were unified using Adobe Illustrator CC (Adobe, San Jose, California, USA) or CorelDRAW graphics suite 8× (Corel, Ottawa, Canada).

Additional methods

Detailed information on drug treatments, RT-qPCR, western blots, nuclear extraction, immunofluorescence staining, mIF, siRNA transfection, analysis of public data sets, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), analysis of scRNA-seq data set, Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-sequencing analysis, and pathway analysis is included in the supplemental material (that contains online supplemental M&M and online supplemental figures). Detailed information of the reagents and antibodies used is described in online supplemental tables 3 and 4.

Results

IFI16 mRNA levels and prognosis of metastatic melanomas

IFI16 plays a role in DNA sensing and promotes cGAMP production,12 but also activates the STING signaling pathway.29 Initially, we examined the IFI16 expression profiles in various types of tissues using the combination of TCGA-SKCM and GTEx data sets.18 SKCM tissue samples had the highest IFI16 mRNA levels. Moreover, IFI16 mRNA levels were significantly upregulated compared with normal skin tissue samples (figure 1A, p<0.0001). The mIF analysis of cutaneous melanoma tissues demonstrated that IFI16 was detected in both the cytoplasm and nucleus of MART1+ melanoma cells (figure 1B). ScRNA-seq data from GSE11597830 revealed that among various cell types (CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, B cells, melanoma cells, macrophage, natural killer cells, endothelial cells, cancer-associated fibroblast) melanoma cells displayed the highest IFI16 mRNA levels (figure 1C). These results suggested that IFI16 is expressed in various cell types present in melanoma tumors, therefore, investigating the role of IFI16 using bulk RNA-seq data may lead to inaccurate conclusions about melanoma cells.

To overcome the above-mentioned issue, deconvolution analyses of bulk RNA-seq data sets were performed using several pipelines such as BayesPrism,16 CODEFACS,17 CIBERSORT,22 quanTIseq,23 EPIC,24 MCP-counter,25 and TIMER2.026 (figure 1D). Deconvolution analyses of RNA-seq data from TCGA-SKCM18 using BayesPrism identified eight cell types (figure 1E,F). Melanoma-specific expression profile showed the highest IFI16 mRNA levels among the eight-cell types considered (figure 1F, p<0.0001). The accuracy of data deconvolution was confirmed by comparing the melanoma cell fractions estimated by BayesPrism and the tumor purities estimated by CPE, ESTIMATE, LUMP, and IHC.31 The melanoma cell fractions obtained from BayesPrism were positively correlated with the tumor purities estimated from previous studies (online supplemental figure 2A,B), suggesting that the deconvolution analyses were accurate. Comparable results were observed when using the CODEFACS algorithm (online supplemental figure 2C). To determine if IFI16 mRNA levels were associated with clinical outcomes, melanoma tumors from the TCGA-SKCM18 were stratified based on low-IFI16 (n=137) and high-IFI16 (n=56) mRNA levels in melanoma cells after deconvolution. High-IFI16 mRNA levels were significantly associated with good prognosis in patients with AJCC stage III and IV melanoma (figure 1G–I). These results suggested that IFI16 in melanoma cells may have a suppressive function to prevent melanoma tumor progression.

IFI16 levels in melanoma cells are associated with the IFN-γ response pathway

Then, single samples gene set enrichment analysis was performed using the deconvoluted profile of the melanoma cells from TCGA-SKCM18 (n=193), PRJEB2370919 (n=91), and GSE9106120 (n=107) data sets to explore the signaling pathways related to IFI16. The IFI16 levels in melanoma cells were positively correlated with the IFN-γ response pathway in the three data sets (figure 2A–F, and online supplemental figure 3A–C, TCGA-SKCM; p=0.00024, r=0.26; PRJEB23709; p<0.0001, r=0.53; GSE91061; p<0.0001, r=0.55). Next, we explored the downstream pathways associated with IFI16 levels in melanoma cells by correlating all genes obtained from the deconvoluted gene profiles for melanoma cells with IFI16 mRNA levels. Among the 109 genes that were commonly identified (Pearson’s correlation coefficient >0.3 or <−0.3) in TCGA-SKCM, PRJEB23709, and GSE90161 data sets, STAT1 and JAK232 were positively correlated with IFI16 (figure 2G). In summary, these results suggested a link between IFI16 expression in melanoma cells and the IFN-γ response pathway.

IFN-γ induces DNA damage induced and the upregulation and translocation of IFI16

To investigate whether IFN-γ treatment modulates the IFI16 levels, we performed analysis using publicly available obtained from GSE44662 data set.21 IFI16 showed a significant upregulation in IFN-γ-treated compared with untreated MM cell lines (online supplemental figure 4A,B). Western blot analyses demonstrated that IFN-γ induced a dose-dependent and time-dependent increase in IFI16 levels (figure 3A,B), suggesting that IFN-γ treatment regulates IFI16 expression.

On binding to its receptor, IFN-γ induces the phosphorylation of Janus kinases 1 (JAK1) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) which dimerizes and acts as a downstream transcription activator of several genes.32 Consistently, IFN-γ induced the phosphorylation of JAK1/STAT1 (p-JAK1/p-STAT1) pathway in MM cell lines (online supplemental figure 5A).

IFN-γ induces the production of nitric oxide (NO), leading to DNA damage, and the accumulation of cytosolic DNA in lung cancer-derived cell lines.29 Therefore, we evaluated the levels of NO production in MM cell lines, after IFN-γ treatment. Consistently, the levels of NO increased on IFN-γ treatment (figure 3C). Of note, the increased levels of NO can be explained by a significant upregulation of inducible nitric oxide synthase mRNA and protein levels after IFN-γ treatment (figure 3D). Then, the levels of phosphorylated Ataxia telangiectasia mutated (p-ATM) were evaluated to determine whether the DNA damage response pathway was activated on IFN-γ treatment (online supplemental figure 5B). Of note, IFN-γ treatment increased p-ATM levels in a dose-dependent manner (online supplemental figure 5C). Also, MM cell lines treated with IFN-γ showed enhanced phosphorylation levels of γ-H2AX (p-γ-H2AX), and in the number of γ-H2AX or 53BP1 foci formation compared with control untreated cells (figure 3E–G and online supplemental figure 5E). Next, we performed IF assays to investigate whether IFN-γ stimulation leads to the accumulation of cytosolic DNA. As expected, IFN-γ treatment significantly increased the levels of cytosolic dsDNA in MM cell lines (figure 3H,I). Consistent with previous reports,29 IFI16 showed increased levels in the nuclear and cytoplasm compartments on IFN-γ stimulation (figure 3J,K). In summary, IFN-γ stimulation induces DNA damage, promotes cytosolic DNA accumulation, and increases the levels of IFI16 in the cytoplasm. These results suggested a downstream role of IFI16 on cytosolic DNA accumulation induced by IFN-γ.

IFN-γ stimulation induces the activation of STING and NF-κB pathways

During STING signaling pathway activation, TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) and STING phosphorylate each other, leading to IRF3 recruitment. Activated IRF3 dimerizes and migrates to the nucleus to promote the transcription of type I IFNs.9 The MM cell lines were analyzed for cGAS, STING, TBK1, and IFI16. While STING and cGAS showed variable expression, the protein levels of TBK1 and IFI16 were more conserved (online supplemental figure 5D). MM cell lines were treated with IFN-γ and then analyzed for STING signaling activation. The levels of phosphorylated TBK1 (p-TBK1) and IRF3 (p-IRF3) significantly increased 2–4 hours after IFN-γ stimulation compared with control conditions (figure 3L,M), suggesting STING pathway activation. Then, NF-κB pathway activation was also analyzed on IFN-γ stimulation. Of notice, the phosphorylated levels of p65 (p-p65) increased 2–4 hours after IFN-γ stimulation compared with control conditions (figure 3L,M). Consistent with NF-κB pathway activation,33 the processing of p105 into p50 was significantly increased after IFN-γ stimulation (figure 3L,M). In summary, IFN-γ stimulation increases IFI16 levels which activates the STING and NF-κB signaling pathways in MM cell lines.

IFI16 is required for the stability of STING and activation of STING and NF-κB pathway

Two MM cell line models with stable knockdown of IFI16 were established to investigate whether IFI16 is essential for the STING pathway activation during IFN-γ stimulation. The knockdown of IFI16 was confirmed by RT-qPCR and western blot (figure 4A–D). IFI16 knockdown significantly decreased the levels of STING compared with control conditions, in both untreated and IFN-γ-treated conditions (figure 4C,D). Concordantly, IFI16 knockdown decreased the levels of p-TBK1/TBK1 and p-IRF3/IRF3 (figure 4C,D). Unexpectedly, Co-IP assays showed that IFI16 did not bind to STING in untreated or during IFN-γ stimulation (online supplemental figure 6A). Additionally, IFI16 knockdown reduced STING mRNA levels compared with control conditions (online supplemental figure 6B,C), suggesting that IFI16 regulates STING levels.

A previous study reported a link between STING and NF-κB signaling pathways.9 Therefore, we investigated whether IFI16 knockdown affects the activation of the NF-κB pathway. Concordantly, IFI16 knockdown decreased the levels of phosphorylated-p65 (p-p65) (figure 4E,F). These results suggested that IFI16 knockdown decreased the STING levels to deactivate STING and NF-κB signaling pathways.

The IFI16 and STING protein levels were evaluated by mIF using a TMA containing patients with AJCC stage III and IV melanoma. The IFI16 and STING protein levels were scored (online supplemental figure 1) and both IFI16 and STING scores significantly correlated in MART1+ areas (figure 4G,H, n=107 p<0.0001, r=0.49). Furthermore, high-IFI16 protein levels in the MART1+ areas were significantly associated with good prognosis (figure 4I, p=0.00025), while high-STING protein levels in the MART1+ areas showed a non-significant trend to a good prognosis (figure 4J, p=0.074). However, the combination of high-IFI16 and high-STING protein levels in the MART1+ areas were significantly associated with a good prognosis (figure 4K, p=0.0019), suggesting potential clinical implications.

EZH2i enhances the stimulatory effects of IFN-γ ton STING pathway

In a previous study, we demonstrated that IFI16 transcription is regulated by EZH2, and the combination of the EZH2i and HDACi significantly reactivates IFI16-dependent immune responses in HER2+ breast cancer tumors.15 Since high-IFI16 mRNA levels were associated with better outcomes and immune response in patients with MM, we investigated whether EZH2i and HDACi may restore IFI16 levels when low. H3K27ac is a histone modification mark for open chromatin regions and is generally targeted by HDAC enzymes. ChIP sequencing analysis using the publicly available GSE14365334 data set revealed an enrichment of H3K27ac in the IFI16 gene promoter in all melanoma cell lines (online supplemental figure 7A,B). In addition, the treatment of MM cell lines with HDACi did not increase the IFI16 levels compared with control conditions (online supplemental figure 7C–F), suggesting that the low-IFI16 levels observed in melanoma cells cannot be explained by a decrease in H3K27ac levels.

Conversely, the analysis of the public GSE1340435 ChIP sequencing data set for melanoma cell lines showed the enrichment of H3K27me3 at the enhancer/promoter regions of the IFI16 gene (onlinesupplemental figure 8 table 7). The treatment of MM cell lines with EZH2i enhanced IFI16 levels while decreasing H3K27me3 levels compared with control conditions (figure 5A,B). Interestingly, IFN-γ stimulation significantly increased IFI16 mRNA levels in MM cell lines that received EZH2i pretreatment (figure 5C). The highest IFI16 levels induced by IFN-γ were observed from 5 to 6 days after EZH2i pretreatment (figure 5D,E). MM cell lines with sequential EZH2i (priming) and IFN-γ (stimulation) treatment showed an upregulation of STING, TBK1/p-TBK1, and IRF3-p-IRF3 as well as p65/p-p65 and p50, compared with IFN-γ stimulation alone (figure 5F,G). In addition, STING, TBK1, and IRF3 as well as p65 and p50 upregulation was not observed in MM cell lines with IFI16 knockdown independent of EZH2i and IFN-γ stimulation. These results indicated that EZH2i decreased histone methylation to prime the response of MM cell lines to IFN-γ, upregulate both IFI16 mRNA and protein levels, and activate STING and NF-κB signaling pathways. The effects mediated by EZH2i treatment on STING and NF-κB signaling pathways during IFN-γ stimulation depend on IFI16 expression.

IFI16 upregulation mediated by IFN-γ and EZH2i increased CXCL10 and IL-8

MM cell lines were analyzed using a proteome profiler human cytokine array to determine the cytokines/chemokines activated by IFI16 after IFN-γ treatment in the presence or absence of EZH2i. Therefore, control (shCTRL) or IFI16 knockdown (shIFI16) MM cell lines were treated with IFN-γ and/or EZH2i and the CMed was collected as indicated in M&M. CXCL10, ICAM1, and IL-8 proteins were significantly decreased in CMed obtained from the shIFI16 MM cell lines compared with shCTRL (figure 5J,K), indicating that CXCL10, ICAM1, and IL-8 cytokine secretion stimulated by IFN-γ depends on IFI16 expression. Surprisingly, EZH2i significantly increased the levels of CXCL10, ICAM1, and IL-8 proteins in CMed obtained from the shCTRL, but not in shIFI16, MM cells treated with IFN-γ (figure 5J,K). These results suggested that the increase in CXCL10 ICAM1, and IL-8 cytokine secretion mediated by EZH2i priming and subsequent IFN-γ treatment are mediated by EZH2i effects on IFI16. Supporting these results, in silico analysis consistently demonstrated that the IFI16 mRNA levels were positively correlated with the mRNA levels of ICAM1 and CXCL10, but not with IL-8 (figure 2G). Overall, these results indicated that IFI16 upregulation induced by IFN-γ after sensitizing MM cells with EZH2i increased the mRNA and protein levels of CXCL10 and ICAM1. Therefore, this suggests that IFI16 may control CXCL10 and ICAM1 by modulating STING and NF-κB signaling pathways in MM cells.

cGAS plays a significant role in sensing cytosolic DNA and activating the STING pathway.13 While the role of IFI16 in activating the STING pathway remains conflictive.12 15 29 There is also conflictive data on the role of cGAS requirement for STING pathway activation mediated by IFI16.12 cGAS knockdown in MM cell lines neither affected IFI16, STING, or pTBK1/TBK1 protein levels, nor modified ICAM1 mRNA levels under untreated conditions (online supplemental figure 9A–F). As expected, IFN-γ treatment raised the mRNA levels of CXCL10 and ICAM1. Of note, cGAS knockdown significantly increased the mRNA levels of CXCL10, but not ICAM1, in MM cell lines stimulated with IFN-γ compared with control conditions (online supplemental figure 9C–F). These results suggested that cGAS is not required to mediate STING pathway activation during IFN-γ treatment to promote CXCL10 and ICAM1 upregulation.

IFI16, absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2), interferon-inducible protein X, and myeloid cell nuclear differentiation antigen belong to a family of proteins that share a common motif known as PYHIN, that binds to cytosolic DNA.36 37 Therefore, we wondered whether AIM2 may be responsible for the changes in the cytokine profile on IFN-γ stimulation. AIM2 knockdown in MM cell lines neither affected IFI16 protein expression, nor modified CXCL10 and ICAM1 under untreated conditions (online supplemental figure 9G–L). In both MM cell lines, IFN-γ treatment increased CXCL10 and ICAM1 mRNA levels. However, AIM2 knockdown significantly increased the mRNA levels of CXCL10, but not ICAM1, in MM cell lines stimulated with IFN-γ compared with control conditions (online supplemental figure 9I–L). To summarize, AIM2 and IFI16 share similar DNA binding domains, however, AIM2 did not have overlapping effects with IFI16 on promoting the upregulation of CXCL10 and ICAM1 on IFN-γ stimulation.

IFI16 levels in melanoma cells are correlated with M1 macrophage infiltration and ICI responses

Since IFI16 downregulation decreased the levels of CXCL10 and ICAM1, we hypothesized that IFI16 downregulation modulates the TIME. In silico analyses were performed on the TCGA-SKCM,18 PRJEB23709,19 and GSE9106120 data sets to determine the correlations between IFI16 mRNA levels in MM cells estimated by BayesPrism16 and the infiltrated immune cell fraction levels estimated by CODEFACS.17 Surprisingly, the IFI16 mRNA levels were positively correlated with the infiltrated levels of M1 macrophages in melanoma tumors from TCGA-SKCM data set (figure 6A, p<0.0001, r=0.17), PRJEB23709 (figure 6B, p=0.0044, r=0.29), and GSE91061 data sets (figure 6C, p=0.017, r=0.23). The results were further validated using CIBERSORT,22 quanTIseq,23 EPIC,24 and MCP-counter25 algorithms. Consistently, IFI16 mRNA levels were positively correlated with M1 macrophages independent of the algorithm used (onlinesupplemental figure 10 table 8). The in silico results were further validated using a TMA for patients with AJCC stage III and IV melanoma (n=98) and two protein panels for mIF (figure 6D, panel 1: IFI16, STING, and MART1; and panel 2: MART1, CD68, CD80, and CD206).38 The M1 macrophage scores and M1/M2 ratio (M1 macrophage: CD68+, CD80+, CD163−; M2 macrophage: CD68+, CD80−, CD163+) were significantly higher in tissue samples with high-IFI16 protein levels in the MART1+ areas (figure 6E, M1 macrophage scores; p=0.0034, figure 6F, M2 macrophage scores; p=0.61, figure 6G, M1/M2 ratio; p=0.034, respectively). Furthermore, high-M1 macrophage scores and high-M1/M2 ratio were significantly associated with better prognosis (figure 6H,I, p=0.0012 and p=0.012, respectively).

Based on the associations between IFI16 and the increased levels in M1 macrophages we hypothesized that IFI16 may contribute to ICIs response in patients with MM. Therefore, the associations between IFI16 mRNA levels and resistance to anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) immunotherapy were assessed in patients with MM, using the deconvoluted data of the PRJEB23709 data set (figure 7A). Surprisingly, IFI16 mRNA levels in melanoma cells were significantly higher in patients with MM who showed complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) than in patients who had stable disease (SD) or progressive disease (PD) to the anti-PD-1 immunotherapy when considering both pretreatment (figure 7B; n=40 CR/PR, n=33 SD/PD, p=0.029) and on-treatment tissue samples (figure 7C; n=9 CR/PR, n=9 SD/PD, p=0.011). To validate the association between IFI16 levels in melanoma cells and the efficacy of adjuvant ICI therapy, we selected a cohort of pretreatment FFPE samples obtained from patients with MM who underwent complete resection and subsequent adjuvant ICI treatment (no-recurrence: n=7, recurrence: n=7, respectively). Using mIF, the levels of IFI16 and STING protein levels in MART1+ areas were compared between patients with MM that recur versus patients that did not recur. Both IFI16 and STING levels in the MART1+ area were significantly higher in patients with non-recurrent versus recurrent MM (figure 7F–I, IFI16, p=0.0016; STING, p=0.016). These results suggested that IFI16 protein levels in MART1+ areas of pretreatment tissue samples are associated with the efficacy of adjuvant ICI therapy.

Then, the mRNA levels of 164 cytokines and chemokines were estimated by BayesPrism in melanoma cells of pretreatment melanoma tissues. ICAM1 and CXCL10 were significantly upregulated in pretreatment samples of patients that had good response (CR/PR) to ICI (online supplemental figure 11A). Since IFI16 was associated with increased ICAM1 and CXCL10 expression, as well as M1 macrophage infiltration, we evaluated the correlation between 164 cytokines and chemokines with IFI16 in melanoma cells and M1 macrophage infiltration. Both CXCL10 and ICAM1 showed a positive correlation with IFI16 and M1 macrophage infiltration (online supplemental figure 11B,C). Finally, the levels of CXCL10 or ICAM1 in melanoma cells estimated by BayesPrism in tissue samples collected at pretreatment or on-treatment samples were explored in the context of ICI responses. Data analyses showed that CXCL10 and ICAM1 were increased in patients with good response to ICI (online supplemental figure 11D–G). To summarize, the levels of both CXCL10 and ICAM1 are associated with a better response to ICI therapies.

Discussion

In the present study, we revealed the clinical importance of IFI16 in patients with MM. Deconvolution analysis of the public data set and the mIF of patients with MM demonstrated the significant associations between high-IFI16 levels in melanoma cells and a better prognosis. Furthermore, the deconvolution analysis and the mIF demonstrated that high-IFI16 levels in melanoma cells were associated with better response to ICI therapies. These results provide evidence that the IFI16 levels in melanoma cells may have a potential prognostication value as well as a predictive value for ICI treatment response assessment for patients with MM.

Importantly, mIF showed strong correlations between IFI16 and STING levels in MART1+ areas in MM tissues. STING signaling pathways are a potential target for anticancer treatment, however, recent reports demonstrated that the objective response rate is less than 30% when combining pembrolizumab and STING agonists in patients with lymphomas or solid tumors.39 The low response rate to STING agonist and/or combinational therapies implies the existence of more intricated interactions between cancer and immune cells in TIME or regulatory mechanisms on STING as proposed by Mule et al13 15 showing that STING is methylated in melanoma. Our findings suggest that IFI16 significantly affects STING levels in MM models and the results of the present study may provide deeper insight into understanding the mechanisms of cancer immune response in relation to STING signaling pathway in MM.

Polarized M1- macrophages eliminate cancer cells by releasing cytotoxic molecules and/or by antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity.40 M1-macrophages polarization induced by chemotherapy drugs and targeted agents enhances immunotherapy sensitivity.41 Tumor cells stimulated in TIME also regulate M1-macrophages polarization by secreting cytokines.42 At the same time, anti-PD-L1 treatment increases the level of M1-macrophage markers by promoting the production of IFN-γ in CD4 T cells, which synergizes the antitumor activity.43 Significant correlations were observed between IFI16 levels in the MART1+ areas and the infiltrated levels of M1 macrophages (CD68+, CD80+, CD163−) in MM tumor samples undergoing ICI treatment, suggesting a critical role for IFI16 in mediating ICI therapy responses.

While several studies focused on the association between IFN-γ reaction and STING signaling pathway in solid tumors,29 44 the functional role of IFI16 linking IFN-γ pathways and downstream pathways is poorly understood. In this study, we demonstrated the mechanisms by which the upregulation of IFI16 induced by IFN-γ stimulation leads to the activation of STING and NF-κB signaling pathways in patients with MM-derived cell lines. Furthermore, the IFI16 knockdown significantly decreased STING expression in melanoma, resulting in the deactivation of STING-TBK1-IRF3 and NF-κB signaling pathways in response to IFN-γ stimulation. The activation of IRF3 and NF-κB signaling pathway led to the secretion of type I IFNs and cytokines.9 IFN-γ stimulation induced increasing CXCL10 and ICAM1 secretions through the upregulation of IFI16. CXCL10 is a downstream cytokine of the STING pathway that induces the accumulation of M1 macrophage.45 ICAM1 functions as a costimulatory molecule on antigen-presenting cells to activate major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II restricted T cells, and on other cell types in association with MHC class I to activate cytotoxic T cells.46 Mediating leukocyte adhesion, ICAM1 also serves as a signaling receptor to transduce outside-in signaling, linking leukocyte adhesive interactions with epithelial and endothelial function.47 The results of this study elucidate the detailed mechanisms triggered in MM cells that are associated with the upregulation of IFI16 to stimulate CXCL10/ICAM1 secretion and promote the infiltration of M1 macrophages to the TIME on IFN-γ stimulation.

Surprisingly, we elucidated that priming treatment of EZH2i enhances the response to IFN-γ in melanoma. We previously showed that EZH2i directly targets the epigenetic regulation of IFI16 and activates IFI16-dependent immune responses to trastuzumab in HER2+ breast cancer.15 In patients with breast cancer, the immune responses are suppressed due to low levels of IFI16.15 EZH2i showed a modest antitumor effect in recent clinical studies in solid tumors, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, and follicular lymphoma.48 49 A preclinical study showed that EZH2i can enhance the antitumor immunity induced by the STING agonist in melanoma.50 In this study, we demonstrated that the EZH2i reversed the epigenetic control on IFI16, reactivated the response to IFN-γ, and restored cytokine secretion.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the crosstalk of IFI16 with STING and NF-κB signaling pathways reacting to IFN-γ in melanoma (Graphical abstract). These interactions increased the levels of M1 macrophage infiltration and the activation of improved immune responses both of which create a better TIME to improve ICI therapy efficacy.
