Evaluation of a Facial Dysmorphology Analysis Algorithm (Face2Gene) in Identifying Treacher Collins Syndrome Amongst Diverse Population
Authors: Jie Han Timothy Sng, Jonas Jun Jie Hue, Josep Rubio‐Palau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTreacher Collins Syndrome (TCS) is an uncommon congenital disease of the craniofacial complex. While there are ‘classic’ facial manifestations of TCS, they present with a wide range of variability. Face2Gene (F2G) is a deep‐learning algorithm that can provide differential diagnoses of syndromes via analysis of 2‐dimensional facial images. This may aid early recognition of TCS, thus improving early multidisciplinary management. Our primary aim is to evaluate and compare the performance of F2G in TCS patients of various races. The secondary aim will be to correlate the pathognomonic facial features of these TCS patients with the diagnostic stratification by F2G.MethodsPublicly available images of TCS patients of White (n = 81), Chinese (n = 23), and Indian (n = 23) race were sourced for and screened prior to inclusion for documentation of facial dysmorphological features and F2G evaluation. The diagnostic sensitivity of F2G and computed gestalt score of TCS for each image were then derived. Statistical analysis for correlation between clinical features and F2G‐derived gestalt scores for the TCS patients was performed.ResultsA total of 127 images of TCS patients were analysed by F2G. A high diagnostic accuracy of 93.7% was obtained. However, F2G was less confident when diagnosing Asians with TCS than White individuals (p < 0.05). Malar hypoplasia was less prevalent in Indian TCS patients (p < 0.001). However, multidimensionality reduction showed no significant differences across the racial groups. Certain facial features were associated with higher model certainty in TCS diagnosis.ConclusionF2G is a useful diagnostic adjunct for TCS. Nonetheless, further training on non‐White datasets may help improve model certainty in diagnosis.Keywords: deep learning, detection algorithms, facial recognition, mandibulofacial dysostosis, Treacher Collins syndrome

Introduction

Treacher Collins syndrome (TCS), also known as mandibulofacial dysostosis, is an uncommon congenital disease of the craniofacial complex. More than half of TCS is due to sporadic mutations and the remaining are inherited in an autosomal dominant manner [1].

In the majority of TCS patients, mutations to the TCOF1 gene are known to disrupt the normal formation of the first and second pharyngeal arches during foetal development [2]. This leads to pronounced facial dysmorphism. Some common clinical features of TCS include bilateral downward slanting palpebral fissures, colobomas, external ear deformities, hypoplasia of the zygoma, microtia, conductive hearing loss, cleft palate, micrognathia, etc. [1, 3].

However, having broad variability in phenotypic expression [4, 5, 6] and resemblance to other conditions [1, 7], diagnosis of TCS via clinical evaluation alone is challenging. As such, definitive diagnosis of TCS can be confirmed with genetic testing [1].

In 2014, an artificial intelligence algorithm, known as Face2Gene (F2G, FDNA Inc., USA, Boston), was launched with the intent to aid clinicians in genetic syndromic recognition. Easily accessible via the Internet, users will first need to upload a picture of a face in the frontal view. The F2G algorithm will then convert and de‐identify this two‐dimensional image via deep convolutional neural networks (DCNN) to a mathematical facial descriptor. One function of F2G known as DeepGestalt will then analyse these facial features and suggest a prioritised list of the top 30 syndromes with similar facial dysmorphologies. With regards to each differential diagnosis, F2G also returns a gestalt score on a spectrum of ‘High’, ‘Medium’ and ‘Low’. Unfortunately, deep learning models are notorious for their black box nature and lack of explainability. The gestalt score provides clinician end‐users with adjunct information ‘under the hood’ about the black box DCNN to aid in their interpretation of the model's outputs. This score can be interpreted as the model's ‘degree of certainty’ of the suggested diagnosis. In other words, the gestalt scoring is based on the similarities between the facial features of the uploaded image and the images that the AI model has been trained on with respect to a specific diagnosis. This certainty grading may influence the trust a clinician may place in the diagnosis returned by the model and provides some insight into the decision‐making of the black‐box deep learning model. Till date, there is no consensus or guidance on how best to interpret these gestalt scores. Some researchers have provided their own thresholds based on their independent cohort validation for gestalt scoring, below which the model may not be trusted as having provided a true diagnosis [8].

Image analysis and artificial intelligence have proven to be invaluable tools in healthcare, with widespread applications across various disciplines from bench to bedside [9, 10, 11, 12]. Algorithms like F2G may play an increasingly important role in an era of telehealth medicine [13, 14] especially in low‐resource nations where awareness of certain syndromes, access to lab testing, and clinicians may be limited [15, 16].

However, the original dataset of patient photos used for training of F2G's DCNN was predominantly of White descent [17]. In addition, clinicians are often educated with clinical genetic resources whereby patients of White descent are featured as the standard of reference [18]. Lastly, the judgement between ‘normal’ and ‘abnormal’ facial features is highly dependent on the race of the subject [19]. These may hinder family members or even clinicians in the recognition of conditions in individuals of different ancestral backgrounds who may present with variable morphological features [18].

Thankfully, efforts to lobby for creation of reference texts on dysmorphology that are more inclusive and representative of the global population have since commenced [18]. A suggestion was to take advantage of the Internet and digital photography in establishment of these multi‐ethnic & racial databases [18]. Nevertheless, the focus of many F2G studies has remained on White populations [20].

With these in mind, the primary aim of this study is to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of F2G amongst TCS patients of various races (White and Asians; namely Chinese & Indian). The secondary goal is to evaluate for possible differences in TCS‐associated facial gestalts amongst the races and any potential correlation to the diagnostics via F2G.

Materials and Methods

Sampling and Data Collection

A search for facial photos of TCS was sourced from publicly available images on the Internet. The sources included published journal articles, textbooks, websites, and videos. No other patient identifiers were retrieved, and images were used solely for research purposes to minimise any risk of misuse. Repeat patients, unclear images, or those with facial prostheses (such as hearing aids) or those rejected by the F2G algorithm were excluded. Lastly, images that had been used for training of the F2G's deep learning algorithm were removed.

Clinical Analysis of Images

TCS‐associated facial gestalts identified in each photo by the first author were graded as: ‘Present’ or ‘Absent’. The phenotypic facial features included are downward slanting palpebral fissures, coloboma, malar hypoplasia, microtia, Tessier cleft 6, Tessier cleft 7, and retrogenia.

F2G Analysis of Images

The same set of images was also sent for evaluation by F2G (http://www.fdna.com/face2gene/). F2G evaluates the phenotypic traits or gestalts based on pattern recognition of frontal pictures of these faces. The top 30 differential diagnoses were noted together with its TCS facial gestalt ranking score of ‘High’, ‘Medium’ or ‘Low’ (Figure 1). We further grouped the patients into those where F2G had achieved the correct top diagnosis of TCS with high certainty versus all other scenarios where the gestalt score was medium or low or the top diagnosis of TCS was not reached. Correlation between the F2G‐derived scoring and facial phenotypes was then performed (Figure 1).

Statistical Analysis

We performed dimensionality reduction via multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) to explore any relationships between the presence of facial phenotypes and race within TCS patients. This was done using the FactoMineR [21] package in the R programming language.

Fisher's exact tests were performed to compare the incidences of various phenotypes between TCS patients of the three races and correlation to that of the F2G algorithm‐derived gestalt scores. We also compared F2G's diagnostic certainty in TCS patients from different races. Finally, we analysed the relationship between the presence of phenotypes and F2G's certainty in the diagnosis of TCS patients. All statistical analyses were performed in R; 95% confidence intervals were reported as necessary, and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. p values were unadjusted as only a small number of comparisons were made and to reduce the chances of a type II error.

Results

F2G Is Highly Accurate at Diagnosing TCS

A total of 127 facial images with TCS (n = 127) were analysed. The racial groups of interests were Whites (n = 81), Chinese (n = 23) and Indians (n = 23). A correct diagnosis was defined as TCS being the top‐1 diagnosis returned by F2G. F2G had an overall accuracy of 93.7%. An accuracy of 82.6%, 95.7% and 96.3% was found in the Indian, Chinese and White samples respectively.

F2G Is Less ‘Confident’ With Confirmation of TCS in Asians

F2G made the correct diagnosis with ‘high’ certainty in 86.4% of White patients, compared to 65.2% in Indian (p = 0.031) and 60.9% in Chinese (p = 0.013) patients (Figure 2). The odds of a White TCS patient achieving a correct diagnosis with high confidence by F2G were 3.35 (95% CI: 1.0, 11.1) and 4.02 (95% CI: 1.2–13.2) times higher than Indian and Chinese patients respectively.

Stacked bar charts of F2G's correct diagnostic rate with high model certainty for White (n = 81), Indian (n = 23) and Chinese (n = 23) TCS patients. *p < 0.05.

Variation in Facial Features Amongst the Races

Clinician evaluation showed that there was a higher prevalence of malar hypoplasia amongst White and Chinese patients compared to Indian patients (p < 0.001). In our cohort of TCS images, malar hypoplasia was identified in 74.1% and 73.9% of White and Chinese patients respectively compared to only 30.4% of Indian patients (Figure 3).

Bar charts showing the prevalence of various facial phenotypes amongst White (n = 81), Indian (n = 23) and Chinese (n = 23) TCS patients. *p < 0.05.

There were no statistically significant differences between the prevalence of the other facial features between the three races. We further performed dimensionality reduction using MCA to assess if there were overall differences between races when considering all the facial features together. The MCA revealed no significant differences between the three races as shown by the overlapping confidence ellipses and little separation of the data by race (Figure 4).

Multidimensionality reduction with multiple correspondence analysis using the presence of facial phenotypes. Confidence ellipses for each racial group overlap one another.

Correlation of Facial Features and F2G's Diagnostic Certainty of TCS

The F2G algorithm accurately predicted TCS with higher confidence when malar hypoplasia (p = 0.006) and Tessier cleft 6 (p = 0.023) were present (Figure 5). Amongst the Indian TCS patients, the absence of downslanting palpebral fissures was associated with a less confident TCS diagnosis (p = 0.006). Similarly, the absence of malar hypoplasia in Indian TCS patients, was associated with lower model certainty but this was not statistically significant (p = 0.052) (Figure 5B). The absence of Tessier cleft 6 in Chinese TCS patients, was associated with lower certainty in diagnosing TCS (p = 0.048) (Figure 5C). The presence or absence of facial features were not associated with model confidence in White TCS patients (Figure 5D).

Discussion

This study characterises TCS patients via 2‐dimensional frontal images subjectively through visual identification of phenotypes, objectively with facial image analysis technology (F2G), and collectively by evaluation of correlation between the data. Despite being the most common form of mandibulofacial dysostosis, clinical diagnosis of TCS can be challenging due to its variable penetrance and high phenotypic heterogeneity [1].

Encouragingly, F2G had displayed a high average accuracy for recognition of TCS amongst different races (93.7%). The accuracy for the White cohort was the greatest (96.3%), albeit non‐significant. However, while overall accuracy in TCS diagnosis was similar amongst races, F2G was significantly more confident at making the correct diagnosis in White compared to Chinese and Indian patients. This trend is similar to that from a previous study comparing accuracy of F2G in recognition of Down Syndrome between White and African faces [16]. Various other studies have also found similar trends with lower facial gestalt [22] scores and impaired accuracy [16] in non‐White populations. This finding may be due to two potential reasons. Firstly, there may be differences in the prevalence of facial phenotypes in White and Asian patients. In addition, an over‐representation of White TCS patients in the F2G training dataset may result in lower gestalt scores in Asian groups.

Especially in deep learning, black box models such as F2G, reporting a quantified ‘match’ or ‘certainty’ score to a diagnosis provides insight for the clinician and guides our degree of trust in the algorithm. This has implications on how much a clinician may feel comfortable trusting in the suggested diagnosis by F2G. Therefore, we highlight two important issues. Firstly, F2G training datasets should continually add non‐White cases to result in a more robust and generalisable model. Secondly, clinicians who predominantly see non‐White patients should exercise caution when applying F2G to their own patients and in interpreting the outputs returned by F2G.

In order to provide a possible explanation to the discrepancies in diagnostic certainties observed in the different racial groups, we compared the prevalence of common facial phenotypes in TCS. We found malar hypoplasia to be significantly absent from the images of the Indian cohort. Indeed, it is not surprising as significant inter‐racial variations were reported in facial photogrammetric study of non‐syndromic subjects [23]. However, in the remaining six TCS‐associated facial dysmorphological features, there were no significant differences found amongst the different racial groups. This suggests the presence of further, subtle differences within the mathematical facial descriptors analysed by F2G. In addition, underscoring the utility of powerful deep learning models that surpass the simple grading by a clinician.

Interestingly, the F2G algorithm accurately predicted TCS with higher certainty when malar hypoplasia and Tessier cleft 6 were present. In the subgroup analysis, a less confident F2G‐aided diagnosis for TCS was associated with an absence of downslanting palpebral fissures and Tessier cleft 6 amongst Indian and Chinese TCS patients respectively. Again, suggesting that the F2G algorithm is well‐suited to recognise TCS in White faces and further training of the AI system may help refine the confidence scoring of TCS amongst Asian faces. By increasing the likelihood for accurate recognition for TCS, these patients may potentially receive earlier confirmation of diagnosis and prompt referral for coordinated care [24]. This can minimise complications and optimise quality of life [25] in aspects such as breathing, feeding, vision and speech.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, there is a relatively small sample size especially with regards to the TCS patients of Asian origins (Chinese & Indian patients). Similar to a previous facial analysis study on Down Syndrome [26], we share the inherent weakness of being unable to capture the entire spectrum of different racial groups and ethnicities around the world. In addition, images were obtained from publicly available sources and thus standardisation of head posture or facial expression cannot be performed. These may serve as confounders towards AI‐based facial analysis. In addition, the possibility of observer bias cannot be excluded as facial phenotyping was undertaken by one observer within a single sitting. Inter‐rater reliability was not assessed as the study design sought to mirror clinical practice, in which syndromic suspicion is typically determined during a single clinical encounter rather than through repeated or standardised assessments. While the facial images of the TCS patients are predominantly taken during childhood, we were also unable to perform age‐matching. Nonetheless, this may be reflective of the actual situation where initial suspicion of a child's facial features to systemic conditions or syndromes are not related to the age of the patient. Regardless of the patient's age or racial group, the ideal goal for either clinician or AI will still be to promptly and confidently recognise specific facial gestalts and associate it with relevant syndromes [15].

Further refinement of current AI algorithms for facial dysmorphology can include considerations for analysis of extra‐ocular regions such as the peri‐oral zone in the frontal view. In addition, AI‐assisted analysis of 2D photos taken from the lateral view or even 3‐dimensional views [27] may potentially increase the recognition of certain syndromes. Some of these features, such as forehead convexity, lip profile, nasal prominence, and chin prominence, are better evaluated from a profile view [28].

In addition, we hope that the collection and publication of larger datasets from non‐White syndromic cohorts can improve existing and future model performance. Via this study, clinicians should have increased awareness of how AI models are developed and if they are validated via training datasets representative of their own patient populations. A previous study has underscored the importance of analyzing how the make‐up of training datasets can influence model performance and impact AI generalisability [29]. Where possible, models should ideally be externally validated on local populations to ensure validity and generalisability.

This study reflects both the potential and current limitations of AI‐assisted diagnosis in identifying TCS amongst racial groups. Better correlation of specific facial features with diagnosis helps to inform clinical phenotyping and support earlier referral for multidisciplinary care [24]. With respect to TCS patients, this allows them to receive appropriate management by orthodontists & craniomaxillofacial surgeons specifically in the management of craniofacial growth, occlusal development, airway management, orthodontics and surgical planning. This underscores the need for improved diagnostic AI models to account for racial‐group variability of TCS and potentially other craniofacial dysmorphologies.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that evaluates the clinical presentation of facial deformities in Treacher Collins Syndrome together with facial analysis technology in diverse populations. Despite the subtle variations in facial features of TCS among races, F2G maintained a high overall diagnostic accuracy of 93.7%. Indeed, F2G can be a promising clinical screening tool for earlier identification of TCS throughout diverse populations. This is especially so in countries without access to genetic services or testing. However, there is still room for improvement by further training the model on non‐White images to improve its confidence in the diagnosis of Asian individuals with TCS. This will allow the model to be more generalisable and provide greater equitability across healthcare settings around the world.
