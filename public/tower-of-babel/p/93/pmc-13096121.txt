Prediction model of postoperative mortality for congenital heart disease: evidence from two large-scale cohorts
Authors: Jia An, Xueying Du, Zihao Bai, Liang Zou, Yang Xu, Di Yu, Xuming Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to identify the risk factors for postoperative mortality in patients under 3 years of age with congenital heart disease (CHD) undergoing cardiac surgery and to develop a predictive nomogram for clinical use. We retrospectively analyzed data from 3409 patients under 3 years who underwent cardiopulmonary bypass surgery. Predictors including baseline characteristics, surgical details, and laboratory parameters were select via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation. Variables with non-zero coefficients were incorporated into a multivariable logistic regression model to construct the final prediction nomogram. Six independent predictors of postoperative mortality were identified: preoperative serum creatinine (OR = 1.045, 95%CI 1.032-1.059), total protein (TP, OR =0.902[0.865-0.939]), glucose (OR = 1.201[1.095-1.317]), triglycerides (OR = 1.569[1.239-2.000]), as well as postoperative lactate (OR = 1.244[1.173-1.324]) and cystatin C (OR = 4.012[1.951-7.744]). The nomogram demonstrated excellent discrimination in the internal validation cohort (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC] = 0.952[0.932–0.972]) with good calibration. External validation yielded a AUC of 0.761 (0.621–0.901) and acceptable calibration. The decision curve analysis confirmed the clinical utility of the nomogram across a wide range of threshold probabilities. We developed and validated a nomogram incorporating sex routinely available clinical variables to stratify postoperative mortality risk in young CHD patients. Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common birth defect, with an incidence of approximately 10 per 1000 live births (1–1.2%)1. Notably, in the United States, the one-year survival rate for complex CHD is 75.2%, while that for simple CHD is 97.1%2. Advances in the diagnosis and treatment of CHD have led to continued improvements in patient survival and a decline in mortality. According to the 35th report of the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) Congenital Heart Surgery Database (CHSD), the overall unadjusted mortality rate for CHD patients was 2.68%, with neonates having the highest mortality rate (7.4%) and children having the lowest rate (1.1%)3. Younger age4, low body weight5, and prolonged duration of cardiopulmonary bypass have been identified as independent risk factors influencing postoperative mortality6. Therefore, improving long-term postoperative survival remains a key clinical priority.

Several models exist for predicting postoperative mortality risk, including the Risk Adjustment for Congenital Heart Surgery-1 (RACHS-1)7, Risk Adjustment for Congenital Heart Surgery-2 (RACHS-2)8, Society of Thoracic Surgeons and European Association for Cardiothoracic Surgery (STS-EACTS) score9, and the Aristotle Comprehensive Complexity (ACC) score10. However, the accuracy of these models in predicting postoperative mortality within specific age groups remains unclear. As these scoring systems rely heavily on subjective assessments of surgical complexity, and variations in surgical techniques across centers may affect their predictive performance, accurately assessing the true postoperative mortality risk in CHD patients remains challenging. Furthermore, none of these models incorporate routine laboratory test results or preoperative and postoperative clinical status, leading to variability in their effectiveness across different centers. The exclusion of preoperative laboratory data, which have been extensively shown to be associated with postoperative mortality, may contribute to the inconsistent performance of these models across different cohorts11.

This study aims to provide a systematic assessment to more accurately predict postoperative mortality risk in CHD patients under three years of age. By collecting data on patient demographic characteristic, preoperative laboratory tests, detailed surgical information, and the first postoperative laboratory results, we aim to develop a postoperative mortality risk assessment model. This model will facilitate early identification of high-risk patients and support clinical decision-making.

Methods

Ethics statement

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Children’s Hospital (Approval No. 202312008-1). Access to the Pediatric Intensive Care (PIC) database was obtained after completing the required training and certification examination (Certification No. 57245534). As a retrospective observational study that involved no interventions, used existing anonymous data, and maintained strict protection of patient privacy, the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Children’s Hospital waived the requirement for informed consent. All patient data were anonymized prior to analysis, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the relevant ethical guidelines and regulations.

Data sources and processing

This retrospective study was conducted using patient data from two CHD centers in China. The training cohort comprised patients treated at Nanjing Children’s Hospital between January 2017 and January 2024, while the external validation cohort was derived from the PIC database, which contains detailed records of 12,881 patients from the Children’s Hospital of Zhejiang University spanning 2010–201812. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of CHD according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes; (2) age ≤ 3 years at the time of surgery; and (3) having undergone cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) surgery. Patients were excluded (1) if they required a secondary cardiac operation during the same hospitalization, and (2) patients with > 30% missing data across all candidate variables. For data with ≤ 30% missingness, multiple imputation was performed using the ‘mice’ package (version 3.16.0) in R. Continuous variables were imputed via predictive mean matching, and categorical variables via logistic regression imputation, generating five imputed datasets. The imputation model incorporated all analytical variables, and final model estimates were pooled according to Rubin’s rules.

Following patients’ selection, comprehensive data on patient characteristics, surgical and clinical outcomes were collected, yielding a total of 98 potentially information features. These comprised key demographic and surgical variables, including age, sex, surgical duration, and RACHS-1 surgical grade, as well as preoperative and first postoperative laboratory parameters such as white blood cell (WBC) count, C-reactive protein (CRP), lymphocyte count, monocyte count, neutrophil count, eosinophil count, basophil count, glucose, triglyceride, total protein (TP), platelet count, red blood cell (RBC) count, RBC distribution width, platelet distribution width (PDW), creatinine, urea, cystatin C, lactate, and various hepatic and renal function indicators. Supplementary Table S1 presents the proportions of key missing variables in two cohorts.

Outcome

We developed a model to predict operative mortality, which was defined as death in-hospital or within 30-days of surgery if discharge prior to 30 postoperative days13.

Predictive variable selection and model building

In the training cohort, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was first applied to all 98 candidate variables. The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was selected via 10-fold cross-validation using the one-standard-error (1-SE) rule to obtain a parsimonious model. Variables with non-zero coefficients at this λ value were remained and directly used to build a logistic regression prediction model to derive odds ratios (OR) with conventional confidence intervals (CI). Based on the coefficients of this logistic regression model, a nomogram for predicting postoperative mortality risk was constructed. Model discrimination was assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) along with its 95% CI. Calibration was evaluated using a calibration curve and the Hosmer-Lemeshow (H-L) goodness-of-fit test. The clinical net benefit of the model was examined by decision curve analysis. Internal validation was performed via 1000 bootstrap resampling to calculate optimism-corrected estimates of the AUC and calibration slope, and the corrected performance metrics are reported.

In the external validation cohort, the generalizability of the model was comprehensively evaluated: the AUC with its 95% CI was computed, a calibration curve was plotted and the H-L statistic was calculated. The optimal cutoff values for continuous variables were determined by maximizing Youden’s index.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed continuous variables with homogeneous variance are presented as mean (standard deviation, SD), and group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test. Continuous variables that did not follow a normal distribution or exhibited heterogeneous variance are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR), and group comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as number (percentage), and group comparisons were carried out using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test (when expected frequencies were < 5). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed using R software (version 4.2.2).

Results

Baseline information

A total of 3409 CHD patients under 3 years of age who underwent CPB surgery were included from two independent CHD cohorts. The training cohort consisted of 2368 CHD patients (including 44 deaths) recruited from Nanjing Children’s Hospital between January 2017 and January 2024. The validation cohort comprised 1041 CHD patients (including 18 deaths) identified from the PIC database between 2010 and 2018. The median age was 348 days (IQR: 163-663.50) in the training cohort and 215.49 days (68.59–444.39) in the validation cohort (Supplementary Table S2). Risk factor analysis

LASSO regression analysis identified six independent risk factors for postoperative mortality from the 98 variables examined in the training cohort. Preoperative serum creatinine (OR = 1.045[1.032–1.059], P < 0.001), glucose (OR = 1.201[1.095–1.317], P < 0.001), triglycerides (OR = 1.569[1.239–2.000], P < 0.001), postoperative lactate (OR = 1.244[1.173–1.324], P < 0.001), and cystatin C (OR = 4.012[1.951–7.744], P < 0.001) were positively associated with mortality. In contrast, preoperative serum TP (OR = 0.902[0.865–0.939], P < 0.001) was negatively correlated with postoperative mortality (Fig. 1).

Forest plot of independent predictors of postoperative mortality. Multivariable logistic regression identified six independent predictors. Odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) are shown.

Nomogram development

These six candidate variables were incorporated into a nomogram to stratify postoperative mortality risk in the training cohort (Fig. 2). In this nomogram, a higher total score corresponds to a greater risk of mortality. For example, a patient with the following values would be assigned the corresponding points: preoperative serum creatinine 40 µmol/L (28 points), preoperative TP 20 g/L (60 points), preoperative glucose 4 mmol/L (22 points), triglycerides 2 mmol/L (27 points), postoperative lactate 5 mmol/L (27 points), and cystatin C 1 mg/L (24 points). A total score of 166 points corresponds to a predicted mortality probability of 4.9%.

Clinical nomogram for postoperative mortality risk estimation. The tool integrates six routinely available predictors (preoperative creatinine, total protein, glucose, triglycerides, and postoperative lactate, cystatin C). The summed points from the top scale correspond to an individualized risk probability on the bottom scale, facilitating bedside assessment.

Predictive accuracy and net benefit of the nomogram

In the training cohort, the model demonstrated an AUC of 0.952 (0.932–0.972), with a corresponding sensitivity of 95.5% and specificity of 84.7% (Fig. 3A). After optimism correction using 1000 bootstrap replicates, the adjusted AUC was 0.949 with an optimism of 0.003, indicating only minimal overfitting and preserved excellent discriminative ability after correction. The calibration curve showed close agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, and the H-L test yielded a non-significant result (P = 0.458), suggesting good model fit in the training data (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, decision curve analysis confirmed that the predictive model provided a substantial net benefit in clinical decision-making (Fig. 5).

External validation

The prediction model exhibited acceptable discriminative performance in estimating postoperative mortality risk, with an AUC of 0.761 (0.621–0.901) in the validation cohort. The corresponding sensitivity and specificity were 66.7% and 85.2%, respectively (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, the model demonstrated moderate calibration consistency, as evidenced by a calibration curve that aligned with the ideal diagonal line, along with a non-significant H-L test result (P = 0.426) (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

This study identified six independent predictors of postoperative mortality in children under 3 years of age with CHD. Based on these six variables, a nomogram was developed to estimate the risk of postoperative mortality, which demonstrated acceptable discriminative ability (AUC) and calibration in the external validation cohort. Decision curve analysis confirmed the clinical utility of the nomogram. During the modeling process, we incorporated preoperative laboratory test results, details of the surgical procedure, and the results of the first postoperative laboratory tests to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the patient’s condition and to enhance the accuracy of our model. Additionally, all six variables were readily measurable and commonly used in daily clinical practice, indicating the broad applicability of the model. Existing models for predicting postoperative mortality, such as the RACHS-1 score, were primarily designed to assess mortality risk associated with the surgical procedure itself7. In this study, the RACHS-1 score achieved an AUC of 0.578 (0.520–0.635), with an optimal cutoff value of 2.5, suggesting limited ability to predict postoperative mortality in children under 3 years of age with CHD. Compared with previous studies, the RACHS-1 score performed less effectively in our cohort, which may be due to the broader age distribution of patients included in prior research, whereas this study focused on children under 3 years of age. The influence of age on the RACHS-1 score has not yet been reported, and it remains to be determined whether the poor predictive performance of the RACHS-1 score in our cohort was due to limitations of the model itself or differences in age. On the other hand, scores such as RACHS-1 and STS-EACTS mainly rely on static anatomical variables (e.g., diagnosis, age, surgical method)14. In contrast, our model integrates dynamic physiological and biochemical markers (e.g., lactate, creatinine, TP), which directly reflect the patient’s baseline physiological state and early postoperative metabolic disturbances, organ dysfunction, and stress levels. Therefore, this model does not replicate or replace existing preoperative anatomical scoring systems but rather provides a ‘physiological lens’ focusing on the preoperative baseline status and early postoperative response. For instance, a patient with a moderate RACHS-1 score may have a significantly elevated actual risk if severe metabolic acidosis and renal dysfunction occur postoperatively. Our model can identify such patients with a mismatch between ‘anatomical risk’ and ‘physiological response’, enabling secondary risk stratification. This is important for identifying high-risk subgroups requiring intensive monitoring and facilitating targeted interventions.

Model performance and generalizability

Although LASSO regularization was applied for variable selection in the training cohort, we acknowledge that the exceptionally high performance in training-particularly given the limited number of events-may still suggest potential overfitting. To partially address this concern, additional internal validation was performed to assess the issue more robustly. After optimism correction using 1000 bootstrap replicates, the adjusted AUC was 0.949 with an optimism of 0.003, indicating only minimal overfitting and preserved excellent discriminative ability after correction. Furthermore, we compared the baseline characteristics between the training and external validation cohorts and observed significant differences in age distribution, operative duration, and the proportion of certain CHD types (based on RACHS-1 score) (all P < 0.05), as detailed in Supplementary Table S2. These differences may partially explain the decline in performance observed during external validation. Additionally, the limited number of endpoint events (n = 18) in the validation cohort increases uncertainty in performance estimation and may underestimate the true generalizability of the model.

Role of age and weight in model selection

Additionally, we observed in the univariable logistic regression analysis of the training cohort that younger age (OR = 0.995[0.993–0.997], P < 0.001) and lower body weight (OR = 0.697[0.614–0.784], P < 0.001) were both significant predictors of postoperative mortality. However, neither variable was selected by the LASSO algorithm during multivariable modeling that included laboratory indicators. This outcome may be attributed to the following factors: first, age and weight were highly correlated (r = 0.79, P < 0.05), resulting in substantial multicollinearity, which LASSO regression effectively penalizes and filters; second, both age and weight showed significant correlations with several laboratory indicators, particularly between age and total protein (r = 0.30, P < 0.05), age and cystatin C (r = -0.60, P < 0.05), and weight and cystatin C (r = -0.56, P < 0.05). Mediation analysis further indicated that cystatin C partially mediated the relationship between age and postoperative mortality risk, accounting for 15.52% of the indirect effect. When age or weight was forcibly incorporated into the final model, neither significantly improved the model’s discriminative ability (AUC after adding age: 0.952 vs. 0.920, DeLong test P = 0.701; AUC after adding weight: 0.952 vs. 0.920, DeLong test P = 0.620) nor enhanced the model’s goodness of fit (likelihood ratio test for age: χ² = 3.588, P = 0.058; for weight: χ² = 3.578, P = 0.059). These findings suggest that, in this cohort, the predictive information provided by laboratory indicators overlaps substantially with that of age and weight, and laboratory indicators may more directly reflect physiological immaturity than chronological age or weight alone. In critically ill pediatric patients, the role of age and weight alone in accurately capturing physiological reserve and adaptive capacity remains to be fully elucidated. The model based on laboratory indicators essentially attempts to quantify this ‘physiological age’ or functional maturity. Therefore, relying on objective laboratory data available at admission, rather than solely on age or weight, may be more applicable in clinical contexts where physiological maturity varies significantly within the same age group.

Risk factors

Preoperative optimization of patient health status has been shown to be associated with improved short- and long-term outcomes15. The predictive model developed in this study incorporates six indicators, four of which are preoperative variables: low serum TP (OR = 0.902[0.865–0.939], P < 0.001), serving as a marker of nutritional deficiency and impaired immune function16, demonstrated a AUC was 0.712 (0.622–0.801) for predicting postoperative mortality, which optimal cutoff value of 57.95 g/L; Elevated serum creatinine level (OR = 1.045[1.032–1.059], P < 0.001) was independently associated with postoperative mortality, consistent with previous studies17, highlighting the significant impact of renal function status on prognosis; high fasting blood glucose (OR = 1.021[1.095–1.317], P < 0.001) and high triglyceride (OR = 1.569[1.239–2.000], P < 0.001) collectively reflected preoperative metabolic disturbances and insulin resistance. It is noteworthy that the triglyceride glucose (TyG) index did not demonstrate superior predictive value in this study (OR = 1.130[0.761–1.364], P = 0.243), which may be attributed to the metabolic characteristics of the pediatric population and sample size considerations18.

Among the early postoperative indicators, cystatin C and lactate exhibited strong predictive capability for mortality risk. Cystatin C, a renal function marker unaffected by nutritional status, reflects multiple pathophysiological processes such as inflammation and oxidative stress when elevated19,20. In this cohort, its AUC for predicting mortality risk was 0.791 (0.717–0.864), with an optimal cutoff of 1.055 mg/L. Lactate, a sensitive indicator of tissue perfusion and oxygenation, showed an AUC of 0.759 (0.672–0.846) at a cutoff value of 4.280 mmol/L, consistent with the literature where > 4.0 mmol/L serves as a high-risk threshold21.

By integrating preoperative metabolic reserve indicators with early postoperative physiological response markers, this model establishes a dynamic risk assessment system spanning the critical perioperative phase. Preoperative TP, creatinine, blood glucose, and triglycerides collectively delineate the patient’s baseline metabolic and organ functional reserve, while postoperative cystatin C and lactate provide real-time reflection of organ functional changes and circulatory-metabolic status induced by surgical stress. This multi-temporal combination of indicators not only covers the entire continuum from baseline state to stress response but also enables the identification of high-risk subgroups that may be overlooked by traditional anatomical scoring systems-specifically, children with moderate anatomical risk but significantly compromised physiological compensatory capacity.

From a clinical translation perspective, this model provides a clear pathway for individualized intervention: preoperative efforts should focus on optimizing nutritional status (TP > 57.95 g/L), controlling blood glucose and lipid levels, and managing renal function according to KDIGO guidelines; postoperatively, close monitoring of lactate and cystatin C levels is essential, with timely initiation of targeted supportive therapy for values exceeding the thresholds. The visual risk stratification tool based on the nomogram assists surgeons in implementing phased interventions at different perioperative stages, thereby improving outcomes for high-risk children.

All variables in this model are derived from routine clinical tests, ensuring good feasibility. Although each indicator individually possesses predictive value, the six-variable combined model demonstrates superior discriminative performance and clinical applicability. This model provides a comprehensive framework for postoperative risk management in children under 3 years of age with CHD, spanning from physiological assessment to intervention decision-making, thereby contributing to the advancement of perioperative care toward precision and individualization.

This study has several limitations. First, although the model has been validated using data from an external pediatric center, enhancing its reliability, all study participants were from China and the sample size was limited. The generalizability of the nomogram to global populations still requires further validation through multicenter, large-scale international prospective studies. Second, to focus on assessing physiological vulnerability based on admission and early postoperative characteristics, we excluded patients who underwent secondary surgery during hospitalization. While this avoided interference from unpredictable intraoperative events, it also limited the model’s applicability to high-risk subgroups experiencing major postoperative complications. Such patients typically enter different clinical decision-making pathways, and future research should develop tailored predictive tools for this specific population.

Regarding the exclusion of age and weight from the model: The absence of age and weight in the model does not imply that these factors are unimportant in pediatric cardiac care. They remain crucial for surgical planning, medication dosage calculation, and prognostic counseling. Instead, our findings suggest that for early mortality risk prediction at admission, laboratory indicators provide more immediate and comprehensive risk information. Although mediation analysis suggests that laboratory indicators may capture the influence of age and weight, there may still be unmeasured confounding factors. For instance, extremely young infants might receive different surgical strategies or postoperative management protocols, which could independently affect outcomes.

Finally, while laboratory testing methods and the definitions of mortality outcomes remained consistent across the study period, the training cohort was derived from our institutional database spanning 2017–2024, whereas the external validation cohort was obtained from the PIC database covering 2010–2018. Temporal differences in surgical techniques and perioperative management between these two periods may exist and represent potential factors to consider when generalizing the model.

Conclusions

Through an analysis of CHD patients under 3 years of age who underwent CPB surgery, this study identified six independent predictors of postoperative mortality. These variables were used to construct a nomogram that effectively stratifies patients according to postoperative mortality risk, demonstrating consistent discriminative performance in an external validation cohort. Proactive monitoring and timely management of postoperative serum creatinine, TP, glucose, and triglyceride levels, as well as the first postoperative lactate and cystatin C values, may contribute to reducing postoperative mortality risk in this vulnerable population.
