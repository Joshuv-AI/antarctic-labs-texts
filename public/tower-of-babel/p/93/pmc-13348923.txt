Isolation of Bacteria and Fungi from Human Pancreatic Tumors and Duodenum

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma has a unique tumor microbiome, and the depletion of gut bacteria or fungi using antibiotic/antifungal cocktails has been shown to decrease pancreatic tumor burden in mice. However, functional studies evaluating the role of tumor-associated microbes are few due to the limited availability of clinically relevant microbiota. Here, we describe in detail an effective workflow for the isolation of bacteria and fungi from the duodenum and tumor of pancreatic cancer patients, specifically optimized for cryopreserved, low biomass samples. Using this workflow we also isolated microbiota from normal pancreatic tissue and duodenum from organ donors, and we confirmed the presence of bacteria and fungi isolated from tissue samples with 16S and ITS sequencing analysis. Isolation and sequencing results show distinct similarities between the pancreatic and duodenal microbiomes and highlight unique bacterial strains that survive in the tumor microenvironment. As a proof of concept, we characterized a select Klebsiella oxytoca strain (UMKO1) isolated from a pancreatic tumor, using whole genome sequencing, metabolomics, and ex- vivo tumor cultures to determine its potential impact on the pancreatic tumor microenvironment. In summary, this optimized workflow allows for the isolation of a variety of bacteria and fungi from low biomass, cryopreserved pancreatic and duodenal tissues, which can then be used for functional studies characterizing clinically relevant tumor-associated microbiota.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is one of the most lethal cancers in the US with an overall 5-year survival rate of 13%. 1 This is in part due to the lack of biomarkers for early detection and its resistance to available chemo- and immunotherapy agents. PDAC cells are surrounded by a dense stroma, mainly consisting of cancer-associated fibroblasts and immunosuppressive myeloid populations. However, PDAC has also been shown to contain a unique microbiome that differs from the healthy pancreas. 2-8 Several studies have observed an increase in bacterial and fungal burden in human pancreatic tumors. 2-6 , 9-12 16S sequencing analysis of the bacteria in murine and human tumors suggests an enrichment of Gammaproteobacteria, 2 , 3 , 12 specifically Klebsiella and Enterobacter species. 12 Similarly, ITS sequencing has identified Ascomycota (Candida sp.) and Basidiomycota (Malassezia sp.) as the most dominant fungi in the pancreatic tumor. 4 , 5 , 10 , 13 In addition to the discovery of these unique microbial niches in the PDAC microenvironment, recent studies have correlated clinical outcomes to the composition of the tumor microbiome. One study found that long-term patient survivors of pancreatic cancer (approximately 10 year of survival after cancer surgery) harbor increased diversity in their tumor microbiome, as well as an enrichment of several bacteria from the Bacillus, Pseudoxanthomonas, Saccharopolyspora, and Streptomyces genera. 6 Furthermore, repopulation of the gut microbiome of tumor-bearing mice with fecal microbiome transplants (FMT) from short-term patient survivors accelerates tumor growth. 6 A similar study identified that the presence of Malassezia globosa in PDAC tumors correlated with worse survival and worse response to chemotherapy in pancreatic cancer patients. 11 Ablation of the gut and tumor microbiome in murine models for pancreatic cancer via oral antibiotic and antifungal cocktails, leads to reduced tumor burden in multiple studies, and this phenotype is reversed with murine or human fecal microbiota transplants (FMT). 3-6 , 8 , 14

Gut and tumor microbes are believed to exert their effect on PDAC progression via several immunological and metabolic mechanisms. Gammaproteobacteria species present in the gut and tumor of PDAC patients have been shown to metabolize Gemcitabine, a chemotherapy agent commonly used to treat PDAC, thereby mediating tumor resistance to the drug. 2 In fact, the colonization of intraoperative bile fluid with Gammaproteobacteria was found to negatively correlate with survival outcomes, such that only patients receiving adjuvant Gemcitabine treatment had worse survival if intraoperative bile cultures were positive for Gammaproteobacteria. 15 The most common bacteria cultured within the bile were Klebsiella pneumonia, Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecium, and Enterobacter cloacae. 15 The presence of tumor-associated microbes has also been shown to drive adaptive immunosuppression in the tumor microenvironment (TME), supporting cancer cell growth and tumor progression. Ablation of gut and tumor bacteria in murine models results in decreased expansion of immunosuppressive macrophage populations and more infiltration of cytotoxic T cell populations in the TME. 3 , 8 Fungal populations support type 2 immunity in PDAC by modulating IL-33 production from cancer cells, resulting in the expansion of tumor-promoting TH2 cells in the PDAC TME. 5 However, gut microbial effects on tumor immunity are not unique to pancreatic cancer. Gut bacteria have been shown to modulate immunotherapy response in melanoma patients 16 , 17 and commensal Candida species in the gut have been implicated in driving myeloid-derived suppressor cells in colorectal cancers. 18 Gut microbes are believed to exert their immunomodulatory effects via their metabolic products. Bacteria-derived tryptophan metabolites drive innate immunosuppression in the PDAC TME and their presence in the blood of PDAC patients correlate with response to chemotherapy. 19 , 20 The metabolism of dietary choline to Trimethylamine N-oxide by gut bacteria has been shown to drive anti-tumor immunity in the PDAC TME, 21 and serum products of polyamine metabolism by gut Proteobacteria parallel PDAC development, possibly serving as early biomarkers for the disease. 9 However, our understanding of specific mechanistic pathways via which select microbes and their metabolites modulate tumor progression is limited by the field’s inability to study tumor-specific microbial strains.

The low biomass of microbes in pancreatic tumors limits our capability to obtain reliable metagenomic sequencing, hindering our ability to gain insight into the biological behavior of tumor-specific microbes. Functional studies to date have utilized gut fecal microbiome repopulation studies in mice, demonstrating microbial translocation from the gut to tumors. 4 , 5 While these studies have revealed important insights into the potential roles of the gut and tumor microbiome, our understanding of the behaviors of specific bacteria or fungi that reside in the TME is lacking. It is well established that the behavior of bacteria and fungi, including their virulence, pathogenicity, and ability to colonize host tissues, varies widely even within the same species. 22-25 Therefore, functional studies using microbiota isolated directly from pancreatic cancer patients and tumors are necessary to understand the protumorigenic capacity of gut bacteria and fungi that translocate to the tumor. To address this gap in the field, we have established an optimized workflow to isolate bacteria and fungi from the duodenum and pancreatic tumors of pancreatic cancer patients. We also applied this workflow to healthy pancreatic and duodenal tissues from organ donors. We demonstrate herein, which is the completed work of a previously published preprint, 26 that we are able to cultivate various bacteria and fungi species from cryopreserved, low-biomass pancreatic and duodenal tissues. Using this workflow, we isolated Klebsiella oxytoca from a PDAC tumor, which we further characterized to assess the unique properties of this tumor-isolated strain, showing the potential for future functional studies on tumor-specific strains. With this manuscript, we offer a methodological process for isolating a variety of bacteria and fungi from small (<500 mg), cryopreserved, low-biomass samples, thereby creating a collection of clinically relevant microbial strains to study the contribution of gut microbes to the pancreatic tumor microenvironment.

Materials and methods

Tissue collection

Tumor and duodenum from pancreatic cancer patients

Pancreatic tumor and adjacent duodenum tissue were collected from pancreatic cancer patients undergoing surgery at the University of Michigan Rogel and Blondy Center for Pancreatic Cancer. Tissue was collected immediately after surgical resection using clinical-grade aseptic technique under the approved IRB protocol (HUM00025339). The tissue was either flash frozen or frozen in cryotubes containing RPMI media and stored at −80 °C until the start of the isolation. Additional tissue samples were flash frozen for 16S/ITS sequencing.

Donor pancreata

Donor pancreata and duodenum used for this study were collected through the Gift of Life Michigan Donor Care Center. After donor tissue harvest, the tissue was sectioned as previously described for research purposes. 27 The use for scientific research was approved by the Gift of Life research review group. For microbial isolation, we used the section of the duodenum (small intestine closest to the pancreas) and tissue from the head of the pancreas unless otherwise stated.

16S and ITS sequencing

DNA was extracted and purified from tissue samples using the Qiagen MagAttract PowerMicrobiome kit (cat #27500-4-EP). Sample tissues were added to each well of the PowerBead DNA plate under sterile conditions, and the plate was covered tightly with the sealing mat. Ten blank wells in the PowerBead DNA plate were also included in DNA isolation and sequencing as background controls to detect any contaminants introduced during tissue transfer or preparation of the tissue for DNA isolation. Water controls and positive controls were also included for the DNA isolation and sequencing process. The V4 region of the 16S rRNA-encoding gene was PCR-amplified from purified DNA using previously published primers and a dual indexing system. 28 The internally transcribed spacer (ITS) between the 18S and 5.8S rRNA-encoding genes of fungi (ITS1) was amplified using dual indexing primers. 29 PCR products were visualized using an E-Gel 96 with SYBR Safe DNA Gel Stain, 2% (Life Technologies cat# G7208-02). Libraries were normalized using the SequalPrep Normalization Plate Kit (Life Technologies cat # A10510-01) following the manufacturer’s protocol for sequential elution. The concentration of the pooled samples was determined using Kapa Biosystems Library Quantification kit for Illumina platforms (KapaBiosystems KK4824). The sizes of the amplicons in the libraries were determined using the Agilent Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA analysis kit (cat #5067-4626). The final library consists of equal molar amounts from each of the plates, normalized to the pooled plate at the lowest concentration. Sequencing was done on the Illumina MiSeq platform using a MiSeq Reagent Kit V2 500-cycles (Illumina cat# MS-102-2003) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with minor modification. 28

16S and ITS analysis

16S rRNA gene sequence analysis

Sequences were processed using mothur (v.1.48.0) (10.1128/AEM.01541-09) following the Schloss Lab MiSeq standard operating procedure 28 (https://mothur.org/wiki/miseq_sop/). Briefly, paired reads were combined and aligned to the SILVA reference database (v132). 28 Taxonomy was assigned using Ribosomal Database Project reference sequences (version 19). A 3% dissimilarity cutoff was used to cluster sequences with Opticlust 30 into operational taxonomic units (OTUs). The mock community sequenced in parallel with our samples had an error rate of 0.065%.

ITS gene sequence analysis

Sequences were processed using mothur (v.1.48.0). 31 Paired reads were combined, then screened for any ambiguous bases, sequence lengths > 600 bp, and homopolymers greater than 8 bp. Then, sequences were preclustered with 2 or fewer differences. Chimeric sequences were removed prior to classifying sequences with the UNITE database (version 10). 32 Sequences with 97% identity were clustered into OTUs.

Decontamination of OTUs

For both 16S and ITS data, OTUs were classified as contamination if they were present in water controls, background controls, or other taxa than those expected in the mock community at levels similar to or greater than those in the samples. OTUs present in the controls were kept if the population was at least 10-fold greater in the samples. OTUs were eliminated if they had fewer than 5 counts or a relative abundance less than 0.02. 33 OTUs were then grouped and summed by genus or phylum and analyzed in R (version 4.4.2) using packages Tidyverse (version 2.0.0) 34 and schtools (version 0.4.1). 35 Additional decontamination steps were applied based on Eckhoff/Fletcher as follows: For 16S, only genera previously identified as being non-contaminants (Rothia, Rhodococcus, Haemophilus, Pseudoxanthomonas, Cloacibacterium, Bifidobacterium, Enterobacter, Enterococcus, Clostridium, Clostridium sensu stricto, Brachybacterium, Bacteroides, Cutibacterium) were included in downstream analysis. 33

Tissue processing for microbial isolation

Tissues previously frozen in RPMI were thawed on ice, and flash-frozen tissues were thawed on ice after the addition of sterile RPMI. Tissues were then cut into equal-sized pieces in a Baker Class II Biosafety Cabinet designated for BSL2 microbes under sterile conditions. Tissue samples were weighed to ensure consistency in the size of tissue used for each isolation. Pancreas tissue used for isolation averaged 20–30 mg in weight, and duodenal samples used for isolation averaged 200–400 mg in weight. After dissection, tissue pieces were added to individual media plates and streaked with sterile forceps before moving the plates to the hypoxic chamber. For anoxic conditions, the tissues were streaked in the anoxic chamber directly. To detect any contamination during the isolation/culture process, we also plated the following as controls: (1) the sterile RPMI media that was used for thawing tissue samples, (2) PBS solution used to rinse the sterile instruments used for preparing and sectioning samples, and (3) empty/unused media plates placed in the sterile biosafety hood at the time of streaking to detect any environmental contamination within the hood or media plates. All three sets of controls did not show any growth when incubated. Plates were incubated in Coy glove boxes (Coy Laboratory Products Inc., Grass Lake, MI) for isolation and cultivation of microbes at 37 °C under anoxic (5% CO2, 1.5 to 3.5% H2 and N2 balance) and hypoxic (2% O2, 5% CO2, balance N2) conditions. Mixed culture plates were inspected for colony formation every 12 hours, and colonies were restreaked to avoid overgrowth. Early optimization work of this isolation process did not show any benefit of plating or spotting the concentrated RPMI solution from tissue storage onto plates.

MALDI-TOF

Colonies of isolated bacteria and fungi were added directly to a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube using a 1 µL loop, along with 300 µL of UHPLC water. 900 µL of absolute ethanol was then added, and the colonies were centrifuged at 13,500 rpm for 2 min. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was dried on a heating block at 40 °C for 15 min. Once dried, the pellet was resuspended in 25 µL of 70% formic acid and mixed by pipetting. Following this, 25 µL of acetonitrile was added, and again mixed by pipetting. The suspension was then centrifuged at 13,500 rpm for 2 min. A total of 1 µL of the supernatant was transferred to an MBT Biotarget 96 Chip (Bruker), air-dried, and overlaid with 1 µL of HCCA matrix solution. Once dried, the plate was transferred to the MALDI-TOF MS instrument (MALDI Biotyper Sirius) and analyzed using the MBT Compass and Flex Control software.

16S and ITS amplicon sequencing

Pure microbial isolates that could not successfully be identified via MALDI-TOF analysis, were used for 16S and ITS sequencing. The DNA was isolated using the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (Cat# 51804) with an additional heat incubation and increased bead beating for fungal isolates, as suggested by the manufacturer. The 16S/ITS PCR amplicon 28 , 29 was then analyzed via the NIH blastn suite (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) using the rRNA/ITS databases.

Genome sequencing of K. oxytoca (UMKO1)

Bacterial genome sequencing was performed by Plasmidsaurus using Oxford Nanopore Technology with custom analysis and annotation. The sequencing of University of Michigan Klebsiella oxytoca 1 (UMKO1) covered a total of 722,506,331 bp with 204,426 total reads, while the longest read was 76,682 bp. The raw coverage was 115x, assembly coverage was 104x, genome size was 6.2 Mb with 2 contigs, and a total of 5944 genes annotated. Contig 1 was identified as species NZ_KQ088007.1 Klebsiella oxytoca strain CHS143 aetza-supercont1.1 with 6,070,641 bp and est. %ID of 99.8; contig 2 was identified as Klebsiella oxytoca strain CAV1335 plasmid pCAV1335-115 with 173,004 bp and est. %ID 97.2.

FASTA files for other Klebsiella strains were downloaded from the National Library of Medicine Nucleotide database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore). FASTA files were annotated using Prokka, 37 and the Genbank annotation files were visualized using Pan-genome Explorer. 38

Metabolomics

Polar metabolites were extracted from bacterial supernatant and media controls using methanol in water 80% v/v, dried, and reconstituted in methanol in water 50% v/v. A detailed description of the LC–MS/MS methods, including targeted and untargeted analyses, can be found in the supplemental methods. All raw data were deposited into the Metabolomics Workbench data repository (www.metabolomicsworkbench.org; Study IDs: ST004180, ST004157, ST004158).

Murine pancreatic cancer orthotopic tumor model and ex vivo system

The ex vivo system was modified from the previously published Air-liquid interface model. 39 In short, 10–14 week old mice (C57BL/6J) were kept on antibiotic water as previously described, 3 and then 50,000 syngeneic pancreatic KPC cells (7940B) were injected into the pancreas. The resulting orthotopic pancreatic tumors were resected from the mice 21 d post-transplantation. Upon extraction, tumors were added to Tumor Interstitial Fluid Media (TIFM) 40 , 41 on ice and sliced within 5 min to about 2–3 mm thickness using sterile scalpels before embedding in the ex vivo system. Tumor Interstitial Fluid Media (TIFM) is formulated to resemble the natural abundance of metabolites found in the murine pancreatic tumor interstitial fluid, including over 118 nutrients and vitamins, many of which are not present in standard media preparations. This includes physiological levels of glucose, glutamine, and metabolites such as lactate, pyruvate, TCA cycle intermediates, and glutathione. Also included is the low abundance of microbiome-derived metabolites such as indole lactic acid. A detailed list of the components of the TIFM is described by Saab et al. 41 The bottom layer of the ex-vivo system was created by combining 8 parts collagen (FujiFilm, Wako Chemicals, Cellmatrix Type I-A), with 1 part reconstitution buffer (2.2g NaHCO3 in 100 ml NaOH solution supplemented with 200 mM HEPES) and 1 part 10x TIFM supplemented with 10% dialyzed FBS. The top layer included only collagen (FujiFilm, Wako Chemicals, Cellmatrix Type I-A). For the outside media feeding the ex vivo system, we used 1:1 ratios of TIFM to bacterial supernatant. Bacterial supernatant was generated by culturing Klebsiella oxytoca, Klebsiella penumoniae, and Klebsiella variicola isolated in this study, as well as E. coli K12, as described in the metabolomics section. Since glucose was depleted in the supernatants collected from the bacteria, we supplemented the bacterial supernatant with 700 mg/l glucose (equivalent to the glucose concentration in TIFM). The ex vivo system did not contain any antibiotics or antifungals, and the media were changed every 18 hours. Endpoints were at 22 and 44 hours, at which time tissue was collected and fixed in Formalin-zinc solution for 24 hours before switching to 70% ethanol. The tissue was then paraffin-embedded and processed for histological evaluation.

Immunofluorescence

CD8 and Granzyme B staining

Paraffin-embedded sections were rehydrated by washing slides twice with xylene, twice with 100% ethanol, and twice with 95% ethanol. Sections were then washed once with water to remove residues from previous washes. Slides were subsequently microwaved in CITRA Plus (BioGenex) for antigen retrieval. For preparation of use of the Alexa Fluor™ 488 Tyramide SuperBoost™ Kit (ThermoFisher), sections were incubated in 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min at room temperature (RT) to neutralize endogenous peroxidase activity. Sections were then blocked in 10% goat serum for 30 min at RT, followed by incubation in primary antibody (Granzyme B [D2H2F], Cell Signaling) overnight at 4 °C. After 24 h, sections were incubated in Poly-HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for an hour at RT, followed by the Tyramide SuperBoost™ Amplification reaction as described in the kit’s protocol. Slides were once again microwaved in CITRA Plus and blocked in 1% BSA for 30 min at RT. Additional primary antibodies (CD8α [D4W2Z], Cell Signaling; E-Cadherin [4A2], Cell Signaling) were then added to the sections and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The following day, sections were incubated in secondary antibody for 45 min at RT, counterstained for DAPI (1:30,000) for 7 min at RT, and mounted with Prolong Diamond Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen). Images were taken using Olympus BX53F microscope of at least 3 random non-overlapping fields and quantified using ImageJ to measure the percentage of positive area and percentage of overlapping positive areas.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and Cytokeratin immunostaining were performed with slight modifications from the methods previously reported. 42 , 43 Briefly paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through a graded alcohol series. Sections were then hybridized at 50 °C overnight with probes EUB388 (5’-GCTGCCTCCCGTAGGAGT-3’) conjugated with Alexa 488 and Klebsiella (5’-CCTACACACCAGCGTGCC-3’) conjugated with Cy3 in hybridization buffer (20 mMTris-HCl, 0.9 M NaCl, 0.1% (w/v) SDS). Following hybridization, the slides were washed at 50 °C for 15 min in wash buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 0.9 M NaCl) and then blocked with 5% BSA in PBS at 4 °C for 30 min. The sections were then incubated with anti-Pan CK mouse monoclonal antibody (NBP2-44368, Novus Biologicals) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, tissue sections were incubated with Alexa 647 conjugated anti mouse secondary antibody (A32787, Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific) at room temperature for 1 h and Hoechst for 10 min. Autofluorescence was quenched by using autofluorescence quenching kit (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA), following manufacturer’s instruction. Observations were performed with a Leica Stellaris 5 confocal microscope.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using PRISM 10 for macOS, Version 10.5.0 (673), May 27, 2025.

Results

Isolation of bacteria and fungi from human pancreatic tumors and duodenum

Human pancreatic tissue (normal and tumor) and duodenum tissue samples were collected shortly after resection in a sterile operating room from patients and organ donors, as detailed in Table 1. The tissue samples were either flash frozen or flash frozen in sterile RPMI media and stored for up to 2 years after collection at −80 °C. A portion of some tissue samples was used for 16S and ITS sequencing to compare the bacteria and fungi isolated from tissue cultures to those identified by direct amplification and sequencing of tissues (Figure 1). A variety of media compositions were used to culture and enrich for specific fungi and bacteria (Table 2). These included routinely used media conditions modified to adjust for the higher pH found in the pancreas, or supplemented with nutrients to enrich for published PDAC-associated bacteria. 2 , 3 , 6 After the initial isolation step, pure colonies were isolated from the mixed colony plates, maintaining hypoxic or anoxic conditions to simulate the small intestine and pancreatic tumor environment. Although similar tissue weights were used across patients for isolation, we observed a great variety of colony forming units (CFU) between patients. In general, duodenal samples had more total CFUs than tumor samples (Figure 2A, B) and yielded more diverse cultivates than tumor samples (Figure 2C, D). Live microbes were isolated from two of the three tumor samples, and the majority of tumor isolates were obtained from one sample (Figure 2C, D). These culture yields were expected, as the only other published studies describing isolation of bacteria from pancreatic tumors and premalignant lesions have consistently demonstrated that only 24%–28% of cultured tumors yield live isolates, and only 11% of tumor cultures yielded more than 1 microbial species. 44-46 Microbes isolated from the tumor samples were also identified in the corresponding duodenal samples, indicating that tumor-associated microbes likely originated from the gut. Klebsiella (K. variicola, K. oxytoca, K. pneumonia, and K. aerogenes) and Enterococcus species (E. faecalis, E. Faecium) were the most commonly isolated species across all duodenal samples (isolated from four of the six samples – patients A, B, D, E) but only Klebsiella and Bacillus species were isolated from both tumors and duodenum (patients E and F) as shown in Figure 2C-D. Of note, there was no difference in cultivate yield based on the method of tissue cryopreservation (Table 1 and Figure 2A, B).

Due to the high risk of contamination when culturing low-biomass samples, we performed 16S sequencing and ITS sequencing on the tissue samples (Figure 2E, F, and Supplemental Figure 1), including several background sequencing controls to better identify microbial contaminants introduced during the tissue handling and sequencing process (Supplemental Figures 2 and 3). Taxa identified in our water and background sequencing controls were deemed contaminants and removed from analyses as discussed in the methods (Supplemental Figures 2 and 3). Multiple studies have identified an increase of Pseudomonadota (previously known as Proteobacteria 47 ) in PDAC compared to normal pancreatic tissue via 16S sequencing. 2 , 3 , 6-8 Similarly, our 16S sequencing analysis identified Pseudomonadota and Enterobacteriaceae as the most dominant phylum and genus in PDAC tumors (Figure 2E, Supplemental Figure 1A–C). Enterobacteriaceae belongs to the Pseudomonadota phylum and includes species in the genus Klebsiella and Enterobacter, which were all cultured from our PDAC tissue and small intestine samples (Figure 2C and D). Bacillota, which includes species in the genus Bacillus and Enterococcus, was the second most common phyla identified in the sequencing of our duodenal and tumor tissues. Thus, the cultivated microbes from our PDAC tumors and duodenum were concordant with the taxonomic identification of bacterial communities from 16S gene sequencing (Figure 2E). To further investigate if our sequencing findings aligned with previously published data sets, only genera previously identified as being non-contaminants of low biomass PDAC tissue by Eckoff et al. (Rothia, Rhodococcus, Haemophilus, Pseudoxanthomonas, Cloacibacterium, Bifidobacterium, Enterobacter, Enterococcus, Clostridium, Clostridium sensu stricto, Brachybacterium, Bacteroides, and Cutibacterium) 33 were included in downstream analyses (Supplemental Figure 1A, B). Even with these more stringent filters, Pseudomonadota and Enterobacteriaceae remained the most dominant phylum and genus in PDAC tumors. Given the prevalence of Klebsiella species in our tissue isolates, we performed Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (FISH) and immunofluorescence on paraffin-embedded sections of PDAC tumor, which confirmed the presence of Klebsiella species in pancreatic tumor samples (Figure 2G, Supplemental Figure 4). We were unable to isolate any species shown to be associated with long-term survival of PDAC (Pseudoxanthomonas, Saccharopolyspora, or Streptomyces). 6 However, it should be noted that based on clinical outcomes of our study participants, our study did not include patient survivors that met the criteria for long-term survival. 6

Fungi are often overlooked in many microbiome studies. Thus, we included media conditions, such as the Sabouraud agar, to specifically enrich for fungi. We isolated several Ascomycota, such as Candida, Saccharomyces, and Aspergillus, from duodenal tissue (Figure 2C, D). Malassezia globosa was previously reported to be enriched in PDAC tumors. 4 , 5 , 11 However, we were unable to successfully isolate M. globosa from PDAC tumor or duodenal samples. ITS sequencing of our tumor and small bowel tissues suggested an abundance of Basidiomycota and Ascomycota in both gut and tumor samples, but again, Malassezia was not detected by ITS sequencing (Figure 2F and Supplemental Figure 1D). No common yeast species were isolated from the human pancreatic tumor, but we did observe growth of several non-identified, filamentous fungi. One of these filament fungi isolated from the tumor of patient F was identified as Thermoascus crustaceus (Figure 2C and Supplemental Figure 5). Overall, our results show concordance between our cultivated isolates and microbes identified by ITS sequencing, highlighting that our workflow can also successfully enrich for complex fungi. The many unidentified and unclassified fungi in our strain collection warrant further investigation to better characterize tissue fungal isolates (Supplemental Table 1).

Isolation of bacteria and fungi from healthy donor pancreatic tissue and duodenum

To determine if the species isolated are unique to PDAC tissue and less prevalent in healthy pancreas tissue, as reported in the literature, we applied our isolation pipeline to healthy donor pancreatic tissue harvested from organ donors (Table 1). Similar to tissue from PDAC patients, we isolated more CFUs from donor duodenal tissue compared to pancreas tissues (Figure 3A, B). We observed predominantly Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Candida colonies in patient G, as well as Staphylococcus and Bacillus species in patient H (Figure 3C, D). 16S sequencing of normal duodenum also showed Pseudomonadota and Bacillota as the dominant phyla, but unlike PDAC tumors, Enterobacteriacee species were far less common among normal tissue samples (Supplemental Figure 6A–C and Figure 3E). Our ITS sequencing of the normal duodenum also confirmed the presence of Candida, but this was only identified in patient G, which is the same patient from which it was cultured (Figure 3C, D, and F). ITS sequencing of the normal pancreas identified predominantly unclassified fungi (Supplemental Figure 6D and Figure 3F). Although Pseudomonas aeruginosa was a dominant isolate in the duodenum and pancreas of patient G, this was assumed to be a contaminant as Pseudomonas species were very prevalent throughout the water and background sequencing controls, and thus eliminated from downstream 16S sequencing analysis (Supplemental Figure 2A, B). Additionally, normal pancreatic tissue samples from donors included cultured strains of Cutibacterium acnes (Actinobacteria phylum), which is most likely a contaminant normally found on the skin that was introduced during tissue handling. Actinobacteria were identified as contaminants in all 16S sequencing background controls and thus removed from the sequencing analysis (Supplemental Figure 2A, B). In accordance with the 16S sequencing data reported in the literature, we found that our PDAC tissue yielded more Pseudomonadota cultivates, specifically Enterobacteriaceae species. 2 , 3 This is in direct contrast to the isolates from normal pancreatic tissue, supporting the hypothesis that pancreatic cancer may be associated with an enrichment of Pseudomonadota species.

Characterization of the tumor isolated Klebsiella oxytoca (UMKO1)

One of the most common Enterobacteriaceae bacteria isolated from both our PDAC duodenum and pancreatic tumor cultivates was Klebsiella oxytoca. This is of particular importance because larger sequencing studies have identified Gammaproteobacteria, and specifically Klebsiella species, as one of the most prevalent bacteria in pancreatic tumors. 2 , 3 , 12 Similar to sequencing studies, isolation studies have consistently isolated Klebsiella species (including K. pneumoniae and K. oxytoca) as common isolates from pancreatic tumors. 44-46 , 48 Given the prevalence of this species in our isolates (Figure 2A–D) and other published studies, we decided to further characterize our isolated Klebsiella oxytoca strain (University of Michigan Klebsiella oxytoca 1, UMKO1) to model how other microbial isolates can be studied to determine their potential impact in the pancreatic tumor microenvironment.

UMKO1 contains unique metabolic-associated genes and produces indoles associated with the reprogramming of the tumor microenvironment

Recent studies have highlighted many microbe-derived metabolites that modulate PDAC immunity. 19-21 Furthermore, the bacterial species isolated from our PDAC tumor and duodenal tissues have been shown to induce metabolic changes that drive pathogenicity in pancreatic cancer cells. 48 We performed bacterial genome sequencing of the UMKO1 strain isolated from a pancreatic tumor, confirming its identity as a Klebsiella oxytoca strain (Figure 4A). Sequences were assembled into a large contig of 6,070,641 bp, with a 99.8% identity of the strain as Klebsiella oxytoca (NZ_KQ088007.1 Klebsiella oxytoca strain CHS143 aetza-supercont1.1) and a second contig of 173,005 bp, with a 97.2% identity of the plasmid pCAV1335-115 (Klebsiella oxytoca strain CAV1335 and plasmid pCAV1335-115). We then compared our sequencing data to several publicly available Klebsiella oxytoca sequencing data sets (Supplemental Table 2) using the Pan-genome explorer. 38 We found several gene clusters that are unique to the newly identified Klebsiella oxytoca strain (Figure 4B), most of which were associated with metabolic processes (Supplemental Table 3). These included genes associated with glutamine (GlnQ) and Glutathione transport (GsiA, GsiB, and GsiD) (Supplemental Table 3). Glutamine plays an important role in physiology as a nitrogen donor for purine and pyrimidine bases and some non-essential amino acids, while glutathione is an important sensor for oxidative stress. 49 To determine which metabolites are produced by the newly isolated Klebsiella oxytoca in the tumor microenvironment, we cultured the UMKO1 strain in Tumor Interstitial Fluid Media (TIFM), 40 , 41 and performed untargeted metabolomics of polar metabolites using Liquid Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) (Figure 4C, D). TIFM closely resembles the nutrient profile of the interstitial fluid in PDAC tumors, allowing for the identification of UMKO1-derived metabolites in a physiologically relevant nutrient environment. 40 We found that Klebsiella oxytoca depleted several amino acids from the media, such as tryptophan, glutamine, phenylalanine, and tyrosine (Figure 4E, Supplemental Figure 7A). We also observed the utilization of other key metabolites, such as alpha-ketoglutarate, connecting catabolic carbon metabolism (tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, energy production) with the anabolic nitrogen metabolism (amino assimilation, building blocks) 50 (Supplemental Figure 7B). We also saw a depletion of citric acid, which can be utilized by the TCA and glyoxylate cycle for energy production 51 (Supplemental Figure 7B). Our untargeted analysis showed that UMKO1 produced polyamines such as putrescine, which was also confirmed by the reduction of its precursor ornithine (Supplemental Figure 7C). In addition, UMKO1 produced butyrate (Supplemental Figure 7D). Putrescine and butyrate both have been implicated in PDAC immunosuppression and response to therapy when produced by gut microbes. 9 , 52

Given the expansive literature on the immunomodulatory functions of tryptophan-derived indoles in PDAC, we further explored tryptophan metabolism by UMKO1. Using untargeted metabolic analysis, we observed that UMKO1 was able to produce 3-indole-ethanol and kynurenic acid (Figure 4C, D) in physiologic TIFM. This is supported by whole-genomic sequencing of UMKO1, which revealed the presence of several enzymes responsible for indole production from tryptophan metabolism, such as tryptophanase and indole pyruvate decarboxylase (Figure 4A). Targeted LC–MS/MS quantification of metabolites produced from the metabolism of tryptophan via the kynurenine and indole pathway was completed to validate our untargeted data. Targeted LC–MS/MS demonstrates that UMKO1not only produces 3-indole ethanol and kynurenic acid, but also indole-3-acetic acid (Figure 4E–G). Kynurenic acid and indole-3-acetic acid are potent aryl hydrocarbon receptor agonists known to reprogram myeloid cells into immunosuppressive phenotypes in the PDAC tumor environment. 19 , 20 , 53 , 54 Given the significant metabolic profile of the UMKO1 strain from these preliminary studies, more in-depth experiments are warranted to evaluate UMKO1’s immunomodulatory effects.

UMKO1 may modulate T cell behavior and chemotherapy metabolism in the pancreatic tumor microenvironment

To evaluate the potential effect of PDAC-associated bacterial metabolites on T cell function in the tumor microenvironment, we utilized an ex vivo system where murine PDAC tumor slices are cultured under physiological nutrient conditions (TIFM). 39 , 41 This allowed us to assess the impact of metabolites produced by our isolated bacteria on the PDAC TME. Murine pancreatic tumor slices were cultured in the ex vivo system as described in the methods and exposed to bacterial supernatants from Klebsiella pneumoniae, Klebsiella variicola, Escherichia coli K12, and UMKO1 for up to 44 hours. We evaluated the effect of bacterial metabolites on Granzyme B-producing CD8+T cells, since anti-tumor immunity is partly dependent upon cytotoxic CD8 T cell function. We quantified CD8+ T cells using immunofluorescence staining (Figure 5A) and found no differences in CD8+ T cell abundance across tumors exposed to control media or bacterial supernatants (Figure 5B,C). However, co-staining for Granzyme B highlighted a significant decrease in Granzyme B-positive CD8 T cells in the tumor explants, only when cultured in the presence of UMKO1 supernatant. There were no significant differences in the number of Granzyme B-positive CD8+T cells in the tumor explants grown in the presence of the other Klebsiella and E. coli strains compared to control media. This finding suggests that bacterial products/metabolites produced by the UMKO1 strain may be associated with tumor immunosuppression, warranting further investigation (Figure 5C, Supplemental Figure 8). Immunofluorescence staining of our patient PDAC tissues for Granzyme B-positive CD8+T cells showed no difference in abundance across the samples tested (Supplemental Figure 9). These results may be explained by the fact that all sequenced PDAC tumors had Pseudomonadota (Klebsiella species) present in the 16S sequencing analysis. Thus, true “Klebsiella negative” tumors may be absent from our small PDAC sample size.

Given the suppressive effects of UMKO1 supernatant on CD8 T cells, we further explored the protumorigenic role of UMKO1 by transplanting the isolated bacterial strain into the gut of tumor-bearing mice. 14-week-old mice were treated with an antibiotic in drinking water for 3 weeks to decrease the bacterial burden in the gut. A week later, mice received oral gavage of whole feces from other healthy wild-type mice, or oral gavage of live UMKO1 cultures. Pancreatic cancer cells were then orthotopically implanted into the pancreas of the repopulated mice, and tumors were harvested 3 weeks later. As previously shown, 3 mice treated with antibiotics to reduce the bacterial burden in the gut had smaller tumors, and tumor size increased when the gut was repopulated with whole feces from untreated mice (Figure 5D). However, there was a notable trend of increased tumor size when the gut was repopulated with single-strain UMKO1, suggesting that UMKO1 alone is partly sufficient to elicit a protumorigenic effect in the pancreatic TME (Figure 5D).

Gemcitabine is a frontline chemotherapy agent used in the treatment of pancreatic cancer. 55 , 56 Its inactivation by the long version of microbially-derived cytidine deaminase to produce an inactive form of the drug has been well described by specific pancreatic cancer-associated Pseudomonadota (Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli K12 strain) 2 . This is of particular clinical importance as a recent study demonstrated that the presence of Gammaproteobacteria (Pseudomonadota) in bile leads to decreased survival, but only in patients treated with gemcitabine. 15 To determine if the newly tumor-isolated Klebsiella strain, UMKO1, possesses the ability to catabolize gemcitabine, we compared it to the predicted protein sequence for cytidine deaminase, and confirmed that the isolated K. oxytoca UMKO1 strain possesses the long version of cytidine deaminase (Supplemental Figure 10A). In addition, we also identified several other strains isolated from the duodenum that were predicted to contain (E. coli and Klebsiella strains) or not contain (Enterococcus faecalis, Micrococcus luteus) the protein sequence for cytidine deaminase 2 (Supplemental Figure 10B). As predicted, UMKO1, E coli, and the other Klebsiella species metabolized gemcitabine within an hour of exposure to the drug in TIFM, while Enterococcus faecalis and Micrococcus luteus were unable to metabolize gemcitabine (Figure 5E). Consistent with this observation, Micrococcus luteus and Enterococcus faecalis had very limited proliferation in TIFM media containing gemcitabine, while E. coli and the Klebsiella strains had robust proliferation in the presence of gemcitabine (Supplemental Figure 10D). Interestingly, Micrococcus luteus showed robust growth in TIFM without gemcitabine (Supplemental Figure 10C), suggesting that the presence of Gemcitabine stunted its growth. Enterococcus faecalis showed minimal growth in nutrient-restricted TIFM with and without gemcitabine, but demonstrated robust proliferation in nutrient-rich Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) media (Supplemental Figure 10C–F). E. coli and Klebsiella are Gammaproteobacteria species, while Enterococcus faecalis and Micrococcus luteus are not. The findings that isolated Gammaproteobacteria species can proliferate in nutrient-poor environments and in the presence of gemcitabine suggest that select microbes may be uniquely able to adapt and survive in the pancreatic tumor environment.

Discussion

One of the major obstacles to studying the pancreatic tumor microbiome is its low biomass. While bacteria and fungi can be identified in the tumor by immunohistochemistry, 2-5 the low abundance and high noise to signal ratio can make it difficult to interpret sequencing analysis. 57 , 58 The challenge with characterizing clinically relevant species and strains in the tumor microbiome limits our understanding of its role in oncogenesis. Current functional microbiome studies circumvent this limitation by using fecal microbiota transplants (FMTs) or bacteria/fungi that were isolated from sources other than pancreatic cancer patient tissues. To address this paucity of clinically relevant microbes, we developed a workflow that allows for the isolation of bacteria and fungi from small (<50 mg), low-biomass, cryopreserved tissues. We were able to reproducibly isolate live microbiota from flash-frozen tissue while minimizing contamination. We isolated microbes from PDAC tumors and adjacent duodenum tissues to determine similarities in their microbial communities. Reports have shown overlap in the duodenal and pancreatic microbiome composition across pancreatic diseases, suggesting translocation of microbiota from the duodenum to the pancreas. 59 Isolates from our tumor samples were also cultured from their corresponding duodenal tissues, supporting the belief that tumor microbes are similar to and derived from the duodenum.

Culture media were selected and optimized to enrich for bacteria found in PDAC tumors based on previously published sequencing data, including long-term survivor-associated bacteria. 6 We were unable to isolate the less common bacteria associated with long-term survivors (Pseudoxanthomonas, Saccharopolyspora, and Streptomyces). However, this was not unexpected given our small sample size and the rarity of patients who are long-term survivors of PDAC. We successfully isolated several bacteria and fungi from the small intestine and tumors of pancreatic cancer patients, mirroring our sequencing results. We cultivated Klebsiella and Bacillus species from the pancreatic tumor, which have been shown to be enriched bacteria in PDAC tumors from 16S sequencing results in several published studies. 2 , 3 , 6 , 12 , 45 Our PDAC tumor cultivates were also similar to bacterial strains isolated by others from pre-malignant lesions and pancreatic cancer. 44 , 46 , 48 In contrast, we isolated fewer strains from the donor pancreas, including one Pseudomonadota, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which based on our sequencing data controls, was likely an environmental contaminant. The rapid overgrowth of P. aeruginosa observed on culture plates under hypoxic conditions likely led to an unusually high number of CFUs and was not a reflection of higher microbial abundance in the pancreas (Figure 3C). This example of P. aeruginosa overgrowth highlights the need for multi-pronged approaches when characterizing low-biomass samples to identify contamination.

The most abundant pancreatic tumor cultivate was Klebsiella oxytoca. Klebsiella species such as K. oxytoca and K. pneumoniae were previously found to be dominant isolates from PDAC and pre-malignant pancreatic cysts harboring early invasive disease. 44 , 45 , 48 Its dominance in tumor cultivates is of particular importance given the growing body of literature on its effect in cancer patients. Early reports indicate that Klebsiella oxytoca in the gut and other Klebsiella species in tumors are associated with cancer cachexia, 60 and promote cell death via DNA-damaging properties. 44 Additionally, our preliminary studies show K. oxytoca’s ability to produce immunomodulatory tryptophan metabolites, metabolize a PDAC chemotherapy agent rendering it inactive, and proliferate in the low-nutrient pancreatic TME. Other bacterial strains isolated from duodenal tissue, such as E. coli, are known to also produce indoles and metabolize Gemcitabine chemotherapy, 2 possibly to a greater degree than K. oxytoca (Figure 5E). However, we did not culture any E. coli strains from tumor tissue, and similarly, other sequencing and isolation studies have consistently identified Klebsiella species in tumor tissue and not E. coli strains. 44 , 45 Preliminary repopulation studies with our K. oxytoca strain resulted in a trend towards larger tumors, suggesting that UMKO1 may contribute to protumorigenic effects in the TME. These findings highlight the clinical importance of K. oxytoca and the need to further investigate the possible pro-tumorigenic role of this tumor-associated strain and its ability to synergistically co-exist in the TME.

Our culture media composition was optimized to enrich for fungi, including yeast and filamentous fungi, which are often found in the small intestine and have been reported in the pancreatic tumor microenvironment. 4 , 5 It should be noted that while yeast can be identified routinely via MALDI-TOF, filamentous fungi require additional DNA isolation techniques for sufficient ITS amplification for sequencing. Even with bead-beating techniques, the fungal DNA yield was low due to the greater complexity of the fungal wall. 61 Thus, the substantial number of unidentified colonies in our culture isolates were possibly filamentous fungi that we were unable to adequately lyse for MALDI-TOF identification (Figure 2 and Supplemental Figure 5A, D). Interestingly, we were not successful in culturing any Malassezia strains, but these were also absent in our ITS sequencing data. While Malassezia has been reported to be one of the most abundant fungus in human pancreatic tumors, our small tumor sizes and sample sizes may have limited our ability to isolate Malassezia.

In summary, this study provides a detailed workflow for the isolation of live bacterial and fungal species from low-biomass samples, while carefully identifying any contaminants in the isolation process. Preliminary characterization of clinically relevant tumor isolate UMKO1, sheds light on its potential protumorigenic impact on the tumor microenvironment. The isolation of clinically relevant microbiota will enable more mechanistic studies with tumor-modulating microbiota, illuminating their influence on pancreatic oncogenesis.

Limitations of this study

A major limitation of the study is the inability to generate comprehensive media combinations to cultivate all members of the low-biomass tumor microbiome. As a result, the isolated microbiota will not accurately represent the true microbial compositions of these tumors, as the isolates obtained are skewed by the selection of culture media. Secondly, we utilized three PDAC tumor samples and six PDAC duodenal samples for isolation. Ten tumor and duodenal samples were used for sequencing. Although our isolation yields are consistent with previously published data, a larger sample size may have led to a wider variety of cultivates from our tumor samples. Lastly, our tissue samples were markedly small (<50 mg for tumors and < 500 mg for duodenal tissues) and cryopreserved prior to isolation. Culturing larger, fresh tissue samples may have resulted in a greater number of cultivates per sample. Despite these limitations, we successfully isolated microbes identified in the PDAC microbiome of other studies. Thus, the methodology described in our study provides a useful resource for culturing low-biomass, cryopreserved pancreatic tumor samples and can be utilized to provide insight into the role of gut microbes in tumorigenesis. Additional mechanistic and in vivo studies with UMKO1 are needed to comprehensively determine the impact of the bacterium on pancreatic cancer.
