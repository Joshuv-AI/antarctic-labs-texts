Application of contrast-enhanced CT-based radiomics with machine learning to predict WHO/ISUP grading in clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Xiaowei Li, Shiyan Song, Wenfei Ge, Haicheng Wang, Wei Liu, Xiaochen Qi, Yuming Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo predict the World Health Organization (WHO)/International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grading of clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) using multiple machine learning models based on contrast-enhanced CT, and to compare their performance, thereby exploring a non-invasive method for preoperative pathological grading.MethodsSeventy-six patients with ccRCC (65 low-grade and 11 high-grade) from September 2021 to August 2023 were retrospectively included. Regions of interest (ROI) were manually delineated on enhanced CT images, and radiomics features were extracted. The dataset was first split into training and test sets (50%/50%). To address class imbalance, the training set was oversampled using the RandomOverSampler algorithm. Feature scaling and selection using the Maximum Information Coefficient (MIC) were applied strictly within the training set. Radiomics models were built using twelve machine learning classifiers. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, area under the curve (AUC), and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsAmong the 12 classifiers, five (GradientBoostingClassifier, RandomForestClassifier, AdaBoostClassifier, XGBClassifier, and CatBoostClassifier) reported an AUC of 1.000 on the test set; XGBClassifier had the highest sensitivity (0.944) and accuracy (0.969). The high AUC values should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size. DCA suggested that most models provided a net benefit across a wide threshold range.ConclusionMachine learning models based on contrast-enhanced CT show potential as a non-invasive tool for distinguishing high-grade from low-grade ccRCC. This preliminary study suggests a promising direction; however, further validation with larger, multi-center cohorts is required before clinical application.Keywords: Clear cell renal cell carcinoma, Contrast-enhanced CT, Tumor grading, Radiomics, Machine learning

Introduction

Kidney cancer accounts for approximately 3% of all cancers. It is one of the ten most common cancers in the population, and its incidence is on the rise [1–3]. Renal cell carcinoma(RCC) is the most common type of kidney cancer, including many subtypes, of which clear cell renal cell carcinoma accounts for about 75% [4, 5]. Relevant studies indicate that for worse outcomes for patients with WHO / ISUP grade stage III or IV than stage I and II, and the treatment strategies for patients with different stages differ [6]. WHO / ISUP is regarded as the relatively authoritative tumor grading system for ccRCC and has been widely used in recent years [7, 8]. Therefore, the prediction of ccRCC grading is important for targeted treatment of patients.

RCC pathological grading can effectively suggest the degree of malignancy as well as the prognosis of a patient’s tumor [9]. Surgical pathology can diagnose the pathological grade of CC RCC. Compared with lower-grade patients, surgery may not bring beneficial efficacy [10–12]. Therefore, in order to avoid unnecessary surgical treatment, preoperative percutaneous puncture pathological examination is increasingly used in preoperative pathological diagnosis [13]. However, relevant studies have shown that the diagnostic rate of percutaneous pathology biopsy methods is 78%-97%, which means that if this method does not confirm the diagnosis, a second biopsy or surgical resection should be considered [14], so compared with non-invasive operations, this invasive procedures may lead to the occurrence of complications, such as bleeding, infection, and even the implant invasion of the tumor cells during the process [15]. In recent years, radiomics has become increasingly popular among researchers, which involves extracting image features from routine radiological images, presenting the extracted features in the form of data, and analyzing the data to create models with features to help predict relevant outcomes [16]. Currently, they are being used by researchers for clinical development of diagnostic, prognostic and predictive related models [17–20]. Currently, radiomics has many applications in predicting benign and malignant RCC, tumor stage, tissue typing differentiation, related gene expression, and prognosis [21–24]. There are fewer studies on WHO/ISUP grading of ccRCC predicted by imaging histology based on preoperative computed tomography (CT) scans of RCC patients.

Therefore, in summary, seeking a method that can diagnose the pathological grading of tumors in patients with ccRCC by non-invasive manipulation will be meaningful for the treatment selection of patients with ccRCC. CT, ultrasound (US) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are common imaging methods for renal examination [25]. This paper aims to predict the WHO/ISUP classification of ccRCC patients using multiple machine learning classification models based on CT and relevant clinical data of the patients, and to statistically analyse and compare the results calculated by multiple classification models to explore a relatively good non-invasive and convenient method of pathological classification diagnosis that is consistent with the clinical diagnosis and treatment of ccRCC patients.

Materials and methods

Patients

The Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Qinhuangdao approved this study. We reviewed all patients with renal tumors who underwent renal enhancement CT prior to treatment between September 2021 and August 2023 and who were enrolled with pathologically confirmed RCC. 76 patients were diagnosed with ccRCC, of which 65 had low-grade pathology (ISUP pathology grade I or II), and 11 had high-grade (ISUP pathology grade III or IV). Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Contrast-enhanced CT of the kidneys was performed; (2) Pathological diagnosis of ccRCC. The exclusion criteria were (1) the patient did not undergo pathological examination; (2) the pathological results showed that the tumor was benign; and (3) the pathological results showed a malignant tumor, but the case type was not clear cell carcinoma (Fig. 1).

Patient enrolment flowchart

CT scan

Patients who underwent abdominal, urinary, or renal enhanced CT scans were performed with a GE OPTIME 680 64-slice spiral CT (GE, American). CT scanning parameters: tube voltage = 140kv, tube current = 600 mA, slice thickness =5 mm, matrix = 512*512. The contrast agent was iodixanol. After injection of the contrast agent, dynamic scanning was performed and when the aortic CT value reached the threshold, the patient was instructed to hold his breath for the arterial phase image acquisition. Venous phase images were collected 30 s after the arterial phase, and renal parenchymal phase images were collected 100 s after the arterial phase.

Tumor segmentation

The target for tumor segmentation was intrarenal masses, and all malignant masses (1 or more) in the altered kidney were selected as regions of interest (ROIs). Segmentation of ROI was performed independently by two experienced urologists. They were unaware of the patient’s clinical and pathological information. One of the urologists (urologist A with 9 years of experience) manually drew the ROI in the CT image using the software 3D Slicer 4.11. Another urological surgeon (urologist B, with 12 years of experience) reviewed the ROI manually drawn by physician A. The study was designed with single-person sketching.

Pyradiomic feature extraction

We used the pyradiomicv3.0 toolkit in the software Python 3.8 (an imageomics feature extraction tool compliant with the mage biomarker standardisation initiative) to perform feature extraction on the captured ROIs, which included: first-order features, shape features, Gray Level Co- occurrence Matrix (GLCM) features, Gray Level Run-Length Matrix (GLRLM) features, Gray Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM) features, and Gray Level Dependence Matrix (GLDM) features. Among them, the first-order features are based on single-pixel analysis, Shape features describe the geometric properties of ROIs based on shape; GLCM, GLRLM, GLSZM and GLDM are texture features, describing the second-order and higher-order spatial distributions of patterns or intensity [26]. The YAML file used for extracting features is available in the attached material. Figure 2 shows a flowchart of the radiomics process for this study.

Data partitioning and resampling

The entire dataset of 76 patients was first randomly split into a training set and a test set at a 50:50 ratio (stratified by grade), ensuring no patient data overlapped between sets. Due to the severe class imbalance (65 low-grade vs. 11 high-grade in the full set), the RandomOverSampler algorithm from the imbalanced-learn Python library was applied only to the training set to balance the class distribution (n = 65 per class in the resampled training set). The test set remained untouched and imbalanced to simulate a real-world scenario.

Data preprocessing and feature selection

Feature scaling (standardization to zero mean and unit variance) was applied using parameters fitted solely on the training set, which were then used to transform both the training and test sets. Feature selection was performed only on the training data using the Maximum Information Coefficient (MIC) to evaluate the relevance of each feature to the pathological grade. The top 40 features with the highest MIC scores were selected for model construction. This strict separation prevented information leakage from the test set into the training process.A 50/50 data split was selected, which is based on the small sample size to ensure sufficient test sets for performance evaluation. Future studies using larger datasets will employ cross-validation methods. The training set trains machine learning through the input and output data sets to find the initial parameters of the model by fitting. Because the extracted data of some features deviate excessively from other features, the objective function is not accurate, so that the parameter estimator cannot correctly learn other features, we used the preprocessing method in the software Python 3.8, sklearn library to adjust the distribution of feature data to the standard normal distribution, also called Gaussian distribution, so that the mean dimension of the data is 0 and the variance is 1.

Model construction and evaluation

Twelve machine learning classifiers were implemented using scikit-learn and other relevant Python libraries: AdaBoostClassifier (Ada), BaggingClassifier (Bag), BernoulliNB (BN), CatBoostClassifier (Cat), DecisionTreeClassifier (DT), GaussianNB (GNB), GradientBoostingClassifier (GB), KNeighborsClassifier (KNN), LogisticRegression (LR), MLPClassifier (MLP), RandomForestClassifier (RF), and XGBClassifier (XGB). Models were trained on the processed training set and evaluated on the original, unseen test set.

Performance was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and F1-score. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical net benefit across different probability thresholds.

Reproducibility

Analysis was performed using Python 3.8. Key libraries included PyRadiomics 3.0, scikit-learn 1.2, imbalanced-learn 0.10, and xgboost 1.7. A fixed random seed (2024) was used for reproducibility. Code and the feature extraction configuration file are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Diagnostic performance of radiomics models based on different classifiers

We used 12 machine learning algorithms to create radiomics models and assessed the diagnostic performance of the radiomics models for ISUP pathology grading of ccRCC using ROC curves. Among the 12 machine learning classifiers, GradientBoostingClassifier, RandomForestClassifier, AdaBoostClassifier, XGBClassifier, and CatBoostClassifier produced the highest AUC of 1.000; XGBClassifier has the highest sensitivity of 0.944; almost all the algorithms obtained results with a specificity of 1.000, except for KNN’s 0.682 and BN’s 0.966, XGBClassifier has the highest accuracy of 0.969, and also, it has the highest F1-score of 0.971 (Table 2, Figs. 5 and 6). By the results, it is evident that the overall performance of the XGBClassifier machine learning algorithm is better than other algorithms.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated enhanced CT-based radiomics models for WHO/ISUP grading of diagnostic ccRCC and compared their performance. We chose the MIC method to screen the extracted features, selecting the top 40 weights as features from 1218 radiomics features, and then selected a total of 12 machine learning models, which all showed satisfactory, reproducible and reliable performance in distinguishing high-quality and low-quality ccRCCs. Among them, GradientBoostingClassifier, RandomForestClassifier, AdaBoostClassifier, XGBClassifier, and CatBoostClassifier produced the highest AUC of 1.000; XGBClassifier has the highest sensitivity of 0.944; almost all algorithms obtained results with specificity of 1.000, except KNN with 0.682 and BN with 0.966; XGBClassifier has the highest accuracy of 0.969, and also, it has the highest F1-score of 0.971.ROC suggests that all the machine learning models show good prediction results.

Many studies have shown that a definite diagnosis of tumor pathological classification helps to predict prognostic significance [27–30]. Furthermore, the pathological diagnosis may determine the treatment modality of the patient [31]. The WHO/ISUP grading system, which was adopted by the World Health Organization in 2016, is divided into grades 1 to 3 according to the apparent degree of tumor cells in the nucleolus at high magnification, and is considered to be grade 4 sarcoma if any of the following three conditions are present: (1) tumor giant cells and/or marked nuclear pleomorphism, (2) sarcomatoid carcinoma, (3) tumor giant cells and/or marked nuclear pleomorphism. Or (3) rhabdomyosarcomatoid dedifferentiated carcinoma, sarcomatoid and rhabdomyosarcomatoid morphology, tumor necrosis with intravascular tumor invasion indicate poor prognosis. Tumor necrosis and intravascular tumor invasion predict poor prognosis of renal cell carcinoma [7, 8, 32]. Therefore prediction of ccRCC grading is important for predicting patient prognosis as well as targeting treatment. Currently, nephrectomy is the treatment for primary and metastatic lesions, and nephrectomy does not require percutaneous puncture biopsy to obtain histopathological evidence [33]. For patients without intervention, percutaneous ablation or mass metastasis, a percutaneous needle biopsy should be performed [13–35]. Although renal biopsy plays an important role in the diagnosis and treatment of renal mass, its application is limited considering its complications, efficacy and safety issues.

Radiomics is the relationship of large-scale image data mining with clinical and biological end points. By using different imaging modalities (such as ultrasound, CT, MRI, etc.), the tumor information can be extracted. The samples extracted from the tumor biopsy-based detection method may not always represent the whole tumor, so it may provide erroneous information. While radiomics can only estimate the three-dimensional structure of tumors by evaluating imaging information [36–38]. This means that applying machine learning models to radiomics features makes it possible to infer the backward problems of genotypes from macroscopic phenotypes, providing valuable predictive information for diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis [39, 40]. In this study, based on CT and patients’ related clinical data, multiple machine learning classification models were used to predict the WHO/ISUP classification of ccRCC patients, and the results computed by multiple classification models were statistically analysed and compared. The final results show that 12 machine learning methods calculated the corresponding model, and all showed accurate prediction results after verification. This suggests that the WHO / ISUP pathological typing of ccRCC patients by enhanced CT examination is feasible in the future.

Our patient dataset was unbalanced in terms of the distribution of lower versus higher level groups, including 65 cases with ISUP pathological classification of grade I versus grade II and 11 cases with grade III versus grade IV. The smaller number of cases may lead to the risk of overfitting in machine learning based classification, and in this case, the results obtained are prone to being confusing. Therefore, considering that in order to avoid such incidents, we used the RandomOverSampler data resampling method in the imbalanced-learn library of the software Python 3.8 to solve the data imbalance problem by expanding the data of the high-level group of cases to be consistent with that of the low-level group, in order to make the results obtained more objective.

In feature selection, we used the MIC method to measure the strength of the linear or non-linear relationship between the two variables. The MIC method is used to measure the strength of the linear or nonlinear relationship between the two variables. It can discover linear and nonlinear functional relationships between variables, not only functional but also nonfunctional. Moreover, the MIC metric is balanced, and it can be used to compare the strength of the same correlation not only vertically but also horizontally across different relationships. However, the statistical power of MIC has been somewhat questioned, and when the null hypothesis does not hold, the statistics of MIC may be affected.

We used multiple machine learning model comparisons and drew DCA to assess the diagnostic performance of the model and the utility of clinically individualized treatment. DCA is a simple method to evaluate clinical prediction models. Compared with sensitivity, specificity and AUC, which can predict the accuracy of machine learning models, DCA can integrate the habitual preferences of patients or decision makers into the analysis in the evaluation process, which is in line with the general trend of personalized service medicine in the current medical development of medicine. This new approach provides insights into clinical outcomes based on threshold probabilities, from which a net benefit can be obtained [41–43].

Due to the strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, the dataset of this study was small overall, and any retrospective single-centre study is likely to suffer from selection bias; therefore, future studies should have larger sample sizes, multi-vendor images, and external test sets [44]. Secondly, our sample distribution was unbalanced, which may lead to overfitting in machine learning classification, and we attempted to address this issue through data resampling, which has proved successful in terms of the results of related studies [45, 46].

Radiomics prediction of clinically relevant outcomes can be extended to many areas, such as histological diagnosis of RCC, benign and malignant diagnosis of tumours in patients with renal masses, prediction of prognostic outcomes in patients with RCC, and prediction of pharmacological outcomes in RCC, which are areas that are currently relatively understudied, and which may be the trend of research on renal tumours for some time to come.

Our study has several important limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the single-center retrospective design and small sample size (n = 76), particularly the limited number of high-grade cases (n = 11), increase the risk of model overfitting and limit the generalizability of our results. The perfect AUCs reported, while observed on an independent test split, should be interpreted with extreme caution as they likely reflect optimistic performance due to the dataset scale. Second, despite using a hold-out test set and rigorously applying preprocessing steps only within the training fold, the possibility of residual optimism cannot be fully ruled out. External validation on a completely independent cohort is essential. Third, the manual segmentation was performed by urologists; although reviewed, the lack of formal inter-reader reliability assessment and involvement of dedicated radiologists may affect ROI reproducibility. Fourth, we did not compare our radiomics models against simple clinical or radiological predictors (e.g., tumor size, enhancement pattern, or radiologist’s impression). Finally, the clinical impact and utility of such a model were not prospectively evaluated.

Future studies should focus on larger, multi-center prospective cohorts, incorporate external validation, compare radiomics against established clinical models, and ideally, include input from expert radiologists in the segmentation process.

Conclusion

This study shows that machine learning models based on contrast-enhanced CT are valuable non-invasive methods, which can distinguish ccRCC with WHO / ISUP grades, and they can help clinicians develop personalized precise management strategies that can be used as valuable clinical tools in the clinical decision-making process.
