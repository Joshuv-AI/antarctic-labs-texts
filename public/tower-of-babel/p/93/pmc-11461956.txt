Computed tomography features and surgical treatment of wandering spleen torsion: A case report
Authors: Hidayatullah Hamidi, Hashmatullah Osmani, Rahman Mohammad Zahin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Wandering spleen is a rare clinical entity characterized by the laxity, absence, or abnormal attachment of splenic supporting ligaments leading to hypermobility and abnormal positioning of the spleen in the abdominal cavity. The abnormally increased mobility of the spleen predisposes it to torsion and infarction. Authors present a case of wandering spleen complicated to torsion and infarct in a middle aged lady. The patient presented with acute abdominal pain, diagnosed by abdominal computed tomography and underwent surgery and splenectomy.

Introduction

Wandering or ectopic spleen is a rare condition in which the spleen is not located in the left upper quadrant [1]. It is characterized by the laxity, absence, or abnormal attachment of supporting ligaments such as lienorenal, gastro-splenic, and phrenic-colic ligaments, leading to hypermobility and abnormal position in the abdomino-pelvic cavity [2,3].

This uncommon abnormality can be congenital or acquired. Reproductive-age females are the most common presenting group in adults [4,5]. The incidence of wandering (ectopic) spleen is 0.2% and 0.5% in reported series of splenectomies [6,7].

The abnormally increased mobility of the spleen due to an abnormally long vascular pedicle predisposes it to various complications, such as torsion, infarction, gangrene, abscess formation, variceal hemorrhage, and pancreatic necrosis [1].

The presentation of wandering spleen ranges from an asymptomatic pelvic or abdominal mass to a surgical emergency [7]. The most common complaint is acute, chronic, and intermittent abdominal pain caused by intermittent torsion of the vascular pedicle [6].

Accurate diagnosis of wandering spleen can be challenging as this condition is rare and has no specific signs and symptoms hence imaging plays important role in its detection [7,8]. The treatment of asymptomatic wandering spleen is splenopexy, while splenectomy is indicated in cases of torsion leading to splenic infarction [4,6].

Case presentation

A 44-year-old, Afghan, married woman presented with chief complain of acute severe abdominal pain, nausea and vomiting for last 3-days. The pain was persistent, and did not relieve with analgesics and antacids. The bowel sounds were normal. No signs and symptoms related to urinary tract were documented. No history of previous surgery was present. The patient was advised abdominal ultrasound which reported a complex mass in mid abdomen and right iliac fossa with suspicious of appendicular abscess. Patient was sent to a tertiary care hospital in the other province to undergo abdominal computed tomography (CT) for further evaluation of the sonographically detected heterogeneous mass.

Triphasic abdominal CT scan (nonenhanced, arterial phase, and porto-venous phase) was performed by 128 slice Siemens scanner. Twisted vessels with Whirls pools sign in the left upper quadrant attracted the attention of radiologist at the first glance, which were going towards a large nonenhancing hypo dense lesion in the mid abdomen. The radiologist assumed it may be cystic mass (like mesenteric cyst/pancreatic pseudo-cyst) with twisting and secondary volvulus of the mesentery, but as soon as the check for normal spleen was failed, soon the diagnosis of the wandering spleen came to mind. A second view of images confirmed that the twisting vessels were the splenic artery and vein (Fig. 1) and the nonenhancing mass was the infarcted spleen itself with abnormal orientation (Fig. 2). Rest of the abdominal organs were unremarkable. The case was reported and soon communicated with the treating surgeon for further management. The surgeon was first hesitant about the CT report as the description of the mass reported by the ultrasound was not given. Discussion

The first description of wandering or ectopic spleen is honored to Van Horne, a Dutch physician who reported this entity as an incidental finding in 1667 with autopsy confirmation [2,7]. Wandering spleen is a rare clinical condition in which the spleen is not located in the left upper quadrant. The ligamentous laxity in this entity has different causes including congenital and acquired etiologies. In congenital form, there is mal-development or malformation of normal splenic suspensory ligaments especially lienorenal and gastrosplenic ligaments. The acquired form can occur in circumstances that weakens the suspensory ligaments including pregnancy with increased levels of estrogen, splenomegaly of any cause and after trauma [2,8]. The cases of wandering spleen are more common in women of reproductive age, most often in multiparous women, believed to be secondary to abdominal wall laxity [7]. The age distribution for wandering spleens ranges from newborn to 81 years of age, with 2 distinct peaks, the first peak occurs in childhood, particularly below 1 year of age, while the second peak is observed in the third decade of life [2].

The abnormally increased mobility of the spleen is due to an abnormally long vascular pedicle predisposing it to various complication. The most serious complication is torsion. In acute form of torsion, it presents as acute abdomen and leads to vascular congestion, infarction and even gangrene; while chronic form of torsion causes venous congestion and splenomegaly and present with periodic abdominal pain. Other complication of wandering spleen are acute pancreatic necrosis, splenic abscess, gastric distension and variceal hemorrhage [1].

Clinically, wandering spleen has a wide range of presentations from an incidental finding of abdominal or pelvic mass in an asymptomatic patient to an abdominal emergency [2,5] often the patients will have complained of nonspecific abdominal pain because of splenomegaly or adjacent organs pressure effects. Clinical examination showing palpable lower abdominal mass with nonspecific lab results [5].

The diagnosis of this rare entity can be difficult on solely based on clinical investigations. The diagnosis is usually achieved through noninvasive imaging modalities [7,8]. Plain radiography may fail to show the typical splenic shadow in left upper quadrant. It can also show an atypical mass anywhere in the abdomino-pelvic cavity [7]. Ultrasonography can detect the abnormal position of the spleen usually low-lying; or absence of spleen in the left upper quadrant, while Doppler ultrasonography can provide details about the vascular flow to the organ; this technique is particularly valuable in diagnosing acute splenic torsion or infarction [7]. Computed tomography (CT) confirms absence of normal spleen in left upper quadrant, ovoid or comma shaped abdominal mass (with classic "whorled" appearance), long vascular pedicle representing the splenic vessels and the surrounding fat of the twisted splenic pedicle. Additional CT findings may include acute thrombosis of the splenic vessels, resulting in a lack of contrast opacification in the areas of the spleen associated with infarction [2].

The treatment of choice in asymptomatic patients is splenopexy, however splenectomy is needed in cases of torsion, which can lead to infarction and diagnosed radiologically [6].

In our case, the diagnosis of wandering spleen with torsion was made by CT and confirmed by surgery followed by splenectomy.

Conclusion

In patients presenting with acute abdomen, torsion of a wandering spleen could also be considered in the differential diagnosis as an uncommon cause [8]. This emphasizes the importance of general practitioners, radiologists, and surgeons being aware of this potentially dangerous condition. Failure to diagnose, may lead to inappropriate invasive procedures, such as blind or US-guided biopsy [4].
