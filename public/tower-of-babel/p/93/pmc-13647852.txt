Facilitators and barriers to participation in physical activity among older adults: a community-based study
Authors: Azam Maleki, Khadijeh Hajimiri, Samira Ahmadi, Maede Feizy, Parvin Mohebbi, Mina Hashemiparast
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical activity is vital for older adults’ physical and mental health, yet participation remains low due to individual, social, and environmental factors.ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine the facilitators and barriers influencing physical activity participation among older adults in 2025.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional study was carried out among 450 older adults receiving services from comprehensive health centers. Participants were selected using a multistage random sampling approach. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire comprising demographic information, a standardized physical activity assessment tool for older adults, and items evaluating potential facilitators and barriers to participation. Statistical analyses included chi-square tests and linear regression model with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsMost participants were in the lower and middle tertiles of the PASE score distribution, while only 22.2% of participants were in the upper tertile. Physical activity was significantly associated with sex, chronic disease status, and monthly income (p < 0.05). Health improvement and physical fitness emerged as the strongest facilitators, whereas individual and social constraints represented the principal barriers. In the adjusted multivariable linear regression analysis, employment was positively associated with barriers (β = 0.14) and facilitators (β = 0.08) and education, housing, and health insurance showed significant negative associations with both barriers and facilitators (p < 0.05).ConclusionPhysical activity levels among older adults were suboptimal, and a wide range of individual, social, and environmental determinants substantially influenced participation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28444-4.Keywords: Aging, Motor activity, Health behavior, Exercise

Background

Aging is a natural and universal biological process that affects all living organisms, including humans. Older adulthood is generally defined as beginning between the ages of 60 and 65 years. The World Health Organization (WHO) considers age 65 years as the threshold in developed countries and age 60 years in developing countries [1]. From a demographic perspective, a society is classified as older adult when the proportion of individuals under 15 years decreases to approximately 20–25%, and the proportion of those were in 65 years and above reaches 8%, or when the share of individuals with 60 and above reaches 12% [2]. Declining fertility rates, advancements in healthcare services, and improvements in overall quality of life have collectively contributed to increased life expectancy and, consequently, a longer lifespan [3]. According to WHO projections, the global aging trend is accelerating at an unprecedented pace: the population with 60 years and upper is expected to rise from one billion in 2019 to 1.4 billion in 2030 and to surpass 2.1 billion by 2050 [4].

The increasing proportion of older adults carries profound implications for health systems, economic stability, and social policy development. Compared with other age groups, older adults are more susceptible to chronic diseases and functional disabilities, which substantially elevate healthcare expenditures. Consequently, population aging represents a major challenge for national health and social planning. However, with appropriate management strategies and targeted policy interventions, this demographic shift can also be transformed into a significant societal opportunity [5]. The active aging framework seeks to improve the quality of life in later years and emphasizes the maintenance of physical and mental health, social engagement, and supportive environmental conditions [6]. In this context, the concepts of healthy aging and successful aging have received increasing attention. Indicators such as the absence of major illness, preservation of independent functioning, optimal cognitive performance, and active participation in daily life are widely recognized as defining attributes of successful aging [7].

Research indicates that successful aging is shaped by a wide range of determinants, including socioeconomic status, lifestyle behaviors, physical activity, social engagement, and emotional well-being [8]. Adopting healthy lifestyle practices such as avoiding tobacco and alcohol use, following a nutritious diet, engaging in regular physical activity, and participating in cognitively stimulating activities play a pivotal role in promoting healthy aging [6]. Moreover, factors such as happiness, social capital, involvement in active aging programs, and a positive attitude toward the aging process have been identified as important contributors to healthy aging [9]. Environmental conditions and living contexts are also critical, as supportive and enabling environments significantly enhance both physical and psychological health among older adults [10].

Among the various determinants of healthy aging, physical activity is recognized as one of the most influential factors affecting health status and quality of life in older adults. Regular engagement in physical activity helps maintain functional independence, enhances resilience to age-related challenges, reduces the risk of chronic diseases, improves cognitive performance, and promotes psychological well-being [11]. In addition, participation in social activities such as cultural programs, friendship circles, and group-based initiatives strengthens social connectedness, fosters interpersonal relationships, and increases overall life satisfaction among older adults [7, 12]. Establishing supportive environments for physical activity, including safe and accessible spaces, age-appropriate exercise facilities, and well-structured community programs, is therefore essential for advancing active aging [12, 13].

Despite the well‑documented benefits of physical activity, participation among older adults in Iran remains suboptimal. Iran encompasses substantial geographic and socioeconomic diversity, with marked variation in climate, urban form, and access to recreational and health supportive infrastructure across regions. Previous studies illustrate this heterogeneity. In Isfahan, a large metropolitan center with dense urban infrastructure, studies have shown that only 13.7% of older adults engage in adequate physical activity, with participation significantly influenced by gender and economic status [12]. Similarly, in Qazvin, a midsized industrial city in the colder northwest, 25.5% of older adults were found to be inactive, with only 10.2% meeting recommended activity levels, highlighting the influence of personal motivation and housing conditions [14]. Other regions reflect equally variable patterns: in West Azerbaijan and Karaj, more than half of the older population is physically inactive, particularly among housewives and those with lower educational attainment [13, 15]. In contrast, studies in Sari, a region with a more temperate and greener landscape, have identified that while 25.96% of older adults remain inactive, the presence of social support and family encouragement acts as a vital facilitator [16]. These cities represent the broad socioeconomic and environmental spectrum of Iran. While metropolitan areas like Isfahan and Karaj benefit from high-density urban development and greater access to public facilities, northwestern cities such as Zanjan and Qazvin face unique environmental challenges, including colder climates and more compact urban layouts that may constrain outdoor mobility. Conversely, regions like Sari and West Azerbaijan offer distinct environmental landscapes that can significantly influence walking behavior and outdoor engagement [12–16].

Beyond these regional differences, the built environment remains a fundamental challenge in promoting physical activity among older adults. Access to green spaces, safe walking paths, appropriate equipment, and supportive facilities are widely recognized as critical environmental factors that facilitate engagement in physical activity [17]. Evidence indicates that well designed urban environments and exercise equipment tailored to the capabilities and limitations of older adults play a pivotal role in increasing their activity levels [18]. Although the recent expansion of outdoor exercise equipment has created new opportunities for physical activity, many of these facilities remain poorly aligned with the specific needs of older adults and require redesign based on gerontological principles [19]. Given the rapid growth of the older adult population, the significant role of physical activity in enhancing physical and mental health, and the persistently low participation rates among older adults in Iran, identifying the determinants of their participation has become essential. Understanding these factors can inform the development of targeted interventions, age-appropriate infrastructure, and strategies that promote a supportive culture for older adult physical activity. The present study aimed to examine the facilitators and barriers to participation in physical activity among older adults in 2025.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was conducted in 2025 as a descriptive cross-sectional investigation among older adults attending urban Comprehensive Health Centers in Zanjan, a city located in northwestern of Iran. Individuals were eligible for inclusion if they were 60–75 years, resided within the city boundaries, had an active electronic health record at one of the affiliated centers, and provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study protocol, measurement framework, and reporting procedures adhered to the STROBE guidelines to ensure methodological rigor, transparency, and reproducibility.

Sample size

The required sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula, assuming an estimated prevalence of adequate physical activity (p = 0. 5), a precision level of 0.05, and a 95% confidence interval, resulting in a minimum sample of 384 individuals. To account for an anticipated 10% nonresponse rate or incomplete data, the target sample size was increased to 450 participants. A post hoc power analysis was conducted using the observed effect sizes and the final sample size (N = 450). For the multivariable linear regression model examining facilitators of physical activity (R² = 0.259, 10 variables, α = 0.05), the achieved statistical power (1 − β) for the overall F-test exceeded 0.99, indicating that the study was highly powered to detect moderate associations. In contrast, the regression model assessing perceived barriers (R² = 0.073, 10 variables) demonstrated a more limited explanatory capacity but still yielded an estimated power of approximately 0.90 for the overall model. Additionally, the main chi-square analyses evaluating associations between physical activity level and key demographic and health variables (age group, gender, chronic disease status) were supported by high statistical power (> 0.95).

Sampling method

A multistage random sampling approach was employed. In the first stage, the four municipal districts of Zanjan were designated as primary clusters. The city contains 21 Comprehensive Health Centers; among these, Centers No. 2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 14, 16, 17, 18, and 20 were selected through simple random sampling. In the second stage, a list of eligible older adults was extracted from the national Integrated Health System (SIB), which functions as Iran’s centralized electronic health record (EHR) platform. Stratified random sampling proportional to the number of registered older adults (stratified by sex) was then conducted within each selected center to ensure representativeness. Individuals with severe cognitive impairment were excluded based on documentation in their clinical records. The determination of cognitive impairment had been previously made by physicians during routine clinical assessments and recorded in the participants’ medical files. Individuals whose records indicated severe cognitive impairment that could interfere with reliable self-reporting were not included in the study. Selected individuals were contacted by phone and invited to participate in the study. Among the 480 older adults screened for eligibility, 30 were excluded: 17 were outside the eligible age range (60–75 years), 10 declined to participate, and 3 had cognitive impairment reported by their family members. Consequently, 450 eligible participants were included in the study. Data were collected through face-to-face interviewer administered questionnaires at the selected health centers.

Data collection instruments

Demographic and socioeconomic characteristics

The demographic and socioeconomic variables included sex, marital status, educational attainment, employment status, perceived economic status (categorized as insufficient, adequate, and more than adequate), and history of chronic conditions. Age was included as a demographic and analytical variable to describe the study population and to examine differences across age groups. A multidisciplinary expert panel, comprising specialists in health promotion, epidemiology, and reproductive health, evaluated a qualitative content validity of the demographic questionnaire to ensure its clarity, relevance, and appropriateness for the study population.

Perceived barriers and facilitators of physical activity

This section employed the 74-item validated instrument developed by [20]. The instrument assesses six domains: individual and personal factors, cultural and social factors, environmental and infrastructural factors, informational and promotional factors, health and wellness factors, and financial and economic factors. Items are rated on a five‑point Likert scale ranging from very low to very high. Domain scores are calculated by summing the items within each domain, and the total score is obtained by summing all domain scores. Higher scores indicate a greater perceived influence of that domain on physical activity. Facilitators and barriers were assessed using separate sets of items. Participants rated how much each factor helped them engage in physical activity (facilitator items) and, in a separate section, how much each factor hindered their participation (barrier items), and the two scores were calculated independently. The original instrument demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.91), and in the present study, the reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s α) was found to be 0.89 (Supplementary 1).

Physical activity assessment (PASE)

Physical activity was assessed using the Physical Activity Scale for the older adult (PASE), a standardized and widely validated instrument designed to measure physical activity over the preceding seven days in adults with ≥ 65 years [21]. The tool evaluates occupational, household, and leisure time activities. Scoring involves multiplying the duration and frequency of activities by activity-specific weights to generate a total score ranging from 0 to 400, with higher scores indicating greater levels of physical activity. For analytical purposes, participants were categorized according to tertile of the sample distribution of the total PASE score: lower tertile (< 97), middle tertile (98–217), and upper tertile (> 217). These categories correspond approximately to the 25th and 75th percentiles of the PASE score distribution in the study sample.

The Persian version of the PASE demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.94, ICC = 0.99, test–retest r = 0.94), acceptable face and content validity (IS > 1.5, CVR > 0.6, CVI > 0.79), and good model fit for the original three factor structure in confirmatory factor analysis [22]. In the present study, PASE data were collected exclusively through face‑to‑face interviewer administered questionnaires. The third author, who received dedicated training in standardized administration and scoring procedures, conducted all interviews. Internal consistency in the current sample was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which was 0.83.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were calculated. Normality was evaluated visually using Q-Q plots, which indicated that all principal variables exhibited approximately normal distributions. The association between demographic characteristics and PASE tertile categories was examined using the chi‑square test. To examine factors associated with perceived facilitators and barriers to physical activity participation, univariate linear regression analyses were performed using the overall facilitator score and the overall barrier score as dependent variables. Variables with a p‑value < 0.20 in the univariate analysis were entered into a multivariable linear regression model using the backward elimination method. Adjusted regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported.

Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF), and regression assumptions) homoscedasticity, linearity, and normality of residuals) were evaluated and met. Collinearity diagnostics indicated no multicollinearity concerns, and the Durbin Watson statistic confirmed independence of errors, supporting the adequacy of the regression model.

Result

Demographic characteristics

The participants were primarily composed of adults with 66–75 years (Mean ± SD: 66.75 ± 4.43), with an almost equal distribution of men and women. Most participants were married and identified as Shia Muslim. Homemakers and retirees constituted the largest employment groups. Nearly half of the sample had low literacy levels, whereas a smaller proportion had completed university education. Home ownership and perceived adequacy of household income were common. Approximately one-quarter of participants reported at least one chronic condition, and the majority were covered by health insurance (Table 1).

Association between demographic factors and physical activity

Chi‑square analysis showed that several demographic characteristics were significantly associated with PASE tertile categories. Chronic disease was significantly related to physical activity level, with participants who had chronic conditions more frequently classified in the lower tertile (p = 0.002). Gender was also significantly associated with activity level, as men were more likely than women to fall into the upper tertile (p = 0.001). Monthly income demonstrated a significant association as well, with participants reporting adequate or higher income more often classified in the middle and upper tertiles (p = 0.015).

In contrast, age group was not significantly associated with physical activity (p = 0.073), although adults aged 66–75 showed a higher proportion in the lower tertile. Similarly, employment status (p = 0.788), marital status (p = 0.252), religion (p = 0.219), educational level (p = 0.149), housing status (p = 0.901), and health insurance coverage (p = 0.131) were not significantly associated with PASE tertile categories (Table 2).

Perceived barriers and facilitators of physical activity score

Among facilitators, the Health and Fitness domain demonstrated the highest mean score (4.65 ± 0.64), suggesting that perceived health benefits and improvements in physical well-being exerted the strongest motivational influence. This was followed by the Financial and Economic domain (4.50 ± 0.76) and the Environmental Facilities domain (4.46 ± 0.76), reflecting that affordable conditions and accessible facilities substantially encouraged participation. Moderately high facilitator scores were observed in the Individual and Personality (4.38 ± 0.68) and Cultural and Social domains (4.15 ± 0.79), indicating that personal motivation and social context further supported engagement, though to a slightly lesser extent. The Information and Advertising domain had the lowest facilitator mean (3.68 ± 0.73), highlighting a relative deficiency in outreach, communication, and promotion of physical activity opportunities. Overall, the total facilitator score (4.32 ± 0.57) pointed to a generally supportive environment for physical activity among the older adult population.

In contrast, barrier scores were moderate, indicating that although perceived obstacles existed, they were not as influential as the facilitators. The Environmental obstacles domain had one of the highest barriers means (3.91 ± 1.10), suggesting that limitations related to accessibility, safety, or the adequacy of physical environments posed notable challenges.

Health-related limitations were also prominent, as reflected in the Health and Fitness barrier score (3.83 ± 1.55), indicating that physical constraints or medical conditions hindered participation for many individuals. The Financial and Economic domain (3.70 ± 1.60) similarly represented a meaningful barrier, underscoring that financial constraints reduced the ability of some older adults to engage in physical activity. Moderate barrier levels were observed in the Individual and Personality (4.08 ± 0.92) and Cultural and Social (4.04 ± 1.01) domains, indicating that personal and social factors contributed to participation challenges but were not overwhelmingly restrictive. The lowest barrier mean was recorded in the Information and Advertising domain (3.35 ± 1.10), suggesting that limited information or promotional messaging was the least influential hindering factor. Overall, the total barrier score (3.82 ± 0.81) demonstrated that although barriers were present, they were generally less impactful than the facilitators identified (Fig. 2).

Univariate linear regression analysis of perceived facilitators and barriers to physical activity

In the univariate linear regression analysis, disease, monthly income, marital status, employment, education, housing, and health insurance were significantly associated with both facilitators and barriers of physical activity participation among older adults (p < 0.05). Among these variables, monthly income, employment, housing, and health insurance showed positive associations with facilitators, while disease, marital status, and education showed negative associations. Discussion

The present study, conducted in 2025 among older adults in Zanjan, aimed to identify factors influencing participation in physical activity by assessing activity levels and examining related facilitators, barriers, and demographic associations. Most participants were in the lower and middle tertiles of the PASE score distribution, while only 22.2% of participants were in the upper tertile. Physical activity was significantly associated with sex, chronic disease status, and monthly income. This finding is consistent with national evidence from the Iranian STEP survey1, which reported a higher prevalence of insufficient physical activity among women (57.87%) compared with men (41.93%) [23]. However, this comparison should be interpreted cautiously because the STEP survey included a much wider age range of participants, whereas the present study focused exclusively on older adults and excluded individuals older than 75 years. Therefore, the comparison is intended primarily to highlight a similar gender-related pattern rather than to directly compare prevalence estimates across studies.

Several contextual factors may help explain this pattern. In Iran, gender related cultural norms, including restrictions on some outdoor activities and compulsory dress requirements, may influence women’s opportunities and comfort in engaging in physical activity. Such factors can affect both participation in physical activity and how women perceive or report barriers. In addition to these sociocultural influences, the local geographic and urban context of Zanjan is also relevant to the interpretation of the findings. As a midsized industrial city in northwestern Iran, Zanjan may present environmental constraints different from those of larger metropolitan or greener regions. Colder weather during much of the year, together with possible limitations in age-friendly walking spaces and recreational infrastructure, may reduce opportunities for regular outdoor physical activity among older adults. These contextual characteristics should be considered when interpreting the relatively low proportion of participants who achieved adequate physical activity in the present study.

Safavi et al. [24] reported that psychological factors, particularly self‑efficacy and social support, were strong predictors of physical activity among older adults in Rasht, and that higher education was associated with greater activity, while chronic disease and poor economic status were related to lower activity levels [24]. The findings of the present study are partly consistent with those results. In both studies, disease status was related to lower participation in physical activity. However, differences were observed regarding the role of socioeconomic and educational factors and the overall levels of physical activity. These differences may be explained by variations in measurement tools (IPAQ‑SF vs. PASE), scoring methods, and the inclusion of psychosocial variables such as self‑efficacy and social support in the study by Safavi et al., which were not examined in the present research. Beyond methodological differences, the geographic and climatic differences between Rasht and Zanjan may also explain some of the variation in findings. Rasht, with its greener and more humid environment, may be more supportive of outdoor activity, whereas Zanjan’s colder climate may limit such opportunities for older adults.

Ahmadi et al. (2016) found that more than half of older adults in Maku had low levels of physical activity, with only 16% achieving adequate activity. Higher activity levels were associated with younger age, higher education, and employment, whereas chronic disease predicted lower activity. Women also demonstrated higher physical activity scores than men [13]. Although some results differed from those of the present study, both studies identified chronic disease as a negative associated of physical activity. Given that both Maku and Zanjan are located in northwestern Iran, these discrepancies are more likely related to differences in population characteristics and local environmental conditions, such as urban form, walkability, and access to recreational facilities, rather than broad climatic variation.

A cross-sectional study by Farrag et al. [25] among 671 older adults in Mansoura, Egypt, reported that 71% had low physical activity levels. Iran multivariate analysis, male gender, younger age, normal body weight, and current employment were associated with higher physical activity, whereas chronic disease and the use of assistive devices were linked to lower activity levels [25]. These findings are generally consistent with those of the present study. Collectively, the results suggest that a substantial proportion of older adults in the Middle East engage in insufficient physical activity, and that individual and health-related characteristics play critical roles in determining activity levels.

A multicenter study of 2,155 healthy European older adults (mean age 74.9 years) reported that about 62% achieved sufficient physical activity, while 37% spent more than 5.5 h per day in sedentary behavior. Higher activity levels were observed among men, younger older adults (70–74 years), and those with lower BMI, whereas women, older participants, and those with higher BMI were more likely to have prolonged sedentary time [26]. These findings differ from those of the present study, which may be explained by demographic, social, and cultural differences between populations, as well as environmental conditions and methodological variations across studies.

Facilitators and barriers to participation in physical activity

The findings of this study suggest that facilitators and barriers to physical activity among older adults span several domains, including individual, cultural, environmental, informational, health-related, and economic factors. Perceived facilitators were generally high, with the highest scores observed in the Health and Fitness domain, followed by financial and environmental factors. Individual and cultural factors showed moderate levels, while Information and Advertising had the lowest facilitator scores. Overall perceived barriers were moderate, with environmental limitations, health‑related issues, and financial challenges reported as the most prominent barriers, whereas informational barriers were less commonly reported. The multivariable analysis reveals that socioeconomic status fundamentally shapes how older adults perceive the Facilitators and barriers factors influencing their physical activity. A notable finding is the dual role of employment, which increases the perception of both facilitators and barriers, suggesting that working older adults are more sensitive to the complexities of staying active. Conversely, higher education and better living conditions (housing and insurance) were associated with a lower emphasis on both facilitators and barriers. This suggests that socioeconomic stability may simplify the perceived environment, making physical activity appear less dependent on external supports or constraints. In a meta-analysis of 17 studies, Esmi et al. [27] identified cultural and social factors, individual characteristics, motivation, environmental conditions, and economic considerations as key determinants of physical activity among older adults in Iran, emphasizing that coordinated attention to all domains is necessary to effectively support participation. Findings from field-based studies are consistent with this multidimensional perspective [27].

Jannat Alipour et al. [28] reported that the major barriers to physical activity included lack of sports facilities, fear of injury, and managerial or organizational constraints, all of which were significantly associated with educational level, history of disease, and weekly exercise duration [28]. Similarly, Veisia et al. found that social and cultural factors were the most influential determinants of older adults women’s participation, while lack of motivation, time constraints, and inadequate facilities constituted the primary personal and environmental barriers [29].

Comparable findings were reported by Naghibi et al. [16], who identified chronic illness, long distances, limited availability of sports venues, fear of injury, and physical limitations as major impediments to participation in physical activity. In contrast, greater activity levels were observed among older adults who received support from family and peers, had access to suitable facilities, and were encouraged by healthcare providers [16]. Qualitative investigations by Nadri et al. [30] and Sarani et al. [31] further emphasized that detailed examination of facilitators and barriers can inform the planning of sports and recreational services for older adults, noting that strengthening motivational and environmental facilitators while reducing key barriers can substantially enhance participation and improve physical and mental well-being [30, 31].

International evidence similarly underscores the wide-ranging benefits of physical activity for physical, psychological, and cognitive functioning in older adults. In a systematic review, Didi Suryadi et al. [32] found that home-based programs, aerobic training, balance exercises, and dance interventions significantly improved physical and cognitive performance and reduced mortality and disability [32]. Likewise, Zhou et al. [33], in a systematic analysis of intergenerational physical activity, identified 73 facilitators and 37 barriers across seven themes and fourteen subthemes, highlighting the need to simultaneously address individual, social, environmental, and organizational factors for effective implementation [33]. Quintero et al. [34] also showed that higher physical activity levels are positively associated with psychological well-being, including better general health and increased positive affect [34]. A systematic review of studies from 20 MENA countries, Chaabane similarly identified inadequate infrastructure, time constraints, lack of social support, low motivation, restrictive cultural and gender norms, and extreme climatic conditions as major barriers. Lower physical activity was associated with older age, female gender, lower education, and marital status, while key facilitators included health motivation, weight control, family support, being male, healthy diet, and the desire for recreation and social interaction [35].

These findings align with the facilitators and barriers identified in the present study. Here, the Health and Fitness domain showed the highest facilitator mean, whereas Information and Advertising was the weakest facilitator. Among barriers, the individual/personality and cultural/social domains had the highest means, while information-related barriers were minimal. This pattern is consistent with Amini et al. [20], who applied the TOPSIS model to rank facilitators and barriers among older adults in Ahvaz and found that cultural and social factors were perceived as the most influential barriers, while individual and personality factors ranked lowest [20]. The present study corroborates these findings, reinforcing the central role of cultural and social barriers in shaping physical activity participation among older adults.

This study has several notable strengths. The use of a population-based sample of community-dwelling older adults with 60–75 years enhances the representativeness of the findings. Standardized and validated instruments were employed to assess physical activity levels and perceived facilitators and barriers, ensuring reliable and comparable measurements. The concurrent examination of demographic, economic, and environmental correlates provided a comprehensive understanding of the multidimensional determinants of physical activity. Additionally, the use of multivariable regression modeling allowed for the identification of independent variables, strengthening the analytical rigor of the study.

Several limitations of this study should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between independent variables and physical activity levels. Second, the reliance on self-reported questionnaires may have introduced recall bias, leading to potential underreporting or overreporting of physical activity and related factors. Third, the study sample was restricted to urban older adults in Zanjan, limiting the generalizability of the findings to rural populations or other regions of Iran. Finally, the study predominantly employed quantitative methods; the absence of complementary qualitative approaches restricted the ability to capture more nuanced perceptions, motivations, and contextual barriers that influence physical activity behaviors.

Conclusion

The findings of this study suggest that physical activity levels among older adults in Zanjan are relatively low, with a significant proportion failing to meet recommended activity thresholds. Several demographic factors, including sex, chronic disease status, and monthly income, were found to be significantly associated with physical activity participation. Among the perceived facilitators, the health and wellness domain emerged as the most influential, while individual or personality-related and cultural or social domains were identified as the primary barriers to engagement. These results highlight the need for comprehensive, evidence-based, and multidimensional interventions that address the individual, social, environmental, and informational determinants of physical activity among older adults. Targeted strategies to raise awareness, mitigate personal and socio-cultural barriers, and strengthen supportive environments are crucial for fostering sustained participation in physical activity and enhancing the overall well-being of this growing population.
