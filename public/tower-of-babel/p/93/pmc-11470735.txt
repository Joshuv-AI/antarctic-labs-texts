Adverse pregnancy outcomes and multiple cancers risk in both mother and offspring: an umbrella review of systematic reviews with meta-analyses of observational studies
Authors: Na Liu, Rui-Han Bao, Yu-Jiao Chang, Fang-Hua Liu, Lang Wu, Jia-Yi Wang, Zi-Ping Niu, Shuai Ma, Yi-Xuan Men, Ke-Xin Liu, Dong-Hui Huang, Qian Xiao, Song Gao, Yue-Yang Zhao, Jian-Hua Fu, Qi-Jun Wu, Ting-Ting Gong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdverse pregnancy outcomes have reached epidemic proportions in recent years with serious health ramifications, especially for diverse cancers risk. Therefore, we carried out an umbrella review to systematically evaluate the validity and strength of the data and the extent of potential biases of the established association between adverse pregnancy outcomes and cancers risk in both mother and offspring.MethodsPubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases were searched from inception until 18 January 2024. Meta-analyses of observational studies investigating the relationship between adverse pregnancy outcomes and multiple cancers risk in both mother and offspring were included. Evidence certainty was assessed using Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation. The protocol for this umbrella review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42023470544).ResultsThe search identified 129 meta-analyses of observational studies and 42 types of cancer. Moderate certainty of evidence, exhibiting statistical significance, has been observed linking per kilogram increase in birth weight to a heightened risk of breast cancer (OR = 1.07, 95% CI = 1.02–1.12), prostate cancer (OR = 1.02, 95% CI = 1.00–1.05), leukemia (OR = 1.18, 95% CI = 1.13–1.23), and acute lymphoblastic leukemia in offspring (OR = 1.18, 95% CI = 1.12–1.23); rubella infection during pregnancy to an increased risk of leukemia in offspring (OR = 2.79, 95% CI = 1.16–6.71); and a linear dose–response association between an increase in the proportion of optimal birth weight and an elevated risk of acute lymphoblastic leukemia in offspring (OR = 1.16, 95% CI = 1.09–1.24), respectively.ConclusionsAlthough some adverse pregnancy outcomes have clinically promising associations with risk of several cancers in both mother and offspring, it is essential to conduct additional research to solidify the evidence, evaluate causality, and ascertain clinical utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-024-03680-w.Keywords: Cancer, Mother, Observational study, Offspring, Pregnancy, Umbrella review

Background

Although maternal deaths have decreased globally by 38% in the last two decades, the occurrence of adverse pregnancy outcomes has been seen with an increasing trend [1–3]. For instance, worldwide incidence of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy increased by 11% from 1990 to 2019 [4]. In developed countries, up to a third of parous women experienced one or more adverse pregnancy outcomes [1–3]. According to the United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund, a trend analysis has revealed more than 14% of babies are born with low birth weight (LBW). Additionally, approximately 10% of infants are born prematurely, and tragically, every 40 s, one of these preterm newborns succumbs [5]. A growing body of evidence indicates that different types of diseases, such as cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and diabetes, have been strongly associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes [6–8]. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that women who suffer from adverse pregnancy outcomes, along with their offspring, frequently face an elevated risk of developing cancer. A guideline of the National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence has pointed that diabetes in pregnancy plays an essential role in the long-term health condition of mothers and offspring [9]. Prior analyses have identified the relationship between gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and risk of thyroid cancer (TC) of mothers [6, 10]; the level of evidence remained unclear. Nevertheless, numerous studies have presented conflicting findings, leading to questions about the associations between certain adverse pregnancy outcomes and various types of cancer [11, 12]. It has been argued that these associations lacked a robust rationale of intervention or evidence.

Umbrella review (UR), a useful tool, aimed for providing a relatively comprehensive understanding of published systematic reviews with meta-analyses on a specific topic recently [13, 14]. Considering the incompleteness of evidence on this topic [15, 16], we conducted a UR of systematic reviews with meta-analyses evaluating the association between adverse pregnancy outcomes of women and cancers risk in both mother and offspring [17]. Further, we assessed the robustness of the evidence to provide decision-makers with a consolidated source on this subject.

Methods

This UR adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses [18], Preferred Reporting Items for Overviews of Reviews [19], and Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology [20] (Additional file 1: Tables S1–3), and the protocol for this UR was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42023470544).

Literature search

PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases were searched from inception until 18 January 2024 (last update) for systematic reviews with meta-analyses of observational studies which investigated the associations between adverse pregnancy outcomes and multiple cancers risk in both mother and offspring. The exact search strategies for each database were provided in the Additional file (Additional file 1: Table S4). Searches of the electronic databases were supplemented by hand-searching the reference lists of included articles for further potentially pertinent publications. Two independent investigators (NL and Y-JC) separately conducted electronic searches to peruse the title and abstract of each citation and meticulously reviewed the full texts of the identified meta-analyses to identify whether they met the inclusion criteria. Any discrepancy was resolved by a third investigator (Q-JW).

Studies were eligible if they met the following PECOS (Population, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome, and Study design) criteria:

Participants: pregnant mothers or their offspring

Participants: pregnant mothers or their offspring

Exposure: adverse pregnancy outcome (a broad term comprising health problems that occur to the mother, the newborn, or both during pregnancy, labor and delivery, and the postpartum period) [21]

Exposure: adverse pregnancy outcome (a broad term comprising health problems that occur to the mother, the newborn, or both during pregnancy, labor and delivery, and the postpartum period) [21]

Comparisons: participants without adverse pregnancy outcomes

Comparisons: participants without adverse pregnancy outcomes

Outcomes: multiple cancers risk of mothers and their offsprings (e.g., breast cancer [BC], ovarian cancer [OC], TC, leukemia)

Outcomes: multiple cancers risk of mothers and their offsprings (e.g., breast cancer [BC], ovarian cancer [OC], TC, leukemia)

Study design: systematic reviews with meta-analyses of observational studies (e.g., cohort, case–control, or cross-sectional studies)

The exclusion criteria for this UR encompassed meta-analyses that examined the association between adverse pregnancy outcomes and maternal tumor before pregnancy; meta-analyses with insufficient data (the number of cases and controls, risk estimates, or total study population size); studies without quantitative results; or non-English studies, as well as animal and cell culture experiments. Furthermore, in the course of evaluating the evidence in our study, we must recalculate the Egger P value for the included articles that are based on three or more original studies. Consequently, we excluded studies that were based on fewer than three original studies in order to accurately recalculate the Egger P value for grading [22–24].

Associations were considered to overlap if they assessed the same research topic and were examined in more than one systematic review with meta-analysis [25]. In cases where multiple meta-analyses identified the same association, only the one encompassing the most considerable number of primary studies was selected to prevent the inclusion of repetitive studies [26–31]. If more than one meta-analysis contained an equal quantity of studies, the most recent publication was chosen for consideration. In addition, if more than one comparison form was analyzed for a given outcome (e.g., dose–response analysis; highest vs. lowest), all comparison forms were included in our study.

Data extraction and analysis

For every eligible systematic review with meta-analyses, we extracted the first author’s name, year of publication, journal, type of adverse pregnancy outcomes and cancers, number of included studies, comparison, study design, population, and the type of effect metric. We further extracted information on each primary study, including the first author, publication year, study design, the study-specific risk estimates (relative risk [RR] and odds ratio [OR]) and corresponding 95% CIs, along with meta-analysis I2, P values, the number of cases and controls, and comparison form (dose–response analysis; highest vs. lowest) for each study. Two investigators (NL and Y-JC) extracted data independently, and any disagreement was determined by a third investigator (Q-JW).

When a forest plot was not provided, we extracted the first author, publication year, study design, the study-specific risk estimates (RR and OR) and corresponding 95% CIs, the number of cases and controls, and comparison form (dose–response analysis; highest vs. lowest) for each primary study. We appraised the summary effect size and its 95% CIs by using random effects models and calculated the I2 and its CIs metric for heterogeneity for each eligible association [32]. The regression asymmetry test was used to calculate the P value of Egger’s test and test for small study effects that can reflect publication bias [33].

Quality assessment

Two investigators (J-YW and SM) independently evaluated the methodological quality of qualified meta-analyses by using AMSTAR (A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews). Any argument was resolved by a third investigator (Q-JW). As a reliable and valid assessment instrument, AMSTAR consists of 11 items and has good face and content validity for measuring the methodological quality of systematic reviews [34].

In instances where studies lacked an assessment of bias risk, we employed the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) to evaluate the quality of cohort or case–control studies [35].

Assessment of evidence credibility

According to the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE), two investigators (J-YW and SM) evaluated the credibility of evidence from the eligible systematic reviews and meta-analyses. A third investigator (Q-JW) adjudicated the decision in case of discrepancies. According to the GRADE, the evidence was graded as high, moderate, low, or very low quality. Observational studies with initially low-quality evidence can be downgraded due to risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias [36]. A risk of bias was assigned when the weight of studies scoring less than 6 points on the NOS constituted more than 50 [37]. Inconsistency was assigned when heterogeneity measured by the I2 statistic was greater than 50%, and a revised cut-off of I2 > 75% for high heterogeneity [38]. Indirectness was identified when there were differences in PECOS between the original studies and meta-analyses [39]. Imprecision was determined based on an event size smaller than 300 for binary exposure and a total population size smaller than 400 for continuous exposure [40]. Publication bias was detected if the funnel plot appeared asymmetrical or if P values for Egger’s test were < 0.05 [41]. Conversely, the quality of evidence may be upgraded for reasons including a substantial effect size, the presence of a dose–response relationship, or the existence of plausible confounding that would, in all likelihood, underestimate the true effect [42]. The large magnitude of effect was determined when the effect size exceeded 2 or 5 [43].

Sensitivity analysis

We performed several sensitivity analyses to assess whether the credibility of the evidence and the strength of the association varied when the following studies were retained in the analysis. If meta-analyses were excluded due to overlap, we did a reanalysis to verify whether their results were consistent with the main analysis. Associations were considered to overlap if they assessed the same research topic and were examined in more than one systematic review with meta-analysis [25]. Besides, the sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding primary studies with number of study participants lower than 25th percentile and those with low quality [14]. Furthermore, the primary studies graded as low quality identified by a total NOS score of < 6 were excluded [44]. Additionally, we performed a sensitivity analysis by including only adjusted estimates to assess the robustness of the main analysis [45]. All statistical analyses were performed in STATA version 17.0.

Results

Literature review

The search retrieved 17,534 records from three databases. After removal of duplicates, and screening of titles and abstracts, 77 articles qualified for full text screening. Table S5 provides the list of excluded studies from full-text reading, with reason for exclusion (Additional file 1: Table S5). Applying the inclusion–exclusion criteria identified 129 meta-analyses from 34 articles for the present UR (Fig. 1) [11, 12, 46–77].

Characteristics of the included systematic reviews with meta-analyses

The eligible 129 meta-analyses, published from 2007 to 2023, which estimated risk of cancer associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes (Additional file 1: Table S6). Of note, all included meta-analyses were based on cohort or case–control studies but without cross-sectional studies. We observed that the median number of primary studies was 7 (range from 3 to 24), that of the participants was 164,362 (range from 1012 to 11,762,911). Recalculations were conducted for 11 associations due to the absence of a forest plot (Additional file 1: Table S6). These associations primarily focused on investigating the relationship of incomplete pregnancy, spontaneous abortion, and ovarian cancer risk in mother.

The outcomes evaluated in these meta-analyses included BC (n = 28), urogenital neoplasms (n = 29, OC, gestational trophoblastic neoplasia [GTN], cancer of urological organs, uterus-related cancers, testicular cancer, and prostate cancer), hematologic neoplasms (n = 39, leukemia, acute lymphoblastic leukemia [ALL], acute myeloid leukemia [AML], Hodgkin lymphoma, and non-Hodgkin lymphoma), neurologic neoplasms (n = 20, astrocytoma, medulloblastoma, neuroblastoma, ependymoma, and primitive neuroectodermal tumor), and other neoplasms (n = 13).

Adverse pregnancy outcomes included birth weight (n = 49), preterm birth (n = 13), GDM (n = 12), preeclampsia (n = 6), and other exposures (n = 49).

Methodological quality of the meta-analyses

Using the AMSTAR tool, 12 (35.3%) articles were categorized as high quality, whereas 21 (61.8%) and 1 (2.9%) articles were categorized as moderate and low quality, respectively (Additional file 1: Table S7). The median number of the AMSTAR score was 7 (range from 2 to 9).

Strength and credibility grades of evidence

Evaluation of evidence certainty using the GRADE framework revealed that, of the 129 meta-analyses, 8 (6.2%), 76 (58.9%), and 45 (34.9%) meta-analyses were rated as moderate, low, and very low certainty of evidence, respectively. The most common reasons for downgrading were inconsistency (n = 33), publication bias (n = 13), and imprecision (n = 6) (Additional file 1: Table S8). Summary of associations between adverse pregnancy outcomes and cancers risk in offspring

Hematologic neoplasms

Thirty-nine meta-analyses between adverse pregnancy outcomes and incidence of hematologic neoplasms in offspring were investigated. Rubella infection during pregnancy (OR = 2.79, 95% CI = 1.16–6.71) and a per kilogram (kg) increase in birth weight (OR = 1.18, 95% CI = 1.13–1.23) were both associated with an elevated risk of leukemia in offspring. The dose–response associations between increase in birth weight (OR = 1.18, 95% CI = 1.12–1.23), in proportion of optimal birth weight (POBW) (OR = 1.16, 95% CI = 1.09–1.24), and an increased risk of ALL in offspring were observed. POBW is a derived value designed to assess the appropriateness of fetal growth, accounting for the child’s gestational age, gender, birth order, and mother’s height [78]. The above associations were identified with moderate certainty of evidence. Notably, the association between species C adenovirus infection during pregnancy and an increased risk of ALL in offspring was not statistically significant, although it was supported by moderate certainty of evidence. Twenty-six meta-analyses were supported by low-certainty evidence.

Breast cancer

Out of 12 meta-analyses, only one demonstrated moderate-certainty evidence supporting an increased risk of BC in offspring per kilogram increase in birth weight (OR = 1.07, 95% CI = 1.02–1.12). Six meta-analyses demonstrated low certainty of evidence.

Urogenital neoplasms

Two meta-analyses were available for prostate cancer and revealed moderate evidence that per kg increase in birth weight was positively associated with prostate cancer risk in offspring (OR = 1.02, 95% CI = 1.00–1.05). However, the meta-analysis of aggressive or lethal prostate cancer showed no statistical significance. Five meta-analyses revealed low evidence.

Neurologic neoplasms

Fifteen meta-analyses demonstrated low-certainty evidence regarding the risk of neurologic neoplasms in offspring.

Other neoplasms

Among 4 meta-analyses focused on osteosarcoma, bone tumor, and any cancer in offspring, 2 demonstrated low certainty of evidence.

Among 20 meta-analyses, ten showed low-certainty evidence, primarily focusing on OC in mother.

Among 9 meta-analyses associated with TC and cancers of the blood, pancreas, skin, and stomach in mother, six revealed low-certainty evidence. GDM was identified as being associated with an elevated risk of TC and cancers of the blood and stomach.

Sensitivity analysis

First, we performed a sensitivity analysis for excluded meta-analyses due to overlap. A total of 37 associations were removed due to overlap [7, 12, 48, 51, 54, 55, 79–93]. The outcomes included 9 types of cancer (AML, BC, leukemia, ALL, OC, TC, neuroblastoma, primary central nervous system tumor, and any cancer). The certainty of evidence in 9 meta-analyses increased from very low to low. However, the strength of evidence provided by two meta-analyses decreased from low to very low, and 26 meta-analyses remained unchanged (Additional file 1: Table S9).

Moreover, sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding primary studies with number of study participants lower than 25th percentile. The certainty of evidence in three meta-analyses escalated from low to moderate pertaining to the correlations between per 0.5 kg increase in birth weight and an increased risk of BC in mother; high birth weight (HBW) and an elevated risk of BC in offspring; viral infection during pregnancy and an increased risk of leukemia in offspring. Evidence for 6 associations increased from very low to low. However, only one piece of evidence supporting the association between per kg increase in birth weight and risk of aggressive prostate cancer downgraded from moderate to low certainty of evidence. Additionally, 11 associations observed decreased levels of evidence from low to very low, while 84 associations remained unchanged (Additional file 1: Table S10).

Furthermore, we conducted a sensitivity analysis on 23 associations, which excluded primary studies with low strength of evidence. As a result, certainty of evidence for 3 associations decreased from low to very low; the other 20 associations remained unchanged (Additional file 1: Table S11).

Additionally, we performed a sensitivity analysis by including only adjusted estimates to assess the robustness of the main analysis. The certainty of 6 associations downgraded from low to very low and 3 associations upgraded from very low to low. The other 36 associations remained unchanged (Additional file 1: Table S12).

Discussion

Our UR constituted the first comprehensive mapping and appraisal of the association between adverse pregnancy outcomes and multiple cancers risk in both mother and offspring, as provided by published systematic reviews with meta-analyses of observational studies. Overall, 129 associations have been examined encompassing 42 types of cancer. Moderate certainty of evidence with statistical significance was observed between per kg increase in birth weight and increased risk of BC, prostate cancer, leukemia, and ALL in offspring; rubella infection in pregnancy and an elevated risk of leukemia in offspring; a linear dose–response association between an increase in POBW and an increased risk of ALL in offspring, respectively.

Birth weight as a proxy indicator of intrauterine environment has been linked with several hormone-related cancers, including testicular cancer [94–96], prostate cancer [97–99], BC [100, 101], and leukemia [102, 103], while our comprehensive assessment showed that moderate level of evidence class existed in the associations between per kg increase in birth weight and BC [54], leukemia [48], and prostate cancer [62]. Our findings were consisting with a result from a UR published in 2016, which suggested per kg increase in birth weight increased risk of BC and leukemia [16]. Another UR conducted in 2022 by Onyije et al. suggested HBW is one of the few birth-related factors that has been associated with childhood leukemia [15].

Estrogen plays a major role in the etiology of BC [100, 101]. Exposures to hormones during early developmental stages may exert a profound impact on the subsequent risk of BC in adulthood, due to the heightened susceptibility of the fetus to transient hormonal fluctuations. These fluctuations in the hormonal milieu of the fetus are capable of modifying the development of target organs, thereby inducing effects that persist throughout the lifespan [104]. In animal studies, exposure to estrogens during fetal or early postnatal stages has been shown to enhance mammary tumorigenesis. This enhancement is facilitated through alterations in both proliferation and differentiation processes of the mammary gland [105]. Pregnancy estrogen levels have been reported positively associated with birth weight [106–108]. Consequently, HBW may serve as an indirect indicator of early life exposure to elevated levels of pregnancy estrogens.

In addition, birth weight has been reported to be associated with multiple growth factors in the intrauterine environments, like insulin-like growth factor-1, sex steroid hormones, and insulin-like growth factor II [94, 109]. In utero, growth factors are related to the increased total number of stem cells, which may increase the risks of transformation into tumor cells and leukemia [110].

Furthermore, estrogens play a crucial role in maintaining normal tissue homeostasis within the prostate; deviations from optimal levels—either insufficient or excessive—lead to disturbances in glandular growth and the onset of pathological conditions. Recent insights have shed light on the significance and distinct functions of estrogen receptors ER-alpha and ER-beta. Notably, the activation of ER-alpha has been associated with aberrant cellular proliferation, inflammation, and the generation of premalignant lesions. In stark contrast, ER-beta activation is fundamental to the signaling between prostatic stromal and epithelial cells, exerting antiproliferative effects that counterbalance the growth-promoting influence of androgens on epithelial tissues. Such findings underscore the critical role and intricate nature of estrogen interactions in the prostate, with both ER-beta and ER-alpha implicated in the potential induction of deleterious effects [111].

In addition to birth weight, we found rubella infection and species C adenovirus were the risk factors of leukemia in offspring, which was graded as moderate evidence. However, the association between species C adenovirus and risk of leukemia was expected to explain carefully for it showed no statistical significance (P = 0.053). In addition to genetic and immunologic factors, fetal infection can lead to immune dysregulation by initially sensitizing B-cells during the programming of central tolerance, which in turn alters the adaptive immune response. This modification can occur regardless of viral load and may obscurely influence the development of leukemia. Another possibility is that maternal infections (especially those leading to severe illnesses) might result in adverse and other pregnancy outcomes, for example, cesarean delivery [112] and shorter gestational length [113] that, in turn, increase the risk of childhood leukemia [114, 115]. Furthermore, the risk of leukemia may be associated with the use of antibiotics or other medications for treating infections and their symptoms, rather than the infections themselves. Determining whether medication use serves as a mediator or directly influences the causal pathway necessitates additional research.

Some meta-analyses of case–control studies tend to utilize unadjusted estimates which can introduce bias in the observed associations. In the present study, we performed a relevant sensitivity analysis, resulting in some changes in the certainty of evidence. This underscores the importance of incorporating adjusted estimates in future meta-analyses.

To our knowledge, this is the first UR examining the relationship between adverse pregnancy outcomes and the risk of multiple cancers in both mothers and their offspring, thus making a novel contribution to the literature. The large sample size with substantial numbers of cases further enhanced the power of our results. This UR was helpful to summarize complicated and vast amounts of research by comparing the results of individual reviews, which provided an efficient overview of the findings for a particular problem.

Nevertheless, our UR has some limitations. First, although similar, each meta-analysis used different sets of search algorithms and inclusion–exclusion criteria. Therefore, merging meta-analyses led to the possibility of artificially introducing heterogeneity and increasing selection and misclassification bias. Second, the current UR only focused on meta-analyses from observational studies, which may suffer from selection or recall bias. Also, it might be an explanation for the low certainty according to the GRADE suggested rating the credibility of evidence from observational studies, for which the reason of the credibility of evidence in our UR is generally very low. However, given that our topic does not correlate with any interventions, randomized controlled trials could not be encompassed in our UR. Therefore, the inclusion of merely observational studies stood as the most viable approach at this point. Third, we included only systematic reviews that could be quantitatively analyzed; systematic reviews that performed qualitative analysis and meta-analyses that did not provide study-specific data were excluded. Other cancers not evaluated by the quantitative synthesis of systematic reviews might have been ignored. However, we further listed findings from such studies to ensure that any unique cancer was considered (Additional file 1: Table S13). Forth, the reliability of the UR depended directly on the included systematic reviews and meta-analyses and indirectly on the original studies. We could not control for bias in the original studies. Fifth, there is no unified consensus on the evidence grading system and systematic framework for the evaluation of adverse pregnancy outcomes and cancer outcomes. Therefore, we selected the NOS and GRADE systems, which are widely used tools for assessing internal validity and grading evidence, respectively. However, the NOS and GRADE employed in our study may fail to explicitly or comprehensively address critical aspects, such as considerations of exposure timing and relative dose, the heterogeneity of disparities related to social determinants and diverse etiological mechanisms, and biases specific to research on pregnancy and childhood [116].

Conclusions

Our study mapped the current status of evidence on 129 associations between adverse pregnancy outcomes and risk of multiple types of cancer in both mother and offspring. Evidence of moderate certainty, which was statistically significant, demonstrated a correlation between each kg increase in birth weight and an elevated risk of BC, prostate cancer, leukemia, and ALL in offspring. Furthermore, rubella infection during pregnancy was associated with an increased risk of leukemia in offspring. Additionally, a dose–response association was observed between an increase in the POBW and a higher risk of ALL in offspring. Policy makers should consider incorporating adverse pregnancy risk factors as part of the risk assessment for cancer in clinical guidelines. Nevertheless, evidence is limited by study quality, heterogeneity, etc. Further research is required to overcome the limitations of the existing evidence.
