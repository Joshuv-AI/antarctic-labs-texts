Indocyanine green angiography in uveitis
Authors: Luca De Simone, Anna Byszewska, Abdulrahman F. Albloushi, Riccardo Genovese, Ioannis Papasavvas, Carl P. Jr Herbort
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) represents an indispensable imaging modality for the precise evaluation and management of posterior uveitis by allowing to explore the choroidal compartment. ICGA leverages near-infrared fluorescence and extensive protein binding to provide an unhindered, dynamic assessment of the choriocapillaris and choroidal stroma. This review explores the principles, standard protocols, and clinical applications of ICGA in posterior uveitis. It details ICGA’s critical role in classifying and diagnosing primary inflammatory choriocapillaropathies, like multiple evanescent white dot syndrome or serpiginous-like choroiditis, and stromal choroiditis, including Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada disease, birdshot chorioretinitis, and sarcoidosis. Furthermore, its utility in infectious uveitis, such as presumed intraocular tuberculosis and syphilitic chorioretinitis, is discussed. ICGA uniquely reveals subclinical choroidal lesions, such as hypofluorescent dark dots and geographic nonperfusion, which frequently exceed clinical or fluorescein angiography findings. By mapping the true extent of choroidal inflammatory activity, ICGA decisively guides the initiation and escalation or safe tapering of systemic corticosteroid and immunosuppressive therapies. Despite emerging noninvasive alternatives, ICGA cannot be fully replaced; it must be integrated into a multimodal imaging approach to optimize long-term visual outcomes and prevent irreversible structural damage in patients with complex chorioretinal inflammation.

INTRODUCTION AND HISTORICAL ASPECTS

Indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) for routine clinical use became available in the early 1990s, with Lawrence A. Yannuzzi’s group at Manhattan Eye, Ear, and Throat Hospital being an early and significant promoter of this imaging modality.[12] Hence, three decades after the advent of fluorescein angiography (FA) which revolutionized imaging of the retina,[3] this second dye angiographic method allowed imaging access to the choroid which was not possible with FA because the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) was a barrier to the diffusion of fluorescein fluorescence emitting in the visible light spectrum. Thanks to the near infrared fluorescence of the ICG molecule, the vascular structures of the choroid could be analyzed with precision [Figure 1].

Indocyanine green (ICG) angiography can analyze precisely the choroidal circulation. ICG fluorescence (and not cyanescence) in the near-infrared spectrum is detected through the retinal pigment epithelium

The first ICGA articles on uveitis conditions were published by Japanese groups between 1993 and 1995, including a case report on multifocal choroiditis (MFC) and two case series on Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada (VKH) disease, both choroidal-centered diseases.[456] In 1995, a European group published the first report on ICGA in a series of several different inflammatory conditions of the choroid.[7] In 1998, the first comprehensive approach to the interpretation of ICGA in inflammatory choroidal diseases was published.[8] During the 1st years, ICGA was predominantly used in Europe and Japan, while it was widely underused in the USA. “In the early years, ICGA was predominantly used in Europe and Japan, whereas it remained underused in the USA. In a review article written after 20 years of ICGA practice, entitled ’Indocyanine green angiography: a perspective on use in the clinical setting,’ Yannuzzi noted that ’the biggest enigma related to ICGA is its low use in the USA compared with the rest of the world.”[9] Therefore, ICGA was integrated with some delay in US uveitis publications. Moreover, the crucial and specific importance of ICGA in inflammatory choroidal diseases had to be reasserted during the trend around 2010, when the general use of ICGA was downplayed by asking the question whether ICGA was still relevant.[1011] In 2010, a dual FA/ICGA angiographic scoring system was designed, allowing quantitative evaluation of both retinal and choroidal inflammatory involvement in posterior uveitis.[12] As early as 2005, ICGA was at the origin of the reclassification of noninfectious choroiditis into choriocapillaritis and stromal choroiditis to replace the obsolete misnomer of white dot syndromes [Figure 2].[1314]

Pattern 1 = Choriocapillaritis entities, including the main entities of multiple evanescent white dot syndrome (MEWDS), multifocal choroiditis (MFC), acute posterior multifocal placoid pigment epitheliopathy (APMPPE) and serpiginous choroiditis (SC). Pattern 2 = Stromal choroiditis including Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada (VKH) disease, birdshot chorioretinitis (BCR), sympathetic ophthalmia (SO), and sarcoidosis choroiditis

Angiography is one of the essential procedures performed in uveitis to complement clinical appraisal of the patient with intraocular inflammation of the posterior segment. Angiography may be performed to confirm elements already revealed by clinical examination or other investigational methods such as optical coherence tomography (OCT). A second reason to perform an angiography is to better grade the inflammation of the fundus. A third reason is to make a good baseline inventory of inflammatory involvement to subsequently use it for follow-up purposes. In follow-up situations, angiography is usually performed to monitor disease intensity and the impact of therapy.

FA has been performed for more than 50 years. Because fluorescein sodium fluoresces in the wavelengths of the visible light, it mainly gives information on the superficial structures of the fundus and therefore mostly only confirms signs already known to the clinician, as in most instances, OCT is already available when FA is performed.

ICGA allows imaging of the choroid that was poorly accessible to imaging beforehand. The procedure, in contrast to FA, often gives additional information, undetected by clinical examination, FA, or OCT. Therefore, to make a proper assessment of inflammatory involvement in uveitis, ICGA is indispensable as it gives information otherwise lost. ICGA has very often a diagnostic value, which is rarely the case for FA. For all these reasons, in most cases, where angiographic workup is decided and choroidal involvement cannot be excluded, dual FA and ICGA should be performed.

For all these reasons, ICGA represents without any doubt a major progress in the global appraisal of posterior uveitis, allowing to measure the contribution of the choroid to the inflammatory process.

PRINCIPLES AND TECHNIQUE OF INDOCYANINE GREEN ANGIOGRAPHY FOR UVEITIS

The molecule: Indocyanine green

ICG fluoresces at 830 nm and therefore gives access to the choroidal vascular structures through the RPE. The physicochemical process for ICG is indeed fluorescence and not cyanescence, a misnomer that came up recently and is repeated in many publications. The principles of ICGA imaging of the choroid are illustrated in Figure 3.

Principles of indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) imaging of the choroid in a noninflamed eye. The ICGA molecule is a small molecule of 775 Daltons (a1). However, 99% of ICG is bound to large proteins forming a macromolecular complex of ±80’000 Daltons. The 1% of free ICG might be taken up by the retinal pigment epithelium with a negligible impact on late fluorescence in ICGA interpretation (a2). The macromolecular ICG-protein complex is freely extruding from the large fenestrations of the choriocapillaris (b1) and is progressively impregnating the choroidal stroma (b2). The large ICG-protein complex remains entrapped in the choroidal stroma because wash-out of this large molecular complex is slow (c)

Luminescence is a physical phenomenon first formally described by the German Physicist Eihard Wiedemann (1852–1928) in 1888.[15] He defined luminescence as the emission of light not resulting from heat. In modern physics, luminescence refers to the spontaneous emission of photons from a substance that has absorbed energy from an external source. The excitation energy may originate from various stimuli. When it concerns light, it is named photoluminescence or fluorescence. The emitted light has a longer wavelength (lower energy) than the absorbed radiation. There is no recognised physical phenomenon termed “cyanescence” in the scientific literature; the term does not correspond to any established mechanism of light emission.

The molecular weight difference between ICG (775 Daltons) and fluorescein (354 Daltons) molecules does not account for the specific angiographic pattern obtained with ICG as compared with fluorescein. Besides the different wavelength at which ICG fluoresces, the crucial difference between these two fluorescing molecules comes from their binding affinity to proteins. The ICG molecule is nearly completely protein-bound, predominantly to large proteins, especially lipoproteins.[1617] Fluorescein leaks readily from slightly inflamed retinal vessels with minor damage to the blood–retinal barrier and readily impregnates tissues, whereas only major damage to retinal vessels allows ICG to leak. In the choroid, however, ICG leaks unimpaired but slowly from the fenestrated choriocapillaris. During recirculation, more ICG is entrapped in the choroidal tissue as the ICG-protein complex (approximately 80,000 Daltons) is only slowly reabsorbed into the circulation. Gradual impregnation of the choroid occurs with time, causing intermediate and late choroidal background fluorescence. A persisting fluorescing background cannot be due to RPE uptake of the 1% free ICG molecules, and uptake of the ICG-protein macromolecule weighing more than 80’000 Daltons is impossible. The choroidal impregnation by ICG fluorescence is disturbed by choroidal inflammatory lesions, causing areas of decreased or absent fluorescence surrounded by increased fluorescence due to leakage of large choroidal vessels. In case of choriocapillaritis, ICG flow is interrupted, causing irregular areas of hypofluorescence. It is the alteration of the slow choroidal impregnation process that is the main parameter studied in ICGA performed for posterior uveitis.[18]

Indocyanine green angiography in the normal eye

ICGA is the method of choice to investigate and follow inflammatory lesions of the choroid. Early frames taken until 3 min (early phase) show the early choroidal circulation [Figure 4]. Two angiographic phases are, however, principally relevant and crucial in inflammatory diseases. In the intermediate phase (9 ± 3 minutes), the ICG molecule is still in the general circulation, and this phase shows both the retinal and the choroidal circulations [Figure 5]. It clearly shows the pattern of choroidal vessels in positive delineated by ICGA fluorescence. Within the retinal and choroidal vessels, during the late phase (25 ± 8 minutes), a choroidal vascular pattern is seen in negative (dark), outlined by the background ICGA fluorescence in the choroidal stroma, while there is no more fluorescence within the choroidal or retinal vessels [Figure 6]. ICGA: Indocyanine green angiography

Standard indocyanine green angiographic protocol for inflammatory diseases

A standard ICGA protocol to analyze choroiditis has been designed. The angiographic procedure comprises 3 main phases: the early phase up to 2–3 min showing superimposed retinal and choroidal large vessels and incipient exudation of the dye through the choriocapillaris into the choroidal stroma, the intermediate phase at about 10 min shows maximum choroidal stromal background fluorescence and the late phase at about 28–32 min shows wash-out of the dye from the general circulation with the large choroidal vessels appearing dark against the background stromal fluorescence.

General preparation

ICG dye contains up to 5% sodium iodide; therefore, patients should be counseled regarding potential thyroid hyperfunction risks and any history of iodide-associated allergic reactions. In that case, iodide-free ICG-dye should be used (Infracyanin®, SERB Pharmaceuticals). Before the proper imaging procedure, the patient should have a peripheral intravenous access placed, as with any procedure involving contrast or dye administration. This is essential for prompt administration of drugs or fluids in case of adverse reactions.

Before dye injection, obtain preinjection fundus autofluorescence (FAF) images and red-free images.

Single indocyanine green angiography protocol

Inject an intravenous bolus of 50 mg ICG (e.g., Verdye, IC-GREEN) diluted in 7.5 ml of 0.9% normal saline (note that brand names may vary by region). The imaging protocol is divided into three distinct stages:

Combined indocyanine green angiography and fluorescein angiography protocol

Often, ICGA and FA are combined in the same session to provide complementary information.

Begin with the ICGA sequence.

At the end of the ICGA intermediate phase (±15 min after ICG injection), inject fluorescein sodium (standard dosage).

If the device enables simultaneous acquisition of both protocols (combined ICG/FA protocol), this option should be used. Then both modalities can be captured continuously until 30 min following the ICG injection.

However, if the combined protocol is not available, proceed with FA imaging alone after completing 15 min of ICG capturing.

FA imaging consists of three phases: early phase (0-2 min) with posterior pole imaging, followed by the midphase (4-7 min) with posterior pole and peripheral imaging, followed by the late phase (10-15 min) with posterior pole and peripheral imaging.

After completing FA imaging, approximately 15 min following fluorescein injection, perform late ICGA frames (±30 min after initial ICG injection) [Figure 7].

HOW TO INTERPRET INDOCYANINE GREEN ANGIOGRAPHY

When analyzing ICG in posterior inflammatory disorders crucial differences with FA interpretation have to be borne in mind to correctly analyze the images obtained.[10] During initial circulation, ICGA is comparable to FA showing the passage through arteriovenous compartments except that it shows superimposed retinal and choroidal circulations. The difference occurs during recirculation time when ICG progressively leaks out from the fenestrated choriocapillaris, gradually and physiologically impregnating the whole choroidal thickness.

This process can be altered in two ways that can be associated in the same disease, either there is decreased fluorescence or increased fluorescence.

Indocyanine green hypofluorescence

The impregnation of the choroidal space can be absent (hypofluorescence) either (1) by a decrease of the physiological extrusion of ICG from the fenestrated choriocapillaris (nonperfusion or hypoperfusion) or (2) by the impairment of the filling of the choroidal tissue by the ICG molecule because of the presence of space-occupying lesions (inflammatory foci). The main mechanisms and angiographic patterns of ICGA hypofluorescence in inflammatory choroidal diseases are summarized in Table 2. These lesions are hypofluorescent in the intermediate angiographic phase. If they remain hypofluorescent in the late phase this signifies that the inflammatory lesion occupies the whole thickness of the choroidal stroma. When lesions become isofluorescent in the late angiographic phase, inflammation causes only partial thickness infiltrates. Therefore, in ICGA performed for inflammatory disorders, the main information is obtained less from the analysis of the early circulatory phase than from the analysis of the altered pattern of the filling of the choroidal space.

Indocyanine green hyperfluorescence

Impregnation of the choroidal space can be enhanced (hyperfluorescence) by increased leakage from the larger choroidal vessels. The vessels appear fuzzy in the intermediate time frames and extrusion of the dye from large vessels normally impermeable causes late diffuse hyperfluorescence.

In case of the presence of inflammatory foci in the choroidal stroma, hyperfluorescence is associated with hypofluorescent dark dots (HDDs) due to inflammatory infiltrates which impede the diffusion of the dye.

Hyperfluorescence occurs in three relevant situations: (1) diffuse late phase hyperfluorescence due to leakage from precapillary or larger nonfenestrated choroidal vessels, (2) disc hyperfluorescence indicating severe inflammation, and (3) hyperfluorescent pinpoints occurring in granulomatous choroidal disease. In most cases, unlike in FA where most pathologies produce hyperfluorescence, the lesions in ICGA are mostly seen in a negative dark pattern (hypofluorescence) due to impaired propagation of physiological choroidal fluorescence.

Most clinicians are very familiar with FA as a diagnostic tool in uveitis which is therefore usually performed routinely whereas ICGA is too often omitted although the information gathered from ICGA reveals often occult inflammatory findings not available otherwise. At the first presentation, it is not possible to know whether choroidal structures are involved justifying routine dual FA/ICGA angiography which, during follow-up can be limited to FA if no choroidal inflammation is identified. ICGA allows to quantify choroidal inflammatory involvement and together with FA allows to score the respective degree of inflammation of the retinal and choroidal compartments determine quantitatively the contribution of both these structures to posterior uveitis.[12]

ICGA differs from FA in several aspects and requires a different approach both in terms of image acquisition and interpretation.

From a practical standpoint, it is essential to complete the full ICGA imaging protocol, including late-phase imaging up to 30 min, to ensure that no valuable diagnostic information is missed. This contrasts with FA in uveitis assessment, which is typically performed for up to 15 min.

FA primarily provides information about the retinal circulation and RPE status. In FA, RPE abnormalities appear as hyperfluorescent spots due to window defects. This phenomenon does not occur in ICGA. Alterations of the RPE are not visualized as window defects because the RPE does not act as a screening layer for ICG dye in the same way it does for fluorescein.

Similarly, hypofluorescence in ICGA is not caused by minor overlying abnormalities such as small hemorrhages, unless the material covering the choroid is sufficiently thick to block the signal. Hypofluorescence may, however, be observed in the presence of heavily pigmented or dense retinal structures, such as myelinated nerve fibers, postlaser scars. These are typically present with sharp, well-defined borders that remain unchanged throughout all phases of the examination.

In ICGA investigation of choroiditis, the optic disc remains hypofluorescent unless the inflammation is very severe such as in severe acute VKH disease, in contrast to FA where the optic disc is hyperfluorescent.

Accurate evaluation of ICGA requires experience. Subtle changes may only become apparent after repeated examinations, allowing clinicians to appreciate differences in certain time periods. Such expertise undoubtedly develops with the number of examinations performed. For this reason, clinicians are encouraged to perform ICGA whenever choroidal involvement in posterior uveitis cannot be excluded.

Importantly, interpretation of ICGA findings should always be integrated into a comprehensive patient assessment. This includes detailed medical history, evaluation of systemic clinical findings, and thorough ophthalmic examination. Multimodal imaging documentation such as color fundus photography, blue-light FAF, FA, and OCT provides complementary information about the structures involved.

THE SPECTRUM OF CHORIOCAPILLARITIS

The lesion process in choriocapillaritis consists of inflammatory nonperfusion of the choriocapillaris occurring at different levels of the choriocapillaris vascular tree determining entities with different grades of severity from the benign multiple evanescent white dot syndrome (MEWDS) disease at one end to the severe choriocapillaritis serpiginous choroiditis (SC) at the malignant end of the spectrum.[19] The consequence of choriocapillaris nonperfusion is ischemia, limited to reversible photoreceptor damage in MEWDS because photoreceptors, metabolically very demanding, are very sensitive to ischemia. On the other side, the RPE cell is more resistant, only damaged in severe choriocapillaritis such as SC [Figure 8].[20]

Vascular and cellular factors at the base of the spectrum on primary inflammatory choriocapillaropathies (PICCPs). Differential sensitivities to hypoxia of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and photoreceptor cells and diverse levels of choriocapillaris nonperfusion determine the spectrum of PICCPs. In mild forms involving end-choriocapillary vessels such as in multiple evanescent white dot syndrome and mild/early multifocal choroiditis only photoreceptors are damaged producing fundus autofluorescence hyperautofluorescence because of loss of the photoreceptor screen of RPE fluorescence

Primary inflammatory choriocapillaropathies or primary choriocapillaritis

To better understand the choriocapillaritis pathomechanism we should refresh our knowledge in choriocapillaris anatomy. The choriocapillaris has numerous anastomotic vessels, but is functionally divided into lobules, with a central arteriolar feeder and an array of draining venules. The terminal arteriole supplies an independent lobule from the center, dividing into choriocapillaris vessels and subsequently into end-capillaries [Figure 9].[21]

Choroidal circulation. Schematic drawing of the vasculature of choroid from the posterior ciliary artery to the stromal arteries, to the feeder vessel of each choriocapillaris lobule and to the end-capillaries of the choriocapillaris

The spectrum of primary inflammatory choriocapillaropathies (PICCPs) is based on the size of the vessels affected, beginning from the more benign entity of MEWDS (affecting the end capillaries) to the most severe namely SC [Figure 10].[19] The latter is a severe inflammation of arterioles of the anterior stroma creating non perfusion of the feeder vessel of multiple choriocapillaris lobules and is presented with the characteristic serpent-like appearance in the fundus. PICCP lesions in ICGA are small to large geographic areas of hypofluorescence and the area size is related to the size of the vessel affected. The geographic areas of hypofluorescent are by far more extended in ICGA compared to the fundus examination.

Different sizes of vessels affected determine the different severity of the primary inflammatory choriocapillaropathy spectrum. Multiple evanescent white dot syndrome is at the benign end as the small end-capillaries are affected while serpiginous choroiditis is the most severe condition, affecting larger vessels and multiple choriocapillaris lobules

Multiple evanescent white dot syndrome

As mentioned above, MEWDS is the most benign PICCP because it is affecting the smallest choriocapillaris vessels, the end-capillaries. Consequently, peripheral inflammation of the peripheral vascular tree in MEWDS produces an ICGA semiology slightly different from other choriocapillaritis entities that led some clinicians away from the vascular explanation of MEWDS. Indeed, early frames only show faint hypofluorescence since the nonperfused areas are very small and overshadowed by the surrounding hyperfluorescence. The nonperfused areas are more clearly identified in the intermediate and late ICGA phases when the surrounding fluorescence decreases.[2223] Thus, ICGA is demonstrating the perfusion loss by multiple hypofluorescent small areas that, in some cases, are present from the early to the late stages of the angiogram [Figure 11], while in other cases, the lesions are visible from intermediate to late phases. The hypofluorescent lesions are better delineated in later stages of the angiogram and disappear in the healing phase.

Acute posterior multifocal placoid pigment epitheliopathy/acute multifocal ischemic choriocapillaritis

Acute posterior multifocal placoid pigment epitheliopathy (APMPPE) was first described by Gass in 1968, who believed that the primary affected tissue was the RPE,[24] giving it the eponym by which the entity is known today. However, Deutman in 1972 observed nonperfusion in early FA frames and correctly put forward the theory of choriocapillaris non perfusion as the cause of this pathology and entitled the entity acute multifocal ischemic choriocapillaritis (AMIC).[2526]

When ICGA became available, this hypothesis was confirmed although the name of the condition remained unchanged.

APMPPE/AMIC is in the middle of the spectrum of PICCPs, as some cases can be mild, while others are more severe functionally especially when macula is involved. Inflammatory occlusion affects the feeder vessel of the choriocapillaris lobule,[27] and thus the whole lobule or several lobules are not perfused, in contrast to MEWDS where only part of the lobule is not perfused. On ICGA, APMPPE/AMIC appears as geographic areas of hypofluorescences which are more widespread and confluent. Interestingly, we could show inflamed hyperfluorescent vessels (linear or pinpoint hyperfluorescence) adjacent to the hypofluorescent geographic areas interpreted as the inflamed vessel at the origin of the occlusion [Figure 12, green arrows].[28] APMPPE rarely, can also cause cerebral vasculitis and brain imaging should performed in the case symptoms.

Idiopathic multifocal choroiditis

Idiopathic MFC should also be placed in the middle of the spectrum of choriocapillaritis. Like APMPPE, the involvement can be minimal to severe. The initial episode can be mistaken for MEWDS as the ICGA findings can be strictly similar. Only when the disease recurs and scars become visible the diagnosis is then revised to MFC. In other cases, the presence of scars in the fundus orients the diagnosis toward MFC.[29] In ICGA, old scars presented dark hypofluorescent from the early to the late stages and the density of the hypofluorescence does not change through the angiogram [Figure 13, green arrows]. New lesions present hypofluorescent from the early or intermediate phase, distributed at random in the fundus, better delineated at late stages [Figure 13, yellow arrows] as hypofluorescence density is more marked in later stages.

Serpiginous choroiditis

SC lies at the most severe end of the spectrum of primary choriocapillaritis as it affects larger vessels, at the limit of the anterior choroidal stroma and the choriocapillaris.[19] SC progresses fast and relentlessly if not treated rapidly and vigorously. In the absence of adequate immunosuppressive treatment, the condition will evolve and cause irreversible chorioretinal atrophy and severe functional impairment. ICGA just confirms the diagnosis as fundus examination is, most of the time, sufficient to recognize the condition.

The signs in ICGA are compatible with severe choriocapillaris non perfusion. Hypofluorescent geographic areas are more prominent than the other choriocapillaries and present as dense hypofluorescent geographic areas [Figure 14] since the early stages of the ICGA. In late stages of the angiogram, we notice a hyperfluorescent area around the affected choriocapillaris which presents a diffuse inflammation of the choroidal stroma, adjacent to the nonperfused choriocapillaris and is a sign of disease progression.[19] More frequently lesions start from the optic nerve and extend in a serpent pattern through the posterior pole and midperiphery if not treated. In some cases, at presentation, lesions do not have the classic serpent aspect but if not treated properly the clinical picture progresses to the classical serpent pattern [Figure 14].

Nonclassifiable choriocapillaritis

Besides the four phenotypically well-determined entities exposed hereabove, many atypical cases of choriocapillaritis can develop. Mixed forms have been described associating the characteristics of two different choriocapillaritis phenotypes such as APMPPE and SC and was called “relentless placoid chorioretinitis” or “ampiginous choroiditis,” conditions that combined the lesions of APMPPE and the evolution of SC.[3031] This condition is well illustrated by the case of a 31-year-old patient had been treated elsewhere for two episodes of APMPPE with short courses of systemic corticosteroids. Between these two episodes lesions had progressed. The patient was seen in our center for novel episodes of photopsias. At presentation visual acuity was 0.5 sc OD and 0.8 sc OS. FAF showed extended areas of hypoautofluorescence bordered with some areas of hyperautofluorescence, indicating disease activity [Figure 15]. ICGA showed colocalized areas of hypofluorescence [Figure 15]. The patient was treated with triple immunosuppression including 60 mg of corticosteroids tapered to 0 over a period of 3 months, cyclosporine (3 mg/kg) tapered over a period of 6 months and a prolonged therapy of mycophenolic acid (720 mg twice daily). Under this therapeutical approach, lesions remained stable and did not progress further.

Moreover, patients have been described who presented as one entity such as MEWDS and subsequently evolved into MFC.[32] or that first presented with a choriocapillaritis, MFC and later developed into a primary photoreceptoritis, acute zonal occult outer retinopathy (AZOOR).[33] Many more reports of the occurrence of diverse PICCP conditions in the same patient have been published.[34]

Parainfectious choriocapillaritis

In PICCPs the trigger is not known although in more than 50% of cases a flu-like episode is preceding or is concomitant with the choriocapillaritis orienting toward a possible implication of a viral agent.

In two choriocapillaritis conditions the infectious trigger at the origin of the immune mediated choriocapillaris nonperfusion is known, tuberculous antigens on one side in case of tuberculosis (TB)-related SC and treponema pallidum in case of acute syphilitic posterior placoid chorioretinitis (ASPPC).

Tuberculosis-related serpiginous choroiditis

SC is a severe progressing and cicatrizing choriocapillaritis that can be caused by occult tubercular antigens persisting in the RPE.[3536] Therefore, in all cases of SC patients, exposure to the tuberculous bacilli should be searched by performing an interferon gamma release assay (IGRA). In case of positivity the case should be considered as a TB-related SC and TB quadruple therapy should be given in addition to multiple immunosuppressants. ICGA is best establishing the extent of lesions (choriocapillaris nonperfusion) and monitoring the response of treatment [Figure 17].

Acute syphilitic posterior placoid chorioretinitis

One of the possible manifestations of ocular syphilis is ASPPC, an immunologically derived closure of the choriocapillaris determining areas of ICGA hypofluorescence corresponding to FAF hyperautofluorescence due to ischemic loss of photoreceptor outer segments shown on OCT [Figure 18]. ICGA precisely shows the hypofluorescent areas of nonperfusion and is crucial in monitoring resolutions of lesions after the introduction of antitreponemal therapy.

THE ROLE OF INDOCYANINE GREEN ANGIOGRAPHY IN THE DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS OF OUTER RETINAL DISEASES

ICGA represents a cornerstone modality for differentiating inflammatory choroidal disorders from primary outer retinal diseases. Despite major advances in OCT and OCT angiography (OCTA), these techniques do not fully replicate the ability of ICGA to dynamically assess choroidal circulation.[1838]

A critical challenge in clinical practice is distinguishing true inflammatory choriocapillaropathies from disorders primarily involving the photoreceptors or RPE. This distinction has direct therapeutic implications, as misclassification may lead to inappropriate immunosuppressive therapy. ICGA provides a decisive advantage by revealing areas of hypofluorescence, which typically correspond to choriocapillaris nonperfusion or inflammatory choroidal stromal infiltration.[183839]

In PICCPs, ICGA consistently demonstrates hypofluorescent lesions that often exceed those visible clinically, on OCT, or on FA. This discrepancy supports the concept that the primary pathology resides at the level of the choroidal circulation rather than the outer retina.[1838] In APMPPE, for example, ICGA reveals large, geographic hypofluorescent areas corresponding to lobular choriocapillaris ischemia, frequently evolving over time and identifying extensive subclinical lesions.[27]

Furthermore, in conditions like MEWDS, ICGA plays a unique role. While primarily considered an outer retinopathy or photoreceptoritis, ICGA often reveals hypofluorescent areas that outnumber the clinically visible white dots. These areas correspond to transient choriocapillaris hypoperfusion, highlighting the complex interplay between the outer retina and the choroid.[40]

Conversely, diseases primarily affecting the outer retina, such as AZOOR and acute macular neuroretinopathy, typically show minimal or no abnormalities on ICGA despite significant structural alterations on OCT.[414243] In these cases, ICGA serves as a negative discriminator, supporting a primary retinal rather than choroidal origin of the pathology.

Beyond choriocapillaropathies, ICGA is unparalleled in evaluating primary stromal choroiditis, such as VKH disease and birdshot chorioretinitis (BCR). In these conditions, OCT may show outer retinal undulations, subretinal fluid, or secondary photoreceptor loss, but ICGA uniquely reveals the presence of deep HDDs representing active granulomatous stromal infiltrates.[4445] Monitoring these ICGA lesions is crucial, as they can persist subclinically despite the complete resolution of outer retinal findings on OCT, dictating the duration and tapering of systemic immunosuppression.

Another critical differential diagnosis involves the pachychoroid disease spectrum, notably central serous chorioretinopathy (CSCR). CSCR can present with outer retinal atrophy, subretinal fluid, and RPE alterations that might mimic posterior uveitis. ICGA is pivotal in this scenario, demonstrating characteristic choroidal vascular hyperpermeability and dilated Haller vessels (pachyvessels) rather than the ischemic or true inflammatory patterns.[46]

ICGA also plays a role in differentiating inflammatory diseases from masquerade syndromes. Intraocular lymphoma, for instance, may mimic posterior uveitis clinically and on OCT. However, ICGA often reveals ill-defined or patchy hypofluorescence without the characteristic lobular or geographic patterns seen in inflammatory choriocapillaropathies.[47] While not pathognomonic, such patterns may raise suspicion and prompt cytological or histological evaluation.

Importantly, ICGA should not be interpreted in isolation. Integration with FAF, OCT, enhanced depth imaging (EDI)-OCT and OCTA is essential for accurate diagnosis. While ICGA provides unmatched sensitivity for detecting active choroidal involvement and remodeling, OCT offers complementary information on photoreceptor and RPE integrity. OCTA provides depth-resolved information on flow deficits, but it is fundamentally limited by its inability to detect low-flow choriocapillaris nonperfusion as in MEWDS, active vascular leakage, projection artifacts, and a limited capability to penetrate deep into thick inflammatory granulomas.[4849]

In conclusion, ICGA remains indispensable in the differential diagnosis of outer retinal diseases by enabling the identification, characterization, or exclusion of choroidal involvement. Its greatest clinical value lies not only in confirming inflammatory choroidal disease but also in preventing misdiagnosis and avoiding unnecessary systemic treatments in conditions where the primary pathology resides strictly within the outer retina.

STROMAL CHOROIDITIS

Stromal choroiditis comprises a distinct group of intraocular inflammatory disorders primarily involving the choroidal stroma, often characterized by granulomatous or diffuse stromal infiltration detectable through multimodal imaging, particularly ICGA and EDI-OCT. From a pathogenic and clinical perspective, these entities can be broadly classified into primary forms, in which the choroid represents the principal target tissue from which inflammation originates, and secondary forms, where stromal choroidal inflammation occurs as part of a broader systemic inflammatory disease. This distinction is clinically relevant, as it guides both diagnostic reasoning and therapeutic strategy.

Primary stromal choroiditis

Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada disease

VKH disease is an autoimmune condition directed against antigens found on or associated with uveal and systemic melanocytes.[505152] The disease typically affects individuals with dark skin and it has a slight female predominance. It is most common in Asians (particularly East/Southeast), Hispanics, Native Americans, and Middle Eastern populations.[53] The initial-onset acute uveitis associated with VKH disease typically presents with bilateral primary stromal granulomatous choroiditis, secondary exudative retinal detachment, and optic nerve head hyperemia, with or without swelling. Subsequently, the inflammation involves the anterior segment if not treated promptly. Consequently, the disease will progress to a chronic, recurrent stage with granulomatous anterior segment inflammation and a typical “sunset glow fundus” (SGF) and chorioretinal atrophy.[54555657]

ICGA is an established method for examining and monitoring choroidal inflammatory lesions, as it allows visualization of choroidal vessels and stroma. During the subclinical phase of VKH disease, the inflammation exclusively starts within the choroidal stroma. The full-blown clinical picture with exudative retinal detachment is a secondary phenomenon. The histopathology of the choroid is characterized by extensive inflammatory infiltrates organized as a diffuse lymphocyte infiltrates and as large granulomas consisting of epithelioid cells and multinucleated giant cells [Figure 19].[5859] Therefore, ICGA remains the gold standard method for assessing cases with an initial onset of acute uveitis associated with VKH disease. In our previous study, we demonstrated that VKH disease may present unilateral exudative retinal detachment; however, proper assessment of the choroid with ICGA will always show bilateral choroidal involvement.[60] This highlights the importance of ICGA in establishing the diagnosis of VKH disease and excluding other diagnoses [Figure 20].

Sympathetic ophthalmia

Sympathetic ophthalmia (SO) is a rare, bilateral diffuse granulomatous uveitis that usually occurs following penetrating ocular trauma or surgery involving the uvea in one eye. The disease is very similar to VKH disease, and the primary differentiator is that SO follows penetrating trauma or surgery, while VKH involves no prior history of trauma.

The inflammation primarily involves the choroid, beginning with presenting as posterior uveitis that may evolve to panuveitis. Rarely, the inflammation may initially involve the iris and ciliary body, followed by panuveitis. The diagnosis of SO is usually based on the history and clinical signs of intraocular inflammation.[7273]

As SO predominantly affects choroidal stroma, ICGA is particularly well suited for assessing choroidal involvement and identifying infiltrative changes. Like the VKH disease, typical ICGA findings in SO include multiple HDDs, which contrast with the hyperfluorescent lesions seen on corresponding FA [Figure 27]. These hypofluorescent areas are thought to represent choroidal granulomas, and ICGA findings have been described in a few case reports.[74757677] Similar to VKH disease, SO needs early and aggressive systemic treatment to prevent complications.

Birdshot retinochoroiditis

Birdshot retinochoroiditis (BRC) is a rare, bilateral, and chronic inflammatory disease that typically affects middle-aged adults of Caucasian descent. It exhibits the strongest known human leukocyte antigen (HLA) disease association in medicine, with close to 100% of patients testing positive for the HLA-A29 allele.[45] Patients typically present with dim vision without significant decline of visual acuity, severe floaters, nyctalopia, dyschromatopsia, and profound glare. Clinically, the disease is characterized by the presence of multiple, ill-defined, cream-colored or depigmented ovoid lesions (the “birdshot” spots or lesions) radiating from the optic disc toward the equatorial fundus.[78]

Pathophysiologically, BRC is uniquely characterized by a dual and independent inflammatory process of the choroid and the retina: a primary, T-cell-mediated stromal choroiditis, accompanied by a primary concomitant retinal vasculitis.[4579] Because the primary pathological focus lies deep within the choroidal stroma, ICGA findings are valuable to both the initial diagnosis and the long-term monitoring of the disease.

The hallmark ICGA feature in BCR is the presence of numerous HDDs. Crucially, these HDDs are typically much more extensive and numerous than the cream-colored lesions visible on standard fundus examination [Figure 28].[38] These HDDs are evenly distributed from the posterior pole to the extreme periphery[80] and represent active choroidal granulomas or inflammatory infiltrates in the midstroma that do not compress or displace the overlying choriocapillaris. The HDDs are often not full-thickness lesions and therefore can disappear in the late phases of the ICGA examination. They frequently precede the appearance of clinically detectable fundus lesions, supporting the concept that ICGA provides unmatched sensitivity in detecting early disease.

In addition to HDDs, ICGA in the active phase of BCR demonstrates fuzzy or indistinct choroidal vessels in the intermediate phase and diffuse choroidal hyperfluorescence in the late phase. These angiographic signs reflect widespread stromal inflammation, vasodilation, and choroidal vascular hyperpermeability.[3881]

A critical aspect of ICGA in BRC is its unparalleled ability to detect subclinical disease. Relying solely on retinal findings to assess disease control is a major pitfall. Some studies have shown that the choroidal compartment can remain inflamed even when visual acuity is 20/20, OCT shows no macular edema, and FA shows concomitant characteristic retinal vasculitis.[798182] This clinicoangiographic dissociation underscores why ICGA is mandatory; without it, physicians risk undertreating active choroiditis, which inevitably leads to irreversible choroidal atrophy and outer retinal damage over time.

Furthermore, ICGA helps differentiate between active inflammatory lesions and permanent atrophic scars. While fundus lesions are midstromal scars and do not appear on ICGA, active HDDs can become less hypofluorescent in the late angiographic phases as they often constitute partial thickness lesions. Conversely, atrophic spots in advanced disease remain sharply demarcated and persistently dark throughout all transit phases.[81]

Consequently, ICGA-guided management has become the gold standard to optimize long-term outcomes in BRC. The presence of persistent HDDs, fuzzy vessels, or the appearance of new lesions on ICGA clearly indicates insufficient disease control and justifies the initiation or intensification of systemic immunosuppressive therapy. Conversely, the complete resolution of these active ICGA abnormalities provides the only reliable objective evidence to support a cautious tapering of medications.[3879]

From a differential diagnostic perspective, ICGA helps distinguish BRC from other posterior uveitis with overlapping clinical features, such as sarcoidosis, VKH disease, or MFC. The combination of evenly distributed, monomorphic HDDs, striking bilateral symmetry, the absence of early choriocapillaris lobular ischemia, and the characteristic HLA-A29 positive clinical context strongly secures the diagnosis of BRC.

Secondary stromal choroiditis

Sarcoidosis chorioretinitis

Sarcoidosis is a multisystem, noncaseating granulomatous disease of unknown etiology that predominantly affects the lungs and lymphatic system. Ocular involvement occurs in up to 30% to 50% of patients and can manifest as anterior, intermediate, posterior, or panuveitis. When the posterior segment is involved, classic clinical hallmarks include retinal periphlebitis (often described as “candle-wax drippings”), vitritis with “snowballs,” and choroidal granulomas. Because choroidal granulomas are deeply seated within the stroma and may lack overlying RPE alterations, they are frequently overlooked on standard funduscopic examination.[6883] It is considered a secondary stromal choroiditis as the lesion process does not primarily originate from the choroid as is the case for VKH, the choroid only being an innocent bystander of a systemic disease.

ICGA is the imaging modality of choice for assessing this deep stromal involvement. It typically reveals multiple, round, HDDs corresponding to the granulomatous infiltrates within the choroid, and sometimes larger choroidal granuloma [Figure 29].[6883]

Multimodal imaging of ocular sarcoidosis. (a) Indocyanine green angiography showing multiple small hypofluorescent spots concentrated in the posterior pole, alongside a large hypofluorescent lesion temporal to the optic disc. (b) Enhanced depth imaging spectral-domain optical coherence tomography revealing a large peripapillary choroidal granuloma causing significant elevation of the overlying retinal layers

A key feature of sarcoid-related ICGA abnormalities is that these lesions are almost always more numerous than the clinically visible choroidal nodules, and they may be present even in eyes with minimal or no funduscopic findings.

Unlike the monomorphic dots seen in BCR, sarcoid granulomas on ICGA often vary in size.[68] They typically remain hypofluorescent through the early, intermediate, and late phases of the angiogram, reflecting sustained stromal space occupation by the granulomas rather than transient choriocapillaris perfusion deficits.[83] In active disease, ICGA may also demonstrate fuzzy or poorly delineated large choroidal vessels, indicative of diffuse inflammatory infiltration surrounding the vasculature. An additional ICGA finding present also in other granulomatous diseases is the presence of peripheral hyperfluorescent pinpoints that respond to therapy.

Importantly, ICGA can detect subclinical choroidal involvement in patients presenting with otherwise limited ocular findings. This is particularly relevant in cases presenting as predominantly anterior or intermediate uveitis, where ICGA may reveal occult stromal granulomas. The detection of these lesions has an important diagnostic value; in fact, the presence of multiple choroidal lesions on ICGA (and/or OCT) is formally recognized as a major intraocular sign in the revised International Workshop on Ocular Sarcoidosis diagnostic criteria.[84] Identifying these subclinical lesions strongly supports the diagnosis of a systemic granulomatous process and justifies a targeted systemic workup (e.g., chest imaging, serum Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme, and lysozyme) [Figure 30].

Anterior and posterior segment findings in ocular sarcoidosis. (a) Slit-lamp photograph of the anterior segment demonstrating mutton-fat keratic precipitates. (b) Indocyanine green angiography of the same eye revealing multiple hypofluorescent lesions with irregular distribution, indicative of choroidal granulomas, mainly concentrated in the posterior pole

From a differential diagnostic standpoint, ICGA findings in sarcoidosis must be distinguished from those seen in infectious granulomatous diseases, particularly TB. While TB may also produce hypofluorescent lesions, tubercular choroiditis often presents with larger, more irregular, and multiloculated lesions that may exhibit late-edge hyperfluorescence or associated exudative features.[85] Conversely, sarcoidosis more commonly demonstrates numerous, strictly hypofluorescent dots of variable sizes.

Finally, ICGA plays a critical role in monitoring disease activity. The persistence, enlargement, or reappearance of hypofluorescences on ICGA indicates ongoing active inflammation, even in the absence of clinical signs, visual acuity drop, or FA leakage. Conversely, as the disease responds to corticosteroids or immunosuppressive therapy, active HDDs shrink, disappear, or are replaced by sharply demarcated atrophic spots.[68] This makes ICGA a useful tool for guiding treatment escalation and determining the appropriate timing for therapeutic tapering.

Tuberculosis chorioretinitis

TB is a chronic, slowly progressive granulomatous infection caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis.[86] Although it primarily involves the lungs, TB can also affect multiple extrapulmonary sites, including the cardiovascular, genitourinary, gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, central nervous, skin, and ocular systems.[8788]

Presumed intraocular tuberculosis (PIOTB) may arise either from direct infection of ocular tissues or from an immune-mediated hypersensitivity reaction to mycobacterial antigens, particularly when there is no evidence of active systemic TB or the systemic lesion is considered inactive.[8990] Clinically, PIOTB can manifest in various forms, including acute anterior uveitis, chronic granulomatous anterior uveitis, intermediate uveitis, vitritis, retinal vasculitis, neuroretinitis, solitary or multiple choroidal tubercles, MFC, choroidal granulomas, serpiginous-like choroiditis (SLC), subretinal abscess, endophthalmitis, and panophthalmitis. Among these, posterior uveitis and panuveitis are reported as the most common presentations.[9192]

ICGA enables detailed visualization of the choroidal vasculature, including the choriocapillaris, stromal vessels, and choroidal stroma[8939495] because the dye fluoresces in the near-infrared spectrum.[96] Although the ICG molecule is relatively small (751 Daltons), it is almost completely protein-bound (98%–99%), giving it macromolecular behavior equivalent to that of 66,500 Daltons or larger. As a result, it does not leak from normal or moderately inflamed retinal vessels but can slowly pass through the large fenestrations of the choriocapillaris.[97] Several studies have documented ICGA findings in PIOTB.[69979899100101102]

Four major ICGA findings were described: HDDs, both transient and persistent; hypofluorescent geographic lesions (HGLs); fuzzy vascular patterns of large stromal vessels; and disc hyperfluorescence. Other minor ICGA signs previously reported included disturbance/delay in early choriocapillaris circulation, hyperfluorescent pinpoints, and diffuse late hyperfluorescence.[69979899100101102]

ICG dye has a strong affinity for plasma proteins and fluoresces in the near-infrared spectrum, enabling visualization beyond the RPE. This makes ICGA particularly valuable for detecting subclinical choroidal involvement that may not be apparent on fundus examination or FA. Such capability is especially important during follow-up, as it helps identify subclinical disease activity requiring timely and appropriate management.[8]

In a large study by Abouammoh et al.,[102] ICGA detected subclinical choroidal involvement in 84.1% of eyes that showed no clinical or FA evidence of choroidal disease. These findings included HDDs in all 37 (100%) eyes [Figure 31]. Among these eyes, 62% had persistent HDDs through late frames, and 38% had transient HDDs that became isofluorescent on late angiographic frames. HGLs were detected in 19% eyes in addition to HDDs. Disc hyperfluorescence was noted in 45% eyes. Early hyperfluorescent choroidal stromal vessels were noted in 30% of eyes, and a fuzzy or lost pattern of choroidal stromal vessels was seen in 38% of eyes. Late diffuse choroidal hyperfluorescence was seen in 16% of eyes, and late pinpoint hyperfluorescence was detected in 25% of eyes.

Similarly, Wolfensberger et al. reported a series of 15 eyes from 8 patients in which ICGA revealed choroidal lesions that were not detectable clinically or on FA, concluding that ICGA is a valuable tool for assessing and quantifying the otherwise unrecognized extent of choroidal involvement in PIOTB.[69]

This hypofluorescent geographic pattern was first described by De Luigi et al.[98] in a case report of multifocal SLC, in which the hypofluorescence was attributed to choriocapillaritis rather than to stromal choroiditis. Syphilitic chorioretinitis

Syphilitic uveitis represents one of the most challenging entities in the differential diagnosis of posterior uveitis due to its highly variable clinical and imaging presentation. Often referred to as “the great masquerader,” syphilis can mimic both inflammatory choriocapillaropathies and primary stromal choroiditis, making multimodal imaging and systemic evaluation essential for accurate diagnosis.[104105]

ICGA plays a supportive role in the evaluation of syphilitic chorioretinitis. Unlike noninfectious inflammatory choroiditis, where ICGA often demonstrates relatively characteristic and reproducible patterns, findings in syphilis are heterogeneous and must be interpreted within the broader clinical and serological context.[104105]

The most frequently reported ICGA finding in syphilitic posterior uveitis is the presence of hypofluorescent areas, which correspond to either choriocapillaris hypoperfusion or inflammatory infiltration of the choroidal stroma [Figure 38].[105106] These lesions are often irregular in shape and distribution, lacking the strictly well-defined lobular or geographic patterns typically observed in diseases such as APMPPE or SC. Additional ICGA features in syphilitic uveitis may include late-phase scattered hyperfluorescent spots.[107]

Multimodal imaging of ocular syphilis. (a) Fluorescein angiography of the left eye showing optic disc hyperfluorescence. (b) Indocyanine green angiography revealing multiple, diffuse hypofluorescent dots distributed throughout the fundus, extending from the posterior pole to the far periphery

In cases of ASPPC, ICGA typically reveals patchy early hypofluorescent areas corresponding to the yellowish placoid lesions seen clinically at the level of the RPE and outer retina[108] and these hypofluorescent areas may persist into the late phases.[109] A highly characteristic feature of ASPPC is the rapid dynamic evolution of these angiographic signs; following appropriate systemic intravenous penicillin therapy, the ICGA abnormalities often resolve quickly.[108109]

The most important clinical implication of ICGA in syphilitic uveitis is not diagnostic specificity, which ultimately relies entirely on treponemal and nontreponemal serological assays, but rather its contribution to disease mapping and monitoring.[104] ICGA may reveal a broader extent of choroidal involvement than suggested by clinical examination alone. However, unlike in noninfectious uveitis where ICGA actively guides immunosuppressive tapering, its role in syphilis is strictly secondary to the primary antimicrobial management.

In conclusion, while ICGA findings in syphilitic uveitis are variable and lack pathognomonic features, its inclusion in a multimodal imaging approach provides essential contextual information, helping to rule out primary retinal diseases and guiding the differential diagnosis toward an infectious etiology.

Toxoplasmic retinochoroiditis

Toxoplasma retinochoroiditis is among the most common causes of infectious posterior uveitis, particularly in younger individuals. Diagnosis is primarily based on characteristic posterior segment findings and can be supported by serologic testing. FA typically adds limited value in classic cases. Still, it is useful for identifying associated features, including retinal vasculitis, vascular occlusion, arteriovenous shunts, retinochoroidal shunts, macular edema, CNV, and optic nerve or neuroretinal involvement.

In contrast, ICGA has proven valuable in evaluating the extent of choroidal involvement and monitoring lesion progression.

Herbort and colleagues have described characteristic ICGA findings in ocular toxoplasmosis.[110-112] These findings include persistent hypofluorescence of the active retinochoroiditis lesion in about 90% of cases throughout the ICGA frames. However, in a few cases, the active focus becomes hyperfluorescent due to superficial involvement and dye leakage, with pronounced damage to the retinal vessels.[111] All active cases will show diffuse, faint hyperfluorescence at the late stage of the angiogram, which resolves after therapy. ICGA can provide additional information, as it can image hypofluorescent satellite dark dots (SDDs) in about 75% of cases surrounding the active lesion that are not visible with either FA or during clinical examination [Figure 39]. The resolution of SDDs after treatment may suggest an infectious component;[110111] however, SDDs have also been reported to resolve spontaneously and could indicate associated choriocapillaritis.[113]

INDOCYANINE GREEN ANGIOGRAPHY VERSUS OPTICAL COHERENCE TOMOGRAPHY AND OPTICAL COHERENCE TOMOGRAPHY ANGIOGRAPHY

The introduction of EDI-OCT, swept-source OCT, and OCTA has significantly expanded the ability to investigate choroidal structure and perfusion. However, despite these advances, ICGA continues to provide unique and clinically decisive information that is not fully replicated by these newer modalities.[3848]

Rather than representing competing technologies, ICGA, OCT, and OCTA should be considered complementary tools, each addressing different aspects of chorioretinal pathology. Their relative value depends on the specific clinical question being addressed.

OCT provides high-resolution cross-sectional imaging of the retina and, with enhanced depth techniques, allows partial visualization of the choroid. It is particularly effective in identifying outer retinal disruption, RPE alterations, subretinal fluid, and choroidal thickness changes. However, OCT does not directly assess choroidal perfusion and therefore cannot reliably detect early or diffuse inflammatory involvement of the choriocapillaris or stromal compartments.[38]

OCTA enables noninvasive visualization of blood flow at the level of the retinal and choroidal microvasculature. It is especially useful for detecting flow deficits in choriocapillaris and for identifying secondary complications such as CNV. In choriocapillaritis entities, OCTA typically demonstrates areas of choriocapillaris flow void that spatially correspond to hypofluorescent regions on ICGA, supporting its role in the detection and follow-up of disease activity, although the full extent of involvement is often underestimated.[48114] OCTA, being based on the detection of flow, is unable to image end-capillaries with very low or absent flow.

Nevertheless, OCTA is inherently limited by projection artifacts, segmentation errors, and a relatively restricted field of view, which may lead to underestimation of disease extent.[48115] In addition, OCTA signal alterations may reflect flow impairment rather than true nonperfusion, particularly in inflammatory conditions characterized by slow or heterogeneous blood flow.[48]

In contrast, ICGA provides dynamic, wide-field imaging of the choroidal circulation, with high sensitivity for detecting both choriocapillaris nonperfusion and stromal inflammatory infiltration. Its ability to capture early, intermediate, and late phases allows a more comprehensive assessment of choroidal pathology over time.[38]

The relative performance of ICGA, OCT, and OCTA varies depending on the underlying disease. In choriocapillaritis entities, ICGA remains the most sensitive modality for detecting the full extent of choriocapillaris involvement. Hypofluorescent areas on ICGA often exceed those detected by OCTA, particularly in early or active disease, where slow or partial flow may not be adequately captured by OCTA algorithms.[48]

This discrepancy further supports the role of OCTA as a complementary technique rather than a substitute for ICGA in inflammatory choriocapillaropathies.[48114]

In contrast, in conditions primarily affecting the outer retina, OCT and OCTA provide the most relevant structural information.

In stromal choroiditis like BRC and sarcoidosis, ICGA is unequivocally superior. Neither OCT nor OCTA can reliably detect the characteristic hypofluorescences corresponding to stromal granulomatous infiltration. As a result, ICGA remains the reference standard for assessing disease activity in these conditions.[8394] Although recent reports suggest that OCTA may show indirect signs of choroidal involvement, such as focal flow deficits or signal attenuation overlying inflammatory lesions, these findings remain nonspecific and do not allow reliable detection or mapping of stromal granulomas.[114]

Nevertheless OCTA has generated significant interest as a noninvasive alternative to dye-based angiography, its limitations are particularly evident. First, OCTA detects motion contrast and therefore depends on the presence of detectable flow. Areas of slow or turbulent flow may be misinterpreted as nonperfusion. Second, segmentation errors are common in the presence of retinal or RPE disruption, leading to inaccurate representation of deeper vascular layers. Third, the limited field of view restricts the ability to assess peripheral or widespread disease, which is frequently encountered in uveitis.[48115] These limitations are clinically relevant, as they may lead to underestimation of disease activity and inappropriate therapeutic decisions if OCTA is used in isolation.

From a practical perspective, the selection of the most appropriate imaging modality should be tailored to the specific clinical scenario and diagnostic question. When a choroidal inflammatory disorder is suspected, ICGA remains the key imaging technique, as it provides the most reliable assessment of choroidal involvement and lesion distribution. By contrast, when the primary aim is to evaluate the structural integrity of the outer retina and photoreceptor layers, OCT represents the first-line modality because of its superior axial resolution and ability to delineate subtle microstructural changes. OCTA serves a complementary role, particularly in cases where localized flow deficits need to be characterized more precisely or when neovascular complications are suspected, allowing a noninvasive assessment of vascular perfusion abnormalities.

CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

ICGA plays an indispensable role in the clinical management of posterior uveitis by providing crucial functional and morphological information that directly influences therapeutic decisions. Because the ICG molecule is highly bound to plasma proteins (98%) and fluoresces in the near-infrared spectrum (around 800 nm), it resists leakage from normal fenestrated choriocapillaris. Unlike other imaging modalities, this allows ICGA to act as a highly specific window into the choroid, enabling the detection of subclinical choroidal inflammation, assessing the exact extent of disease activity, and guiding both treatment initiation and long-term management strategies.[183894]

One of the most relevant clinical applications of ICGA is its ability to guide the initiation and intensity of systemic corticosteroid and immunosuppressive therapy. In inflammatory choroidal diseases, ICGA frequently reveals a significantly greater involvement than suggested by routine clinical examination, FAF, or FA.

For example, in conditions such as MFC, BRC, or VKH disease, ICGA may demonstrate numerous hypofluorescent lesions in eyes with relatively preserved visual acuity and minimal funduscopic changes. In acute VKH, early recognition of diffuse choroidal hyperfluorescence and late fuzzy leakage on ICGA allows for immediate, aggressive, high-dose corticosteroid therapy combined with immunosuppressives, which is critical to preventing chronic evolution and irreversible outcomes like “sunset glow” fundus.[116] This discrepancy between clinical presentation and ICGA findings indicates active subclinical inflammation and supports the early initiation or rapid escalation of systemic therapy to prevent irreversible photoreceptor and outer retinal damage.[94]

Conversely, in cases where ICGA shows no evidence of choroidal involvement despite severe outer retinal abnormalities, the likelihood of a primary retinal or RPE disorder increases, allowing clinicians to safely avoid unnecessary systemic immunosuppression. In this context, ICGA contributes not only to treatment escalation but also to treatment restraint.

ICGA is also valuable for longitudinal monitoring of disease activity. In many chronic inflammatory conditions, ICGA abnormalities may persist despite apparent clinical quiescence and normal visual acuity. HDDs, for instance, may remain detectable in BRC or sarcoidosis even when symptoms have resolved and FA shows minimal to no retinal vascular leakage. These HDDs typically represent ongoing subclinical choroidal granulomas or dense inflammatory cell infiltrates, which cannot be captured by other imaging modalities.[8394117] Serial ICGA examinations allow clinicians to track dynamic changes in lesion number, size, and distribution. This is particularly crucial in insidious chronic diseases, where subtle progression can lead to severe visual field loss if left unchecked.

Another critical application of ICGA is driving the complex tapering of corticosteroids and steroid-sparing immunosuppressive agents. Premature reduction of therapy in the presence of persistent subclinical choroidal inflammation invariably leads to disease recurrence, cumulative tissue damage, and gradual visual deterioration. ICGA can definitively identify residual choroidal activity even in clinically and angiographically “quiet” eyes. The persistence of hypofluorescent lesions, late choroidal vessel staining, or the appearance of new hypofluorescent spots on ICGA should prompt strict caution in tapering therapy; such findings strongly justify maintaining or even intensifying the current treatment regimen.[118] Conversely, the complete resolution of ICGA abnormalities provides a highly reliable, evidence-based green light for the de-escalation of immunomodulatory therapy [Figures 40 and 41].

ICGA is uniquely sensitive in detecting early disease recurrence. In a vast majority of cases, new hypofluorescent lesions or recurrent choroidal vasculitis appear on ICGA weeks or even months before clinical symptoms, visual acuity changes, or FA leakage become evident to the patient or physician. This early detection capability is a game-changer in diseases characterized by a relapsing-remitting course, such as SC, VKH, or MFC. Identification of a subclinical angiographic relapse allows for prompt therapeutic intervention, effectively halting the inflammatory cascade before irreversible structural damage (such as chorioretinal scarring or secondary CNV) can occur.[119]

Today, the clinical value of ICGA is maximized when integrated into a multimodal imaging approach. While EDI-OCT provides excellent structural metrics (like choroidal thickness) and OCTA provides noninvasive insights into superficial choroid flow, ICGA remains the only modality that gives a complete view of choroidal inflammation.[120] From a practical standpoint, OCTA can show an area of nonperfusion, but ICGA tells the clinician whether that area is an old, inactive scar (dark in all phases) or an active inflammatory lesion (fuzzy borders, changing fluorescence). Therefore, ICGA contributes directly to pivotal management decisions by:

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Despite its well-established role in the evaluation of inflammatory choroidal diseases, ICGA presents several limitations that have contributed to the growing interest in alternative imaging modalities. However, these limitations must be interpreted within the context of its unique diagnostic capabilities, which remain unmatched for the assessment of stromal choroidal inflammation.

ICGA is an invasive procedure requiring intravenous dye injection, which introduces potential risks, including allergic reactions and, rarely, more severe systemic complications. Although generally safe, these risks limit its routine or repeated use in certain patient populations.[121]

In addition, ICGA is time-consuming and requires specialized equipment and trained personnel, reducing its accessibility compared to noninvasive imaging modalities such as OCT and OCTA. This limitation is particularly relevant in high-volume clinical settings and in centers without dedicated uveitis expertise.

ICGA interpretation remains partly subjective and dependent on the clinician’s experience. While certain patterns, such as HDDs in stromal choroiditis or geographic hypofluorescence in choriocapillaritis, are well described, there is no universally accepted quantitative scoring system.

This lack of standardization may lead to interobserver variability and limits the use of ICGA as an objective endpoint in clinical trials. Efforts to develop reproducible grading systems have been proposed but are not yet widely adopted.[12]

Although OCTA enables semiquantitative assessment of blood flow, ICGA may also provide semiquantitative vascular information when interpreted using a standardized angiographic scoring protocol. The evaluation of lesion number, size, and intensity is based on visual interpretation rather than automated measurement. This limitation reduces its utility in longitudinal studies where objective quantification of disease progression or treatment response would be advantageous.

Although wide-field ICGA systems are available, conventional ICGA is still limited in its ability to comprehensively assess the peripheral retina and choroid. Given that inflammatory diseases may involve midperiphery or far periphery, incomplete visualization may result in underestimation of disease extent.

The development of ultra-widefield ICGA has the potential to overcome field-of-view limitations, allowing more comprehensive assessment of peripheral choroidal involvement. Early studies suggest that peripheral lesions may be more common than previously appreciated and may have implications for disease classification and management.[80]

Future approaches will likely emphasize the integration of ICGA with OCT, OCTA, and FAF into a unified diagnostic framework. Rather than replace ICGA, newer modalities are expected to complement it, providing structural and functional information across different layers of the eye. The challenge lies in defining standardized multimodal algorithms that can guide clinical decision-making in a reproducible manner.

Artificial intelligence-based image analysis represents a promising avenue for improving the reproducibility and quantitative assessment of ICGA findings. Automated detection and segmentation of hypofluorescent lesions could enable objective measurement of disease burden and response to therapy. However, such applications remain in early stages of development and require validation in large, well-characterized datasets before clinical implementation.

OCTA will continue to evolve and may address some of its current limitations, including restricted field of view and susceptibility to artifacts. Advances in swept-source technology and widefield OCTA may improve its ability to detect choriocapillaris alterations. Nevertheless, it is unlikely that OCTA will fully replace ICGA in the assessment of stromal choroidal inflammation, as it remains inherently limited to flow detection and does not capture inflammatory infiltration within the choroidal stroma.

While ICGA has recognized limitations related to invasiveness, accessibility, and lack of quantification, it remains the most informative modality for assessing choroidal inflammation in uveitis. Emerging technologies, including ultra-widefield imaging, multimodal integration, and artificial intelligence, are expected to enhance its utility rather than replace it.

ICGA should therefore be viewed not as an outdated technique, but as a central component of a modern, multimodal approach to inflammatory chorioretinal disease.

In conclusion, ICGA remains an irreplaceable tool in the clinical management of posterior uveitis, offering unparalleled sensitivity in detecting subclinical choroidal involvement and early recurrences that often elude other imaging modalities. Its ability to precisely map choroidal inflammatory activity not only guides the initial intensity of therapy but also provides the necessary objective criteria for safe pharmacological tapering, minimizing the risk of irreversible structural damage. Therefore, far from being outdated, ICGA represents an essential pillar of a modern multimodal approach, fundamental for optimizing long-term visual outcomes in patients with inflammatory chorioretinal diseases.
