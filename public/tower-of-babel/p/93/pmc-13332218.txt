Optimized flood scene segmentation with swin transformer-based architecture
Authors: S. Preetha, Siva Priya M S, P. Manikandan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
One of the most damaging natural disasters, floods pose a serious risk to human life, infrastructure, and economies. Effective disaster response depends on the timely and precise identification of areas affected by flooding. A deep learning-based semantic segmentation framework designed for flood scene analysis with satellite or drone imagery is presented in this paper which facilitates speedy flood response and post-event mapping for successful disaster management. The input images are preprocessed using bilateral filtering and Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) to improve visual clarity and class separability. The segmentation model has a lightweight custom decoder for accurate pixel-wise classification and an encoder based on Swin Transformer for capturing hierarchical features. Training and evaluation are conducted using the FloodNet dataset, which comprises ten flood-relevant semantic classes. To enhance generalization and further refine spatial boundaries, postprocessing techniques like Test-Time Augmentation (TTA) and Dense Conditional Random Fields (DenseCRF) are used. Compared to traditional CNN-methodologies, the proposed approach has been able to achieve a validation mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) as high as 84.1% at 224 × 224 input resolution with DenseCRF when dealing with visually complex flooding conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39188-x.

Introduction

Among natural disasters, floods are considered one of the most destructive and cause widespread socioeconomic loss of life around the world. Rapid urbanization, coupled with changing climatic patterns, has increased the frequency and magnitude of floods. For mitigating the damage and aiding recovery, effective flood monitoring and response systems are necessary. Semantic segmentation of flood scenes-classification of every pixel into water, flooded structures, road, or vegetation-is a crucial part of these systems.

Deep learning advancements, especially CNNs, have recently achieved remarkable success in image segmentation; however, these CNN-based methods often fail to model long-range dependencies and preserve fine-grained details for complex scenarios of floods. This work presents a hybrid approach that incorporates modern deep learning with traditional image preprocessing for overcoming these limitations.

The image quality is enhanced using CLAHE and Bilateral Filtering to enhance the contrast and smoothness, respectively, before a Swin Transformer-a state-of-the-art hierarchical vision transformer for learning local and global features-performs segmentation by leveraging a light-weight decoder tailored specifically for this task. Comprehensive experiments on the FloodNet dataset reveal substantial improvements over state-of-the-art CNN-based models in terms of mIoU and pixel accuracy.

Hydrologically, the derived maps can subsequently serve for flood-extent estimation, hydrodynamic model calibration, and post-event assessment, while offering valuable real-time tools for flood management and response planning.

Literature review

Deep learning-driven solutions have overtaken conventional image processing techniques in scene segmentation and flood detection, with a phenomenal turn in these applications. Sanyal and Lu discussed numerous remote sensing approaches to detecting floods with emphasis on spectral index-based classification methods like NDWI and thresholding1. These are effective for rural and large-scale mapping but often fail in complex urban areas.

A web based flood decision support system integrating remote sensing and GIS was even suggested by Di et al.2, though it was short on pixel-level granularity for local flood impact. Starting from models such as U-Net3, which brought forth an encoder- decoder architecture with skip connections, image segmentation was transformed by deep learning. Such models proved particularly useful for biomedical and environmental segmentation applications. On top of this, SegNet was developed by Badrinarayanan et al. and utilized pooling indices in the decoder to preserve spatial information more effectively, a useful capability for flood segmentation4. With the aid of spatial pyramid pooling and atrous convolutions to handle multi-scale context, DeepLabv3 + improved segmentation precision further5.

Vision Transformers (ViTs) emerged as a powerful alternative to convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for modeling global dependencies. By considering images as patch sequences, Dosovitskiy et al.‘s ViT architecture achieved state-of-the-art classification results6. Yet, ViTs consumed massive computing resources and enormous datasets. The Swin Transformer, a shifted window-based hierarchical vision transformer, was suggested by Liu et al. to solve these problems. It enables competitive performance on dense prediction tasks such as semantic segmentation and efficient multi-resolution feature extraction7. Meanwhile, flood scene understanding models have been compared on the basis of datasets like FloodNet8. FloodNet is a challenging real-world dataset consisting of annotated aerial images with ten semantic classes. Even though CNN-based models had given initial baselines, they did not handle scenes with a lot of visual clutter well. While their ability to generalize in poor lighting conditions was still limited, Zhou et al. attempted to overcome this using attention-augmented DeepLab models for floodwater mapping9.

Classic preprocessing techniques have proven effective in enhancing segmentation in visually impaired images. Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE), initially introduced by Pizer et al., enhances image contrast without amplifying noise, making it suitable for flood images captured under cloudy weather10. Moreover, Ma et al. demonstrated that incorporating these preprocessing steps can yield enhanced boundary preservation and minor improvements in segmentation accuracy for various deep learning models11. All of these works demonstrate a clear trend away from traditional thresholding and CNN-based methods to transformer-driven architectures, with hybrid models which merge modern learning methods with traditional preprocessing offering promising results for flood scene analysis. Recent advances in transformer models have greatly influenced semantic segmentation issues. Chen et al.12 introduced the Multiscale Vision Transformer (MViT), which builds hierarchical features for dense prediction tasks and is more accurate than baseline ViTs.

Gao et al.13 developed a flood map model with deep learning using Sentinel-1 SAR data, which had strong performance under various hydrological and environmental conditions. Their approach emphasizes the value of multi- modal remote sensing imagery under disaster scenarios. Zhang et al.14 proposed a self-supervised semantic segmentation approach for Earth Observation. Leveraging contrastive learning, their method reduces dependence on large-scale labeled training data and maintains competitive accuracy. The MediaEval benchmark15 provided a dataset and evaluation process tailored for flood scene understanding, encouraging diverse model experimentation and reproducibility. Dosovitskiy and Brox16 emphasized the necessity of reversing learned visual representations to enhance knowledge of deep model behavior, offering interpretability benefits to remote sensing pipelines.

Methodology

This study offers a comprehensive framework for semantic segmentation of flood scenes that combines a contemporary transformer-based architecture with traditional image preprocessing methods. The preparation of the dataset, dual- step image preprocessing, semantic mask transformation, Swin Transformer-based model architecture, training process, and evaluation are the main elements of the methodology. We used a Swin Transformer-based encoder, which benefits from spatial attention similar to that investigated in gated-attention mechanisms17, to efficiently capture hierarchical representations of flood scenes. Recent models such as the Masked-Attention Mask Transformer (Mask2Former)18 serve as inspiration for segmentation-specific enhancements to the transformer architecture. Dataset Preparation

The FloodNet dataset8, which includes high-resolution aerial RGB photos taken in actual post-flood situations, is used for the experiments. The aerial images used in this study was sourced from the public FloodNet-Supervised v1.0 data source produced by the BinaLab research team for academic research use. The dataset was captured using a small unmanned aerial system (sUAS), specifically DJI Mavic Pro quadcopters, in the aftermath of Hurricane Harvey. It contains a total of 2,343 images, which are partitioned into training (≈ 60%), validation (≈ 20%), and test (≈ 20%) subsets. Each pixel in the manually annotated segmentation masks in the dataset is assigned a class from the ten predefined categories.

Without any image filtering, the official FloodNet dataset split (Train: 1,445, Validation: 450, Test: 448) was rigorously adhered to. To guarantee reproducibility, the dataset was used exactly as supplied. Instead of oversampling, class imbalance was addressed implicitly through stratified batching and augmentation, taking into account all classes. Color to class conversion

Each color corresponds to a distinct class in the RGB format of the ground truth masks included in the dataset. We use a color-to-label dictionary to transform these RGB masks into single-channel integer-class masks so that we can train the model with CrossEntropyLoss. In order to prevent label corruption during resizing, nearest-neighbor interpolation is used to resize each RGB mask to 224 × 224. These colored masks are transformed into 2D class label maps using a mask transformation function.

Architecture of the model

A Swin Transformer encoder and a specially designed decoder that is optimized for semantic segmentation make up the suggested model architecture.

During training, simple data augmentation methods are used to improve generalization and reduce overfitting. There are several advantages of using Dense Conditional Random Fields (DenseCRF) for segmenting flood images. This is because DenseCRF ensures the consistencies of the labels for similar pixels in the neighborhood, thereby ensuring that the outputs are not noisy or incorrect. The accuracy of the boundaries, specifically for similar classes of flood images such as road and building floods, is also improved by DenseCRF.

In this research, DenseCRF was implemented in a series of key steps. Firstly, to calculate the first-class probabilities for every pixel, unary potentials were used, which were obtained directly from the softmax output layer. In addition, pairwise potentials based on Gaussian kernels for both spatial location and color similarity were used to calculate the image relationships. Mean-field inference was done for five iterations for every image to enhance spatial consistency for the image segmentation task.

Hyperparameters of DenseCRF: The model involves a pairwise Gaussian kernel to add a smoothness term with a spatial standard deviation of σxy = 3 and a compatibility weight of 3, along with a pairwise bilateral filter to encourage edge-aware segmentation with a spatial standard deviation of σxy = 80, a color standard deviation of σrgb = 13, and a compatibility weight of 10.

Test-time augmentation (TTA)

During inference, the model’s robustness is increased through the use of Test-Time Augmentation (TTA). This entails making predictions on both the original and augmented images after applying transformations, like horizontal flipping, to each test image. To create the final output, the predictions from the flipped images are unflipped and averaged with the initial predictions. There are two important advantages to Test-Time Augmentation (TTA). First of all, it improves performance metrics like Dice Score and mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) without changing the current model architecture. Second, by combining several augmented inference outputs, it increases the prediction’s resilience to changing flood conditions, allowing the model to more effectively generalize to unknown or complicated situations.

These post-processing steps made a large impact on the final segmentation accuracy, particularly in identifying fine structures and disambiguating between visually similar classes.

Error analysis

The most frequent instances of misclassifications are between visually similar classes, like flooded buildings and roads, where the minute variations in context and texture can cause misunderstandings. Similarly, because of their similar visual traits, it can be difficult to distinguish between open water and pools. Vehicles that are partially submerged or obscured also have a tendency to be difficult to detect. Future developments might incorporate auxiliary edge-detection branches to improve feature discrimination and boundary clarity, or attention-augmented decoders to overcome these limitations. The suggested Swin Transformer-based flood segmentation model with CLAHE and bilateral preprocessing is state-of-the-art on the FloodNet dataset in terms of pixel- wise accuracy and mIoU. The model shows robustness to noise, excellent generalization, and much improved performance on difficult flood scene classes compared with previous methods.

Discussion

The proposed framework showcases how transformer-based models can be adapted for flood segmentation tasks efficiently. This combination of preprocessing, Swin transformer-based encoding, and a lightweight decoder offers a good balance between accuracy and efficiency.

While the results are promising, performance on small or spectrally similar objects remains challenging. Future integration with multi-modal datasets, including but not limited to SAR or LiDAR, might improve robustness. The model’s inference speed supports its potential for near-real-time flood monitoring.

Computational efficiency and inference time analysis

Inference time was tested on a single image (batch size 1) at the input resolution of 224 × 224 pixels. It took about 2.3 s on average for the entire end-to-end system including TTA and Dense Conditional Random Field (DenseCRF) post-processing. Statistical significance of inter-model difference between the proposed approach and the other approaches was determined through paired hypothesis test on IoU scores per image on the validation dataset (N = 450 images). The normality of the differences of the pair of IoU was checked through the Shapiro-Wilk test, which indicates there is a deviation from normality. Therefore, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. The results were further confirmed using the paired t-test. Both tests revealed significant improvement by the proposed approach over the other approaches (p = 0.01). Effect size was calculated by the value of Cohen’s d for a large effect (1.02). Even though the approach provides pixel predictions, which are correlated in the spatial dimension, the analysis was performed at the image level to avoid the assumption of independence.

Significance testing and reliability

The performance of the suggested model was statistically compared with baseline CNN models like DeepLabv3 + and U-Net in order to verify the noted improvements. The statistical significance of the Intersection over Union (IoU) scores was evaluated using a paired t-test, and the consistency of the dice scores was assessed using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. To gauge the improvements’ practical significance, Cohen’s d was also computed.

The improvement is statistically significant, as indicated by the results, which showed a p-value for the IoU comparison of less than 0.01. A Cohen’s d value of 1.05 was also noted, indicating a sizable effect size and validating the suggested method’s significant performance improvement over the baseline models.

A p-value < 0.01 indicates that the improvement is statistically significant and unlikely to have occurred by random chance. These findings validate that the improvement is statistically significant and not based on random fluctuation. Similar to Audebert et al.28, our hierarchical design reflects multi-scale learning strategies in remote sensing. The model performs consistently in both major and minor classes. Stable class-wise metrics are produced by improving feature discrimination through preprocessing with CLAHE and bilateral filtering. The robustness of the model is validated by statistical tests, which verify notable gains over baseline models. RefineNet-style refinements29 may be used in future post-processing to produce outputs that are sharper.

The segmentation of overlapping flood features is improved by combining spatially adaptive features with hierarchical attention, confirming the performance improvement over models such as Mask2Former18 and SAM23. Additionally, our tests indicate that Swin transformer ‘s windowed self-attention provides superior scalability across image sizes, whereas gated-attention17 selectively amplifies important features.

Conclusion

In this paper, we integrated a transformer-based deep learning model with conventional image enhancement techniques to offer a robust semantic segmentation paradigm for post-flood scene understanding. Swin Transformer-based encoder and lightweight custom decoder are utilized for accurate pixel-wise label prediction after the proposed method enhances the visual quality of the flood images before segmentation by integrating Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) and Bilateral Filtering. The FloodNet dataset, which captures real flood conditions in ten semantic categories, was employed to comprehensively evaluate the framework. From quantitative evaluation, our model performs better than baseline CNN architectures and even vanilla Swin Transformer, reporting a mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) of 84.1% at 224 × 224 input resolution. Even on low-contrast and cluttered scenes, the model is able to accurately predict detailed flood-related features such as water, flooded roads, and submerged buildings, based on qualitative analysis.

In addition, ablation studies proved that bilateral filtering and CLAHE both contribute markedly towards improvement in performance, validating the importance of effective preprocessing for flood segmentation tasks. Although these encouraging results, the performance of the model might be further enhanced by other directions. For future studies, the approach may be applied to satellite or UAV imagery for more generalization across broader areas, combining temporal modeling to capture changing patterns of floods, and inference optimization towards real-time application in emergency response systems. The use of domain adaptation strategies may also be explored to enhance robustness across geographies with different environmental textures30,31.

Training for 24 epochs on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 required approximately 2.5 h, with each epoch averaging around 6–7 min and using 7–8 GB of GPU memory, demonstrating that the model can support near-real-time flood assessment. This balance of training cost and fast prediction makes the method suitable even for time-critical disaster response scenarios.

The findings demonstrate the model’s suitability for operational flood mapping, which could aid in quick emergency response and post-event evaluation. Since FloodNet dataset, being limited to RGB imagery, it may lead to misclassification under poor visibility and also in order to increase generalization and enhance detection in challenging visual conditions, future research will concentrate on incorporating multi-spectral or hydrological inputs.
