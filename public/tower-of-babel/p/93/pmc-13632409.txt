Hospital system resilience in the face of armed conflicts; a cross sectional study from two regions in Lebanon
Authors: Josiane Abi Khattar, Theresa El Khoury, Roger Maroun, Dunya Nohra, Maha Nehme, Danie Khawaja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSince 2020, Lebanon has faced a succession of financial, health, and security crises that have severely weakened its hospital system. In this context, marked by armed conflicts and recurrent disasters, the resilience of healthcare facilities has emerged as a major public health and human security concern. This study aims to assess the resilience of Lebanese hospitals in the context of armed conflict by identifying implemented strategies, encountered challenges, and potential avenues for improvement.MethodsA quantitative, observational, and analytical design was adopted. A structured questionnaire was administered to 412 healthcare professionals working in hospitals located in Beirut and South Lebanon. Data were analyzed using SPSS software, employing descriptive statistics, the Chi-square test of association, and Pearson’s correlation coefficient to examine relationships among the studied variables. A multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of hospital resilience while controlling for the effects of the studied variables.ResultsSignificant positive correlations were observed between hospital resilience and crisis management protocols (r = 0.377, p < 0.001), cooperation with NGOs and international organizations (r = 0.567, p < 0.001), integration of artificial intelligence (r = 0.340, p < 0.001), and logistical and security challenges (r = 0.389, p < 0.001). While artificial intelligence shows potential, its independent contribution remains limited. Background

Armed conflicts exert sustained and indiscriminate pressure on civilian populations and essential infrastructure, with hospital systems among the most severely affected. Operating in hostile and unstable environments, hospitals are increasingly exposed to direct attacks and indirect disruptions that extend beyond physical destruction to include progressive erosion of structural integrity, functional capacity, and human resources. These dynamics substantially reduce access to healthcare services and exacerbate humanitarian crises, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where prolonged conflict is frequently accompanied by displacement, resource scarcity, and disruption of essential services [1–3]. Despite protections under international humanitarian law, attacks on healthcare facilities and personnel have intensified, resulting in damage ranging from partial functional impairment to complete destruction [4].

The hospital system refers to the network of hospitals—encompassing both public and private institutions—that provide acute, emergency, and specialized inpatient care within a defined geographic area. The Lebanon’s healthcare sector faces major structural, cultural, and socio-political challenges that weaken hospital performance and care delivery. Recurrent crises, resource constraints, and the pressure created by migrant and refugee populations have further strained the system [5, 6]. In Lebanon, this system is highly fragmented: public hospitals, primarily funded through the Ministry of Public Health, account for a relatively small share of total bed capacity, while private hospitals, which constitute the majority of infrastructure, rely on diversified funding sources including out-of-pocket payments, private insurance, and third-party payers such as the National Social Security Fund and military funds. This dual structure — operating largely without a robust primary healthcare tier to serve as a gatekeeper — results in significant inequities in access, service quality, and regulatory oversight [7, 8]. In addition, Lebanon’s strong reliance on hospitals, rather than primary healthcare, remains a key characteristic of its health system [9, 10]. The term health system, used more broadly in this paper, refers to all organizations, institutions, and resources involved in health action, in line with the World Health Organization definition.

The COVID-19 pandemic resulted in approximately 11,000 deaths and placed extraordinary strain on hospitals, while the Beirut port explosion in August 2020 caused extensive damage to several facilities already overwhelmed by the health emergency [11, 12]. Concurrently, an ongoing financial collapse—described as one of the most severe globally—has sharply reduced hospital funding, disrupted procurement, and generated persistent shortages of medicines and medical supplies. Between 2019 and 2023, nearly 30% of physicians and 40% of nurses emigrated, transforming a previously high-performing health system into one characterized by workforce depletion and structural fragility [13, 14].

These systemic vulnerabilities have been further exacerbated by recurrent insecurity and armed conflict. Hospitals in South Lebanon and Beirut have repeatedly been affected by violence, from the 2006 war to more recent hostilities. During 2023–2024, airstrikes damaged at least 15 health facilities and rendered six nonfunctional, further constraining service delivery and emergency preparedness [11, 15]. This pattern of repeated shocks underscores the need to strengthen hospital systems not only for emergency response but also for sustained functionality under chronic instability [16].

The human consequences of conflict-related disruption are profound. Healthcare workers operating in insecure settings face persistent physical threats, psychological trauma, ethical dilemmas, and chronic resource constraints. These conditions are associated with elevated levels of burnout, moral distress, and post-traumatic stress disorder, undermining individual well-being and system performance [17, 18]. Targeted attacks on medical personnel further accelerate workforce attrition, as professionals are injured, killed, or forced to migrate, compounding shortages of qualified staff and weakening continuity of care [19, 20].

Conflict also disrupts hospital logistics, utilities, and information systems. Interruptions in electricity, fuel, water, transportation, and procurement processes lead to acute shortages of medicines, equipment, and functional infrastructure. These constraints delay treatment, reduce quality of care, and increase morbidity and mortality [21, 22]. As a result, hospitals are often forced to suspend elective procedures, oncology services, and maternal care, while simultaneously managing sharp surges in emergency and trauma cases that overwhelm already fragile systems [3, 23, 24]. The breakdown of health information systems further limits data collection and surveillance, obscures the scale of health needs, and weakens coordination among health actors [25, 26].

In response to these challenges, increasing attention has focused on hospital resilience as a core component of health system performance in crisis settings. Resilience is defined as the capacity of systems to resist, absorb, adapt, transform, and recover while preserving core structures and functions [27]. Within hospital systems, resilience enables institutions to maintain functionality during internal and external shocks, protect prior health gains, and deliver safe and effective care while adapting to evolving conditions [28–30]. Operationally, resilience encompasses absorptive strategies that support short-term continuity, adaptive strategies involving reorganization and role adjustment, and transformative strategies that reinforce system robustness over the long term [31].

Hospital resilience is supported by multiple interrelated domains. These include robust physical and operational infrastructure—encompassing structural and non-structural elements such as power, water, communications, medical gases, fire safety, and transport systems—with redundancy and mobile service delivery enhancing continuity when facilities are damaged or inaccessible [32–36]. Workforce and administrative resilience depend on preparedness planning, targeted recruitment and retention, crisis-ready training, clear incident command structures, and post-event learning mechanisms [32, 33, 37, 38]. Staff engagement, rooted in professional ethics and social commitment, plays a critical role in sustaining service delivery under extreme conditions, while psychosocial support mitigates distress and preserves workforce functioning [39–43].

Additional resilience mechanisms include human-resource optimization and surge capacity through rapid communication, internal reorganization, recall of retirees, engagement of students, and provision of adequate personal protective equipment [33, 42, 44]. Assured access to medicines and health technologies relies on diversified supply chains, transparent tracking, strategic stockpiles, forecasting, decentralized decision-making, and local innovation during protracted crises [27, 39, 40, 45, 46]. Intersectoral and public–private collaboration—particularly salient in Lebanon—highlights the importance of diversified financing, Ministry of Public Health leadership, and partnerships with NGOs and international agencies to sustain service delivery and policy coordination [27, 28, 46, 47]. Effective patient-flow management and triage further strengthen resilience through mass-casualty planning, space zoning, role clarity, and integrated triage systems aligned with prehospital services [27, 30, 44, 48, 49].

Emerging digital and artificial intelligence–enabled solutions offer additional opportunities to enhance hospital resilience, particularly in resource-constrained and conflict-affected settings. AI-enabled tools support operational efficiency, resource forecasting, automation, and coordination through electronic health records, natural language processing, machine learning, and explainable AI [50, 51]. Telemedicine and mobile health applications facilitate continuity of care for vulnerable populations, while context-specific innovations—such as portable electronic health records and AI-based early-warning systems—improve preparedness and coordination in conflict environments [35, 52–54]. AI-enabled triage, wearable technologies, hybrid clinician–AI workflows, unmanned aerial vehicle–assisted assessment, and cybersecurity applications further enhance responsiveness, decision-making, and information integrity during crises [35, 55, 56]. AI-supported clinical decision-making, remote training, and mental-health screening also contribute to strengthening workforce capacity under conditions of insecurity and resource constraint [24, 57–60].

Within this context of compounded crises, recurrent armed conflict, and constrained resources, empirical assessment of hospital resilience in Lebanon remains limited. This study addresses this gap by examining the extent to which hospital systems in South Lebanon and Beirut are able to ensure and strengthen resilience in the face of armed conflict, considering existing strategies, persistent challenges, and opportunities for improvement. Using a quantitative analytical design, data were collected from healthcare professionals through a structured questionnaire and analyzed using SPSS to generate evidence-based insights into crisis management, coordination mechanisms, and resilience-enhancing practices relevant to conflict-affected hospital systems.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional, observational, quantitative study was conducted to examine organizational and system-level factors associated with hospital resilience in contexts of armed conflict in Lebanon. The study was situated within hospitals located in South Lebanon and Beirut, two regions repeatedly exposed to security instability and disruptions in healthcare service delivery. Cross-sectional designs are widely used in health services research to evaluate health system performance, resource availability, and organizational capacity at a given point in time, particularly in fragile and crisis-affected settings [29].

Study population and sampling

The study population comprised healthcare professionals working in public and private hospitals, including clinical, administrative, and managerial staff involved in hospital operations and service provision during periods of armed conflict. According to the official directory of the Lebanese Ministry of Public Health, Beirut includes 19 hospitals, of which 17 are private hospitals and 2 are public hospitals, whereas the South Governorate also includes 19 hospitals, distributed between 16 private hospitals and 3 public hospitals [61]. Then 12 hospitals were selected from two strategic geographic areas in Lebanon, Beirut and the South Governorate. This choice is justified by the significance of these two regions within the Lebanese healthcare landscape.

A non-probability purposive sampling strategy was employed to ensure the inclusion of respondents with relevant institutional knowledge and direct exposure to conflict-related operational challenges, and without discrimination. However we recognize that this approach does not substitute for random sampling. A total of 419 questionnaires were distributed, of which 412 were completed and included in the final analysis, corresponding to a response rate of 98.3%.

Data collection and measurement

Data were collected in May 2025 using a structured, self-administered bilingual (French/English) questionnaire. The questionnaire was developed specifically for the purpose of this study based on a review of the health services and hospital systems resilience literature [27, 28, 32, 33, 38, 49].

The survey instrument consisted of 33 questions organized into six sections. Section 1 included 11 questions on general, professional, and institutional characteristics. Section 2 included four questions assessing the implementation of crisis management protocols and emergency preparedness plans. Section 3 included five questions on cooperation with international organizations and non-governmental organizations. Section 4 included five questions on the integration of artificial intelligence in hospital management. Section 5 included four questions on logistical and security-related challenges. Section 6 included four questions assessing perceived hospital resilience in conflict settings. The full questionnaire is provided as Appendix 1.

Most analytical items were closed-ended statements measured using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. In the descriptive analysis, only responses of “agree” and “strongly agree” were considered positive or affirmative responses, while “neutral,” “disagree,” and “strongly disagree” were not considered positive. For the domains of crisis management, cooperation with NGOs and international organizations, artificial intelligence integration, and hospital resilience, positive responses indicated the presence or favorable perception of the assessed capacity. For the logistical and security challenges domain, responses of “agree” or “strongly agree” indicated greater perceived exposure to operational and security constraints and were therefore interpreted as affirmative responses indicating the presence of challenges rather than favorable outcomes.

Perceived hospital resilience was operationalized as the hospital’s capacity to maintain essential health services, adapt to disruptions, and respond effectively to unforeseen challenges during armed conflict. It was assessed using three Likert-scale items related to service continuity, staff adaptation to instability, and the hospital’s ability to manage extreme situations. Each item was scored from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree, resulting in a total resilience score ranging from 3 to 15. Higher scores indicated greater perceived hospital resilience. For interpretation, scores of 3–8 were considered low resilience, 9–11 moderate resilience, and 12–15 high resilience. The internal consistency of the resilience scale demonstrated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.855.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize respondent characteristics and key study variables. Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine associations between hospital resilience and the main independent variables, including crisis management protocols, cooperation with NGOs and international organizations, integration of artificial intelligence, and logistical and security challenges. In addition, chi-square tests were applied to assess associations between categorical levels of hospital resilience and selected organizational and contextual factors. A multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of hospital resilience while controlling for the effects of other variables. Statistical significance was assessed using conventional thresholds (p < 0.05).

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Université Sainte-Famille (USF), Lebanon. All participants were informed about the purpose of the study and provided informed consent prior to participation. Participation was voluntary, and all data were collected anonymously. All procedures performed in this study involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration.

Results

Respondents and hospitals’ characteristics

The study included 412 healthcare professionals working in hospitals located in Beirut and South Lebanon. The sample was predominantly female (64.3%, n = 265). The majority of respondents were under 45 years of age, with the largest proportions in the 25–34 and 35–44 age groups.

Regarding professional roles, nurses represented 79.9% (n = 329) of participants, followed by physicians (10.9%, n = 45), administrative staff (6.3%, n = 26), and pharmacists (2.9%, n = 12). Most respondents reported less than 10 years of professional experience, while a smaller proportion had more than 10 years of experience (Table 1).

Hospitals’ characteristics

Descriptive results showed that 72.3% (n = 298) of respondents reported the existence of clear and updated crisis management protocols within their institutions, while 30.1% (n = 124) reported regular simulation exercises or practical training. Additionally, 65.2% (n = 269) indicated that existing protocols contributed to strengthening hospital response capacity. In Lebanon, crisis management protocols and training are supported by the Ministry of Public Health through national guidance and training initiatives.

Selected descriptive findings related to crisis management, cooperation with NGOs and international organizations, artificial intelligence, and logistical and security-related challenges are reported directly in the text and are not presented in separate tables.

Collaboration with NGOs and international organizations was reported by 81.8% (n = 337) of respondents, and 72.6% (n = 299) indicated that such cooperation contributed to maintaining healthcare services and infrastructure.

Regarding artificial intelligence, 84.7% (n = 349) of respondents reported that their institution uses or plans to use AI in emergency management. Perceptions of its effectiveness were mixed, with 44.4% (n = 183) reporting positive perceptions and 41.3% (n = 170) reporting negative perceptions.

Logistical and security-related challenges were reported by 79.4% (n = 327) of respondents as significantly disrupting continuity of care, while 79.1% (n = 326) indicated that security risks limited access to hospitals.

Hospital resilience

Perceived hospital resilience was assessed using three items related to the ability of the hospital to maintain basic healthcare services during conflict, the ability of medical teams to adapt to instability, and the perceived capacity of the institution to manage extreme situations.

Regarding service continuity, 71.6% (n = 295) of respondents agreed or strongly agreed that their institution was able to continue providing basic healthcare during conflict situations. In relation to staff adaptability, 86.6% of respondents reported that medical teams were able to adapt quickly to instability. In addition, 96.4% of respondents considered that their institution had a sufficient level of resilience to manage extreme situations.

The mean resilience score was 11.57 (SD = 1.97), indicating a moderate to high level of perceived hospital resilience. The theoretical resilience score ranged from 3 to 15, with higher scores indicating greater perceived resilience. Based on the predefined cut-off points, scores of 3–8 were considered low resilience, 9–11 moderate resilience, and 12–15 high resilience.

Respondents’ recommendations for strengthening hospital resilience were also analyzed. The most frequently reported priority was increasing funding (77.0%), followed by developing intersectoral cooperation (58.1%), integrating digital technologies (57.6%), improving continuous training (48.2%), upgrading infrastructure (38.3%), and enhancing physical site security (15.5%).

Bivariate analysis

Pearson correlation analysis showed statistically significant associations between hospital resilience and all studied variables (p < 0.001). Correlation coefficients were as follows: crisis management protocols (r = 0.377), cooperation with NGOs and international organizations (r = 0.567), integration of artificial intelligence (r = 0.340), and logistical and security challenges (r = 0.389) (Table 3).

Discussion

Building on the structural, human, and operational vulnerabilities outlined in the background, this study examined key determinants of hospital resilience in a conflict-affected setting using both bivariate and multivariate analyses. Overall, the findings confirm that hospital resilience is a multidimensional construct shaped by institutional preparedness, interorganizational collaboration, and contextual constraints, while the role of technological innovation remains more limited.

Overall, the findings suggest a moderate to high level of perceived hospital resilience among the surveyed healthcare professionals. Hospital resilience was assessed through three core components: the ability to maintain basic healthcare services during conflict, the capacity of medical teams to adapt quickly to instability, and the perceived ability of the institution to manage extreme situations. The results showed that most respondents reported favorable perceptions across these components, particularly regarding staff adaptability and the ability to manage extreme situations. This indicates that, despite repeated exposure to armed conflict and systemic constraints, hospitals in the studied regions have developed adaptive capacities that support continuity of care. Similar findings have been reported in other fragile and crisis-affected settings, where hospital resilience is often shaped by the ability to reorganize services, maintain essential care, mobilize staff, and adapt operational processes during crises.

The regression model explained 30.2% of the variance in the hospital resilience score, indicating that crisis management protocols, cooperation with NGOs and international organizations, AI integration, and logistical and security-related challenges together accounted for an important proportion of differences in perceived resilience. This suggests that hospital resilience is partly shaped by modifiable organizational and contextual factors. However, the remaining unexplained variance also indicates that other factors not included in the model, such as leadership, hospital governance, financial resources, workforce availability, infrastructure quality, and previous crisis experience, may also influence hospital resilience.

First, the findings consistently demonstrate that the implementation of crisis management protocols and emergency plans is a significant determinant of hospital resilience. Both correlation and regression analyses confirm this association, indicating that structured preparedness mechanisms contribute independently to resilience (B = 0.163, p < 0.001). This aligns with previous research emphasizing that well-defined emergency protocols improve triage efficiency, patient flow management, and continuity of care during crises [42]. From a theoretical perspective, these findings support frameworks proposed by the International Committee of the Red Cross, which identify institutional preparedness as a cornerstone of healthcare functionality in conflict settings. In the Lebanese context, where healthcare systems are repeatedly exposed to instability, formalized crisis planning appears to play a central role in strengthening adaptive capacity. Similar conclusions were drawn by Kalaji et al. (2024), who showed that the proper implementation of triage protocols improves emergency response efficiency and reinforces hospital resilience.

Second, cooperation with NGOs and international organizations emerged as the strongest predictor of hospital resilience in the multivariate model (β = 0.274, p < 0.001). This finding highlights the critical importance of external support mechanisms in fragile health systems. In this study, such cooperation may include several forms of support, including the provision of medical equipment and supplies, logistical assistance, staff training, technical expertise in emergency preparedness, and support for crisis management and coordination. These forms of assistance can help hospitals maintain essential services despite resource shortages and security constraints. In conflict-affected regions such as South Lebanon, collaboration with organizations such as the ICRC, Médecins Sans Frontières, and WHO may strengthen hospital resilience both directly and indirectly. Direct support can help hospitals sustain service delivery, while the presence of humanitarian actors and independent clinics can also contribute to broader health system resilience by reducing pressure on hospitals and improving access to care for affected populations. This result is consistent with Ahizoune et al. (2023), who argue that humanitarian cooperation strengthens both immediate response capacity and long-term resilience.

Third, while the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in hospital management showed a significant positive association with resilience in bivariate analysis (r = 0.340, p < 0.001), it was not a significant predictor in the multivariate model (p = 0.143). This discrepancy suggests that the apparent contribution of AI may be mediated by other factors, such as institutional capacity or external support. Although prior studies highlight the potential of AI-driven tools—including telemedicine, predictive analytics, mobile health applications, and automated triage systems—to enhance healthcare delivery in crisis settings [35, 54], as well as the use of drones for logistics support [49, 50], the present findings indicate that AI remains a secondary or emerging component of resilience in Lebanon. Structural barriers, including limited digital infrastructure, high implementation costs, and insufficient technical expertise, likely constrain its independent impact.

Finally, logistical and security challenges were significantly associated with hospital resilience in both bivariate and multivariate analyses (B = 0.214, p < 0.001). While the statistical relationship is positive, this finding requires careful interpretation. Rather than indicating that such challenges enhance resilience, it likely reflects adaptive mechanisms developed under conditions of sustained stress and resource scarcity. This interpretation is consistent with existing evidence showing that exposure to repeated disruptions can lead to organizational learning and the development of coping strategies. However, extensive literature also demonstrates that logistical constraints, supply chain disruptions, attacks on healthcare facilities, shortages of fuel and medical equipment, and restricted access to hospitals severely undermine healthcare delivery in conflict zones [4, 21, 22]. Moreover, prolonged exposure of healthcare workers to insecurity and psychological stress has been shown to exacerbate burnout and reduce system-wide performance [12, 18]. Therefore, the observed association should be understood as resilience emerging despite these challenges, not because of them.

Taken together, these findings underscore the complex and layered nature of hospital resilience in conflict settings. While institutional preparedness and interorganizational cooperation appear to be robust and actionable drivers of resilience, technological innovation currently plays a more limited role, and contextual stressors continue to pose significant threats. These results highlight the need for integrated resilience strategies that combine emergency planning, strengthened partnerships, targeted technological investments, and structural interventions aimed at mitigating logistical and security vulnerabilities.

Strengths and limitations

This study presents several strengths, notably its relatively large sample of 412 healthcare professionals, which enhances the statistical reliability of the findings, as well as its focus on 12 hospitals in Beirut and South Lebanon, two highly relevant and conflict-affected regions that provide important contextual insight into hospital resilience. Another strength is that hospital resilience was assessed through specific components rather than through a single general question. The questionnaire examined service continuity, staff adaptability, and the ability to manage extreme situations, which allowed a more detailed assessment of resilience and helped reduce the risk of response bias associated with broad or general questions. The study also benefits from the use of a structured questionnaire with good internal consistency and addresses a topic of strong practical and policy relevance in fragile health systems.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The concept of artificial intelligence (AI) was not strictly defined and may have been interpreted differently by respondents, which may have introduced measurement bias. The use of purposive non-probability sampling limits the representativeness of the sample and therefore the generalizability of the findings, while the inclusion of only 12 hospitals may not fully capture the diversity of all hospitals in the targeted regions. In addition, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inferences, and the reliance on self-reported data may introduce response or social desirability bias. Finally, the predominance of certain professional categories among respondents may have influenced the balance of perspectives reflected in the results. Furthermore, the positive association observed between logistical and security challenges and hospital resilience may reflect adaptive responses developed under adverse conditions rather than a direct beneficial effect, and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This study assessed the resilience of Lebanese hospitals in contexts of armed conflict within a national environment marked by security instability and systemic fragility. Using a cross-sectional quantitative approach, data collected from 412 healthcare professionals in Beirut and South Lebanon examined the relationships between organizational preparedness, external cooperation, technological integration, logistical challenges, and perceived hospital resilience.

These findings have implications not only at the hospital level but also for national and humanitarian health system governance in conflict-affected settings.

The findings indicate that crisis management protocols and emergency preparedness plans are significantly associated with higher hospital resilience, while cooperation with NGOs and international organizations emerges as the strongest contributing factor.

Cooperation with NGOs/international organizations showed the strongest correlation (r = 0.567), which is considered a large effect size according to Cohen (1988) [62]. Although the integration of artificial intelligence showed a significant association with hospital resilience in bivariate analysis, it was not identified as an independent predictor in the multivariate model, suggesting that its role remains complementary and context-dependent.

Logistical and security challenges were also significantly associated with hospital resilience; however, this relationship should be interpreted cautiously as reflecting adaptive responses developed under adverse conditions rather than a direct positive effect.

Overall, the findings suggest that strengthening hospital resilience in Lebanon requires an integrated strategy combining institutional preparedness, humanitarian partnerships, technological modernization, and the mitigation of persistent logistical and security vulnerabilities.

Future research could involve a larger and more representative sample, include hospitals from other Lebanese regions, compare public and private institutions, and adopt longitudinal or qualitative approaches to better understand how hospital resilience develops over time. It may also explore the role of leadership, digital transformation, artificial intelligence, governance, and collaboration in strengthening resilience, while extending the analysis to other fragile or conflict-affected contexts.
