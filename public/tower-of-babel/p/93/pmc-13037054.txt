Heart rate variability to objectively measure fear of falling in post fall patients: a protocol study
Authors: Marie-Caroline Play, Adrien Juranville, Bastien Poirson, Alexandre Kubicki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAt least one-third of older people experience falls each year, often resulting in a fear of falling that leads to a cycle of negative consequences. Fear of falling activates the amygdala, which triggers a complex response involving cognitive, physiological, and behavioral components. Physiologically, the stress response includes autonomic changes like increased heart rate and reduced Heart Rate Variability (HRV). Behaviorally, heightened alertness leads to fight, flight, or freeze responses, influenced by amygdala and Periaqueductal Gray activations. In conditions like post-fall syndrome fear of falling leads to freezing, involving motor rigidity and disrupted motor control.MethodsThis protocol aims to objectively assess fear of falling in older patients with post-fall syndrome by measuring HRV in various conditions and compare these data with short FES-I results. A gap between physiological and self-reported measures could be reported. The secondary objective will be to measure the potential increase in muscle activation during fear. Hypotheses are that HRV changes will reflect fear levels and muscle activation will increase with fear. At least 30 patients recruited in geriatric care facility, with post-fall syndrome will be included in this study. Heart Rate Variability (HRV) and Electro-Myo-Graphy (EMG) of trapezoids muscles will be measured during initial sitting position and two stand-up positions with and without worktop (involving postural threat). The primary outcome will be the Root Mean Square of successive RR interval differences (RMSSD). Secondary outcome will include standard deviation of normal-to-normal intervals (SDNN), percentage of successive RR intervals with difference bigger than 50 ms (pNN50), low-frequency power (LF) and high-frequency power (HF), short-term analysis (SD1), as well as Root Mean Square (RMS) muscle activity. All these outcomes will be computed relatively to the control condition (i.e. slow effortless breathing). ANCOVA based on HRV and EMG will assess the differences across positions, and correlation between HRV and EMG will be investigated. Key variables, such as orthostatic hypotension and depressive status, will be considered as co-variables.DiscussionIf successful, the results could highlight some discrepancies between self-reported and physiological measures of fear and support the use of HRV as an emotional marker for fear of falling during rehabilitation. This would offer a valuable tool for tailoring sessions to patients’ anxiety levels, using a simple ECG sensor in clinical practice.Trial registration2024A0093542.Keywords: Fear of falling, Aging, Post-fall syndrom, Heart rate variability, Freezing

Background

At least one in three older people falls every year. These falls result in several negative health consequences, including the development of a fear of falling, which itself increases the risk of falling, keeping the person in a vicious cycle ( [1] for a review).

Fear of falling is defined as the self-assessment of a low ability to avoid falls in daily activities [2]. It has negative health consequences, such as impaired gait ( [3] for a review), onset of depression, restriction of daily activities [4], increased risk of falling [5], and decrease in quality of life [6].

Fear is one of the primary emotions as defined by Ekman [7]. Fear of falling, which can range from anxiety to a true phobia [8], shares common characteristics for all individuals affected. In response to a perceived threatening context, fear triggers stimulation in the amygdala, a deep area of the brain. Once stimulated, the amygdala “acts” by producing a fear-type emotion through a complex reaction, which includes three identifiable components: cognitive, physiological, and behavioral.

On a cognitive level, activation of the amygdala causes attention to be directed toward the perceived threat. Toby Ellmers and his team proposed that fear of falling is “an emotional response to a real or imagined threat to balance”, highlighting the confusion that is often made between this emotional “state” (at one moment I am afraid to fall) and the latent “trait” (I am always afraid to fall) that is frequently found with aged adults with an history of fall(s) [9]. For these authors, this last state named “Concerns about falling” should be defined as “a lasting feeling of dread and apprehension about situations that are believed to threaten of challenge balance.”

Physiologically, two main consequences result from amygdala activation and the vegetative response that leads to changes in the functioning of the autonomic nervous system. The sympathetic nervous system takes over from the parasympathetic system, causing a rapid increase in heart rate and a decrease in Heart Rate Variability (HRV), along with other autonomic manifestations (increased temperature and sweating). These physiological changes prepare the body for a behavioral response, first theorized by Walter Cannon in the early 20th century as the “fight or flight” reaction [10].

The body enters a state of “alert” and this heightened vigilance leads to changes in behavior. As shown in Fig. 1, fighting behavior is associated with dorsal activation. Dorsolateral or lateral activation triggers a flight behavior, leading to active movement. Excessive ventral activation results in freezing, leading to global motor rigidity (i.e. Increased muscles activation).

Columns of the Periaqueductal Gray (PAG) (From Depaulis et al. 1985). As the hormonal and autonomic systems enter a heightened state of alert, amygdala activation stimulates a subcortical structure of the brainstem. Antoine Depaulis and his colleagues have repeatedly demonstrated [11–13] the importance of this PAG activation. Depending on the activated area, motor responses differ significantly. If the most dorsal column is activated, the subject enters “fight” mode; if the intermediate columns are activated, the subject enters “flight” mode. Finally, if the most ventral column is activated, the subject “freezes.”

In post-fall syndrome, backward shift and oppositional hypertonia could result from freezing, with intense fear of anterior void and verticality. Then, freezing behavior during fear of falling drives to increased muscular activity and global rigidity [14]. Fear of falling involves “motor breakdowns” as a disruption of feedforward and feedback loops of motor control, based on top-down prediction and bottom-up sensory inputs [15].

Clinicians work to reduce fear of falling in rehabilitation practice, in psychotherapeutic or physiotherapy treatments, by means of gradual exposure to the threat. One of their difficulties is to objectively measure this emotion in ecological situations [16–18]. Several authors reported an impaired anticipatory postural adjustment and increased in postural sway which could be interpreted both as a negative (i.e. instability) and positive (i.e. adaptative mechanism) behavior. Moreover, questionaries have been validated, as the FES-1 or the ABC scale [19, 20]. They are relevant with some patients but in geriatric care or community dwelling, several older adults have cognitive deficiencies [21]. As these questionaries refer to external imagined situation and not to the present one, it could exist a discrepancy with these self-reported measures and the physiological state of the patients when they are afraid.

In this context, the objective of this protocol is to implement a quantified assessment of the fear of falling, through short-term Heart Rate Variability (HRV) evaluation [22, 23] in different positions on patients with post-fall syndrome, and to compare with the self-reported measures given by the short FES-I.

Methods/Design

Design and objectives of the study

A cross-sectional study will be conducted according to the STROBE guidelines [24]. This study will be conducted in geriatric rehabilitation care facilities in the Nord Franche-Comté hospital (Franche-Comté, France). This study will be supervised by doctors, physiotherapists and academic researchers.

Our principal objective is to measure HRV in different positions, involving different postural threats, on patients with or without post-fall syndrome. One can hypothesize that HRV will be a responsiveness measure of fear of falling during different level of threat in patient presenting fear of falling.

The secondary objective will be to measure the potential increase in muscle activation during fear. Muscle activity will be measured by Electro-Myo-Graphy (EMG) data during the different positions adopted by participants.

Participants

Patients of post-fall group will be eligible for the study if they met the following inclusion criteria: 1) a confirmed diagnosis of post-fall syndrome according to poor mobility, retropulsion and fear of falling [25] ; 2) > 65 years of age; 3) Functional Reach Test score < 26 cm; 4) Mini-Mental State Examination up to 20 and 5) ability to stand up without human or technical help.

Patients of control group will be eligible for the study if they met the following inclusion criteria: 1) > 65 years of age; 2) Functional Reach Test score > 26 cm; 3) Mini-Mental State Examination up to 20 and 4) ability to stand up without human or technical help.

For both groups, non-inclusion criteria will include the presence of diseases known to affect HRV (cardiac arrythmia including atrial fibrillation, acute heart failure (NYHA III-IV)) and uncontrolled arterial hypertension, implemented pacemaker, myocardial infarction in the past twelve months.

Antidepressant (except tricyclic medication) and conventional cardiac medications including beta-receptor blockers treatment but excluding casual antiarrhythmic drugs (flecainidine, amiodarone) will be allowed. Orthostatic hypotension will be evaluated and allowed except if symptoms do not allow testing. Patients with depressive and/or neurological disorders will be included and Geriatrics Depression Scale (GDS) [26] will measure their depressive status. These two symptoms will be integrated as covariables, as they have an impact on HRV [27].

The exclusion criteria will include any discomfort during the experiments, the patient’s desire to stop the study, or the impossibility of correctly performing the Control Condition, i.e. slow effortless breathing (see part. Procedures).

Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants after a detailed explanation of the procedures. The National Committee for the Protection of Persons has studied the conformity of the protocol and recommended minor corrections. These corrections are currently under consideration with the reference number 2024-A00935-42.

Procedures

All trials will be performed in a single visit by a physiotherapist. At the beginning of the session, the short FES-I will be administered to the participant. Then, the participant will be equipped and HRV will be studied under five conditions [1–5] in relation to the control condition. Conditions 3,4, and 5 will be done in a standing condition on a force plate, with feet shoulder width apart on the footprints.

Control Condition (CC): Slow effortless breathing in sitting position. Subjects will be required to inhale and exhale at a rate of six breaths per minute, if possible [28]. The score of breaths reached by each participant will be computed to used it as an exclusion criterion. If the participant is unable to breath less than nine cycles per minute (until eight), he/she will be excluded from the study.

To avoid any fatigue/learning interference between condition 3 and 4, we will reverse the order of conditions 3 and 4 (then condition 4 and 3) for half the subjects.

In the different conditions, participant will be asked to avoid any movements of their arms.

Evaluations

HRV examinations will be performed during a pre-defined time slot for all participants to avoid circadian HRV variations. A wireless ECG-recording device (Bittium Faros 180, Bittium Inc., Oulu, Finland; sampling frequency: 1 000 Hz) will be secured by a hypoallergenic strap during trials explained above.

The muscle activity of 4 muscles will be collected with wireless EMG sensors (Wave, Cometa, Italy; sampling frequency: 2 000 Hz). Activation of both superior and median trapezoids muscles will be measured bilaterally. In case of upper limbs movements, the trial will be removed to accurately measure the increased activation linked to freezing behavior.

Skin preparation and electrodes position will be conducted according to the European recommendations for surface EMG (seniam.org). The EMG signal will be previously checked before trials recording by asking the participant to shrug his shoulders.

Center of pressure (COP) profiles in the anterior-posterior and medio-lateral directions will be examined using a stabilometric platform (Techno concept® with Posturewin software, France, 40 Hz).

Signal processing

HRV signals will be processed under Kubios software (UKU, Kuopio, Finland). The implemented QRS detector algorithm will be applied to extract the beat-to-beat RR intervals from the ECG data (RR interval is defined as the time distance between nearest R peaks in electrocardiogram). The time-domain parameters will include the mean RR interval, standard deviation of normal-to-normal intervals (SDNN), root mean square of successive RR interval differences (RMSSD), and percentage of successive RR intervals with difference bigger than 50 ms (pNN50). SDNN is known to be an index of physiological resilience against stress whereas RMSSD and pNN50 are recognized indexes of parasympathetic activity. The frequency-domain parameters will contain low-frequency power (LF − 0.04–0.15 Hz linked to the vagal and sympathetic activities) and high-frequency power (HF − 0.15 –0.14 Hz connected to the vagal activity). The LF/HF ratio is an important marker of sympathetic modulation or sympathovagal balance on HRV control. Short-term analysis (SD1), reflecting the instantaneous beat-to-beat variability mediated by the vagal activity, will be extracted from the non-linear domain analysis [29].

HRV parameters will be computed relatively to control condition, i.e. slow effortless breathing challenge. To assess the heart rate response to slow effortless breathing, we will detect the longest RR interval observed at the end of the expiration phase and the shortest RR interval observed at the end of the inspiration phase. EMG signals will be processed using Matlab (Matlab R2023b, Mathworks, Inc., Natick, MA). EMG data will be full-wave rectified. To create a linear envelope, EMG data will be smoothed with a moving average of 100 ms. Root Mean Square (RMS, in µV) will be calculated.

Anterior-posterior and medio-lateral displacements, as well as mean velocity and velocity variance of COP will be extracted.

Sample size

Cavalade et al. [30] measured RMSSD in fear (19.1 ± 7.2 ms) vs. no fear (26.0 ± 7.7 ms) situation on skydivers before and during jump. Based on the hypothesis that this difference would be transposed on patients with post-fall syndrome, it would be necessary to include 30 subjects (80% power and 5% type I error, using one-trailed hypothesis).

After normality assessment using the Shapiro-Wilk test, ANCOVA on Normalized HRV parameters and RMS will assess the differences across conditions (co-variables: orthostatic hypotension, depressive status).

In addition, Pearson coefficient will evaluate correlation between HRV parameters and RMS. Statistical significance level will be set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The expected results should reveal a main effect of the condition. Statistical analysis is anticipated to show [1] a decrease in HRV across conditions 1 to 4 (HRV1 > HRV2 > HRV3 > HRV4> HRV5) and [2] an increase in trapezius muscles RMS over the same conditions (RMS1 > RMS2 > RMS3 > RMS4> RMS5). Consequently, a correlation between HRV and RMS is expected. We also anticipate an increase of the CoP kinematic parameters across the conditions.

Detecting a significant difference between condition 1 and 2 may be challenging, as the only variable changing will be the environment: participants will listen to instructions from their physiotherapist. While no demanding movement is required in condition 2, simply knowing they will need to stand up may elicit a stress response.

However, two covariates could influence these results. If ANCOVA indicates an effect of orthostatic hypotension – commonly causing reactive tachycardia after the sit-to-stand transfer - it will be necessary to analyze this subgroup separately within the sample or exclude these subjects if their number is insufficient. Similarly, if depressive status independently affect HRV and/or RMS regardless of the conditions, the same approach will be applied.

If these results are confirmed, clinicians could use HRV as an emotional marker. Rehabilitation sessions must be carefully tailored to the patient’s anxiety/fear of falling, to [1] avoid setting them up for failure and [2] support their progress in managing this fear. A simple 3-electrodes ECG sensor could enable the measurement of this valuable marker, facilitating its integration into optimal clinical practice.
