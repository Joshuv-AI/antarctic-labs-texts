Systematic Review, Meta-analysis, and Trial Sequential Analysis of Randomised Controlled Trials Comparing Balloon Expandable with Self Expanding Stents for the Treatment of Iliac Artery Occlusive Disease
Authors: Chee Yee Hew, George A. Antoniou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis review aimed to investigate the efficacy and safety of balloon expandable vs. self expanding stents in the treatment of iliac artery occlusive disease.MethodA systematic review of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) comparing balloon expandable with self expanding stents for the treatment of iliac artery occlusive disease was conducted following PRISMA guidelines. MEDLINE and Embase were searched using the Ovid interface. Meta-analysis and trial sequential analysis were performed. Dichotomous data were pooled using fixed effects models (Mantel–Haenszel method). Trial sequential analysis assumed alpha = 5% and power = 80%. The revised Cochrane tool and GRADE framework were used to assess risk of bias (RoB) and certainty of evidence, respectively.ResultsThree RCTs reporting 891 patients were included. Meta-analysis found no statistically significant difference in target vessel revascularisation (TVR) (odds ratio [OR] 1.55, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.43–5.58; p = .50), primary patency (OR 1.24, 95% CI 0.34–4.48; p = .74), amputation (risk difference 0.00, 95% CI –0.02 – 0.02; p = .99), and major adverse vascular events (OR 1.89, 95% CI 0.90–3.97; p = .090) when balloon expandable stents were compared with self expanding stents. For target lesion revascularisation (TLR), the results favoured self expanding stents (OR 2.26, 95% CI 1.00–5.11; p = .050) with borderline statistical significance. Odds of death were lower for self expanding stents (OR 3.25, 95% CI 1.16–9.05; p = .020). Trial sequential analysis confirmed that further trials are required to demonstrate a significant effect. There were “some concerns” in RoB for TLR and TVR, and high RoB in one study for primary patency. Certainty of evidence was low.ConclusionMeta-analysis of highest level data showed no significant difference in certain clinical outcomes between balloon expandable and self expanding stents in the treatment of iliac artery occlusive disease. Overall, there are insufficient data in the current literature to ascertain the superiority of one stent strategy over another, and trial sequential analysis confirmed that further trials are required.

INTRODUCTION

The treatment of atherosclerotic iliac artery disease has shifted towards endovascular techniques. Stenting of the iliac artery is commonly employed to treat iliac artery lesions, especially occlusive lesions.1 There is, however, a paucity of data in the current literature on a specific stenting strategy as to whether balloon expandable or self expanding stents are superior in terms of efficacy and safety. Balloon expandable stents are more rigid, with greater outward radial force allowing for more precise deployment, whereas self expanding stents have reduced radial force, with higher elasticity and therefore greater conformability.

There are numerous studies examining the use of balloon expandable and self expanding stents in the treatment of iliac artery lesions, which have shown satisfactory outcomes for both techniques.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 However, these studies are non-comparative and, therefore, unable to answer the question of whether one technique is superior to the other.

Current European Society for Vascular Surgery (ESVS) guidelines recommend that self expanding stents may be considered over balloon expandable stents for patients who require stenting of iliac artery lesions (Class IIb, Level B).1 A previously published global vascular guideline on the management of chronic limb threatening ischaemia, which stemmed from a collaboration between the ESVS, Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS), and World Federation of Vascular Societies (WFVS), made no specific recommendation as to the optimum stent choice in the treatment of inflow or aorto-iliac disease;9 this highlights the lack of high quality evidence in this area to guide clinical practice.

The number of comparative studies on this subject is low. This systematic review and meta-analysis screened and examined all published randomised controlled trials (RCTs) in the current literature, with the aim of evaluating the efficacy and safety of balloon expandable vs. self expanding stents in the treatment of iliac artery occlusive disease. This review is the first to specifically examine the use of balloon expandable vs. self expanding stents in the treatment of iliac artery occlusive disease and intended to provide a high quality, evidence based analysis to bridge the current knowledge gap and guide recommendations for clinical practice.

METHOD

Review registration and conduct

This systematic review was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (registration number: CRD42024604037). The review was structured in accordance with the updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines.10,11

Eligibility criteria

RCTs comparing balloon expandable with self expanding stents for iliac artery occlusive disease were included. Only completed studies with fully published manuscripts were considered. Studies were deemed eligible if they clearly reported the use of balloon expandable vs. self expanding stents to treat iliac artery occlusive disease, with a minimum follow up of 12 months. Both covered and bare metal stents were accepted as eligible for this review.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were target lesion revascularisation (TLR), target vessel revascularisation (TVR), and primary patency. To be included, studies had to report at least one of these primary outcomes, within a clear follow up time frame. Secondary outcomes included amputation, major adverse vascular events (MAVE), and death. MAVE was included as a secondary outcome in this review if studies provided a clear definition of this outcome, which could include composite endpoint of death, including cardiovascular death, stroke, target limb revascularisation, and amputation.

Information sources and search strategy

An electronic search of the literature was performed using the Ovid interface, which included databases such as Medline and Embase. A combination of controlled vocabulary (thesaurus) and free text terms was used to search the databases. There were no language or date constraints. The literature search was last performed on 4 October 2024. References from eligible articles were also screened. The search strategies are presented in Supplementary Material S1.

Study selection and data collection

Two independent review authors (C.Y.H., G.A.A.) conducted the literature search and screening process. The data were extracted by one reviewer (C.Y.H.) and cross checked by the second reviewer (G.A.A.) to maintain consistency and precision. Data were extracted from manuscripts’ texts, tables, and figures, which were recorded using a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft, Seattle, WA, USA). Collected data included study characteristics, patient demographics, and study outcomes.

Study risk of bias and certainty of evidence assessment

Risk of bias (RoB) of the studies was assessed using the Risk of Bias assessment (RoB2) tool developed by the Cochrane Collaboration.12 This was performed by two reviewers (C.Y.H., G.A.A.) independently, followed by discussion surrounding any discrepancies or disagreement to achieve resolution. Trial investigators were not contacted for any further information.

A summary of findings table was generated, and the certainty of evidence was graded using the system developed by the Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) working group using the GRADEpro GDT online platform (https://gdt.gradepro.org/app/).13

Data analysis

Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated as pooled estimates for dichotomous outcome data using the Mantel–Haenszel method for the measure of treatment effects. Risk difference (RD) was calculated if any study reported zero events in both groups using the Mantel–Haenszel method. Forest plots were created for each treatment effect.

The Cochran's Q (χ2) test was used to study heterogeneity between studies. Inconsistency was quantified by calculating I2 and interpreted using the following guide: 0–40% might not be important; 30–60% may represent moderate heterogeneity; 50–90% may represent substantial heterogeneity; and 75–100% may represent considerable heterogeneity.14

Trial sequential analysis was performed to control for type 1 and type 2 errors and to identify the need for further clinical trials. To control the risk of type 1 error, thresholds for the z values were adjusted with the O'Brien–Fleming α spending function, while the risk of type 2 error was controlled with the β-spending function and futility boundaries.

Statistical softwares used were RevMan version 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark) and Trial Sequential Analysis Software 0.9.5.5 Beta (Copenhagen Trial Unit, Copenhagen, Denmark).

For the outcome of primary patency, the SENS-ILIAC and Amin et al. trials reported this outcome.16,17 The overall risk of bias for SENS-ILIAC was judged to be high. For this study, there was high RoB in the Measurement of outcome domain and “some concerns” for Selection of reported results. This judgment was based on imaging not being used to determine the primary patency, which was the originally planned method for the measurement of this outcome.16 Instead, this was replaced with clinical patency rate based on clinical judgement and ankle brachial index, which was a deviation from the trial's registered protocol.16 The trial investigators in this study were also non-blinded in the assessment of outcome.16 As for the study by Amin et al., the overall RoB was judged to be “some concerns”. Firstly, there were no further details regarding the randomisation manner.17 Secondly, the study did not provide information regarding blinding of outcome assessors; therefore, the assumption was that outcome assessors were not blinded and likely to be aware of the intervention received by the study participants.17 Thirdly, there was no prospective registration of the trial protocol.17

Certainty of evidence

Certainty of evidence is presented in the summary of findings in Supplementary Table S3. The certainty of evidence for TLR and TVR was judged to be low. The certainty assessment was downgraded for RoB because there were “some concerns” for overall RoB stemming from the outcome measurement due to unblinded assessors. Imprecision was also downgraded due to wide confidence intervals. For the outcome of primary patency, the certainty of evidence was judged to be very low due to high overall RoB in one study16 and “some concerns” in another study.17 Imprecision was downgraded due to wide confidence intervals.

Primary outcomes

The ICE and SENS-ILIAC trials reported TLR and TVR.15,16 The SENS-ILIAC trial and study by Amin et al. reported primary patency.16,17 There was no statistically significant difference in TVR and primary patency when balloon expandable stents were compared with self expanding stents in the treatment of iliac artery occlusive disease (TVR: OR 1.55, 95% CI 0.43–5.58, p = .50; primary patency: OR 1.24, 95% CI 0.34–4.48, p = .74). For TLR, the results favoured self expanding stents (OR 2.26, 95% CI 1.00–5.11, p = .050) with borderline statistical significance. The test for statistical heterogeneity was low (I2 = 0%).

Secondary outcomes

All three trials reported data on amputation, whereas two trials (ICE and SENS-ILIAC) also reported MAVE.15, 16, 17 For the secondary outcomes of amputation and MAVE, there was no statistically significant difference between balloon expandable stents and self expanding stents in the treatment of iliac artery occlusive disease (amputation: RD 0.00, 95% CI –0.02 – 0.02, p = .99; MAVE: OR 1.89, 95% CI 0.90–3.97, p = .090). ICE and SENS-ILIAC15,16 reported data on death. The odds of death were statistically significantly lower in the self expanding stent group compared with the balloon expandable stent group (1.3% in the self expanding stent group vs. 4.3% in the balloon expandable stent group; OR 3.25, 95% CI 1.16–9.05, p = .020).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding the study by Amin et al. for the outcome of amputation. This showed no statistically significant difference between the two types of stents (RD 0.00, 95% CI –0.01 – 0.01, p = .55). The forest plot is presented in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Similarly for MAVE, the obtained information size was lower than the required information size, and the cumulative z score did not reach statistical significance by crossing either the conventional or O'Brian–Fleming boundaries, meaning that the results of the meta-analysis were inconclusive and further trials are required to demonstrate the presence or absence of a significant effect (Fig. 4B).

DISCUSSION

Comparative data for endovascular treatment of aorto-iliac disease are lacking,18 and thus far only three RCTs comparing the use of self expanding with balloon expandable stents in the treatment of iliac artery occlusive disease have been identified. This review showed that there was no statistically significant difference in TVR, primary patency, amputation, and MAVE when balloon expandable stents were compared with self expanding stents.

The recommendation from the ESVS 2024 guidance for peripheral artery disease (PAD) states that self expanding rather than balloon expandable stents may be considered for patients requiring stent placement for iliac artery lesions, due to lower risk of re-stenosis and TLR for self expanding stents, based on results from the ICE trial.1,15 The more recent SENS-ILIAC study did not show any difference in TLR between the two groups.16 From this meta-analysis, which included both studies, borderline statistical significance for TLR was observed in favour of self expanding stents (OR 2.26, 95% CI 1.00–5.11, p = .050), which lends some support to the recommendation from the ESVS. As for the outcome of death, there was a statistically significant difference in the odds of death favouring the self expanding stent group vs. balloon expandable stent group. It is difficult to ascertain the precise cause for this observation. The authors believe that the most plausible explanation is a type 1 error due to limited data, which is further supported by results from sequential analysis of the trial. Other notable points to consider include the lower percentage of patients with coronary artery disease in the self expanding stent group for the ICE trial,15 and higher rates of pre- and post-dilatation, longer stented length, and larger stent diameter in the self expanding stent group for both the ICE and SENS-ILIAC trials.15,16 The more flexible nature of a self-expanding stent is also thought to result in less neointimal hyperplasia; yet, there was no difference in the reported technical success between the two groups in either trial.15,16 Further robust, prospective, randomised studies with larger numbers of patients are needed to investigate the superiority of one stent over another.

There were several notable differences in the conduct of the studies. The ICE trial included patients with Rutherford stage 1–4 PAD due to a single significant common iliac or external iliac artery lesion, not extending to the aorta or common femoral artery.15 In the SENS-ILIAC trial, investigators included patients with Rutherford stage 2–6 PAD, and patients had to have at least one patent tibioperoneal run off vessel.16 The study by Amin et al. did not specify the PAD Rutherford stage, and the inclusion criteria were symptomatic common iliac artery atherosclerosis with severe stenosis of >3 cm in length.17 The inclusion of patients who were claudicants, plus those with relatively simpler iliac lesions, especially in the ICE trial,15 limited the external validity of the findings because patients and lesions treated in real world practice can be significantly more complex.

It is also noteworthy that there was a difference in the timeframes for enrolment in the ICE and SENS-ILIAC trials, with recruitment periods of 2010–2013 and 2013–2019, respectively.15,16 Although the types of stents used in both studies are still available for use, there may be a difference in operator experience over time, which may have influenced the results. The evolution of technology in the endovascular era is rapid, with the emergence of numerous improved stents, including drug eluting stents. The use of intravascular shockwave lithotripsy is also gaining popularity and has been shown to be efficacious and safe.19,20 As a result, there could be an increase in the use of self expanding stents, as the problem with calcification is mitigated.21 All these emerging technologies may alter patient outcomes and any future studies need to take this into consideration when comparing results with older studies.

It is recognised that appropriate post-procedure antithrombotic therapy is crucial for any vascular interventions. All three studies implemented post-procedure dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT); however, the duration was strikingly different: one month for ICE, 12 months for SENS-ILIAC, and three months for the study by Amin et al.15, 16, 17 The longer duration DAPT in the SENS-ILIAC trial could potentially have contributed to similar patency outcomes between the two stent groups, based on the hypothesis that antiplatelet agents reduce neointimal hyperplasia.22,23 However, there are various other significant differences in the conduct of all three trials, thereby making it challenging to distinguish any impact of DAPT duration on the outcomes.

The strength of this review is that this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to specifically examine whether balloon expandable or self expanding stents are superior in terms of safety and efficacy. Trial sequential analysis was also performed for the outcomes of TLR, MAVE, and death, using O'Brien–Fleming boundaries, which showed that boundaries were not crossed and hence the results of the meta-analysis were inconclusive to demonstrate the presence or absence of a significant effect. This indicated that more trial data are required to determine whether balloon expandable or self expanding stents are superior.

One of the limitations of this meta-analysis was the lack of stratification for calcification and location in the studies, thereby making the results challenging to interpret. It is recognised that the common iliac and external iliac arteries differ in vessel properties, with some studies showing poorer outcomes post-endovascular treatment of the external iliac artery, presumably thought to be due to the smaller diameter and higher chance of diffuse disease.24, 25, 26, 27 The common iliac artery, on the other hand, is larger and frequently calcified; therefore, it may be better suited to be treated with balloon expandable stents, which have higher outward radial forces.28 Yet, a study by Lee et al. found no difference in the anatomic patency rates for common iliac and external iliac artery stenting;29 however, this study only examined male patients, thereby precluding generalisation,29 as there is some evidence that patency rates of iliac stents for women are less favourable.24,30 Further studies on this subject should strongly consider the factors of sex, location of lesion, degree of calcification, and lesion length to identify the subset of patients who would benefit from a particular type of stent.

Additionally, there was a notable difference in the method of outcome measurement for the studies: the ICE study utilised duplex ultrasound to assess for re-stenosis,15 whereas SENS-ILIAC clinically evaluated primary patency with the ankle brachial index, which deviated from their initial trial protocol of using imaging studies,16 thereby portraying a significant RoB. The study by Amin et al. did not provide clarity regarding their method of measuring primary patency,17 which raised some concerns for RoB. It is also worth noting that both ICE and SENS-ILIAC were underpowered, and the study by Amin et al. was a small study with a total of 30 patients.15, 16, 17 However, this review performed a sensitivity analysis for the outcome of amputation by excluding the study by Amin et al., and this showed no difference in the effect estimates.

Finally, it is recognised that the number of trials in this review was small and they were heterogenous in their conduct. The search was performed using two of the largest and well recognised medical electronic databases; however, Cochrane Central was not searched. Additionally, the comparison between bare metal stents with covered stents was not evaluated because there are already multiple existing reviews in the current literature on this subject.31, 32, 33, 34, 35 These reviews, however, do not distinguish whether the stents were balloon expandable or self expanding. The potential merits of including other relevant observational, non-comparative studies in this review were considered;36, 37, 38, 39 however, current Cochrane guidelines do not recommend combining effect estimates from studies with fundamentally different designs as there is potential for bias and high heterogeneity,40 which can make the results unreliable and even more difficult to interpret.

Conclusion

Meta-analysis of the highest level data showed no significant differences in certain clinical outcomes between balloon expandable and self expanding stents in the treatment of iliac artery occlusive disease. Overall, there are insufficient data in current literature to ascertain the superiority of one stent strategy over another due to the limited number of comparative studies and low certainty of evidence, and trial sequential analysis confirmed that further trials are required.
