Right-sided infective endocarditis with septic pulmonary emboli and secondary pneumothorax: A case report
Authors: Fatima Nasreddine, Abdallah Haj Hassan, Zeinab El Mawla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRight-sided infective endocarditis (RSIE), particularly prevalent among intravenous drug users (IVDU) and often caused by Staphylococcus aureus, frequently leads to septic pulmonary emboli (SPE). While pneumothorax (PTX) is a rare but severe complication of SPE, its occurrence significantly complicates patient management and necessitates prompt recognition. We present the case of a 32-year-old male IVDU with RSIE and MRSA bacteremia who developed acute respiratory deterioration due to cavitating septic pulmonary emboli and a secondary pneumothorax. This case highlights the critical importance for radiologists to carefully evaluate for cavitary septic emboli and pneumothorax, especially when lesions are subpleural, in patients with RSIE and sudden respiratory worsening, underscoring the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in this vulnerable population.Keywords: Infective endocarditis, Septic pulmonary emboli, Pneumothorax, Intravenous drug use, MRSA

Introduction

Infective endocarditis (IE) remains a serious condition associated with significant morbidity and mortality worldwide. Right-sided infective endocarditis (RSIE) predominantly affects the tricuspid valve and is strongly associated with intravenous drug use (IVDU), representing a growing public health concern [1]. The incidence of IE in IVDU populations has been on the rise, with Staphylococcus aureus, particularly methicillin-resistant S aureus (MRSA), being the most common causative organism due to its prevalence in skin flora and its ability to adhere to damaged endothelial surfaces [2].

A frequent and often severe complication of RSIE is septic pulmonary embolism (SPE), occurring in approximately 75-80% of cases [3]. These emboli originate from infected vegetations on the tricuspid valve, fragment, and travel to the pulmonary vasculature, leading to a spectrum of pulmonary manifestations including infiltrates, nodules, and cavitations. While less common, the rupture of subpleural cavitary lesions can result in pneumothorax (PTX), a life-threatening event that significantly complicates the clinical course and management [1]. This report details a challenging case of RSIE with MRSA bacteremia in an IVDU patient who developed severe pulmonary complications, including SPE and secondary pneumothorax, and subsequently left against medical advice. This case underscores the diagnostic and therapeutic complexities, as well as the unique psychosocial challenges, encountered in this vulnerable patient population. It also emphasizes the crucial role of imaging in identifying these complications, particularly the need for radiologists to carefully assess for cavitating septic emboli and pneumothorax, especially when lesions are subpleural, in patients with RSIE presenting with acute respiratory distress.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old male presented to the emergency department with a 3-day history of high-grade fever (up to 39.5°C), chills, progressive dyspnea, tachypnea, and pleuritic chest discomfort. He reported generalized fatigue but denied hemoptysis or syncope. His medical history included chronic hepatitis C infection and a previous episode of infective endocarditis. He was a heavy smoker and an active intravenous drug user, injecting morphine daily. He had no known drug allergies and was not on regular medications.

On initial evaluation, the patient was febrile (39°C) and mildly dyspneic, but hemodynamically stable. Vital signs included a heart rate of 110 bpm, blood pressure of 130/70 mmHg, respiratory rate of 22 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. Cardiovascular examination revealed normal S1 and S2 heart sounds with a holosystolic murmur best heard at the lower left sternal border, consistent with tricuspid regurgitation. Pulmonary examination showed bilaterally decreased air entry. Bilateral lower limb edema (grade II) was noted. No peripheral stigmata of infective endocarditis were observed. Electrocardiography showed normal sinus rhythm.

Initial laboratory investigations showed a white blood cell count of 9.6 × 10⁹/L, which later increased to 15 × 10⁹/L with neutrophilic predominance (85%). C-reactive protein was markedly elevated at 296 mg/L. Renal function was preserved (serum creatinine 0.5 mg/dL). Pro–B-type natriuretic peptide was elevated at 1109 pg/mL. Two sets of blood cultures grew MRSA.

Chest radiography demonstrated pulmonary congestion. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), performed shortly after admission, revealed preserved left ventricular systolic function (ejection fraction 60%) and large, mobile vegetations on the tricuspid valve (2.3 × 0.5 cm and 1.9 × 0.6 cm). Right ventricular function was not explicitly quantified but appeared preserved. Moderate tricuspid regurgitation and severe pulmonary hypertension were also present. A diagnosis of right-sided infective endocarditis involving the tricuspid valve was established, and intravenous vancomycin therapy was initiated immediately following blood culture collection.

During hospitalization, on hospital day 5, the patient experienced acute clinical deterioration with worsening dyspnea, increased work of breathing, and sudden oxygen desaturation to 80% on room air. Septic pulmonary embolism was strongly suspected. An urgent contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest, performed on the same day, demonstrated multiple bilateral, peripheral, and lower lung field predominant nodular opacities, several with cavitary transformation (thin-walled cavities, up to 2 cm in diameter). There was also evidence of pleural thickening. A significant secondary pneumothorax was identified in the right hemithorax, estimated to be approximately 30% of the hemithorax volume, likely due to rupture of a subpleural cavitary lesion. No empyema or significant pleural collection was noted at this time (Fig. 1).

Contrast-enhanced axial chest CT images demonstrating multiple bilateral peripheral cavitating septic emboli, a large right-sided pneumothorax, and associated pleural thickening. The cavitating lesions are predominantly subpleural, with one likely having ruptured to cause the pneumothorax.

A chest tube was inserted immediately, leading to partial lung re-expansion and stabilization of the patient's respiratory status. However, follow-up imaging revealed persistent pleural inflammation and extensive cavitary disease. Thoracic surgical consultation was obtained, and surgical decortication was planned to address the persistent pleural inflammation, remove necrotic tissue, and promote lung healing, given the extensive cavitary disease and the risk of chronic complications. Despite medical advice and the planned surgical intervention, the patient elected to leave the hospital against medical advice on hospital day 10 and was subsequently lost to follow-up.

Discussion

This case vividly illustrates the severe and complex pulmonary complications that can arise in patients with RSIE, particularly in the context of IVDU and MRSA bacteremia. The patient's clinical presentation, characterized by high-grade fever, dyspnea, and pleuritic chest pain, coupled with positive blood cultures for MRSA and echocardiographic evidence of tricuspid valve vegetations, is highly characteristic of RSIE in an IVDU [1,2]. The presence of large vegetations further increases the risk of embolic events.

The development of septic pulmonary emboli is a well-recognized and frequent complication of RSIE, occurring when infected vegetations fragment and travel to the pulmonary circulation [3].

These emboli cause a localized inflammatory response and vasculitis, leading to infarction and necrosis of lung parenchyma. This process often results in the formation of peripheral lung nodules, which can rapidly cavitate due to enzymatic destruction and liquefaction [4]. The characteristic CT findings, as observed in our patient, include bilateral, peripheral, and often subpleural nodules with varying degrees of cavitation [5].

The subsequent development of a secondary pneumothorax, as observed in this case, represents a rare but critical and life-threatening complication of SPE [1]. The underlying pathophysiology involves the necrosis and liquefaction of lung parenchyma due to the septic emboli, leading to the formation of cavitations or pneumatoceles. When these cavitary lesions are subpleural, their rupture into the pleural space can directly cause a pneumothorax [6]. Additionally, check-valve mechanisms in small bronchioles can contribute to air trapping and further pneumatocele formation, exacerbating lung injury [1]. Our review of the literature indicates that pneumothorax in the setting of SPE is uncommon, with only a limited number of cases reported [1,7,8], highlighting the rarity and severity of this particular complication. Our case shares similarities with previously reported cases in IVDU patients with RSIE and MRSA bacteremia developing SPE and subsequent pneumothorax [7,8], However, the extent of cavitary disease and the need for planned surgical decortication in our patient underscore the severity of the pulmonary involvement.

Managing such complex cases presents significant challenges. Initial treatment focuses on prompt administration of appropriate antibiotics, such as vancomycin for MRSA, guided by antimicrobial sensitivity testing, and comprehensive supportive care. The acute pneumothorax necessitated immediate chest tube insertion, which is the standard of care for symptomatic pneumothorax to facilitate lung re-expansion and improve respiratory mechanics [8]. However, the presence of persistent pleural inflammation and extensive cavitary disease, as noted in follow-up imaging, suggested ongoing infection and a high potential for chronic complications. This often necessitates more aggressive interventions, such as surgical decortication, to address persistent infection, remove necrotic tissue, and promote lung healing [1]. Surgical decortication was planned in this patient to address the extensive cavitary disease and persistent pleural inflammation, aiming to facilitate lung re-expansion, prevent chronic empyema, and improve long-term pulmonary function.

A significant and recurring challenge in the management of RSIE, particularly within the IVDU population, is the high rate of patients leaving against medical advice (AMA). This decision, as seen in our patient, can severely compromise the effectiveness of prolonged antibiotic therapy and planned surgical interventions, leading to suboptimal treatment outcomes, higher rates of recurrence, and increased morbidity and mortality [1]. This phenomenon underscores the critical need for comprehensive care strategies that extend beyond acute medical treatment to address the complex social, psychological, and addiction-related factors influencing treatment adherence in IVDU patients. Integrated care models involving addiction specialists, social workers, and patient navigators may improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Conclusion

This case report describes a rare but severe presentation of right-sided infective endocarditis complicated by septic pulmonary emboli and secondary pneumothorax in an intravenous drug user with MRSA bacteremia. The clinical course underscores the importance of early diagnosis, particularly the role of imaging in identifying cavitating septic emboli and pneumothorax, especially when subpleural lesions are present, in patients with RSIE and acute respiratory worsening. It also highlights the challenges posed by patients leaving against medical advice in this vulnerable population. Further research into effective strategies for improving treatment adherence is warranted.
