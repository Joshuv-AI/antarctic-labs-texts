Bilateral Adrenal Mass Secondary to Bilateral Infantile Neuroblastoma in an Infant With Beckwith‐Wiedemann Syndrome
Authors: Mulualeme Nigusie, Gashaw Arega, Abel Hailu, Abreham Kassahun, Getasew Fikad, Tigest Chalachew, Bargude Balta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInfants with Beckwith‐Wiedemann syndrome face a high risk of embryonal tumors, necessitating early and consistent surveillance. This case illustrates how timely imaging and biochemical monitoring enabled successful non‐surgical management of bilateral adrenal neuroblastoma. Multidisciplinary care and structured follow‐up are essential to ensure early detection, effective treatment, and favorable outcomes.

Introduction

Beckwith‐Wiedemann syndrome (BWS) is a multisystem genomic imprinting disorder characterized by variable clinical expression and a complex molecular etiology. First described by John Bruce Beckwith and Hans‐Rudolf Wiedemann in 1960, the condition has since been the subject of over 1500 published studies. Although BWS can manifest prenatally or in adulthood, it is most frequently diagnosed during the neonatal period or early childhood, with an estimated prevalence of 1 in 10 live births [1, 2].

BWS is a heterogeneous disorder that has highly variable clinical manifestations; abnormal growth is the most common feature, which can result in macrosomia, hemihypertrophy, enlarged tongue, and visceromegaly. Other presentations include abdominal wall defects, ear lobe creases, cleft palate, genitourinary abnormalities, refractory hyperinsulinemia, and polydactyly [3, 4].

Patients with Beckwith‐Wiedemann syndrome (BWS) have an elevated risk of developing embryonal tumors and benign neoplasms, particularly within the first 8 years of life. The overall tumor risk is estimated at 5%–10%, with Wilms tumor being the most prevalent (52%), followed by hepatoblastoma (14%), neuroblastoma (10%), rhabdomyosarcoma (5%), and adrenal cortical carcinoma (3%).

The risk of malignancy is highest during the first 2 years of life, gradually declining before puberty and eventually approaching that of the general population. Common adrenal lesions in BWS patients include adrenal hyperplasia, cysts, adenomas, and neuroendocrine tumors such as neuroblastoma, adrenocortical carcinoma, and pheochromocytoma. Given this heightened susceptibility, regular tumor screening is crucial for early detection and improved outcomes [3, 5, 6]. Here, we present a case of a three‐month‐old female infant with features suggestive of BWS, including a bilateral adrenal mass, to highlight the importance of early detection of intra‐abdominal neoplasms in patients with this syndrome.

Case History/Examination

A three‐month‐old female infant was born full‐term via normal spontaneous vaginal delivery to a 29‐year‐old primiparous mother. Birth weight was 4100 g, and no congenital anomalies or hypoglycemia were noted. At 2 months, asymmetric limb and trunk growth was observed. There was no family history of genetic disorders. Clinical examination revealed right‐sided hemihypertrophy, macroglossia, and exophthalmos. Growth parameters were within normal limits; blood pressure was normal, and no abdominal masses were palpable. Asymmetric limb hypertrophy was evident, with a 3 cm discrepancy.

An abdominal ultrasound identified multiple well‐defined hypoechoic bilateral adrenal lesions (three on the right, one on the left). The largest lesion was 1.8 × 1.2 cm. No hepatic or renal masses were observed. Abdominal CT revealed small, well‐defined bilateral suprarenal masses with peripheral rim enhancement and enlargement of the right adrenal gland and kidney. Mild hepatomegaly and lower anterior abdominal wall asymmetry were also noted.

A 24‐h urine catecholamine analysis revealed elevated dopamine and increased metanephrine and normetanephrine ratios. The infant appeared well overall on examination, with notable findings of right‐sided hemihypertrophy, macroglossia, and exophthalmos. Based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention growth chart, her height was 67 cm (50th percentile) and her weight was 7 kg (25th percentile). Blood pressure measurements were within normal limits, and there was no evidence of a palpable abdominal mass, hernia, or omphalocele. The external genitalia were normal, consistent with female sex. Asymmetric limb hypertrophy was observed, with a 3‐cm discrepancy between the lengths of the right and left limbs (Figure 1).

During tumor screening, an abdominal ultrasound identified multiple well‐defined hypoechoic bilateral adrenal lesions—three on the right side and one on the left—the largest measuring 1.8 × 1.2 cm on the right. No renal or hepatic masses were detected.

Pre‐contrast abdominal CT revealed small, discrete, well‐defined, round, homogeneously soft tissue‐attenuating bilateral suprarenal masses. Post‐contrast imaging demonstrated avid peripheral rim enhancement. The lesions were more numerous on the right side, with associated enlargement of the right adrenal gland and kidney. A 24‐h urine catecholamine analysis revealed elevated dopamine levels (1407 μg), along with an increased metanephrine‐to‐creatinine ratio (789 μg/g) and a normetanephrine‐to‐creatinine ratio (911 μg/g). At the moment, the image‐guided biopsy was not feasible.

Following a tumor board discussion, the patient was diagnosed with Beckwith‐Wiedemann syndrome and bilateral infantile neuroblastoma. After consulting with the family, a clinical follow‐up approach was selected, incorporating imaging and monitoring of catecholamine levels. Abdominal ultrasound, performed every 3 months, demonstrated a progressive reduction in both the size and number of adrenal lesions, with no detectable masses at present. Urine catecholamine levels were assessed twice, with the most recent results within the normal range. Clinical evaluations at each follow‐up visit confirmed normal blood pressure.

Screening for hepatoblastoma and Wilms tumor using ultrasound and alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP) testing yielded no abnormal findings. Apart from obstructive sleep apnea secondary to adenotonsillar hypertrophy, successfully managed with surgical intervention, her follow‐up has been uneventful. She is now a thriving three‐year‐old toddler, achieving all developmental milestones. As per protocol, follow‐up will continue with abdominal ultrasound until she reaches 7 years of age and AFP testing until the age of four.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Based on imaging, biochemical results, and clinical features, the infant was diagnosed with BWS and bilateral neuroblastoma. A conservative approach with regular clinical and imaging follow‐up was adopted. Over time, imaging showed complete regression of adrenal masses and normalization of urine catecholamines. Screening for Wilms' tumor and hepatoblastoma remained negative.

She developed adenotonsillar hypertrophy, causing obstructive sleep apnea, which was successfully managed surgically. Currently, the child is 3 years old, thriving, and meeting developmental milestones. Surveillance will continue until age seven with abdominal ultrasounds and AFP testing until age 4.

Discussion

Beckwith‐Wiedemann Syndrome (BWS) is the most common congenital overgrowth disorder in infancy, with an estimated incidence of approximately 1 in 10,000 live births. It is characterized by macroglossia, somatic and lateralized overgrowth, hyperinsulinism, abdominal wall defects, and a predisposition to cancer. Additional minor or less common features may also be present. BWS is considered a clinical spectrum, with affected individuals exhibiting a variable presentation, ranging from multiple hallmark features to only one or two characteristic manifestations [1, 3, 4, 5].

Children with Beckwith‐Wiedemann syndrome (BWS) and isolated hemihypertrophy have an increased risk of developing certain cancers during childhood. The overall cancer risk is estimated at 5%–10%, though it may vary depending on the specific genetic cause of the condition. Mortality associated with neoplasia is particularly linked to Wilms' tumor and hepatoblastoma, but neuroblastoma, adrenocortical carcinoma, and rhabdomyosarcoma are also observed. Additionally, a wide range of both malignant and benign tumors has been reported. The heightened risk of neoplasia is most pronounced during the first 8 years of life, gradually declining thereafter [1, 5].

Initial and ongoing surveillance for embryonal tumors such as Wilms' tumor, hepatoblastoma, and neuroblastoma is essential. The recommended follow‐up screening includes abdominal ultrasound every 3 months for the first 8 years and alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP) testing for the first 4 months. If a tumor develops, treatment is determined based on the tumor type and disease stage.

In this case, initial screening identified bilateral adrenal masses, though histologic confirmation was not feasible. However, elevated urine catecholamines and imaging findings strongly suggested bilateral neuroblastoma. Given the patient's age, absence of metastasis, and lack of emergent complications, an expectant management approach was adopted, incorporating strict follow‐up with clinical evaluations, imaging, and metabolic panel assessments, alongside surveillance for other malignancies per established recommendations.

Currently, the patient shows no evidence of adrenal masses, and urine catecholamine levels have normalized. A high index of suspicion, thorough screening, and further evaluation remain crucial for children with Beckwith‐Wiedemann syndrome and other overgrowth syndromes, given their increased tumor predisposition.
