A critical realist exploration of hospital staff experiences of using nutrition dashboard technology
Authors: Erin Fisher, Leanne Brown, Kerith Duncanson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Nutrition Dashboard is a validated nutrition informatics tool to display hospitalised patient food intake data. Despite demonstrated potential for malnutrition identification, research regarding the conditions required for successful implementation of the Nutrition Dashboard is lacking. This study explores dietitians’ and food service staff’s experiences with the Nutrition Dashboard to understand contextual factors and mechanisms shaping its use.MethodsUsing critical realism methodology, semi-structured interviews and focus groups were conducted with 17 dietitians and seven food service staff across nine hospitals in New South Wales, Australia. Data were thematically analysed before retroductive reasoning to generate Context-Mechanism-Outcome (CMO) statements that explore Nutrition Dashboard use.ResultsFive themes and CMO statements were evident: 1) Visibility and recognition: when food service staff see their data influence patient care, their increased engagement and data accuracy contributes to professional efficacy. 2) Partners in nutrition care: valued partnerships between food service staff and dietitians are enabled through communication and recognition, strengthening collaboration and nutrition care. 3) Education makes data meaningful: training on dashboard functionality and clinical value improves data accuracy and use. 4) Optimising data and systems: Nutrition Dashboard data has potential to enable efficient, technology-supported nutrition care through improved workflows and automation. 5) Proactive nutrition care: nutrition risk surveillance through Nutrition Dashboard data offers potential for objective, proactive and targeted care and improved nutrition outcomes.ConclusionThe Nutrition Dashboard’s value and acceptability depend on more than technical functionality and accuracy. Relational mechanisms, meaningful training and workplace structures and supports are essential to effective implementation, especially when the technology spans professional boundaries. Genuine partnership between food service staff and dietitians, including mutual respect and communication are identified key factors contributing to acceptability of the Nutrition Dashboard. These findings offer actionable recommendations for health services implementing nutrition informatics technology, such as the Nutrition Dashboard.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14738-1.Keywords: Personnel, hospital; Malnutrition; Medical informatics; Interprofessional relations; Nutrition assessment; Hospital information systems; Data visualisation

Background

The exponentially increasing digitisation of healthcare means that extensive amounts of clinical data are now routinely collected and made accessible. This data is often patient-centred, comprehensive, and readily available; however, available data does not always equal actioned data [1]. Health informatics research shows that health data can be utilised to inform and improve care; it also demonstrates an established implementation gap, with guidelines on best practices for presenting, accessing, and using data still lacking [2]. Health informatics research also explores barriers and enablers that impact the meaningful use of healthcare data in practice.

The Nutrition Dashboard is an example of a nutrition informatics tool; it is available at 47 hospitals in New South Wales (NSW), Australia [3]. It was developed in collaboration with HealthShare and NSW Health dietitians and first implemented in 2017 [3]. The Nutrition Dashboard presents extensive food ordering and intake data for patients admitted to hospital for three or more days. The intake data displayed is collected by trained food service staff using the CBORD™ Mobile Intake application (supplementary file 1). Staff visually estimate patient intake at the time of tray collection, using a five-point scale for each ordered food item, in a process validated as accurate and time-efficient [4]. The data is integrated with the CBORD™ food service suite software, intake data is converted to nutrient values (including kilojoules and protein) and visualised in the Nutrition Dashboard (Fig. 1). The Nutrition Dashboard categorises nutrition risk using user-set thresholds, it also presents ordering and intake trends that inform dietetic management for individualised patient care (Supplementary file 2).

Graphic representation of data collection process and presentation for the Nutrition Dashboard

Nutrition support provided by dietitians in hospital settings may be enhanced through the use of the Nutrition Dashboard. Previous research has demonstrated the Nutrition Dashboard’s potential to contribute to the identification of hospital malnutrition [3, 5], a prevalent, costly, and burdensome health condition [6]. While these findings support the Nutrition Dashboard’s technical accuracy, research demonstrates that the technical performance of health technology does not automatically translate to meaningful use in practice [1]. The implementation of health technology in hospital settings is shaped by organisational, social, and professional factors, including how different user groups experience and incorporate the technology into their practice [7, 8]. This research is relevant to the Nutrition Dashboard as it operates across two distinct professional groups: food service staff who collect patient intake data and dietitians who can access it to inform clinical care. A 2017 study exploring staff experiences of hospital nutrition technology has highlighted the importance of understanding how different professional groups perceive and engage with shared technology systems [9]. To date, no research has explored the experiences of staff using the Nutrition Dashboard, including the conditions that shape its use across the food service and dietetic workforce, or the factors that contribute to its value and sustainability in practice.

This study aimed to examine the contextual factors and mechanisms that shape outcomes associated with the use of the Nutrition Dashboard. By investigating what works for whom and under what circumstances, we have developed Context- Mechanism-Outcome (CMO) statements and subsequent recommendations to inform the future implementation and use of the Nutrition Dashboard.

Methods

Theoretical design

This qualitative study was informed by critical realism, which provides a structured approach to understanding the interplay between individual experiences and the systemic conditions that shape them [10]. Critical realism distinguishes between what is experienced, what occurs, and the underlying mechanisms that generate events. This methodological lens was applied to identify contextual factors and mechanisms that shape the use and acceptability of the Nutrition Dashboard. The context mechanism outcome (CMO) statement configurations were used to link contextual factors to the mechanisms they initiate and the outcomes they produce. Exploration of contextual factors and generative mechanisms enables identification of what works in what contexts; it acknowledges that successful implementation is shaped by circumstances [11]. Within the hospital context, this lens recognises that individual agency and structural conditions influence how dietitians and food service staff engage with the Nutrition Dashboard and with each other [12]. This methodological approach contributes to practical recommendations that acknowledge broader causal factors.

Recruitment

Ethics approval was obtained (Hunter New England Research Ethics Committee ETH13100) to undertake online focus groups or semi-structured individual interviews with food service staff experienced in collecting intake data for the Nutrition Dashboard and dietitians who have used the Nutrition Dashboard in the NSW Health’s Central Coast and Hunter New England Local Health Districts. Participants were recruited via convenience and snowball sampling. The researchers initially contacted dietitians and food service staff managers who distributed approved invitation emails via existing distribution lists of all staff members. All potential participants had equal opportunity to participate. Recruitment aimed to capture participants from various acute care settings in metropolitan, regional, and remote locations. This approach sought to explore a range of contextual factors, including variations in hospital size, location, length of dashboard use, and local processes and procedures regarding the implementation of the Nutrition Dashboard. Interested participants received information statements and consent forms. Study participants provided written consent to participate and were provided the opportunity to choose whether to participate in a focus group or an interview. Because of the highly structured nature of their workday, food service staff received a $50 gift card after their interview or focus group to compensate for their time, whereas dietitians could complete the interviews within their work hours.

Data collection

Once written consent was obtained, interviews and focus groups were scheduled at times suitable for participants and researchers. Focus groups and interviews were conducted online using Zoom™ videoconferencing software between November 2024 and June 2025, lasting approximately 30 min for interviews and 60 min for focus groups. Participants were informed that participation was voluntary and that responses would be anonymised. The sessions were guided by piloted question sets (Supplementary file 3). Experienced, female PhD-qualified qualitative researchers and dietitians (KD, LB) led the focus groups and interviews, with contributions and field note taking from EF (dietitian, PhD candidate), only study authors and participants were present. Facilitators shared their backgrounds and the study rationale before commencing. All sessions were recorded online and stored locally on a password protected drive. Audio recordings were sent to a confidential transcription service via a secure transfer. The audio files were transcribed verbatim by a professional service; transcripts were checked for accuracy and de-identified by the PhD candidate (EF). Field notes captured observations, non-verbal cues, and initial analytic reflections, which were considered when analysing and interpreting data. Recruitment, data collection and ongoing preliminary analysis were guided by the concept of information power [13]. Participants had direct experience with the Nutrition Dashboard, and the analysis was structured around CMO statements which supported the information power of the sample. Dietitians were recruited from nine hospitals (representing three health districts), and food service staff were recruited from two hospitals (representing two health districts). This variation reflected the operational constraints of accessing the food service workforce within participating sites.

Data analysis

Data analysis was completed in four stages, as detailed below. Microsoft Word and Excel were used for analysis, supported by an annotated data codebook that recorded codes, definitions, analytic decisions and an audit trail across all stages.

Familiarisation and initial coding: Research team members familiarised themselves with focus group/interview data through repeated reading of transcripts with noting of initial ideas. Key quotes were copied verbatim to a spreadsheet, each quote was assigned an initial descriptive code by EF, with explanatory notes added for each code in the codebook. A second researcher (KD) reviewed the data and confirmed or suggested alternative codes.

Regular meetings were held in which research team members compared independent coding, discussed alternative interpretations, and reached agreement on the final themes through collaborative discussion, with reference to the codebook and audit trail.

Rigour and reflexivity

Reflexivity involves the researcher critically examining how their positioning, assumptions, and prior experiences may influence the research process and interpretation of data [14]. Within a critical realist framework, reflexivity acknowledges that while mechanisms and structures exist independently of the researcher, our understanding of them is always mediated through the researcher’s own perspective and experience [15]. Several strategies were applied to ensure the trustworthiness of this study. The interview and focus group guides (Supplementary file 3) included planned prompts to share findings from previous quantitative studies with the participants as a form of analytical triangulation [16], their responses to past findings contributed to theme and CMO statement development. Data analysis was iterative, with preliminary findings explored after each interview and focus group. These preliminary findings were paraphrased and shared in subsequent focus groups to expand on the emerging data and to test and refine the developing analysis.

Each researcher who independently engaged with the data had varying levels of experience with the Nutrition Dashboard. One clinician-researcher (EF) had used the technology in clinical practice and therefore brought insider knowledge and potential assumptions about its value and use. Two experienced qualitative researchers (KD & LB) had no practical experience with the dashboard (and therefore distance from assumptions around use) but had familiarity with the technology through previously conducted quantitative research on the Nutrition Dashboard. A reflexivity journal was maintained by EF. It acknowledged and explored underlying assumptions regarding the dashboard as a clinician researcher, the journal included initial responses to data and emerging themes. This journalling allowed for informed and reflexive discussion regarding interpretations of data amongst the research team.

The study has been reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (Supplementary file 4).

Results

Seventeen dietitians and seven food service staff consented and participated. Five focus groups were conducted with two to five participants in each; 16 dietitians and two food service staff participated in focus groups. Four food service staff participated in interviews. One participant who has worked with the Nutrition Dashboard as a dietitian (current role - Dietitian 9) and as a food service staff member (previous role – Food Service 1) participated in an interview. Participants worked across nine hospitals (seven metropolitan, two regional/rural). Twenty-three participants were female, and one dietitian was male. Food service staff and dietitian participants had varying levels of experience with data collection and the Nutrition Dashboard, ranging from one to more than five years.

Five themes were evident and are explored below.

Theme 1) Visibility and recognition

Both professions discussed food service staff’s work as invisible in the clinical context.

I didn’t talk very much to non-food services staff. We tended to be pretty invisible to everyone except the patients, was my experience. Food Service 7

Data indicated that employment structures and perceived hierarchy contributed to food service staff feeling peripheral to the hospital system.

I guess sometimes they (food service staff) underestimate what they do. And some of that will come from some of the feedback they’re getting from upstairs (clinical staff). Food Service 5

Despite this, participants reported that food service staff interacted with patients and were knowledgeable about their food preferences and intake, and were highly visible to patients, who valued their input:

I’ve really seen that the food service staff, they know the patients Dietitian 4

Food service staff expressed concern for patients, especially when they noticed poor intake, but reported varying levels of confidence in communicating these concerns to clinical staff. Food service staff discussed being unaware of how the dashboard could influence patient care. When its potential uses were explained during the interviews, they reported feeling more motivated, understanding its significance, and feeling that they were contributing to the hospital’s overall culture of patient care. This response suggests that awareness of the Nutrition Dashboard’s clinical purpose may function as an activating condition for engagement for food service staff.

It is a good tool…you get to see what the patient’s eating and whether or not they’re getting better or getting worse, because food is almost as important as medication to get a patient better. Food Service 5

CMO 1 describes the relationship between food service staff recognition, work satisfaction, and data accuracy:

ContextMechanismOutcomeFood service staff work closely with patients: they observe and communicate concerns for patients; feel structurally invisible in hospital systems and experience low agency in patient care.Seeing their data collection directly influence patient care decisions and outcomes through the Nutrition Dashboard enhances motivation and sense of professional efficacyThis was perceived to enhance engagement in data collection and therefore accuracy, as well as improved professional identity and job satisfaction

Theme 2) Partners in nutrition care

Dietitian participants communicated the importance and value of partnership between food service staff and dietitians in providing and optimising nutrition care:

The role of food service staff in the hospital, I think they’re like pivotal in the supply and also the engagement of patients Dietitian 4

Whilst food service staff acknowledge and value this partnership, they expressed varying levels of active recognition of this:

I mean we (food service) can interact with them (dietitians) on the floor like ask them questions or tell them what’s going on but I mean they’re really short-staffed so half the time we don’t see them. Food Service 4

Participants commented that whilst the groups work together in many aspects their priorities diverge:

from a kitchen point of view getting the food out on time is probably a higher priority than what’s actually being consumed. I think it’s competing priorities. Dietitian 14

Divergent priorities between employment structures were explored as a complexity to navigate in building and securing collaborative working relationships:

I think while the staff collecting that data report through a different organisational structure, which is not a clinical structure, it’s going to be hard to implement lasting change where that data is being used for clinical care. Dietitian 16

Mechanisms such as mutual respect, role recognition, communication, and collaboration were emphasised as key factors in motivating and fostering genuine, enacted partnership rather than just a perceived one:

Because we had such a good relationship with our food service site manager and then the day-to-day staff, we were able to actually provide a better outcome for the patients, that wouldn’t have happened otherwise. Dietitian 4

CMO statement 2 synthesises experiences of dietitians and food service staff, and underlying mechanisms that influence individual agency and structural conditions in the context of partnership:

ContextMechanismOutcomeWhen dietitians and food service staff engage as valued partnersthrough communication and recognitiontheir collaboration strengthens and nutrition care improves

Theme 3) Education makes data meaningful

Both groups reported limited understanding of the Nutrition Dashboard’s functionality. Despite being responsible for data collection, none of the participating food service staff reported that they had seen a demonstration or explanation of its clinical application:

“I don’t think anyone knew what the point was, what the value was. They were just like this is the thing we have to do” Dietitian 9/Food Service Staff 1 (participant works as a dietitian currently at a site with access to the Nutrition Dashboard and has previously worked with the Nutrition Dashboard as a food service staff member)

Limited promotion of and education about the dashboard was common across both professions. Dietitians reported little or no training on dashboard use. Food service staff described receiving varying levels of training on data collection for the Nutrition Dashboard. Food service staff who were employed when the Nutrition Dashboard was initiated reported having more extensive training, while others described ‘shadowing’ colleagues as their training:

When My Food Choice came into the site, all staff who were here at the time were trained. There was a team that came in and trained everyone. And then it really becomes like a train the trainer. Food Service 2

Dietitians consistently highlighted that training in and promotion of the Nutrition Dashboard were limited, creating barriers to uptake.

I didn’t have much of an understanding of how to use it or even the benefits that it would have in terms of helping the patients. Dietitian 8

Food service staff reported confidence in their ability to collect the data presented in the dashboard; however, they expressed concerns that dietitians were not reviewing or acting on the data.

I’m quite happy to do it like I said as long as people actually use that data. Food Service 4

Both professions emphasised the importance of the data being meaningful and used for patient care. This was discussed as a motivator for food service staff to collect it accurately.

I do feel like if food service staff were to understand the importance of it and how that information really does help, maybe the data collection would be better, but I think the culture was, we’ve just got to do this thing Dietitian 9 and past Food Service 1

Communication, collaboration, and information sharing among food service staff and dietitians were considered essential for effective education and training, and for the ultimate acceptance of the Nutrition Dashboard. Analysis of the data found that a lack of consistent education and promotion acts as a barrier to the engagement and motivation described in CMO 1. Our findings suggest that food service staff understanding the clinical application of data collection contributes to improved engagement, motivation and therefore accuracy, resulting in the generation of CMO statement 3:

ContextMechanismOutcomeWhen hospital food service staff and dietitians who are unfamiliar with the purpose and capabilities of the Nutrition Dashboardreceive consistent education about the dashboard’s functionality and clinical value, and training to optimally and efficiently use it (including shared responsibility and meaningful data collection)participants anticipated improved data accuracy, greater engagement in data collection, and increased uptake and acceptability of the Nutrition Dashboard by all users

Theme 4) Optimising data and systems

Dietitian participants discussed the Nutrition Dashboard’s data collection process, commenting on its strengths over traditional food charts. They discussed the value of capturing intake immediately after meals by food service staff familiar with menus, with automated macronutrient conversion:

The food service staff, they are collecting the tray, and it is part of the process to enter it into the iPad, which it just makes so much more sense to me Dietitian 11

However, data accuracy emerged as a critical determinant of the dashboard’s perceived value.

I think if the data is collected correctly, it’s so good, but if it’s not, it’s worthless Dietitian 1

Participants discussed that a lack of system integration acts as a technical barrier to its use.

It is a little bit clunky Dietitian 9 (and past Food Service staff member 1)

Despite these barriers, participants spoke of system enhancements to create a tool that is valued by clinicians with real-world benefit. Participants also speculated about future possibilities including machine learning applications, triage capabilities, and the automation of processes to reduce avoidable workload on dietitians.

there could be some sort of automatic way that this tool puts something very generic and safe into place for the patients that might be more effective for them based off the data that that tool has collected…that could be a way that it could be used to save us time and be effective. Dietitian 5

CMO statement four therefore reflects the potential capability for Nutrition Dashboard to be optimised, resulting in improved workflows, with noticeable, immediate improvements to patient outcomes possible:

ContextMechanismOutcomeThe Nutrition Dashboard presents comprehensive nutrition data but relies on time constrained dietitians to action this dataThrough integration with clinical workflows and automated translation of intake data into actionable nutrition supportNutrition care becomes more efficient and timely through technology-enabled decision support, improving patient outcomes

Theme 5) Proactive malnutrition care

Dietitians and food service staff discussed the limitations of hospital settings in meeting nutrition needs and preventing poor patient outcomes. Current malnutrition screening and referral practice were described as inaccurate and incomplete. All participants discussed the potential benefits of the Nutrition Dashboard for nutrition surveillance:

Having a tool like this that could allow you to intervene sooner or allow you to intervene more effectively…it could be the difference between someone developing hospital acquired malnutrition or not Dietitian 5

The benefits of the dashboard in providing dietitians with objective nutrition data were discussed:

it wouldn’t discriminate, it would be collecting that data on every person, whereas potentially in the hospital system, people assume that someone’s nutrition is fine because of the way they look Dietitian 6

Dietitians based in metropolitan centres discussed how the Nutrition Dashboard has been used effectively to triage referrals and provide proactive care.

It was quite a helpful triaging tool at times because we never get to our whole caseload each day. We were able to see, or at least have some idea of how patients were eating and drinking throughout the day Dietitian 14

However, this proactive nutrition care theme was contrasted with system limitations that exist within hospitals, including a stretched food service and dietetics workforce.

it’s a double-edged sword. What happens if you see someone who’s not eating well at all that hasn’t been referred so it can help you with the workload you know about but might also uncover a workload that you don’t know about. Dietitian 1

I don’t think there’s any acute facility in New South Wales that’d have enough staff to be able to go and proactively follow every patient up that looked like potentially they should be seen. Dietitian 3

Identifying and caring for clients who “slip through the cracks” (Dietitian 6) was discussed, the Nutrition Dashboard was identified as providing information to address this in an ideal scenario. CMO 5 explores this aspiration:

ContextMechanismOutcomeWhen incomplete screening, inadequate referrals, and insufficient staffing lead to poor identification of patients at risk of malnutritionfood service staff and dietitians envisage the potential for the Nutrition Dashboard to provide data for nutrition surveillance to identify nutrition risk and triage referrals objectivelyto provide proactive, targeted nutrition care

Discussion

Results from this study provide insights into the conditions, experiences, and attitudes that shape the use of the Nutrition Dashboard and offer actionable recommendations in the form of CMO statements.

From a critical realist perspective, structure, culture, and agency are stratified and relational. Structures and cultures precede individuals within an organisation but are reproduced or transformed through individual agency [17]. Our findings illustrate how these layers interact in the context of the use of the Nutrition Dashboard across nine Australian hospitals. Structurally, the dashboard was described as introduced to some participants and imposed on others. While it holds substantial potential to improve patient outcomes through nutrition risk screening [3, 5], this potential can best be realised if food service staff and dietitians understand and value its application.

Our findings reveal tensions around food service staff work; they are highly visible to patients but invisible within clinical systems due to perceived hierarchical structures. Star and Strauss (1999) describe this as creating a “non-person,” where the work itself is visible but the person performing it is rendered socially invisible [18]. This phenomenon is common in domestic and service work, where employers may closely oversee tasks while the workers themselves remain invisible. Although food service staff and dietitians in our study described food service staff as “really knowing patients,” this knowledge was largely invisible or under-recognised, consistent with research on other non-clinical health support roles [19]. This aligns with Star and Strauss’s observation that, in service work, the better it is performed, the less visible it becomes to those who benefit from it, resulting in essential work becoming background work [18]. Importantly, the knowledge of food service staff was identified as not being integrated into clinical care. Critical realist analysis (and CMO statement 1) identifies capacity for the Nutrition Dashboard to formalise food service staff’s patient-facing role and awareness of patient intake into a clinical system, thereby increasing their visibility and contribution and therefore their work satisfaction.

Realising this capacity depends on the partnership between food service staff and dietitians, who can serve as key advocates to increase food service staff visibility. This can be activated through communication, mutual respect and framing the value and contribution of data collection for the Nutrition Dashboard. Collaboration between food service staff and dietitians is recognised as important for effective hospital nutrition care [20], with recent research identifying dietitians and food service staff as key facilitators of nutrition improvement initiatives in hospital settings [21]. Communication between the professions is frequently reported as a key factor in nutrition outcomes such as improved meal accuracy, patient satisfaction, and intake [20, 22, 23]. Our findings expand on this by highlighting how relational mechanisms, such as mutual respect and recognition, strengthen the partnership between food service staff and dietitians, ultimately influencing nutrition outcomes. Hospitals are complex settings with multiple institutional reasonings and priorities operating simultaneously within and across professional and role boundaries [24]. CMO statement 2 acknowledges the interplay between differing priorities among food service staff and dietitians. Operational priorities (timely, safe delivery of meals) and clinical priorities (optimising nutrition outcomes) create competing demands that shape perceptions of the Nutrition Dashboard’s value. Although these structural constraints exist, results from this study demonstrate that mutual respect and regular communication help to foster a genuine nutrition partnership between food service staff and dietitians, bridging the divide and improving nutrition care.

Where visibility and partnership provide the structural and relational foundations, the culture surrounding data collection shapes whether the Nutrition Dashboard is actively used. Our findings indicate that a culture amongst food service staff that views dashboard data collection as either not valuable or an inconsequential task, results in a lack of engagement from both food service staff and dietitians, who view the data and therefore the Nutrition Dashboard as unreliable. The agency of both roles in valuing the Nutrition Dashboard appears to be activated when the dashboard’s purpose is explicit and meaningful, that is, when staff understand its impact on patient care. This is supported by a workplace culture that provides adequate time and training for data collection and access. Findings from this study highlight that CMO 1 and CMO 3 are interconnected. The outcomes of engagement, professional identity, and work satisfaction described in CMO 1 were discussed as anticipated. CMO 3 identifies meaningful education as a mechanism with the potential to activate these outcomes by generating understanding, accurate data collection via engagement.

Uneven training is a major barrier to the adoption and acceptance of healthcare technology [2], including technology that spans multiple professional groups [25]. Our analysis identified that effective training regarding health technology such as the Nutrition Dashboard must include two components: first, the development of practical skills and guidelines for accurately collecting, accessing and implementing nutrition data; and second, and perhaps more importantly, communication that conveys the meaningfulness of the technology for patient care outcomes. With modest investment of resources in training for food service staff and dietitians, there is potential for the Nutrition Dashboard to become a valuable data management tool that improves hospital nutrition care and outcomes. Whilst initial implementation strategies for many health interventions provide supports and structures (such as training and leadership) they can become unstable or diminished as time goes on, thus weakening the outcomes of the intervention [26]. Our study found that whilst training for food service staff was strong initially, it changed over time, becoming less structured and supported. This highlights the importance of sustainability when implementing healthcare interventions, including those that utilise technology such as the Nutrition Dashboard. Protocols, education, and training around interventions, including the Nutrition Dashboard must be embedded and ongoing to ensure interventions and potential outcomes are sustainable.

If progress towards the outcomes from CMOs 1, 2 and 3 can be made, visibility, partnership, and meaningful education, it is feasible that the broader potential of the Nutrition Dashboard could be achieved. Theme four explored this potential: the capacity for healthcare data to improve care through automation. Big data has the potential to transform healthcare; however, data accuracy and impact are essential to the value and acceptability of data-driven care [2]. McCoy et al. [27] demonstrate successful implementation of digital technology through a protocolised malnutrition care model, which utilises the same mobile intake technology as the Nutrition Dashboard.

Strategies to enhance data collection and accuracy, including education, protocolised processes, and motivation through an understanding of value, have been identified and discussed above. Participants discussed barriers and enablers to the Nutrition Dashboard’s use in clinical practice, including future enhancements and concepts for dashboard technologies that contribute to safe, effective, and timely nutrition interventions. Participants commented on the importance of the Nutrition Dashboard providing real value, rather than being “another piece of information” to include or access. This concept is supported by a 2025 study which found that healthcare professionals’ use of dashboard data can be impeded by ‘information overload’ [28]. The importance of actionable data is a consistent finding in healthcare dashboard research [28].

Malnutrition screening and referral guidelines have been widely implemented in hospitals for approximately 25 years [29]. Despite this, research indicates that malnutrition remains inadequately identified and managed in hospitals, resulting in significant adverse health outcomes [6, 30]. Our findings demonstrate that achieving the Nutrition Dashboard’s potential for proactive malnutrition care requires comprehensive implementation strategies, structural supports, and, importantly, adequate workforce capacity to engage with and actively use the technology. Time pressures emerged as barriers to proactive nutrition care across themes, with many participants identifying that nutrition stakeholders, including food service staff, nurses, dietitians face increasing workloads. Participants spoke of wanting to care for patients’ nutritional status proactively. Within the context of staffing constraints, the potential for optimised malnutrition surveillance was discussed as both promising and problematic. Concerns regarding an increased dietetics workload created through increased identification of patients at risk of malnutrition were raised as a significant barrier to implementation, alongside increased workload on food service staff collecting intake data. Existing literature identifies a marked increase in dietetics referrals associated with the digitisation of screening processes [31]. These findings suggest that further investment in the comprehensive and sustainable implementation of the Nutrition Dashboard, including partnership between food service staff and dietitians, supports and training pose a novel strategy to address malnutrition care gaps that currently exist in the hospital setting.

This study utilises qualitative data from dietitians and food service staff to describe the contextual conditions and mechanisms shaping Nutrition Dashboard use and acceptability. The study focusses on user experiences and perspectives to provide actionable recommendations (Table 1) for the implementation of hospital nutrition informatics technology which acknowledge the integral role of the health staff behind the data. The study highlights that technology is only effective when its users are capable, supported, and valued.

Strengths and limitations

The study was strengthened by diverse hospital dietitian representation from regional and metropolitan areas and the interprofessional perspectives gained from both clinicians and support staff. One participant brought experiences as both a former food service staff member and a current dietitian, providing valuable insights into both roles and connections that might not otherwise have been possible. The use of focus groups for most dietitian participants and individual interviews for most food service staff is acknowledged as a methodological difference that may have influenced the depth or nature of responses gathered from each group. This approach was taken to meet the needs of participants, acknowledging the differing structures of their work. The two food service staff who participated in a focus group reported experiences consistent with those who were interviewed individually. Despite focussed recruitment strategies to broaden participation, food service participants were representative of only one regional and one metropolitan hospital, however they were located in two separate health districts across NSW Health. Despite this limitation, food service staff members reported consistent experiences and attitudes regarding data collection for the Nutrition Dashboard, as well as thoughts on its application.

CMO statements 1 and 3 are informed primarily by food service staff perspectives, drawn from two hospital sites across two health districts. The contextual factors influencing visibility, recognition, and education in these settings may differ from those in other hospitals. Consistent with critical realist evaluation [11], these CMO statements represent initial programme theories that require testing across diverse sites and food service contexts.

Conclusion

The Nutrition Dashboard is a previously validated [3, 5] nutrition informatics tool with unrealised potential. This study found that the Nutrition Dashboard’s acceptability and application are shaped by relational, cultural and structural mechanisms, not accuracy or technical functionality alone. The findings recommend that the food service staff’s role in Nutrition Dashboard data collection and the broader hospital nutrition care system should be more visible and valued. The study also identifies shared ownership of the Nutrition Dashboard by an enacted professional partnership between dietitians and food service staff as contributing to its successful implementation, particularly in navigating competing operational and clinical priorities. The study suggests that future education and training strategies should be implemented across both professional groups and focus on the clinical potential of the Nutrition Dashboard for both food service staff and dietitians to improve staff engagement. These strategies all contribute to the Nutrition Dashboard’s ability to support proactive nutrition care. Future research is warranted to examine how addressing the contextual and mechanistic factors identified in this study shapes staff acceptance of the Nutrition Dashboard and its effects on patient care outcomes.
