Microbial interaction networks as climate thermometers: redefining temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy in freshwater ecosystems
Authors: Qiong Tang, Lunhui Lu, Yan Xiao, Dianchang Wang, Zhe Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Methane-oxidizing bacteria (MOB) serve as critical biogeochemical gatekeepers, mitigating methane (CH4) emissions from freshwaters through aerobic methanotrophy. However, their global biogeography, interaction networks, and thermal responsiveness are poorly understood. We synthesize global patterns revealing a latitudinal succession, with Methylococcaceae dominating in tropical and temperate regions, Beijerinckiaceae prevailing in the polar region, and temperate region exhibiting the highest richness (10.7 ± 0.32) and Shannon diversity (1.51 ± 0.02). Climate-driven interactions show tightly intertwined MOB-associated subnetworks in tropical and polar regions, thereby amplifying the temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy (0.24 and 0.16 μmol·L-1·d-1·°C-1), while in the temperate region, the subnetworks are sparser and show low temperature sensitivity. Crucially, our results reveal that nontrophic interactions, rather than taxonomic diversity, drive the temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy. These insights position MOB interactions as predictive bioindicators of CH4 flux under warming, improving climate models and guiding strategies for emission mitigation.

Introduction

Freshwater ecosystems represent Earth’s most significant natural methane (CH4) source (117–212 TgCH4·yr−1), surpassing wetland emissions documented in the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report1. CH4 flux is highly sensitive to climate warming, as temperature-driven acceleration of methanogenesis creates a concerning positive feedback loop2,3. Aerobic methanotrophy—the biological oxidation of CH4 to CO2 at the oxic-anoxic interfaces—serves as Earth’s ultimate biogeochemical barrier against aquatic CH4 release, mitigating 10–90% of potential emissions before atmospheric evasion4. While this microbial process provides a critical climate safety valve5, its temperature sensitivity remains inadequately defined across geographic gradients. Current understanding is disproportionately derived from forest soils6 and thermokarst lakes7, leading to a significant knowledge gap given the disproportionate warming rates and CH4 stock vulnerability of freshwater ecosystems.

Methane-oxidizing bacteria (MOB) are specialized bacteria that primarily use CH4 as their carbon and energy source under oxic conditions. They initiate the oxidation process by employing particulate or soluble methane monooxygenases (pMMO/sMMO), which are enzymes encoded by the (pmoCAB/mmoBCDXYZ) gene clusters8. Traditionally, MOB were viewed as obligate organisms that relied exclusively on CH4. However, facultative MOB—capable of utilizing multi-carbon compounds or hydrogen—emerged through horizontal gene transfer (HGT) events that acquired membrane transporter genes (facilitator transporters, periplasmic binding proteins) encoding specific substrate transporters (e.g., acetate, or other organic acids). This expanded their portfolio of carbon and energy sources, thereby increasing their metabolic flexibility9. Different types of MOB are differentiated based on their physiology, biochemistry, and morphology8,10,11, which underpin divergent eco-physiological strategies. The intracytoplasmic membrane of type I MOB is uniformly arranged and distributed in the cytoplasm of the cell in bundles, whereas the membrane of type II MOB is arranged in a less orderly manner, and the intracytoplasmic membrane is distributed in the periphery of the cell wall. Type II MOB (Methylocella, Methylocapsa, Methylocystis) expanded their substrate range, transitioning from obligate to facultative lifestyles9,12–14. The evolution of MOB is closely linked to that of MOB enzymes, and their evolutionary characteristics were presented in the Supplementary Note 1. MOB primarily belong to Pseudomonadota (including type I and type II MOB), Verrucomicrobiota, and Candidatus Methylomirabilota, and their phylogenetic lineages have been extensively reported15–17. They exhibit divergent metabolic adaptations, such as carbon assimilation pathways, membrane structure, and substrate utilization, which collectively constitute metabolic versatility that governs their role in the global CH4 budget8. This versatility is exemplified by methanotrophs from extreme habitats, such as Verrucomicrobia methanotrophs isolated from some geothermal areas18, and by organisms employing unique mechanisms, such as Candidatus Methylomirabilota (mainly the Methylomirabilis genus), an anaerobe17 that performs aerobic methanotrophy by producing O2 through the dismutation of nitric oxide (NO) and using it to oxidize CH419. Recent discoveries have significantly expanded the known taxonomic diversity of MOB. Novel species, such as Mycobacterium (Actinobacteria) from acidic cave biofilms20, Methylovirgula thiovorans HY1 capable of sulfur-driven growth21, and the thermoacidophilic Methylacidiphilum fumariolicum SolV, which utilizes H2S as an energy source22, demonstrated their adaptability to extreme niches and non-traditional substrates, redefining the eco-physiology boundaries of MOB in global ecosystems.

Type I MOB, which utilize the ribulose monophosphate (RuMP) pathway, are often adapted for rapid growth under high resource availability but can be susceptible to environmental stress. Type II MOB employ the serine cycle; they exhibit high resilience and an ability to survive in low-resource conditions (e.g., surface soils)8,10,23–25. Metabolic strategies of MOB can also reflect by niche-specific behaviors, optimizing resource utilization under fluctuating conditions26. For instance, taxon shifts from Methylococcales (type I MOB) in methane-deficient conditions, further to Candidatus Methylomirabilis (originally called NC10 phylum) abundantly populated in anoxic zones, illustrating how distinct metabolic guilds occupy specific niches along the O2 - CH4 gradient. Kinetic analysis of aerobic methanotrophy can reveal temperature-driven adaptations of MOB substrate affinity27. CH4 half-saturation constant (Km), the concentration of CH4 at which half the maximum CH4 oxidation rate is reached, is highly variable27. The specific affinity a0s (the slope of the linear part of the Michaelis-Menten curve) was also regarded a more appropriate parameter for comparing CH4 oxidation rate between MOB types at low CH4 concentrations28,29. Kinetic analysis revealed that type II MOB possess a higher affinity for CH4 than type I MOB, and that this substrate affinity exhibits a discernible relationship with temperature (Supplementary Note 2; Supplementary Fig. 1, 2; Supplementary Table 1). Despite their consistently low relative abundance in freshwater systems (e.g., constituting < 1% of the total bacterial community based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing), MOB can maintain a high methane oxidation efficiency30,31. This paradox, where functional influence is decoupled from numerical dominance, can be explained by their resilient cross-feeding networks and functional redundancy among MOB taxa, which collectively stabilize the aerobic methanotrophy32–34. However, current research places disproportionate emphasis on local MOB diversity, composition or enzymatic kinetic rather than the ecological interactions that elucidate climate responsiveness.

Our research argues that traditional models focused on the enzymatic kinetics of aerobic methanotrophy do not adequately represent ecosystem-scale responses of aerobic methanotrophy, necessitating a shift to frameworks driven by interaction networks for understanding temperature sensitivity. It seeks to bridge this critical gap by examining four underexplored dimensions: (ⅰ) Latitudinal succession distribution patterns of MOB; (ⅱ) Distribution of aerobic methane oxidation rate (MOX) and its temperature sensitivity across geographical zones; and (ⅲ) Microbial interaction perspectives on temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy. The above three points, together with their corresponding methodology, data used and figure presentation, were detailed in the Supplementary Table 2. This mainly includes: (1) meta-analysis of MOB distribution in global freshwater ecosystems; (2) a comprehensive literature survey of methane oxidation rates across global freshwater ecosystems; and (3) microbial interaction (especially MOB interaction) using co-occurrence network analysis. Our synthesis establishes the nontrophic interaction of MOB as master regulators of CH4-climate feedback.

Results

Global biogeography of MOB in freshwaters

A meta-analysis of global literature survey revealed that the distribution and diversity of MOB exhibit clear latitudinal gradients, with variations across different geographic zones (Fig. 1A, B). Tropical region hosts the most abundant MOB populations (1.48% of total ASVs, n = 478), followed by temperate (0.95%, n = 2820) and polar regions (0.14%, n = 96). MOB diversity metrics showed a slightly different hierarchy: temperate region exhibits the highest mean richness (10.7 ± 0.32) and Shannon diversity (1.51 ± 0.02), exceeding tropical (9.61 ± 0.30; 1.16 ± 0.03) and polar regions (4.09 ± 0.31; 0.78 ± 0.06). This latitudinal pattern unfolds through climate-driven succession of MOB lineages: Methylococcaceae (type I) accounted for 5.95% of the total bacterial community in the tropical region and 8.08% in the temperate region (Fig. 1C). Further delineation showed that type Ib MOB accounted for 3.60% of the total bacterial community in the tropical region, whereas type Ia represented 4.67% in the temperate region. According to Ho et al.’s35 functional classification of MOB life strategies, type Ia (e.g., Methylobacter, Methylomonas, etc.) usually represents competitor (C) taxa optimized for resource acquisition, type Ib (e.g., Methylococcus, Methylocaldum) corresponds to ruderal (R) with metabolic plasticity, and type II aligns with stress-tolerator (S) strategists adapted to environmental extremes. The relative abundance of Methylocystaceae (type II) was lower in both tropical (4.42%) and temperate (6.67%) regions compared to type I MOB. Their dominance shifts rapidly in the polar region, where Beijerinckiaceae (type II) emerge as cold-adapted taxa (comprising 0.57% of the total bacterial community but a striking 58.9% of the total MOB ASVs), highlighting their role as the dominant MOB taxa thriving in environments with low temperatures36. Such a distribution pattern also applies to the interpretation of another type II, Methylobacteriaceae. Thus, type II MOB collectively become the dominant methanotrophic group (0.93% of the total bacterial community), outnumbering type I MOB (0.04%), reflecting their differential thermal adaptation across latitudes. This is consistent with the life at low energy paradigm, where MOB with higher substrate affinities, such as type II, are better suited to such conditions24,37. The global distribution pattern illuminates a fundamental ecological strategy (Fig. 1D): type I MOB’s “C” and “R” strategy versus type II MOB’s energy-efficient “S” tactics, whose adaptive outcomes are determined by both endogenous (e.g., enzyme properties) and exogenous factors. The global biogeographic patterns of MOB, especially the distribution characteristics in various habitats, were further conducted. Firstly, MOB is most frequently detected across sampling sites in the temperate region (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B), dominated by Methylococcaceae in freshwater and marine ecosystems, and followed by Beijerinckiaceae in freshwaters, which also had the highest MAG abundance in the temperate region (Supplementary Fig. 3C). This indicated that type I MOB is more inclined to survive in an aqueous environment compared to type II MOB. Broadly, type II MOB performed remarkable habitat plasticity, colonizing aquatic environments (freshwater and marine ecosystems), terrestrial ecosystems, and engineered environments (wastewater treatment plants) (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Notably, Methylococcaceae predominantly occupied aquatic ecosystems, especially for type Ia, while Beijerinckiaceae demonstrated broader habitat versatility across freshwater and wastewater treatment systems. This niche partitioning underscores a striking evolutionary divergence: Type I MOB has evolved as aquatic generalists, capitalizing on comfortable zone in temperate waters to dominate freshwater and marine ecosystems, while type II MOB adopts “S” strategy, thriving in low-resource conditions where CH4 ebbs and flows with environmental pulses. An unexpected observation was the near-ubiquitous distribution of Corynebacteriaceae across all examined ecosystems except plant-associated environments, suggesting undetermined ecological roles requiring further investigation. However, it is important to note that global sampling efforts remain uneven, and these biogeographical patterns should be interpreted within this context.

The functioning of aerobic methanotrophy in global freshwaters

The aerobic methane oxidation rate (MOX) of MOB is a critical metric for understanding CH4 dynamics in freshwater ecosystems. MOX in aquatic systems is primarily regulated by three key factors: CH4 concentration, dissolved oxygen (DO), and temperature38. Notably, MOX exhibits heightened sensitivity to CH4 concentration fluctuations compared to other variables38. Additionally, dissolved organic carbon (DOC) may indirectly modulate MOX by reducing DO levels in benthic zones39,40 or alleviating photoinhibition within the euphotic layer41,42. However, MOX activity varies markedly across various systems. While some research reported that oxidized CH4 accounts for nearly or even exceeding 100% of total CH4 production in certain systems39,43, others document negligible contributions ( < 1%) from aerobic methanotrophy44. Most published literature of MOX was focused on the temperate region (Fig. 2A). Comparative analysis of the collected data revealed that the mean MOX was highest in the tropical region (205.6 ± 199.2 μmol·L−1·d−1), intermediate in the temperate region (44.2 ± 31.8 μmol·L−1·d−1), and lowest in the polar region (3.37 ± 1.7 μmol·L−1·d−1). Overall, neither latitudinal nor environment trophic state had significant correlations with MOX (Fig. 2A). The activity of microbial metabolic processes is modulated by temperature. The temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy reflects the thermal response of MOX (defined as the change in MOX per °C, μmol·L−1·d−1·°C−1), which directly regulates the trajectory of CH4 emissions under climate change and serves as a key parameter of ecological importance for predicting carbon-climate feedbacks across geographic gradients. This metric exhibited distinct patterns across geographic zones, with regional estimates of the temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy being highest in the tropical region (0.24 ± 0.08), intermediate in the polar region (0.16 ± 0.07), and lowest in the temperate region (0.13 ± 0.03) (Fig. 2B). These patterns implied that aerobic methanotrophy in the tropical region exhibits stronger thermal dependence compared to temperate and polar regions. Members of the phylum Verrucomicrobiota exhibit thermophilic adaptability, and notably, Verrucomicrobiota serve as the sole keystone taxa in the tropical region (Supplementary Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table 3), likely due to their unique metabolic resilience and dominance in aerobic methanotrophy under persistently warm conditions46. The temperature sensitivity in the polar region ranks second, suggesting a moderate thermal adaptation of MOB communities under cold climatic conditions. Temperate region exhibited the lowest temperature sensitivity, allowing efficient aerobic methanotrophy with minimal metabolic cost. This functional performance is likely facilitated by the balanced abundance of type I and type II MOB (with a near-equal ratio of 1.37, Fig. 1C), which may enhance resistance to seasonal fluctuations. The variability in temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy highlights the role of climate in modulating CH4 dynamics.

The temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy shifts rooted in microbial interaction

MOB exhibit broad nontrophic interaction preferences in both natural and laboratory environments. The core strategies of these interactions include metabolic cross-feeding, alleviating the toxicity, signal communication, and changes in the microenvironment, etc. (Supplementary Fig. 5). In metabolic cross-feeding, MOB can release methanol (via enzyme-type switching, e.g., XoxF to MxaF) for non-methanotroph utilization47. This is exemplified by marine seep sediments where Methylococcaceae oxidize CH4 via pMMO, supplying methanol to methylotrophs like Methylophilaceae to sustain growth and detoxify intermediates48. Heterotrophs (e.g., rhizobia) enhance MOB activity by secreting cobalamin/vitamin B12 and degrading toxic byproducts (e.g., methanol, formaldehyde)10,49,50. Volatile organic compounds (VOCs, e.g., dimethylsulfide) act as infochemicals in the interactions between heterotrophs and MOB, regulating their growth and oxidation51. Labile organic matter variably impacts MOB lineages, such as Methylococcales and Candidatus Methylomirabilis52. Furthermore, in stratified lakes, interactions between MOB and photosynthetic algae are not only facilitated by in situ oxygen production, but also reinforced by the tight coupling of metabolite exchange or co-metabolism of carbon compounds53,54. These mechanisms of nontrophic interaction can shape the microbial community and enhance the functioning of aerobic methanotrophy51.

Nontrophic interactions among MOB, which govern microbial community dynamics, are systematically decoded through network topology analysis, uncovering their pivotal role in driving ecosystem functioning50. Distinct topological architectures of nontrophic interaction were explored through this method (Fig. 3). For whole network, temperate region exhibited exceptional topological architecture with 776 nodes interconnected through 16,801 edges, and characterized by the highest average degree, highest closeness centrality and shortest average path length (Figs. 3, 4A; Supplementary Table 4). A pronounced latitudinal gradient in microbial interaction was revealed, with temperate region showing the strongest cohesion, followed by the tropical and polar regions (Fig. 4D). Even though there was no significant difference in closeness centrality between the temperate and polar regions, the temperate region had a higher mean value (0.331) compared to the polar region (0.303). Additionally, the temperate region also contains the largest number of keystone taxa, with a total of 29 keystone taxa (8 modular hubs and 21 connectors) attributed to Pseudomonadota, Cyanobacteria, and Bacteroidetes (Supplementary Fig. 4; Supplementary Table 3). However, MOB-associated subnetworks exhibited contrasting characteristics among these three geographic zones. Average degree, closeness centrality and eigenvector centrality are key indicators of resource distribution, community stability and connectivity55. They were significantly higher in the MOB-associated subnetwork in the tropical and polar regions than in the temperate region, reflecting the tightly interconnected small-world topology of specialized CH4 oxidation in these two regions (Fig. 4A). Additionally, their short average path length further underscores rapid signal propagation, enabling swift ecosystem responses (Supplementary Table 4)56. These structural features align with biome-specific ecological strategy that prioritizes metabolic diversity through dense connectivity in tropical and polar regions, enabling aerobic methanotrophy to exhibit greater temperature sensitivity in these regions than in the temperate region (Fig. 4B). Conversely, the temperate region exhibited sparser subnetwork structure, with lower temperature sensitivity as measured by degree (Fig. 4A). This structure isolated CH4 oxidation modules from other communities, as evidenced by higher modularity and greater average path length (Supplementary Table 4). Furthermore, the temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy increases with the proportion of positive correlations in the MOB-associated subnetwork relative to the whole network (Fig. 4C). This amplification is mechanistically driven by positive microbial interactions, which enhance the metabolic rate of individual microorganisms or total biomass accumulation through cross-feeding networks57, thereby reinforcing the temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy. These findings underscore the importance of analyzing MOB-associated subnetwork topological parameters shaped by nontrophic interaction to explore the temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy.

Additionally, the coupling relationship between MOB and their electron donor (CH4, primarily derived from methanogenic archaea in anaerobic niches) and electron acceptor source (O2, largely supplied by Cyanobacteria via oxygenic photosynthesis) exhibited significant variation across geographic zones. Analysis of the co-occurrence networks revealed a latitudinal increase in the number of significant correlations between MOB and Cyanobacteria, with the count of links rising from 11 in the tropical network, to 13 in the temperate network, and 81 in the polar network (Fig. 3). This latitudinal pattern arises from a reciprocal exchange mechanism58 wherein MOB-supplied CO2/metabolites enhance cyanobacterial growth while Cyanobacteria sustain MOB through O2 provision and redox optimization—a synergy particularly critical in high latitudes where reduced photosynthetic efficiency increases cyanobacterial metabolite dependency, and prolonged ice cover creates microaerophilic niches favoring co-activity59. In contrast, despite the detection of methanogenic archaea in the community sequencing data, no direct metabolic interactions were identified between MOB and methanogenic archaea in the co-occurrence network. This phenomenon is consistent with their ecological niche segregation—MOB thrive in oxic zones, whereas methanogenic archaea are restricted to anoxic/anaerobic sediments or water layers. Indirect linkages exist wherein methanogen-produced CH4 diffuses into oxic zones where MOB oxidize it, creating a cryptic but critical carbon loop that mitigates CH4 emissions, though such relationships lie beyond the scope of this study. Additionally, MOB and methanogenic archaea may establish an internal CH4 cycle within Microcystis aggregates53, but this requires further experimental verification (e.g., via targeted microscopic studies such as SIP or co-culture).

Discussion

Microbial interactions regulate microbial growth, survival, and resource use, becoming fundamental drivers of ecosystem function such as carbon cycling57. These interactions critically influence climate-carbon feedbacks, as shifts in interaction types (e.g., from competitive to facilitative) amplify or dampen the temperature sensitivity of ecosystem processes. Thus, microbial interactions can be an early indicator of ecosystem functioning due to their high reactivity and sensitivity60. Blouin et al. 61 reported that simplified microbial networks release less CO2 emissions.

MOB interactions span trophic (e.g., predation, parasitism) and nontrophic (e.g., cooperation and competition) dimensions. MOB fuels microbial food webs for trophic interaction by converting CH4 into bioavailable organic carbon (i.e., methane-derived carbon)62,63. Sanseverino et al. 64 revealed methane-derived carbon assimilation across aquatic food webs, even up to fish. A New Zealand CH4 seep hosts the highest biomass heterotrophic benthic community (84,000 organisms/m2) known from deep-sea methane seeps, also fueled by methane-derived carbon65. Although MOB is demonstrated to support aquatic food webs directly, studies like Jones & Lennon66 challenge this view. In humic lakes, zooplankton (e.g., Daphnia) exhibit no direct grazing on MOB, with their 13C-depleted signatures arising from protist-mediated pathways67. While MOB-mediated carbon transfer supports secondary production in rich-CH4 ecosystems (especially under phytoplankton scarcity), nontrophic interactions—such as Daphnia can regulate MOB activity. Specifically, Daphnia establish a unique CH4 cycling system by providing a microenvironment in their intestines that facilitates metabolic exchanges between MOB and methanogenic archaea68. Thus, while MOB-driven carbon flows are locally significant, they also have wide ecological impacts through nontrophic interactions.

Network topology analysis decodes nontrophic MOB interactions, revealing their key role in microbial community dynamics and ecosystem function69. The synergistic effects of subnetwork topological features in tropical and polar regions may enhance functional redundancy, facilitating different taxa adaptation to temperature shifts while ensuring CH4 oxidation efficiency in MOB communities through compositional turnover buffered by their flexible interaction with heterotrophs70,71. For instance, rewiring of cross-feeding partnerships during desiccation-rewetting cycles buffers oxidative activity, even as non-MOB taxa falter72. Meanwhile, the effects of these features in tropical and polar regions can also boost the efficiency of substrate transformation, which accelerates microbial community adaptation to environmental changes by optimizing metabolic resource allocation. In contrast, the sparse subnetwork structure characteristic of temperate region may reflect niche differentiation between type I and type II MOB, providing a buffer against temperature fluctuations but potentially limiting cross-cluster substrate affinity coordination. Additionally, the temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy elevates with the proportion of positive correlations within the MOB-associated subnetwork relative to the whole network (Fig. 3E). Mechanistically, this amplification is driven by positive microbial interactions—such as those facilitated by cross-feeding—which enhance either the metabolic rate of individual microorganisms or total community biomass accumulation57. Consequently, these interactions reinforce the overall temperature response of aerobic methanotrophy.

Although warming simultaneously enhance aerobic methanotrophy and methanogenesis, several factors could limit MOB activity. These factors include substrate limitation, the thermal sensitivity of enzymes involved, and reduced CH4 solubility at higher temperatures73–75, particularly in ecosystems like rice paddies75 and ponds2, where methanogenesis exhibits greater temperature sensitivity than methanotrophy. Even as MOB upregulate starvation response genes (e.g., glycogen synthesis)29, their carbon conversion efficiency drops by approximately 17% under warming2. This temperature-dependent acceleration of methanogenesis establishes a self-reinforcing feedback loop, intensifying atmospheric warming. Additionally, physical bypass mechanisms (e.g., ebullition, plant-mediated transport) probably escape methanotrophy76, and this portion of CH4 needs to be considered separately in the future.

Earth’s biogeochemical evolution since the Archean ( ~ 3.5 Ga) is evidence of critical links between microbial consortia, atmospheric dynamics, and mean surface temperature77 (Fig. 5A). CH4 production by methanogenic archaea began approximately 3.5 billion years ago, marking the earliest stages of biological evolution78. The emergence of photosynthetic autotrophic microorganisms during the Archean, around 3 billion years ago, drove transformative shifts in Earth’s surface and carbon cycle78,79. MOB, originating approximately 2.8 Ga, became critical CH4 sinks that regulated atmospheric composition80. This timeline indicates aerobic respiration predated substantial atmospheric oxygenation, potentially catalyzing oxygenic photosynthesis in Cyanobacteria81. The coupling of MOB and Cyanobacteria during the Archean period highlights their oxygen dependence (Fig. 5B) as a key regulator of atmospheric CH4 and O2 levels82. However, the Great Oxidation Event (2.43–2.33 Ga) triggered a paradigm shift81. Rising O2 levels decoupled MOB from Cyanobacteria, driving novel partnerships with methanogenic archaea82 (Fig. 5C). This niche rewiring underscores that MOB have the capacity to adapt greenhouse gas regimes through microbial interaction reorganization. The Permian-Triassic extinction event, one of the most severe in Earth’s history, provides further evidence of the interplay between climate change and microbial communities83. During this period, the intensification of microbial CH4 cycling in paleo-Lake Junggar in Northwest China contributed to ancient global warming, demonstrating the feedback mechanisms between MOB interaction and climate change83. The biological relationships of MOB during this time likely resembled those of the Archean period, driven by earliest stages of biological influence on Earth’s atmosphere and temperature (Fig. 5D). Accelerating climate change is continuing to reshape microbial interactions in the Earth’s atmosphere and carbon cycle (Fig. 5E)84. Modern microbial interaction networks reveal critical CH4-converting taxa that reflect latitudinal disparities in MOB partnerships. This latitudinal gradient aligns with declining riverine CH4 emissions at higher latitudes85, suggesting enhanced MOB-Cyanobacteria coupling may suppress CH4 release in colder ecosystems. Nevertheless, model projections indicate that MOB could reduce global surface temperature rise by 0.22 °C over the next century by regulating CH4 emissions, demonstrating their critical but constrained role in controlling climate feedbacks86. Tropical and polar regions exhibit higher connectivity and clustering coefficients, alongside greater temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy. Despite their current high microbial diversity and cohesion, temperate region has more dispersed MOB-associated subnetworks with low temperature sensitivity, and future changes will require sustained attention. For those MOB that associate with Cyanobacteria, their interaction may intensify under future warming, leading to the epilimnion zone CH4 cycle increasingly dominated by their direct coupling—a system like during Archean period. This potential shift is primarily driven by projected increases in atmospheric CH4 and reduced dissolved oxygen solubility87 (increasing the local value of cyanobacterial O2 production). Under certain conditions, such as if warming strengthens and prolongs water column stratification88, the physical limitation of sediment-derived CH4 flux could further favor this local cycle, potentially supplemented by cyanobacteria-derived CH489. Although warming also stimulates methanogenesis, its influence on the MOB community may be modulated by these physical and atmospheric changes, potentially elevating the relative importance of the MOB-Cyanobacteria interaction. Our findings underscore that microbial interactions are an important controller to aerobic methanotrophy. To accurately project future CH4 emissions, a critical next step is to explicitly quantify the plasticity of these interaction subnetworks under sustained warming.

Methods

Microbial data collection and bioinformatics analysis

First, we used meta-analysis to collect literature to investigate the geographical distribution of MOB in freshwater systems. Advances in sequencing have provided a more accurate understanding of the worldwide distribution and diversity of the underestimated MOB. To ensure comprehensive coverage of MOB in global freshwater ecosystems, our literature search on Web of Science database (http://apps.webofknowledge.com/) employed broad terms such as “bacteria” and “bacterioplankton” alongside “river”, “reservoir” and “lake” for the following rationale: MOB, as a specialized functional subgroup within bacterial communities, has often failed to receive sufficient attention in studies directed at their taxonomy. The detailed process of literature screening was illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 6. By adopting this inclusive search strategy, we captured implicit MOB-related data embedded in a broader bacterial community analysis90. Additionally, the number of studies and geographic coverage of total bacterial communities significantly surpass those focusing on MOB via the functional gene pmoA; thus, employing this broader methodology yields more robust global biogeographic distribution patterns for MOB.

The following criteria were used to select suitable literature: (1) bacterial communities were investigated by high-throughput sequencing; (2) the corresponding primer sequences and accession numbers were given in the article, and studies that were able to obtain sequencing metadata from public databases by using this accession number; (3) the primers and amplification regions are capable of simultaneously identifying bacteria and archaea. Based on the above principles, 64 publications with a total of 3,720 samples were screened for subsequent analyses in this article.

The raw sequencing data were obtained from NCBI SRA (Sequence Read Archive, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra) and NGDC (National Genomics Data Center, https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/research) using the accession number provided in the literature. Each data set obtained was processed by the modified EasyAmplicon pipeline91. In detail, paired-end reads were merged using the fastq-mergepairs command in VSEARCH 2.7.1 and the merged sequences were excised from the double-ended primers and filtered for quality (fastq_maxee_rate 0.01). The quality was set to 0.01 to filter out sequences with quality control score < 20. The filtered sequences were dereplicated (miniuniqusize > 10) and then denoised using the UNOISE392 algorithm to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and chimera and low-abundance sequences (n < 8) were discarded. To integrate and compare data from studies that used different variable regions of the 16S rRNA gene, we employed a closed-reference workflow based on a database. The obtained fragments were mapped to non-redundant full-length 16S rRNA sequences in the SILVA database with sequence identity ≥ 97%93,94. The taxonomy assignment for each ASV was completed using the RDP classifier algorithm and the SILVA reference database (v123)95. Fragments that cannot be mapped are defined as unknown sequences and non- (bacteria and archaea) sequences were screened out. The ASV tables from different studies are then generated from the full-length 16S sequence of the map and they were combined into an integrated ASV table for further analysis (particularly focus on MOB). The abundance of each MOB taxon (at the family level) was divided by the total bacterial abundance to obtain its relative abundance within each zone. All accession numbers were listed in Supplementary Table 5.

Genomes from Earth’s microbiomes catalog

A second approach has also been used to investigate the global biogeographic patterns of MOB. A large-scale meta-analysis was conducted utilizing the Genomes from Earth’s Microbiomes (GEMs) catalog of 52,515 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAG) from diverse ecosystems, derived from over 10,000 metagenomic samples96. The data in this database is publicly available96.

Aerobic methane oxidation rate (MOX) data collection

In order to explore the distribution of aerobic methane oxidation rate (MOX), which is the function performed by MOB, we conducted another comprehensive literature research in Web of Science database (http://apps.webofknowledge.com/) by using the search strategy of keywords: “methane oxidation”, “aerobic methane oxidation”, “aerobic methane-oxidizing bacteria”, and “methane oxidation rate”. Researches were screened based on relevance to freshwater ecosystems. After excluding duplicates and non-quantitative studies, 40 publications met the inclusion criteria. These encompassed 39 lakes, 2 reservoirs, 1 river, and 7 estuarine/coastal systems. Data on MOX, geographical locations, and trophic states were extracted and analyzed to assess global distribution patterns and temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy. This category is then used to describe the biogeographical distribution of MOB along latitudinal gradients rather than climate mechanisms. Temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy analysis

Based on this, we calculate the temperature sensitivity of MOX data obtained in the previous Section. Temperature sensitivity specifically refers to the apparent, process-level response of aerobic methane oxidation capacity (i.e., MOX), and is quantitatively defined as the change in MOX per degree Celsius (μmol·L−1·d−1·°C−1). The response of biological processes to temperature change can often be fitted using different functional forms97. In this study, we evaluated the performance of three widely used models (linear, non-linear, and Arrhenius equations) to analyze the relationship between aerobic methanotrophy and temperature. The results showed that the linear equation provided the best fit for these three geographic zones (Supplementary Table 7). Therefore, we determined the temperature sensitivity of aerobic methanotrophy as the slope of the linear relationship between them, as follows: MOX=a×T + b. MOX, aerobic methane oxidation rate (μmol·L−1·d−1); T, temperature (°C), and a and b are fitting parameters.

Co-occurrence network analysis

Microbial co-occurrence network was constructed using pairwise Sparse correlation method to determine pairwise association of ASV. Species with occupancy more than 20% and a relative abundance greater than 0.01% in any sample were chosen for total network construction98. Sparse correlation between ASVs was calculated through 20 iterations, and pseudo p-values were estimated by 100 bootstrap samples (two-sided)99. Subsequently, all possible pairwise correlations (r) between these ASVs were calculated. Only correlations that were statistically significant and robust (p < 0.05, |r | > 0.3) were considered valid and further analyzed100. The correlation thresholds were selected in accordance with established practices in microbial ecology to ensure robust and ecologically interpretable associations by balancing sensitivity and specificity99,101. The SpiecEasi package in R (v4.1.1) was used for the calculation of Sparse correlations. Topological analysis and network visualization were performed using the igraph package for R (v4.1.1) and Gephi (v0.9.2). Subnetworks were abstracted using MCODE in Cytoscape (v3.8.2)102.

Nontrophic interactions refer to non-consumptive relationships between species that mutually benefit each other or where one species influences another by altering its condition or resource availability103. We based our selection of statistically robust correlations on the presence of significant nontrophic interactions (p < 0.05, |r| > 0.3) within both the whole network and the MOB-associated subnetwork, which are increasingly recognized as pivotal drivers of microbial community assembly and stability104.

Defining keystone species

Keystone species are highly connected and can significantly impact microbial community structure and function, regardless of their abundance105. Their removal can lead to dramatic changes in the structure and function of microbial community. Keystone species were identified using two key topological parameters: within-module connectivity (Zi) and among-module connectivity (Pi). Zi measures how strongly a node is connected to other nodes within its own module, while Pi reflects the extent to which a node is connected to nodes in other modules106. Node topologies were divided into four categories: (ⅰ) peripherals (Zi ≤ 2.5, Pi ≤ 0.62); (ⅱ) module hubs (Zi > 2.5, Pi ≤ 0.62); (ⅲ) connectors (Zi ≤ 2.5, Pi > 0.62); (ⅳ) network hubs (Zi > 2.5, Pi > 0.62)107,108.

Cohesion analysis

Cohesion provides valuable insights into the relationships between taxa, which can be influenced by positive and negative interactions as well as similarities and differences in microbial niches109. Positive cohesion measures the level of cooperative behaviors within a sample, while negative cohesion indicates the extent of competitive behaviors among community members. For each sample, two cohesion values (positive and negative) were calculated by summing significant positive or negative correlations between taxa, weighted by their abundances. The formula was detailed in this article110. Negative cohesion ranges from -1 to 0, while positive cohesion ranges from 0 to 1. Higher absolute values indicate stronger correlations. Therefore, the ratio of their absolute values was used to assess the cohesion of microbial community109.
