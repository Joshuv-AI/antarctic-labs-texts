The molecular basis underlying the differential development of immune organs between Huaifu and Cherry Valley ducks during early growth
Authors: Zhiyu He, Shuai Xin, Yunyi Chen, Xiaopeng Li, Jiwei Hu, Hua He, Liang Li, Hehe Liu, Jiwen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProduction experience has shown that local meat duck breeds possess stronger immune capacities than commercial breeds. However, systematic comparisons of their immune differences remain insufficient, and the molecular basis underlying these differences is still unclear.ResultsIn this study, we compared body weight, serum immune molecule levels (IgA, IgM, IgG, IFN-γ, IL-6, and IL-10), and immune organ indices during early growth stages (1, 14, and 28 days of age [D]) between Huaifu meat ducks (HF) and Cherry Valley ducks (CV). The results showed that no significant differences were observed in these parameters at 1 and 14 D. At 28 D, the body weight of CV was significantly higher than that of HF. Meanwhile, ELISA results showed significantly higher levels of serum IgA, IgM, IL-6, and IL-10 in HF. In addition, the thymus index of HF was significantly greater. Furthermore, transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on immune organs at 28 D. Subsequent analysis identified 443, 451, and 449 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the thymus, spleen, and bursa of Fabricius (BF), respectively. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis identified six, five, and two immune-related pathways in the thymus, spleen, and BF. IL6, CCR9, IL10, IL12A, RIPK2, RNASEL, SPP1, TLR5, and CASR, which were enriched in these pathways, might play key regulatory roles. Then, we performed Spearman correlation analysis between them and the DEGs in their corresponding tissues and constructed gene sets based on the results. A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was further established for the gene sets from the three tissues. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were primarily associated with metabolism.ConclusionsIn summary, 28 D represented a critical stage for the growth and immune system development of HF and CV. The genes IL6, CCR9, IL10, IL12A, RIPK2, RNASEL, SPP1, TLR5, and CASR were identified as key candidates, which might participate in essential metabolic processes, collectively influencing immune organ development with particularly pronounced effects on the thymus. In addition, serum levels of IgA, IgM, IL-6, and IL-10 appeared to be coordinately regulated by the thymus and spleen. Background

In the poultry industry, disease-induced reductions in production performance and increases in mortality can lead to substantial economic losses. Breeding for stronger immunity helps reduce this risk [1]. Production experience has shown that local duck breeds not only have better meat quality [2, 3] but also stronger immunity than fast-growing commercial breeds. The latter has received indirect support from emerging research findings. For instance, a comparative genomic study between the Jianshui yellow–brown duck and the Pekin duck revealed strong selection signals in the Jianshui duck genome surrounding ITGA4, a gene implicated in immune regulation [4]. Under identical infection conditions, the local breed (Ji'an Red-feathered duck) exhibited lower morbidity (85.63% vs. 100%) and mortality (68.75% vs. 88.76%) compared to the commercial breed (White Kaiya duck) [5]. However, systematic comparisons are still lacking, and the molecular basis underlying these differences remains unclear.

The thymus and bursa of Fabricius (BF) are primary lymphoid organs in poultry and undergo age-dependent changes [6]. The thymus serves as the central site for T cell development, maturation, and differentiation [7, 8]. Early life is a critical period for its development, during which T cells not only eliminate pathogens and establish immune memory [9], but also develop tolerance to harmless antigens [10]. The early development status of the thymus will affect its future size, cell-mediated immune function throughout life, and the rate of thymic atrophy [11, 12]. Research on chickens demonstrated that the thymus was not only the earliest fully developed but also the most active lymphocytopoietic organ, followed by the BF [13]. In embryos and young chicks, lymphocytes appeared to originate from the thymus and BF rather than the bone marrow [13].

Located dorsally to the cloaca, the BF is a unique lymphoid organ in birds [14]. Similar to other lymphopoietic tissues, it is composed of various immune and stromal cells, including lymphocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, and reticular cells derived from mesenchymal or epithelial origins [15]. The organ contains 8,000–12,000 follicles (with approximately 1,000 bursal cells per follicle), each comprising a cortex, cortico-medullary border, and medulla [14]. These follicles provide a specialized microenvironment that supports B cell proliferation, differentiation, and maturation [16]. Mature B cells exit the BF, migrate to peripheral lymphoid organs, and further differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells, thereby participating in humoral immune responses [17]. In addition, bursal extracts were reported to contain various peptides that could modulate immune responses and enhance both innate and adaptive immunity [18–20].

The spleen, as a secondary lymphoid organ, performs various functions such as immune responses, hematopoiesis, and blood filtration and storage [21]. During post-embryonic development, spleen function improves alongside the migration of T and B cells and the development of the red and white pulp. The formed blood–spleen barrier helps resist and eliminate antigens in the blood [22, 23]. After hatching, the duck spleen develops rapidly and displays marked structural differences compared to the chicken spleen [24, 25].

In summary, these organs serve as the core immune system components in birds, collectively forming the structural and functional basis of the immune response [26]. Their early developmental status may exert a lifelong influence on immunity and be a key factor in the divergent immunity between local and commercial ducks. However, relevant research remains lacking. In this study, we compared body weight, serum immune molecule levels (IgA, IgM, IgG, IFN-γ, IL-6, and IL-10), and organ indices between a local duck breed (Huaifu meat duck, HF) and a commercial breed (Cherry Valley duck, CV) during early growth stages (1, 14, and 28 days of age [D]). Further, we constructed transcriptional expression profiles of the three immune organs at key time points using transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) to elucidate the molecular basis underlying the observed immune differences, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for subsequent disease-resistant breeding.

Methods

Animals

The experimental ducks comprised 100 male ducklings per breed (HF and CV), all hatched from the same batch at the meat duck breeding farm of Henan Xurui Food Co., Ltd. All ducks were reared under standardized conditions with free access to feed (Table 1) and water and housing in separate pens by group. Sample collection

At 1, 14, and 28 D, five healthy HF and five healthy CV male ducks were randomly selected from each group, fasted for 12 h, weighed, and then euthanized. To minimize the suffering of the experimental animals, we employed professional poultry euthanasia equipment (a water bath electric stunner, model DMJ-II, Nanjing, China). The device operates at 50–120 V, which instantly induces unconsciousness in the animals. Once unconsciousness was confirmed by the absence of rhythmic breathing and corneal reflexes, the experimenters promptly performed cervical dislocation to ensure death. The entire procedure was conducted while the animals were fully unconscious, ensuring a rapid process that prevented any fear or stress.

Blood and immune organs (including the thymus, spleen, and BF) were collected immediately thereafter. Blood samples were incubated at room temperature for 30 min to allow clotting. Serum was then prepared by centrifugation at 3000 r/min for 15 min. Following centrifugation, the serum was carefully collected, transferred into pre-labeled EP tubes, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent quantification of serum immune molecule levels (IgA, IgM, IgG, IFN-γ, IL-6, and IL-10) using duck-specific ELISA kits (Huding, Shanghai, China). According to the instructions, sequentially add standards or test samples and enzyme-labeled reagents to the microplate wells to form a complex. After washing, add the chromogen solution. The color intensity was positively correlated with the concentration of the target analyte in the sample. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm, and the analyte concentration was calculated using a standard curve. The organs were weighed to calculate the corresponding organ index (organ weight [g] / body weight [kg]). The collected samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and then transferred to a −80 °C freezer for short-term storage prior to RNA-seq.

RNA extraction and sequencing

A portion of the samples was ground, and total RNA was extracted from the thymus, spleen, and BF using RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). RNA samples (≥ 1 μg) were selected for library preparation using the NEB Next Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, USA). The quality of the resulting libraries was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA). The total library concentration was quantified using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) on a Quantifluor-ST fluorometer (Promega, Madison, USA), while the effective library concentration was determined by qPCR on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA). The qualified libraries were normalized and sequenced on an Illumina platform in PE150 mode.

Bioinformatics analysis of transcriptome data

Raw data were processed with fastp (v0.22.0) [27] to obtain high-quality clean data by: (1) removing adapter sequences at the 3' end, and (2) discarding reads with an average quality score below Q20. All subsequent analyses were performed using these clean data. The clean reads were aligned to the duck reference genome (GenBank: GCA_015476345.1) using HISAT2 (v2.1.0) [28] with a pre-built genome index. Read counts for each gene were calculated using HTSeq (v0.9.1) [29]. To mitigate potential biases from sequencing depth and gene length differences, raw read counts were normalized using the TPM (Transcripts Per Million) method.

Identification and functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

Differential expression analysis between breeds was performed using DESeq2 (v1.38.3) [30] based on read counts, with a threshold of |log2FoldChange|≥ 1 and P < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional enrichment analysis was subsequently performed using KOBAS 3.0 (http://bioinfo.org/kobas/genelist/) [31]. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks for the DEGs were retrieved from the STRING database [32] (v12.0; https://cn.string-db.org/), followed by network visualization and analysis using Cytoscape software (v3.10.2) [33].

Statistical analysis

Differences in body weight, serum immune molecule levels, and immune organ indices between breeds were assessed by Student’s t-test in SPSS 27.0, with results visualized using GraphPad Prism 9. Spearman's correlation analysis was applied to evaluate the associations between genes. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

At 28 D, significant differences were observed. CV exhibited a significantly higher body weight than HF (P < 0.01). Meanwhile, HF demonstrated significantly higher serum levels of IgA and IL-6 (P < 0.05) as well as IgM and IL-10 (P < 0.01). RNA-seq overview and identification of differentially expressed genes

We performed RNA-seq on thymus, spleen, and BF at 28 D. The assessment report of transcriptome sequencing data from 18 samples showed that Q20 values ranged between 99.1% and 99.4%, Q30 values were in the range of 97.11% to 97.97%, and mapping rates varied from 91.63% to 95.34%. The data quality met the requirements for subsequent analysis (Table S1).

To evaluate the gene expression patterns in the thymus, spleen, and BF of HF and CV, we performed principal component analysis. The results revealed distinct differences in expression patterns between the two breeds, particularly in the BF (Fig. 2 A-C). We then identified 443 DEGs in the thymus (281 up-regulated and 162 down-regulated); 451 DEGs in the spleen (251 up-regulated and 200 down-regulated); and 449 DEGs in the BF (317 up-regulated and 132 down-regulated) (Fig. 2D). Subsequently, we generated clustered heatmaps of the DEGs for the thymus (Fig. 2E), spleen (Fig. 2F), and BF (Fig. 2G). Functional analysis of differentially expressed genes

We identified 233, 194, and 187 GO terms in the thymus, spleen, and BF, respectively, with the top 30 terms presented in Fig. 3A-C. Furthermore, we identified 77 KEGG pathways in the thymus, 11 of which were significantly enriched (Fig. 3D), including six pathways related to immune function (intestinal immune network for IgA production, c-type lectin receptor signaling pathway, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, toll-like receptor signaling pathway, cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway, and RIG-I-like receptor signaling pathway). In the spleen, 58 pathways were enriched, with eight being significant (Fig. 3E); five of these were immune-related, such as the toll-like receptor signaling pathway and intestinal immune network for IgA production. In the BF, 63 pathways were enriched, 14 of which were significant (Fig. 3F), and two of these (toll-like receptor signaling pathway and NOD-like receptor signaling pathway) were associated with immunity (P < 0.05). The immune-related pathways enriched in the three tissues were listed in Fig. 3G, which revealed substantial overlap between the thymus and spleen. Meanwhile, genes directly associated with the levels of immunoglobulins (IL6, CCR9, and IL10 [intestinal immune network for IgA production]) and cytokines (IL6, IL10, and IL12A) showed significant differential expression in both tissues. Notably, the toll-like receptor signaling pathway and the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway were enriched in all three tissues (Fig. 3H). Correlation analysis of key candidate genes and differentially expressed genes

Among these nine key candidate genes, six were identified in the thymus (IL6, CCR9, IL10, IL12A, RIPK2, and RNASEL), two in the spleen (IL6 and SPP1), and three in the BF (SPP1, TLR5, and CASR). We performed Spearman correlation analysis between the key candidate genes and the DEGs in their corresponding tissues to elucidate the potential regulatory relationships of the key candidate genes on the DEGs. In the thymus, the numbers of DEGs significantly correlated with IL6, CCR9, IL10, IL12A, RIPK2, and RNASEL were 117, 113, 114, 61, 114, and 87, respectively (Fig. 4A). In the spleen, the numbers of DEGs significantly correlated with IL6 and SPP1 were 77 and 34 (Fig. 4B). Construction of regulatory network for key candidate genes

To elucidate the molecular basis of key genes and their associated DEGs in the thymus (IL6, CCR9, IL10, IL12A, RIPK2, RNASEL), spleen (IL6, SPP1), and BF (SPP1, TLR5, CASR), we constructed a PPI network for the three tissues. IL6 and DUSP26 showed differential expression both in the thymus and spleen, SPP1 was differentially expressed both in the spleen and the BF, and EPSTI1 and TDRD9 were differentially expressed in the thymus and BF (Fig. 5A). Discussion

Compared to commercial breeds, local duck breeds exhibit stronger immunity [5]. However, there is still a lack of systematic comparative analysis, and the underlying molecular basis remains unclear. Our study comprehensively evaluated the early growth performance, serum immune molecule levels, organ indices, and transcriptome profiles between the HF and CV, and further identified key candidate genes. These findings provided a theoretical foundation for subsequent disease-resistant breeding.

Growth performance, serum immune molecule levels, and organ indices all differed significantly at 28 D, suggesting that this time point represented a critical developmental period for the divergence between the two breeds. Ducks at 28 D are in a period of rapid growth [34]. At this time point, although HF showed slightly lower growth performance compared to CV, higher levels of immunoglobulins (IgA and IgM) [35, 36] and cytokines (IL-6 and IL-10) [37, 38] might have contributed to maintaining a robust immune system. Research indicated that a competitive relationship for resource allocation existed between growth and immune function within the organism [39]. Establishing and maintaining an efficient immune system might necessitate a trade-off between growth performance and immune function [40]. Therefore, we surmised that HF allocated more resources to the immune system, whereas CV prioritized growth and development. In addition, evidence suggests that high-intensity selection for growth performance impairs immune function [41, 42]. Thus, the difference in selection intensity may be a key factor in the divergent growth performance and immunity observed among breeds.

Further analysis identified nine key DEGs, including IL6, CCR9, IL10, IL12A, RIPK2, RNASEL, SPP1, TLR5, and CASR. Although IL6, IL10, and IL12A all participate in encoding cytokines, their functions differ. Differences in serum immune molecule levels between HF and CV were probably attributable to their differential expression. IL6 encodes interleukin-6, a cytokine crucial for host defense that targets diverse cell types, including T cells [43], B cells [44], hemopoietic progenitors [45], and neurons [46]. It is primarily produced by monocytes and macrophages, and helps protect the tissues from damage by activating immune, hematological, and acute phase responses [47]. IL-6 deficient mice developed normally but exhibited an impaired ability to control viral and bacterial infections. Furthermore, they could not initiate an effective acute inflammatory response following tissue damage or infection [48]. IL10 encodes interleukin-10, a cytokine primarily produced by monocytes (with minor contributions from lymphocytes) [49, 50]. IL-10 can suppress pro-inflammatory responses and limit unnecessary tissue damage caused by inflammation. Additionally, members of the IL10 family participate in maintaining the integrity and homeostasis of epithelial tissues [51]. Results in ducks showed that IL-10 receptor gene (IL10R1) was most abundantly expressed in the thymus, spleen, peripheral blood mononuclear cells, and lung [52]. IL12A encodes a subunit that can heterodimerize with IL-12B to form IL-12 [53], or with EBI3/IL-27B to form IL-35 [54]. IL-12 is a pro-inflammatory cytokine composed of the p35 (IL12A) and p40 (IL12B) chains. It is primarily produced by professional antigen-presenting cells, such as dendritic cells [55]. IL-35 was reported as an anti-inflammatory factor involved in regulating various diseases [56]. In poultry, reports on these cytokines were limited. Our findings suggested a key role for these cytokines in modulating immune differences between duck breeds. The protein (chemokine receptor 9) encoded by CCR9 is a G protein-coupled receptor with seven transmembrane domains [57]. Chemokines and their receptors are key regulatory factors for thymocyte migration and maturation under normal and inflammatory conditions [58]. In mammals, CCR9 helps bone marrow-derived precursors settle in the thymus, a process crucial for T cell development [59]. Studies in chickens indicated that the expression of CCR9 in thymocytes and splenocytes was regulated by cytokines [60]. The differential expression of CCR9 in the thymus between HF and CV corresponded to the significant difference in their thymus index, indicating its contribution to the regulation of thymus development. RIPK2, RNASEL, and CASR belong to the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, whereas SPP1 and TLR5 are components of the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, both of which are involved in innate immune system [61, 62].

To further investigate the functional mechanisms of key genes, we constructed their potential regulatory networks through correlation analysis. Our results showed that the genes involved in the network were primarily associated with metabolic processes, including amino acid, cofactor and vitamin, nucleotide, and carbohydrate metabolism. As discussed earlier, establishing and maintaining an efficient immune system is costly and resource-intensive. Amino acids are considered fundamental components for the origin of life [63]. Amino acid metabolism profoundly regulates immune cell function by influencing their metabolic status during dynamic activation in response to infections and environmental changes [64]. Correspondingly, cytokines such as IL-6 were reported to alter protein metabolism. This process produced amino acids that were used to synthesize inflammation and immune-related proteins, which supported immune cell proliferation [65]. Previous studies demonstrated that under experimental conditions, amino acid intake (isoleucine + leucine + valine) significantly impacted the weights of the chicken thymus and BF [66]. And in ducks, glutamine supplementation increased toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) levels and enhanced immune function [67]. In our study, we observed the differential metabolism of tyrosine, tryptophan, arginine, proline, phenylalanine, valine, leucine, and isoleucine in the immune organs of two duck breeds with different immunity. Multiple vitamins are also known to support immune function, such as vitamin A [68], vitamin B [69], and vitamin K [70]. In ducks, vitamin E supplementation can improve growth performance and survival rates [71], while vitamin C enhances humoral immunity by boosting levels of IgA, IgM, and IgG [72]. Moreover, nucleotide and carbohydrate metabolism are also crucial [73]. Nucleotide signals alert the immune system to abnormal cell death, and high doses of nucleotides accelerate the release of immune molecules, thereby protecting the organism [74]. And carbohydrate metabolism can serve as a marker for immune cell activation and function [75]. Our results further validated the bidirectional interaction between the immune system and metabolism.

Conclusions

In conclusion, 28 D represented a critical stage for the growth and immune system development of HF and CV. The genes IL6, CCR9, IL10, IL12A, RIPK2, RNASEL, SPP1, TLR5, and CASR were key candidates, which might participate in essential metabolic processes, collectively influencing immune organ development with particularly pronounced effects on the thymus. In addition, serum levels of IgA, IgM, IL-6, and IL-10 appeared to be coordinately regulated by the thymus and spleen.
