HEAL: health-enhanced adaptive LoRaWAN for AI-based real-time soldier monitoring and status prediction
Authors: Atheer Alghamdi, Reem Alotaibi, Hanan Alahmadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Continuous and reliable monitoring of soldiers’ health in remote and resource-constrained environments is essential for operational readiness and timely emergency interventions. Long Range Wide Area Network (LoRaWAN) provides an energy-efficient, long-range communication solution for wearable Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) devices, but dynamic factors such as packet loss, latency, and bandwidth limitations can compromise real-time health inference. This paper presents HEAL, Health-Enhanced Adaptive LoRaWAN, an AI-enabled IoMT framework designed to predict soldiers’ health under dynamic physiological and communication conditions. “Adaptive” denotes AI-level robustness, where models sustain stable and accurate health predictions under varying communication constraints and transmission parameters. HEAL employs an end-to-end workflow in which a publicly available multivariate physiological time-series dataset is preprocessed, structured, and automatically labeled using transformer-based large language models. Multiple Deep Learning (DL) architectures are trained and compared to identify models suitable for real-time health status classification, with the best-performing model fine-tuned and deployed in a LoRaWAN simulation to evaluate AI inference under realistic communication constraints. Experimental results show that a BiLSTM model achieves up to 94% accuracy and 95% Macro F1-score on structured physiological data. Within the LoRaWAN simulation, the fine-tuned model maintains approximately 93% accuracy, while the network achieves a packet delivery ratio of up to 91% under favorable conditions (SF7–SF8, 200–400 m). HEAL provides a scalable and energy-efficient framework for systematically evaluating AI models alongside communication reliability metrics, demonstrating that accurate AI-driven health monitoring can be sustained despite dynamic LoRaWAN effects and bridging the gap between model development and realistic operational deployment.

Introduction

In modern military operations, ensuring the health and safety of personnel is paramount, especially in remote or hostile environments where timely medical interventions can be life-saving. The advent of Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) and advanced Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques has opened new frontiers in continuous health monitoring. However, deploying such systems in resource-constrained environments characterized by dynamic physiological conditions and fluctuating communication reliability remains a significant hurdle. The integration of IoMT into healthcare care has transformed how physiological data are collected, analyzed, and utilized for timely decision-making. In military contexts, wearable IoMT devices are being used more frequently to continuously monitor’ vital signs and environmental conditions of soldiers. This improves mission readiness in isolated and dangerous settings and allows for proactive medical interventions. Wearable healthcare systems have been widely investigated in the literature. Daou et al.1, Gunavanth et al.2, and Theivanayaki et al.3 employed Bluetooth, Zigbee, and Wi-Fi technologies, respectively, to support short-range communication between soldiers and their local units. Similarly, Sheeba et al.4 and Ramachandra et al.5 proposed wireless-based frameworks for soldier tracking and health monitoring.

However, situations using short-range or infrastructure-dependent communication technologies are clearly limited. Researchers have looked into Low Power Wide Area Networks (LoRaWAN) as a long-range, low-power solution to get around these limitations. Veerasamy et al.6, Jain et al.7, and Devi et al.8 developed wearable systems that use LoRaWAN to send GPS and physiological data over many kilometers. This concept is further developed by Mane and Patil9 and Hamilton et al.10, who integrated LoRa-enabled sensors into battlefield simulation environments to enhance emergency response and continuous monitoring. AI methods have been used concurrently to enhance healthcare monitoring classification, forecasting, and decision-making. In order to identify anomalous patterns and facilitate effective soldier monitoring, Sujitha et al.11, Kang et al.12, and Manikandan et al.13 presented AI-driven systems using K-means clustering, Zigbee-based inference, and LSTM models, respectively. In a comparable manner, Sharma and Aggarwal14 optimized diagnoses in real time using neuro-fuzzy techniques.

Considering these developments, most existing research focuses on either AI-based techniques or LoRaWAN-based communication in isolation. Only few studies15–17 investigate integration of AI methods with LoRaWAN-enabled communication. Although these contributions demonstrate the promise of integration, they mostly rely on fixed rule-based logic or simple predictive models, which frequently lack robustness to time-varying physiological patterns and LoRaWAN communication impairments, limiting their effectiveness in real-time health monitoring. The promise of AI–LoRaWAN integration for predictive, energy-efficient, and real-time health monitoring in military scenarios is highlighted by collective prior research. Nevertheless, most existing approaches remain limited in scope and do not provide a comprehensive framework that jointly addresses proactive emergency prediction, data processing, and real-time analytics tailored to military operational environments. This study addresses these limitations by presenting an end-to-end, AI-enabled Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) framework that leverages LoRaWAN for real-time soldier health monitoring and emergency prediction in combat scenarios. The main contributions of this research are summarized as follows:Design of the HEAL (Health-Enhanced Adaptive LoRaWAN) framework, which supports AI-driven soldier health monitoring that adapts to dynamic physiological states and varying LoRaWAN communication conditions, enabling reliable and energy-efficient operation in resource-constrained environments.Development of an advanced AI pipeline that integrates transformer-based Large Language Models (LLMs) for zero-shot health state labeling with Deep Learning (DL) architectures to enable accurate, real-time prediction of physiological status from multivariate wearable IoMT sensor data.Comparative simulation-based evaluation of 13 AI models in a LoRaWAN environment, assessing both health prediction performance and communication efficiency under low-power network constraints.Evaluation using both public datasets and LoRaWAN-simulated data, demonstrating the framework’s effectiveness, scalability, and suitability for deployment in resource-constrained and tactical military environments.The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section "Related work" reviews related work concerning IoMT deployments, LoRaWAN integration, and AI methods used in soldier health monitoring, highlighting existing limitations in integrated solutions. Section "Methodology" details the methodology, including the data preprocessing steps, the design of the DL pipeline, and the comprehensive architecture of the HEAL framework, specifying the integration of AI with the LoRaWAN simulation. Section "Results and discussion" presents the experimental results, covering the comparative performance of various DL models and the evaluation of the integrated AI–LoRaWAN system. Finally, Section "Conclusion" summarizes the main findings and discusses directions for future research.

Related work

In recent years, IoMT has transformed soldier healthcare by providing continuous real-time monitoring, ensuring rapid medical response and sustained operational effectiveness in the field. For example, to improve situational awareness during combat operations, Sheeba et al.4 created a system to track soldiers’ mobility and health status utilizing wearable sensors such GPS modules, humidity sensors, fire sensors, and pulse sensors. Similarly, a system that tracks Blood Pressure (BP), Temperature (Temp), and Heart Rate (HR) is proposed by Ramachandra et al.5 to assist early diagnosis and enable timely medical response for personnel in distant or hazardous places.

Daou et al.1 integrated Bluetooth communication into a smart uniform with , HR, and bullet detection sensors in addition to GPS to improve communication capabilities within the IoMT framework. This allowed for real-time monitoring and quick injury response. Similar to this, Gunavanth et al.2 used Zigbee technology in an IoT-based system to continuously monitor vital signs like HR and Temp, enabling quick emergency assistance. In a different strategy, Theivanayaki et al.3 employed Wi-Fi connectivity in an IoT system that combines GPS and biomedical sensors to increase soldier safety in combat. A GSM-based health system with vibration sensors to detect falls is proposed by Singh et al.18 to support these efforts and guarantee real-time alerts during military operations.

Long-range, low-power data transmission is another feature of LoRaWAN that has drawn a lot of interest beyond short-range communication technologies. This makes it especially appropriate for military environments with limited infrastructure and remote locations. Jain et al.7 created a portable gadget that transmits soldiers’ position and physiological data in real time via LoRa. Similarly, Veerasamy et al.6 deployed a LoRaWAN-based system that continuously tracks Temp, HR, and oxygen levels in addition to GPS data to facilitate prompt medical intervention. In order to improve health tracking and facilitate prompt emergency response in the event of anomalies, Devi et al.8 proposed a smart jacket that is embedded with multiple sensors and LoRa communication.

Building upon these initiatives, Mane and Patil9 presented an Internet of Things (IoT)-based monitoring framework that combines environmental and biometric sensors with LoRa connectivity to track battlefield conditions and soldier health in real time. A LoRaWAN-based communication system is also created by Hamilton et al.10 for emergency signaling and real-time location tracking during military simulation exercises. Their system showed dependable, energy-efficient communication over up to 10 kilometers using embedded Internet of Things devices with GPS receivers and low-power displays.

Singh et al.19 advanced this field further by creating a reliable LoRa-based system that uses an Arduino microcontroller to track location and vital signs and has an SOS alert system for emergency situations. In support of this work, Umamaheswari et al.20 suggested a wearable Internet of Things gadget that gathers GPS and biological data, preprocesses it on an ESP32 microcontroller, and sends it over LoRa for in-field monitoring in real time. These studies emphasize efficient data collection and transmission, but they typically lack AI-driven predictive analytics capabilities, underscoring the need for for automated systems that can both monitor and predict potential hazards in real time.

Additionally, AI methods have been used to improve predictive health analysis and decision-making. K-means clustering is used by Sujitha et al.11 to analyze ongoing health and positional data, allowing for the early identification of critical conditions and prompt medical response. Using a Zigbee-based system, Kang et al.12 processed medical data in phases using the Maximum Inference Algorithm (MIA), which reduced data volume while maintaining critical information and enhancing analytical accuracy. In a similar vein, Manikandan et al.13 combined Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) models in an AIoT framework via Wi-Fi, allowing soldiers and monitoring units to communicate instantly. Additionally, Sharma and Aggarwal14 optimized real-time tracking of health parameters using portable biosensors by implementing Artificial Neuro-Fuzzy Inference Systems (ANFIS) within a GSM-based IoT system.

Although both LoRaWAN and AI have shown promise individually to enhance soldier health monitoring systems, their combined integration is still quite restricted. One notable exception is the work of Bandopadhaya et al.15. They proposed a layered IoT architecture that supports fuzzy logic, time-series analysis, and LoRa communication for data filtering and emergency response. Their approach demonstrated the need for more comprehensive AI integration to provide independent emergency management, even though it significantly reduced response times and removed unnecessary data.

Similarly, Sourabh et al.16 proposed an AI-enabled IoMT system that integrates GPS modules, LoRa communication, environmental sensors (e.g., humidity), and physiological sensors to monitor soldiers’ location and health status in real time. The system employs Machine Learning (ML) algorithms to process mobility, environmental, and physiological data for predictive health assessment, thereby enhancing operational safety and situational awareness through early detection of health risks and timely emergency response.

In addition, Sirisha et al.17 created a military wearable Internet of Things system that continuously measures temperature and gas exposure. The system uses machine learning models like Random Forest and Logistic Regression, along with communication technologies like LoRaWAN, Narrowband Internet of Things (NB-IoT), and 5G, to forecast health hazards such as heat exhaustion and dehydration up to ten minutes ahead of time. These studies are extremely pertinent, but they do not provide a comprehensive AI–LoRaWAN framework that can handle emergencies on its own and predict health in real time in dynamic military settings.

Unlike prior studies that evaluate AI models or communication performance in isolation, HEAL provides a cross-layer evaluation framework that jointly captures physiological dynamics, LoRaWAN communication constraints, and AI-based inference within a unified simulation environment. Methodology

This section presents the design rationale and operational workflow of the proposed HEAL framework, an AI-enabled IoMT system for real-time health monitoring and health status prediction over LoRaWAN in defense environments. The methodology integrates heterogeneous physiological sensing, lightweight feature preprocessing, long-range low-power communication, and DL–based prediction into a unified end-to-end pipeline tailored to resource-constrained field deployment scenarios.

Figure 1 illustrates the methodological workflow of the HEAL framework, organized into three sequential stages that collectively enable AI-driven health monitoring over LoRaWAN. The first stage represents the offline AI model development phase, in which a public multivariate physiological dataset is acquired, relevant physiological features are selected, and health status labels (Well, Alarming, and Critical) are generated using LLM-based labeling.

Fig. 1Methodological workflow of the HEAL framework for AI-driven IoMT-based health monitoring over LoRaWAN.

Methodological workflow of the HEAL framework for AI-driven IoMT-based health monitoring over LoRaWAN.

In the second stage, the labeled physiological data are preprocessed and used to train and evaluate DL architectures, encompassing recurrent, bidirectional, stacked, hybrid, feedforward, and lightweight attention-based models commonly employed in physiological time-series analysis and IoMT-based health monitoring. This selection facilitates a systematic comparison of the capacity for temporal modeling, architectural complexity, and computational efficiency, enabling the identification of models that balance predictive accuracy with the feasibility of deployment under communication-constrained conditions. Model performance is assessed using appropriate evaluation metrics to determine the most effective architectures for reliable health status prediction across varying data conditions.

In the third stage, physiological measurements generated by wearable IoMT nodes are transmitted over the LoRaWAN network and received at the application server using an adaptivity-aware low-power communication mechanism. In this context, adaptivity denotes the robustness of AI models to communication constraints under varying transmission parameters evaluated through simulation, rather than implying dynamic reconfiguration of the network. The models are deployed online to assess predictive accuracy and stability in realistic operational scenarios, supported by system-level evaluation through joint analysis of health inference outcomes and LoRaWAN communication metrics under identical experimental conditions.

System architecture overview

Figure 2 presents the HEAL framework, which integrates wearable IoMT sensors, LoRaWAN communication, and AI-driven inference to enable continuous, real-time soldier health monitoring. Each soldier is equipped with wearable sensors that measure key physiological parameters. These sensors operate as LoRa nodes, transmitting the acquired data to a LoRaWAN gateway, which subsequently forwards the information to a centralized server for analysis. The deployed AI models process the physiological time series and classify the soldier’s health status into one of three categories: Well, Alarming, or Critical.

Schematic of the HEAL framework for real-time soldier health monitoring and status prediction.

The proposed architecture is designed to deliver reliable, low-latency health monitoring while minimizing energy consumption and maintaining stable performance within the constraints of low-power wide-area networks. To evaluate AI-level robustness, the models maintain stable and accurate health predictions under varying communication-induced constraints and multiple data transmission parameters, accommodating dynamic network conditions and evolving physiological patterns. Model inference is performed centrally at the server, enabling timely detection of abnormal physiological states.

Offline AI model development using automatically labeled physiological data

This section describes the offline development of AI models for soldier health monitoring using automatically labeled multivariate physiological time-series data. Before integration into the HEAL framework and its adaptive LoRaWAN communication layer, the models are trained to capture temporal dynamics and clinically meaningful transitions between health states. The offline phase encompasses data preprocessing, health state label generation using large language models, feature configuration aligned with LoRaWAN transmission constraints, and the comparative evaluation of multiple DL architectures to identify the most suitable model for real-time deployment.

Data acquisition and preparation

A publicly available multivariate physiological time-series dataset is employed to support a systematic and unbiased evaluation of AI time-series models under realistic yet controlled conditions. Moreover, the selected dataset closely reflects sensing modalities and temporal characteristics typical of wearable IoMT systems, making it suitable for pre-training and validating models intended for deployment in LoRaWAN-based health monitoring frameworks.

Pre-training AI time-series models is critical prior to their integration with the communication layer, as it allows the models to learn intrinsic temporal dependencies, cross-parameter correlations, and physiological dynamics independent of network-induced distortions. This separation enables a clearer assessment of model learning capability before evaluating performance under communication constraints. Accordingly, this study utilizes a multivariate time-series dataset from IEEE DataPort to simulate real-time health monitoring scenarios21. The dataset records physiological measurements at 10-minute intervals, which prior work identifies as optimal for maintaining reliable data delivery in LoRaWAN environments with simultaneous node and gateway mobility22.

Feature selection and communication constraints

Transmitting all physiological indicators in real time is sometimes impractical in LoRaWAN networks due to inherent bandwidth and energy constraints, which could compromise operational reliability. This study addresses the issue by evaluating two complementary feature selection configurations: Subset Monitoring Dataset (SMD) and Full Monitoring Dataset (FMD).

These indicators are selected based on their clinical significance and the accuracy achievable with small, energy-efficient wearable sensors. HR and Temp monitor physical exertion and detect early signs of heat stress, while blood oxygen level provides vital information on respiratory function. This minimal feature set is particularly suitable for communication scenarios with constrained resources, ensuring low-power operation and efficient use of LoRaWAN bandwidth. LLM-based health status labeling

Rule-based and threshold-driven labeling approaches provide a straightforward mechanism for health status assignment but are limited in their ability to incorporate complex relationships among multiple physiological parameters. To address this, state-of-the-art LLMs are employed for zero-shot health status labeling to generate consistent and interpretable annotations in the absence of predefined labels24.

Health status is categorized into three clinically relevant classes-Well, Alarming, and Critical-to support consistent labeling and evaluation across diverse physiological patterns. To generate reliable annotations, we employ a heterogeneous ensemble of three zero-shot LLMs that process structured vital-sign summaries, with final labels determined through majority voting to improve robustness and reduce individual model bias.

The ensemble combines complementary strengths: DeBERTa-v3 provides strong zero-shot semantic reasoning through disentangled attention, XLM-RoBERTa contributes multilingual adaptability via large-scale cross-lingual pretraining, and ClinicalBERT offers domain-specific understanding through training on biomedical and clinical corpora such as MIMIC-III. Together, this diversity enables more reliable health-state inference by leveraging generalization, linguistic coverage, and clinical specialization within a unified voting-based labeling strategy.

Two datasets of equal size (10,000 records each) are used to evaluate the influence of characteristics: SMD contains HR, Temp, and SpO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}_2$$\end{document}, while FMD includes all six vital signs. As shown in Fig. 3, SMD produces a skewed distribution (Well 91.8%, Critical 8.1%, Alarming 0.1%), whereas FMD is more balanced (Well 81.7%, Alarming 11.6%, Critical 6.8%). The class distributions provide a clear perspective on the representation of different health states in each dataset.

Data preprocessing

Effective data preparation is essential for building reliable AI models in health monitoring, as dataset quality and structure directly influence predictive performance. In this study, the raw physiological data are carefully processed to ensure clinical plausibility, temporal coherence, and class balance. Key steps include handling class imbalance, constraining physiological ranges, organizing temporal sequences by subject, balancing class distributions, and feature engineering. This systematic pipeline produces a structured, clinically credible, and temporally ordered dataset, providing a solid foundation for training advanced AI models capable of accurate real-time health state prediction.

The health monitoring dataset exhibits a substantial class imbalance, with the majority class Well dominating the minority classes Alarming and Critical, which can bias learning algorithms and reduce sensitivity to clinically significant events29. To address this, a hybrid rebalancing strategy is employed. Random undersampling reduces the Well class to 3000 representative instances, while the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE)30 generates additional Alarming and Critical samples through feature-space interpolation, enhancing diversity and mitigating overfitting associated with naive duplication31. SMOTE is applied only after sequence construction to avoid temporal leakage across consecutive time steps. For example, the FMD dataset originally contains 8168 Well, 1155 Alarming, and 677 Critical instances. After applying the described hybrid rebalancing strategy, each class contains 3000 instances, as summarized in Table 5, providing a solid foundation for model training and evaluation.

Selected deep learning models

This study evaluates a diverse set of DL architectures to determine the most effective temporal modeling approach for real-time soldier health monitoring under constrained IoMT–LoRaWAN environments. The selected models span recurrent, bidirectional, stacked, hybrid, feedforward, and lightweight attention-based paradigms that have demonstrated effectiveness in physiological time-series analysis and wearable health monitoring applications. The evaluated architectures include Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM), Bidirectional GRU (BiGRU), Stacked LSTM (StackedLSTM), Stacked GRU (StackedGRU), Stacked BiLSTM (StackedBiLSTM), Stacked BiGRU (StackedBiGRU), Convolutional Neural Network–LSTM (CNN-LSTM), a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), and lightweight attention-based models (Attention-LSTM, Attention-GRU, and a compact TinyTransformer).

LSTM and GRU serve as foundational recurrent models due to their ability to capture long-term temporal dependencies. LSTM employs gating mechanisms to model clinically relevant variations, such as sustained HR drift or Temp elevation, while GRU offers a simpler structure with faster convergence and lower computational cost, suitable for resource-constrained IoMT environments35,36. Bidirectional variants (BiLSTM, BiGRU) incorporate past and future context, enhancing detection of subtle physiological trends37,38. Stacked architectures (StackedLSTM, StackedGRU) deepen temporal representation, improving robustness to noisy sensor readings, whereas their stacked bidirectional counterparts (StackedBiLSTM, StackedBiGRU) capture complex multi-timescale patterns39,40. Hybrid CNN-LSTM models extract localized temporal features before sequential modeling, improving performance in IoMT anomaly detection41,42. Transformer-based and self-attention models have additionally demonstrated strong capabilities in capturing global temporal patterns in wearable and health-monitoring time-series data43. An MLP baseline is included to benchmark temporal model advantages, highlighting the importance of sequential modeling for physiological data44.

Together, these architectures provide a balanced framework for assessing accuracy–complexity trade-offs under real-time IoMT–LoRaWAN simulation scenarios. Evaluating them under identical conditions identifies models that optimally balance predictive accuracy, robustness to noisy inputs, and operational feasibility, guiding the selection of architectures suitable for real-time soldier health monitoring in resource-constrained LoRaWAN-enabled IoMT environments.

LoRaWAN integration

Continuous health monitoring in defense and operational settings requires communication infrastructure that supports ultra-low power operation, long-range coverage, and reliable data delivery under intermittent connectivity constraints. Wearable IoMT devices must therefore rely on technologies capable of reliably transporting physiological data under dynamic and resource-constrained conditions. LoRaWAN is selected as the communication protocol due to its suitability for battery-powered devices, long-range coverage, and scalability for distributed sensing46. Within the HEAL framework, LoRaWAN serves as the primary layer for transmitting physiological measurements from wearable devices to centralized processing infrastructure, maintaining energy efficiency and operational sustainability.

AI-enhanced LoRaWAN integration

A pre-trained AI model is loaded at simulation startup and executed at the LoRaWAN application server to evaluate robustness across different transmission settings. This enables real-time inference without compromising the low-power requirements of end devices. At each uplink transmission, the sensor nodes acquire physiological measurements, update their local buffers, construct fixed-length input sequences, and transmit the data using standard LoRaWAN protocols. To support AI-driven health prediction, LoRaSimPlus47 is extended by modifying the application server component to host the AI model and perform real-time evaluation of soldiers’ health status upon data reception. The server-side module processes incoming physiological sequences, executes AI predictions, and generates health-status outputs. These enhancements enable a synchronized and predictive health monitoring architecture over LoRaWAN, allowing realistic evaluation of AI-enabled IoMT systems for soldier health monitoring and emergency prediction in operational scenarios.

The LoRaWAN network is configured to reflect realistic IoMT deployment conditions reported in prior studies. The simulation models a single gateway serving 20 LoRa nodes distributed within a 200–1000 m radius, representing a small-to-medium scale monitoring scenario. Each node transmits a 20-byte physiological payload, consistent with healthcare-oriented LoRaWAN applications designed to minimize airtime and energy consumption48. The transmission interval is set to 600 s, following mobility-aware IoT simulation studies applied to soldier health monitoring over LoRaWAN to balance communication overhead and battery longevity22. The simulation runs for one day to evaluate sustained operational behavior.

The network operates in the EU 868 MHz band, widely adopted due to its propagation characteristics and regulatory suitability49, with a transmission power of 14 dBm to balance range and energy efficiency. A bandwidth of 125 kHz is selected as a standard LoRaWAN configuration that provides an effective trade-off between coverage and data rate under narrowband modulation50. Spreading factors SF7–SF12 are used to model heterogeneous link conditions and capture the trade-off between airtime, reliability, and energy consumption51. Consequently, the Python-based simulation generates synthetic time-series data in which HR rises with elevated Temp or reduced SpO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}_2$$\end{document}, and SpO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}_2$$\end{document} varies according to oxygenation and thermal conditions, accurately capturing clinically observed physiological dynamics in soldiers under operational environmental stresses. These clinically established ranges and inter-parameter relationships are used solely to generate synthetic physiological readings for the LoRaWAN simulation, emulating sensor data collected during deployment58. The AI models are trained and evaluated offline using the public dataset and are subsequently applied to the simulated sensor data only at inference time.

LoRaWAN evaluation metrics

To assess the reliability and efficiency of the communication layer within the proposed AI-enabled IoMT framework, the performance of LoRaWAN is evaluated using a set of key metrics, including Packet Delivery Ratio (PDR), collision rate, average throughput, and energy consumption. These metrics quantify the network’s ability to reliably transport physiological data under varying spreading factors (SFs 7–12), transmission distances (200–1000 m), and temporal configurations. Such evaluation is essential in IoMT deployments, where communication reliability and energy efficiency directly influence the accuracy and timeliness of health status prediction.

Packet Delivery Ratio (PDR): PDR represents the fraction of transmitted packets that are successfully received at the gateway and serves as a primary indicator of link reliability. It is influenced by channel conditions, interference, spreading factor selection, and network congestion. PDR is computed as59:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{PDR} = \frac{N_{\text {received}}}{N_{\text {sent}}} \times 100 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\text {received}}$$\end{document} denotes the number of packets successfully received at the gateway and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\text {sent}}$$\end{document} denotes the total number of packets transmitted by the IoMT devices. Higher PDR values indicate more stable connectivity and increased resilience to channel impairments60.

Energy Consumption Energy consumption captures the total transmission energy expended by all IoMT devices during the simulation period and is measured in joules (J). Efficient energy usage is critical for battery-powered IoMT devices to ensure sustained operation in resource-constrained environments61. Following the LoRaSimPlus energy model47, the transmission energy for each packet is computed as:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} E_{\text {tx},i} = P_{\text {tx}} \times T_{\text {air},i} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{\text {tx},i}$$\end{document} is the energy consumed to transmit the i-th packet, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{\text {tx}}$$\end{document} is the transmit power converted from dBm to watts, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{\text {air},i}$$\end{document} is the corresponding time-on-air determined by the LoRaWAN configuration and payload size62.

The total energy consumed during the simulation is then obtained as:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} E_{\text {total}} = \sum _{i=1}^{N} E_{\text {tx},i} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where N denotes the total number of transmitted packets.

To further assess energy efficiency, the average energy consumed per successfully received packet is computed as:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} E_{\text {succ}} = \frac{E_{\text {total}}}{N_{\text {received}}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This metric captures the effective energy cost of reliable data delivery and is particularly relevant for evaluating long-term battery sustainability in IoMT deployments.

Collision Rate Collision rate quantifies the proportion of transmitted packets that experience collisions due to simultaneous channel access, which is inherent to the ALOHA-based medium access mechanism employed by LoRaWAN. Collision rate, defined as the proportion of collided to transmitted packets, is widely used to quantify channel access contention in LoRaWAN performance studies. This metric provides insight into network congestion and medium contention, particularly under higher traffic loads and dense deployment scenarios63. The collision rate is computed as:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{CR} = \frac{N_{\text {collided}}}{N_{\text {sent}}} \times 100 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\text {collided}}$$\end{document} represents the number of packets that collided during transmission. A higher collision rate directly contributes to reduced PDR and throughput, thereby degrading overall network performance60.

Average Throughput Average throughput quantifies the effective data delivery rate of the network and is defined as the ratio of successfully received payload bits to the total simulation duration. This metric reflects the combined impact of packet losses, collisions, and transmission parameters on network capacity. Throughput, computed as the successfully received payload rate over the simulation duration, is a standard performance metric in recent LoRaWAN evaluations64. It is computed in post-processing based on packet reception statistics reported by the simulator as:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{Throughput} = \frac{N_{\text {received}} \times L_{\text {payload}} \times 8}{T_{\text {sim}}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{\text {payload}}$$\end{document} denotes the payload size in bytes and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{\text {sim}}$$\end{document} represents the total simulation time in seconds. This formulation ensures consistency across scenarios and aligns with standard throughput definitions used in LoRaWAN performance evaluations.

Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive characterization of LoRaWAN performance under realistic operational conditions. The analysis offers critical insight into how communication reliability, collision behavior, throughput, and energy efficiency jointly influence the responsiveness and robustness of AI-driven health monitoring systems.

LoRaWAN performance

Within the HEAL framework, communication reliability directly impacts the completeness of input time-series used by the AI models, while energy efficiency determines the operational lifetime of battery-powered sensor nodes. Accordingly, this section evaluates LoRaWAN performance using four complementary metrics: PDR, energy consumption per successfully received packet, collision rate, and throughput. Performance is evaluated for a fixed AI sequence length of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$TS=5$$\end{document} across spreading factors SF7–SF12 and node-to-gateway distances ranging from 200 to 1000 m.

Figure 6 illustrates that PDR, energy consumption per successfully received packet, collision rate, and throughput exhibit strong dependence on both the SF and the node-to-gateway distance. At short distances (200–400 m), PDR remains high across most SFs, indicating stable communication supported by strong received signal strength and limited path loss. In this region, variations in SF have a relatively moderate impact on PDR, particularly at SF7 and SF8, where packet airtime remains short.

Performance evaluation of the LoRaWAN network across different SFs and distances.

At longer distances (800–1000 m), a consistent decline in PDR is observed, especially at higher SFs. Although higher SFs provide increased receiver sensitivity, the associated increase in symbol duration and packet airtime leads to prolonged channel occupancy. This increases exposure to interference and raises the probability of packet overlap, which reduces the effective PDR. Consequently, the sensitivity advantage of high SFs does not fully compensate for the combined effects of extended airtime and distance-induced signal attenuation.

Energy consumption per successfully received packet exhibits a clear increasing trend with both SF and distance. At low-to-mid SFs (SF7–SF8) and short distances, energy per packet is minimized, reflecting short transmission durations and a high probability of successful delivery without retransmissions. As SF increases, packet airtime grows exponentially due to longer symbol durations, directly increasing the energy required for each transmission attempt.

Across all considered distances, energy consumption per successfully received packet remains relatively moderate up to SF11, typically below approximately 20 J. In contrast, SF12 exhibits a sharp increase, with energy consumption rising to approximately 60 J. This escalation is primarily driven by the substantial increase in packet airtime at SF12, which significantly prolongs channel occupancy and raises the energy required per transmission attempt. The extended airtime also increases vulnerability to collisions and unsuccessful receptions, particularly at longer node-to-gateway distances where link quality is reduced. As a result, additional transmission attempts are often required before successful packet delivery, further amplifying energy expenditure. Thus, the steep energy increase at SF12 stems from the combined effects of exponential airtime growth and reduced delivery reliability, rather than higher transmission power.

The collision results provide further insight into the observed PDR and energy trends. At short distances and low SFs, collision rates remain low, indicating efficient channel utilization and limited packet overlap. However, as SF increases, collision rates rise steadily across all distances, with the effect becoming more evident at longer ranges. This increase is attributed to the extended airtime associated with higher SFs, which enlarges the temporal footprint of each packet and raises the probability of concurrent transmissions overlapping at the gateway. While distance does not directly cause collisions, it amplifies their impact by reducing the margin for successful decoding when packets partially overlap or experience interference.

Throughput follows an inverse trend relative to SF and distance. At short distances and low SFs, throughput is maximized due to high PDR and short airtime, enabling efficient delivery of sensor data. As SF increases, throughput decreases because longer airtime reduces the number of packets that can be successfully delivered per unit time. At longer distances, throughput degradation becomes more significant, primarily due to reduced PDR and increased collision rates. Although packets may still be transmitted, a smaller fraction is successfully received, resulting in lower effective data rates. This behavior highlights the trade-off between range extension and data delivery efficiency in LoRaWAN-based monitoring systems.

While all performance metrics degrade with increasing distance, the results indicate that distance alone is not the dominant limiting factor. Instead, distance interacts with SF-dependent airtime and collision behavior to shape overall performance. In practical soldier health monitoring scenarios, wearable nodes are typically deployed within tactical units, where node-to-gateway distances commonly fall within a few hundred meters. In this range, the impact of distance remains moderate and manageable.

The considered node-to-gateway distances of 200–400 m are consistent with LoRaWAN-based soldier health monitoring deployments reported in16, which employ AI-driven solutions over ranges of approximately 200 m. These distances reflect realistic operational scenarios in wearable soldier monitoring, where nodes are carried by personnel operating within tactical units. The proposed HEAL framework extends this range up to 1000 m while maintaining acceptable performance, demonstrating its robustness under extended communication distances. However, within this range, increasing SF can degrade performance due to the exponential growth in packet airtime, which intensifies exposure to channel impairments, interference, and packet collisions. As a result, higher SFs lead to reduced PDR, increased energy consumption per successfully received packet, elevated collision rates, and diminished throughput, highlighting the inherent trade-off between reliability, energy efficiency, and communication range in practical deployments.

In general, the results indicate that the optimal operating region for the HEAL framework lies in the low-to-mid SF range (SF7–SF8) at node-to-gateway distances of 200–400 m, where high PDR, limited collisions, maximum throughput, and efficient energy consumption per successfully received packet are jointly achieved. While communication distances up to 1000 m remain feasible, they incur reduced efficiency due to increased airtime and collision probability. These findings confirm that careful selection of the spreading factor and deployment distance enables HEAL to balance communication reliability, energy efficiency, and data throughput, ensuring dependable delivery of physiological data for AI-driven health monitoring in realistic operational environments.

To ensure realistic deployment under LoRaWAN communication constraints, a LoRaWAN-aware fine-tuning strategy is employed to adapt pretrained temporal models to communication-induced distortions. A two-stage training procedure is adopted, where recurrent layers are initially frozen to retain temporal representations, followed by full model fine-tuning with a reduced learning rate to enable gradual adaptation to noisy and lossy signal characteristics. Class-weighted loss functions are incorporated to improve sensitivity to rare but critical health states. Furthermore, soldier-independent evaluation is enforced to simulate real deployment conditions, where models must generalize to unseen individuals. This fine-tuning approach enhances robustness to packet loss, temporal sparsity, and noise commonly observed in LoRaWAN-based health monitoring systems.

By evaluating these architectures on LoRaWAN-simulated data under best-case wireless conditions, this analysis establishes a conservative baseline for HEAL’s deployment readiness. Practical limitations and future directions

While HEAL demonstrates strong potential for AI-enabled soldier monitoring over LoRaWAN, several limitations must be acknowledged. These constraints define the current scope of the study and motivate key insights for future research toward fully operational IoMT deployments.

LLM-based labeling provides a scalable alternative to manual expert annotation, particularly for large-scale IoMT datasets where clinical labeling is costly and impractical. By reasoning jointly across multiple physiological variables, LLMs enable consistent zero-shot classification without extensive rule engineering. Nevertheless, LLM inference remains probabilistic and may introduce uncertainty or conservative bias near clinical threshold boundaries. Therefore, deterministic validation using rule-based criteria remains essential to constrain predictions and ensure medical reliability. In general, combining LLM-driven annotation with threshold-based validation safeguards offers a balanced strategy that supports both scalability and safety.

The health-status categories considered in this study exhibit class imbalance, which can bias learning toward majority classes. To mitigate this, SMOTE-based oversampling is applied after fixed-length sequence construction, where each training sample corresponds to a complete physiological window. This ensures oversampling occurs at the sequence level rather than across consecutive time steps, thereby avoiding temporal leakage and preserving within-sequence temporal dynamics. Although this strategy improves training stability, synthetic oversampling may not fully capture complex physiological variability. Future work will investigate imbalance-aware methods for sequential IoMT data, including cost-sensitive learning and time-series augmentation strategies that better maintain temporal consistency.

This work adopts several simplifying assumptions to enable a controlled and reproducible simulation-based evaluation of AI-driven monitoring over LoRaWAN. First, parameters such as BP and BG are treated as continuously available signals. In practice, wearable acquisition of BP and BG may require intermittent sensing, estimation, or specialized hardware. Accordingly, SMD represents the most deployment-feasible configuration, whereas FMD is included mainly to explore richer multivariate modeling under idealized sensing assumptions. Second, both the dataset and simulation assume fixed sampling intervals (10-min updates). Real-world monitoring may instead involve irregular or event-triggered sampling due to duty-cycling, energy constraints, and mission-specific requirements. Third, the LoRaWAN model primarily considers uplink-only traffic, without explicitly incorporating downlink feedback, acknowledgments, or closed-loop autonomous control.

While these assumptions are acceptable for establishing a baseline framework, future work will extend HEAL toward more realistic operational settings. Promising directions include event-driven transmission policies, lightweight estimation of intermittently measured parameters, and closed-loop LoRaWAN adaptation with downlink support for urgent alerts and resource allocation in dynamic tactical environments.

Energy consumption results are derived from the LoRaSimPlus transmission energy model under the configured LoRaWAN settings and primarily reflect baseline uplink communication. Additional overhead from confirmed-message retransmissions, downlink acknowledgments, or advanced duty-cycle enforcement is not explicitly modeled beyond simulator defaults. The reported energy values should therefore be interpreted as baseline estimates rather than complete operational power budgets. Future work will incorporate retransmission-aware energy accounting, bidirectional traffic, and duty-cycle scheduling to better reflect deployment-level energy constraints in tactical IoMT environments.

Deploying automated health assessment in military settings demands careful attention to ethical, privacy, and operational concerns. HEAL provides real-time physiological monitoring under LoRaWAN constraints, but it is designed as a decision-support tool rather than an autonomous diagnostic system. Its outputs should assist medical personnel in prioritizing interventions, without influencing punitive or non-medical decisions.

Real-world use requires stringent safeguards for data privacy, security, and governance, given the sensitivity of physiological signals. Secure communication, strict access control, and adherence to medical and defense regulations are essential. Predictions may be affected by uncertainty, bias, or incomplete data, underscoring the need for human oversight, transparency, and ongoing validation. Future work will focus on privacy-preserving learning, explainable AI, and ethical frameworks specifically tailored for defense-oriented IoMT applications.

Conclusion

This study presents the HEAL framework, a scalable and energy-efficient solution for real-time soldier health monitoring in remote and resource-constrained environments. HEAL integrates wearable IoMT sensors with an tailored LoRaWAN communication protocol and advanced AI techniques to enable continuous physiological data acquisition and accurate health state prediction. Transformer-based LLMs perform zero-shot data labeling, reducing manual annotation effort, while DL classifiers, particularly BiLSTM with a sequence length of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$TS=5$$\end{document}, achieve 94% accuracy and 95% Macro F1-score on the public SMD dataset, enabling timely detection of Well, Alarming, and Critical health states. LoRaWAN performance evaluation shows that low SFs (7–8) and moderate transmission distances (200–400 m) maximize PDR (up to 91%) and energy efficiency, indicating that the network reliably supports real-time AI integration for health monitoring in resource-limited environments.

Future work will extend HEAL toward more realistic deployments by developing AI models tailored to LoRaWAN constraints and capable of early prediction of critical physiological deterioration. In addition, integrating additional sensor modalities and refining event-driven communication and downlink-supported adaptation will further improve robustness and scalability. Ongoing efforts in privacy-preserving and explainable AI will also be essential for safe real-world adoption in tactical IoMT environments.
