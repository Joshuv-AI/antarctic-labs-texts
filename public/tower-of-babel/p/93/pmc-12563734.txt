A Study to Evaluate the Relationship between Cardiovascular Disease and Periodontitis
Authors: Pooja Arora, Shinu Singla, B. Radhika, Rajkumar Bajaria, Lipika Gopal, Chhaya Mangla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Periodontitis and cardiovascular disease (CVD) are both chronic inflammatory conditions with growing evidence of a potential link.Objective:This study aimed to assess the relationship between periodontitis and cardiovascular disease by evaluating clinical parameters and systemic inflammatory markers.Methods:A cross-sectional observational study was conducted involving 90 patients aged 35–70 years, divided into three groups: Group A (periodontitis with CVD), Group B (periodontitis only), and Group C (CVD only). Periodontal parameters such as probing pocket depth (PPD), clinical attachment loss (CAL), plaque index (PI), and bleeding on probing (BOP) were recorded.Results:Group A showed the most severe periodontal disease (PPD: 5.2 ± 1.1 mm; CAL: 4.8 ± 0.9 mm) and the highest inflammatory marker levels (CRP: 5.8 mg/L; IL-6: 12.5 pg/mL). Significant correlations were found between CAL and CRP (r = 0.62, P = 0.001), PPD and systolic BP (r = 0.47, P = 0.003), and BOP and IL-6 (r = 0.51, P = 0.002). Group A also had poorer oral hygiene practices and a higher prevalence of CAD (80%).Conclusion:It is concluded that periodontitis is significantly associated with cardiovascular disease, primarily through systemic inflammation.

INTRODUCTION

Cardiovascular disease is one of the leading causes of mortality and morbidity worldwide, representing a significant burden on public health systems. It encompasses a group of disorders involving the heart and blood vessels, including coronary artery disease, heart failure, stroke, and peripheral arterial disease. Medical research demonstrates that hypertension alongside smoking behaviors and dyslipidemia together with diabetes mellitus, obesity and physical inactivity serve as long-standing risk factors that advance cardiovascular outcomes.[1] Increasing medical findings show that chronic inflammatory conditions from outside cardiovascular regions significantly boost the development of these disorders. The medical community shows great interest in studying periodontitis as a condition. Periodontal bacteria cause an infectious inflammatory condition that destroys the teeth’ supportive tissues which involve the gingiva together with the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone.[2] Microbial plaque triggers periodontitis as the disease expands through excessive host immunologic reactions which weakens supporting tissues until the disease eventually causes tooth loss when not treated. Research now directs increased attention toward periodontitis effects on the body system and specifically toward its possible link with cardiovascular disease.[3] Research has confirmed through multiple epidemiological investigations that periodontitis is linked with different types of cardiovascular disease.[4] Patients diagnosed with periodontitis experience increased occurrences of myocardial infarction together with stroke and peripheral vascular disease compared to people without periodontal disease.[5] Many research studies that adjusted for variables including age, smoking habits diabetes and socioeconomic factors still showed a documented link between periodontal disease and cardiovascular issues. The appearing evidence supports the idea that periodontitis exists as a self-sustaining risk factor for cardiovascular disorders. Research on the biological basis of this link exhibits several well-documented pathways.[6]

OBJECTIVE

This study aimed to assess the relationship between periodontitis and cardiovascular disease by evaluating clinical parameters and systemic inflammatory markers.

METHODOLOGY

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted in which a total of 90 adult patients were included.

Inclusion criteria

Patients with confirmed diagnosis of cardiovascular disease by a cardiologist (coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, or stroke history), presence of at least 20 natural teeth, and willingness to provide informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with antibiotic or anti-inflammatory drug use within the past 3 months, presence of systemic diseases other than cardiovascular disease or diabetes, history of periodontal treatment in the last 6 months, and pregnancy or lactation.

Data Collection involved categorizing patients into three groups:

Group A (n = 30): Periodontitis and cardiovascular diseaseGroup B (n = 30): Periodontitis without cardiovascular diseaseGroup C (n = 30): Cardiovascular disease without periodontitis.

Periodontal examination included probing pocket depth (PPD), clinical attachment loss (CAL), bleeding on probing (BOP), and plaque index (PI), assessed by a single calibrated examiner according to the 2017 World Workshop Classification. Cardiovascular assessment was based on medical records and cardiologist consultation, documenting disease type, medication use, blood pressure, lipid profile, and fasting glucose. Venous blood samples were collected to measure inflammatory biomarkers (CRP, IL-6, TNF-α) using standardized ELISA kits. Statistical Analysis was performed using SPSS v26. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables.

RESULTS

A total of 90 participants were included in the study with a mean age of 57.1 ± 8.2 years in Group A, 55.6 ± 8.7 years in Group B, and 56.0 ± 8.3 years in Group C. Group A exhibited the highest periodontal destruction, with a mean probing pocket depth (PPD) of 5.2 ± 1.1 mm and clinical attachment loss (CAL) of 4.8 ± 0.9 mm, compared to Group B (PPD 4.6 ± 0.8 mm, CAL 4.2 ± 0.7 mm) and Group C (PPD 2.1 ± 0.5 mm, CAL 1.8 ± 0.4 mm). Plaque index and bleeding on probing were also highest in Group A (2.7 ± 0.5 and 63%, respectively). Cardiovascular parameters showed higher systolic blood pressure in Groups A (142.3 ± 10.6 mmHg) and C (140.8 ± 9.8 mmHg) compared to Group B (124.2 ± 7.6 mmHg), alongside elevated total cholesterol (Group A: 215 ± 32 mg/dL; Group C: 210 ± 28 mg/dL). Inflammatory markers were significantly raised in Group A, with CRP levels at 5.8 mg/L, IL-6 at 12.5 pg/mL, and TNF-α at 9.3 pg/mL, compared to lower values in Groups B and C [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

The present study evaluated the relationship between periodontitis and cardiovascular disease, with a particular focus on systemic inflammatory markers and clinical correlations. Our findings demonstrated that patients with both periodontitis and cardiovascular disease (Group A) exhibited significantly worse periodontal parameters, including higher probing pocket depth (5.2 ± 1.1 mm) and clinical attachment loss (4.8 ± 0.9 mm), compared to those with either condition alone. The subject data from Group A demonstrated increased inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein to 5.8 mg/L and interleukin-6 to 12.5 pg/mL which strengthened the idea about periodontal and cardiovascular condition connections through inflammation. The analysis of relationship between periodontal destruction and systemic inflammation through CRP levels showed a strong positive association (r = 0.62, P = 0.001).[7] The association between PPD and systolic blood pressure yielded a moderate significant value (r = 0.47, P = 0.003) as did the correlation between BOP and IL-6 (r = 0.51, P = 0.002). Previous research supports the finding that periodontal inflammation shares a causal connection with systemic vascular dysfunction as well as atherogenesis. Healthcare professionals should integrate periodontal health assessment into cardiovascular risk evaluation because coronary artery disease affects 80% of patients in Group A compared to periodontitis-only patients.[8] Scientists associate these two conditions through three key pathophysiological processes which include bacterial invasion along with systemic inflammatory mediator spread and endothelial cell dysfunction. The study does contain certain limitations which need to be considered. The study design as a cross-sectional analysis prevents the identification of causes and effects between variables.

CONCLUSION

It is concluded that there is a significant association between periodontitis and cardiovascular disease, with systemic inflammation acting as a key mediating factor. Patients with both conditions demonstrated more severe periodontal destruction, elevated blood pressure, and higher levels of inflammatory biomarkers such as CRP and IL-6. The observed correlations between periodontal parameters and cardiovascular indicators further support the hypothesis that poor oral health may contribute to cardiovascular risk.
