Impact of SARS-CoV-2 on intrapartum cardiotocography: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Nelson Rabbachin, Maria Breugelmans, Monika Laubach, Gilles Faron, Michel Boulvain, Leonardo Gucciardo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSince the acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 emerged, pregnant women were identified as a vulnerable group. Infection will induce a hypercoagulable state and a proinflammatory response, with a possible cytokine storm. This can be associated with thrombosis and infarction of the placenta. The fetus would have a reactive response to maternal inflammation and fever, reflected in the fetal heart rate changes on the cardiotocography (CTG). Some studies report an increased frequency of CTG abnormalities in pregnant women infected with SARS-CoV-2. The objective of our study was to evaluate frequency of abnormal intrapartum CTG patterns in pregnant infected with SARS-CoV-2.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary hospital center. The exposed group consisted of pregnant women who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2, both symptomatic and asymptomatic were included in the analysis. The control group consisted of randomly selected SARS-CoV-2 negative pregnant women. CTG anomalies were identified according to the physiological interpretation of cardiotocography traces and classified according to the 3-tier system and Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynecologist (RCOG)-system. We compared the frequency of CTG anomalies between SARS-CoV-2 positive pregnant women and controls, and between symptomatic and asymptomatic infections.ResultsWe included 119 pregnant women in the case group, of whom 31 were symptomatic (26%), and 116 in the control group. We found no significant differences in maternal and labor characteristics between groups. Statistical analysis showed no significant differences between cases and controls. Perinatal outcomes were similar in the two groups. Major CTG abnormalities were present in 66% of cases versus 72% of controls. Using the three-tier fetal heart rate system, cases and controls can be divided into category I (39 vs. 36%), category II (50 vs. 55%), and category III (12 vs. 9%). CTG characteristics were similar between SARS-CoV-2 positive asymptomatic and symptomatic pregnant women, with no significant differences in the rate of abnormalities.ConclusionUnlike suggested in earlier case-series, CTG abnormalities do not seem more frequent in mild or moderate maternal SARS-CoV-2 infection. No specific CTG-abnormalities were found in these fetuses.

Background

Pregnant women were identified as a vulnerable group to the acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) since they have been prone to complications and severe disease due to other coronaviruses. The impact on the fetus of maternal infection seems to be limited to the consequences of placental damage, given that the virus does not cross the placenta [1, 2].

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) is caused by a single-stranded RNA virus SARS-CoV-2 [3]. Host cell pyropoptosis will induce a proinflammatory response resulting in inflammation and tissue damage [4, 5] The inflammation caused by SARS-CoV-2 may also result in a cytokine storm, an uncontrolled systemic inflammatory response leading to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and multisystem organ failure. Disease severity is thus related to both viral infection and the host response [5, 6]. Pregnancy related adaptations of the immune system result in an altered immune response. In the general population COVID-19 is associated with high rates of thromboembolic complications. Activation of the coagulation cascade is likely attributed to a cytokine-induced inflammatory response [3, 7]. Since pregnancy can be considered a hypercoagulable state, pregnant women with COVID-19 are at higher risk for thrombosis. Although the mechanism of viral invasion of the placenta has yet to be established, preliminary reports show vascular and thrombotic effects in the placenta of women infected with COVID-19 [1, 8, 9].

The above mechanisms may damage the placenta and impair oxygen transport to the fetus [10]. Cardiotocography (CTG) reflects the activity of the fetal autonomic and somatic nervous system, as well as the oxygenation of the fetal myocardium. Due to hypoxia, the fetus will decrease the myocardial workload to maintain an aerobic metabolism in the myometrium [11].

Maternal hypoxia due to ARDS, cytokine storm and hypercoagulability are associated with thrombosis and infarction of the placental intervillous space [12, 13]. Fetal tachycardia on the CTG may be the result of maternal inflammation and fever [14]. Increased placental metabolism and a hypercoagulable state leading to thrombosis and uteroplacental insufficiency may cause decelerations and acute bradycardia. Inflammatory mediators affecting the fetal nervous system with secondary autonomic instability may cause loss or increase in CTG variability. Irritation of the myometrium would increase contractility and decrease utero-placental oxygen transfer. In addition, severe maternal hypoxia in infected pregnant women may cause a sinusoidal CTG pattern [14–20].

The objective of our study was to evaluate whether maternal SARS-CoV-2 infection was associated with specific intrapartum CTG abnormalities.

Methods

Design and setting

This retrospective study was conducted between March 2020 and January 2022, at the UZ Brussels, a tertiary hospital center in Brussels with approximately 2500 deliveries per year. During this period, all pregnant women had systematic screening by quantitative reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) at hospital admission in the labor ward. We included all pregnant women, both symptomatic and asymptomatic, who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2. Women who delivered before 36 weeks of gestation, those with multifetal pregnancy, breech presentation, and fetal chromosomal or structural anomalies were excluded. Woman scheduled for an elective cesarean section did not undergo CTG monitoring and were therefore excluded. There were no other exclusion criteria, we did not exclude women with maternal complications.

After exclusions were made in the case group, the control group consisted of randomly selected SARS-CoV-2 negative pregnant women, with the same inclusion criteria, who were admitted to labor during the same period. For each SARS-CoV-2 positive woman, a control was selected as the woman who delivered just before or just after the case. Because the date and time are marked on the CTG tracings, if we had systematically included as control the woman who delivered after the case, the status of case or control would have been known to the assessor of the CTG. To ensure blinding in the process of CTG interpretation, we created a computer-generated list (https://www.sealedenvelope.com), to select either the previous or the next delivery.

We collected data on maternal variables including age, weight, height, body mass index (BMI), parity, previous cesarean section and gestational age in weeks. Vaccination was available as from January 2021, and we collected vaccination status. Pregnant women were classified as symptomatic using the clinical criteria included in the WHO classification [19]. The following characteristics of labor and delivery were recorded: spontaneous labor or induction of labor, duration of 1 st and 2nd stage, and mode of delivery. Neonatal outcomes included APGAR-score at 5 min < 7, umbilical cord arterial pH < 7.05, admission in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) and need for resuscitation at birth.

CTG-analysis

Upon arrival at the labor ward until delivery, the fetal heart rate (FHR) and uterine contractions were continuously recorded, using either external Doppler ultrasound transducers on the maternal abdomen or a fetal scalp electrode. All CTG traces were recorded electronically using MOSOS CTG monitoring and an archiving software (BMA Healthcare Solutions, Houten, The Netherlands). The first 30 anonymized CTG were interpreted by two investigators from our obstetrics department, to assess interobserver variability. The agreement was very good (kappa 0.7 to 1.0 depending the anomaly), thus the remaining CTGs were analyzed by only one staff member. CTG features were classified according to the physiological interpretation of cardiotocography traces, as defined by Chandraharan [14]. We analyzed the CTG abnormalities suggested to be associated with COVID by Garcia-Perez-Bonfils et al. [18] The CTG abnormalities were also classified according to RCOG-classification system and the 3-tier system.20–21−22 Finally, we also defined major and minor abnormalities according to their likely impact on fetal wellbeing. We considered major CTG abnormalities as fetal tachycardia, acute fetal bradycardia, a single prolonged deceleration (> 30 bpm drop for > 3 min) and a persistent sinusoidal pattern. Minor abnormalities included increase in fetal heart rate > 10% from initial baseline, increased or decreased variability, loss of cycling, late or shallow decelerations with delayed return to baseline, atypical variable decelerations and excessive uterine activity. We classified abnormalities in the last hour before delivery as rare (< 10 min), infrequent (10–30 min) or frequent (> 30 min). The analysis of the CTG was not in any core outcome set.

Statistical analysis

We compared the frequency of CTG abnormalities between SARS-CoV-2 positive women and controls, and between symptomatic and asymptomatic infections. Statistical analysis was performed using STATA® version 17. The Fisher exact test was used to test the differences in categorical variables, while the Student t-test was used for continuous variables. An adjusted analysis was performed using logistic regression. A p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

From March 2020 to January 2022, 190 pregnant women tested positive for COVID-19 (Fig. 1). After exclusions, 119 women, of whom 31 symptomatic (26%), were included in the case group. As defined by the WHO-classification [19]there were only mild and moderate cases in our study. The randomly chosen control group included 116 pregnant women. There were no significant differences in parity, history of cesarean section, gestational age, maternal age, weight, height, and BMI between the groups. Vaccination rates were similar in the two groups (Table 1). In the unvaccinated group, 28 women were symptomatic (29%), compared to 3 (14%) in the vaccinated group (OR 0.42; 95% CI, 0.11–1.53; P = 0.14). There were no significant differences in induction of labor, duration of labor and mode of delivery between the two groups (Table 1). There was one case of arterial umbilical cord pH < 7.05 in the control group and one case of APGAR < 5 at 5 min in the case group. Unexpected NICU admission was noted in 4 cases (3%) in the case group, compared to 1 (1%) in the control group. No neonatal life support was required in either group. Adjusting for gestational age and parity did not modify these results.

Discussion

Main findings

We found no association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and CTG abnormalities. Although the proposed mechanisms behind CTG changes in pregnant women infected with SARS-CoV-2 appear to be logical, we were unable to detect specific patterns, or a significant increase in the frequency of CTG abnormalities [16, 18, 20]. This may have a significant clinical impact, as the indications for additional testing to assess fetal well-being and/or urgent operative delivery may not be modified for women with SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Interpretation in the light of other evidence

In a retrospective analysis, Garcia-Perez-Bonfils et al. [18] examined the CTG readings of 12 pregnant women symptomatic for COVID. In contrast to our results, all 12 cases showed an increase in baseline FHR of more than 10%, absence of accelerations and excessive uterine activity. The majority of cases also displayed late or prolonged decelerations and absence of cycling. The authors suggested that these changes were due to maternal inflammation rather than fetal hypoxia and raised concerns about the potential for unnecessary operative interventions when following the International Physiological CTG Guidelines. A limitation was that only 12 cases were included, because the study was conducted during the first months of the pandemic.

Sinaci et al. [16] analyzed the relationship between COVID severity and CTG characteristics in 224 symptomatic pregnant women without a control group. CTG’s were both antenatal and intrapartum. They reported a high frequency of elevated baseline FHR, absent or increased variability, decelerations, and myometrial irritability. No association was found between CTG abnormalities and disease severity in pregnant women with COVID when using the tree-level system [22].

Fahran et al. [23] conducted a study comparing CTG anomalies in 30 pregnant women infected with SARS-CoV-2 to those in 60 uninfected women. They showed a higher frequency of abnormal CTG patterns in SARS-CoV-2 infected women, compared to non-infected women. Specifically, fetal tachycardia was present in 27%, while reduced variability in 23% of the cases. These abnormal patterns were almost absent in the control group. In contrast with our study, all CTG abnormalities were significantly more frequent compared to a SARS-CoV-2 negative control group, despite all cases exhibiting only mild or moderate symptoms.

The presence of CTG abnormalities in the final hour before delivery was not different than in COVID negative controls. The other available studies did not address this outcome.

Regarding route of delivery, Garcia-Perez-Bonfils et al. provided no data [18]. Sinaci et al. report a high frequency of cesarean Sect. (65%), of which 31% for fetal distress [16]. Farhan et al. showed a higher frequency of cesarean Sect. (70%) in the case group compared to the control group (53%), and distress was the indication in 33% and 16% respectively.22 We did not find any difference in delivery route in our study.

Consistent with our findings, favorable neonatal outcomes were reported in the available literature. Garcia-Perez-Bonfils et al. showed normal Apgar scores at 5 min and no evidence of metabolic acidosis [18]. Sinaci et al. and Farhan et al. reported that only 4% and 7% of neonates, respectively, had an Apgar score less than 7 at 5 min [16, 23]. These results suggest that usual obstetric care should be provided during labor, regardless of SARS-CoV-2 status, except when the maternal condition mandates interventions. However, Farhan et al. reported 10% of neonatal mortality [23].

Strengths and limitations

The two previous studies reporting CTG anomalies in SARS-CoV-2 positive pregnant women were uncontrolled case series and lacked blinding in assessing the CTG traces. Only one study used a control group, but without blinding the CTG analysis. In our study, we addressed these limitations by including a randomly selected control group and blinding the assessment of the CTG.

While including a relatively large sample size, our study may not have had sufficient power to detect differences in rare events, such as neonatal unfavorable outcomes. In particular, we included only mild and moderate cases in our study, which limits the generalization in cases with a severe COVID-19. During that period, there were no pregnant women with severe COVID admitted for delivery outside the labor ward (e.g. the intensive care unit).

The effects of vaccination on the CTG cannot be determined due to the small number of vaccinated pregnant women.

Other maternal diseases may cause uteroplacental insufficiency. The number of cases was too small to analyze the effect of SARS-CoV-2 in specific subgroups, as hypertension and diabetes. No systematic analysis of the placental pathology has been performed.

Conclusion

In contrast with earlier studies, we did not show CTG abnormalities specific to a maternal SARS-CoV-2 infection. This highlights the importance of comparing cases with a valid control group, when evaluating the association between an exposure (here SARS-CoV-2) and an outcome (here CTG abnormalities). As we have not included pregnant women with severe SARS-CoV-2 disease, we cannot generalize the results to this subgroup.
