Oral health, orofacial pain and dysfunction, and orofacial trauma in athletes: a scoping review
Authors: G Bousché, F Lobbezoo, R de Vries, M C Verhoeff
Published: 2026

Background

According to the World Health Organization, oral health is an important key element of overall health, quality of life, and well-being [1]. So far, the link between oral health and sports has been mainly investigated by means of studies focused on maxillofacial and/or dental trauma. In addition, Needleman et al. [2] found poor oral health amongst athletes, including dental erosion, caries, and periodontal diseases. Insufficient oral health can induce a systemic inflammatory response and may reduce the quality of life [3, 4]. Because it is well-known that physical and mental health influence sports performance, [2, 5–7] it can be assumed that poor oral health could affect athletic performance [8]. According to Gallagher et al. [6], 30% of the performance impact is caused by oral pain.

Orofacial pain is defined as “a frequent form of pain perceived in the face and/or oral cavity”. It consists of different types of pain syndromes and/or disorders [9]. The most common acute form of orofacial pain is dental pain [10]. Besides, chronic orofacial pain commonly arises from musculoskeletal pain, which is known as temporomandibular disorders (TMD). TMD is the most prevalent cause of non-dental orofacial pain [11, 12]. TMD can be described as a group of musculoskeletal and neuromuscular conditions that may affect the temporomandibular joint, the masticatory muscles, and adjacent structures [13]. Symptoms of TMD can include pain in the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) and/or the masticatory muscles, TMJ noises (i.e., crepitus and/or clicking) during function, and limited jaw movements. [13] The prevalence of TMD is estimated between 3 and 11% of the general population [14], with the majority of sufferers being women [15]. Although not fully elucidated, the aetiology of TMD is complex and multi-factorial, including bruxism [16–18]. Bruxism is defined as “a repetitive jaw-muscle activity characterized by clenching or grinding of the teeth and/or by bracing or thrusting of the mandible” and can occur during sleep (indicated as sleep bruxism) or during wakefulness (indicated as awake bruxism) [19]. Sleep bruxism is defined as “a masticatory muscle activity during sleep that is characterised as rhythmic (phasic) or non-rhythmic (tonic) and is not a movement disorder or a sleep disorder”. Awake bruxism is defined as “a masticatory muscle activity during wakefulness that is characterised by repetitive or sustained tooth contact and/or by bracing or thrusting of the mandible and is not a movement disorder” [20].

Only a few studies have been conducted on the prevalence of TMD in athletes. Two studies found a higher prevalence of TMD pain in amateur and professional athletes compared to controls [21, 22].

One case–control study found a significantly higher prevalence of TMJ noises (crepitus and clicking sounds) in amateur athletes compared to controls [21]. Besides, two studies described the prevalence of bruxism in athletes. Gay-Escoda et al. [23] found a prevalence of 30% in professional soccer athletes, and Macêdo-Filho et al. [24] found a prevalence of 5% in amateur jiujitsu athletes.

In this scoping review, we aimed to provide an extensive overview of the available literature on (i) oral health, (ii) orofacial pain and dysfunction, and (iii) maxillofacial and/or dental trauma in athletes, including their possible associated factors and the impact of oral health and orofacial pain and dysfunction on sport performances and quality of life.

Materials and methods

To identify the relevant publications, we conducted systematic searches in the bibliographic databases PubMed, Embase.com, APA PsycINFO (Ebsco), and SPORTDiscus (Ebsco) from inception up to May 13, 2025, in collaboration with a medical information specialist. To optimize the precision and sensitivity of the search, we developed a search strategy based on the 2020 PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic.

Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [25]. The following terms were used (including synonyms and closely related words) as index terms or free-text words: "Oral health", "Dental health", "Pain", "Dysfunction", "Performance", "Sports", "Athletes". The references of the identified articles were searched for relevant publications. All languages were accepted. Duplicate articles were excluded by a medical information specialist using Endnote X20.0.1 (Clarivatetm), following the Amsterdam Efficient Deduplication (AED) method [26] and the Bramer method [27]. The search strategy and the PRISMA checklist can be found in the additional files 1 and 2.

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (i) all studies about athletes and sports; (ii) information on oral health-related factors (viz., oral hygiene, periodontal problems, dental caries, dental erosion, painful TMD, TMD dysfunction, bruxism, muscle output, soft dental tissue trauma, hard dental tissue trauma, trauma related to the TMJ, miscellaneous orofacial trauma, sports performance, and quality of life); (iii) cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, case–control studies, case-reports, qualitative studies, and randomized clinical trials; and (iv) no language restrictions. In addition, we excluded studies based on the following criteria: (i) studies based on an intervention with a medical device, such as the use of mouthguards; (ii) studies with athletes younger than 16 years old (to ensure homogeneity in the study population); (iii) the full text could not be retrieved, or it was not available; and (iv) publication types that did not yield original data (e.g., (systematic) reviews of the literature and editorials).

Three reviewers (viz., GB and two master’s students in dentistry) independently screened all potentially relevant titles and abstracts for eligibility after removing duplicates. Upon completion of the screening, one reviewer (GB) independently screened the full-text articles of the included abstracts. Disagreements or doubts were resolved through a consensus procedure with a third researcher (MV).

All included studies were analyzed using descriptive statistics, mainly percentages and means, including the p-value.

Studies were categorized into (i) oral health (viz., oral hygiene, periodontal problems, dental caries, and dental erosion); (ii) orofacial pain and dysfunction (viz., TMD pain, TMD dysfunction, bruxism, and muscle output); (ii) maxillofacial and/or dental trauma (viz., soft dental tissue trauma, hard dental tissue trauma, trauma related to the TMJ, and miscellaneous orofacial trauma); and (iv) sports performance and quality of life.

Miscellaneous orofacial trauma is described as trauma to the orofacial region other than soft dental tissue trauma, hard dental tissue trauma, and trauma related to the TMJ.

Athletes were divided into (i) Olympic or Paralympic athletes: athletes on podium potential and/or into placement programs for the Olympics; (ii) professional athletes: athletes with an A-status (i.e., a global top 8 performance during the Olympic Games, Paralympic Games, World Cup, or another international top sporting event that is equal to a World Cup in terms of field of participants and competition provided (Dutch Olympic Committee * Dutch Sports Federation); terms as ‘A1 league athletes’, ‘Elite 1, Elite 2, and national 1 athletes’, ‘elite athletes’, ‘high-performance athletes’, ‘collegiate athletes’, ‘national league athletes’, ‘first league athletes’, and athletes performing at the Pan American Games and at the Central American and Caribbean Sports Games were categorized as professional athletes; and (iii) amateur athletes: these athletes were divided into competitive athletes (viz., athletes performing during competition) and recreational athletes (viz., athletes who did not perform during competition). Terms as ‘A2 league athletes’, ‘semi-professional athletes’, ‘senior and high school student-athletes’, and ‘student-athletes’ were categorized as amateur athletes. The term ‘non-competitive athletes’ was categorized as recreational athletes. Controls were described as non-athletes (e.g., ‘non-athletic’) or as a group of athletes with a specific sport to compare results. In addition, studies that described 6 or more different types of sports or did not specify the type of sport were categorized as ‘wide variety of sports’. Furthermore, the term ‘oral examination’ was used as a synonym for the following terms: ‘dental screening’, ‘directed examination’, ‘oral health examination’, ‘dental screening’, ‘oral health assessment’, ‘standardized clinical examination’, ‘oral health screening’, ‘clinical examination(s)’, ‘comprehensive assessment’, ‘dental status’, ‘physical examination’, ‘dental clinical examinations’, ‘clinical dental screening’, and ‘functional examination’. The term ‘periodontal examination’ was used as a synonym for ‘periodontal status’. In addition, the term ‘self-reported questionnaire’ was used when the questionnaire was not specified or was not based on a validated questionnaire. Finally, the term ‘history’ was used as a synonym for the terms ‘interview’, ‘history assessment’, ‘data collection’, and ‘record history’.

Results

The literature search (conducted on May 13, 2025) generated a total of 4520 references: 1508 in PubMed, 1719 in Embase.com, 116 in APA PsycINFO, and 1177 in SPORTDiscus. After removing duplicates of references that were selected from more than one database, 2842 references remained. Following the study-selection procedure, 55 studies performed in 23 different countries and published between 1988 and 2025 were included: 40 cross-sectional studies, nine case–control studies, four cohort studies, one RCT, and one descriptive epidemiological study. The flow diagram of the search and selection process (Additional file 3) and the characteristics of all included studies (Additional file 4) can be found in the additional files.

Oral health

Oral hygiene

Of the eleven studies that reported on oral hygiene [2, 23, 28–36], five studies described dental and/or dental hygiene consultation within 12 months [2, 28, 31, 32, 35]. The prevalence of dental and/or dental hygiene consultation varied between 45 and 81% of the Olympic, Paralympic, professional, and amateur athletes. Furthermore, Minty et al. [34] found a significantly lower frequency of dental consultations in the professional rugby athlete group compared to controls (p = 0.035). Moreover, 60–94% of the Olympic, Paralympic, and professional athletes brushed their teeth ≥ 2 times per day [23, 29, 32]. Besides, Solleveld et al. [36] found a positive association between poor oral health and age (e.g., older respondents reported more types of oral health problems) in professional soccer athletes, but the results were not based on a validated questionnaire.

Dental caries

In total, twelve studies described dental caries in athletes [2, 6, 23, 30–34, 37–40]. Of these, two studies used the International Caries Detection and Assessment (ICDAS) (-II) classification [6, 39]. Frese et al. [39] found a mean of decayed surfaces of 17.04 ± 6.09 in amateur athletes. Besides, Gallagher et al. [6] found a higher prevalence of ICDAS ≥ 1 (i.e., initial stage decay) in Olympic and professional athletes who perform in mixed sports (56%) than in endurance sports (38.5%). Also, 49% had a carious lesion with an ICDAS code ≥ 3 (i.e., moderate decay) in at least one tooth. Furthermore, four studies described dental caries based on the Decayed, Missing, and Filled Teeth index (DMFT) [23, 31, 32, 34]. Gay-Escoda et al. [23] found a mean DMFT of 5.7 ± 4.1 in professional soccer athletes. Besides, Kragt et al. [32] found a prevalence of 20% of dental caries in the Olympic and Paralympic athletes.

Of these, 15% had a DMFT-score > 10, which indicates poor oral health. Also, Minty et al. [34] found that the DMFT index was significantly higher in professional athletes compared to controls (p = 0.01). Moreover, Medeiros et al. [40] found Non-Carious Cervical Lesions (NCCL’s) in 40% of the amateur athletes.

Dental erosion

Of the seven studies that examined dental erosion [2, 6, 31, 32, 35, 38, 41], six studies described their results based on the Basic Erosive Wear Examination (BEWE) index [2, 6, 31, 32, 35, 38]. The prevalence of dental erosion varied between 1.8% and 53% of the Olympic, professional, and amateur athletes [2, 6, 35, 41]. Besides, Needleman et al. [35] found a severity from moderate to severe in 21% of anterior and posterior teeth in professional athletes. Also, Gallagher et al. [6] found a higher prevalence of a BEWE score ≥ 7 (i.e., wear greater than normal physiological wear) in Olympic and professional male athletes (49%) compared to women athletes (28%). Furthermore, Needleman et al. [2] found a positive association between the frequency of the usage of sports drinks and erosion on anterior teeth (p = 0.036) in Olympic athletes.

On the contrary, Antunes et al.40 and Needleman et al.34 found no association between sports drink usage and dental erosion in amateur and professional athletes. Finally, a significant positive association was found between gastroesophageal reflux and erosion (P = 0.05) in amateur athletes [41].

Orofacial pain and dysfunction

TMD pain

In total, eight studies analyzed TMD pain in amateur and professional athletes [21–24, 42–45]. Out of these, three studies included a control group [21, 22, 43]. Crincoli et al. [21] found that amateur athletes had significantly more muscle pain (p < 0.01), myofascial pain (p = 0.03), and myofascial pain while chewing (p = 0.002) compared to controls.

Also, Mendoza-Puente et al. [22] found a moderate occurrence of TMD in professional boxing athletes compared to no occurrence in the control group. On the contrary, Freiwald et al. [43] found a higher prevalence of TMD symptoms in the control group (61%) compared to amateur athletes (53%).

Furthermore, Bonotto et al. [42] found a significantly higher prevalence of TMD and myofascial pain in professional karate-do and Mixed Martial Arts (MMA) athletes (p = 0.002) compared to amateur athletes (p = 0.045).

Besides, a cross-sectional study in amateur athletes found a higher positive TMD screening in competitive athletes (46%) compared to recreational athletes (25%) [45].

TMD dysfunction

Four studies described TMD dysfunction in amateur and professional athletes [21, 23, 42, 43]. Only two studies included a control group [21, 43]. Crincoli et al. [21] found significantly more closing clicks and crepitus in amateur athletes compared to the control group (p = 0.00; p = 0.02). They also described more mandibular deviations in amateur athletes (50%) compared to the control group (20%). Besides, a cross-sectional study in karate-do and MMA athletes found a significantly higher prevalence of disc displacement in professional athletes compared to amateur athletes (p = 0.012) [42]. Furthermore, Freiwald et al. [43] described an almost similar prevalence of grating or clicking noises from the temporomandibular joints in the control group (41%) and the competitive (37%) and recreational (37%) amateur athletes.

Bruxism and muscle output

Three studies analyzed bruxism and/or muscle output in amateur and professional athletes [23, 24, 46]. One study included a control group [46]. They found that amateur athletes showed significantly higher bioelectrical activities of the masseter muscle during clenching in maximum intercuspation (p = 0.002) and during maximum voluntary clenching on cotton rolls (p = 0.001) compared to the control group [46]. Besides, Gay-Escoda et al. [23] described a prevalence of 30% of bruxism in professional soccer athletes. Furthermore, Macêdo-Filho et al. [24] found that 5% of the amateur jiujitsu athletes had a presumptive diagnosis of bruxism.

Performance and quality of life

In total, seven studies described the impact of oral health on sports performance and/or on the quality of life in Olympic, Paralympic, and professional athletes [2, 6, 31, 32, 35, 36, 38]. Out of these, four studies reported the impact of oral health on sports performance [2, 6, 31, 35]. Gallagher et al. [6] found that 32% of the Olympic and professional athletes experienced one or more oral symptoms that had an impact on their performance within the previous 12 months (e.g., 30% oral pain, 9% difficulty participating in training and competition, 6% affecting performance, 4% reduced training volume). Besides, Needleman et al. [2, 35] described an impact of oral health on sports performance of 18% in Olympic athletes and 7% in professional athletes.

In both studies, they found a statistically significant negative association between dental caries and the self-reported impact on training and sports performance (p < 0.001; p = 0.007).

Furthermore, Gallagher et al.6 and Needleman et al. [35] both found an association between self-reported oral health problems and impacts on well-being or sports performance (e.g., pain related to teeth, tooth sensitivity to cold or hot, history of wisdom tooth swelling or infection). The impact of oral health on quality of life in athletes is reported in four studies [6, 32, 36, 38]. Gallagher et al. [6] found that 49% of the Olympic and professional athletes reported one or more psychosocial impacts within the previous 12 months (e.g., 35% difficulty eating or drinking, 17% laughing, smiling, or showing teeth without embarrassment, and 15% difficulty relaxing). Also, Kragt et al. [32] described that 27% of the Olympic and Paralympic athletes reported that their oral health impacted their quality of life. Moreover, Solleveld et al. [36] found a positive association between poor oral health and age (i.e., older athletes reported more types of oral health problems). They also found a positive association between psychophysical stress and dissatisfaction with the trainer or team, and injury anxiety.

Maxillofacial and/or dental trauma

Soft dental tissue trauma

In total, twenty studies reported soft dental tissue trauma in Olympic, professional, and amateur athletes [2, 23, 31, 37, 44, 47–61]. Out of these, eleven articles reported a prevalence of facial or soft tissue lacerations. This prevalence varied between 0.9% and 92% of the Olympic, professional, and amateur athletes [2, 23, 31, 48, 51–53, 55, 58–60]. The most common sites affected by facial or soft tissue lacerations were the lips, cheeks, and tongue [2, 31, 37, 44, 47, 48, 52, 53, 57, 58]. Furthermore, Seifert et al. [58] reported an incidence of soft tissue lacerations of 92% in professional athletes compared to 9% in amateur athletes.

Hard dental tissue trauma

In total, thirty-two studies reported hard dental tissue trauma in Olympic, professional, and amateur athletes [2, 23, 31, 36, 37, 44, 48–56, 58–74]. Out of these, eighteen studies reported a prevalence of hard dental tissue trauma. This prevalence varied between 2.1% and 81% of the Olympic, professional, and amateur athletes [31, 44, 48, 49, 52, 53, 58–61, 64, 66, 69–72, 74]. The most commonly reported hard dental tissue trauma was a fracture of the maxillary central incisors, with a prevalence varying from 22 to 77% [23, 48, 53, 62, 64, 73]. Furthermore, avulsion, luxation, and dislocation were also commonly reported dental hard tissue trauma [51, 52, 55, 58, 59, 61, 67, 69, 70].

Hard dental tissue trauma was mostly treated by dentists in 19.2% and 75% of the cases, and between 12 and 25% by a medical doctor or in a hospital [48, 52, 58]. Moreover, Ilia et al. [52] reported a prevalence of dental hard tissue trauma in 76% during competition compared to 25% during training in amateur rugby athletes.

TMJ-related trauma

In total, nine studies analyzed trauma to the TMJ in amateur and professional athletes [23, 44, 52, 53, 57, 59, 61, 70, 75]. Overall, two studies reported an incidence of trauma directly to the TMJ of 27% in amateur soccer athletes and 40% in professional soccer athletes [23, 75]. Out of the seven studies, four reported dislocations of the mandible [52, 59, 61, 70].

Ilia et al. [52] reported a mandible dislocation in 4% of the amateur rugby athletes, and Shirani et al. [59] reported an incidence of 7% in amateurs and professional martial arts athletes. Besides, Máxen et al. [70] reported an incidence of 5 dislocations of the mandible in amateur floorball athletes, and Yamada et al. [61] reported an incidence of 11 dislocations of the mandible in amateur soccer players and 5 in amateur rugby players. Furthermore, Scoggin et al. [57] reported 1 TMJ sprain in the professional MMA athletes, while Máxen et al. [70] reported 6 TMJ injuries (5 dislocations, 1 contusion) in amateur floorball athletes.

Miscellaneous orofacial trauma

Thirteen studies described miscellaneous orofacial trauma in amateur and professional athletes [37, 48–52, 57, 59, 64, 70, 71, 74, 75]. The incidence of miscellaneous orofacial trauma varied between 5 and 79% in amateur and professional athletes [45, 48–50, 59]. The most commonly described miscellaneous orofacial trauma are bone fractures (viz., mandible, nose, orbital and zygoma fractures) [48–52, 57, 59, 74, 75] and injuries to the eye and mouth [74]. Besides, Ilia et al. [52] found an incidence of orofacial injury of 76% during competition and 24% during training in amateur rugby athletes. Tozoglu et al. [75] also reported more injuries caused by an impact against another soccer player (64%) compared to an impact against equipment or the ground (18%). Furthermore, Shirani et al. [59] reported more maxillofacial injuries in professional MMA-athletes (86%) compared to amateur martial arts athletes (42%).

Discussion

In this scoping review, we aimed to provide an extensive overview of the available literature on (i) oral health, (ii) orofacial pain and dysfunction, and (iii) maxillofacial and/or dental trauma in athletes, including the possible associated factors and the impact of oral health and orofacial pain and dysfunction on sport performances and quality of life. Further, the aim was to generate hypotheses for future research regarding this topic. In total, 55 studies were included. Studies focusing on oral health found a prevalence of 20% of dental caries in Olympic and Paralympic athletes. Soft tissue lacerations and fractures of the maxillary central incisors were the most commonly reported maxillofacial and/or dental trauma.

Findings from this scoping review suggest that Olympic, Paralympic, professional, and amateur athletes exhibit a high prevalence of oral health conditions, such as dental caries [6, 32, 40] and dental erosion. [2, 6, 35, 41] These findings indicate poor oral health, which is not a new finding in athletes [76]. In the literature, various possible causes for poor oral health are described, such as dietary habits [77], decreased salivary flow during exercise [78], and the level of knowledge and beliefs related to oral health [2].

It is noteworthy that oral health issues, like dental caries and dental erosion, are preventable through appropriate oral hygiene practices and routine dental care.

Besides, the effects of poor oral health on athletes in later life are unknown. These oral diseases may result in substantial consequences, including extensive dental treatments, tooth loss, decreased oral function, and adverse psychological effects. Since life expectancy is growing in the global population, it is of importance to address that poor oral health in older adults is associated with physical frailty [79], cognitive impairment [80], sarcopenia [81], age-associated physiological burdens, and accumulating multimorbidity [82]. Given that potential professional and Olympic athletes start their training during early childhood or adolescence, the integration of oral health promotion at the onset of their athletic careers is of importance to prevent oral disease in later life. Consequently, the prevention of oral diseases and the promotion of oral health should be recognized as integral components of the healthcare framework for athletes.

Oral hygiene can be described as maintaining the cleanliness of the oral cavity through regular personal and professional care to promote oral health and prevent oral diseases. Athletes often tend to focus more on maintaining and/or improving physical fitness and health [83], while ignoring other aspects of health, such as oral health [8]. Regarding the findings of this scoping review, 60–94% of the Olympic, Paralympic, and professional athletes brushed their teeth ≥ 2 times per day [23, 29, 32]. Besides, 45–81% of the Olympic, Paralympic, professional, and amateur athletes had a dental and/or dental hygiene consultation within 12 months [2, 28, 32, 35]. Despite these findings, 32% of Olympic and professional athletes reported one or more oral symptoms (e.g., oral pain, bleeding when cleaning teeth) that negatively affected their performance within the preceding 12 months. The most common one was difficulty participating in training and competition (9.0%) [6]. Oral diseases and conditions have been shown to affect athletic performance and physical fitness [5, 6]. Ashley et al. [5] described that an inflammation like gingivitis or the presence of an oral disease might affect the physical immunity of athletes and, therefore, negatively influence their performance. Specifically in endurance athletes (e.g., triathletes), intense exercise can lead to immunosuppression, which alters saliva composition and therefore further increases the risk of poor oral health [84]. Regular dental and/or dental hygiene screening and the use of effective oral health care programs may minimize performance impacts from poor oral health.

Oral health contains psychological dimensions such as well-being, self-confidence, and the ability to socialize and work without pain, embarrassment, and discomfort [85]. Oral diseases have consequences that can affect various aspects of life and impair quality of life [86]. For example, caries and periodontitis can have a negative effect on pain, oral function, and appearance [87]. In addition, Gallagher et al. [6] found that 49% of the Olympic and professional athletes reported one or more psychosocial impacts within the 12 months. The most common ones were difficulty eating or drinking (35%), smiling, laughing, or showing teeth without embarrassment (17%). Besides, Solleveld et al. [36] found a positive association between psychophysical stress and dissatisfaction with the trainer or team. On the other hand, stress is also a risk factor for sports injuries. The “stress-injury” model of Williams and Andersen [88] suggests that stress is associated with an increased risk of injury, and is directly, as well as indirectly, influenced by personality traits and coping mechanisms. Since the included studies did not describe or clarify this model, the explanation relies on literature beyond the scope of this review.

This model hypothesizes that individuals possessing personality traits that intensify stress responses, especially those with a history of stressors and inadequate coping resources, are more vulnerable to sports injuries because they are more likely to perceive novel or competitive situations as threatening.

This can lead to heightened physiological arousal and attentional disruptions, thereby increasing the likelihood of (re)injuries. During the stress response, muscle tension, distractibility, and perceptual narrowing have been implicated as key mechanisms contributing to increased injury risk [89]. Besides, individuals with chronic TMD reported higher mean levels of psychosocial stress compared with pain-free individuals [90]. Several cross-sectional studies found that psychological distress was positively correlated with reported TMD pain and pain-related disability [91–93]. In conclusion, comprehensive dental and/or dental hygiene screening, and more awareness regarding the psychological dimensions can also contribute to the prevention of (re)injuries.

A strength of this study is that it provides a comprehensive overview of oral health status, orofacial pain and dysfunction, and maxillofacial and/or dental trauma prevalence among athletes. In addition, to maximize both precision and sensitivity, we developed a search strategy in accordance with the 2020 PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines (additional file 2) [25]. This study also has several limitations. First, the included studies used different methodologies and outcome measures. Therefore, a meta-analysis could not be performed, and the results should thus be interpreted with caution. In addition, due to the design of the included studies, causal inferences could not be established. Future research should therefore adopt longitudinal designs that will enable drawing causal conclusions. Second, the reviewed studies used heterogeneous terminology when referring to maxillofacial and/or dental trauma. Terms used to describe maxillofacial trauma included ‘head injury,’ ‘orofacial injury,’ ‘facial/dental trauma,’ and ‘facial injuries.’ In contrast, terminology for dental hard tissue trauma included ‘oral injury,’ ‘dental trauma,’ ‘dental injury,’ and ‘orofacial injury.’ Therefore, future studies should aim to establish consensus on terminology, particularly concerning orofacial trauma and hard dental tissue injuries. Third, a limitation of this study is the use of different self-administered questionnaires in the studies. Most studies did not clearly report the instrument used for the self-administered questionnaire. Besides, a limitation of questionnaire-based research designs is that respondents tend to give positive answers when answering questions on satisfaction [94]. In addition, respondents tend to give socially desirable answers, where undesirable answers can be under-reported [95]. Therefore, the use of validated questionnaires is recommended for future questionnaire-based research designs.

Fourth, the studies examined a broad range of sports, varying from strength-based sports (e.g., powerlifting, wrestling) and endurance-based sports (e.g., cycling, triathlon) to a combination of strength- and endurance-based sports (e.g., soccer, rugby). Therefore, a meta-analysis could not be performed, and the results could not be categorized or distinguished across the different sports.

A recommendation for future research could be to categorize sports into strength, endurance, and mixed (strength and endurance) sports to obtain more specific insights into oral health and orofacial pain and dysfunction in athletes. In addition, the population in the included articles varied from amateur (recreational and competitive) and professional athletes to Olympic and Paralympic athletes. The distinction between professional and Olympic athletes was not always conclusive in the articles. Moreover, the search strategy was not limited to one country, which could be indicative of the variation in terminology of the level of athletes.

Besides, various terms were used for professional athletes (viz., ‘elite athletes’, ‘high-performance athletes’, ‘collegiate athletes’, and athletes performing at the Pan American Games). Therefore, a meta-analysis could not be performed, and the results could not be categorized or distinguished across the different athletes’ levels. Fifth, although this scoping review did not include a quality assessment, the majority of the included studies were hampered by various sorts of bias (e.g., small sample size, study design, and the lack of validated questionnaires). Thus, the outcomes of the articles might not represent the oral health and orofacial pain for all athletes. A recommendation for further research could be a comprehensive epidemiological study with a representative sample of all types of athletes.

Conclusion

This scoping review showed poor oral health among Olympic, Paralympic, professional, and amateur athletes, especially regarding the prevalence of dental caries and poor oral hygiene. Oral diseases are associated with a decline in sports performance and an impaired quality of life. On the other hand, stress responses influenced by personality traits and coping mechanisms can also be a possible risk factor for (re)injuries.
