Optimizing Middle-Phase Microemulsions for Enhanced Oil Recovery: An HLD-NAC Theoretical Framework and Experimental Validation
Authors: Wenjie Tang, Patiguli Maimaiti, Hongzhi Shao, Tingli Que, Shixun Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Middle-phase microemulsions (Winsor III) are critical for enhanced oil recovery (EOR) due to their ultralow interfacial tension (IFT) and high oil solubilization capacity. In this study, hydrophilic–lipophilic deviation (HLD) and net average curvature (NAC) theories were employed to design a salinity-optimized microemulsion system using coconut oil diethanolamide (6501) for a specific crude oil. Emulsification tests confirmed that increasing salinity expanded the middle-phase volume, while low salinity favored Winsor I (O/W) microemulsions. Polymer destabilized middle-phase formation but improved recovery by 40–45% due to enhanced sweep efficiency. Core flooding revealed that traditional surfactant–polymer (SP) flooding outperformed salinity-tuned microemulsions, likely due to insufficient oil-surfactant mixing after water flooding. Results of this work provide a framework for optimizing microemulsion-based EOR processes through integrated theoretical and experimental approaches and offer potential optimization strategies for field-scale application.

Introduction

The use of surfactants significantly contributes to the oil recovery from reservoirs, , and one important mechanism is the generation of microemulsions during surfactant injection. Microemulsions are thermodynamically stable, optically isotropic dispersions of oil and water stabilized by surfactants, often with the aid of cosurfactants or short alcohols. , These systems are classified into three types based on their phase behavior: Winsor I (oil-in-water microemulsion in equilibrium with excess oil), Winsor II (water-in-oil microemulsion in equilibrium with excess water), and Winsor III (bicontinuous microemulsion in equilibrium with both excess oil and water). , Among these, Winsor III microemulsions are particularly significant for enhanced oil recovery (EOR) due to their ability to achieve ultralow interfacial tension (IFT) between oil and water, which is critical for mobilizing trapped residual oil. − The formation of middle-phase microemulsions (Winsor III) is influenced by factors such as surfactant type, salinity, and temperature, which determine the system’s phase behavior and efficiency in oil displacement. ,−

The phase behavior of oil–water–surfactant systems is critical to understanding the formation and stability of middle-phase microemulsions, which are central to enhanced oil recovery (EOR) applications. , These systems emerge under specific conditions of surfactant type, salinity, and temperature, balancing hydrophilic and lipophilic properties to achieve optimal oil solubilization. The evolution from micellar to bicontinuous structures, as observed by Matsen et al., highlights the role of temperature in driving phase transitions. Kahlweit et al. further demonstrated how salinity and surfactant architecture dictate microstructure transitions in ternary systems.

Polymers commonly used in EOR, such as partially hydrolyzed polyacrylamides (HPAM) and xanthan gum, can modify the phase behavior of microemulsions, either by altering solubilization ratios or by influencing the rheology of the middle phase. ,, For instance, de Castro Dantas et al. demonstrated that a single-phase microemulsion combining alkali and polymer achieved a 97% oil recovery factor by reducing interfacial tension and shifting the wettability. Notably, Klimenko et al. found that ultralow surfactant concentrations paired with polymers can enhance flood efficiency without costly high-surfactant formulations, offering a cost-effective field solution. Polymers also mitigated surfactant retention. Koparal et al. showed that sodium polyacrylate reduced surfactant adsorption in Berea sandstone by nearly 50%, improving feasibility. These findings collectively illustrate how polymers enhance microemulsion systems through viscosity modification, surfactant retention reduction, and phase behavior optimization, ultimately improving oil recovery efficiency.

The complexity of microemulsion systems necessitates robust theoretical frameworks to predict and optimize their phase behavior. The hydrophilic–lipophilic deviation (HLD) and net average curvature (NAC) theories are most often resorted to as a powerful toolset for designing microemulsions tailored to specific applications such as EOR. −

The HLD theory is fundamental for understanding and predicting the phase behavior of microemulsions. HLD quantifies the balance between the hydrophilic and lipophilic properties of surfactants, which determines the affinity of surfactants for oil or water phases. The mathematical formulation of HLD is given by HLD = ln­(S) – 0.17 × EACN – α­(T-25) + Cc, where S represents salinity, EACN is the equivalent alkane carbon number of the oil, α is an empirical constant and adopts the value of 0.01 for ionic surfactants and −0.06 for nonionic ones, T accounts for temperature, and Cc is the characteristic curvature of the surfactants that does not have a physical meaning but essentially describes the contribution of the surfactants to the overall curvature of the oil/water/surfactant interface. The Cc value is usually a relatively small number that does not have a clear correspondence with the surfactant structures. The HLD equation enables the prediction of microemulsion type (Winsor I, II, or III) based on system conditions, providing a quantitative measure of surfactant behavior.

The NAC theory is pivotal for understanding the microstructure of microemulsions, complementing the HLD theory by providing insights into the curvature properties of surfactant films. Basically, the curvature of the surfactant film influences the phase behavior and solubilization capacity of microemulsions. The NAC theory is particularly valuable for characterizing the microstructure of microemulsions as it accounts for the interplay between surfactant geometry, oil–water interactions, and environmental conditions such as temperature and salinity. The theory’s mathematical formulation incorporates parameters like the characteristic length (ξ), which reflects the extent of solubilization in middle-phase microemulsions. The combined use of HLD-NAC theories has been proved to effectively predict solubilization trends of surfactant systems. ,,,

The middle-phase volume (MPV) is a critical parameter determining the efficiency of microemulsion applications, an example being the higher oil recovery with larger MPV, as it indicates higher oil solubility within the middle phase and therefore easier removal from the formations. Based on the HLD-NAC theories, several approaches can be adopted to increase the middle-phase volume. To begin, the adjustment of the surfactant chain length (l) and headgroup area (a) can effectively modify the NAC value. For example, increasing the number of EO groups enhances hydrophilicity and reduces NAC; conversely, extending the hydrophobic chain increases NAC. However, a tailor-designed combination of EO groups and hydrophobic carbon chain length is not always readily available. Another method is the tuning of the temperature. Elevated temperatures reduce surfactant orderliness, shifting the HLD toward negative values. For instance, nonionic surfactants (e.g., C12EO5) tend to form O/W emulsions at high temperatures but transition to W/O emulsions at low temperatures, during which the middle phase also experiences changes. However, for an oilfield scenario, the formation temperature cannot be arbitrarily varied. The only feasible means by which MPV can be altered is the adjustment of the salinity. Salinity alters MPV by affecting surfactant adsorption density (Γm) and interfacial tension (γ). At high salinity, counterions compress the electric double layer, promoting surfactant aggregation and expanding the middle-phase region. Therefore, in this work, the regulation of salinity was attempted to strengthen the role of the middle-phase microemulsion to increase the oil recovery.

The combined use of HLD and NAC theories was implemented in this work to design a middle-phase microemulsion formulation for a specific oilfield, and flooding experiments were conducted to determine the concentrations and injection volumes, as well as to optimize the injection schemes. The rest of the paper is structured as follows. First, the necessary parameters for the HLD and NAC models were quantified through a series of experiments, followed by the design of the microemulsion formulations using both theories. Flooding tests were then conducted to test the EOR abilities of the formulations, and the underlying mechanisms were proposed to rationalize the results before conclusions were drawn.

Experimental Materials

Dehydrated crude oil from Block Qi-8 Daowan (containing asphaltenes and resins), the viscosity was measured to be 27.4 mPa·s and the density was 822 kg/m3. Methods

Determination of Interfacial Tension

The spinning drop method was used to measure the interfacial tension between the surfactant (or surfactant/polymer) solutions and crude oil using a KRUSS SDT Spinning Drop Tensiometer.

Determination of HLD Parameters

The HLD (hydrophilic–lyophilic deviation) equation requires two critical parameters: the characteristic curvature (Cc) of the surfactant and the effective alkane carbon number (EACN) of the oil. Both parameters were determined experimentally. The Cc value was initially established using n-hexadecane as the oil phase. A series of saline solutions with varying salinities were prepared by dissolving sodium chloride in distilled water. These solutions were mixed with 6501 surfactant at 3% (the concentration is intentionally significantly higher than what was used in the flooding experiment to obtain more distinctive emulsification). Equal volumes of the surfactant-brine mixtures and n-hexadecane were mixed, shaken, and were allowed to stand for phase separation observation. According to Zarate-Muñoz et al., the emulsion exhibits the fastest phase separation at HLD value of zero; therefore, this criterion was used to quickly determine the condition where HLD equals zero. By substitution of the known EACN value of 16 for n-hexadecane into the HLD equation, the Cc value of the 6501 surfactant was calculated. Subsequently, the EACN of the crude oil was determined following a similar protocol. A set of brine solutions with differing salinities were prepared and mixed with 6501 surfactant. Equal volumes of these surfactant-brine systems were combined with crude oil, mixed using a loopster, and monitored for phase separation. The solution demonstrating the most rapid layer formation was assumed to have an HLD value close to zero. This enabled calculation of the crude oil’s average carbon number using the predetermined Cc value of 6501.

Emulsifying Ability Determination

Equal volumes of crude oil and water phase (formation water, 6501 solution with and without polymer of different concentrations), approximately 5 mL each, were added into capped tubes, and emulsification experiments were conducted using an IKA loopster at 30 rpm for 2 h. The tubes were then allowed to settle overnight. The tubes were then observed for phase separation.

Long Core Displacement Experiment

Berea sandstone cores 30 cm in length and 2.54 cm in diameter were utilized for the flooding tests. Permeabilities of all cores were measured to be approximately 200 mD using formation water at 30 °C. The flooding experiment is a common practice in the oil and gas discipline, the setup of which is therefore not elaborated upon. The flooding procedure is concisely described as follows. The cores were first vacuumed and saturated with formation brine, and oil was injected into the cores at a rate of 0.05 mL/min to displace the water. Oil injection was continued until no water was produced at the outlet to establish residual water saturation. Water flooding then commenced at 0.3 mL/min until the water cut reached 98%, the formulation, either surfactant alone or with polymer, was injected for 0.5 PV (pore volume), and a subsequent formation brine flooding was continued until the water cut reached 98% again.

Results and Discussion

HLD and NAC Parameters

Using NaCl as the reference, salinities were calculated as the equivalent salinity as recommended by Abbot, which simultaneously accounts for the valency and concentration of ions, using eq . 14 Seq=(∑MwCl·MwClMwNa·MwcationValencyanion2+MwNa·MwNaMwCl·MwcationValencyanion2)·S where S is the salinity of the solution in g/100 mL.

The resulting surfactant solutions and hexadecane oil were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and were allowed to settle. Mixtures with brine salinities of 11.6 and 13.6 g/100 mL were observed to exhibit faster phase separation, and a midvalue of 12.7 g/100 mL was therefore taken as the salinity that led to the most unstable emulsion, corresponding to HLD of 0. With EACN, temperature, and equivalent salinity known, the Cc number of 6501 surfactants can be calculated to be 0.378.

The EACN of crude oil was determined with a similar method, except that the set of known values includes the temperature, equivalent salinities, and Cc number of 6501 surfactant. By observation, an equivalent salinity of 3.7 led to the most unstable emulsion, corresponding to an HLD of 0. The EACN was therefore calculated to be 6.2. It needs to be noted that this is not to be interpreted as the oil phase being light. EACN does not necessarily indicate an average component similar to that of hexane. Instead, the EACN of benzene is 0 and that of cyclohexane is 3, and for trichloroethylene, the EACN is even negative at −4. A EACN of 6.2 may suggest a non-negligible portion of aromatics in the oil. The EACN and Cc values, along with the other NAC parameters in the following paragraphs, were used as obtained and went through no additional rigorous calibration with dedicated phase behavior tests. Therefore, the prediction ability of the method may not be precise, but it is still a valid tool to provide guidance on adjustment directions in system properties such as salinity.

HLD Values at Different Equivalent Salinities

As shown in Figure , when the hydrophilic-lyophilic deviation (HLD) equals 0, the middle-phase volume is minimal. However, as the HLD value approaches either −1 or +1, the middle-phase volume expands. To achieve a higher EOR, which requires greater solubilization of the oil phase, the HLD should be adjusted to approach 1. As already explained in the Introduction section, the most convenient method to regulate HLD is by increasing salinity. Through calculations using eq , an equivalent salinity of 1.8698 g/100 mL was obtained. When the HLD equals 0, the required equivalent salinity should be approximately 5. To further enhance the solubilization effect of the middle phase on oil, an equivalent salinity of 10 was designed. These salinities represent 2.7 and 5.3 times the salinity of the original formation water. Additionally, a low-salinity formulation at 0.5 times the formation brine was designed, and to avoid the potential clay swelling in the cores, NaCl in the diluted formation brine was substituted into KCl.

Due to the appearance of the microemulsion of crude oil, especially the Winsor II (w/o) type and Winsor III (middle phase) type, which is visually similar to the crude oil itself, to more clearly distinguish between phases, ultraviolet light was used to exhibit the different levels of fluorescence of the crude oil and the emulsions. Figure indicates that for 6501 solution prepared with formation water, no middle phase was generated during the mixing, and judging from the larger volume of oil over water phases, an w/o emulsion was formed. An increase in the salinity to 2.7 and 5.3 times that of the formation water led to the disappearance of the water phase and less than 0.5 mL crude oil, with the rest over 9.5 mL entirely being the middle-phase microemulsion. By comparison, the mixture with low salinity led to no visible middle phase, as it is likely that the salinity lowers the HLD out of the Winsor III domain. Rather, low salinity led to a clear Winsor I (oil in water) microemulsion. Though this does not entirely agree with the calculation (Figure ), there is a clear consensus between the experimental results and the theoretical prediction regarding the trend of the phase volume change.

Influence of Polymer on Middle-Phase Volume

To study how the polymer affects microemulsion phase behavior, different concentrations of polymer were incorporated into the formulation. Emulsification experiments were then conducted to assess the polymer’s influence on the middle-phase volume. The results are presented in Figure , and it can be seen that with the addition of polymer, the one prepared with formation water and 0.12% polymer exhibited a small volume of middle-phase microemulsion of approximately 1 mL, while neither 0.15%, 0.18%, nor 0.2% polymer resulted in any middle phase. This suggests that the addition of polymer is not conducive to the formation and stabilization of a Winsor type III microemulsion. Surfactant–polymer solutions prepared with low- and high-salinity brines did not exhibit any middle phase, either. Therefore, for this particular combination of surfactant and polymer, the Winsor type III microemulsion is significantly impacted by the presence of polymer and is unlikely to substantially contribute to the oil recovery. The influence of polymer on microemulsion volume and stability is occasionally claimed to be positive. For example, Biruss et al. suggest that the addition of polymers stabilized a microemulsion they prepared, which they attributed to the increased viscosity, possibly impeding droplet coalition. However, the polymer was added to the solution after microemulsion formation, and the microemulsion itself was of an oil-in-water nature, which makes the process fundamentally different from this work, and the proposed mechanism may not apply. Other reports emphasize on the interaction of the polymers to the surfactant film. , Especially, Lu et al. and Nilsson et al. respectively found that amphiphilic polymers distribute in both the water and oil phases and generally increase the middle phase. By comparison, a polymer that dominantly dissolves in water or oil shrinks the middle phase. Although the addition of polymer reduces the middle-phase microemulsion, from the perspective of the viscosifying abilities of polymer, which usually enhances the sweep efficiency, polymer was still included in the formulations as a comparison in the subsequent section.

Recoveries Using Different Formulations

Incremental recovery refers to the % OOIP after the initial water flooding.

For the solution with just 0.5% 6501 surfactant, the incremental recovery was 24.03%, and neither an increase nor a decrease in the salinity caused any significant variation in the recoveries. This is contradictory to the emulsifying abilities of the corresponding solutions. A possible explanation is that the surfactant solution was introduced into the cores after the formation water flooding, and as a result, the surfactant only propagated within the cores along the water phase path established by the formation water. This led to insufficient mixing between the water and oil phases and thus significantly weakened the emulsification between the two. By comparison, with the addition of polymer, the incremental recoveries rose to above 40%. The 0.5% 6501 and 0.15% polymer formulation prepared with just formation brine led to higher incremental recovery (44.73%) compared with the ones prepared with higher salinities (41.12% and 40.25%, respectively). With the knowledge that the presence of polymer is not conducive to middle-phase microemulsion formation, differences in recoveries cannot be due to emulsifying abilities but instead can be attributed to variations in the solution viscosities. Higher salinities compressed the electric double layers around polymers, and the molecules tend to adopt a curled conformation, making intermolecular entangling difficult. The viscosities decreased from 38.8 mPa·s with formation water and 30.5 and 27.6 mPa·s with high-salinity brines, corresponding well with the recoveries. For the formulation prepared with low-salinity brine, the viscosity was 53.4 mPa·s, the highest among the surfactant–polymer formulations, yet the incremental recovery was 43.46%, comparable to that prepared using formation water. It is likely that the cores used are relatively homogeneous, viscosities around 40 mPa·s are sufficient to compensate for the low level of heterogeneity, and higher viscosities cannot contribute to additional oil recoveries.

Nevertheless, it is clear that the presence of polymer can lead to higher oil recoveries, but it is more related to their roles as viscosifying agents and not due to the emulsifying abilities of the formulations. It may seem from the results that middle-phase microemulsion design did not contribute to the ultimate recovery, and the resulting formulation seems more like a traditional combination flooding than a microemulsion flooding. However, adjustments of the injection method may be implemented to encourage a more significant mixing of the water and oil phases. Therefore, an additional flooding experiment was conducted as follows. A 0.1 PV slug of polymer solution was first injected after formation of water flooding before a 0.5 PV 6501 surfactant slug. This was subsequently followed by another 0.1 PV polymer injection before another formation of water flooding. This is to ensure that the surfactant can be diverted to pores with higher oil saturation for effective mixing. The incremental recovery was 38.45%, and the ultimate recovery reached 78.39%. This is higher than that using 6501 surfactant alone but still lower than that with the combined use of polymer. This, combined with the fact that no microemulsion was observed at the effluent, indicates that the middle-phase microemulsion was not effectively sustained during the flooding. One possible explanation is that the continuous flooding, especially after the initial formation water flooding, leaves the volume fraction of the water phase substantially higher than that of the oil phase. Under such circumstances, even if a middle-phase microemulsion was generated during flooding, it will be inverted to the O/W type microemulsion and eventually become demulsified as the surfactant solution gets diluted by subsequent formation water flooding. To fully take advantage of the oil solubilizing capacity of the middle-phase microemulsion, either the amount of surfactant injection needs to be significantly increased or more strict conformance control is required, both of which are aimed at enhancing the mixing between the surfactant-containing formulation and the oil phase. In the scenario of water flooding in an oilfield, this would mean either to commence the formulation injection in the very beginning or to implement conformance control after the water channel has been established between the injector and the producer. Larger amounts of formulation are required to aid the mixing and to avoid dilution by water. This may be substituted with alternate injection of the formulation and brine, but the validation is feasible only with much longer core flooding experiments. Relevant tests will be conducted in the future with a more appropriate apparatus.

Conclusions

In this work, the design and application of middle-phase microemulsions (Winsor III) for enhanced oil recovery (EOR) were systematically implemented using the hydrophilic–lipophilic deviation (HLD) and net average curvature (NAC) theories. Key findings and implications are summarized as follows:Emulsifying tests under different salinities basically agree with the theoretical prediction. Specifically, increasing salinity expanded the middle-phase volume (Winsor III), while low salinity not only suppressed it but also induced a clear Winsor I microemulsion.Polymer destabilized middle-phase microemulsions; however, the presence of polymer increased the incremental recovery (40–45% vs 24% for surfactant-only) due to improved sweep efficiency.Core flooding experiments demonstrate that, in terms of enhanced recovery mechanisms, traditional SP combination flooding outperformed salinity-tuned microemulsions, which is likely due to insufficient oil-surfactant mixing after water flooding.Future work should test varying injection schemes to sustain microemulsions in porous media. While HLD-NAC theory effectively guides formulation design, field success may depend on balancing emulsification, viscosity, and conformance control and should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis.
