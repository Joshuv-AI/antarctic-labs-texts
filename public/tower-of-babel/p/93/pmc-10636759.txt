Conformationally Restricted Glycopeptide Backbone Inhibits Gas-Phase H/D Scrambling between Glycan and Peptide Moieties
Authors: Christian Code, Danwen Qiu, Ilia A. Solov’yov, Jung-Goo Lee, Hyeon-Cheol Shin, Christopher Roland, Celeste Sagui, Damian Houde, Kasper D. Rand, Thomas J. D. Jørgensen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Protein glycosylation is a common post-translational modification on extracellular proteins. The conformational dynamics of several glycoproteins have been characterized by hydrogen/deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HDX-MS). However, it is, in most cases, not possible to extract information about glycan conformation and dynamics due to the general difficulty of separating the deuterium content of the glycan from that of the peptide (in particular, for O-linked glycans). Here, we investigate whether the fragmentation of protonated glycopeptides by collision-induced dissociation (CID) can be used to determine the solution-specific deuterium content of the glycan. Central to this concept is that glycopeptides can undergo a facile loss of glycans upon CID, thereby allowing for the determination of their masses. However, an essential prerequisite is that hydrogen and deuterium (H/D) scrambling can be kept in check. Therefore, we have measured the degree of scrambling upon glycosidic bond cleavage in glycopeptides that differ in the conformational flexibility of their backbone and glycosylation pattern. Our results show that complete scrambling precedes the glycosidic bond cleavage in normal glycopeptides derived from a glycoprotein; i.e., all labile hydrogens have undergone positional randomization prior to loss of the glycan. In contrast, the glycosidic bond cleavage occurs without any scrambling in the glycopeptide antibiotic vancomycin, reflecting that the glycan cannot interact with the peptide moiety due to a conformationally restricted backbone as revealed by molecular dynamics simulations. Scrambling is also inhibited, albeit to a lesser degree, in the conformationally restricted glycopeptides ristocetin and its pseudoaglycone, demonstrating that scrambling depends on an intricate interplay between the flexibility and proximity of the glycan and the peptide backbone.

Introduction

Extracellular proteins are very often glycosylated, and the glycans play an essential role in many biological processes.1,2 Hydrogen/deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HDX-MS) offers a nonperturbing label to characterize glycoprotein conformation, dynamics, and interactions in solution.3−11 However, the difficulty remains in finding a suitable analytical approach to determine deuterium labels on the protein and the attached glycans by means of mass spectrometry. Determining such specific deuterium labels could provide direct information about glycan dynamics. Glycans are an integral part of many proteins, and conformational transitions of glycans have recently been suggested to exert a conformational control of the polypeptide part of certain glycoproteins.12 Elucidating the conformational state of the glycan can invariably give an indication of its function in glycoproteins and potentially show how a glycan might be recognized by interacting proteins.13 Recent HDX-MS studies have demonstrated that deuterium can be retained at the acetamido group in GlcNAc residues in glycans,6,14 and gradual deuterium uptake in the glycan–hydroxyl groups has been monitored by virtue of μs-deuteration using theta-electrospray emitters.15 Likewise, intermolecular interactions in protein–glycan complexes can be studied by gas-phase HDX-MS experiments.16 These observations suggest that deuterium labeling of glycans could represent an approach to understanding the structure and conformation of the glycan and ultimately how this relates to the function. For instance, a single glycan can bind to different proteins via distinct conformers, thereby facilitating different biological processes.17 Although the binding mode can be obtained through a combination of techniques like X-ray crystallography, NMR, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, using all of these is quite a demanding task.18 We have previously demonstrated that the comprehensive analysis of the conformation of N-glycosylated proteins can be achieved by HDX-MS if deglycosylation of N-linked glycans is performed under quench conditions with acid-stable glycosidases.19,20 While this allows for the determination of the deuterium content of the deglycosylated peptides (and, in principle, also the released glycans), there is no equivalent acid-stable glycosidase for O-linked glycans. Also, it is not possible to obtain saccharide-specific deuterium contents by further enzymatic trimming at quench conditions, as such acid-stable glycosidases remain to be discovered. Thus, HDX-MS combined with gas-phase fragmentation could be an effective way to assign the hydrogen-bonding status of heteroatom-bound hydrogens in a glycan and thereby distinguish between glycan conformers. However, this requires careful analysis of the occurrence of positional migration of hydrogens and deuterium (H/D scrambling) in the glycan upon CID before any inference about the structure of a glycan from the extent of labeling can be deduced from an HDX-MS experiment. In HDX-MS experiments, it has been shown that the complete H/D scrambling of the solution-phase labeling pattern occurs upon gas-phase fragmentation of protonated and deprotonated peptides by slow-heating fragmentation techniques like CID, resulting in a random distribution of the deuterium label across all heteroatom-bound hydrogens in the peptide.21−35 Thus, if reversible intramolecular proton/deuteron transfers are prevalent, a consequence of the mobilization of protons according to the mobile proton model,36,37 then the deuterium distribution of the gas-phase fragment ions will no longer reflect the veritable labeling pattern in solution. However, residue-specific information can be obtained during an HDX-MS experiment if prompt gas-phase fragmentation methods are employed (ETD/ECD/MALDI ISD/UVPD).22,38−48 It is unclear whether gas-phase MS/MS techniques can retain the solution-specific labeling of glycans and glycopeptides. In protonated N-glycopeptides, the dissociation threshold energy for the O-glycosidic bond cleavage is lower than that of the peptide bond cleavage.49 Therefore, low-energy CID fragmentation of N-glycopeptides is dominated by the O-glycosidic bond cleavage while the peptide backbone stays intact.49,50 This raises the theoretical possibility that glycan fragmentation might occur without undergoing H/D scrambling with the peptide backbone amides. This would be the case if the dissociation threshold for the glycosidic bond cleavage is below the threshold energy for reversible proton/deuteron transfer with backbone amide hydrogens. Furthermore, proton/deuteron transfers between the peptide and the glycan moieties require the formation of transient hydrogen bonds between these moieties. This means that also conformational requirements must play an important role for H/D scrambling to occur in glycopeptides.

Here, we investigate the occurrence of H/D scrambling in glycopeptides obtained from the glycoprotein artemin (ART) and the conformationally restricted glycopeptide vancomycin. Artemin is a suitable model protein due to its small size and its similar glycan moiety to those found in many other human glycoproteins, and we have previously examined the effect of its glycan on the physicochemical and conformational properties of the protein.7 Furthermore, ART is produced recombinantly using an expression system (CHO) that is used for the production of many other proteins of pharmaceutical use and thus comprises the type of glycans commonly found on biopharmaceuticals. To explore the nature of scrambling, several glycovariants of ART were produced, and these were analyzed by HDX-MS and targeted CID. We find that there is a significant amount of deuterium retained in the acetamido groups in the glycan during HDX-MS. Upon CID, there is a complete redistribution of this deuterium label across all heteroatom-bound hydrogens including both those of the peptide backbone and the glycan when the full glycan is present and has freedom to interact with the peptide. The probability of proton/deuteron transfer between the glycan and the peptide was investigated further using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, revealing extensive hydrogen bonding between the peptide and glycan moieties in the ART glycopeptide. Surprisingly, H/D scrambling between the peptide and glycan moieties is completely absent in the glycopeptide vancomycin upon CID. MD simulations clearly show that the conformationally restricted peptide backbone of vancomycin prevents H/D scrambling as the glycan essentially does not form any hydrogen bonds with the backbone amide hydrogens, and this effectively prevents any proton/deuteron transfers between the two moieties. The conformationally restricted backbone in ristocetin and its pseudoaglycone also inhibited scrambling, albeit to a lesser degree than that in vancomycin. Our results show that unless the backbone is conformationally restricted, scrambling is prevalent upon the glycosidic bond cleavage in protonated glycopeptides due to the presence of several intramolecular hydrogen bonds between the glycan and peptide moieties.

Experimental Section

Materials

The ART protein samples made with different degrees of glycosylation were obtained from Biogen Idec. (Cambridge, MA). All chemicals used were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO) unless otherwise noted. Tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP) was obtained from Thermo Scientific (Waltham, MA). Vancomycin hydrochloride and ristocetin A were kindly donated by Dumex-Alpharma (Copenhagen, Denmark). Ψ-Aglycoristocetin was prepared from ristocetin A as previously described.51

Preparation of ART-Glycoforms

Artemin’s N-linked glycan was modified as described in Danwen et al.7 Briefly, wild-type ART (WT-ART), desialylated ART (DS-ART), degalactosylated ART (Degala-ART), Endo F3-treated ART (Endo-ART), and deglycosylated ART (Degly-ART) were provided by Biogen Idec. All ART samples were prepared in succinate buffer (10 mM sodium succinate, 75 mM NaCl, and 100 mM l-arginine HCl, pH 5.5). WT-ART was obtained at a concentration of 4.7 mg/mL. DS-ART was prepared by treating WT-ART with sialidase A (Prozyme, Hayward, CA). Degala-ART was prepared by treating WT-ART with sialidase A and β-galactosidase (Prozyme). Endo-ART was prepared by treating WT-ART with endoglycosidase F3. Degly-ART was prepared by treating WT-ART with PNGaseF (Prozyme). For DS-ART, Endo-ART, and Degly-ART, a final concentration of 4 mg/mL was obtained.

Preparation of Maximally Labeled ART Glycopeptides

Buffers and labeling conditions were adapted from Danwen et al.7 The glycopeptides were generated by pepsin digestion of disulfide-reduced ART-glycoforms and subsequently purified by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS). Without the protecting tertiary structure of artemin, the glycopeptides were readily completely deuterated by dissolution into a deuterated buffer. ART-glycoforms (60 pmol glycoform in 50 μL succinate buffer) were disulfide-reduced by mixing 1:1 (v/v) with quench buffer A (6 M GdmCl, 0.25 M TCEP, 300 mM phosphate, pH 2.3) and incubated at 25 C for 24 h. Samples were desalted and digested using an HDX-Manager (Waters Corporation, MA) that maintained reversed-phase columns at 0 C and the column for online pepsin digestion at 20 °C. A nanoAcquity ultraperformance liquid chromatography (UPLC) Auxiliary Solvent Manager and Binary Solvent Manager (Waters) delivered the mobile phases for desalting and gradient elution, respectively. Solvent A (0.23% (v/v) aqueous formic acid) was used for desalting; the gradient consisted of solvent A and solvent B (0.23% (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile). The glycoprotein sample was online-digested with immobilized pepsin (Poroszyme Cartridge, 30 × 2.1 mm, Life Technologies, Gaithersburg, MD) at a flow of 200 μL/min solvent A. The resulting peptides were trapped and desalted for 6 min at 200 μL/min solvent A using a reversed-phase trap column (Waters Acquity UPLC HSS T3 VanGuard Precolumn, C18, 1.8 μm, 2.1 × 5 mm). Subsequently, the trap column was switched in-line with the analytical column (Waters Acquity UPLC HSS T3 Column, C18, 1.8 μm, 1 × 100 mm), and the peptides were eluted with a gradient of solvent B, increasing from 8 to 40% over 7 min. The glycopeptides eluted at retention times from 3 to 6 min, and the fractions containing the peptides were manually collected. Solvents were removed by vacuum centrifugation at 60 °C for 1 h (Vacufuge vacuum concentrator, Eppendorf, Germany), and the dried samples were redissolved in 50 μL of D2O, followed by solvent removal again by vacuum centrifugation to minimize the amount of protium originating from the sample itself. Subsequently, the peptides were dissolved in 25 μL of deuterated succinate buffer (10 mM sodium succinate, 75 mM NaCl, 100 mM l-arginine HCl in D2O, pDread 5.7), incubated for 2 h at 25 C, then mixed 1:1 (v/v) with quench buffer B (8 M GdmCl, 0.5 M TCEP in 0.15% formic acid, pH 1.12), and placed on ice for 1 min; the pH was 2.5. Afterward, 50 μL of 0.15% (v/v) formic acid, pH 2.3, was added and the sample was immediately frozen and stored at −80 °C.

Preparation of Endo-ART Glycopeptide 83–95 and Degly-ART Peptide 83–95 for CID Experiments

Buffers and labeling conditions were adapted from Danwen et al.7 The Endo-ART glycopeptide 83–95 and Degly-ART peptide 83–95 were obtained from proteins deuterated in their native state. Consequently, the labeling pattern of the peptide backbone amides results from the natively folded proteins. Deuterated glycopeptide (residues 83–95) of Endo-ART, containing fucose and N-acetylglucosamine, was obtained by online pepsin digestion of deuterated Endo-ART (320 pmol). The deuteration was carried out by a 10-fold dilution of Endo-ART in succinate buffer (320 pmol, 2.5 μL) into deuterated succinate buffer and allowed to exchange for 10 min at 25 °C. Isotopic exchange was quenched, and disulfide bonds were reduced by a 2-fold dilution with an ice-cold quench buffer (25 μL, 8 M GdmCl, 0.5 M TCEP in 0.15% formic acid, pH 1.12) on ice for 1 min; the pH was 2.5. Subsequently, the quenched sample was diluted 2-fold with aqueous acidic solution (50 μL, 0.15% formic acid, pH 2.3) and immediately frozen and stored at −80 °C. The same procedure was used to deuterate the Degly-ART peptide 83–95. Unlabeled Endo-ART glycopeptide 83–95 and Degly-ART peptide 83–95 were prepared as described above by replacing the deuterated succinate buffer with succinate buffer and using 160 pmol of protein.

Mass Spectrometry Experiments of ART Glycopeptides

The peptides were analyzed by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry using a quadrupole time-of-flight (ToF) mass spectrometer equipped with Tri-Wave Ion Mobility (Synapt G2, Waters, MA). CID experiments were carried out by mass-selecting the gaseous peptide ions of interest with the quadrupole and accelerating these ions into the collision cell filled with argon gas, where they underwent fragmentation; the resulting fragment ions were mass-analyzed by the ToF analyzer. CID experiments of deuterated peptides were carried out in triplicate. The identities of peptic peptides were verified by CID experiments. The acquired mass spectra were lock mass-corrected against the internal standard Glu-1-Fibrinopeptide B (500 fmol/μL in 0.1% formic acid in 50/50% (v/v) acetonitrile/H2O). The average masses of peptide isotopic envelopes were determined by HX-Express2.52

Preparation of Maximally Labeled Glycopeptide Antibiotics

Solutions of maximally labeled glycopeptide antibiotics (10 μM) were prepared by dissolving them in D2O with 1% (v/v) monodeutero acetic acid (CH3COOD) followed by incubation at 4 °C for 12 h. For the CID experiments carried out on the Q-ToF Ultima mass spectrometer, D-to-H exchange was performed on-column by desalting deuterated antibiotics (300 pmol) for 2 min at 0 °C on a reversed-phase trap column (POROS 20 R2) with a flow of 0.2% (v/v) formic acid in H2O at 300 μL/min using a cooled LC–MS setup previously described.53 Elution of the sample from the trap column into the electrospray ion source was done with a flow of 70% acetonitrile, 0.2% (v/v) formic acid in H2O at 40 μL/min at 0 °C. For the CID experiments carried out on the Synapt G2 mass spectrometer, D-to-H exchange was initiated by diluting a solution of maximally labeled glycopeptide antibiotics (1 mM) 200-fold into cold acidic water–methanol (0.2% (v/v) formic acid in 50% (v/v) CH3OH). The solution with D-to-H-exchanged antibiotics was subsequently infused into the electrospray ion source (15 μL/min) while maintaining the solution at subzero temperature by cooling the glass syringe with dry ice as described previously.38,54 Equilibrium-labeled (i.e., uniformly labeled) vancomycin was prepared by incubation of the solution with D-to-H-exchanged vancomycin for 5 h at 37 °C.

CID of Glycopeptide Antibiotics

The peptides were analyzed by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry using a quadrupole ToF mass spectrometer (Q-ToF Ultima, Waters, MA). CID experiments were carried out at the following instrumental settings: cone voltage 55 V, RF lens 1 = 45 V, ion source temp = 80 °C; argon was used as a collision gas at an indicated manifold pressure of 6 × 10–5 mbar and a collision cell potential of 13 V (Ψ-aglycoristocetin), 15 V (vancomycin), and 20 V (ristocetin). The CID spectra represent the average of three on-column D-to-H exchange experiments. Additional CID experiments were carried out with electrospray ionization mass spectrometry using a quadrupole ToF mass spectrometer equipped with Tri-Wave Ion Mobility (Synapt G2, Waters, MA) at the following conditions. Ion source temp = 100 °C; sampling cone = 20 V; transfer collision cell potential 5, 10, or 15 V at an argon pressure of 6.7 × 10–3 mbar.

Molecular Modeling of ART

Energy minimization and classical MD simulations were carried out on the truncated Endo-Art (MDVNSTWRTVDRL peptide and GlcNac with a fucose) by using the NAMD program55 and employing the CHARMM force field within the VIKING interface.56−60 The Asp182 residue of the peptide was considered protonated. An all-atom simulation of the system in vacuo was carried out for 1 μs at 300 K, employing the Langevin thermostat. To avoid the peptide being trapped in one conformation, prior production simulation (that was used to collect data for analysis) of the system was initially heated up to 800 K and then cooled down to 300 K by performing 1 ns long MD simulations of the system at 800, 600, and 400 K. The final structure was rendered using VMD software.61 The contacts between the peptide and the sugars were calculated over time and permitted us to conclude about the different hydrogen bonds in the system. Relative probability statistics were performed to show the average contacts between the 10 hydrogen bonds on the peptide and sugars. The probability of hydrogen bond formation depends on both the distance between the donor/acceptor, which is expected to be about 3 Å, and a bonding angle of ca. 20°; the probability of hydrogen bond formation decreases for larger separation distances and higher bonding angles.

Molecular Modeling of Vancomycin

The MD simulations of vancomycin were conducted as follows. The structure of vancomycin and other glycopeptide antibiotics is known from previous large-scale quantum chemistry simulations.62,63 We took the known aglycovancomycin structure and added the glycans. This structure was used to execute the AMBERTOOLS antichamber followed by the parmchk modules.64 These modules, in turn, created the Generalized AMBER Force Field (GAFF) for simulating vancomycin in the gas phase. The original SANDER optimized zero-temperature vancomycin structure was then gradually heated to 300 K and then equilibrated by means of a 10 ns. AMBER MD run: a production run of 1100 ns was then carried out using the Langevin thermostat (with parameters ntt = 3 and collision frequency γ ln = 1.0), differing random number seeds and a time step of 1 fs. Data were taken every 50 ps.

Results and Discussion

Artemin (ART, also called enovin or neublastin) is part of the glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF)-related family whose members are important for signaling during the development and maintenance of the central and peripheral nervous systems.65 Specifically, ART can bind with high affinity to the glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored GDNF receptor-α3 (GFRα3) and, in turn, activate and induce downstream signaling through the receptor tyrosine kinase RET.66 ART has been shown to support the survival of both peripheral neurons and midbrain dopaminergic neurons66 and has received some interest for development as a biopharmaceutical for the treatment of neurodegenerative disorders.67,68 ART is a 113 residue homodimeric glycoprotein with an N-linked glycosylation site located at Asn86 (Figure S1).7,68,69 We enzymatically trimmed the glycan and for each ART glycoform applied HDX-MS to measure the deuterium content of the resulting peptic glycopeptide that spanned residues 83–95 (Figure 1 and Table 1). The glycan clearly retains deuterium, as evidenced by the gradual decrease in the deuterium content upon trimming the glycan (Table 1). HDX-MS analyses of other glycoproteins have shown similar deuterium contents in N-linked glycans with structures similar to those in Table 1.6,9,14 For example, the deuterium content of complex-type biantennary glycan from fetuin (2.3 Da)6,14 is identical to our measurements of the same glycan structure in ART (10.7–8.4 = 2.3 Da; Table 1). Previous studies have shown that the deuterium is retained at the acetamido groups in glycans14 and that the isotopic exchange of acetamido groups is slowed down by hydrogen bonding with other sugar residues.70 Note that a similar mechanism of protection of backbone amide hydrogens occurs in protein structures, albeit at a much larger magnitude.

The peptic glycopeptide 83–95 from Endo-ART served as a useful model system to investigate the occurrence of H/D scrambling in a glycopeptide. Endo-ART was therefore subjected to an HDX-MS experiment with targeted fragmentation of the glycopeptide in the gas phase by CID. Expectedly, the O-glycosidic bond between fucose and GlcNAc has the lowest dissociation threshold, resulting in complete depletion of the precursor ion population by loss of fucose followed by further fragmentation, yielding a series of additional fragment ions (Figure 2). The most abundant fragment ion is accordingly the peptide with N-linked GlcNAc without fucose (i.e., peptide+GlcNAc2+ in Figure 2). This fragment ion undergoes further dissociation by cleavage of the amide bond linking GlcNAc with Asn, yielding the doubly protonated peptide without any sugars attached (i.e., peptide2+). In addition, the backbone amide bond cleavage occurs in competition with the loss of GlcNAc, yielding peptide fragment ions with GlcNAc attached, e.g., y12 + GlcNAc. Importantly, the fucose moiety does not contain any acetamido group, and therefore, the fucose does not retain any deuterium in HDX-MS experiments as deuterated hydroxyl groups rapidly undergo back-exchange with the protiated solvents at quench conditions.14,71 Nevertheless, the neutral loss of fucose is associated with a deuterium loss equivalent to that expected for complete H/D scrambling (compare the normalized deuterium content for the neutral loss of fucose with the red curve in Figure 3). Expectedly, the normalized deuterium content of all peptide fragment ions from deuterated Endo-ART closely mimics the theoretical values for 100% scrambling (compare black crosses with the red curve; Figure 3). Consequently, this nearly perfect agreement between the experiment and theory strongly suggests that extensive positional randomization among all labile sites has taken place prior to the O-glycosidic bond cleavage. For the sake of completeness, we also analyzed the deglycosylated peptide obtained from the deglycosylated protein, i.e., the Degly-ART peptide 83–95 from Degly-ART. Expectedly, the results are in excellent agreement with the occurrence of 100% scrambling (Figure S2).

In order to shed light on the interactions between the glycan moiety and the peptide that facilitate scrambling, we conducted molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Importantly, the MD simulations show that the fucose participates in several hydrogen bonds with both side chains and the peptide backbone in the Endo-ART glycopeptide (Figure 4). The presence of these hydrogen bonds shows that the fucose moiety interacts directly with the peptide moiety, which in turn provides a possible route for the intramolecular hydron traffic that results in scrambling. When a peptide is collisionally activated, backbone deuterons will be mobilized through reversible endothermic hydron transfers from basic side chains to backbone amide groups and back again to other labile sites according to the mobile proton model.37 Our experimental results clearly show that this mobilization also includes the labile sites in the glycan of the Endo-ART glycopeptide. Furthermore, the MD simulation results shown in Figure 4 point at the interactions that are likely to facilitate the extensive intramolecular migration of hydrons between the peptide and glycan that occurs before the O-glycosidic bond is cleaved; i.e., the loss of fucose occurs after all labile sites in the glycopeptide have undergone complete scrambling.

Next, we investigated the occurrence of scrambling in vancomycin, which is a glycopeptide antibiotic with a conformationally restricted backbone due to cross-linked side chains (Figure 5A). The backbone forms a rigid binding pocket for nascent bacterial cell wall peptides that inhibits the formation of the mature peptidoglycan cell wall in Gram-positive bacteria.72,73 Vancomycin is used for patients allergic to β-lactam antibiotics, and it is considered a drug of last resort against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus Gram-positive bacterial infections.74 Vancomycin and its interaction with cell wall model peptides have been extensively studied by mass spectrometry.51,75−86 Residue No. 4 of the heptapeptide scaffold, 4-hydroxyphenylglycine, is glycosylated with a disaccharide (l-vancosamine and d-glucose).72,73 The dominant fragmentation pathway of doubly protonated vancomycin upon CID is O-glycosidic bond cleavage, yielding the singly protonated amino sugar, vancosamine, and singly protonated desvancosamine–vancomycin at m/z 144.1 and 1305.3, respectively (Figure 5B). This bond cleavage most likely occurs by the mechanism depicted in Figure S4, i.e., similar to that for deprotonated oligosaccharides.87 A minor fraction of the desvancosamine–vancomycin ions undergoes further dissociation by the neutral loss of the remaining glucose, yielding singly protonated aglycovancomycin at m/z 1143.3. An additional minor dissociation pathway for doubly protonated vancomycin is the neutral loss of the disaccharide, yielding doubly protonated aglycovancomycin at m/z 572.1. We use the dominant vancosamine ion (m/z 144.1) as a scrambling reporter ion as it does not contain any acetamido group, and it is therefore devoid of deuterium in HDX-MS experiments due to the rapid back-exchange of amino and hydroxyl groups with protiated solvents during quenching. Surprisingly, the vancosamine ion does not contain any deuterium upon CID of deuterated vancomycin as evidenced by zero difference (Δmexp = 0.00 ± 0.002 Da) in average mass of vancosamine obtained from protiated and deuterated vancomycin (compare isotope distributions for vancosamine at m/z 144.1 in Figure 5B,C). If complete scrambling over all labile sites had occurred in deuterated vancomycin prior to covalent bond cleavage, then vancosamine would have significantly increased in mass due to deuteration (theoretical mass shift in the case of 100% scrambling, Δm100% = 0.79 Da). Our results clearly demonstrate that none of the backbone amide deuterium atoms have migrated to the amino sugar prior to the O-glycosidic bond cleavage. We note that the CID spectra of vancomycin shown in Figure 5 were obtained on a Q-ToF MS instrument (i.e., Q-ToF Ultima) different from that used for the CID experiments on the Endo-ART glycopeptide shown in Figure 2 (i.e., Q-ToF Synapt G2). To rule out that any instrument-specific parameter was decisive for the absence of scrambling between vancosamine and the backbone amides, CID experiments on vancomycin were also carried out with the Q-ToF Synapt G2 instrument at a range of collision energies (Figure S5). These results clearly show that scrambling is absent irrespective of the collision energy used (Figure S5; see data points for vancosamine and the 0 and 100% scrambling graphs). Consequently, the identical results obtained with the two different Q-ToF MS instruments eliminate any possibility of instrument-specific parameters influencing the absence of scrambling. Similarly, none of the other fragmentation pathways show any evidence of scrambling involving the glycan as the deuterium content is completely retained in the peptide moiety (Figure S5).

To provide further insight into the molecular basis for the absence of scrambling in gaseous vancomycin ions upon CID, we conducted MD simulations. These simulations demonstrate that, due to the conformationally restricted backbone, neither vancosamine nor glucose forms any significant hydrogen bonds with the peptide backbone (as seen in Figure 6 and Movies S6 and S7). A singular transient hydrogen bond, measuring 3.09 Å, was observed between the ammonium group of vancosamine (–NH3+) and the carbonyl group (C=O) of residue No. 3. This hydrogen bond occurred only at 568 ns in the MD simulation (Movie S7). Thus, the MD simulation indicates that vancomycin has an exceptionally low likelihood of forming hydrogen bonds between its glycan and peptide components. This minimal presence of hydrogen bonds accounts for the observed absence of intramolecular deuterium migration from the backbone to the glycan in vancomycin. Conversely, the pliable backbone in the Endo-ART glycopeptide allows for intramolecular hydron migration between the glycan and peptide, facilitated by many high probability hydrogen bonds between these segments. This observation leads us to infer that a flexible backbone might be crucial for scrambling to occur.

To further explore whether a flexible backbone is an essential prerequisite for scrambling, we turned to ristocetin A,88,89 which is another glycopeptide with a conformationally restricted backbone, and its pseudoaglycone, Ψ-aglycoristocetin A (Figures S8 and S9). The cross-linked side chains of ristocetin form a tetracyclic structure that is even more rigid than the tricyclic structure of vancomycin. However, the glycosylation of ristocetin is more extensive than that of vancomycin. Ristocetin contains four sugars (l-rhamnose, d-glucose, d-mannose, and d-arabinose) at residue No. 4, 4-hydroxyphenylglycine; an amino sugar (l-ristosamine) at residue No. 6, β-hydroxy-tyrosine; and d-mannose at residue No. 7, 3,5-dihydroxyphenylglycine.89 Ψ-Aglycoristocetin only carries the amino sugar ristosamine at residue No. 6. First, we studied the occurrence of scrambling in doubly protonated Ψ-aglycoristocetin upon CID using the amino sugar product ion, ristosamine at m/z 130.1, as a scrambling reporter (Figure S8). Intriguingly, the fragmentation occurred with a limited degree of scrambling (∼10%) as a minor increase in mass was observed (Δmexp = 0.09 ± 0.02 Da; Δm100% = 0.87 Da; degree of scrambling is Δmexp/Δm100%). Consequently, the conformationally restricted backbone has substantially inhibited, but not completely stopped, the intramolecular migration of deuterium from the peptide backbone to the amino sugar. Note that ristosamine at the β-hydroxy group is in closer proximity to the peptide backbone than the corresponding amino sugar in vancomycin, which explains the occurrence of partial scrambling in ristosamine.

Next, we subjected ristocetin to CID and used the disaccharide L-rhamnose-d-glucose oxonium ion at m/z 309.1, the hexose oxonium ion at m/z 163.1, and ristosamine at m/z 130.1 as scrambling reporters (Figure S9). Surprisingly, the disaccharide and hexose oxonium ions both increased significantly in mass (0.7 and 0.4 Da, respectively) when the backbone amide groups of ristocetin were deuterated. These mass shifts correspond to ∼70 and ∼60% degrees of scrambling, respectively. Intriguingly, the loss of ristosamine at m/z 130.1 proceeded with a significantly lower increase in the mass and degree of scrambling (0.07 Da and ∼ 10%), similar to that observed for Ψ-aglycoristocetin. The glycan-dependent levels of scrambling clearly show that a substantial degree of intramolecular migration of hydrons has occurred between certain glycan moieties and the peptide moiety before the O-glycosidic bond cleavage in ristocetin. The other fragmentation pathways with the O-glycosidic bond cleavage yielding neutral losses of sugar fragments (i.e., Rha, Glc, Man, and Ara) also result in the substantial deuterium depletion of the peptide fragment ion, yielding scrambling levels ranging from ∼70 to ∼80% corroborating the occurrence of extensive scrambling involving certain glycans (Figures S9–S12). The high scrambling levels (60–80%, Figure S12) for all of the glycans (i.e., Rha, Glc, Man, and Ara) except ristosamine result from intramolecular hydron transfers facilitated by hydrogen bonds that are likely formed within the glycan moieties as well as between the glycans and the peptide moiety. Loss of neutral arabinose proceeds with the highest degree of scrambling, so we speculate that the scrambling process might be initiated by arabinose forming hydrogen bonds with the backbone amides. Hydron transfer to the other sugar residues could then occur either through direct hydrogen bonding or indirectly via other groups within the peptide moiety. We attribute the low degree of scrambling for the loss of ristosamine m/z 130.1 as a result of limited participation in the hydrogen-bonding network due to steric restraints. In contrast, the scrambling processes occur readily for Rha, Glc, Man, and Ara despite the severely conformationally restricted peptide backbone of ristocetin.

Our vancomycin results clearly demonstrate that the O-glycosidic bond cleavage is possible without inducing scrambling in glycopeptides. Our MD simulations suggest that a prerequisite for the absence of scrambling is that the glycan moiety essentially does not form any hydrogen bonds with the peptide moiety. The absence of scrambling further indicates that the O-glycosidic bond cleavage occurs without protonation of the oxygen atom in the glycosidic bond, i.e., without a mobile proton (Figure S4). The presence of limited scrambling (∼10%) in Ψ-aglycoristocetin and ristocetin provides direct evidence for an interaction between the amino sugar ristosamine and the peptide moiety, most likely the backbone amide group of residue No. 6, which is in close proximity. However, steric and/or energetic factors clearly inhibit the intramolecular migration of hydrons resulting in a low degree of scrambling for ristosamine in Ψ-aglycoristocetin and ristocetin. By contrast, in ristocetin, substantial scrambling (60–80%) precedes the O-glycosidic bond cleavage for Rha, Glc, Man, and Ara. Clearly, the extended glycan moieties in ristocetin have formed hydrogen bonds with the peptide moiety and the mechanisms for intramolecular migration of hydrons have been energetically favorable in ristocetin. The presence of mono- and disaccharide oxonium fragment ions indicates the presence of mobile protons that have been involved in the O-glycosidic bond cleavage.87,90−92 Mobile protons and charge-directed fragmentation mechanisms are well-known to accompany scrambling processes in protonated peptides22 and rearrangement reactions in glycans.93−98 In this regard, we note that several mechanisms for hydron migration99 are likely to be operative in ristocetin; however, it is beyond the scope of the present work to elucidate the exact mechanisms. Finally, complete scrambling preceded the O-glycosidic bond in the conformationally unrestricted glycopeptide Endo-ART. Consequently, upon the CID of standard protonated N-linked glycopeptides, one should expect complete scrambling among all labile sites in the molecule, including those of the glycan.

In previous CID experiments, we demonstrated that gaseous noncovalent vancomycin–ligand and Ψ-aglycoristocetin–ligand complexes maintain specific interactions as they transition from solution to the gas phase (Figure S13). Conversely, the gaseous ristocetin–ligand complexes did not align with the established solution binding specificity.51 Our current findings provide valuable insights into the retention of solution structures by these glycopeptide antibiotic–ligand complexes in the gas phase. The results from our MD simulations, combined with the observed lack of scrambling in vancomycin, reveal that the glycan does not engage in intramolecular interactions with the backbone amide groups within the ligand-binding pocket. Notably, these amide groups form specific hydrogen bonds with the natural ligand in solution. This absence of intramolecular interactions positions vancomycin as an optimal model for exploring molecular recognition in gaseous complexes.51,75−82,86 For Ψ-aglycoristocetin, the amino sugar is situated away from the ligand-binding site, ensuring it does not directly interfere with the ligand-binding amide groups. However, the pronounced scrambling observed in ristocetin’s glycans suggests interactions with multiple backbone amide groups, inclusive of those within the binding site. Such interactions contest and interfere with specific interactions in a gaseous ristocetin–ligand complex. This could account for the observed disconnect between the solution- and gas-phase behaviors.

Conclusions

We have investigated whether H/D scrambling precedes glycosidic bond dissociation upon the CID of protonated glycopeptides. Importantly, these bonds typically dissociate at a lower collision energy than peptide bonds, and this results in a facile loss of the glycan. If the degree of scrambling is negligible upon glycosidic bond dissociation, then CID can be used as a method to elucidate the solution-specific deuterium content of the glycan. We have measured the degree of H/D scrambling upon the glycosidic bond cleavage in glycopeptides that differ in the conformational flexibility of their backbone, as well as in their glycosylation pattern. The glycopeptides with a conformationally unrestricted backbone, i.e., “normal” glycopeptides, are derived from pepsin digestion of the glycoprotein artemin (ART). By selective cleavage of the N-linked glycan of ART using specific glycosidases, we have shown that the glycopeptides retain distinct amounts of the deuterium label corresponding to the labeling of several sites in the glycan in addition to labeling of the amide hydrogens of the peptide backbone. Inspection of the deuterium content of fragment ions resulting from the CID of the labeled peptic glycopeptide reveals a complete redistribution of the deuterium label across all heteroatom-bound hydrogens. We find that the level of scrambling upon the glycosidic bond cleavage is equally pronounced in the glycan heteroatom-bound hydrogens as those of the backbone amide hydrogens, thus resulting in a complete loss of solution-specific selective labeling of both the glycan and peptide. MD simulations of the gaseous ART glycopeptide reveal multiple hydrogen bonds between the glycan and peptide, providing possible routes for the intramolecular migration of hydrons between these entities. Surprisingly, we find that H/D scrambling is completely absent upon the O-glycosidic bond cleavage in the glycopeptide antibiotic vancomycin. MD simulations reveal that the conformationally restricted backbone of vancomycin essentially prevents the formation of any hydrogen bonds between the glycan and peptide, thereby effectively shutting down any possible routes for intramolecular hydron migration between these moieties. To further explore the effect of a conformationally restricted backbone on H/D scrambling upon glycan fragmentation, we also investigated ristocetin A and its pseudoaglycone. Intriguingly, a very limited degree of scrambling (∼10%) was observed upon the loss of ristosamine in Ψ-aglycoristocetin and ristocetin, reflecting a restricted access to the peptide part of the molecule. In contrast, the remaining glycans of ristocetin exhibited substantial scrambling (∼60–80%) upon the cleavage of the O-glycosidic bond cleavage. Our results show that H/D scrambling precedes cleavage of the O-glycosidic bond during CID of protonated N-linked glycopeptides that are derived from glycoproteins. However, a conformationally restricted backbone inhibits scrambling, either completely or partially, depending on the extent of access of the glycan to the peptide part of the molecule. If the glycan is located in close proximity to a conformationally restricted backbone, enabling extensive hydrogen bond formation, we expect that complete scrambling could occur. We note that prompt methods for the fragmentation of glycopeptides, like ECD/UVPD/ETD/EDD, may, as is the case for protonated peptides, occur without the scrambling of heterobound hydrogens in the glycan. Further studies are needed to clarify this.
