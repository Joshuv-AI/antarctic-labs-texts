Fortuitous Persistent Müllerian Duct Syndrome Diagnosis in an Adult Patient With Hematuria: A Case Report
Authors: Alexi Boitsios, Tobias Schick, Romane Brawermann, Alexis Buggenhout
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPersistent Müllerian duct syndrome is a genetic disorder. It consists of Müllerian duct remains due to improper anti‐Müllerian hormone. Early life presentation is often associated with undescended testes or inguinal hernias. Late diagnosis is rarer and poses a diagnostic challenge. We present a case of robotically treated PMDS in an adult who presented with hematuria.Keywords: cryptorchidy, hematuria, persistent Müllerian duct syndrome, robotic surgery

Introduction

Persistent Müllerian duct syndrome (PMDS) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder of sex development that results in male pseudohermaphroditism. It is associated with completely normal phenotypical males with female reproductive organs derived from the Müllerian duct structures.

During gestation, Sertoli cells in the testes start releasing anti‐Müllerian hormone (AMH) around the 7th week, which results in the involution of the Müllerian ducts. PMDS results from a lack of production of AMH or an inactive form of AMH [1]. This condition is often found in children with undescended testes or with inguinal hernias. These patients are phenotypically male, with normal external genital organs, with a urethra that opens at the tip of the penis. However, Müllerian duct structures, namely fallopian tubes and/or uterus, are still found.

When exposed to AMH, Müllerian ducts usually disappear at 10 weeks of gestation in normal 46 XY males. This allows the testes to be released from their attachments to the broad ligament and descend through the inguinal canal to their final location in the scrotum. In PMDS patients, the persistence of the Müllerian duct structures prevents normal descent of the testes, which can result in cryptorchidism, or inguinal hernias [2, 3].

Diagnosis is usually done in children. Workup during cryptorchidism is sufficient to diagnose PMDS. Pelvic sonogram, computerized tomography are often prescribed, and magnetic resonance (MRI) will provide a better description of the lesions [4]. Delayed presentation is rarer, and often asymptomatic.

Case History

We present a case of a 56‐year‐old male who was referred to our academic hospital for a pelvic mass found during workup for macroscopical hematuria 5 years prior. The patient's medical history included ischemic cardiomyopathy, which required a coronary bypass and a percutaneous stent. There was no other prior surgery. The patient's clinical examination was unremarkable, showing no cryptorchidy or hernias. A digital rectal examination showed a palpable mass on the anterior aspect of the rectum.

Methods

Investigations

Blood and urinalysis did not show any abnormalities. Pelvic imaging using MRI showed a cystic round mass joined to a second cystic tubular mass, located caudally to the bladder, measuring 7 × 4 × 2 cm, suspected to be a hamartoma. Further workup consisted of echo‐endoscopy, which confirmed the MRI findings and showed continuity with the right seminal gland (Figures 1 and 2). Positron emission tomography (PET‐CT) showed no hypermetabolic activity in the pelvis. After 4 years of yearly follow‐up, the patient decided to have the mass removed. Therefore, we decided to perform a robotic excision using the da Vinci Xi platform (Intuitive Surgical, USA).

Treatment and Progress

Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the supine position with legs abducted and an intravesical catheter was placed. Pneumoperitoneum was achieved, and five trocars were placed, four for the robot and one for the surgical assistant. Trendelenburg position was used to free the pelvis from the bowel loops. Using the robotic platform, we incised the peritoneal reflection and dissected the first lesion (Figure 3). This lesion was joined to a secondary mass that was also resected (Figure 4). Caudally, a tubular structure was ligated, then the mass was cut free (Figure 5). The peritoneum was then closed with a running suture of PDS 3‐0 (Video 1).

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged the following day. On follow‐up in the outpatient clinic, the patient did not present any more hematuria and showed no discomfort or pain. Pathology showed endometrial cells with no signs of malignity concordant with a PMDS in the form of a remnant uterus. The patient declined any further genetic analysis.

Discussion

PMDS is an autosomal recessive inherited disorder resulting in 85% of the cases from either a genetic disorder coding for the AMH (Type 1 PMDS, 45%) or in the AMH‐receptor (Type 2 PMDS) [5]. In the remaining 15% of the cases, the cause of PMDS is unknown and is called idiopathic PDMS. During gestation, normal AMH levels and receptors cause the regression of the Müllerian duct remnants and, in doing so, free the testes from their attachments in the pelvis that prevent them from descending into their adult position in the scrotum. In PMDS patients, failure of the remnants to involute will result in cryptorchidy. Being an autosomal recessive disorder, consanguinity can increase incidence [6].

Testing of serum AMH levels could help categorize PMDS in type 1 or 2. Unfortunately, our patient declined further tests.

It should also be noted that this condition can often be associated with Transverse Testicular Ectopia (TTE) which presents as both testes that have descended on the same side. Type 1 TTE is associated with inguinoscrotal hernia; Type 2 TTE is associated with PMDS, and Type 3 presents with hypospadias, horseshoe kidney, cysts in the seminal glands, and scrotal abnormalities [7]. Our patient had both testes descended in the correct location.

Treatment for PMDS will depend on the age of presentation. In the early stages of life, cryptorchidism will often be the first symptom, and correction of this condition will be necessary by means of an orchidopexy of the affected testes. Surgical excision of the Müllerian duct remnants remains controversial, as earlier studies did not show a potential for malignancy. Farikullah et al. found 11 cases of malignancy, of which two died of metastatic uterine adenocarcinoma [8]. In 2023, Gagliardi et al. found only one more reported case of malignancy, further corroborating the low risk of malignancy in PMDS patients [9]. However, surgical excision should be performed if the presence of Müllerian ducts prevents the orchidopexy.

Even though our patient had children of his own, infertility is often associated with PMDS [10]. His fertility before and after the surgery is unknown. Unusually in our patient, clinical examination did not show undescended testes. His only complaint was hematuria, which disappeared after treatment. Hematospermia has also been described as the only complaint [11] which our patient did not complain of. In this particular case, endoscopic ultrasound showed a connection between the remnants and the right seminal gland, which can explain the hematuria and hematospermia in other cases. This connection has been ligated in our patient, which resolved his symptoms.

In this case, the patient was at first reluctant to undergo surgery. Yearly follow‐up did not show any size improvement or sign of malignancy. However, the patient decided after 4 years to have the mass removed.

Studies have shown that laparoscopic and robotic treatments are adequate options for treating PMDS [12]. This article is, to the best of our knowledge, only the third time robotic excision has been performed for PMDS in an adult setting [13]. We believe treatment of PMDS is safe and feasible using the robotic platform, as it provides excellent visualization of the pelvis and its complicated anatomy and improves the range of motion.

Although rare, PMDS should be suspected in infants with undescended testes. Systematic surgical removal is still unclear, although we should counsel the patients on the risk of malignancy and infertility. Robotic excision is safe and should decrease the risk of neurovascular complications.
