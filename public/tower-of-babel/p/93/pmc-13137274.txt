Clinical significance of renal histological diagnosis in patients with type 2 diabetes and proteinuria atypical for diabetic nephropathy
Authors: Shogo Kuwagata, Masami Chin‐Kanasaki, Aki Yamada, Tomonori Sakae, Nahomi Ishimoto, Yoshimi Imamura‐Uehara, Kosuke Yamahara, Mako Yasuda‐Yamahara, Sho Sugahara, Yuki Tanaka‐Sasaki, Natsumi Kuwata, Kimihiro Nishimura, Koichiro Murata, Itsuko Miyazawa, Shogo Ida, Natsuko Ohashi, Shinji Kume
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground/AimMost patients with proteinuria are considered to have typical diabetic nephropathy (DN). However, when proteinuria occurs without diabetic retinopathy, with hematuria, or persists despite strict glycemic and blood pressure control, it is considered atypical for DN and warrants further evaluation for non‐DN via kidney biopsy. Nevertheless, comprehensive information on renal histopathology and prognosis following kidney biopsy remains limited. This study aimed to clarify these issues through a retrospective analysis.MethodsAmong 490 patients who underwent kidney biopsy at Shiga University of Medical Science Hospital between 2013 and 2024, 51 Japanese patients with type 2 diabetes who met at least one criterion for proteinuria atypical for DN were included. Pre‐ and post‐biopsy changes in proteinuria levels and eGFR decline rate, as well as renal histology, were analyzed.ResultsAmong the 51 patients, 17 (33.3%) were histologically diagnosed with typical DN, whereas 34 (66.7%) were diagnosed with non‐DN, including membranous nephropathy, immunoglobulin A (IgA) nephropathy, renal sclerosis, interstitial nephritis, vasculitis, and others. Compared with the DN group, patients with non‐DN were significantly older and had a lower prevalence of diabetic retinopathy. Additionally, the non‐DN group showed significant improvements in both proteinuria levels and in the eGFR decline following biopsy and appropriate disease‐specific therapy.ConclusionA kidney biopsy identified a wide range of kidney diseases as the underlying causes of atypical proteinuria in DN, particularly in elderly patients, thereby improving renal outcomes. These findings underscore the clinical importance of heightened awareness of atypical proteinuria in diabetes management, particularly in aging populations.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is the leading cause of kidney failure requiring renal replacement therapy 1 , and is characterized by the onset of proteinuria and glomerulosclerosis due to chronic hyperglycemia 2 . Reducing proteinuria is a primary therapeutic goal for improving renal prognosis in patients with typical DN. Numerous clinical studies have demonstrated that multidisciplinary interventions 3 , 4 , including strict glycemic control, blood pressure management with renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors 5 , 6 , and additional use of sodium‐glucose co‐transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors 7 , 8 , 9 and/or glucagon‐like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RA) 10 , 11 , 12 , can reduce proteinuria and improve renal prognosis. Thus, the introduction of various renoprotective agents has heightened expectations for improved renal outcomes 13 . Consequently, the prevalence of DN and the number of patients requiring renal replacement therapy due to diabetes have been decreasing in Japan 1 .

Nonetheless, in clinical practice, some patients have treatment‐resistant proteinuria and subsequently progress to kidney failure 14 , highlighting an ongoing clinical challenge. When proteinuria does not respond to standard diabetes treatment, the underlying kidney damage may be unrelated to diabetes 15 , 16 , 17 . These conditions may worsen if not properly diagnosed and treated. Patients diagnosed with non‐diabetic nephropathy (non‐DN) by kidney biopsy often present with specific clinical features, such as the absence of diabetic retinopathy, hematuria, rapid deterioration of renal function, and a relatively short duration of diabetes 15 , 16 , 17 . Based on this evidence, the Japan Diabetes Society (JDS) and the Japanese Society of Nephrology (JSN) have established referral criteria. However, the histopathological profiles of patients who meet these referral criteria and undergo kidney biopsy remain incompletely characterized in current clinical practice, in which obesity and population aging have progressed, and the clinical phenotypes of diabetes have become increasingly heterogeneous. Moreover, few studies have evaluated renal outcomes after kidney biopsy in this patient population.

We retrospectively analyzed renal histology and outcomes in 51 Japanese patients with type 2 diabetes who underwent kidney biopsy for proteinuria atypical for DN (2013–2024). We characterized non‐DN diagnoses and evaluated changes in proteinuria and eGFR slope before and after biopsy. We found that approximately two‐thirds had non‐DN and that disease‐specific therapy was associated with improved renal outcomes, with older age emerging as a clinical feature of non‐DN.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Shiga University of Medical Science and received approval from its ethical committee (Approved No. R2009‐006). All patients provided written informed consent.

Patients and criteria of atypical proteinuria for DN

This study was a retrospective analysis conducted at a single center using renal biopsy specimens and clinical data from patients in routine clinical practice for whom the attending physician deemed the procedure necessary. Among these cases, we selected and analyzed patients based on three proteinuria‐related criteria, as recommended by the JDS and JSN referral guidelines, which suggest referral from diabetologists to nephrologists when non‐DN should be considered.

The patient selection flow for the analyzed patients is shown in Figure 1. Among 490 patients who underwent kidney biopsies at Shiga University of Medical Science Hospital from 2013 to 2024, 78 were diagnosed with type 2 diabetes. Additionally, 51 patients with type 2 diabetes who met one of the criteria for proteinuria atypical for DN were included in this study. Based on the referral criteria between the JDS and the JSN, we defined the following three conditions as proteinuria atypical for diabetic nephropathy (DN): proteinuria exceeding 0.5 g/gCr, accompanied by (1) the absence of diabetic retinopathy, (2) resistance to multidisciplinary treatment, or (3) the presence of glomerular‐type microscopic hematuria. “Resistance to multidisciplinary treatment” was defined according to the diabetes care guidelines of the JDS that were in effect during the period when renal biopsies were performed. Specifically, the condition was defined as meeting the following therapeutic targets: HbA1c <7.0%, blood pressure <130/80 mmHg, and treatment with an angiotensin II receptor blocker or an angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitor, when tolerated. Because the observation period was 2013–2024, SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP‐1RAs were excluded from this definition. Hematuria was defined as ≥5 cells per high‐power field (HPF). Dysmorphic RBCs are assessed via phase‐contrast microscopy; a proportion greater than 20% is considered positive for glomerular hematuria.

Evaluation of renal histology

Histological DN was defined according to the “Manual for pathological diagnosis of DN and hypertensive nephrosclerosis” 18 . Glomerular lesions defined as DN include diffuse lesions, nodular lesions, glomerular basement membrane doubling, subendothelial lumen enlargement, leaching lesions, mesangial melting, and glomerular portal microangiogenesis. Basement membrane thickening in electron microscopy was not examined in this study. In addition, tubulointerstitial and vascular lesions were scored as follows: interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (IFTA) (score 0–3), interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration (score 0–3), arteriolar hyalinosis (score 0–3), and arteriosclerosis (score 0–2) 18 . In contrast, non‐DN was defined as cases with histological features of diseases distinct from DN, such as membranous nephropathy, immunoglobulin A (IgA) nephropathy, and interstitial nephritis. Patients with non‐DN as the primary diagnosis but with concomitant DN lesions were categorized as the non‐DN group. Histological diagnoses were made by at least 2 nephrologists. As a result, among the 51 patients, 17 were diagnosed with DN alone, whereas 34 were diagnosed with non‐DN (Figure 1).

Definition of clinical variables

Hypertension is defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg or current use of antihypertensive medication. Body mass index (BMI) is calculated by dividing body weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters (kg/m2). Diabetic retinopathy is diagnosed by an ophthalmologist through fundus examination or retinal photography and classified based on the presence of microaneurysms, hemorrhages, exudates, or neovascularization. Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels are measured by high‐performance liquid chromatography and reported according to the National Glycohemoglobin Standardization Program (NGSP). Diabetes is defined as an HbA1c level ≥6.5% or current use of antidiabetic medication. Proteinuria is quantified as the protein‐to‐creatinine ratio (g/gCr). The eGFR is calculated using the Japanese equation based on serum creatinine: eGFR (mL/min/1.73m2) = 194 × serum creatinine−1·094 × age−0·287 (×0.739 for females) 19 .

Evaluation of proteinuria and eGFR

Proteinuria was assessed using spot or early‐morning urine samples collected in the outpatient setting, using a consistent sampling method for each patient. Urinary protein excretion was quantified by the protein‐to‐creatinine ratio (g/gCr). The relative change in proteinuria was calculated as follows: (post‐biopsy value − pre‐biopsy value)/pre‐biopsy value. Pre‐ and post‐biopsy values were collected 1 month prior to the kidney biopsy and between 9 and 12 months following the procedure, respectively. The relative change in proteinuria from pre‐ to post‐biopsy was evaluated in 44 patients (16 with DN and 28 without DN) with available pre‐ and post‐biopsy proteinuria data.

Renal function was evaluated using the eGFR, calculated using the serum creatinine‐based equation for Japanese individuals 19 . To assess renal functional trajectory, eGFR slopes pre‐ and post‐biopsy were estimated using linear regression in 37 patients (n = 14 with DN, n = 23 without DN) with longitudinal data spanning at least 12 months before and after biopsy (a minimum of 3 measurements per period). The change in slope (ΔeGFR slope) was defined as: ΔeGFR slope = post‐biopsy slope − pre‐biopsy slope.

We also examined differences in renal outcomes between patients with non‐DN who received additional disease‐specific treatment after kidney biopsy and those who did not, as described above. Among 34 patients histologically diagnosed with non‐DN, 18 received disease‐specific treatment for their kidney conditions; one patient with IgA nephropathy underwent tonsillectomy alone, and the remaining 17 received either steroids or immunosuppressive agents. Among the 18 patients who received disease‐specific treatment and the 16 whose treatment remained unchanged, we evaluated the rate of change in proteinuria and eGFR decline, as described above, in those for whom data on urinary protein levels and eGFR before and after kidney biopsy were available. Regarding proteinuria, analyzable data were available for 10 patients in the untreated group and 18 patients in the treated group. Regarding the eGFR decline rate, analyzable data were available for 9 patients in the untreated group and 14 patients in the treated group.

Statistics

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. For comparisons between two groups, Welch's t‐test was used for continuous variables with a normal distribution, and the Mann–Whitney U‐test was applied for nonparametric data. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square test. To assess trends across ordered categories, such as urinary RBC levels and histological scores for tubulointerstitial and vascular lesions, the Cochran–Armitage trend test was performed. For the longitudinal analysis of clinical parameters before and after kidney biopsy, within‐patient changes in proteinuria and eGFR slope were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test in each group. Univariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify clinical variables associated with a diagnosis of non‐DN. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Inc.) and GraphPad Prism version 10. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Due to missing data, the sample sizes analyzed differed across variables.

Clinical characteristics of patients histologically diagnosed with DN and non‐DN

When comparing the clinical characteristics of patients with DN and without DN, no significant differences were observed in BMI, diabetes duration, HbA1c, systolic blood pressure, urinary protein level, eGFR, or eGFR decline rate at the time of kidney biopsy (Table 1). However, patients with non‐DN showed a trend toward a higher frequency of glomerulotubular‐type microscopic hematuria and a significantly lower prevalence of retinopathy (Table 1). In addition to the two variables used as referral criteria, the mean age of non‐DN patients was substantially higher than that of patients diagnosed with typical DN (Table 1).

Furthermore, exploratory univariate logistic regression analyses were performed to examine clinical variables associated with non‐DN among patients with type 2 diabetes presenting with atypical proteinuria. Older age and the absence of retinopathy were significantly associated with a diagnosis of non‐DN (Table 3).

Change in proteinuria and eGFR decline rate before and after kidney biopsy

Finally, the clinical significance of the differential diagnosis of kidney diseases in patients with type 2 diabetes with proteinuria atypical for DN was examined. Within 1 year after kidney biopsy, among patients diagnosed with non‐DN, one case of anti‐GBM antibody disease progressed to kidney failure requiring dialysis, while another case of amyloidosis due to hematologic disorder resulted in death due to the primary disease. These findings suggest that certain non‐DN kidney diseases may harbor extremely poor‐prognosis conditions, underscoring the importance of early and accurate diagnosis. In the present study, early identification of such conditions in non‐DN patients may be attributable to improved clinical outcomes, as reflected in the rate of improvement in proteinuria and the decline rate in eGFR before and after biopsy.

Patients diagnosed with DN had a median proteinuria of 2.3 g/gCr before kidney biopsy and a mean eGFR slope of −5.9 mL/min/1.73 m2/year, neither of which changed significantly after kidney biopsy (Figure 4a,b). In contrast, patients diagnosed with non‐DN showed significant improvements in median proteinuria from 1.6 to 0.3 g/gCr (P < 0.001) and in the mean eGFR slope from −7.7 to −0.4 mL/min/1.73m2/year (P < 0.05) before and after kidney biopsy (Figure 4a,b). Most patients diagnosed with DN did not experience changes in their treatment before and after a kidney biopsy (Figure 4a,b). In contrast, disease‐specific therapies appeared to improve renal outcomes for those diagnosed with non‐DN. Therefore, we analyzed changes in proteinuria and the eGFR slope in non‐DN patients who received or did not receive a disease‐specific treatment, for whom we had available data on proteinuria and eGFR. The results indicated that non‐DN patients who did not receive additional treatment had no significant changes in the median proteinuria from 2.0 to 1.2 g/gCr (P > 0.1) or the mean eGFR slope from −5.6 to −7.2 mL/min/1.73m2/year (P > 0.99) before and after kidney biopsy (Figures 4c and 4d). However, patients who received disease‐specific treatment showed significant improvements in median proteinuria, from 1.4 to 0.2 g/gCr (P < 0.0005), and in mean eGFR slope, from −9.0 to 3.9 mL/min/1.73 m2/year (P < 0.01) (Figures 4c and 4d).

DISCUSSIONS

This study demonstrates that about two‐thirds of patients with type 2 diabetes and proteinuria atypical for DN were ultimately diagnosed with non‐DN. These patients experienced improvements in both proteinuria and the eGFR slope. Conversely, patients diagnosed with DN did not show these improvements. Although renal biopsy findings in patients with diabetes and atypical proteinuria have been reported previously, there have been very few reports of subsequent improvement after biopsy and treatment in patients ultimately diagnosed with non‐DN. Moreover, demonstrating improvement in proteinuria and in the eGFR slope before and after renal biopsy is a novel aspect of this study. These findings highlight the clinical utility of kidney biopsy in patients with diabetes and atypical proteinuria.

In this study, membranous nephropathy, IgA nephropathy, interstitial nephritis, AAV, and amyloidosis were identified as causes of non‐DN. If these conditions remain undetected and untreated, renal function is likely to deteriorate. Therefore, timely treatment following a renal biopsy diagnosis is crucial to improve kidney outcomes. Patients with non‐DN tended to be older, highlighting the risk that kidney diseases may be overlooked in older individuals with type 2 diabetes.

In the non‐DN group, notable improvements were seen in both proteinuria and eGFR slope after kidney biopsy. This indicates that an accurate diagnosis facilitated the initiation of disease‐specific treatments, which helped maintain kidney function. For example, treatable conditions like membranous nephropathy and IgA nephropathy were likely addressed with immunosuppressive therapy, AAV with corticosteroids, and interstitial nephritis through removal of causative agents. These diagnostic differences directly impacted treatment decisions. Conversely, patients diagnosed with DN probably had limited additional treatment options beyond standard diabetes care, which may explain why their renal outcomes did not improve.

Previous studies have recognized that non‐DN can be present in some diabetic patients with proteinuria 15 , 16 , 17 , and have reported no significant difference in renal prognosis between patients diagnosed with DN and those with non‐DN. Indeed, in our study, two patients with non‐DN had poor outcomes. However, these findings should not be interpreted as proof that differentiating between DN and non‐DN is useless. Instead, our data indicate that increased awareness of unusual proteinuria and early treatment can lead to better outcomes for many non‐DN patients, suggesting that poor prognosis may partly reflect delayed diagnosis. These findings underscore the importance of identifying and actively pursuing different diagnoses when proteinuria appears atypical for DN.

Clinical features traditionally suggestive of non‐DN in diabetic patients, such as the absence of diabetic retinopathy, glomerular hematuria, and persistent proteinuria despite intensive diabetes care, have been incorporated into the referral criteria jointly established by the JDS and the JSN. Using these criteria, our retrospective analysis of kidney biopsy findings showed that advanced age may remain a predictor of non‐DN even among patients meeting these clinical indications. This observation is particularly relevant in rapidly aging societies, where CKD prevalence and dialysis initiation are increasingly concentrated in older individuals. As age‐related renal pathologies become more common, the risk of misattributing non‐DN to DN may also increase. Therefore, without overestimating the prevalence of DN solely on the basis of long diabetes duration, kidney biopsy may be useful in elderly patients with atypical proteinuria.

The duration of diabetes helps differentiate DN from non‐DN. Non‐DN is more common within 5 years of onset, while DN is more likely after longer periods (>12 years) 20 . While this pattern may still apply, in our cohort, the mean diabetes duration in the non‐DN group was almost 10 years, and no clear difference was observed between the DN and non‐DN groups. This likely reflects contemporary shifts, including improved diabetes care with fewer severe DN cases and an increasing burden of age‐related renal pathologies in elderly patients. Consequently, the coexistence of DN and non‐DN may be becoming more frequent in older individuals, increasing the risk of misclassifying non‐DN as DN. Therefore, elderly patients with atypical proteinuria should be considered an important target population for kidney biopsy, without overestimating the prevalence of DN solely on the basis of long diabetes duration. Our findings highlight the need for heightened diagnostic vigilance and individualized management in elderly patients with diabetes.

In this study, DN lesions were identified based on the “Manual for Pathological Diagnosis of Diabetic Nephropathy and Hypertensive Nephrosclerosis.” Although GBM thickening, detected via electron microscopy, is an early indicator of DN, EM was not performed in all cases. Consequently, GBM thickening was not part of the diagnostic criteria here. Adding EM assessments could increase the number of cases showing DN features; however, because the non‐DN group includes cases with coexisting non‐DN features despite DN lesions, this would not significantly alter the total number of non‐DN cases or affect our overall findings.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the clinical significance of proteinuria atypical for DN; therefore, cases with decreased eGFR without proteinuria were not assessed. Although renal biopsy in such non‐proteinuric cases is currently rare, an aging society includes kidney diseases without proteinuria, such as tubulointerstitial disease, hematologic disorders, and drug‐induced nephropathy, which may be overlooked as nephrosclerosis. The need to differentiate these non‐proteinuric, non‐diabetic kidney diseases is likely to increase, highlighting the importance of future investigations in this population.

This study has several limitations. It was a single‐center, retrospective observational study conducted at a tertiary care institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The indication for kidney biopsy was based on clinical judgment, introducing potential selection bias. The follow‐up period was not standardized, and long‐term renal endpoints such as progression to renal replacement therapy or mortality were not evaluated. The sample size was small, particularly for subgroup analyses, potentially reducing statistical power. With a larger sample size, it may be possible to further evaluate the clinical significance of hematuria, particularly the number of urinary red blood cells. Furthermore, multivariate analysis was not performed to avoid overfitting; therefore, the results of univariate analyses should be interpreted as exploratory. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow‐up are needed.

This study demonstrates that kidney biopsy is useful for diagnosis and prognosis in patients with type 2 diabetes presenting with proteinuria atypical for DN (Figure 5). In particular, it reaffirms the importance of recognizing the diversity of kidney diseases in elderly patients and seizing opportunities for appropriate therapeutic intervention. Moving forward, it is necessary to reconsider clinical algorithms that incorporate kidney biopsy and to conduct prospective studies evaluating therapeutic interventions for non‐DN and their long‐term outcomes. To improve renal prognosis in patients with diabetes, individualized assessment and intervention, rather than reliance on typical clinical patterns, are essential.

Algorithm for identifying a kidney disease in patients with type 2 diabetes and proteinuria atypical for diabetic nephropathy. For patients with type 2 diabetes and proteinuria ≥0.5 g/gCr, the presence of any of the following—absence of diabetic retinopathy, resistance to multidisciplinary treatment, or glomerular‐type microscopic hematuria—justifies actively considering a renal biopsy to diagnose potential treatable kidney diseases, whether diabetic nephropathy is present. This flowchart illustrates the referral criteria recommended by the Japan Diabetes Society and the Japanese Society of Nephrology. While it shows that renal biopsy should be actively considered when these criteria are met, it does not imply that biopsy isn't necessary for patients outside these guidelines. Clinicians should also perform renal biopsies when clinically appropriate, even if the criteria are not met.
