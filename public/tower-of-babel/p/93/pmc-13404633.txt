Neuroimaging biomarkers for predicting acupuncture treatment response in chronic pain: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Ji Wu, Qiongxia Yu, Zhangmeng Xu, Biao Wang, Ziwen Chen, Qifu Li, Tao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionAcupuncture is increasingly recognised as an effective treatment for chronic pain conditions, yet inter-individual variability in treatment response remains a major clinical challenge. Recent neuroimaging studies suggest that baseline brain characteristics may serve as objective biomarkers for predicting therapeutic outcomes, with some achieving classification accuracies exceeding 80%. These findings, however, have not been systematically synthesised. This review will be the first to systematically evaluate whether pre-treatment neuroimaging biomarkers can predict acupuncture treatment response in adults with chronic pain.Methods and analysisThis protocol is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines. We will search MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, PubMed, Web of Science and four Chinese databases (China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang, VIP and CBM) from inception to the search date without date restrictions. Eligible studies must include adults with chronic pain (≥3 months duration), baseline neuroimaging assessment (functional MRI, structural MRI, positron emission tomography (PET) or single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT)) and needle-based acupuncture intervention with post-treatment clinical outcomes. Two reviewers will independently screen studies, extract data and assess risk of bias using the Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool, Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 and Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Primary outcomes include predictive performance metrics (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve) and identification of specific brain regions with predictive value. Meta-analysis will be performed using random-effects models when sufficient homogeneous studies are available. Where studies classify responders versus non-responders, diagnostic test accuracy meta-analysis (bivariate/hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic (ROC models)) will pool sensitivity and specificity. Coordinate-based meta-analysis using activation likelihood estimation will be conducted if 10 or more studies report stereotactic coordinates. The completed review will be reported following the PRISMA 2020 statement.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required as this study involves secondary analysis of published data. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication and conference presentations. Results are expected to support the development of neuroimaging-based clinical decision tools and to guide future biomarker validation studies.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420261290372.Keywords: Acupuncture, Chronic Pain, Systematic Review, Meta-Analysis, Neurology

Introduction

Chronic pain, defined as pain persisting or recurring for more than 3 months, is one of the most prevalent and burdensome health conditions worldwide. Global estimates indicate that approximately 20% of adults experience chronic pain, with prevalence and associated disability continuing to rise worldwide,1 and the condition ranks among the leading causes of years lived with disability.2 In the USA alone, over 50 million adults are affected,3 with direct and indirect costs estimated at $722.8 billion annually.4 The economic costs are substantial, but chronic pain also takes a wider toll. It diminishes quality of life, contributes to depression and anxiety, and in many patients remains poorly controlled by conventional pharmacological approaches.

Acupuncture has gained increasing acceptance within mainstream healthcare for chronic pain management. Large-scale individual patient data meta-analyses from the Acupuncture Trialists’ Collaboration, encompassing nearly 21 000 patients across 39 randomised controlled trials, have shown that acupuncture provides statistically and clinically significant benefits compared with both sham controls and no-acupuncture controls for chronic musculoskeletal pain, headache disorders and osteoarthritis.5 These therapeutic effects persist over time, and emerging evidence continues to refine our understanding of acupuncture outcomes across different chronic pain conditions.6 Systematic reviews and meta-analyses support acupuncture for migraine prophylaxis,7 chronic low back pain8 and tension-type headache,9 and clinical practice guidelines from organisations including the American College of Physicians now recommend acupuncture as a first-line non-pharmacological treatment option.10

Despite this growing evidence base, a fundamental challenge in clinical practice persists: treatment response to acupuncture varies substantially across individuals. Population-level efficacy is established, but predicting which patients will experience meaningful improvement remains elusive. Analysis of response distributions in the Acupuncture Trialists’ Collaboration data revealed no evidence of exceptional responders5; pain score distributions were similar in shape between acupuncture and control groups, underscoring how difficult it is to identify responders from clinical characteristics alone. This variability complicates treatment selection, healthcare resource allocation and the management of patient expectations.

Precision medicine approaches offer a way forward. Instead of treating heterogeneous populations uniformly, precision pain medicine aims to match interventions to individual patients using composite biomarker signatures. Recent expert reviews have accordingly identified objective biomarkers that predict treatment response as a key priority for advancing precision pain medicine.11

Neuroimaging offers a particularly attractive avenue for biomarker discovery in this context. Chronic pain conditions are associated with characteristic alterations in brain structure and function, including changes in grey matter volume, functional connectivity patterns and metabolic activity within pain-processing networks.12 13 Key regions implicated include the insula, anterior cingulate cortex, prefrontal cortex, somatosensory cortices and components of the default mode network. These neural signatures are thought to reflect the pathophysiology of chronic pain, and there is growing interest in whether they may also carry information about who will respond to treatment.

Recent studies have begun to show that baseline brain characteristics can predict acupuncture treatment outcomes with appreciable accuracy. Tu and colleagues14 demonstrated that pre-treatment resting-state functional connectivity patterns predicted symptom changes following acupuncture in chronic low back pain patients, with different connectivity signatures predicting responses to real versus sham acupuncture. Wang and colleagues15 achieved classification accuracy exceeding 80% in predicting acupuncture response in knee osteoarthritis using a combination of psychological variables and neuroimaging features including posterior cingulate cortex thickness and striatal activity. In migraine, baseline grey matter volumes in frontal and parietal regions achieved 83% accuracy in distinguishing responders from non-responders.16 Together, these findings point to objective, measurable neural features that could inform treatment decisions at the individual level.

This evidence, however, has never been systematically synthesised. Existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses of acupuncture neuroimaging have focused on treatment mechanisms,17 18 examining how brain activity changes after acupuncture, rather than on whether pre-treatment characteristics can predict who will respond. No review has specifically examined baseline neuroimaging as a predictor of therapeutic outcomes. This is a meaningful gap: the clinical utility of neuroimaging biomarkers ultimately rests on their predictive value, not their mechanistic interest.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to fill this gap. Specifically, we will quantify the predictive performance of different brain biomarkers and identify the brain regions and networks with the strongest predictive value. We will also compare predictive accuracy across neuroimaging modalities and chronic pain conditions, and assess whether combining neuroimaging with clinical and psychological factors improves prediction. The findings should inform the development of clinical prediction tools and help set priorities for future biomarker validation research.

Methods and analysis

This protocol is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 guidelines19 and has been registered prospectively with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO). The completed review will be reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement.20 Any protocol amendments will be documented in the PROSPERO registry with dates and rationale, and reported in the final publication with justification. The planned review start date is 1 January 2027, with anticipated completion by 31 December 2027. Literature searching and study selection are planned for January–March 2027; data extraction and risk of bias assessment for April–June 2027; data synthesis and manuscript preparation for July–December 2027.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not directly involved in the design of this systematic review protocol. The research question was informed by clinical observations of treatment response variability and patient preferences for personalised treatment selection. Dissemination plans include patient-facing summaries of findings distributed through chronic pain patient associations and traditional Chinese medicine community platforms in China, as well as plain language summaries accompanying the published review.

Eligibility criteria

Population

Adults aged 18 years or older with chronic pain, defined as pain persisting or recurring for 3 months or longer. Eligible chronic pain conditions include, but are not limited to, chronic low back pain, osteoarthritis, migraine and other headache disorders, fibromyalgia, neuropathic pain and chronic musculoskeletal pain. Studies must include baseline neuroimaging assessment conducted prior to treatment initiation. Both sexes will be included without restriction.

For analysis, eligible chronic pain conditions will be grouped into pre-specified pain phenotypes, following the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP)//International Classification of Diseases 11th Revision (ICD-11) chronic pain classification: chronic musculoskeletal pain (eg, chronic low back pain, osteoarthritis and other chronic musculoskeletal conditions); chronic neuropathic pain (eg, painful peripheral neuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia and radicular pain with a confirmed neuropathic component); chronic visceral pain (eg, chronic pelvic or abdominal pain) and chronic primary pain, encompassing nociplastic conditions such as fibromyalgia and chronic widespread pain, as well as chronic primary headache disorders (migraine and tension-type headache). Studies enrolling mixed populations will be assigned to the predominant phenotype when at least 80% of participants share a single category, and will otherwise be analysed as a separate mixed group. Neuroimaging biomarkers will be categorised by modality (resting-state functional MRI, task-based functional MRI, structural MRI and positron emission tomography (PET)/single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT)) and, where reported, by analytic feature type (eg, functional connectivity, regional activation, grey-matter morphometry or metabolic measures). These pain-phenotype and biomarker-modality categories define the primary strata for subgroup analysis, and quantitative pooling will be restricted to studies sharing a comparable phenotype and modality so that biologically distinct predictors are not pooled.

Intervention

Needle-based acupuncture interventions including manual acupuncture, electroacupuncture and warm needling administered at traditional acupuncture points. Any acupoint selection, treatment frequency or protocol will be accepted. The minimum treatment duration is 2 weeks with at least four treatment sessions. Interventions must be administered by qualified practitioners, including licensed acupuncturists, trained physicians or traditional Chinese medicine practitioners. Stable concurrent analgesic medications will be permitted.

Comparator

Any comparator will be acceptable, including sham acupuncture, usual care, waiting list control, active treatment comparators or no comparator. Single-arm cohort studies examining baseline neuroimaging predictors of treatment response will be eligible.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes are predictive performance metrics of neuroimaging biomarkers, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), positive and negative predictive values and correlation coefficients between baseline neuroimaging features and clinical outcomes. Secondary outcomes include identification of specific brain regions or networks with predictive value, comparison of predictive performance across neuroimaging modalities (functional MRI, structural MRI, PET, SPECT) and comparison between neuroimaging-only and multimodal prediction models incorporating clinical and psychological variables.

Timing

Baseline neuroimaging must be conducted before treatment initiation, defined as within 2 weeks prior to the first acupuncture session. Clinical outcomes must be assessed following the treatment period, typically at treatment completion (2–12 weeks) or at follow-up (1–6 months). Studies must report the relationship between baseline imaging features and post-treatment clinical outcomes.

Study design

Randomised controlled trials (primary design of interest), prospective cohort studies and retrospective cohort studies (if baseline imaging clearly preceded treatment) will be eligible. The minimum sample size is 10 participants per group for randomised trials or 10 total for cohort studies. Cross-sectional studies without treatment outcomes will be excluded. Information sources

We will conduct a search across multiple electronic databases from inception to the search date without date restrictions. English-language databases will include MEDLINE (via Ovid), Embase (via Ovid), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), PubMed (for records not indexed in MEDLINE) and Web of Science Core Collection. Chinese-language databases will include China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, VIP Database (Chinese Scientific Journals Database) and Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (CBM/SinoMed). The inclusion of Chinese databases is warranted given that China is a major contributor to acupuncture research, and a substantial body of relevant literature is published in Chinese.

Clinical trial registries will be searched to identify ongoing or completed but unpublished studies, including ClinicalTrials.gov, the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform and Chinese Clinical Trial Registry. Reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews will be screened to identify additional eligible studies. Forward citation searching will be performed using Web of Science for key included studies. Authors of included studies will be contacted to request unpublished data or clarify methodological details when necessary.

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed in consultation with a medical librarian with expertise in systematic review methodology. The strategy combines controlled vocabulary terms (MeSH, Emtree) and free-text keywords for three core concepts: acupuncture and related techniques, neuroimaging modalities and chronic pain conditions. An additional fourth concept (prediction/biomarker terminology) was applied in MEDLINE and Embase to improve specificity; this concept was intentionally omitted from CENTRAL, PubMed, Web of Science and the Chinese database searches to maximise sensitivity, as these databases have more limited indexing for prediction-related terminology. The complete search strategy for MEDLINE is provided in online supplemental appendix 1. This strategy will be adapted for each database, accounting for differences in indexing and syntax.

There will be no language restrictions. Studies published in English or Chinese will be included directly. Studies in other languages will be assessed for eligibility based on title and abstract, with full-text translation obtained if potentially eligible.

Study selection

Search results will be exported to EndNote V.X9 for deduplication using the Bramer method for systematic deduplication. Unique records will then be imported into Covidence systematic review software for screening. Title and abstract screening will be performed independently by two reviewers. Studies deemed potentially eligible by either reviewer will proceed to full-text review. Full-text articles will be assessed against the eligibility criteria independently by two reviewers. Disagreements at either stage will be resolved through discussion, with referral to a third reviewer if consensus cannot be reached.

A PRISMA flow diagram will document the study selection process (figure 1), including numbers of records identified, screened, excluded (with reasons) and included. Inter-rater reliability for study selection will be reported using Cohen’s kappa statistic.

Data extraction

Data will be extracted into a standardised Microsoft Excel spreadsheet based on the Checklist for Critical Appraisal and Data Extraction for Systematic Reviews of Prediction Modelling Studies.21 The extraction form will be piloted on the first five eligible studies and refined as needed. Two reviewers will extract data independently. After the first five studies, extraction results will be compared and calibrated; if agreement is high, the remaining studies will be extracted by one reviewer and verified by the second. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion or referral to a third reviewer.

Extracted data will include: study characteristics (first author, year, country, study design, sample size, funding source), participant characteristics (age, sex distribution, chronic pain condition, pain duration, baseline pain severity), neuroimaging characteristics (modality, scanner field strength, acquisition parameters, brain regions or networks analysed, analysis software), acupuncture intervention details (type, acupoints, frequency, duration, practitioner qualifications), comparator details, clinical outcome measures and assessment timing, prediction model characteristics (predictors, model type, internal and external validation methods) and predictive performance metrics with 95% CIs. For studies using machine learning, the specific algorithm, feature selection method and cross-validation approach will be recorded. Missing data

Authors will be contacted if essential data are missing or unclear. If no response is received after two attempts over 4 weeks, available data will be used and the limitations noted. When predictive performance metrics are not directly reported but can be derived from available statistics (eg, constructing 2×2 tables from reported sensitivity and specificity or estimating SEs from CIs), we will calculate these values and document the derivation. Studies with missing data that cannot be obtained or reasonably estimated will be retained in narrative synthesis but excluded from the corresponding quantitative analysis. The effect of missing data on pooled estimates will be examined through sensitivity analyses comparing results with and without studies that required imputation or had incomplete reporting.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias will be assessed using multiple validated tools matched to study design and outcome type. For prediction model development and validation studies, the Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool (PROBAST) will be used,22 evaluating four domains: participants, predictors, outcome and analysis. For randomised controlled trials, the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB-2) tool will be applied,23 assessing bias arising from the randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome and selection of the reported result. For non-randomised studies, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) will be used, evaluating selection, comparability and outcome domains.

Neuroimaging study quality will additionally be assessed using a customised checklist adapted from prior coordinate-based meta-analyses. This checklist evaluates sample characteristics (sample size adequacy, demographic matching, clinical characterisation) and methodological reporting (scanner parameters, preprocessing pipeline, statistical thresholding, multiple comparison correction, coordinate reporting). Data synthesis

Quantitative synthesis

Meta-analysis will be performed when three or more studies report comparable outcomes with sufficient methodological and clinical homogeneity. When fewer than three studies are available for a given outcome or when clinical or methodological heterogeneity is too great for meaningful pooling, findings will be synthesised narratively rather than quantitatively. Different pooling approaches will be used depending on the outcome measure. Correlation coefficients will be transformed using Fisher’s Z transformation, pooled using random-effects models with the DerSimonian-Laird estimator and back-transformed to r for interpretation. Where studies classify patients as responders versus non-responders, we will perform a diagnostic test accuracy meta-analysis. Paired sensitivity and specificity will be pooled using the bivariate random-effects model, which, in the absence of study-level covariates, is equivalent to the hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic model, to generate summary estimates of sensitivity and specificity, a summary receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and 95% confidence and prediction regions, following the methods recommended in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Diagnostic Test Accuracy.24 25 For studies reporting sensitivity and specificity, or providing sufficient data to reconstruct the 2×2 contingency table, the bivariate model will be fitted directly; when only the AUC is reported, AUC values will be summarised separately using random-effects meta-analysis with variances derived from the reported CIs or the Hanley-McNeil method, and will not be combined with the bivariate analysis. When few studies are available (typically fewer than four) and the bivariate model does not converge, we will simplify the covariance structure or pool sensitivity and specificity separately using univariate random-effects models, interpreting the results with appropriate caution. Standardised effect sizes will be pooled using random-effects inverse variance weighting. Analyses will be conducted in R (packages metafor, mada, meta) and Stata.

Coordinate-based meta-analysis

If 10 or more studies report stereotactic coordinates (Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) or Talairach space) for brain regions predictive of treatment response, coordinate-based meta-analysis will be performed using activation likelihood estimation with GingerALE software. This analysis identifies brain regions showing consistent predictive value across studies while accounting for spatial uncertainty in neuroimaging results. Statistical significance will be assessed using family-wise error correction at p<0.05 with cluster-level inference. If fewer than 10 studies report coordinates, reported brain regions will be tabulated and synthesised qualitatively, identifying areas of convergence across studies without formal spatial meta-analysis.

Synthesis of high-dimensional and machine-learning prediction data

A substantial proportion of neuroimaging prediction studies use high-dimensional feature sets and machine-learning or multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA) rather than single-predictor models. These approaches raise specific analytical challenges that make the direct pooling of reported accuracies inappropriate, including the curse of dimensionality, overfitting, optimistic performance arising from inadequate validation and information leakage between feature selection and model evaluation. We have therefore pre-specified the following strategy, which complements the model-level data extraction and PROBAST appraisal described above. For each machine-learning or MVPA study we will additionally record the dimensionality of the feature space and the events-per-variable ratio (the number of outcome events relative to the number of candidate predictors); the dimensionality-reduction or feature-selection procedure and whether it was performed within or outside the cross-validation loop (to identify information leakage); the resampling and validation scheme (k-fold or repeated cross-validation, nested cross-validation, bootstrapping or independent external validation); any correction for multiple comparisons in mass-univariate or searchlight analyses; and whether performance was reported as apparent, internally validated (optimism-corrected) or externally validated. Reporting completeness and risk of bias specific to prediction and machine-learning models will be appraised against the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis + Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD+AI) statement,26 in addition to PROBAST, with explicit judgements on sample-size adequacy and overfitting. Because algorithms, feature sets and validation designs are expected to differ markedly across studies, we will not combine heterogeneous models into a single algorithm-level estimate. Predictive performance will first be synthesised following the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) guideline, stratified by the pain-phenotype and neuroimaging-modality categories defined above and by validation type, using tabulation and effect-direction (harvest) plots to display the magnitude and consistency of discrimination across studies. Only where a sufficient number of conceptually comparable models (pre-specified as three or more) report the same discrimination metric under comparable validation (preferably external validation or rigorous internal validation) will we quantitatively pool model performance, using random-effects meta-analysis of the c-statistic (AUC) following the methods of Debray and colleagues for the meta-analysis of prediction-model performance,27 with calibration assessed separately from discrimination. Apparent (non-validated) performance will not be meta-analysed; where models report only apparent accuracy, results will be presented descriptively and flagged as being at high risk of optimism. The dependence of pooled discrimination on validation type, feature dimensionality and risk of bias will be examined in the pre-specified subgroup and sensitivity analyses.

Heterogeneity assessment

Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed using the I² statistic, interpreted as not important (0%–40%), moderate (30%–60%), substantial (50%–90%) or considerable (75%–100%). The τ² statistic with 95% prediction intervals will quantify between-study variance. Cochran’s Q test will assess the presence of heterogeneity. When substantial heterogeneity is detected (I²>50%), sources will be explored through pre-specified subgroup analyses and meta-regression.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Pre-specified subgroup analyses will be conducted when three or more studies are available per subgroup; subgroups with fewer studies will be reported descriptively (table 4). Subgroups will include chronic pain phenotype (musculoskeletal, neuropathic, visceral and primary/nociplastic pain) and, where sufficient data permit, individual condition type (low back pain, osteoarthritis, migraine, fibromyalgia); neuroimaging modality (resting-state fMRI, task-based fMRI, structural MRI, PET/SPECT); prediction method (traditional statistical methods vs machine learning); model type (neuroimaging-only vs multimodal); study design (randomised vs observational); risk of bias level (low vs high) and validation type (internal only vs external). Meta-regression will be conducted if 10 or more studies are available, with publication year, sample size, mean age, pain duration, baseline pain severity and scanner field strength examined as potential moderators. If fewer than 10 studies are available, these variables will be explored descriptively rather than through formal meta-regression. Sensitivity analyses will assess the stability of findings by excluding studies with high risk of bias, excluding studies with very small samples (<20 participants), comparing results with and without studies that required data imputation and using alternative statistical models (eg, restricted maximum likelihood estimator in place of DerSimonian-Laird).

Narrative synthesis

When quantitative pooling is not feasible due to insufficient studies or excessive heterogeneity, narrative synthesis will follow the SWiM guidelines.28 This will include vote counting with effect direction, harvest plots and qualitative synthesis of consistently reported brain regions and networks. Results will be structured around the primary and secondary objectives.

Assessment of reporting bias

Publication bias will be assessed when 10 or more studies are available for a given meta-analysis. Funnel plot asymmetry will be evaluated visually and statistically using Egger’s regression test for standard meta-analyses and Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test for diagnostic accuracy meta-analyses. If publication bias is suspected, the trim-and-fill method will be used to estimate the potential impact on pooled estimates.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence for primary outcomes will be assessed using the GRADE approach. Evidence will be evaluated across five domains: risk of bias (based on PROBAST, RoB-2 and NOS assessments), inconsistency (assessed via I² statistics and prediction intervals), indirectness (evaluated by examining alignment with the review question), imprecision (judged by sample sizes and CI widths) and publication bias (assessed via funnel plots and statistical tests when sufficient studies are available).

Evidence will be rated as high, moderate, low or very low certainty. High certainty indicates strong confidence that the true effect lies close to the estimate; very low certainty indicates that the true effect may differ substantially from the estimate. Evidence may be upgraded for large effect sizes (pooled AUC >0.80), dose-response relationships or when all plausible confounding would reduce the observed effect. Two reviewers will perform GRADE assessments independently, with disagreements resolved through discussion. Findings will be presented in GRADE evidence profile tables and summary of findings tables for the primary outcomes of prediction accuracy and identified biomarkers.

Ethics and dissemination

This systematic review involves secondary analysis of published aggregate data and does not require ethical approval or informed consent. The review findings will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed open-access journal and presented at relevant international conferences, including meetings of the IASP and the Society for Acupuncture Research.

We anticipate that this review will have implications at several levels. On the research side, identifying the most promising neuroimaging biomarkers and brain regions will help shape prospective validation studies with adequate power and standardised protocols; methodological gaps and sources of heterogeneity uncovered here should also guide improvements in study design and reporting. Clinically, if strong predictive biomarkers are confirmed, the review will lay the groundwork for decision support tools that match patients to treatments based on individual brain characteristics. At the policy level, evidence supporting the feasibility of biomarker-guided treatment selection could inform coverage decisions and resource allocation for acupuncture services.

We also plan to prepare plain language summaries for patients and clinicians and to share key findings through infographics, social media and professional networks. The full dataset and analysis code will be deposited in a public repository to support transparency and replication.

Discussion

This systematic review will provide the first synthesis of evidence on neuroimaging biomarkers for predicting acupuncture treatment response in chronic pain. By focusing on pre-treatment prediction rather than post-treatment mechanisms, this review addresses a clinically actionable question: can we identify, before treatment begins, which patients will benefit from acupuncture?

Several challenges are anticipated. The field is relatively young, and the number of eligible studies may be small; we have therefore adopted broad eligibility criteria to maximise inclusiveness without sacrificing methodological rigour. Heterogeneity across neuroimaging modalities, pain conditions, acupuncture protocols and analytical approaches may limit quantitative pooling, which is why we pre-specified subgroup analyses and a narrative synthesis framework. Another concern is that many existing studies have small sample sizes and lack external validation, potentially inflating reported prediction accuracy. The PROBAST assessment will help identify these limitations, and sensitivity analyses will test the robustness of pooled findings.

Despite these challenges, the potential clinical value of identifying valid neuroimaging predictors is considerable. Chronic pain affects millions worldwide, and acupuncture is a safe, widely accessible treatment. If biomarkers can reliably identify likely responders, clinicians could direct patients to the therapies most likely to help them while avoiding unnecessary treatments and their associated costs. This review will map the current state of the evidence and highlight where future work is most needed.
