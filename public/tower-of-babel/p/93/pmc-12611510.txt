Combined thoracic paravertebral block and pectoralis nerve I and II block as a sole technique for awake breast surgery - A case report -
Authors: Sarah Easby
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRegional anesthetic techniques are becoming increasingly more common for a variety of surgeries. Current literature regarding regional anesthesia for breast surgery also involves sedation or general anesthesia (GA).CaseA 53 year old female underwent wide local excision breast surgery and sentinel node biopsy under combined thoracic paravertebral block and pectoralis nerve I and II block without concurrent sedation or GA.ConclusionsThis case report demonstrates patient acceptability for completely awake breast surgery with good surgical conditions. This technique could be used for high risk or motivated patients where a GA and its associated complications could be avoided.Keywords: Breast cancer, Breast surgery, Nerve block, Paravertebral block, Pectoralis muscles

Breast cancer is the most common cancer in the UK, accounting for 15% of all new cancer cases. This totals around 55,900 new breast cancer cases in the UK every year (2016–2018), with incidence rates increasing by 18% since the 1990s, with a quarter of new diagnoses in people aged 75 and over [1]. Data from the 7th National Audit Project of the Royal College of Anaesthetists (NAP7) in the UK shows rising age, rates of obesity and comorbidity in surgical patients requiring anesthesia [2] which could have impacts on the elective surgery day case target of 75% [3]. Regional techniques for breast surgery have been shown to improve analgesia, opioid spare and enhance recovery [4] therefore can be employed as techniques for patients considered to be anesthetic high risk and reduce inpatient stay. This manuscript was written in accordance with the CARE checklist. The patient gave written informed consent for this case report.

CASE REPORT

A 53 year old (height, 1.64 m; weight, 66.8 kg; body mass index, 24.8), American Society of Anesthesiologists status II female, was scheduled for wide local excision breast surgery and sentinel node biopsy. She was diagnosed with a left sided upper outer quadrant, T1bN0, 7-mm ductal carcinoma with surrounding ductal carcinoma in situ up to 13 mm. The patient did not have any other co-morbidities to consider her anesthetic high risk however she was highly motivated not to have a general anesthetic or sedation.

No premedication was administered. Standard monitoring as per Association of Anaesthetists of Great Britain and Ireland guidelines was applied in the anesthetic room. This included pulse oximetry, three lead electrocardiogram and non-invasive blood pressure. A thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) was done at level T2 and T4 with the patient in a sitting position. The skin was prepared using a chlorhexidine gluconate (20 mg/ml) and isopropyl alcohol (0.70 ml/ml) solution (ChloraPrep with Tint, Becton Dickinson). A Fujifilm SonoSite L12-3 MHz linear transducer was used to identify the parietal pleura, transverse process and desired paravertebral space. A 50 mm, 22G SonoPlex Facet II NRFit (Pajunk) was guided in an in-plane technique towards the desired space. Once negative aspiration confirmed, 10 ml of 0.25% levobupivacaine was infiltrated at T2 with a further 10 ml of 0.25% levobupivacaine at T4.

Pectoralis nerve (PECS) block was undertaken with an in-plane approach with the patient in the supine position with the arm abducted. The transducer was placed under the mid clavicle then moved caudally and laterally to identify the third rib and the pectoralis major, minor and serratus anterior muscles. An in-plane approach was used to advance the needle between the pectoralis minor and serratus muscle, 20 ml 1% prilocaine was infiltrated at PECS II. Followed by 10 ml of 1% prilocaine between the pectoralis major and minor muscles at PECS I, visualising the spread of anesthetic between the muscle layers.

It took 15 min to perform the TPVB and 10 min to complete the PECS block. Appropriate surgical conditions were achieved within an additional 30 min. Confirmation was tested with ethyl chloride and the surgeon tested sensation with forceps prior to the incision.

Intra-operatively, dexamethasone 6.6 mg was administered as there is evidence to suggest that it increases the length of analgesia duration or regional blocks [5]. A total of 600 µg of alfentanyl was administered divided into 200 μg aliquots over a total operating time of 45 min for mild to moderate patient discomfort with good effect. Observations remained stable throughout the operation. There were no signs of local anesthetic toxicity or pneumothorax in recovery. The patient was able to eat and drink immediately postoperation and was discharged the same day. No additional analgesia was required in recovery. There was an immediate return to baseline activities postoperatively, one dose of 30 mg dihydrocodeine and 1 g of paracetamol was taken on the first postoperative day with no other analgesia required on following days. There were no anesthetic related postoperative complications.

DISCUSSION

The skin of the breast, breast gland and nipple are supplied by the second to sixth intercostal nerves (T2–T6) which are adequately blocked with TPVB by injecting local anaesthetic into the space where the nerves reliably leave the intervertebral foramina [6].

The pectoralis major and minor muscles underlie the breast and are supplied by the medial and lateral pectoral nerves. The serratus anterior muscle is supplied by the long thoracic nerve. PECS I targets the lateral and medial pectoral nerves between the major and minor muscles [6]. These nerves and the long thoracic nerves are more reliably blocked distally via a PECS II block, hence the inclusion in this case to cover the field of lymph node biopsy.

The current literature describes multiple combinations of TPVB, PECS block [7] or serratus anterior plane block alongside sedation with propofol [8], remifentanil or dexmedetomidine. Various levels of sedation were achieved, with some maintaining verbal contact, none provided no sedation.

Approximately 40% of women who undergo breast surgery with or without axillary dissection suffer chronic pain after surgery [9]. Regional anesthesia has been shown to reduce acute postoperative pain and incidence of chronic pain after breast surgery [9]. A systematic review showed that awake breast surgery appears advantageous in terms of cost and recovery after surgery, without increasing the surgical burden on the patient or on the result of the operation [10]. TPVBs have been shown to reduce postoperative pain scores, analgesia consumption and the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting were documented to be lower when pectoral nerves block were performed [11]. It also has less impact on respiratory function than general anesthesia (GA) therefore decreasing the incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications [12]. PECS block has the advantage of no risk of sympathectomy that is usually associated with TPVB [13].

There is a risk of local anesthetic systemic toxicity when performing multiple blocks therefore there was careful calculation of toxic doses of local anesthetic prior to administration. Prilocaine was chosen as its toxic dose is 6 mg/kg compared to 2 mg/kg for levobupivacaine, therefore allowing the operator to give more volume over multiple areas. Both agents had a suitable duration of action to cover the operating time. However, there was a reasonably long period required to achieve suitable surgical conditions at 40 min from the end of the first block. This patient was highly motivated to avoid GA and was counselled on the risks and benefits. By being awake and being able to communicate with the surgeon and anesthetist throughout the surgery she felt in control of the situation. Overall, this case shows good patient acceptability to awake breast surgery without concurrent sedation and more cases could be performed awake in a well selected patient population.
