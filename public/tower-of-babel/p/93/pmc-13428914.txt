The Role of the Fear of Positive Evaluation in the Relationship Between Social Anxiety and the Pride Experience and Expression
Authors: Gorana Birovljević, Petra Anić, Asmir Gračanin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There is a significant gap in research concerning the relationship between social anxiety and the expression of pride. This study examines the mediating role of fear of positive evaluation in the relationship between social anxiety and both the tendency to express pride and the momentary experience of authentic and hubristic pride. A sample of 703 first-year undergraduate students from Croatia completed a survey assessing social anxiety, fear of positive evaluation, experience of authentic and hubristic pride and expression of pride. Fear of positive evaluation mediated the link between social anxiety and the tendency to express pride. Additionally, it also mediated the relationship between social anxiety and both authentic and hubristic pride. The results are consistent with evolutionary theories that explain the importance of social hierarchy in shaping the emotional experiences of socially anxious individuals. Findings highlight the significant role of fear of positive evaluation in determining how socially anxious individuals experience and express pride, emphasizing the need for further research on its effects in domains of social status and psychological well-being.

Introduction

Social anxiety refers to the experience of excessive fear and anxiety in social situations where an individual feels exposed to others’ judgment. Most people experience some fear of negative evaluation at times, with intensity varying both across and within individuals (Stein et al., 1994). Accordingly, research shows that social anxiety exists on a continuum, ranging from mild social fears that cause heightened arousal and inhibition to the more severe anxiety and avoidance seen in Social Anxiety Disorder (El-Gabalawy et al., 2010; Ruscio, 2010). The lifetime prevalence of Social Anxiety Disorder is estimated at approximately 12% in the general population; however, more recent studies report a substantially higher prevalence of elevated social anxiety, with estimates indicating that between 23% and 58% of individuals experience clinically significant social anxiety symptoms (Jefferies & Ungar, 2020). Elevated social anxiety is most common among adolescents and young adults, with up to 90% of cases emerging before age 23 (Kessler et al., 2005), which is not surprising, given that this period is marked by increased social interactions (Crone & Dahl, 2012; Leigh & Clark, 2018), and heightened sensitivity to social exclusion (Gunther Moor et al., 2010). The impact of social anxiety varies by severity, but even mild symptoms can reduce quality of life (Fehm et al., 2008). Higher levels are associated with difficulties in relationships, academic and work performance (Van Ameringen et al., 2003; Whisman et al., 2000), and increased risk of depression, suicidal thoughts, and substance use disorders (Buckner et al., 2019; Herres et al., 2019; Ohayon & Schatzberg, 2010).

Fear of negative evaluation, a core aspect of social anxiety, involves the belief that others will judge one harshly due to undesirable traits, socially inappropriate behavior or visible anxiety. The Bivalent Fear of Evaluation Model (Weeks & Howell, 2012) expands the understanding of social anxiety by introducing fear of positive evaluation as a separate but related construct. While distinct from fear of negative evaluation (Fredrick & Luebbe, 2024), fear of positive evaluation also correlates with symptoms of social anxiety (Karp et al., 2018; Reichenberger et al., 2015; Weeks et al., 2008, 2008). Individuals high in fear of positive evaluation worry they will be unable to meet the heightened expectations that may follow positive evaluation (Heimberg et al., 2010; Weeks et al., 2008). The Bivalent Fear of Evaluation Model aligns with Gilbert's (2001) evolutionary theory of social anxiety. Evolutionary theories suggest social anxiety serves an adaptive role in managing social hierarchies (Sapolsky, 2005). Specifically, submissiveness and behavioral inhibition, as core aspects of social anxiety, help individuals avoid conflict with dominant group members (Gilbert, 2014; Gilbert & Trower, 1990). Socially anxious individuals perceive themselves as lower in social rank (Berger et al., 2017; Peschard et al., 2018; Rodebaugh et al., 2016) and tend to withdraw or act submissively in response to dominant others (Dijk et al., 2018; Galili et al., 2013; Gilboa-Schechtman et al., 2014; Gilboa-Schechtman, 2020). Fear of positive evaluation is similarly linked to submissiveness, lower perceived rank, and the belief that praise may provoke conflict with higher-status individuals (Weeks et al., 2008, 2009, 2010; Weeks & Howell, 2012).

Unlike other anxiety disorders, social anxiety is linked not only to negative affect but also to low levels of positive affect (Kashdan, 2007; Kashdan et al., 2013), which is believed to play an important role in maintaining disorder symptoms (Naragon-Gainey et al., 2013). The emotion of pride accounts for a substantial proportion of the variance in the relationship between positive affect and social anxiety (Chin et al., 2023; Cohen & Huppert, 2018). Individuals with social anxiety report lower levels of pride. On the other hand, even momentary increase in the experience of pride is associated with reductions in social anxiety (Oren-Yagoda et al., 2023). The experience of pride has various positive effects, many of which are related to improvements in social status (Tracy et al., 2010). According to Sznycer et al.'s (2017) advertisement-recalibration theory, pride evolved to motivate individuals to acquire traits and engage in behaviors that elicit respect and positive evaluation from others, allowing them to benefit from the advantages such evaluation confers. An additional function of pride is “signaling”—pride ensures that information about an individual's desirable traits and behaviors is conveyed to others. This theory highlights the dynamic nature of social evaluation, where new information continually reshapes an individual's value attached to self and future interactions. Supporting this view, research shows that pride promotes more adaptive responses to success and failure, along with greater motivation to pursue socially valued goals (Tracy & Robins, 2007, 2007; Weidman et al., 2016). The advertising function of pride (Griskevicius et al., 2010; Sznycer et al., 2017) is supported by studies showing that the expression of pride is linked to perceptions of higher social rank and status (Brosi et al., 2016; Shariff et al., 2012; Steckler & Tracy, 2014; Williams & DeSteno, 2009; Witkower et al., 2020). Expressing pride can be adaptive for both the individual and observers, as it may help to avoid conflict and encourage behavior that reflects the individual's social status (Henrich & Gil-White, 2001; Martens et al., 2012).

Research on pride identifies two dimensions, each linked to different strategies for gaining social status (Carver et al., 2010; Cheng et al., 2010; Tracy & Robins, 2007). Authentic pride refers to feelings of accomplishment and self-confidence and typically results from successes attributed to controllable and unstable causes (e.g., personal effort). It is associated with positive emotions, better mental health outcomes (Dickens & Robins, 2022; Mercadante et al., 2021; Tracy & Robins, 2007), and lower social anxiety (Cohen & Huppert, 2018). In contrast, hubristic pride is characterized by feelings of arrogance and egotism, stemming from success attributed to uncontrollable and stable causes (e.g., talent). It is often linked to antisocial behaviors, impulsiveness, and aggression (Bureau et al., 2013; Dickens & Robins, 2022; Tracy et al., 2009), as well as higher levels of depression and social anxiety (Dickens & Robins, 2022). Authentic pride aligns with prestige and gaining status through valued skills while hubristic pride is linked to dominance, or status gained through force and control (Cheng et al., 2010).

The function of expressing pride is to attract attention, whereas one of the functions of social anxiety is to avoid it. It is therefore plausible to expect a functional link between social anxiety and pride expression. On a more general level, people with social anxiety often feel the need to control their emotional expressions to prevent unwanted attention and social rejection (Morrison & Heimberg, 2013). Research shows that socially anxious individuals tend to suppress their emotions more, a pattern that remains even after accounting for depressive symptoms (Blalock et al., 2016; Kashdan & Steger, 2006; Kivity & Huppert, 2019). However, a review of the current literature suggests a significant gap in research on the relationship between social anxiety and the expression of pride specifically. The only study that explored the links between social anxiety and pride expression did not find unique contribution of pride expression in explaining social anxiety, with the authors concluding that the experience of pride, rather than its expression, is more relevant (Cohen & Huppert, 2018). This conclusion contrasts with the above presented theorizing about the roles of pride expression and social anxiety in domain of social rank hierarchies, suggesting the need for further investigation. At the same time, understanding such links certainly requires exploration of potential intervening variables. As shown above, fear of positive evaluation is an important concomitant of social anxiety, and is theoretically well grounded in social rank negotiation processes. Furthermore, Reichenberger et al. (2015) reported that fear of positive evaluation was linked to reduced pride experiences, while fear of negative evaluation was associated with increased pride. They proposed that these fears serve different functions within social hierarchies: fear of positive evaluation limits upward mobility, whereas fear of negative evaluation guards against downward shifts (Reichenberger & Blechert, 2018). Socially anxious individuals appear to want to maintain a stable position in hierarchy to avoid conflicts with dominant and higher-ranking individuals who may see them as a threat. This goal can be achieved by avoiding positive evaluations that can raise their rank (Gilbert, 2001; Weeks et al., 2008). In line with the proposed function of fear of positive evaluation that is evident in preventing advancement in rank through avoidance of making a positive impression, we hypothesized that fear of positive evaluation will significantly mediate the relationship between social anxiety and tendency to express pride. We expect that higher social anxiety will be related to increased fear of positive evaluation, which will in turn predict lower tendency to express pride (H1).

Given the clear differences between authentic and hubristic pride, we further argue that research linking pride and social evaluation should distinguish between these two types of pride expression. However, current measures assess only general pride expression and do not differentiate between authentic and hubristic pride. To date, no tools exist to separately measure tendencies to express these two forms of pride. Therefore to explore whether fear of positive evaluation plays a different role depending on the type of pride, we also investigated its role in the relationship between social anxiety and the experience rather than the expression of authentic and hubristic pride. We hypothesize that fear of positive evaluation will serve as a significant mediator in the relationship between social anxiety and authentic pride (H2) and hubristic pride experience (H3). Considering the high comorbidity between depression and social anxiety, along with its substantial influence on emotional experiences and expression, we deemed it necessary to control for depressive symptoms in our analyses.

In the current study we simultaneously assessed both the experience of pride and the self-perceived expression of pride, each meant to capture a process anchored in the first weeks at the university. The timing of data collection at the beginning of the academic year serves as a natural prime for status-related emotions and behaviors (Anderson et al., 2012), making this transition period particularly suitable for capturing both the experience and the outward display of pride as they unfold in a real-world context of hierarchy formation. Our research targeted specifically the beginning of first academic year, when students are in a situation where the social hierarchy within the student group has just begun to form. In this way, their previous achievements do not necessarily have as much influence on social rank in their newly formed group in class, given the new situation where rank is assigned according to rules of the new forming group. Notably, an individual can belong to different hierarchies in which they may occupy different positions and roles. Someone may have high status in a group that gathers around a hobby, music, or a sports club, while they may not have high status in their group at university. In each group, rank can be assigned depending on the local relationships (Zaccaro, 2007; Ridgeway, 2019) and the task for which the group was formed (Mazur, 2005). According to the functional account of both social anxiety (Gilbert, 2014) and pride experiences (Sznycer et al., 2017), we consider it important to assess pride as a state-level construct. This approach best corresponds to the dynamics of the current social hierarchy and allows us to capture reactions to current academic achievements. Unlike trait measures, which reflect generalized emotional patterns over time, state measures capture emotional responses to current situations and environments (Conner & Barrett, 2012), and as such are more likely influenced by the current social dynamics in the academic setting. The current experience of pride, which may result from success in current academic tasks, may not be much related to the general tendency to feel pride. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the dispositional tendency toward pride is a robust predictor of state pride (Tracy & Robins, 2007). State measures are also less vulnerable to memory biases, cultural norms, and socially desirable responding, all common issues with trait-based assessments (Scollon et al., 2003). This is especially important for hubristic pride, which involves socially undesirable emotions (e.g., arrogance) and has been linked to social desirability bias (Kusano & Kemmelmeier, 2022). Finally, assessing current experience of pride allows for a more precise test of the mediation model by examining more transitory experiences rather than stable tendencies. In contrast to pride experience, the most plausible way to measure its expression in a given period is to refer to an undefined number of social situations in which pride is potentially experienced, rather than to measure pride expression during a specific moment (i.e., during assessment). Thus, when measuring individual tendency to express pride during a given period, we turned to habitual behavior, as the most fitting option given the survey type of our design. Furthermore, in line with impression management theory (Leary & Kowalski, 1990), self-reports of self-presentation always involve an implicit or imagined audience. At the beginning of the academic year, the most accessible and psychologically salient audience consists of newly met peers and professors. Thus, we expected that even without an explicit instruction to focus on the current university group, responses to expression items are to certain extent spontaneously anchored in the present social context. In this way we tried to capture individual differences in the tendency to express (or inhibit) pride in the life period that is marked by forming new hierarches and in which the costs and benefits of self-display are particularly salient.

Method

Participants and Procedure

The study included 703 first-year undergraduate students from the University of Rijeka (66.3% women and 32.9% men), aged between 18 and 24 years (M = 19.14, SD = 0.94). Participants older than 24 years were previously excluded from the sample because older participants may not share comparable experiences with younger students in their first year of study. A total of nine participants were excluded for this reason. The majority of participants were enrolled in social sciences (38.48%) and technical studies (32.30%), followed by students from the fields of biomedicine and health (16.85%), the humanities (6.60%), and biotechnical sciences (5.76%).

The study was conducted at the University of Rijeka between October and November 2024. After being informed about the study's purpose and procedure and providing informed consent, participants completed questionnaires in a classroom setting at their respective faculties. The questionnaires took between 10 and 30 min to complete. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, with the option to withdraw at any time.

Measures

The Social Interaction Anxiety Scale (SIAS) and The Social Phobia Scale (SPS) (Mattick & Clarke, 1998) were used to assess two dimensions of social anxiety. Each scale includes 20 items (e.g., “I tense up if I meet an acquaintance in the street” for SIAS; “I am worried people will think my behavior is odd” for SPS), rated on a 0–4 Likert scale. Earlier research on Croatian samples supported a single-factor structure for each scale; social interaction anxiety and fear of negative evaluation (Juretić, 2018). However, more recent findings recommend using a general social anxiety factor instead of interpreting the scales separately (Gomez & Watson, 2017). Accordingly, the present study used the combined total score from both scales as a measure of general social anxiety. The combined scale demonstrated good internal consistency reliability, with Cronbach's α = .953, McDonald's ω = .955, and composite reliability = .955.

The Fear of Positive Evaluation Scale (FPES; Weeks et al., 2008) consists of 10 items (e.g., “I am uncomfortable exhibiting my talents to others, even if I think my talents will impress them”) rated on a 10-point Likert-type scale. The original one-factor structure of the scale has been confirmed in a Croatian sample (Đurasek, 2020). In our sample, the scale demonstrated acceptable to good internal consistency reliability, with Cronbach's α = .835, McDonald's ω = .880, and composite reliability = .841. The average variance extracted was 0.407, which falls slightly below the recommended threshold of 0.50 (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Overall, the scale shows adequate reliability for research purposes, but its convergent validity is somewhat limited.

The Authentic and Hubristic Pride Scale (AHPS; Tracy & Robins, 2007) measures two distinct forms of pride and can be used to assess pride either as a state or as a trait. In the present study, pride was measured as a momentary experience. Participants rated how they currently feel on a 5-point Likert-type scale (e.g., productive, arrogant). The scale has been validated on a Croatian sample (Lukić, 2020). In our sample, Authentic pride factor demonstrated good internal consistency reliability, with Cronbach's α = .896, McDonald's ω = .897, and composite reliability = .920. The average variance extracted was 0.623, which exceeds the recommended threshold of 0.50. Hubristic pride factor showed acceptable internal consistency reliability, with Cronbach's α = .790, McDonald's ω = .817, and composite reliability = .854. The average variance extracted was 0.480, which falls slightly below the recommended threshold of 0.50.

The Pride Expression Questionnaire (PEQ; Cohen & Huppert, 2018) consists of ten self-report items assessing the tendency to express pride (e.g., “When I feel proud, I show it”). Participants respond on a 7-point scale. The one-factor structure of the scale has previously been confirmed in a Croatian sample (Lukić, 2020). In the present study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency reliability, with Cronbach's α = .874, McDonald's ω = .908, and composite reliability = .880. However, the average variance extracted was 0.442, falling below the recommended threshold of 0.50 (Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

The Depression Anxiety Stress Scales—21 (DASS-21; Henry & Crawford, 2005) assesses emotional symptoms in three domains: depression, anxiety, and stress. It includes 21 items—seven per subscale, rated on a 4-point scale based on how much each statement applied in the past week. The scale's factor structure has been validated in Croatian samples (Ivezić et al., 2012). In this study, only the depression subscale was used (e.g., “I felt that I had nothing to look forward to”). The scale demonstrated good internal consistency reliability, Cronbach's α = .882, McDonald's ω = .910, composite reliability = .885. The average variance extracted was 0.527, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.50 (Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

Statistical Analysis

The dataset contained a small proportion of missing values (0.9%), of which those that could be imputed (0.2%) were replaced using the multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) method using mice package (van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011) in R (version 4.4.0; R Core Team, 2024).

Mediation analysis was conducted using PROCESS Model 4 (Hayes, 2022) in IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0; IBM Corp., 2019). Assumptions of normality, multicollinearity and homoscedasticity were checked for all mediator models prior to analysis. A complete summary of all assumption tests is provided in Appendix A. Across models, the most common violations were non-normality of residuals (Shapiro–Wilk p < .05) and heteroscedasticity (Breusch–Pagan p < .05). No model showed evidence of problematic multicollinearity (all VIFs <3). Therefore, heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors (HC3) and bootstrapped confidence intervals (10,000 resamples) were used for all indirect effects (Hayes & Cai, 2007; Hayes, 2022).

Given the higher prevalence rates of social anxiety disorder in women (Asher et al., 2017) and known differences in emotional expression in men and women (Kring & Gordon, 1998) we explored the role of gender by conducting separate analyses for women and men. Among women, effect sizes and their directions were comparable to those in the full sample. Among men, the effects were in the same direction as those observed in women but some of them did not reach statistical significance. This may be attributable to the smaller sample size of men, which reduced statistical power. Separate analysis by gender are presented in the Appendix B; results for both genders combined are reported in the main text.

The total effect of the mediation model with authentic pride (see Figure 2) was significant (β = −0.223, p < .001). Social anxiety was a significant positive predictor of fear of positive evaluation (β = .623, p < .001), which was a significant negative predictor of authentic pride experience (β = −0.132, p < .01). Social anxiety and fear of positive evaluation together explained significant portion of variance in authentic pride (R2 = .33, F(3, 683) = 89.452, p < .001). A significant negative indirect effect of social anxiety on authentic pride experience through fear of positive evaluation was found (β = −0.082, p < .05). After including the mediator, the effect of social anxiety on authentic pride experience became weaker and remained significant (β = −0.140, p < .001). These results indicate significant mediation, with higher social anxiety predicting greater fear of positive evaluation, which in turn predicts lower authentic pride experience.

The total effect of the model with hubristic pride (see Figure 3) was significant (β = −0.143, p < .01). Social anxiety was a significant positive predictor of fear of positive evaluation (β = .625, p < .001). Fear of positive evaluation was a significant negative predictor of hubristic pride experience (β = −0.110, p < .05). Social anxiety and fear of positive evaluation together explained significant but very small portion of variance in hubristic pride (R2 = .02, F(3,682) = 4.845, p < .001). The negative indirect effect of social anxiety on hubristic pride experience through fear of positive evaluation was significant (β = −0.068, p < .05). After including the mediator, the effect of social anxiety on hubristic pride experience became weaker and nonsignificant (β = −0.075, p > .05). These findings indicate significant mediation of the relationship between social anxiety and hubristic pride experience by fear of positive evaluation, whereby higher social anxiety predicts greater fear of positive evaluation, which in turn predicts lower hubristic pride experience.

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the relationship between social anxiety and the tendency to express pride, and to determine the role of fear of positive evaluation in explaining this relationship. The results indicate that fear of positive evaluation mediates the relationship between social anxiety and the tendency to express pride. Given the important differences between types of pride, we further sought to determine which type of pride this mediation is based on. Our findings showed that fear of positive evaluation significantly mediates the link between social anxiety and both types of pride experiences: hubristic pride and authentic pride.

The results of this study support the general hypothesis that hierarchical systems dynamics contribute to deficits in specific positive emotions, such as pride, among individuals with social anxiety (Gilboa-Schechtman et al., 2014; Oren-Yagoda et al., 2023). According to evolutionary perspective, the function of social anxiety contrasts with the function of experiencing and expressing pride (Gilbert, 2001; Gilboa-Schechtman et al., 2014; Williams & DeSteno, 2009). Sznycer et al. (2017) suggest that pride helps individuals anticipate how others will revise their evaluation of the focal individual after a success, thereby facilitating one's planning and selection of socially valued behaviors, as well as advertising one's value to others. These functions of pride are likely impaired in socially anxious individuals due to numerous negative distortions in how they believe others perceive them (Cook et al., 2019), as well as significant deficits in self-esteem. To function properly, pride experience must be proportional to the value attributed to the individual by others. If experienced pride is lower than that value, it may result in dysfunctional behavioral inhibition (Sznycer & Cohen, 2021), which can explain many avoidant behaviors in socially anxious individuals. This can hinder upward movement within the social hierarchy, prevent improvements in social status, and thereby contribute to the persistence of social anxiety.

This study is the first to examine fear of positive evaluation as a potential mediator between social anxiety and the tendency to express pride, highlighting its possibly important role in shaping pride expression among socially anxious individuals. Our findings extend those of Reichenberger et al. (2015), who proposed that fear of positive evaluation limits upward mobility in social hierarchy by blocking experiences of pride. As argued by Sznycer et al. (2017), expressing or “advertising” pride is crucial for advancing within social hierarchies. Our results suggest that fear of positive evaluation may represent a mechanism through which expression of pride is dampened in socially anxious individuals, which in the end prevents upward social mobility.

We further argue that when examining the mediating role of fear of positive evaluation, it is essential to distinguish between the tendencies to express authentic and hubristic pride, given their associations to distinct status acquisition strategies (Cheng et al., 2010; Dickens & Robins, 2022). Because no existing measure empirically differentiates these tendencies when it comes to expression, we instead focused on the role of fear of positive evaluation in the momentary experience of both pride types. Hubristic pride involves feelings of grandiosity, dominance, and superiority (Tracy et al., 2020), and individuals prone to this type of pride often turn to manipulative behavior when they feel that their social status is threatened (Mercadante & Tracy, 2022). Despite these socially undesirable traits, fear of positive evaluation was found to mediate the relationship between social anxiety and the experience of hubristic pride. It is possible that socially anxious individuals, due to cognitive distortions in their perceptions of others (Clark, 2001), struggle to accurately judge socially appropriate behavior. As a result, they might incorrectly associate hubristic pride with positive evaluation, which could in part explain the mediation. However, given the small magnitude of observed mediation effect and small amount of explained variance of hubristic pride, this assumption should be taken with a degree of caution, and future research should examine how fear of negative evaluation affects the experience of hubristic pride to further clarify this relationship.

Authentic pride is associated with a prestige-based strategy for gaining social rank, characterized by prosocial traits and the suppression of aggression, rather than displays of power or dominance (Cheng et al., 2010; Weidman et al., 2016). Because fear of positive evaluation inhibits upward social mobility by blocking positive social evaluation, one might expect it to completely explain the relationship between social anxiety and authentic pride experience. The observed significant direct effect of social anxiety on authentic pride after accounting for fear of positive evaluation indicates that we need to examine additional mediators. Considering that social anxiety might have developed mainly as a reaction to threats related to dominance, it is possible that socially anxious individuals view authentic pride (which is associated with prestige rather than dominance) as not particularly relevant to achieving higher status. A related possible explanation can be found in the different success attributions and social costs associated with each type of pride. Authentic pride stems from controllable behaviors like effort, making it easier for individuals to manage success by adjusting their actions and thus reducing the risk of upward status shifts or envy from others. Hubristic pride, in contrast, is linked to uncontrollable traits such as talent or intelligence, which are harder to regulate and may provoke stronger negative social reactions. This aligns with Lange and Crusius (2015), who found that personal control and perceived “deservingness” of success are related to benign envy, while lack of control and low deservingness correspond to malicious envy. As a result, authentic pride may be differently connected to fear of positive evaluation, although this interpretation should be treated with caution and should be further investigated experimentally.

In addition to providing basic scientific insights into potential dynamics of social anxiety in the context of social valuation and emotional expression, the current research should be informative for treatments for Social Anxiety Disorder. Most existing therapies overlook positive emotions, with few exceptions (e.g., Craske et al., 2014; Taylor et al., 2017). Even interventions that do target positive emotions rarely focus specifically on pride, despite evidence showing socially anxious individuals experience deficits in this emotion and its key role in improving social status. Novel therapeutic interventions could include cognitive restructuring specifically targeting distortions related to positive evaluation, as well as barriers to experiencing pride and fears associated with its expression. Furthermore, in the treatment of socially anxious individuals, it would be beneficial, beyond exposure to social situations, to incorporate exposure to situations involving the public presentation of one's own achievements and receiving praise for one's efforts. Additionally, therapeutic interventions might include psychoeducation on the importance of authentic pride for adequate social functioning and its role in managing social anxiety.

Limitations and Future Directions

There are several limitations to these findings. First, to establish causal relationships between fear of positive evaluation and expression and experience of pride, experimental studies manipulating fear of positive evaluation and assessing authentic and hubristic pride are needed. In that way we can determine if fear of positive evaluation influences expression and experience of pride. Second, participants self-reported their tendency to express pride, which depends on their ability to accurately estimate how often they display this behavior. This limitation was partly addressed by also examining momentary pride experiences, which are less susceptible to memory biases. However, future research would benefit from objective assessments by independent observers using tools such as the Pride Expression Coding System (Tracy & Robins, 2007). Importantly, focusing on objective behavior would additionally accentuate the issue of time-frame in which the assessed pride expression (as well as other processes in the focus of the study) occurred, since behavioral observations would most likely focus on more narrow time frames. In the present study, we deliberately posed questions about current feelings of pride and did not specify time frame for other processes, with the idea that participants would at least partly refer to most recent and thus most salient events. It has to be noted that the experience of pride can be assessed at any moment with a reasonable expectation that the individual is able to report current feelings of pride. However, expression of pride represents a behavior that occurs only in specific social situations, in contrast to its subjective experience that can be felt at any moment (i.e., during assessment). Therefore, while our aim was to assess pride expression in this specific life situation, the measure used was slightly different from that aimed to measure experience, since it had to refer to (typical) situation in which pride is experienced and then potentially expressed. Nevertheless, given the conceptual differences between the measurement of pride expression as a habitual tendency and the measurement of the experience of pride as a state-level construct, this somewhat limits the comparability of the tested models. Future research could gain from strictly specifying the focal time frame, with the goal of distinguishing the links among processes of interest during the specific phase from those in the domain of stable individual differences. Future research would also benefit from developing measures of pride expression that differentiate between the expression of authentic and hubristic pride. Such advancements would contribute not only to a deeper understanding of how socially anxious individuals function within social hierarchies, but also to a more nuanced understanding of the distinct functions of the dimensions proposed in the Bivalent Fear of Evaluation Model.

Another concern involves the Authentic and Hubristic Pride Scale: recent studies have questioned the validity of the hubristic pride subscale (Dickens & Murphy, 2023; Holbrook et al., 2014), urging caution in interpreting these results and calling for further refinement of these measures (Tracy et al., 2023). A significant limitation is the sample, which included only first-year university students, a group experiencing heightened social anxiety due to developmental transitions and new social challenges (Crone & Dahl, 2012; Leigh & Clark, 2018). This restricts the generalizability of the findings to broader or clinical populations. Future studies should examine these variables in more diverse samples, especially among individuals diagnosed with Social Anxiety Disorder. Future research should also examine gender more thoroughly as a potential moderator of the obtained effects. Given that we did not obtain significant indirect effects concerning authentic and hubristic pride in the male subsample, further investigation is needed to determine whether this is due to differences in key variables between men and women, or rather to the significantly smaller sample of men included in our study. Future research with larger and more balanced samples is needed to examine these possibilities.

Despite its limitations, this study provides insights into how socially anxious individuals function within social hierarchies and the mechanisms that sustain social anxiety. Our findings demonstrate that fear of positive evaluation mediates the relationship between social anxiety and pride expression. Moreover, this fear is connected not only to expression but also to the momentary experience of pride, acting as a significant mediator for both hubristic and authentic pride, which points to the robustness of the tested model on a more general level. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the role of fear of positive evaluation in pride expression among socially anxious individuals. Our results support evolutionary perspectives on both the function of this type of fear and the adaptive role of pride. Beyond its theoretical contribution, the study identifies specific targets for therapeutic intervention and lays the groundwork for future research on the complex interplay between social anxiety and pride.
