Limiting 20S proteasome assembly leads to unbalanced nucleo-cytoplasmic distribution of 26S/30S proteasomes and chronic proteotoxicity
Authors: Gabriel Ruiz-Romero, María Dolores Berdún, Mark Hochstrasser, Silvia Salas-Pino, Rafael R Daga
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn addition to the degradation of cell-cycle proteins, short-lived, damaged, or unfolded proteins are constantly cleared from cells by the proteasome. During proliferation, the proteasome localizes to the nucleus and cytoplasm; however, the functional relevance of this compartmentalization remains unclear. Here, we show that folding stress increases 26S/30S proteasome activity, which correlates with the upregulation of Ump1, a chaperone involved in 20S assembly. Conversely, ump1 inactivation results in a drop of 20S and 26S/30S proteasomes. Limited 26S/30S proteasomes in ump1-deficient cells accumulate in the nucleus where they degrade mitotic substrates, allowing cells to proceed through mitosis; however, these cells present cytoplasmic aggregates and constitutive activation of the heat shock response. Thus, our data suggest that an increase in proteasome assembly induced by folding stress functions as an additional layer to proteasome regulation and highlight the importance of balanced proteasome compartmentalization to sustain cell proliferation while maintaining proper cytoplasmic proteostasis.Subject areas: Molecular biology, Organizational aspects of cell biology, Functional aspects of cell biology

Introduction

In eukaryotic cells, the main mechanism of selective protein degradation is via the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS). Short-lived proteins such as cell-cycle regulators as well as terminally misfolded proteins are targeted by the UPS for proteasomal degradation. This proteolytic activity is key for proper cell-cycle progression and plays a critical role in preventing the accumulation of aberrantly folded proteins, which negatively impact cellular and tissue fitness.1,2,3 The proteasome is involved in the regulation of many biological processes such as the cell cycle, DNA repair, apoptosis, immune responses, signal transduction, transcription, metabolism, and protein quality control. Deficiency of proteasome function is associated with aging and various human pathologies, including cancer, immune-related diseases, and neurodegenerative diseases, among others.2,4

The proteasome is formed from two subcomplexes; the 20S proteasome or core particle (CP), and the 19S regulatory particle (RP). The 20S CP is made of four stacked heteroheptamers, organized into two outer rings composed of α subunits and two inner rings composed of β subunits that bear the proteolytic activity. The 19S RP is in turn made up of two subassemblies, the base and lid, and its main function is to provide substrate specificity and unfolding activity to the proteasome. The RP recognizes, deubiquitinates, and unfolds ubiquitinated substrates for their translocation into the catalytic chamber of the CP, where they are eventually degraded.5

The assembly of the proteasome is a complex process in which both proteasome particles (20S CP and 19S RP) are separately assembled in various sequential steps.6,7,8 The maturation of the CP requires hemiparticle proteasome assembly and dimerization, which are assisted by the specialized chaperone Ump1.6,9,10,11,12,13,14,15 The CP can be found free or attached to one or two RPs, giving rise to the 26S (RP-CP) and 30S (RP-CP-RP) proteasomes, although other proteasome configurations are also present from yeasts to metazoans.12,16,17,18,19

The proteasome is regulated at multiple levels in physiological conditions and in response to proteotoxic stress. It is known that proteotoxic stress induced by proteasome inhibition leads to the transcriptional upregulation of proteasome subunit genes dependent on the Rpn4 transcription factor (TF)20 in budding yeast and Nrf1 in humans.21,22 RPN4 is activated by Yap1, an oxidative stress-specific TF, by Hsf1, the master HSR TF, and by Pdr1/3, involved in drug resistance responses. The collaborative, stress-responsive transcriptional regulation of the UPS through Rpn4 has been named the proteasome stress response (PSR).23 On the other hand, in mammalian cells, heat shock (HS) results in an increase in proteasome peptidase activity without changes in the levels of proteasome subunits or assembly.24 HS is a condition that promotes protein misfolding not only in mature proteins but also affects folding of nascent polypeptides during their synthesis.25,26,27 Therefore, cytoplasmic proteasome activity must be critical to counteract proteotoxicity induced in these conditions.

Intriguingly, the yeast proteasome is enriched in the nucleus and nuclear envelope (NE), raising the question of how cells handle cytoplasmic proteostasis, especially in conditions of increased proteotoxic stress. In fission yeast, the NE adaptor Cut8 is required for nuclear localization of the proteasome which allows proteolysis of mitotic regulators such as cyclin B/Cdc13 and securin/Cut228 and consequently mitotic progression. In budding yeast, nuclear proteasome localization requires Sts1, which is orthologous to Cut8,29 whereas in human cells this function is performed by AKIRIN2, which is unrelated to Cut8 or Sts1.30

Here, we show that in fission yeast, protein unfolding induced by heat stress or treatment with the L-arginine amino acid analog canavanine results in increased 26S/30S proteasome activity that is independent of new proteasome subunit production. The increased proteasome activity correlates with the upregulation of the 20S CP assembly chaperone Ump1 and with an increase in proteasome assembly. Ump1 inactivation compromises 20S proteasome assembly, resulting in a drop in mature 26S/30S proteasomes. Importantly, this limited pool of proteasomes accumulates in the nucleus, allowing cell-cycle progression. However, this condition consequently leads to cytoplasmic proteotoxicity and activation of the HSR, and can be partially alleviated by hindering proteasome nuclear import.

Results and discussion

Protein folding stress triggers the assembly of 26S/30S proteasomes

To investigate the cellular response to a heightened demand for proteasome activity upon HS, we switched cells from 25°C to 36°C or 39°C for 1 h and determined the activity of the proteasome in native extracts using the SucLLVY-AMC fluorogenic peptide substrate. Consistent with published data this assay showed that HS leads to a subtle increase in 26S/30S proteasome activity24,31 whereas the activity of 20S proteasomes was slightly decreased (Figure 1A). This increase in 26S/30S proteasome activity was confirmed by using an artificial green fluorescent protein (GFP)-based proteasomal substrate,32,33,34 whose levels are reduced upon incubation of cells at 36°C or 39°C (Figure 1B).

Strikingly, in native extracts from wild-type cells we detected a band between those corresponding to 20S and 26S proteasomes that we named intermediate proteasome (i-P) and that possess SDS-independent proteasome activity, which indicates that the 20S channel is open36,37,38 (Figure 1A, asterisk). Although the most common proteasome forms are 20S, 26S, and 30S, other proteasomal species can be formed by the addition of different regulators to the 20S CP. Such regulators include Blm10/PA200,39,40 PA28,41 or the evolutionarily conserved AAA-ATPase Cdc48,42,43 and have been shown to associate with 20S proteasomes, promoting ubiquitin-independent proteolytic activity on unstructured proteins.

We determined that the i-P was not a complex of Cdc48 with the 20S CP (Figure S1A), and S. pombe lacks a Blm10 or PA28 orthologue. We then compared the bands by in-gel proteasome activity assays of native gel-resolved extracts from S. pombe and S. cerevisiae cells. Using otherwise identical experimental conditions, we did not detect the i-P in extracts from S. cerevisiae (Figure S1B), suggesting either that the i-P represents a proteasome species not found in S. cerevisiae or one present only under distinct conditions. The composition of the i-Ps was studied by detecting proteasome subunits tagged with GFP in native gel electrophoresis. 19S base components Rpn1 and Rpn2 were present in the i-P, whereas virtually all lid components analyzed were absent except for the lid-associated ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase Uch2 (Figure S1C), suggesting that i-Ps might represent proteasome assembly intermediates in which a base is pre-assembled on a 20S CP, although we cannot fully rule out that the i-P results from lid dissociation from 26S/30S complexes during cell lysis and native PAGE.

We then tested whether the increased 26S/30S proteasome activity observed upon HS is due to an increase in the synthesis of proteasome subunits. To this end, we analyzed protein levels of several proteasome subunits after switching cells from 25°C to either 36°C or 39°C for 1 h. We analyzed the levels of α1-7 subunits of the 20S CP, as well as subunits of the base and lid of the 19S RP by immunoblot analysis. Neither incubation at 36°C nor 39°C resulted in an increase in the levels of proteasome subunits (Figure 1C), suggesting that the increased proteasome activity observed in native gels is not due to an increase in proteasome subunit synthesis.

Ump1/POMP is a small proteasome-assembly chaperone, conserved from yeast to humans, that assists half-CP assembly and dimerization.9,10,14,15 S. cerevisiae UMP1 is transcriptionally induced in response to DNA-damaging agents44 and overexpression of UMP1 results in increased proteasome activity, enhanced stress resistance and increased lifespan.45 To test whether HS induces ump1 upregulation, we analyzed the levels of endogenous Ump1-HA. Cells expressing Ump1-HA presented normal morphology and growth rates similar to wild-type cells, suggesting that this tagged version of Ump1 is functional. We found that in fission yeast Ump1-HA is rapidly induced upon HS (Figure 1D), which suggests that the increased 26S/30S proteasome activity observed under HS is accompanied by upregulation of Ump1-dependent 20S proteasome assembly.

We next determined whether the upregulation of Ump1 levels and the increase in proteasome assembly observed upon HS are specific to this type of stress or is a common response to protein folding stress. To this end, we exposed cells to the L-arginine amino acid-analogue canavanine, which is incorporated into nascent polypeptides and induces protein misfolding.46 Like HS, canavanine treatment induced proteasome assembly (Figure 1E) and Ump1 upregulation after 1–2 h of treatment at 25°C (Figure 1F).

Thus, our results suggest that upregulation of Ump1 and the increase in proteasome assembly under conditions of high demand for protein degradative activity represent additional levels of regulation of the PSR.

Deficiency of ump1 compromises proteasome assembly and survival after HS

To study the functional relevance of Ump1 upregulation in conditions inducing protein unfolding, we deleted the ump1 gene. ump1 deletion was strongly deleterious, causing aberrant cell morphology (Figure S2A), a severe slow-growth phenotype at 25°C relative to wild-type cells (Figure S2B), and an inability to survive at high temperatures (36°C) (Figure S2C). To study the impact of ump1 deletion on proteasome activity, we performed in-gel proteasome activity assays using gel-resolved native extracts from wild-type and ump1Δ cells incubated at 25°C or after 1.5 h incubation at 39°C, a condition in which protein synthesis is mostly inhibited31,47 and therefore changes in proteasome composition should mostly result from the assembly of preexisting subunits. A marked reduction in proteasome activity was observed for ump1Δ cells relative to wild-type at 25°C (Figure S2D). In wild-type cells, incubation at 39°C resulted in increased activity of 26/30S proteasomes whereas 20S activity was slightly reduced (Figures 1A and S2D). In ump1Δ cells, the temperature increase led to a moderate increase in 26/30S activity and a complete exhaustion of 20S proteasome activity (Figure S2D, arrow), which suggests that a higher demand for proteasome activity in response to HS promotes the assembly of 20S and 26/30S proteasomes. In the absence of Ump1, the reduced pool of preexisting 20S particles would limit the production of 26S/30S (Figure S2D, +SDS). Since both 20S and i-P particles were exhausted during HS in the ump1Δ mutant in favor of higher proteasomal forms (26S/30S), our data suggest that i-Ps are not artifactually disassembled 26S/30S particles but can serve as bona fide assembly intermediates whose biological significance remains to be fully studied.

The proteasome is essential for cell cycle progression and its inactivation or defective nuclear accumulation causes lethal mitosis and a “cut” phenotype (“cell untimely torn”).28 Therefore, we determined the percentage of cells showing the cut phenotype as an indirect readout of proteasome activity in wild-type and ump1Δ cells using cut8Δ cells as control. ump1Δ cells showed minor defects in mitosis (8.1 ± 4.9% of cut phenotypes, n100), compared with the mitotic defects observed in the cut8Δ mutant (93 ± 7.1%, n100), a mutant that is lethal at 36°C due to defective 26S/30S proteasome nuclear accumulation but that otherwise has no defects in proteasome structure28 (Figures S2E and S2F). Thus, our results show that the reduced proliferation rates of ump1Δ cells do not seem to arise from mitotic defects as occurs in cut8 deficiency.

We determined by immunoblot analysis that the accumulation of poly-ubiquitinated proteins of ump1Δ cells kept at 25°C was comparable to wild-type cells exposed to acute HS (42°C) (Figure S2G). Such HS-induced polyubiquitin-protein accumulation is likely due to ubiquitination of misfolded proteins reaching levels that cannot be cleared rapidly by proteasomes. Thus, the slow-growth phenotype of ump1Δ cells is likely caused by the excess accumulation of misfolded poly-ubiquitinated proteins and proteotoxicity even under non-stress conditions due to their reduced proteasome activity.

We were unable to perform genetic crosses between ump1Δ cells and other strains, making it difficult to analyze certain aspects of Ump1 physiology. Therefore, we generated a thermosensitive (ts) allele of ump1 by random mutagenesis. The selected allele contained a deletion of one T in position 382 that generated a premature stop codon and produced a protein deleted for the last 37 amino acids (ump1-ts).

The ump1-ts strain grew like the wild-type strain at 25°C and 30°C but was not able to grow at 36°C on solid medium (Figure 2A), while in liquid medium this strain showed a marked reduction in growth (Figure 2B). These data suggest that the ump1-ts allele possesses partial functionality at 25°C and 30°C, as phenotypic defects are milder than those of the ump1Δ strain. Proteasomal activity was reduced in ump1-ts mutant at 25°C compared to wild-type cells and was further reduced upon inactivation by incubation at 36°C, as judged by the increase in the levels of (GFP)-based proteasomal substrate (Figure 2C).

In-gel proteasome activity assays confirmed a reduction in proteasome activity of ump1-ts cells at 25°C (Figure 2D, left panel, time 0 h), a further reduction upon ump-1ts inactivation by temperature shift to 36°C and an increase in the activity of 26S/30S proteasomes at earlier time points (Figure 2D, left panel, 2 h, 36°C). At later time points, 26S/30S proteasome activity is also reduced compared to wild-type cells (Figure 2D, left panel, 4–6 h, 36°C), suggesting that upon inactivation of ump1-ts, exhaustion of 20S proteasomes limit further assembly of 26S/30S proteasomal forms. This was confirmed by native gel immunoblot with anti-α1-7 antibody that showed reduced levels of 20S proteasome complexes (Figure 2D, right panel, arrow) and the presence of immature 20S precursors, lacking in-gel proteolytic activity (Figure 2D, right panel, asterisk) in ump1-ts cells already at permissive temperature. These phenotypes are stronger upon temperature shift, leading to the complete exhaustion of 20S proteasomes and the increase in the levels of 20S inactive proteasome precursors. By fluorescence microscopy, we also observed an enlargement of cytoplasmic foci marked by a 20S α-subunit Pre6-Tomato fusion upon ump1-ts inactivation (Figure 2E). Thus, the decreased proteasome activity in the ump1-ts mutant is due to impaired hemiparticle assembly leading to decreased levels of mature 20S proteasomes which in turn limit the assembly of 26S/30S. Although ump1-ts cells still respond to HS by promoting the assembly of more 26S/30S proteasomes, the 20S particles become limiting for further assembly, resulting in an overall reduction of 26S/30S proteasome species (Figure 2D) that is not due to a reduction in proteasomal subunit synthesis (Figure S2H).

We next analyzed the localization of Rpn1-GFP, a component of the 19S RP base, upon inactivation of ump1-ts. We found that Rpn1-GFP levels were increased in the nucleus upon inactivation of ump1-ts relative to wild-type cells (Figure 2F). While in wild-type cells the nucleus/cytoplasm ratio of Rpn1-GFP signal remained constant upon temperature shift to 36°C, in ump1-ts cells Rpn1-GFP was accumulated in the nucleus (Figure 2G). This contrasted with the inactivation of the cut8-563 t allele, a condition that results in depletion of the nuclear proteasome pool28 (Figure 2G). Importantly, the nuclear enrichment of Rpn1-GFP observed in ump1-ts cells was reversed in the double mutant ump1-ts cut8-563 (Figure 2G), suggesting that Rpn1-GFP measurements indeed reflect 19S RP or full 26S/30S proteasomes and not simply free Rpn1 subunits. Thus, our results show that the limited pool of proteasomes produced in ump1-ts is predominantly nuclear.

The limited proteasomes produced in ump1-ts cells are sufficient to degrade cyclin B and securin and to exit mitosis

In S. pombe, nuclear localization of the proteasome depends on Cut8, and the cut8 mutant dies during mitosis with a lethal cut phenotype due to its inability to degrade the securin and cyclin-B mitotic regulators.28 We found that ump1Δ cells showed a subtle increase in the frequency of cut phenotypes compared to wild-type cells, but this increase was far milder than that of cut8 mutants (Figure S2E), suggesting that despite the overall reduction in the pool of 26S/30S species in the absence of Ump1, their nuclear enrichment was sufficient to degrade these key mitotic substrates and promote mitotic exit.

To test this hypothesis, we followed mitotic progression by live confocal microscopy in wild-type and ump1-ts cells by measuring the time from mitosis onset (spindle pole body [SPB] separation) to the metaphase-to-anaphase transition (separation of sister chromatids). We monitored the SPBs (the functional equivalent of centrosomes in metazoans) with Sid2-mCherry and kinetochores with inner kinetochore component Mis6-GFP in wild-type and ump1-ts after 6 h at 36°C.

While wild-type cells activated anaphase after 15 ± 5 min from the time of SPB separation, this took on average almost twice as long in ump1-ts cells (Figure 3A). We then analyzed nuclear proteasome activity by monitoring in vivo cyclin B (Cdc13-GFP) decay at anaphase onset. Cdc13 is enriched in the nucleus during interphase and additionally at the SPBs and the short spindles during mitosis. Consistent with published data, wild-type cells rapidly degraded Cdc13 upon anaphase onset28 (Figure 3B, upper panel). Treatment of these cells with the proteasome inhibitor bortezomib prevented Cdc13 degradation and resulted in lethal cut phenotypes (Figure 3B). In the cut8-563 mutant cells, Cdc13-GFP signal decay was delayed as previously described, and cells died in mitosis with a cut phenotype28 (Figure 3B).

By contrast, ump1-ts cells, despite showing higher levels of Cdc13-GFP at mitotic onset, presented very similar Cdc13 decay kinetics relative to wild-type cells and exited mitosis successfully without generating cut phenotypes (Figure 3B). As expected, in the absence of nuclear proteasome activity (bortezomib or cut8-356 at restrictive temperature), mitotic spindles were shorter (as judged by the distance between SPBs) than in the wild-type cells as sister chromatids remain tethered together by cohesins, which cannot be degraded in these conditions. The ump1-ts cells, however, had normal spindles as judged by the normal separation of the two SPBs during spindle elongation (Figure 3B), suggesting cohesin degradation takes place in this mutant.

We further corroborated this result using Cut2-GFP. Cut2 encodes mitotic securin, a separase inhibitor that prevent cohesin degradation until chromosomes are properly attached by the mitotic spindle. Cut2 localizes in the nucleus and early mitotic spindles during mitosis and is polyubiquitinated and degraded by the nuclear proteasome at the metaphase to anaphase transition.28,48 As in the case of cyclin B, mitotic levels of Cut2-GFP were higher at mitotic onset in ump1-ts cells relative to wild-type; however, the kinetics of Cut2 decay in ump1-ts were very similar to the wild-type strain (Figure 3C, graph). Thus, these data indicate that the nuclear proteasomes produced in ump1-ts cells were sufficient to degrade mitotic substrates and to proceed through mitosis successfully without impinging on cell viability.

Interplay between proteasome activity and the HSR

Both proteasomes and molecular chaperones whose expression is activated by the master TF Hsf1 act together to cope with the proteotoxicity generated by HS.31,49,50 Moreover, proteasome inhibition leads to the accumulation of aggregation-prone proteins49 and activation of the HSR through activation of members of the HSF TFs.51,52,53,54,55 We reasoned that in the ump1-ts mutant, cytoplasmic proteasomes could become limiting, causing protein aggregates to build up and cytoplasmic proteostasis to be maintained by the activation of the HSR.

To test this hypothesis, we analyzed the state of the HSR by monitoring the activation of Hsf1 in ump1-ts. For that we first tested the expression of the previously characterized Hsf1 activity reporter UTRsHsp104:GFP56 in wild-type and ump1-ts cells incubated at 25°C or after a shift to 36°C. We found that the level of the UTRsHsp104:GFP reporter was already increased at permissive temperature in ump1-ts mutant relative to wild-type cells and was strongly upregulated upon ump1-ts inactivation at 36°C (Figure S3A). In unstressed conditions, HSP70 binds and sequesters Hsf1 in the cytoplasm. However, upon HS, this chaperone binds unfolded or misfolded proteins that titrates it away from Hsf1, thus allowing Hsf1 activation and translocation into the nucleus to reprogram gene expression. The gene encoding Hsp70 is among the targets of Hsf1, ensuring a feedback regulatory mechanism that results in a rapid but transient HSR.27,56,57,58,59,60 We initially determined the dynamics of Hsf1 localization after the chemical inactivation of the proteasome in normal temperature growth conditions (25°C). Treatment of wild-type cells expressing GFP-Hsf1 with bortezomib led to a rapid enrichment of Hsf1 in the nucleus (Figure 4A). We next checked Hsf1 localization in wild-type and ump1-ts after a temperature switch from 25°C to 36°C. As expected, in wild-type cells, the shift to 36°C led to the rapid accumulation of GFP-Hsf1 in the nucleus before its re-accumulation in the cytoplasm at later time points (Figure 4B). Mutant ump1-ts cells followed a similar Hsf1 localization behavior, but by 4 h, by which time the proteasome had decreased in the cytoplasm, GFP-Hsf1 returned to the nucleus (Figure 4B, arrows). The unusual reactivation of Hsf1 suggested that the mutant cells might be experiencing defective cytoplasmic proteostasis, possibly due to the limited pool of cytoplasmic proteasomes.

We further checked by fluorescence microscopy and by western blot the levels of Hsp104 disaggregase and Hsp70 chaperones Ssa1 and Ssa2 in wild-type and ump1-ts cells switched from 25° to 36°C. The levels of Hsp104 and Ssa1 increased in both wild-type and ump1-ts strains incubated at 36°C for 1–2 h (Figures 4C, 4D, and S3B); however, while fluorescence intensity of both Hsp104 and Ssa1 remained constant or decreased in the wild-type strain at later time points, in ump1-ts cells the intensity of both HS factors continued to increase over time (Figures 4C and 4D). Ssa2 levels were not significantly different between the wild-type and the ump1-ts (Figures S3B and S3C), suggesting a differentiated function of these Hsp70 chaperones. Together these data indicate that the limitation of the cytoplasmic pool of proteasomes leads to the reactivation of Hsf1, which in turn results in pronounced cytoplasmic accumulation of Hsf1 transcriptional targets such as the disaggregase Hsp104 and Ssa1.

Finally, we checked levels of Hsf1 and Hsp104 in ump1Δ cells. For that, we integrated by homologous recombination the GFP-Hsf1 and Hsp104-RFP fusions into the ump1Δ background. Similar to ump1-ts cells incubated at 36°C, ump1Δ accumulated high levels of Hsp104 both in the nucleus and the cytoplasm (Figure S3D). Both ump1-ts and ump1Δ cells were sensitive to the amino acid analog canavanine (Figure S3E) suggesting that the absence of ump1 function results in chronic proteotoxic stress. However in ump1Δ cells GFP-Hsf1 levels were increased in the cytoplasm and decreased in the nucleus relative to wild-type at 25°C (Figure S3F). This might be due to the massive amount of cellular chaperones observed in ump1Δ cells, including Hsp70-Ssa1 (Figure S3G), which might titrate a fraction of Hsf1 away from DNA.27,57,58 Interestingly, incubation of ump1-ts cells at 37°C results in a subtle growth improvement compared to incubation at 36°C (Figure S3H), which might be due to the optimal temperature for activation of the HSR.31,61 Accordingly, deletion of hsp104 or ssa1 suppresses this effect at 37°C, and these chaperones are essential for cell viability after inactivation of ump1 (Figure S3H).

Importantly, several HSPs have been shown to promote proteasome assembly. Hsp90 promotes the assembly and maintenance of 26S/30S proteasomes,62,63 while Hsp70 and Hsp110 chaperones are required for the assembly of α-ring precursors.64,65 Therefore, a mechanism of HSR-dependent compensation of ump1-ts deficient proteasome activity might be achieved through increased expression of these chaperones to promote proteasome assembly. To test this hypothesis, we first analyzed whether Ssa1-YFP in native extracts from wild-type and ump1-ts cells associates with any proteasomal species. In wild-type cells, Ssa1-YFP signal overlapped with 20S precursors but not with 20S particles, and its signal increased after the temperature increase (Figure 4E, right). Shifting the double mutant ump1-ts ssa1Δ to high temperature led to the accumulation of 20S precursors relative to the single ump1-ts mutant (Figure S3I, arrow), consistent with a role of Ssa1 in α-ring precursor assembly.64,65 However, deletion of ssa1 in the ump1-ts mutant did not significantly decrease 20S proteasome activity or levels compared with ump1-ts single mutant at 25°C, showing that Ssa1 does not promote 20S particle assembly in these conditions (Figure S3I). Thus, altogether these results point to a critical role of the cytoplasmic proteasome in maintaining proteostasis even at temperatures that do not induce protein denaturation (25°C) and demonstrate that deficient cytoplasmic proteostasis caused by limiting proteasome assembly and its unbalanced compartmentalization leads to chronic proteotoxicity, which can be partially buffered by the HSR.

Increasing the pool of cytoplasmic proteasome in ump1-ts helps to decrease its cytoplasmic proteotoxicity

We have shown that compromising the function of Ump1 not only results in an overall decrease of proteasome activity, but also in unbalanced proteasome distribution between the cytoplasm and the nucleus. If cytoplasmic proteotoxicity in the absence of Ump1 is the result of unbalanced proteasome localization between the nucleus and the cytoplasm, then increasing cytoplasmic proteasomes in the ump1-ts mutant might suppress the appearance of misfolded or aggregated proteins.

In S. cerevisiae, importin α (Srp1) is required to localize 20S proteasomes to the nucleus,29,66,67,68 and we have shown previously that the S. pombe importin-α Imp1 physically interacts with several proteasome subunits,69 which suggest that Imp1 might also be involved in the nuclear import of proteasome particles in S. pombe. Indeed, imp1Δ cells partially prevented the accumulation of Rpn1-GFP in the nucleus relative to wild-type cells (Figure 5A), but to a lesser extent than the cut8-563 mutant (Figure 2G). In contrast to the cut8-563 mutant, the slight reduction of the nuclear proteasome pool in imp1Δ does not interfere with chromosome segregation and mitotic exit.69

We next analyzed the levels of Hsp104-RFP in the double mutants cut8-563 ump1-ts and imp1Δ ump1-ts in comparison to the corresponding single mutants and wild-type cells. In all strains, Hsp104-RFP levels increased upon temperature shift from 25°C to 36°C, as expected (Figure S4A). However, cut8-563 ump1-ts and imp1Δ ump1-ts double mutants displayed reduced levels of Hsp104 in the cytoplasm relative to the single ump1-ts mutant (Figure S4A, 4–6 h), which would be consistent with reduced levels of cytoplasmic proteotoxicity. Although deletion of imp1 in the ump1-ts background reduced cytoplasmic proteotoxicity, this was not enough in the long term to restore viability in the absence of proteasome activity (ump1-ts) (Figure S4B). We also analyzed the kinetics of Hsf1 activation upon HS. Both imp1Δ and cut8-563 single mutants behaved like wild-type cells and transiently accumulated Hsf1 in the nucleus (Figure 5B, 5 min), although we noticed that a fraction of imp1Δ cells showed a slight delay in Hsf1 clearance from the nucleus. Interestingly, both mutants suppressed the late reactivation of Hsf1 observed in ump1-ts mutant (Figure 5B, graph arrows). Thus, together these data indicate that the chronic cytoplasmic proteotoxicity of ump1-ts can be reduced by increasing cytoplasmic proteasome levels through inhibition of proteasome nuclear localization.

Concluding remarks

A complex proteostatic network involving chaperones, the UPS and autophagy maintains cellular proteostasis in normal and stressed conditions and its disfunction has been associated with chronic proteotoxicity and aging.70 Our work shows that protein folding stress induces the rapid assembly of 26S/30S proteasomes that is coupled with assembly of 20S CPs driven by stress-induced upregulation of 20S chaperone Ump1 (Figure 5C). Consequently, Ump1 inactivation impairs 20S proteasome production, leading to a decrease in the pool of mature 26/30S proteasomes, which compromises cell viability upon folding stress. Importantly, the limited 26S/30S proteasomes produced upon ump1 deficiency accumulated in the nucleus. Accordingly, ump1-deficient cells are competent for proteasomal degradation of nuclear substrates (securin and cyclin B), whereas cytoplasmic misfolded substrates build up. Therefore, Ump1 upregulation upon folding stress provides a distinct mechanism for the proteostasis network to favor proteasome assembly.

It is known that proteotoxic or folding stress leads to HSR activation. This response depends on the severity of the stress, from a rapid and transient response to a more sustained or delayed activation.31,71 Accordingly, whereas ump1Δ cells show constitutive activation of Hsf1, inactivation of ump1-ts results in a rapid response as a consequence of temperature shift and a late reactivation likely due to accumulation of terminally unfolded proteins caused by proteasome deficiency. Interestingly, in S. cerevisiae the Hsp70 proteins Ssa1 and Ssa2 have been shown to associate with 20S proteasome precursors and assist in their assembly,64,65 and Hsp90 has a role in the assembly of 26S/30S proteasomes.62,63 Therefore, is it possible that the upregulation of HSPs upon ump1 deficiency might be a proteostatic mechanism to cope with protein folding and to promote proteasome production.

The data presented here highlight the importance of balanced proteasome assembly and proteasome nucleo-cytoplasmic compartmentalization as well as the robustness of the cell’s proteostasis network in maintaining a healthy proteome both in physiological conditions and under stress. This work could be relevant to the understanding of the role of proteostasis networks during aging and may help in the design of new pharmacological strategies for manipulating proteasome function or subcellular localization in the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases and cancer.

Limitations of the study

Our study shows that folding stress induces the assembly of 26S/30S proteasomes. This requires the prior assembly of 20S particles promoted by Ump1, whose levels increase upon folding stress. However it is not known how the increased assembly of 20S proteasomes is coordinated with the assembly of 19S or the subsequent assembly of 19S and 20S into 26S/30S or whether other proteasome chaperones facilitating assembly of 19S particles and 19S-20S particles are also induced upon stress. In addition, we have not been able to generate a functional N- or C-terminal GFP-tagged version of Ump1 to determine whether the increase in the levels of Ump1 upon folding stress correlates with a change in its localization. Finally, future studies will address the composition and functional relevance of the i-P proteasome complex.

Experimental model and study participant details

Fission yeast strains and general methods

Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Schizosaccharomyces pombe strains in this work are shown in Table S1. Standard methods for S. pombe growth and genetics were used.73,74 In general, unless otherwise stated, liquid cultures were grown in rich media (YES) at 25°C in shaking incubators. S. pombe strains expressing Ub-G76V-GFP were grown in minimal media. Strains expressing Cdc13-GFP were grown in minimal media with 5 μg/ml thiamine at 25°C and then shifted to minimal media without thiamine at 25°C for around 24 h. Temperature-sensitive strains were grown at 25°C before incubation at restrictive temperature (36°C) for the indicated times.

Method details

Strain construction

Gene deletion and tagging was mostly performed using PCR-methods from pFA6a plasmids and homologous recombination as previously described.75 Selection markers used were ura4+, kanMX6 (Kan) and natMX6 (Nat).75,76 Yeast transformation was performed by the lithium acetate method.73,77 Unless otherwise stated, the proteins used and generated in this study were tagged at either the N- or C-terminus and expressed from their endogenous loci. Constructs were checked by PCR. Fusion proteins functionality was confirmed by testing strain growth on solid plates and liquid media. Strain crosses and sporulation were performed in sporulation medium plates (SPA), incubated at 25°C, and selected by tetrad dissection, using a dissection microscope (MSM 400; Singer Instruments) on YES agar plates. ump1-ts mutant was obtained by random mutagenesis. First, KanMX6 cassette was introduced after the ump1 gene by transformation and homologous recombination. Mutagenic PCR was performed by using NZYTaq II DNA Polymerase (from nzytech), by amplifying Ump1-Kan with primers listed in key resource table. PCR products were then transformed into a wild type strain and transformants were selected in geneticin-containing plates incubated at 25°C. After that, replica plates were incubated at 36°C to look for temperature-sensitive strains, which were further confirmed by PCR. DNA was sequenced by STAB VIDA laboratories. Strains expressing Ub-G76V-GFP were generated as follows: Ub-G76V-GFP DNA fragment was amplified from plasmid pYES2-Ub-G76V-GFP (Addgene #11954) with primers Ub-G76V fwd and Ub-G76V rev and cloned into the pINTL41 plasmid77 using the NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly (NEB). Digestion of this recombinant plasmid with NotI generated a cassette containing leu11/2-nmt41 promoter-Ub-G76V-GFP-nmt terminator-Ura4+-leu11/2, which was transformed into an ura4.d18 host strain (wild type or ump1-ts). Correct transformants were selected as those colonies growing in minimal media without uridine and not growing in minimal media without leucine as the disruption of leu1+ by the vector sequences flips auxotrophy from leu+ ura− to leu− ura+.

Heat shock treatment and inactivation of thermosensitive mutants

For HS treatment, cells were grown at 25°C to mid-log phase in air shaking incubators and then were shifted to 36°C or 39°C by transferring them to a shaking water bath incubator.

For inactivation of ump1-ts or cut8-ts mutants, cells were grown at 25°C to mid-log phase and then were shifted to 36°C in an air shaking incubator.

Drug treatments

Bortezomib (Quimigen) was added at a final concentration of 1 mM.

Canavanine (Sigma-aldrich) was added to liquid minimal media to reach a final concentration of 120 mg/L, and to solid minimal media plates to reach final concentrations of 30 or 60 mg/L.

In-gel activity assay

Protein extracts for in-gel activity assays in native conditions were prepared as follows: 50–100 mL of mid-log phase cultures were collected and washed once with cold PBS buffer. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 4°C, frozen with liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. Cells were thawed in ice in Protein Extraction Buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 2 mM ATP, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 15% (v/v) glycerol) with 5mM of the lysine-reactive crosslinker disuccinimidyl suberate to inhibit potential partial dissociation of 26S/30S particles, and lysed with acid-washed glass beads (G9268, Sigma-Aldrich), using either a FastPrep (MP Biomedicals) or a mortar and pestle in liquid nitrogen. Cell extracts were cleared by centrifugation at 15.000 rpm, 4°C during 10 min. Protein concentration was measured using PierceTM BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225, Thermo Fisher). Protein sample concentrations were adjusted to the same total protein concentration and resuspended in 6X-Loading Buffer (200 mM Tris-HCl pH6.8, 60% (v/v) glycerol, 0.05% (w/v) bromophenol blue). Protein samples were loaded on 4% polyacrylamide native gels [separating gel (TBE buffer, 4% Acrylamide/Bis Solution 37.5:1, 10% sucrose, 10% MAD buffer (50 mM MgCl2, 50 mM DTT, 10 mM ATP)) and stacking gel (1M Tris-HCl pH 6,8, 2,5% acrylamide)]. Electrophoresis buffer consisted of 90 mM Tris-HCl, 80 mM boric acid, 0.1 mM EDTA, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM ATP, 1 mM DTT, pH 8,3. Electrophoresis was surrounded by ice and run at 150 V for 80 min. Gels were incubated with 0.130 mM SucLLVY-AMC at 36°C for 15 min. Activity was analyzed with a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad) using the Oriole method and the Image LabTM software. For GFP visualization, the Pro-Q Emerald 488 method was used. SDS was added to a final concentration of 0,04%, gels were incubated for another 15 min at 36°C and activity was analyzed again. For Western immunoblots, proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes and subjected to immunoblot analysis, using as primary antibody an anti-proteasome monoclonal antibody that recognizes the 20S α1, 2, 3, 5, 6 & 7 subunits (MCP231; Enzo Life Sciences) at a 1:2000 dilution. Protein detection was performed using Supersignal West Femto (34095, Thermo Fisher).

Quantification of 20S bands were performed in native gels in the presence of SDS, whereas quantification of 26S/30S bands were performed in images from native gels in the absence of SDS.

Western blot analysis

To analyze proteasome subunits amounts, cultures were added to a Falcon tube containing ice-cold TCA to a final concentration of 10% and kept in ice for 30 min. After that cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 4°C and washed twice with acetone. After completely removing acetone, pellets were resuspended in beating buffer (8 M urea, 5 mM EDTA, 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate). Cell disruption was achieved with acid-washed glass beads (G9268, Sigma-Aldrich), using a FastPrep (MP Biomedicals). Cell extracts were cleared by centrifugation at 15.000 rpm, 4°C during 10 min. Protein concentration was measured using PierceTM BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225, Thermo Fisher). Protein sample concentration were adjusted to the same total protein concentration and resuspended in 2X-Loading buffer (100mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 4% SDS, 200mM DTT, 0.005% Bromophenol blue, 20% glycerol) and boiled for 5 min. Protein samples were loaded onto 10% TGX-Fast Cast acrylamide gels (161–0183; Bio-Rad) and blotted on nitrocellulose membranes (Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System, Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with PBS +0,1% Tween 20 + 10% (w/v) non-fat dry milk. Blots were probed o/n with primary mouse anti-HA (12CA5; 11583816001; Roche) at a 1:1000 dilution, with primary mouse anti-GFP (11814460001; Roche) at a 1:2000 dilution, or with primary mouse anti-ubiquitinylated proteins (BML-PW8810-0100; Enzo) at a 1:1000 dilution, followed by the secondary antibody anti-mouse IgG at a 1:2000 dilution (A3562; Sigma-Aldrich). Primary anti-RFP (PM005; MBL) at a 1:1000 dilution was followed by the secondary antibody anti-rabbit IgG at a 1:1000 dilution (A8275; Sigma-Aldrich). Total protein normalization was performed using the stain-free technology.35 Protein detection was performed using ECL (170–5060, Bio-Rad) and Chemidoc XRS+ (Bio-Rad).

For Figure S2G, protein extraction was performed as in.78 Briefly 50 mL of mid-log phase cultures were collected by centrifugation and resuspended in 250 μL lysis buffer A [20 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 0.5% NP-40, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, protease inhibitor cocktail, 1 mM PMSF and aprotinin (0.03–0.07 TIU)]. Cells were lysed with acid-washed glass beads (G9268, Sigma-Aldrich), using a FastPrep (MP Biomedicals). Lysates were centrifuged at 2000 g for 5 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant (Total fraction) was then centrifuged at 16000 g for 10 min to obtain pellet and supernatant fractions. They were resuspended in 5 μL 5X-SDS-sample buffer and boiled. Western blot was performed as previously described using mono- and polyubiquitinylated conjugates monoclonal antibody (FK2, BML-PW8810-0100, Enzo). at a 1:1000 dilution.

Drop assay for measuring yeast growth

Mid-log phase S. pombe cultures were diluted to an initial OD600 of 0.3. 1/5 serial dilutions were spotted onto rich or minimal media plates. They were imaged after three-six days of growth at the specified temperatures.

Microscopy and image analysis

Mid-log phase cultures grown at 25°C were treated as indicated and concentrated by centrifugation (3000 rpm), resuspended in conditioned media (media in which cells have been growing) and deposited in microscope slides or μ-Slide 8-well dishes (80827; Ibidi), previously coated with soybean lectin (L1395; Sigma-Aldrich). Live-cell imaging was performed either with a spinning-disk confocal microscope (Ix-81; Olympus; Evolve camera, Plan Apochromat 100 Å∼, 1.4 NA objective; Roper Scientific) with Metamorph software (Molecular Devices) or with a spinning disk 3i (Intelligent Imaging Innovations) (Zeiss Axio Observer 7 inverted, PRIME 95B camera, 1.46 NA objective; Zeiss) with SlideBook6 software. Images were obtained in 18 z sections with a step size of 0,3 μm. Temperature controlled live-cell imaging was performed at 36°C in an environmental chamber. Images were analyzed with Fiji software.72

Quantifications of nuclear Rpn1-GFP and GFP-Hsf1 were performed measuring the mean intensity on the whole nuclear area delimited by the NE signal in a single central z section. Quantification of cytoplasmic Rpn1-GFP and GFP-Hsf1 was performed measuring the mean intensity of a cytoplasmic region in a single central z section. Quantification of Pre6-Tomato foci was performed at a single z section. Quantifications of Ub-G76V-GFP, 5ÚTRhsp104-GFP-3ÚTRhsp104, Hsp104-RFP, Ssa1-YFP and Ssa2-GFP were performed on the whole cell area, on average projections of 18 z sections (step size 0,3 μm). Quantifications of Cdc13-GFP and Cut2-GFP were performed on the whole nuclear area, on average projections of 18 z sections (step size 0,3 μm). In every case, background signal intensity in every region of interest was subtracted.

Growth curves

Mid-log phase cultures grown at 25°C were diluted to an initial OD600 of 0.1 in a 96 well-microtiter plate (900011; Deltalab). Plates were incubated at the indicated temperature with agitation, and OD measurements were taken every 10 min for 22 h using the EVOware Standard software (Tecan), the microplate reader Spark (Tecan) and a Freedom EVO platform (Tecan).

DAPI/calcofluor staining

DAPI and calcofluor staining was performed as previously described.79 Briefly, mid-log phase cultured cells were incubated at the indicated temperatures and then fixed by adding 100 μL formaldehyde (37%) to 900 μL of culture. Cells were incubated in ice for 15 min, and then washed three times with cold 1X PBS. A small fraction of cells was extended on a coverslip and dried out. Cells were then stained with 5 μL of mounting solution [38 μL glycerol 50%, 46.5 μL H2O, 10 μL Antifade (p-phenylenediamine, Sigma P6001, 10 mg/mL in phosphate-buffered saline pH 8.2), 3 μL DAPI (4′, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, Sigma D1388, 0.1 mg/mL) and 2.5 μL calcofluor white (Sigma F6259, 0.35 mg/mL in water)].
