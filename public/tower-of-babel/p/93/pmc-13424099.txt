Modeling the impact of rural infrastructure on the population of rural settlements: a case study of Saqqez County, Iran
Authors: Gangren Zhang, Davood Jamini, Hossein Komasi, Ramin Atashbahar, Moslem Savari, Sahar Erfanian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough rural communities play a vital role in securing food supplies and hold the key to addressing many challenges faced by urban areas, the rural population in Iran-mirroring global trends-continues to decline. Existing evidence indicates that, despite the critical importance of rural infrastructure across multiple dimensions of sustainable rural development, the comprehensive impact of such infrastructure on rural population dynamics has received comparatively limited scholarly attention. Recognizing this research gap, the present study aims to identify the most influential categories of rural infrastructure and to model their effects on the population of rural settlements.This research draws on 37 infrastructural elements and is conducted as a case study of 263 villages in Saqqez County, located in Kurdistan Province. The required data were obtained from the Kurdistan Provincial Management and Planning Organization (using the latest Farhang-e Abadiha dataset from 2016) and through interviews with 30 key informants. Data analysis was performed using SPSS and Amos Graphics. The results of the exploratory factor analysis show that the most significant rural infrastructure components can be synthesized into nine major factors, which together explain 63.166% of the variance in the dependent variable. Correlation analysis between these factors and the rural population reveals that seven factors-namely Financial–Communication, Recreational, Security and Agriculture, Fundamental, Cultural and Service, Public Utility and Energy, and Vitality and Transportation and Sports-Beliefs-are positively and significantly associated with population levels. In contrast, the Religious and Sanitary factors show no statistically significant relationship. The key informants confirmed both the relevance of the nine identified factors and their thematic labels, although their perceived ranking of the most important infrastructural categories differed from that suggested by the exploratory factor analysis. Modeling the impact of the key infrastructure factors on rural settlement populations indicates a well-fitting structural model (P = 0.175; CMIN/DF = 1.378; RMR = 0.191; GFI = 0.986; NFI = 0.981; CFI = 0.995; PRATIO = 0.393; RMSEA = 0.038; HOELTER = 340). In the final model, the total effect (0.921) and the coefficient of determination (0.85) underscore the substantial and Significant association of rural infrastructure in being associated with patterns. Given the global tendency toward declining rural populations, the findings offer a valuable analytical framework that can be adapted by rural development planners and policymakers in similar regional contexts seeking to sustain population levels. It is therefore recommended that the identified key rural infrastructures be strengthened—both quantitatively and qualitatively—at the earliest opportunity and without delay.Keywords: Rural sustainable development, Population stability, Rural infrastructure, Rural settlementSubject terms: Development studies, Environmental social sciences, Environmental studies, Social policy

Introduction

Rural communities play an indispensable role in shaping national structures by contributing to economic growth, safeguarding cultural diversity, and reinforcing social cohesion1. Michael Todaro similarly argues that the key to resolving urban problems lies in improving rural environments; hence, rural development should take precedence over urban development2. Despite this central role, today’s rural landscapes face a host of interrelated challenges, including population decline and rising out-migration3, inadequate rural infrastructure4, 5, weak agricultural development institutions, technological constraints6, widespread poverty and low income levels7, stagnating economic activity8, and increasingly severe climatic fluctuations9. These challenges form a chain of mutually reinforcing pressures that expose rural communities to various shocks3 and pose serious threats to their long-term viability8. Among their many consequences, the accelerating decline of rural populations has emerged as a major global concern10, 11, 12. In response to this growing concern, rural infrastructure-regardless of how it is classified, whether as physical infrastructure and human capital13, capital-intensive, low-capital, and institutional infrastructure14, or hard and soft infrastructure15-is widely recognized as a cornerstone for sustaining production systems and supporting rural livelihoods3. Rural infrastructure constitutes the foundation of everyday life; without it, viable living conditions in rural areas are simply unattainable16. Its direct and indirect contributions to poverty reduction, empowerment of the poor14, narrowing inequalities, enabling equitable access to essential services and opportunities17, improving quality of life, and enhancing productivity have become increasingly evident9. Conversely, inadequate infrastructure-whether due to limited public funding or other constraints-poses a serious barrier to sustainable rural development18, 19. Given these multifaceted functions, the expansion of essential rural infrastructure has rightly become a policy priority for many governments20. Scholars, too, have examined the influence of rural infrastructure on a wide range of development outcomes, including farmers’ income growth21, 19, rural community development and sustainability1, 22, reductions in disparities in access to basic services and opportunities between urban and rural areas17, the livelihoods of smallholders23, household inequality and income24, 25, rural out-migration10, agricultural development and productivity6, 26, rural housing development4, and poverty reduction9. Moreover, despite the critical importance of maintaining population stability in rural settlements, the effects of rural infrastructure on settlement-level population dynamics remain largely understudied. In Iran, an examination of rural population trends over recent decades (1991–2016) reveals a clear downward trajectory. During this period, the rural population declined from 23,636,591 in 1991 to 20,730,625 in 2016-a decrease of 2,905,966 people over 25 years. In contrast, the urban population during the same interval rose sharply from 31,836,598 to 59,146,847 (Statistical Centre of Iran)62,63–65. More recent estimates indicate that this downward rural trend has intensified, with the rural population dropping further to 19,754,000 by 2024 (Statistical Centre of Iran)66. Such sustained depopulation reflects the limited effectiveness of national and local policies aimed at maintaining population levels in rural settlements27. Following the national pattern, Kurdistan Province has also witnessed a significant decline in its rural population over the past 25 years-from 646,339 to 468,778, representing a loss of 177,561 people (Statistical Centre of Iran)62,63–65. Weak transport, educational, and health infrastructure, limited internet access, and the broader concentration of services and facilities in urban centers are among the key drivers of rural depopulation in the province Ghaderzadah et al.,56. Among the ten counties of Kurdistan Province, Saqqez County similarly mirrors these national and regional shifts. Its rural population has decreased markedly-from 82,527 to 58,020 (Statistical Centre of Iran)62,63–65. What emerges from these figures is a consistent pattern of rural population decline at the national, regional, and local levels. This trend is particularly concerning given that rural communities in Iran play a pivotal role in securing food supplies; even under the most conservative estimates, they produce more than half of the nation’s primary food inputs28. Moreover, national, regional, and local development plans strongly emphasize the need to maintain viable populations in rural areas (Kurdistan Provincial Management and Planning Organization)57–59,60,61.

Despite the central role of villages in national and regional development-and despite policy documents underscoring the importance of stabilizing rural populations-there remains a significant research gap regarding the impact of rural infrastructure on the population dynamics of rural settlements. The available literature offers little empirical modeling on this subject, highlighting a clear need for further investigation.To address this gap and strengthen the literature on rural infrastructure and its demographic implications, the present study focuses on 263 villages within Saqqez County-the county with the highest number of inhabited villages in Kurdistan Province. The study seeks to answer the following key questions:

Literature review

Given the diversity of natural, political, and socio-cultural conditions across countries, the concept of a “village” has been defined in various ways. One of the most common criteria in these definitions is population size. Rural population may be classified based on density, the dominant occupations of residents, or a settlement’s position within the local administrative hierarchy. In general, villages tend to have low population density, employment is predominantly concentrated in the agricultural sector, and they are administered through rural municipalities12. In Iran, a rural settlement is defined as a locality situated within a rural district (dehestan) that possesses distinct legal or customary boundaries, lies outside the limits of cities or other villages, and is identifiable due to human activity occurring in all or part of its territory. Such settlements typically carry a specific name and include both agricultural and non-agricultural lands (Statistical Centre of Iran)65.

In many developing countries, public investment in rural infrastructure expanded substantially during the 1960s and 1970s29, and the critical role of infrastructure in enhancing agricultural productivity became widely recognized by the mid-1980s25. The concept of infrastructure, however, has long been marked by definitional ambiguity and has evolved considerably over time. Hirschman (1958) described infrastructure as a set of essential services without which primary, secondary, and tertiary productive activities could not function. According to a World Bank report (1994), the term serves as an umbrella encompassing a broad range of activities that development economists such as Arthur Lewis, Rosenstein-Rodan, Ragner Nurkse and Albert Hirschman conceptualized as “social overhead capital”26. For Lewis, infrastructure referred to public services, ports, and the provision of water and electricity. Rostow, in his “Stages of Economic Growth,” identified social overhead capital-particularly in transport and communication-as one of the key preconditions for economic takeoff30.

In development literature, infrastructure is generally understood as the set of services and facilities that are indispensable to human life. These include transport systems, communication networks, electricity, water supply, education, healthcare, irrigation, drainage, and various other forms of public service provision25. Within this broader framework, rural infrastructure-considered the cornerstone of rural welfare31-refers to the basic public facilities that support agricultural production and sustain the livelihoods of rural residents3. Another definition describes rural infrastructure as a combination of diverse foundational services that not only facilitate agricultural activities but also support broader rural development and, in some cases, even urban functions30.

Overall, the presence-or absence-of adequate infrastructure in rural settlements can shape their trajectory, contributing either to their advancement or to their decline1.

Given the significance and multidimensional nature of infrastructure, various classifications have been proposed in the literature. Patel categorizes infrastructure components into three groups: capital-intensive infrastructure, low-capital infrastructure, and institutional infrastructure14. Among these, capital-intensive infrastructure carries greater visibility and importance in rural areas. This category is commonly divided into three subsets: Rural Social Infrastructure (RST)-including health centers, educational facilities, and essential rural services such as water and electricity; Rural Physical Infrastructure (RPI)-such as transportation networks, storage and processing facilities, soil conservation systems, and irrigation; and Rural Institutional Infrastructure (RII), which encompasses organizations established to support agricultural sectors, including research institutions, credit and marketing services, and cooperative associations23, 24.

Other scholars have suggested alternative classifications-for example, physical infrastructure versus human capital infrastructure13, or soft versus hard infrastructure15.

Overall, because infrastructure exerts wide-ranging effects across numerous dimensions of development, investing in infrastructure has been identified as one of the United Nations’ sustainable development goals19. Given the breadth of infrastructural elements found in rural settlements, these components influence rural development processes in various ways. Key impacts of rural infrastructure on rural settlements include accelerating agricultural productivity26, 29, 32, 33, strengthening food security6, reducing poverty9, 18, 21, 34, narrowing the urban–rural divide, fostering the foundations of a knowledge-based society8, reducing production costs29, enhancing quality of life, improving energy efficiency35, supporting livelihood improvement and community sustainability8, increasing income growth32, raising labor productivity, expanding access to employment and welfare14, 36, facilitating access to productive opportunities, increasing the asset value of low-income households37, and satisfying basic human needs22. Given the critical role of rural infrastructure across different dimensions of rural development, a substantial body of research has been devoted to this topic. Several key studies are summarized below.

Achjar et al.13, in their study on the impact of rural infrastructure development on poverty reduction in Indonesia, revealed that both physical infrastructure (e.g., roads, bridges, electricity, irrigation) and non-physical infrastructure (human capital, such as education and health) significantly contribute to poverty reduction in rural areas. They found that this impact is achieved directly and indirectly by enhancing the economic capacity of rural inhabitants. Goswami and Chatterjee,33 highlighted the influence of infrastructure and technology on agricultural productivity in the state of Uttar Pradesh, India. Similarly, Llanto,34 affirmed the positive impact of infrastructure on agricultural productivity in the Philippines. In Nigeria, Adepoju and Salman,14 conducted a study in the Oyo and Osun states, demonstrating that various infrastructural elements-including electricity, water supply, transportation networks, health centers, storage facilities, research institutions, and extension services-had positive and significant effects on agricultural productivity. Finally, Tanga et al.,5 emphasized that road improvements in the Phamong region of Lesotho not only expedited residents’ access to essential social services (which were previously inaccessible) but also considerably improved rural livelihoods.

Charlery et al.,24 demonstrated that newly established transportation infrastructure had a significantly positive impact on rural household income and reduced inequality in Nepal. Similarly, a field study by Emokaro and Oyoboh23 in Edo State, Nigeria, revealed that rural infrastructure improvements-across income generation, education, health, water access, transportation, and electricity-led to enhanced livelihoods and living standards for residents. Manjunath and Kannan,26 found that the availability and utilization of infrastructure in India’s Karnataka region had a positive and significant effect on agricultural productivity growth. Manggat et al.,31 emphasized that successful infrastructure development in rural Malaysia necessitates collaboration and integration among all relevant parties, particularly development planners and social workers, ultimately improving the quality of life in rural communities. In Indonesia, Fajri and Rarasati,36 showed that infrastructure development in the Batang Regency villages positively influenced various dimensions of rural sustainable development.

The findings of Chaurey & Li..55 indicate that the implementation of the national Rashtriya Sam Vikas Yojana program-designed to promote infrastructure development-led to increases in employment, the number of micro-enterprises, workers’ wages, and total working days. González-González and Nogués,17 likewise show that long-term investment in transportation infrastructure in rural areas of northwestern Spain has narrowed the accessibility gap between urban and rural regions, thereby providing rural residents with more equitable access to essential services, goods, and opportunities. Ko et al.,8 report that ICT infrastructure in rural communities in South Korea has enhanced digital literacy, strengthened local economies and community activities, and played a substantial role in community sustainability. Van Brug38 and Jamini & Mohammadi54 highlight the contribution of religious infrastructure to local economic development in Indonesia and Iran, respectively. Kwarciński35 demonstrates that the expansion of road networks has had a positive effect on rural population levels in Poland, noting that transportation infrastructure serves as a supportive factor in socio-economic development. Aldashev and Batkeyev,39 conclude that although broadband infrastructure in Kazakhstan does not necessarily stimulate economic growth, it positively influences the retail sector while exhibiting no significant effect on agricultural or manufacturing industries. Wang et al.,40 acknowledge that investment in rural infrastructure contributes to income growth among farmers in China. Hussain et al.,22 show that rural infrastructure projects in Pakistan influence local development through improvements in social and economic indicators. Cañal-Fernández & Álvarez12 emphasize the pivotal role of both social and physical infrastructure in sustaining rural populations in Asturias, Spain. Omotoso et al.,6 find that rural infrastructure significantly affects the production efficiency of farmers in Ogun State, Nigeria. Qin et al.,9 highlight the influence of infrastructure-particularly irrigation and drainage facilities and rural transport systems-on poverty reduction in China’s rural regions. Finally, Tveter10, in a study involving eight remote islands in Norway, shows that road investment and improvement gradually affect migration patterns: once rural islands become connected to urban centers through roadway networks, migration tends to intensify.

Kaiser and Barstow41, in a comprehensive review, showed that the improvement, upgrading, and expansion of transportation infrastructure generate substantial benefits for rural residents in low- and middle-income countries. The findings of Mousavi et al.,42 in Iran and Guo et al.43 in China indicate that access to sports facilities and infrastructure enhances local community participation in sports-related activities as part of the broader process of sustainable rural development. Lu et al.,21 found that road infrastructure significantly affects the income levels of rural residents in China. Kaur et al.,29 emphasized a positive and significant relationship between rural infrastructure and agricultural growth in India. Yuan and Wang,19 also demonstrated that infrastructure investment contributes to income growth among rural populations in China. According to Ezeudu and Fadeyi1, inadequate infrastructure adversely influences the economic conditions, educational attainment, and healthcare access of rural communities. Jain,4 showed that improvements in transportation infrastructure have stimulated housing development and rural population growth across Indian states. Li et al.,3 further demonstrated that the resilience of rural infrastructure shapes rural population levels in Longxi County, China. A review of the related literature reveals several key insights. First, a wide range of definitions and classifications exist for infrastructure and rural infrastructure. Although rural infrastructure encompasses numerous essential elements-such as roads, electricity, water, gas, health centers, ICT offices, and internet access-most empirical studies focus on the impact of a single infrastructural component (particularly roads and transportation). This narrow approach may be misleading, as infrastructure represents a broad, interconnected system whose components collectively influence various dimensions of rural development and even rural population dynamics. Second, most studies prioritize economic outcomes, especially agricultural development, while the modeling of rural infrastructure effects has received limited attention. Third, very few studies have examined the impact of infrastructure on rural population change. Significant empirical gaps persist despite the fact that several international development frameworks have offered substantial insights into the connection between infrastructure and rural development. The World Bank’s rural development frameworks and UN-Habitat’s perspectives on sustainable settlements are two of the most significant policy-oriented contributions that highlight the strategic significance of infrastructure in promoting balanced territorial development and enhancing rural communities’ quality of life. In a similar vein, the structural elements affecting rural population shifts have also been studied in the literature on regional demographic transition. These studies, however, concentrate more on national or regional development patterns and are mostly conceptual or macro-scale in character. Because of this, they offer little empirical data regarding the interactions between various aspects of rural infrastructure and how those interactions affect demographic results at the level of rural communities. Specifically, few studies have examined the effects of several infrastructure components on rural population patterns at the same time using integrated statistical modeling methodologies. Therefore, empirical research that methodically examines the combined effects of several infrastructure characteristics on population dynamics at the rural settlement level is necessary to address this constraint. In light of these research gaps, the present study aims to identify the most critical elements of rural infrastructure and to analyze the collective effect of a comprehensive set of infrastructural components on the population of rural settlements using an empirical modeling approach. This research strategy can provide a foundation for future studies in the field. Research methodology

This section begins with a brief introduction to the study area, followed by a detailed description of the research process.

Study area

The county of Saqqez has a total population of 226,451 inhabitants, of whom 25.6% reside in rural settlements and 74.4% live in urban areas. Among the ten counties of Kurdistan Province, Saqqez contains the largest number of rural settlements. According to the most recent Population and Housing Census, the county encompasses 263 rural settlements with a combined population of 58,020 people. On average, approximately 221 individuals reside in each village. The literacy rate among the rural population stands at 71.49% (80.38% for men and 62.41% for women).

Evidence from the last three national censuses (2006, 2011, and 2016) indicates that the rural population of Saqqez County has declined by roughly 15,300 people over this period (Statistical Centre of Iran)64, 65. This decline may be attributed to several factors, including the reclassification of rural settlements into urban areas, migration, and the abandonment of villages as a result of inadequate quantity and quality of key infrastructures such as education, healthcare, recreation, and transportation Fig. 2.

Location of the study area in Kurdistan Province and Iran.

Research materials and methods

From the perspective of its objectives, the present study is applied in nature. In terms of its design and methodology, it employs a combination of descriptive, correlational, and causal approaches. The study area encompasses 263 villages located within Saqqez County. To collect the necessary data, both secondary and primary sources were used. In addition to official documents published by the Kurdistan Provincial Management and Planning Organization (as secondary data), the study also incorporated insights from key informants-gathered through in-person interviews-to enhance the validity of the findings. Given the main objective of the study, the overall research process can be seen in the chart below Fig. 3. To achieve the final research goal, the following steps were performed hierarchically:

Overall research process.

Phase 1: Review of studies relevant to the research topic.

During this phase, the scientific literature, particularly published articles concerning the research topic, was meticulously reviewed, leading to several notable observations. These included: the presentation of diverse definitions for the concepts of infrastructure and rural infrastructure, alongside numerous classifications provided for them; the limited attention researchers have dedicated to the specific subject of infrastructure’s impact on the population of rural settlements; a predominant focus on the effect of a single infrastructural element on the rural economy, particularly in the agricultural sector; and the non-utilization of the Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) approach in data analysis. Despite the extensive range of definitions and classifications offered for rural infrastructure, this study adopts a definition that is a combination of the concepts and typologies presented in the studies of14, 23, 24.

Phase 1: Data collection.

In this stage, the required data was obtained by visiting the Kurdistan Province Planning and Management Organization. The booklet containing data for the villages of Saqqez County was acquired, and the necessary information was extracted into an Excel file. The raw data included the population of 263 villages and information related to 37 types of rural infrastructure (Table 1). These infrastructural elements represent a combination of both public and private infrastructure. Given the limitations in accessing the necessary data, the analysis focused on the presence or absence of these elements within the villages. The data utilized in this research, therefore, solely comprises the existence or non-existence of specific infrastructural components across the 263 villages under study.

To incorporate local viewpoints on rural infrastructure needs, a qualitative discussion procedure was carried out in addition to the statistical study. In order to do this, thirty key informants-local administrators, village council members, agricultural specialists, and community leaders who were acquainted with the state of development in the villages of Saqqez County-were interviewed in a semi-structured manner. Since this study mainly used the official “Farhangabadi” dataset, the goal of these discussions was to establish the significance of the identified infrastructure components and to understand local viewpoints on their relative importance rather than to create infrastructure variables.

By using this method, the research was able to incorporate stakeholder views into quantitative modeling and make sure that the selected infrastructure characteristics accurately reflected the concerns and development objectives of rural areas. Additionally, since the current analysis recognizes that different villages may have different infrastructure needs, the empirical model evaluates how the availability of various infrastructure components is statistically related to population patterns rather than assuming that all settlements have the same infrastructure demand.

Phase 3: Data analysis.

In this phase, given that the main objective of using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) is to condense data into a few key and significant factors44, 45, the 37 variables under investigation were subjected to an EFA test using SPSS software. Through this analysis, the most significant rural infrastructure elements were summarized into nine key and critical factors.

Phase 4: Validating the results of exploratory factor analysis by key informants.

In this stage, the validity of the results obtained from the Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)-which condensed rural infrastructure into nine key factors-was assessed by 30 key informants residing in the villages (including dehyars (village heads), council members, educated individuals, and community trustees). The selection criteria for these individuals were a combination of factors such as education, residency history in the village, management background (as a council member or dehyar), and good reputation/trustworthiness within the rural community.

This process was conducted through face-to-face interviews with the key informants and involved two main questions:

The key informants were asked whether the nine identified factors, along with their assigned names, represented the most significant rural infrastructure elements, and were requested to provide supplementary explanations in this regard.

The key informants were asked to assign a score between 1 and 9 to each of the nine identified factors (based on their importance in the process of rural development). This was done to prioritize the rural infrastructure identified through EFA, leveraging the expert opinions of the key informants.

Phase 5: Examining the relationship between the most significant rural infrastructure and the population of rural settlements.

In this phase, the statistical correlation between the most significant rural infrastructure elements (the nine identified key infrastructure factors as independent variables) and the village population (as the dependent variable) was investigated using the Pearson correlation coefficient in SPSS software. The analysis revealed that among the nine variables examined, seven showed a statistically significant correlation, while two variables did not have a statistically significant relationship with the dependent variable of the research.

Phase 6: Modeling the impact of infrastructure on the population of rural settlements.

Subsequently, to present an empirical model of the effects of the most significant infrastructure on the population of rural settlements, Amos Graphics software was employed. Initially, the first-order measurement model for the most important rural infrastructure (the seven factors with a statistically significant relationship with the rural population) was drawn, and its fit was examined and confirmed. Following this, the impact of the most significant infrastructure on the population of rural settlements was depicted in the form of the final research model, and the results indicated an appropriate fit for the drawn model. Prior to designing and validating the final research model, multicollinearity among the variables was assessed. To evaluate the degree of collinearity, Tolerance, VIF, and Condition Index values were calculated. According to established standards, the thresholds for these criteria are defined as greater than 0.4, less than 2.5, and less than 15, respectively45. Based on these benchmarks, the results confirmed the absence of multicollinearity among the variables used in the model.

Phase 7: Discussion and conclusion.

In this final stage, the relevant discussions were conducted based on the obtained results. Finally, while presenting the analytical findings, the proposed solutions, the limitations of the research, and suggestions for future studies were provided.

Validation of exploratory factor analysis results by key informants

To validate the findings, the key infrastructures identified through exploratory factor analysis were presented to the key informants. The results showed that all informants endorsed the nine extracted factors as the core infrastructures shaping rural settlements. Nevertheless, they emphasized that the mere presence of infrastructure in a village is insufficient; its quality must also be improved. One informant explained this point as follows: “Imagine a village with a health house that lacks adequate equipment and trained personnel; such an infrastructure cannot meaningfully contribute to rural development.”

Most informants echoed similar concerns, highlighting the generally poor quality of existing infrastructures. They pointed to issues such as narrow and substandard roads, the low quality of construction materials used in many physical facilities, the deterioration of existing structures, and inadequate equipment in rural infrastructures, including kindergartens, sports halls, and ICT offices. They also noted severe shortages in certain critical infrastructures, such as middle and high schools, sports facilities, agricultural service centers, and water treatment systems in many rural settlements. To determine the relative importance of the nine factors identified through exploratory factor analysis, key informants were subsequently asked to assign each factor a score ranging from 1 to 9 based on its perceived contribution to rural development. The scores provided by the 30 interviewees were then aggregated, and the average score for each factor was calculated to establish their overall ranking. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the prioritization offered by the key informants differs somewhat from the ranking produced by exploratory factor analysis. Examining the relationship between infrastructure and rural settlement populations

After identifying the key rural infrastructures through exploratory factor analysis and confirming them with the key informants, the study proceeded to determine which infrastructures are most closely associated with rural population levels. To this end, the statistical relationships between the nine extracted factors and the population of rural settlements were examined using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (Table 5).The findings indicate that seven of the factors—Financial-Communication, Recreational, Security and Agriculture, Fundamental, Cultural and Service, Public Utility and Energy, and Vitality and Transportation and Sports-Beliefs—demonstrated a statistically significant and positive relationship with the rural population. This suggests that changes in the levels of these infrastructures are associated with corresponding shifts in rural population. Consequently, these seven factors represent the key infrastructures that demonstrate a significant relationship with the population of rural settlements. In contrast, the analysis showed that the statistical relationship between the factors of Religious and Sanitary infrastructures and the rural settlement population was not significant. This implies that while these two factors are considered important infrastructures in rural settlements, they do not show a statistically significant association with population size in the context of this model.

As shown in Table 6, all estimated criteria demonstrated a strong concordance with the standard values. Discussion

Given the broad range of infrastructural elements in rural settlements, identifying the most significant ones can provide a crucial cognitive basis for revising and targeting policies and plans. In this study, rural infrastructures were categorized into nine key factors using exploratory factor analysis. This factor identification and classification process is statistically affirmed.

Nevertheless, in order to validate the results-derived from data provided by the Statistical Centre of Iran-the researchers presented the findings to the key informants and asked them to judge both the labeling and the number of extracted factors. Their evaluations confirmed all nine identified factors. However, the informants also stressed that the mere nominal or symbolic presence of an infrastructure in a village-particularly one with limited service capacity-does not substantially contribute to rural development. For infrastructure to exert its intended impact, its quality must reach an acceptable and functional threshold. In practice, a village may contain several types of infrastructure, yet due to inadequate equipment, insufficient services, or even underqualified personnel, these facilities may be unable to provide meaningful support to the rural population. The informants further noted that the absence of essential educational infrastructures, particularly middle and high schools, as well as health-care facilities, is strongly evident in many villages. They emphasized the need for a fundamental re-evaluation and expansion of these infrastructures. The ranking of key rural infrastructures based on the perspectives of key informants revealed several differences compared with the statistical prioritization. These discrepancies, however, do not invalidate the analytical results. A closer inspection of the two approaches shows that in both cases, the four most critical infrastructures-those with the highest eigenvalues and scores-occupy the top four positions, albeit with slight variations in their order. Overall, the findings of this section highlight both the convergences and divergences between software-based results (derived from Statistical Centre of Iran data) and the judgments of key informants. Under these circumstances, it is possible to state with greater confidence that the most important infrastructures for the development of rural settlements, consistently identified by both approaches, are:

Financial-Communication; Recreational, Security and Agriculture; Fundamental; Cultural and Service; Public Utility and Energy; Vitality and Transportation; Religious; Sports-Beliefs; and Sanitary. Regarding the Financial-Communication factor, which emerged as the most critical rural infrastructure, the findings align with several international studies. Goswami and Chatterjee33 identified technological infrastructure as a major rural asset in India; Ko et al.8 emphasized ICT infrastructure in South Korea; and Aldashev and Batkeyev and39 highlighted broadband infrastructure as a key component in Kazakhstan. These parallels reinforce the validity of the present study’s results. Similarly, the analysis of the Recreational, Security and Agriculture factor, ranked second in importance, shows consistency with prior research. Adepoju and Salman,14 underscored the significance of storage facilities and agricultural extension centers, while Qin et al.,9 pointed to irrigation and drainage systems as crucial rural infrastructures. Thus, the findings of this study correspond closely to these earlier works.

With respect to the Fundamental factor, identified as the third most important rural infrastructure, the analysis showed strong alignment with numerous international studies. Achjar et al.13 in Indonesia, Cañal-Fernández & Álvarez12 in Spain, and Adepoju and Salman14 in Nigeria highlighted the role of both physical and non-physical infrastructures in rural development. Likewise, a wide range of studies-Tanga et al.,5 in Lesotho; Charlery et al.24 in Nepal; González-González and Nogués17 in Spain; Kwarcinski35 in Poland; Qin et al.,9 in China; Tveter10 in Norway; Kaiser and Barstow41 in lower- and middle-income countries; Lu et al.,21 in China; and Jain and4 in India-consistently emphasized road and transportation networks as among the most critical infrastructures in rural areas. Taken together, these observations indicate that the findings of the present study are fully consistent with prior research.

Regarding the Cultural and Service factor, the results showed that Achjar et al.13 identified educational infrastructure as one of the most essential components of rural development.

For the Public Utility and Energy factor, the review revealed that both Achjar et al.13 in Indonesia and Cañal-Fernández & Álvarez12 in Spain underscored the importance of public utilities in supporting rural development processes. Concerning the Vitality and Transportation factor, the findings were again in line with earlier studies. Research conducted by Achjar et al.13 in Indonesia, Adepoju and Salman14 and Emokaro and Oyoboh23 in Nigeria, González-González and Nogués17 in Spain, and Qin et al.,9 in China all emphasized the crucial role of transportation and vitality-related infrastructures in rural development. The findings of Van Burg38 and Jamini and Mohammadi54 further highlighted the importance of religious infrastructures in shaping the economic development of local communities in Indonesia and Iran.With respect to the Sports-Beliefs factor, the review indicated that Mousavi et al.,42 in Iran and Guo et al.,43 in China acknowledged the influence of access to sports facilities on community participation in recreational and athletic activities-an important dimension of sustainable rural development. Additionally, both Van Burg38 and Jamini and Mohammadi54 emphasized the role of religious beliefs and related infrastructures in supporting rural development in Indonesia and Iran.

Finally, for the Sanitary factor, identified as the last of the key infrastructures, earlier research consistently recognized its importance. Studies by Achjar et al.13 in Indonesia, Adepoju and Salman14 and Emokaro and Oyoboh23 in Nigeria, and Cañal-Fernández & Álvarez12 in Spain all described health-related infrastructures as essential components of rural settlement systems. The alignment between the results of this study and those of previous research underscores the significance of the identified infrastructures in rural development and can be regarded as a strength of the present work. However, a notable distinction is that this study identifies a broader and more integrated set of infrastructures as the core elements of rural development-offering a more comprehensive perspective than many previous studies.

The correlation analysis between the major identified infrastructure categories and the population of rural settlements revealed that the variables Financial–Communication, Recreational, Security and Agriculture, Fundamental, Cultural and Service, Public Utility and Energy, Vitality and Transportation, and Sports-Beliefs all exhibit significant and positive associations with rural population levels. In other words, these infrastructures constitute the most influential factors shaping population patterns in rural settlements. Accordingly, effective policymaking and the design of sustainability-oriented programs aimed at stabilizing rural populations must prioritize enhancing both the quantity and quality of these infrastructures and their constituent elements. This finding aligns with previous research, including studies by Fajri and Rarasati36, Kwarcinski35, Cañal-Fernández & Álvarez12, Jain4, and Li et al.3, all of which emphasize the strong relationship between infrastructure development and population stability or growth in rural areas. The distinguishing feature of the present study, however, lies in its simultaneous empirical assessment of a comprehensive set of infrastructure types, offering a broader and more integrated perspective than earlier studies. One notable exception relates to the work of Tveter10, who found that improved road infrastructure in certain Norwegian islands contributed to increased rural out-migration. The current research diverges from this conclusion. Tveter’s study focused on a uniquely isolated rural population residing across eight remote islands, where enhanced mobility encouraged migration to offset geographic isolation. By contrast, rural communities in Saqqez County are not characterized by such extreme remoteness from essential services or urban centers, which may explain the contrasting demographic responses to infrastructure improvements.

In contrast to the seven infrastructure categories that demonstrated significant relationships with population, the Religious and Sanitary factors showed no statistically meaningful association. Insights obtained from key informants clarify these results. In predominantly Muslim societies, religious structures—particularly mosques—are almost universally present in villages regardless of population size, making the lack of correlation with population unsurprising. Similarly, the absence of a significant relationship between the Sanitary factor and population reflects its limited availability and inadequate quality. Among the 263 villages assessed, only 12 were equipped with water treatment systems, and the performance of these facilities has deteriorated markedly over time. Such limited coverage and substandard conditions significantly diminish the potential influence of sanitary infrastructure on population distribution.

Structural modeling results further affirmed the analytical rigor and validity of the proposed framework. The model met al.l standard criteria for empirical adequacy and effectively captured the influence of key infrastructures on rural population dynamics. The total effect value of 0.921 and an explained variance of 0.85 indicate a substantial role of infrastructure in shaping demographic outcomes. While previous studies3, 4, 35, Cañal-Fernández & Álvarez12 have also demonstrated links between infrastructure and rural population growth, the unique contribution of this research lies in its integrated evaluation of a broad set of infrastructure dimensions within a single empirical model—a level of comprehensiveness seldom found in the existing literature. Overall, the theoretical foundations and empirical results of the present study highlight the multidimensional nature of infrastructure effects in rural development processes. Strengthening infrastructure not only supports demographic stability but also enables the expansion of diverse economic activities-including rural tourism, a domain with significant yet underexploited potential in many rural areas. Gradual enhancement of these infrastructures can therefore help lay the groundwork for sustainable rural development.

Conclusion

Rural communities have played a pivotal role in the development of major sectors of economic activity (agriculture, industry, and services) from ancient times to the present day.

Throughout history, rural communities have played a pivotal role in the primary sectors of economic activity, including agriculture, industry, and services. Their central function in producing raw food materials and ensuring food security is just one aspect of their broader contributions to society.

Despite the critical role of infrastructure in rural development, numerous weaknesses and gaps remain in the existing literature, as discussed in earlier sections. Acknowledging these limitations, the present study seeks to address this gap by identifying the most important rural infrastructures and examining their effects on the population of rural settlements within a comprehensive empirical model.

The main findings can be summarized as follows:

The findings show that the primary types of rural infrastructure fall into nine categories: Financial–Communication; Recreational, Security, and Agriculture; Fundamental; Cultural and Service; Public Utility and Energy; Vitality and Transportation; Religious; Sports–Beliefs; and Sanitary. Future studies should take a holistic approach that assesses their overall effects rather than looking at each one separately because of their interconnectedness. Seven of these infrastructures demonstrated statistically significant correlations with rural population levels: Financial–Communication; Recreational, Security, and Agriculture; Fundamental; Cultural and Service; Public Utility and Energy; Vitality and Transportation; and Sports–Beliefs. Therefore, in order to sustain population stability in rural communities, development planning should give priority to the qualitative and quantitative enhancement of these important infrastructures. The total association and variance explained by the final model highlight the significant role of rural infrastructures in relation to population outcomes. In effect, the interconnected structure of these infrastructure components, collectively conceptualized as “rural infrastructures,” represents a major predictor associated with population stability in rural settlements. This insight provides a valuable scientific foundation for future research on rural populations and infrastructures. Overall, presenting a validated empirical model that captures the associations between infrastructure and rural population constitutes the primary innovation of this study and offers a basis for further model development.

This study’s primary contribution is its integrated analytical method to investigating the connection between rural population dynamics and infrastructure. This study simultaneously evaluates a wide range of infrastructure elements and integrates them into a single structural model using nine empirically derived categories, whereas prior research has frequently concentrated on individual infrastructure components—particularly transportation or agricultural infrastructure. By doing this, the current study offers a more thorough knowledge of the relationship between various infrastructure characteristics and population trends in rural areas.

Strong associations rather than conclusive causal effects are indicated by the statistical correlations found in this study; the particular socioeconomic and geographic features of Saqqez County also have an impact on the empirical findings. However, this modeling framework adds to the body of knowledge on rural infrastructure and demographic sustainability by offering a transportable analytical tool that can be modified and tested in different rural contexts. Considering that rural populations form the backbone of social life in Iran, and given the central role of rural communities in local, regional, and national spatial development-as well as the multidimensional impacts of rural infrastructures-any effort to strengthen the quantity and quality of infrastructure in rural settlements is commendable. In general, due to the role of infrastructure in sustaining rural populations, the interdependent nature among various infrastructure elements, the consistency between the results of the exploratory factor analysis and the insights of key informants, and the infrastructure factors included in the final empirical model, the ideal scenario is the simultaneous development of all seven identified key infrastructures. However, in cases where financial and temporal constraints limit the possibility of improving all infrastructures at once, priority should be given to those infrastructures that achieved the highest importance in both the exploratory factor analysis and the evaluations of key informants. The results of this study offer a preliminary analytical model for evaluating the demographic resilience of rural communities. However, given the single-case regional design of this research, the model should be tested and validated in other geographical contexts before broader generalization can be made. In such cases, greater emphasis should be placed on enhancing: Financial-Communication; Recreational, Security and Agriculture; Fundamental; Cultural and Service. Although the statistical modeling and analytical methodology used in this study are robust, there are a number of constraints to be aware of. First, rather than conducting in-depth surveys of rural populations, the study mainly relied on secondary statistical data from official sources and the opinions of key informants. The opinions and priorities of a wider range of rural inhabitants were not directly captured, despite the fact that the insights of key informants offered important contextual confirmation. Therefore, by using household surveys or participatory methods involving a larger spectrum of local stakeholders, future research could improve the empirical foundation of these findings. Second, the research method was impacted by certain operational limitations, such as the dependence on comparatively outdated statistical data on rural populations and the lack of collaboration from certain government agencies and institutions in supplying reliable statistical information. Additionally, we occasionally found that some rural locals had little faith in the researchers during field trips, which made direct data collecting more difficult.

Third, the results should be interpreted as indicating meaningful correlations rather than necessarily deterministic causal effects because the cross-sectional nature of the available data prevents the extraction of definitive causal conclusions, even though structural equation modeling demonstrates strong statistical relationships between rural infrastructure and population levels.

Finally, because Saqqez County in the Kurdistan Province of Iran is the specific focus of the empirical analysis, the results should be cautiously extrapolated to other rural areas because variations in socioeconomic circumstances, institutional settings, and cultural contexts may result in distinct dynamics in the relationship between infrastructure and population. However, this study’s analytical framework offers a helpful foundation for testing comparable models in different rural settings and conducting comparative analyses.

To further advance and enrich the current line of inquiry, the following research directions are recommended for future studies: developing an empirical model to examine the impact of rural infrastructure on indicators of sustainable rural development; identifying focal villages suitable for the allocation of key infrastructure in order to provide services to surrounding settlements; identifying and prioritizing the underlying causes of inadequate quantity and quality of infrastructure in rural settlements and proposing strategies for improvement; and conducting scenario-based analyses of infrastructure development in rural areas.
