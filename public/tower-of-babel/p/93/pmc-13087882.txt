Additional treatment strategies for hypothyroidism: a network meta-analysis
Authors: Xingang Lv, Wentao Qin, Jiarui Li, Likun Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypothyroidism often impairs quality of life (QoL). This network meta-analysis (NMA) evaluated the efficacy and safety of additional interventions alongside levothyroxine (LT4).MethodsThis study adhered to the PRISMA guidelines and is registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251085765). PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases were systematically searched for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on the treatment of hypothyroidism. A Bayesian NMA was performed, reporting standardized mean differences (SMDs) or risk ratios (RRs) with 95% credible intervals (CrIs). Evidence certainty was assessed using the CINeMA framework.ResultsThirty-five RCTs involving 3,508 patients were included. Compared with placebo, levothyroxine (LT4) + aerobic training (AT) + resistance training (RT) significantly reduced thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels (SMD = −3.97, 95% CrI: −5.76, −2.18, CINeMA: high certainty); LT4 + zinc (Zn) + magnesium (Mg) + vitamin A (VA) raised free thyroxine (FT4) (SMD = 1.95, 95% CrI: 1.37, 2.53, CINeMA: high certainty); and LT4 + Zn increased free triiodothyronine (FT3) (SMD = 1.46, 95% CrI: 0.40, 2.51, CINeMA: low certainty). In addition, LT4 + AT + RT significantly improved QoL score (SMD = 1.62, 95% CrI: 0.78, 2.46) and mental health score (MHS) (SMD = 2.1, 95% CrI: 1.19, 3.01, CINeMA: low certainty) compared with LT4 alone. LT4 + RT significantly improved physical function score (SMD = 1.59, 95% CrI: 0.76, 2.43). Liothyronine (LT3) increased adverse events versus placebo (RR = 15.54, 95% CrI: 2.68, 501.14, CINeMA: very low certainty).ConclusionThe combination of LT4, AT, and RT may be the preferred strategy for reducing TSH, enhancing QoL, and improving MHS. Clinical interventions should be tailored based on individual patient profiles.

Background

Hypothyroidism (i.e., thyroid hormone deficiency) is one of the most prevalent endocrine disorders worldwide, affecting almost all systems of the body. If left untreated, it can have adverse effects on multiple organs and tissues and, in rare cases, may even be life-threatening (1). The prevalence of hypothyroidism varies significantly worldwide. In countries with adequate iodine nutrition, the prevalence in the general population is roughly 2%, with a higher rate among older adults, reaching up to 7% in the 85–89 age group. In Europe, the prevalence ranges from 0.2 to 5.3%, while in the United States, it ranges from 0.3 to 3.7%. In India, the incidence has risen to 10%. In the United Kingdom, there is a notable sex difference in the incidence of primary hypothyroidism, with a prevalence of 3.5–5.0 per thousand among women and 0.6–1.0 per thousand among men (2). The prevalence of subclinical hypothyroidism in China has risen to 16.7%, and approximately 2–5% of cases may progress to overt hypothyroidism each year (3, 4). A European study estimated that the prevalence of undiagnosed hypothyroidism was 5–6% (5). These data collectively indicate that hypothyroidism and its related clinical features pose significant challenges to global public health.

Current clinical consensus clearly states that all patients with overt or subclinical hypothyroidism exhibiting thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels > 10 mIU/L require treatment. Levothyroxine (LT4), as the standard therapeutic agent, is widely recognized for its safety and cost-effectiveness. It effectively restores patients’ thyroid function-related biochemical indicators to the reference range and improves clinical symptoms in most patients (1). However, significant limitations exist in clinical practice. Even when TSH and free thyroxine (FT4) levels return to normal, approximately 10% of patients continue to experience poor health, which in turn impacts their quality of life (QoL) (6). To address this issue, various adjunctive treatment strategies have been proposed and applied in clinical practice, with the core objective of further optimizing symptom control and QoL. These include monotherapy with liothyronine (LT3) or combination therapy with LT4, specific exercise interventions, and nutritional supplementation (such as selenium (Se) and inositol). Previous studies have demonstrated that combined exercise interventions markedly improve QoL in hypothyroidism patients, providing evidence-based support for comprehensive treatment (7, 8); however, the efficacy of LT3 + LT4 therapy remains controversial (9, 10).

Previous meta-analyses have explored various interventions for hypothyroidism treatment, including long-term exercise (11), T3-containing treatment regimens (12), combined supplementation of Se and inositol (13), herbal therapies (14), and yoga therapy (15). While these studies provide evidence for diverse treatment options in hypothyroidism, they have focused on single or limited categories of interventions, failing to achieve systematic integration and comparisons of multiple strategies.

Given the urgent need for better options among hypothyroidism patients and the limitations of existing monotherapies, it is imperative to systematically evaluate and compare the relative efficacy and safety of all major therapeutic strategies, including LT4, LT3, exercise, nutritional supplements, and their combinations. Traditional pairwise meta-analyses cannot simultaneously integrate and compare multiple interventions. Therefore, this study proposes to employ network meta-analysis (NMA), an advanced statistical method, to synthesize existing direct and indirect comparative evidence. This approach can quantitatively rank the interventions based on key outcomes, including improvements in thyroid function indicators (e.g. TSH, FT3, and FT4), safety profiles (e.g. adverse effects), and QoL. The core objective is to identify the optimal strategy based on current evidence, providing evidence-based guidance for individualized clinical decision-making and future research directions.

Methods

This study adhered to the PRISMA guidelines and their requirements for NMA. The study protocol was registered in the PROSPERO (CRD420251085765).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria encompassed the following: i) subjects: patients with confirmed diagnosis of hypothyroidism, including clinical hypothyroidism (elevated serum TSH and decreased FT4) and subclinical hypothyroidism (elevated serum TSH and normal FT4); ii) interventions: thyroid hormone replacement therapy (e.g. LT4 monotherapy and LT4 + LT3 therapy), nutritional supplementation (e.g. single or combined supplements of Se and vitamin D), non-pharmacological interventions such as exercise and yoga, and conventional drug therapy; the control measures were placebo or standard treatment; iii) study type: randomized controlled trials (RCTs); and iv) outcome measures: serum TSH, FT3, FT4, total triiodothyronine (TT3), total thyroxine (TT4), adverse events during treatment, QoL score, physical function score (PFS), and mental health score (MHS).

Studies with the following characteristics were excluded: i) animal or cell experiments, case reports, scientific experiment plans, reviews, letters, editorials, and conference papers; ii) studies with missing or critically flawed research data; iii) duplicate publications; iv) articles for which full text could not be obtained; v) studies with overlapping participants; and vi) studies including populations without hypothyroidism.

Data extraction

The retrieved literature was imported into EndNote. Two researchers (XGL and WTQ) independently screened the titles and abstracts against the inclusion and exclusion criteria, followed by a second screening after reading the full texts. Disagreements were tackled through discussion or by consulting a third researcher (LKD) for guidance before final assessment. Data extraction was conducted independently using a pre-established spreadsheet, including the first author, publication year, country, randomized and blinded design, intervention and control measures, treatment duration, outcome measures, and basic information of study subjects.

Quality appraisal

Two authors (XGL and WTQ) independently appraised the quality of eligible RCTs using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (ROB 2.0) in five domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding, missing data, and selective reporting. Each domain was rated as ‘high risk’, ‘moderate risk’, or ‘low risk’. When conducting an overall quality assessment of an article, if all five domains were rated as low risk or only one item was rated as moderate risk with the rest being low risk, the RCT was classified as low risk. If four or more domains were moderate risk or any single domain was high risk, the RCT was classified as high risk. In other cases, the RCT was classified as moderate risk. Two authors independently appraised literature quality, and any discrepancies were tackled by a third author (JRL).

Statistical analysis

All outcome measures were continuous variables and dichotomous variables. The risk ratio (RR) or standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95% credible intervals (CrIs) were used as effect size measures. This study employed a Bayesian NMA model constructed using Markov chain Monte Carlo methods. The model underwent iterative refinement to estimate the relative efficacy of different treatment regimens. During the validation process, the model chain length was set to 4, the annealing value to 10,000, the iteration count to 50,000, the step size for each check to 10, and the initial value to 2.5. This procedure aimed to obtain the posterior distribution. The NMA was executed based on three fundamental assumptions: transmissibility, homogeneity, and consistency. Heterogeneity analysis was performed via the mtc:anohe function in the GeMTC package. I2 < 50% implied acceptable heterogeneity, satisfying the homogeneity assumption. The mtc.nodesplit function in the GeMTC package was utilized to examine inconsistency between direct and indirect comparisons via node splitting. P > 0.05 indicated no inconsistency, satisfying the consistency assumption. Convergence of results was assessed via the potential scale reduction factor (PSRF), with 1 serving as the benchmark. A PSRF value between 1 and 1.05 was considered successful convergence. Network diagrams were drawn, with head-to-head comparisons between interventions forming the lines and each intervention serving as a node. Cumulative probability ranking plots were analyzed to estimate all cumulative ranking probabilities, which were reported as the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA). Funnel plots were utilized to determine potential publication bias. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.1) and STATA (version 15.1) software. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity and their impact on treatment effect estimates, we performed meta-regression analyses on the primary outcome measures. Three pre-specified covariates were included: type of hypothyroidism, intervention duration, and study sample size.

The CINeMA (Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis) tool (available at https://cinema.ispm.ch) was adopted to assess the quality of evidence for all pairwise comparisons. It considered six key domains: intra-study bias (risk of bias), inter-study bias (publication or reporting bias), indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and inconsistency. Based on the degree of bias present, each domain was categorized into no concern (no downgrade), concern (one-level downgrade), or major concern (two-level downgrade). Ultimately, each pairwise comparison of evidence was rated as ‘high’, ‘medium’, ‘low’, or ‘very low’.

Search strategy

PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases were searched until July 1, 2025, with the language restricted to English. The search employed a combination of subject headings and free-text terms. Medical Subject Headings terms included hypothyroidism and randomized controlled trial. In addition, references and gray literature in the relevant studies were manually searched to identify eligible studies. The detailed search strategy is provided in Appendix 1.1 (see section on Supplementary materials given at the end of the article).

Results

Literature search and screening

15,409 documents were retrieved. After removing duplicates, 5,451 articles remained. Following preliminary review of titles and abstracts, 9,779 articles were excluded. Full-text articles were then rigorously assessed against inclusion and exclusion criteria, resulting in the final inclusion of 35 RCTs. The specific screening process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

NMA results

Network diagram

In the network diagram, each dot represented an intervention, with dot size positively correlated with the number of studies involving that intervention, larger dots indicating more included studies. Lines connecting two dots signified direct comparative studies between those interventions, with line thickness reflecting the number of studies between the two treatment options, thicker lines indicating more comparative studies (Fig. 2).

Network diagrams. (A) TSH, (B) FT4, (C) FT3, (D) TT3, (E) TT4, (F) adverse events, (G) QoL, (H) PFS, and (I) MHS.

Deviance information criterion values were consistently below 5, indicating overall model consistency across outcomes (Appendix 2.1). Node splitting analysis was applied to each closed-loop outcome. Results showed P-values exceeding 0.05 for TSH, confirming no inconsistencies between direct and indirect comparisons (Appendix 2.2). Convergence diagnostics and density plots (Appendix 2.3) showed that PSRF values were uniformly equal to 1, confirming successful convergence of all outcome models (Appendix 2.4). CINeMA results are presented in Appendix 3.

TSH

Thirty-four RCTs reported TSH levels. The yoga intervention failed to form an effective network of connected evidence across all included RCTs and thus was excluded from subsequent effect size pooling and SUCRA ranking analysis. Compared with placebo, LT4 markedly reduced serum TSH (SMD = −0.98, 95% CrI: −1.67, −0.3, CINeMA: low), LT4 + LT3 significantly decreased serum TSH (SMD = −1.14, 95% CrI: −1.67, −0.3, CINeMA: low), LT4 + SRT3 greatly reduced serum TSH (SMD = −1.66, 95% CrI: −3.26, −0.07, CINeMA: low certainty), LT4 + aerobic training (AT) greatly reduced serum TSH (SMD = −2.29, 95% CrI: −3.69, −0.9, CINeMA: high certainty), LT4 + resistance training (RT) notably reduced serum TSH (SMD = −2.86, 95% CrI: −4.25, −1.45, CINeMA: high certainty), LT4 + AT + RT significantly reduced serum TSH (SMD = −3.97, 95% CrI: −5.76, −2.18, CINeMA: high certainty), LT4 + Se markedly reduced serum TSH (SMD = −1.14, 95% CrI: −2.17, −0.1, CINeMA: low), VD significantly reduced serum TSH (SMD = −1.44, 95% CrI: −2.86, −0.03, CINeMA: low), and LT4 + synbiotic notably reduced serum TSH (SMD = −1.19, 95% CrI: −2.38, −0.01, CINeMA: low) (Appendix 1.3).

SUCRA probability ranking indicated LT4 + AT + RT (98.54%) > LT4 + RT (92.25%) > LT4 + AT (83.66%), suggesting LT4 + AT + RT offers the greatest advantage in reducing TSH levels (Fig. 3A).

SUCRA ranking results. (A) TSH, (B) FT4, (C) FT3, (D) TT3, (E) TT4, (F) adverse events, (G) QoL, (H) PFS, and (I) MHS.

A subgroup analysis of studies involving participants with subclinical hypothyroidism was conducted, incorporating 13 RCTs. While LT4 + baduanjin (BDJ) showed the greatest trend toward TSH reduction, the difference was not notable (SMD = −1.5, 95% CrI: −4.78, 1.76, CINeMA: very low certainty). SUCRA ranking showed LT4 + BDJ (74.01%) > vitamin D (73.51%) > LT4 (67.06%) (Appendix 2.5). Due to the absence of significant correlation, the SUCRA ranking results should be interpreted with caution.

FT4

Twenty-two RCTs reported FT4 levels. Compared with placebo, LT4 greatly increased FT4 (SMD = 1.42, 95% CrI: 1.03, 1.81, CINeMA: moderate certainty), LT4 + herbal medicine markedly increased FT4 (SMD = 1.41, 95% CrI: 0.92, 1.91, CINeMA: moderate certainty), LT4 + Se significantly increased FT4 (SMD = 1.15, 95% CrI: 0.72, 1.59, CINeMA: low), LT4 + synbiotic notably increased FT4 (SMD = 1.34, 95% CrI: 0.67, 2.01, CINeMA: low), LT4 + zinc (Zn) significantly raised FT4 (SMD = 1.39, 95% CrI: 0.55, 2.24, CINeMA: low), LT4 + Zn + magnesium (Mg) + vitamin A (VA) significantly raised FT4 (SMD = 1.95, 95% CrI: 1.37, 2.53, CINeMA: high certainty), LT4 + Zn + Se greatly increased FT4 (SMD = 1.83, 95% CrI: 1.03, 2.64, CINeMA: high certainty), and Se + myo-inositol markedly increased FT4 (SMD = 0.53, 95% CrI: 0.11, 0.94, CINeMA: low certainty) (Appendix 1.4).

SUCRA probability ranking showed LT4 + Zn + Mg + VA (95.89%) > LT4 + Zn + Se (90.96%) > LT4 (76.73%), indicating that this micronutrient combination regimen is most effective in elevating FT4 (Fig. 3B).

A subgroup analysis of studies involving participants with subclinical hypothyroidism included ten RCTs. Compared with placebo, LT4 significantly raised FT4 (SMD = 1.42; 95% CrI: 1.03, 1.81, CINeMA: very low certainty), LT4 + herbal medicine markedly increased FT4 (SMD = 1.60; 95% CrI: 1.05, 2.16, CINeMA: very low certainty), and Se + myo-inositol greatly increased FT4 (SMD = 0.53; 95% CrI: 0.11, 0.94, CINeMA: very low certainty). SUCRA ranking indicated LT4 + herbal medicine (97.31%) > LT4 (88.10%) > Se + myo-inositol (57.46%), further confirming the superior efficacy of this combination in elevating FT4 (Appendix 2.5).

FT3

Eleven RCTs reported FT3 levels. LT4 + Zn considerably increased FT3 compared with placebo (SMD = 1.46, 95% CrI: 0.40, 2.51, CINeMA: low certainty) (Appendix 1.5).

SUCRA probability ranking indicated LT4 + Zn (97.75%) > LT4 + Zn + Se (79.72%) > LT4 + synbiotic (56.16%), suggesting LT4 + Zn holds the greatest potential for enhancing FT3 (Fig. 3C).

TT3

Twelve RCTs reported TT3 levels. Compared with placebo, herbal medicine alone showed the greatest trend toward increasing TT3, although the difference was not notable (SMD = 1.19, 95% CrI: −1.21, 3.6, CINeMA: very low certainty) (Appendix 1.6).

SUCRA ranking revealed herbal medicine (79.12%) > LT4 + LT3 (69.23%) > LT4 + Zn (63.08%), indicating that herbal medicine holds the greatest potential for elevating TT3 (Fig. 3D). Due to the absence of significant correlations, the SUCRA ranking should be interpreted with caution.

TT4

Eight RCTs reported TT4 levels. Compared with LT4 alone, LT4 + LT3 markedly increased TT4 (SMD = 1.56, 95% CrI: −2.14, −0.98), LT4 + AT significantly raised TT4 (SMD = 1.66, 95% CrI: 0.95, 2.51), LT4 + RT greatly increased TT4 (SMD = 1.23, 95% CrI: 0.44, 2.03), LT4 + AT + RT markedly increased TT4 (SMD = 1.62, 95% CrI: 0.44, 2.03), LT4 + Se increased TT4 (SMD = −4.57, 95% CrI: −6, −3.13), and LT4 + Zn + Se increased TT4 (SMD = 1.35, 95% CrI: 0.57, 2.14) (Appendix 1.7).

SUCRA probability ranking revealed LT4 + AT (88.78%) > LT4 + AT + RT (87.75%) > LT4 + Zn + Se (80.06%), indicating that LT4 + AT achieved the greatest increase in TT4 levels (Fig. 3E).

Adverse events

Twelve RCTs reported adverse events. LT3 monotherapy substantially increased adverse events compared with placebo (RR = 15.54, 95% CrI: 2.68, 501.14, CINeMA: very low certainty) (Table 2).

QoL score

Five RCTs reported QoL score. Compared with LT4 alone, LT4 + AT significantly improved QoL score (SMD = 1.23, 95% CrI: 0.44, 2.02), LT4 + RT significantly improved QoL score (SMD = 1.46, 95% CrI: 0.65, 2.28), and LT4 + AT + RT significantly improved QoL score (SMD = 1.62, 95% CrI: 0.78, 2.46) (Appendix 1.8).

SUCRA probability ranking showed LT4 + AT + RT (89.07%) > LT4 + RT (83.86%) > LT4 + AT (75.92%), indicating that LT4 + AT + RT had the greatest increase in QoL score (Fig. 3G).

PFS

Five RCTs reported PFS. Compared with LT4 alone, LT4 + AT significantly improved PFS (SMD = 1.17, 95% CrI: 0.39, 1.96), LT4 + RT significantly improved PFS (SMD = 1.59, 95% CrI: 0.76, 2.43), LT4 + AT + RT significantly improved PFS (SMD = 1.38, 95% CrI: 0.57, 2.19), and LT4 + synbiotic significantly improved PFS (SMD = 0.61, 95% CrI: 0.05, 1.18) (Appendix 1.9).

SUCRA probability ranking showed LT4 + RT (89.59%) > LT4 + AT + RT (82.27%) > LT4 + AT (74.33%), indicating that LT4 + RT achieved the greatest increase in PFS (Fig. 3H).

MHS

Eight RCTs reported MHS. Compared with LT4 alone, LT4 + AT significantly improved MHS (SMD = 1.26, 95% CrI: 0.47, 2.05, CINeMA: very low certainty), LT4 + RT significantly improved MHS (SMD = 1.35, 95% CrI: 0.54, 2.15, CINeMA: very low certainty), and LT4 + AT + RT significantly improved MHS (SMD = 2.1, 95% CrI: 1.19, 3.01, CINeMA: low certainty) (Appendix 1.10).

SUCRA probability ranking showed LT4 + AT + RT (97.11%) > LT4 + RT (80.82%) > LT4 + AT (78.68%), indicating that LT4 + AT + RT achieved the greatest increase in MHS (Fig. 3I).

Meta-regression analysis was performed on the main outcome indicators to analyze the modification effects of the types of hypothyroidism, treatment duration, and study sample size as continuous variables. Discussion

This Bayesian NMA integrated 35 RCTs to quantify the synergistic optimization effects of combining LT4 with exercise and trace elements on key therapeutic targets for hypothyroidism. Findings revealed the following: i) regarding the core indicator TSH control, LT4 + AT + RT significantly reduced TSH levels; ii) for abnormal thyroid hormone profiles, LT4 + Zn + Mg + VA greatly elevated FT4, adding Zn increased FT3, and adding AT further elevated TT4, and iii) LT3 monotherapy substantially increased adverse event risk, although this estimate carried significant uncertainty due to overly broad CIs.

In the mechanism by which LT4 and exercise jointly regulate thyroid function, leptin may be a key mediator. Exercise directly downregulates leptin secretion from adipocytes by reducing body fat mass, consistent with leptin’s positive correlation with body fat content (47, 48). Decreased leptin levels exert dual inhibition on the thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH)–TSH axis. It attenuates direct stimulation of TRH neurons in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (via the STAT3 phosphorylation pathway). In addition, it reduces the activation of POMC neurons in the arcuate nucleus and the indirect regulation of TRH mediated by α-MSH. Ultimately, these effects collectively inhibit the secretory activity of pituitary TSH (49, 50). Concurrently, the local regulation of 5′-deiodinase type 2 (D2) constitutes another crucial pathway. D2 is widely expressed in the hypothalamus, white adipose tissue, brown adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle, serving as a key molecule mediating adaptive thermogenesis (51). Patients with hypothyroidism exhibit compensatory elevations in plasma norepinephrine levels. Exercise further drives this pathway by activating the sympathetic nervous system: sympathetic excitation during exercise increases the release of catecholamines such as norepinephrine. These catecholamines stimulate specific deubiquitinating enzyme activity, thereby upregulating D2 function. This process accelerates T4 synthesis and release while promoting its conversion to the active form T3 (51, 52, 53). This process may elevate circulating T4 levels while simultaneously reducing TSH through the negative feedback effect of locally available T3. Clinical research findings provide partial support: AT + RT can elevate T4 levels while reducing TSH levels (8, 11). However, research conclusions remain significantly contested. Individuals with high daily physical activity levels exhibit lower T4 levels; conversely, resting T4 levels decrease during 24 weeks of strength training, contradicting the aforementioned findings (54, 55). These discrepancies may relate to exercise type, intervention duration, and baseline population characteristics. Although our NMA found that combined AT increased T4 levels, the direction of exercise regulation on T4 remains influenced by multiple factors, and its specific mechanisms require further research and validation. Regarding the regulation of thyroid function through LT4 + trace element supplementation, maintaining balanced intake of various minerals is a critical component in the prevention and treatment of thyroid disorders (56). Zn plays multiple critical roles in thyroid function. It is an essential component of thyroid peroxidase (TPO), which serves as the core enzyme for thyroid hormone synthesis. TPO catalyzes the iodination of thyroglobulin and mediates the coupling of iodotyrosine residues to generate T4 and T3 (57). In addition, Zn influences hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis signaling by participating in the conversion of TRH precursor and the transcription of thyroid transcription factors. Simultaneously, as a cofactor for type I and type II deiodinases, it accelerates the conversion of T4 to active T3 (58). Adequate dietary Zn intake can elevate levels of TSH, TT4, and FT4 (59). A review by Antonis Zavros et al. further confirmed that Zn supplementation specifically enhanced FT3 levels in overweight or obese individuals, corroborating its regulatory role in active hormone metabolism (60). Mg, in its coenzyme form, participates in both the synthesis and secretion of thyroid hormones, potentially providing energy support for hormone transport and metabolism by improving cellular energy metabolism. VA and its metabolites (particularly retinoic acid) exert effects by regulating key components of the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis (61). The entry of iodides into thyroid cells via sodium-iodide symporter, their transport into the lumen via pendrin for binding to thyroglobulin, and the TPO-catalyzed oxidation and organification of iodides are all regulated by retinoic acid (62). This explains why VA deficiency reduces iodine uptake, thyroglobulin synthesis, and hormone production. Importantly, in the treatment of primary hypothyroidism, the core mechanism of LT4 replacement therapy is to restore circulating thyroid hormone levels through exogenous supplementation of T4, thereby reducing the secretion of pituitary TSH through a negative feedback inhibition mechanism and rebuilding the homeostasis of the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis. The potential value of the combined approach explored in this study should lie in optimizing this process. For example, regular exercise may affect the turnover rate of thyroid hormone by improving the overall metabolic state, and trace elements, such as zinc and selenium, as cofactors of deiodinase, may theoretically support the effective conversion of T4 to T3 in peripheral tissues, thereby more accurately meeting the tissue’s hormone needs and promoting the physiological downregulation of TSH. Therefore, the reduction in TSH during treatment must be the result of sufficiently improved thyroid hormone rather than non-physiological isolated suppression of pituitary TSH secretion through other pathways. If it breaks away from the foundation of hormone supplementation and simply pursues the decrease in TSH value, it may destroy the compensatory mechanism and cause hormone deficiency, thus bringing about clinical risks. The literature included in this NMA generally lacks unified reporting on the TSH standard-reaching rate, which limits us from a more detailed evaluation of the combination program from the perspective of treatment adequacy. Future studies should standardize the reporting of this metric to more fully evaluate whether the combined intervention truly optimizes it.

Some people with hypothyroidism who receive LT4 report a persistent and distressing set of symptoms known as brain fog. Brain fog is a variable symptom complex in patients with hypothyroidism that causes significant distress and reduced QoL (63). Therefore, combining exercise and nutritional supplements may be an optimization strategy. For patients with hypothyroidism, LT4 basic treatment combined with regular exercise is effective in relieving their residual symptoms. Clinical research shows that structured exercise programs can significantly reduce patients’ fatigue and anxiety and comprehensively improve their QoL (64). RT can specifically enhance muscle function and neuromuscular coordination, while regular AT or endurance training can help improve overall health, emotional state, and psychological and physiological scores on health-related QoL scales (65, 66). Regular exercise is a key physiological intervention to induce mitochondrial biosynthesis and improve cellular energy metabolism. This may be one of the core mechanisms to alleviate hypothyroidism fatigue (67). AT can effectively upregulate neurotrophic factors and regulate neurotransmitter homeostasis, which may provide a reasonable physiological explanation for exercise improving patients’ emotional state and cognitive function (68). Different from exercise, the supplementation of specific trace elements may create conditions for symptom relief by optimizing the internal environment for hormone production. The potential alleviation effect of specific nutritional supplements on hypothyroidism symptoms is mainly due to their specific roles (such as selenium, zinc, and iron) in the thyroid hormone metabolic pathway or their participation in the overall metabolic and immune status. Its value may be mainly reflected in correcting nutrient deficiencies and creating a more ideal physiological environment for levothyroxine treatment, which may indirectly alleviate symptoms. In the clinical management of hypothyroidism, combining AT and RT serves as an important adjunct to non-pharmacological treatment, offering additional options for clinical decision-making. For patients whose TSH levels remain uncontrolled, integrating exercise with standardized medication may serve as a beneficial supplement to optimize therapeutic outcomes. This combined approach aids in regulating TSH levels and helps alleviate clinical symptoms, such as fatigue and lethargy, to a certain extent, thereby indirectly enhancing patients’ daily functional capacity and QoL. Compared to relying solely on adjusting LT4 dosage, combined exercise interventions offer a new approach for patients who are sensitive to medication doses or have limitations on dosage adjustments. Although yoga was not included in the NMA, as an exercise intervention, yoga has been demonstrated to considerably improve QoL while enhancing thyroid function (7, 20). In addition, subgroup analysis of subclinical hypothyroidism revealed that while LT4 + BDJ showed no statistical difference in reducing TSH levels, BDJ demonstrated clear benefits in improving patients’ QoL (16). This NMA provides evidence-based support for trace element supplementation, recommending individualized nutritional supplement strategies tailored to different patient subgroups to maximize potential improvements in clinical indicators and symptom control.

Treatment duration exerts a considerable influence on the outcomes of each intervention. LT4 + AT + RT significantly reduced TSH levels, potentially due to the rapid manifestation of synergistic effects on hormone regulation during the medium-to-short intervention period, accelerating TSH decline. However, shortening the treatment duration may weaken the combined effect as exercise rhythms have not yet been established. The efficacy of LT4 + trace elements may depend on a specific treatment duration. Short-term supplementation can rapidly correct hormone synthesis deficiencies caused by elemental deficiencies. However, long-term supplementation requires vigilance against the risk of excess, particularly as accumulated Zn and Mg may interfere with the absorption of other minerals, thereby disrupting thyroid function homeostasis. Future research should employ time-stratified designs to validate the reliability of findings. Meta-regression results showed that in TSH outcomes, the NMA stability of LT4 and LT4 + synbiotic was affected by sample size, types of hypothyroidism, and treatment duration. This may be due to the fact that synbiotic does not act directly on the thyroid axis, but through regulating the complex intermediary pathway of intestinal flora, potentially affecting nutrient absorption, systemic inflammatory status, or the enterohepatic circulation of LT4. This complex mechanism means that its efficacy may vary significantly across the individual’s basic flora, intervention duration, and study size.

This study employed a NMA within a Bayesian framework to enable direct and indirect comparisons of multiple interventions, thereby enhancing the transparency and comprehensiveness of the results. With an adequate sample size, it integrated evidence from multidimensional interventions, including medication, exercise, and nutritional supplementation. The analysis considered multiple outcomes, such as TSH levels, thyroid hormone levels, and adverse events while demonstrating robustness against publication bias. These findings provide robust support for clinical decision-making. However, the study still has certain limitations. As a NMA, some results rely on indirect comparisons, which are less robust than direct head-to-head comparisons. Heterogeneity among included studies in patient characteristics and intervention details (such as exercise protocols and nutritional supplement dosages) may affect the reliability and generalizability of the findings. The evidence of this study is primarily derived from patients with primary hypothyroidism without thyroid deficiency. Hence, the conclusions may not be directly applicable to patients with complete thyroid deficiency. Furthermore, since the original studies do not provide data stratified by the degree of thyroid function impairment, it is impossible to assess whether the efficacy varies with the severity of the condition. Data supporting subjective indicators, such as patient QoL, remain relatively weak, making it difficult to fully reflect the clinical value of the intervention. SMD cannot directly reflect the original magnitude of change in the outcome indicator, which may make it difficult to intuitively understand the actual degree of change in the indicator. In addition, the follow-up periods in the included RCTs were relatively short, preventing adequate assessment of the long-term efficacy, patient compliance, and potential long-term risks associated with the combined strategy.

Conclusion

The findings provide evidence-based guidance for individualized interventions in hypothyroidism. Regarding the comprehensive management of hypothyroidism, LT4 + AT + RT has the most significant effect on reducing TSH levels, enhancing QoL, and improving MHS, making it a clinically preferred combination regimen. For patients with reduced FT4 levels, LT4 + Zn + Mg + VA significantly improves FT4. For those with decreased FT3 levels, LT4 + Zn is an effective option for improving FT3 levels. Combined RT may be a preferred option in improving PFS. Exercise interventions not only optimize hormone levels but may also offer potential benefits in improving QoL. Trace element supplementation provides critical support for hormone synthesis and metabolism. The synergistic effects of these approaches with LT4 provide evidence-based support for multidimensional management of hypothyroidism. However, due to heterogeneity, the relatively weak evidence base for some interventions, and limitations in follow-up duration, the generalizability of these conclusions must be considered in the context of clinical practice. Future research should focus on accumulating long-term follow-up data, refining exercise protocols, and establishing individualized standards for trace element supplementation dosages. In addition, more head-to-head comparative studies should be conducted to further enhance the quality of evidence and provide a more robust foundation for the precision treatment of hypothyroidism.
