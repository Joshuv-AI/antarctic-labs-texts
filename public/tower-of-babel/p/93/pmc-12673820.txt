Atrial Fibrillation Screening Driven by Monitoring Technologies
Authors: Hao Wang, Yutao Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Advanced age is a well-established independent risk factor for both atrial fibrillation (AF) and AF-related stroke. The prevalence of AF increases significantly with age, particularly beyond 65 years, and age contributes substantially to stroke risk scores. Early detection through screening and timely oral anticoagulation (OAC) therapy are critical for reducing stroke risk.

Current European Society of Cardiology guidelines recommend opportunistic or systematic electrocardiographic (ECG) screening for AF in individuals aged ≥65 years.1 However, significant controversy exists regarding the value of population screening, as well as age thresholds for stroke risk and screening benefits.2,3 Nevertheless, the available epidemiologic data indicate that stroke risk escalates in Asian patients with AF who are aged >55 years, and OAC may benefit such patients.4 Nonetheless, the net benefit of systematic screening and anticoagulation initiation specifically in the “younger” Asian group (eg, aged 55-64 years) remains uncertain.

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Sun et al5 investigate AF screening and stroke risk in a Chinese cohort aged 55 to 64 years. This prospective cohort study evaluated opportunistic AF screening in 3,926 Chinese outpatients aged 55-64 years by using a handheld single-lead ECG device. Among 3,588 participants without previous AF, initial screening yielded new AF cases in 0.8% (n = 28) of patients, with the overall yield reaching 1.0% (n = 35) after repeat screens. The screen-detected AF cohort had a mean CHA2DS2-VASc score of 1.9, with 74% having ≥1 nonage, nonsex stroke risk factor. Over a median follow-up of 5.0 years, AF patients without OAC therapy had the highest risk of ischemic stroke (adjusted subdistribution HR: 3.4; 95% CI: 1.3-8.5 vs no AF). Conversely, OAC therapy significantly reduced stroke risk in AF patients (adjusted subdistribution HR: 1.6; 95% CI: 0.9-3.1), thus achieving a low risk level similar to that in patients without AF. This finding suggests that despite the low screening yield in this younger Asian cohort, identified AF patients have a substantially elevated stroke risk that is effectively mitigated by OAC.

Optimal Age for Screening and Screening Strategies

The age-stratified approach to AF screening and its clinical benefits remains nuanced. Current international guidelines advocate opportunistic screening for individuals aged ≥65 years and systematic screening for those ≥75 years or with high stroke risk (eg, CHA2DS2-VASc ≥2). This stratification is grounded in epidemiologic data showing that AF prevalence escalates with age, exceeding 7.5% in octogenarians.

Systematic screening (eg, population-wide ECG monitoring) outperforms opportunistic approaches (eg, pulse palpation during routine visits) in detection rates. For example, systematic methods yield 3% to 5.3% new AF diagnoses in targeted older adult cohorts aged >65 years,6, 7, 8, 9 vs 1.6% to 1.7% with opportunistic methods.10,11 For example, the STROKESTOP (Systematic ECG Screening for Atrial Fibrillation Among 75 Year Old Subjects in the Region of Stockholm and Halland, Sweden) trial demonstrated a 3.0% detection rate in participants aged 75 to 76 years by intermittent ECG monitoring, compared with a 1.6% to 1.7% AF detection rate in opportunistic trials such as D2AF (Detecting and Diagnosing Atrial Fibrillation) and a pragmatic trial integrating routine screening for AF at primary care visits (VITAL-AF [Screening for Atrial Fibrillation in an Ambulatory Clinic Population]).10,11

In contrast, Sun et al5 reported that their new AF yield was 0.8% in patients aged 55 to 64 years with opportunistic handheld ECG screening. Indeed, in lower-risk groups, intermittent ECG screening yields <1% detection (eg, 0.76% in VITAL-AF for participants aged 65 to 69 years).

Clearly, screening approach is clearly associated with AF detection. Quarterly single-lead ECG screenings increased AF detection by 71% compared with annual screenings (6.7 vs 4.1 in 1,000 person-years; HR: 1.71; P = 0.029).12 Single-lead ECG screenings typically rely on symptom-triggered or scheduled recordings, possibly missing asymptomatic episodes. Longer monitoring significantly increased AF detection.

In the mAFA-II (Mobile Health Technology for Improved Screening, Patient Involvement and Optimizing Integrated Care in Atrial Fibrillation II) Trial Long-Term Extension Cohort study involving in 2.85 million Chinese participants, the AF detection rate in individuals aged 55 to 64 years was 1.79% when using a photoplethysmographic smartwatch.13 Continuous photoplethysmographic monitoring in patients aged >75 years achieved 7.26% detection over 3 years.13 In individuals aged 55 to 64 years, whose AF burden may be low but stroke risk escalates, continuous consumer-led screening, possibly as a scalable strategy to identify subclinical AF, may be considered.

AF Screening and Clinical Benefits

Screening identifies asymptomatic AF, thereby enabling timely OAC initiation. Sun et al5 found that 74% of screen-detected AF patients had ≥1 nonage or nonsex stroke risk factors and an annual stroke risk of 1.22%. This finding aligns with robust data from Taiwan and Singapore showing that stroke risk escalates sharply after age 55 years in Asians (0.53%/year to 1.78%/year),4 and it reaches levels comparable to those in Western populations aged ≥65 years.

Thus, AF screening in Asians aged 55 to 64 years identifies a clinically significant cohort with an elevated stroke risk despite modest CHA2DS2-VASc/CHA2DS2-VA scores.14 The steep rise in stroke risk after age 55 years in Asian AF patients with the marked efficacy of OACs underscores the high clinical value of early AF detection in this demographic group. Although absolute numbers of screen-detected AF may be modest in “younger” cohorts, the relative risk reduction achievable with OACs justifies targeted screening from age 50 years onward, particularly in individuals with even minimal stroke risk factors.

Evolving Screening Technologies and Stroke Risk Stratification

The technologic evolution in AF detection has revolutionized the understanding of AF progression and stroke risk. AF monitoring technologies, wearable and implantable devices, have shifted management from episodic diagnosis to continuous burden quantification, thereby enabling stroke prevention tailored to dynamic risk.

Even device-detected AF is associated with an increased risk of stroke and systemic embolism. Indeed, low-burden device-detected AF (median 0.13%) carried a stroke risk (∼1%/year without anticoagulation).15 For example, short episodes (>5-6 minutes) conferred a 2.5-fold increased stroke risk.16 Stroke risk escalates with increasing AF burden, but actionable thresholds are context dependent.

In the future, artificial intelligence–enhanced analytics and technology integration will simulate the patient-specific impact of AF burden on stroke risk, thus guiding therapy escalation or deescalation.17,18 AF management is more than OAC alone, with guidelines advocating a holistic or integrated care management approach that has been associated with improved clinical outcomes in trials and real-world data.19, 20, 21

In conclusion, Sun et al5 provide pivotal evidence demonstrating that AF screening in Chinese adults aged 55 to 64 years identifies a high-risk group that derives significant benefit from anticoagulation, thus challenging conventional age-centric screening paradigms and advocating for ethnicity-specific protocols. Larger studies of Asian cohort analyses are needed, especially given the reported racial differences in clinical epidemiology and AF-related outcomes.22, 23, 24 Despite methodologic limitations, this study also validates handheld ECG use as a scalable tool for opportunistic detection and highlights the critical need to address OAC underuse across Asian populations. These findings underscore the imperative for future research to refine precision risk stratification, optimize real-world implementation strategies, and develop ethnically tailored anticoagulation approaches—essential steps toward achieving equitable, evidence-based AF management and stroke prevention. Future research can compare yields and outcomes by using more sensitive monitoring strategies.
