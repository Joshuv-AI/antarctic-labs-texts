Surgical management of type IV thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm in a 4-year-old child: A case report and review of the literature
Authors: Rocco Pasqua, Jean-Michel Davaine, Louise Koskas, Priscilla Nardi, Giulio Illuminati, Fabien Koskas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms in children are rare, associated with syndromic, congenital, or acquired disorders, and can be life threatening. Although early intervention is advocated, optimal management remains undefined, and vessel size and aortic growth pose technical challenges. We report the case of a 4-year-old boy with type IV thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm, celiac trunk occlusion, and stenoses of the superior mesenteric and renal arteries from inflammatory disease causing renovascular hypertension. He underwent thoracoabdominal bypass using a C-shaped Dacron graft, indirect reimplantation of the superior mesenteric artery and renal arteries with cryopreserved allografts, and orthotopic autotransplantation of the left kidney. The outcome was favorable, supporting open repair in selected pediatric patients.

Aortic aneurysms are exceptionally rare in pediatric patients and occur at a markedly lower incidence compared with adults.1 When present, they can be congenital2,3 or, more frequently, associated with genetic connective tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome or Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, which compromise the structural integrity of the aortic wall and predispose to progressive dilation.4 Inflammatory vasculopathies, including Takayasu's arteritis and Kawasaki disease, can also lead to aneurysm formation by inducing chronic damage and remodeling of the arterial wall.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Pediatric aortic aneurysms are often silent and discovered incidentally; when symptomatic, they are usually related to compression of adjacent structures, or compromise of involved aortic branches. Rupture remains the most feared catastrophic complication.10 Because of the rarity of this condition, the literature is predominantly composed of case reports and small series and standardized management guidelines are lacking.11, 12, 13, 14 Treatment is further challenged by the small caliber of pediatric vessels and the need to accommodate future somatic growth, influencing timing of intervention, operative technique, and long-term surveillance.

Case report

A 4-year-old boy presented with abdominal pain. At clinical examination, no signs of connective tissue diseases such as arachnodactyly or dolichostenomelia were present, but an abdominal pulsatile mass was revealed in association with high pressure levels (pressure persistently >150/90 mm Hg). No family history of connective tissue disease or aneurysmal disease was present. Baseline laboratory evaluation did not suggest systemic inflammation (including complete blood count and inflammatory markers), and a targeted vasculitis workup for Kawasaki disease and Takayasu arteritis was negative based on clinical assessment and imaging findings. Given the absence of fever or other signs of systemic infection, no dedicated preoperative infectious etiology screening was pursued. Genetic examinations searching for mutations in the genes encoding for fibrillin and collagen proteins revealed no abnormalities. There was no history of umbilical vein catheterization, which can disrupt endothelial integrity and trigger pathological remodeling. A computed tomography angiography showed an aortic aneurysm extending from the level of the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) down to the origin of the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA), with a maximum diameter of 18 mm; the nondiseased segment was 8 mm. A near-occlusive stenosis at the origin of both renal arteries and the SMA was present. The origin of the celiac trunk was occluded but supplied from the SMA by a large pancreaticoduodenal artery (Fig 1, A). We considered midaortic syndrome in the differential diagnosis. However, midaortic syndrome typically presents with segmental aortic narrowing, often associated with stenoses of the renal and mesenteric branches, whereas our patient showed a different pattern which is less consistent with the classic presentation of midaortic syndrome. Scintigraphy demonstrated preserved renal function. Under medical treatment, despite quadruple antihypertensive therapy, blood pressure control could not be achieved (nicardipin 60 mg/d, labetolol 280 mg/d, minoxidil 20 mg/d, and prazosin 5 mg/d). However, medical therapy was considered bridging rather than definitive, because the primary indication for intervention was the substantial perceived rupture risk, and the near-occlusive renal artery stenoses raised concern for progression to complete occlusion and irreversible renal ischemic injury. Open surgery was offered to the patient and his family. Surgical approach consisted in a left thoracophrenolaparotomy. The inferior one-third of the descending thoracic aorta and all of the abdominal aorta were exposed (Fig 1, B). The operative field demonstrated sequelae of previous inflammation. To obtain optimal exposure of the visceral aorta, and as part of the planned renal preservation strategy, the left kidney was temporarily explanted and protected with cold Custodiol perfusion at 4 °C.15 A thoracoabdominal bypass was performed between the descending thoracic aorta and the abdominal aorta just above the level of the IMA using a 12-mm Dacron synthetic graft (Gelweave Vascutek, Terumo). The graft was intentionally oversized in diameter and length (12 mm relative to an 8-mm nondiseased aortic segment, corresponding with approximately 50% diameter oversizing), and the proximal anastomosis was performed as a beveled side-to-end anastomosis from the native descending thoracic aorta, thereby increasing the anastomotic circumference and facilitating future growth-related diameter mismatch. For SMA and right renal artery revascularization, we preferred a cryopreserved iliac artery allograft (Banque des tissus, Hôpital Saint-Louis, AP-HP), because the common iliac axis and its bifurcation provided a ready-made Y-shaped conduit and simplified the reconstruction by eliminating one anastomosis. Finally, orthotopic left kidney autotransplantation was performed with the interposition of another external iliac allograft (Fig 1, C). Given the limited thoracic extent of the repair, intraoperative spinal cord protection was limited to strict hemodynamic management, with particular emphasis on avoiding prolonged hypotension. The patient was monitored in the intensive care unit for 24 hours, where preventive measures were directed at optimizing spinal cord perfusion and oxygen delivery, including maintenance of elevated mean arterial pressure and adequate supplemental oxygenation. The postoperative period was uneventful, no neurological deficits or increase in the serum creatinine level was noted and the patient was discharged 10 days after surgery on aspirin 100 mg/d. During follow-up, medical therapy included single antiplatelet therapy and antihypertensive medications, which were progressively reduced over time; no immunosuppressive therapy was prescribed. Histopathological examination showed marked intimal hyperplasia and medial destruction, with no adventitial involvement and no features supporting an infectious etiology or ongoing active inflammation. Overall, the findings did not allow a definitive etiologic diagnosis.

Follow-up included a clinical visit at 1 month after discharge and, thereafter, 6-monthly clinical and duplex ultrasound surveillance of the abdominal aorta. Because duplex ultrasound examination during routine follow-up raised concern for possible anastomotic abnormalities, computed tomography angiography was performed at the 3-year follow-up and showed patency of the main graft, with an ostial stenosis at the level of the cryopreserved allograft used for the revascularization of the SMA, and compensatory hypertrophy of the IMA, which ensured bowel perfusion. The scan also demonstrated longitudinal accommodation of the surgical graft, as was intentionally planned at the time of implantation (Fig 3). After 40 months of follow-up, this young patient has resumed a normal active life and his antihypertensive treatment has been significantly reduced (amlodipine 10 mg/d, enalapril 5 mg/d, labetolol 280 mg/d). In this child, persistent hypertension despite technically successful renal revascularization likely reflects the severity and chronicity of renovascular disease, with residual renal parenchymal/microvascular injury. Discussion

Thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms are infrequent in children and can be attributed to syndromic, congenital, or acquired diseases. The natural history of pediatric aortic aneurysms is not well-known; however, intervention is justified by the risk of rupture. Timing and indications for intervention as well as the optimal surgical treatment is unclear, and further efforts are needed to expand the existing knowledge. It is suggested that survival of this population is correlated with early diagnosis and elective repair.16

Regarding the technique, established criteria are lacking. Beyond case reports, the available literature mainly consists of small series, and no consensus exists regarding optimal timing or technique; therefore, management must remain individualized.16, 17, 18, 19, 20 In children, reconstruction must account for vessel caliber, durability, and anticipated somatic growth, which can limit the applicability of aneurysmorrhaphy or patch-based strategies, particularly when the diagnosis is uncertain and progressive wall deterioration is possible.14,21, 22, 23, 24 In our patient, the predominant technical challenge was the need for multivisceral revascularization in the setting of extensive ostial disease. We, therefore, favored an open thoracoabdominal bypass with deliberate graft oversizing, combined with a tailored visceral reconstruction. Specifically, a cryopreserved iliac artery allograft with its bifurcation provided a ready-made Y-shaped configuration for simultaneous revascularization of the SMA and right renal artery, and a separate cryopreserved external iliac allograft was used to facilitate left renal autotransplantation and optimize exposure. Although minimally invasive alternatives have been reported in highly selected pediatric patients, mostly limited to the descending thoracic segment, their role remains restricted and long-term durability is still uncertain.25,26

At the 3-year follow-up, computed tomography angiography revealed an ostial stenosis at the level of the cryopreserved allograft used for SMA revascularization. Although this finding was not associated with symptoms or laboratory abnormalities, it raised the possibility of a vasculopathic process contributing to late branch compromise. Nevertheless, given the absence of systemic manifestations and the lack of a definitive clinicopathological diagnosis, systemic corticosteroid or immunosuppressive therapy was not initiated postoperatively, and the patient was managed with close clinical and imaging surveillance. Although establishing an etiologic diagnosis preoperatively is desirable whenever an inflammatory vasculopathy is suspected, this is not always achievable in presentations requiring prompt repair to mitigate rupture risk and prevent progression of critical branch lesions. In our patient, the absence of systemic manifestations and the lack of a definitive clinicopathological diagnosis supported a surveillance-based strategy rather than empiric immunosuppression; nonetheless, ongoing multidisciplinary follow-up remains warranted to reassess for a treatable inflammatory driver, and the SMA conduit stenosis will be followed, focusing on symptoms of mesenteric ischemia. This approach may differ in patients with stronger clinical-biological or imaging evidence of active inflammatory vasculopathy. In selected patients with a strong clinical suspicion of inflammatory vasculopathy, early immunosuppressive therapy may be considered to reduce medium-to long-term sequelae, even when histopathology is nondiagnostic.27,28

Despite the anatomical narrowing observed at follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable. No signs of mesenteric ischemia were noted, and blood pressure control continued to improve. This can be attributed to the compensatory enlargement of the IMA, which developed sufficient collateral circulation to support bowel perfusion in the territory typically supplied by the stenosed SMA. Intraoperatively, preservation of potential collateral pathways was intentionally prioritized, including the IMA, given the extent of visceral involvement and the possibility of late branch-related complications. This adaptive response validates one of the key intraoperative choices made during the procedure: the deliberate preservation of the IMA. In this case, that decision played a critical role in mitigating the hemodynamic impact of the late stenosis.

Limitations

This report describes a single, highly selected case; therefore, broad conclusions regarding the optimal approach to pediatric thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm, graft selection, and long-term growth accommodation cannot be drawn. In addition, the discussion on open vs endovascular strategies reflects the limited and heterogeneous nature of the available evidence in young children, and longer follow-up is required to better define durability and reintervention patterns.

Conclusions

The management of thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms in children presents considerable challenges owing to the rarity of the condition, the complexity of the surgical anatomy, and the need to anticipate future somatic growth. This case demonstrates that open surgical repair, when tailored to the anatomical and physiological characteristics of the patient, is not only feasible, but can result in favorable outcomes. The use of oversizing techniques and the preservation of potential collateral pathways, such as the IMA, proved critical in maintaining vascular stability, even in the presence of late complications. Clarifying etiology to decrease the risk of recurrence is essential.
