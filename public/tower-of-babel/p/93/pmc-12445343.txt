Chromosome‐level genome assembly and annotation of Ulmus minor reveal dynamic intrageneric clusters of resistance genes
Authors: Jorge Pallares‐Zazo, Angel Baron‐Sola, María Valbuena‐Carabaña, Juan Antonio Martin, David Macaya‐Sanz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe field elm (Ulmus minor) is an emblematic species of the European landscape, which has been severely affected by Dutch elm disease (DED) since the 20th century. To support restoration and conservation of this species and congeners, we present a de novo assembly and annotation of a wild genotype tested positive for DED resistance, along with a comprehensive phylogenetic analysis with related species. Cutting‐edge sequencing technologies were used alongside high‐throughput chromosome conformation capture technologies for chromosome conformation capture. Our assembly spans approximately 2.1 Gb, with scaffold and contig N50 sizes of 133.765 and 8.189 Mb, respectively. Repetitive elements account for 81.45% of the genome size. Using transcriptomic information from 19 tissues in varying developmental stages and gene model prediction techniques, a total of 46,357 protein‐coding genes were annotated, 99.70% of which were functionally characterized. An analysis of the presence and arrangement of resistance genes on U. minor, and sister species Ulmus glabra and Ulmus parvifolia, revealed a clustered and syntenic distribution across all of them, with a higher density in U. minor. This genomic resource and its analytical dissection herein introduced represent valuable tools for future in‐depth studies on the resistance mechanisms to DED. Additionally, this study completes the phylogenetic context within the order Rosales and provides a robust framework for future research.

INTRODUCTION

Elm populations in Europe and North America are severely degraded due to the impact of two Dutch elm disease (DED) pandemics during the last 100 years. The two pandemics were respectively caused by two vascular pathogens: Ophiostoma ulmi Buisman (Nannf.) and the more aggressive Ophiostoma novo‐ulmi Brasier, with the latter having now replaced O. ulmi in most locations (Brasier, 2000; Martin et al., 2023, 2010). Trade networks led to the rapid spread of both pathogens on a global scale (Brasier, 2000). DED has caused one of the largest known impacts on the plant world, and it is estimated that only 1% of the ecosystems once dominated by elms remain (Martin et al., 2019).

Ulmus is a genus of 20–40 species divided into two well‐differentiated subgenera: Ulmus and Oreoptelea (Whittemore & Xia, 2017). Most of the species belong to the subgenus Ulmus, mainly distributed in East Asia (Dunn, 2000), from where O. ulmi likely emanated (Masuya et al., 2010). Only three elm species are native to Europe: Ulmus glabra Huds. and Ulmus minor Mill. belong to the subgenus Ulmus; while Ulmus laevis Pall. belongs to subgenus Oreoptelea (Whittemore & Xia, 2017). All three species are highly susceptible to O. novo‐ulmi, although their vulnerability in the field greatly varies depending on environmental factors (Domínguez et al., 2024; Ghelardini & Santini, 2009) and their attractiveness to the DED insect vectors, being U. laevis the least preferred and thus normally the least affected (Webber, 2004). U. minor, the most abundant and also the most affected species in Europe, occupied extensive areas in riparian formations prior to DED outbreaks, often playing a dominant role and supporting important biodiversity hotspots (Mackenthun, 2000; Richens, 2012). It had crucial ecological and economic roles in urban and rural landscapes, where the DED outbreaks have resulted in large economic losses due to control, management, and replacement efforts, besides the important sociocultural impact caused by the loss of emblematic elms in many locations (Martin et al., 2019).

Although no successful control or management solutions to DED have been found to date, the selection and breeding of disease‐resistant elm genotypes is a promising strategy for elm recovery (Martin et al., 2015; Santini et al., 2004; Solla et al., 2005), supported by the moderate to high heritability of DED resistance (Domínguez et al., 2022; Smalley & Guries, 1993; Solla et al., 2014). Early since the emergence of the disease, several breeding programs in North America and Europe have been developed for Ulmus spp. (Heybroek, 1993; Mittempergher & Santini, 2004; Smalley & Guries, 1993). Given that many Asian elm species have high resistance levels (Smalley & Guries, 2000), maybe due to coevolution in East Asia of elms and O. ulmi, resistant cultivars have been obtained through crossbreeding of these resistant Asian elms and susceptible elm species (Mittempergher & Santini, 2004). In Spain, on the contrary, the breeding program has adopted an intraspecific strategy, avoiding crosses with exotic Ulmus species, aiming at maintaining U. minor genetic resources free of introgression. This approach has been feasible thanks to the occurrence of Spanish genotypes exhibiting outstanding resistance levels (Domínguez et al., 2022; Martin et al., 2015).

In that regard, efforts in breeding and in disentangling genetic mechanisms of resistance are facilitated by the availability of fully assembled and annotated reference genomes (Worley et al., 2017). Synergy between reference genomes and increasingly available, cheap, and accurate data from new sequencing technologies is boosting research on population, quantitative and functional genetics, and transcending into acceleration of breeding and conservation strategies. Dissecting resistance genetic components should gain reliability due to improved transcriptomic analyses (due to a better representation of genic elements) and the access to other phenotype‐genome associations (such as quantitative‐trait locus delimitation in progenies or genome‐wide associations in diversity panels). Breeding would benefit from the more efficient genotyping and estimation of relatedness and possible introgression together with the likely understanding of resistance mechanisms, that could eventually lead to effective genomic selection. In the same line, a genomic reference would aid in population genetic studies with an immediate translation into conservation programs, and would also help to understand evolutionary aspects of genus Ulmus from phylogenetic and adaptive perspectives (Worley et al., 2017), through comparative genomic studies with related taxa or forest species with available reference genomes (such as Prunus spp. or Corylus spp.). Comparative genomics can discern syntenic relationships, phylogenetic status, and, more importantly, genic content evolution (Alföldi & Lindblad‐Toh, 2013). Conserved, divergent, duplicated, or lost genes can be identified within taxonomic branches. Common genes are often involved in fundamental biological functions whereas divergent genes are often phenotype‐related genes and thus closely related to environmental adaptation and defense (Kozak et al., 2011). The movement and duplication of transposable elements (TEs) can spawn genes or gene fragment copies, providing substrates for genic innovation (neofunctionalization; Alföldi & Lindblad‐Toh, 2013).

Resistance genes (R genes) are a class of elements particularly prone to rapid evolution through neofunctionalization, leading to the formation of genomic clusters (Leister, 2004; van Wersch & Li, 2019), where R genes are especially enriched (“hotspots”). These clusters play a pivotal role on plant pathosystems and the comparative analyses across species or intraspecific panels of these groups and their genomic layout would help elucidating the mechanistic basis and evolutionary pathways of pathogen resistance. For instance, there is uncertainty as to whether warmer and wetter climates promote wider arrays of R genes as consequence of higher pressure of microbial pathogens; whether Asian elm species’ ability to resist DED is as an outcome of coevolution with the pathogen, having imprinted some commonalities in their R gene clusters; or whether the discovery of highly resistant U. minor individuals exclusively in the Iberian Peninsula is a consequence of higher diversity in R gene clusters in glacial refugia of Southern Europe peninsulas. Properly annotated and assembled genomes can thus instill the aforementioned technical approaches, and mechanistic and comparative studies, sparking new theses concerning pathogen resistance within genus Ulmus, and namely against DED. Indeed, the existence of highly resistant individuals indirectly suggests the involvement of canonical R genes in qualitative resistance to DED; however, direct evidence of their role has not yet been reported. Other sources of resistance have been identified by association and inference, involving diverse traits such as xylem anatomical structure, or the induction of antifungal defense compounds and pathogenesis‐related (PR) proteins (Büchel et al., 2016; Martin et al., 2019), which are typically associated with more gradual, quantitative resistance mechanisms (Ennos, 2015). Some of these resistance mechanisms may have also evolved through neofunctionalization.

Despite the ecological and cultural importance of elms in the temperate areas of the Northern Hemisphere, comprehensive genomic resources on the genus have been oddly scarce. The first significant genomic resource was published in 2015, featuring the transcriptome of U. minor (Perdiguero et al., 2015), but not until 2024, high‐quality chromosome‐level genome assemblies for some Ulmus species have become public on different repositories. First, two genome assemblies of subgenus Ulmus presented excellent contiguity and completeness statistics: an U. glabra unannotated assembly (GenBank GCA_964106905.1) and an Ulmus parvifolia genome, annotated on a leaf tissue transcriptome basis (GenBank GCA_041381145.1). These two resources likely lack information about gene content relevant to DED resistance, since they omit transcriptomic data on xylem tissue, which is the main colonization pathway for the DED fungi (Sherif et al., 2017), perhaps failing to capture key xylem‐specific genes involved in triggering responses and resistance mechanisms, such as tylosis formation and antimicrobial compound synthesis (Micco et al., 2016). The absence of a genomic resource for subgenus Oreoptelea, for which transferability from subgenus Ulmus might be questionable due to genomic divergence between both subgenera (Whittemore & Xia, 2017), has been lately ironed out by the release of a state‐of‐the‐art annotated genome assembly for Ulmus americana (Phytozome genome ID: 881).

Here, we present a de novo chromosome‐level assembly and multi‐tissue annotation of a DED‐resistant U. minor genotype, developed by combining several sequencing technologies (PacBio, PacBio high‐fidelity [HiFi], high‐throughput chromosome conformation capture [Hi‐C], and Illumina) and the transcriptome of five plant organs in different developmental phases (N = 19). Our goal was to create a high‐quality genome annotated for both genic and repeat elements, to subsequently identify and delimit hotspots of R genes associated with qualitative resistance, and genes involved in other mechanisms of quantitative resistance to DED, while assessing their syntenic conservation among congeneric species.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant tissues, nucleic acid isolation, and sequencing techniques

Plant material for sequencing was collected from several ramets of the U. minor clone V‐AD2 conserved at the Centro Nacional de Recursos Genéticos Forestales Puerta de Hierro (Martin et al., 2015). Additional potted ramets were used for specific pre‐collection treatments. Unless otherwise specified, tissue samples came from young leaves, flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, and pulverized. Due to leaf mucus interfering with DNA isolation, prewashing was conducted with 5 M NaCl saline solution (three 15‐min 10‐ml washes on ice for 0.5 g of ground leaf tissue).

Nucleic acid isolation for all techniques and sequencing libraries preparation for proximity ligation (Hi‐C) and RNA‐seq were carried out at domestic facilities. Illumina, PacBio, and PacBio HiFi libraries were prepared at external facilities (Macrogen). All libraries were sequenced as well in those external services. Isolate qualities were assessed through NanoDrop UV spectrophotometry and agarose gel electrophoresis and quantities via Qubit fluorometry (both devices from Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Genomic DNA (gDNA) for short‐read sequencing was extracted using the InviSorb Spin Plant kit, with libraries constructed using a TruSeq Nano DNA kit and sequenced in two lanes of an Illumina Hiseq2500 platform (250‐bp paired‐end [PE]). For PacBio long‐read sequencing, potted ramets underwent a 72‐h dark treatment at 20°C to reduce polysaccharide content. High‐quality gDNA was extracted following Healey et al. (2014) and used to generate 10 libraries, sequenced in eight PacBio Sequel SMRT cells and two Sequel II cells. Isolation of gDNA for PacBio HiFi followed a protocol with a nuclei isolation (PacBio's protocol 102‐574‐800) trailed by high‐molecular‐weight DNA extraction with Nanobind kits (PacBio). Libraries were sequenced in one SMRT cell of a PacBio Revio platform. Hi‐C sequencing libraries were prepared using the Proximo Hi‐C (Plant) kit (Phase Genomics) and sequenced in one Illumina HiSeqX lane (150‐bp PE).

RNA isolation for gene annotation involved 19 tissues, comprising roots, stems, buds, leaves, flowers, and fruits at various developmental stages (Table S1). RNA was extracted using the Plant/Fungi Total RNA kit (Norgen Biotek) and libraries were prepared with the xGen RNA Library Preparation Kit (Integrated DNA Technologies), and sequenced in one lane of an Illumina NovaSeqX platform (150‐bp PE).

Data quality control and preprocessing

Illumina reads were examined with FastQC v.0.11.9 (Andrews, 2010), and adapters were trimmed using Trimmomatic v.0.39 (Bolger et al., 2014), which also removed low‐quality sequences (hereafter, specific parametrization for various programs in Supporting Information Text S1). Hi‐C read quality was assessed with the hic_qc pipeline (Supporting Information Text S2), focusing on long‐distance contig interactions. PacBio subreads were evaluated with SequelTools (Hufnagel et al., 2020).

Genome size estimation

Analyses of k‐mer distributions assessed genome size, repetitive sequences, heterozygosity, and genic regions, using Jellyfish v.2.3.0 (Marçais & Kingsford, 2011), and processed with GenomeScope v.2.0 (Ranallo‐Benavidez et al., 2020; Vurture et al., 2017) and SGA‐preqc (Simpson, 2014). Such estimates were limited by the high repetitive content. Since Jellyfish's coverage limitation (10,000x) is advised to be avoided to account for the repetitive elements (Jaron, 2020), we enabled unrestricted k‐mer counting by means of KMC 3 (Kokot et al., 2017).

Genome assembly

The assembly strategy integrated multiple software tools in sequential steps. Initially, PacBio reads were corrected, trimmed, and assembled using Canu v.2.2 (Koren et al., 2017), producing the Canu assembly. Several polishing rounds followed, incorporating Illumina reads via the Polca module in MaSuRCA v.4.0.9 (Zimin et al., 2013). Simultaneously, HiFi reads were assembled using the HiCanu module of Canu, resulting in the HiCanu assembly. These processes produced two long‐read assemblies.

The MaSuRCA SAMBA module played a pivotal role by filling gaps in genomic assemblies, significantly extending contigs. Using SAMBA (Supporting Information Text S1), the HiCanu genome served as a reference, and the Canu genome was integrated to improve continuity. However, the assembly length exceeded expectations due to Canu and HiCanu limitations in distinguishing haplotypes. To address this, Purge Haplotigs v.1.1.2 (Roach et al., 2018) separated primary from alternative haplotypes and classified artifacts, resolving the primary genome.

Hi‐C sequencing data were then integrated into the primary genome following Arima's methodology, aiming at organizing and orienting sequences into the actual number of chromosomes of genus Ulmus (2n = 28) (Whittemore & Xia, 2017). YaHS v.1.2 (Zhou et al., 2023) processed Hi‐C reads through mapping, quality filtering, merging, and duplicate removal. The purged genome and quality‐aligned reads enabled a higher level assembly, generating pseudo‐chromosomes. Juicer Tools v.2.20.00 (Durand, Shamim et al., 2016) and Juicebox v.2.20 (Durand, Robinson et al., 2016) facilitated visualizing contact point density to organize sequences.

Finally, pseudo‐chromosomes were fine‐tuned using JBAT tools (Dudchenko et al., 2018). Manual curation, based on contact map data, positioned sequences with specific characteristics, such as small size or low contact point density (Figure S1; H. Mangelson, personal communication, 2023). This meticulous process addressed sequences not initially integrated into chromosome levels due to their repetitive content, ensuring accurate organization.

Assembly quality assessment

The assembly's quality was assessed using three key parameters: (i) N50 statistic measured assembly continuity; (ii) mapping Illumina reads to the final assembly evaluated the percentage of reads effectively contributing to the assembly; and (iii) BUSCO v5.2.2 (where BUSCO is Benchmarking Universal Single‐Copy Orthologs) (Simão et al., 2015), using viridiplantae_odb10 and embryophyta_odb10 databases, assessed gene completeness. Quality checks were conducted at various stages: Canu assembly, HiCanu assembly, SAMBA joint assembly, purged assembly, and the final assembly.

Repeat annotation

For repetitive content annotation, a de novo repeat library was generated using RepeatModeler v2.0.4 (Flynn et al., 2020), which integrates RECON v1.08 (Z. Bao & Eddy, 2002) and RepeatScout v1.0.6 (Price et al., 2005). Full‐length long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons were identified with LTRharvest v1.6.2 (Ellinghaus et al., 2008) and LTR_FINDER v1.0.7 (Xu & Wang, 2007) (Supporting Information Text S1). LTR_retriever v2.9.0 (Ou & Jiang, 2018) generated high‐quality intact LTR retrotransposons and a nonredundant LTR library.

TE sequences were classified via homology searches against Dfam v3.7 (Storer et al., 2021) and RepBase v262018 (W. Bao et al., 2015) using RepeatMasker v4.1.5 (Tarailo‐Graovac & Chen, 2009). Unknown repeats were classified through five iterations of the repclassifier program (Card, 2022) (Table S2). Tandem repeats were annotated with Tandem Repeats Finder (Benson, 1999).

A species‐specific nonredundant repeat library was constructed by integrating results iteratively in RepeatMasker. Simple repeats were soft‐masked (Supporting Information Text S1), followed by hard‐masked annotations based on homology with RepBase database (source Viridiplantae). The de novo classified repeats from RepeatModeler and LTR_retriever were incorporated first, followed by unclassified repeats, ensuring comprehensive repeat annotation.

Noncoding RNA prediction

Using tRNAscan‐SE v.2.0.12 (Chan & Lowe, 2019) tRNA genes were discovered. Additionally, a variety of other noncoding RNAs, including rRNAs, snRNAs, and miRNAs, were predicted through comparisons with the established Rfam v.141123 database (Griffiths‐Jones et al., 2003) of known noncoding RNA sequences.

Gene annotation and functional annotations

Gene structure annotation involved three strategies: de novo prediction, homology‐based prediction, and transcriptome analysis using RNA‐Seq data. De novo predictions were made with MAKER v3.01 (Cantarel et al., 2008), integrating Augustus v3.3 (Stanke et al., 2008) and SNAP v2.4.0 (Korf, 2004). Three MAKER rounds refined the models based on annotation edit distance, gene count, lengths, and BUSCO completeness. Homology‐based predictions used GeMoMa v1.7 (Keilwagen et al., 2016) with reference models from Arabidopsis thaliana, Cannabis sativa, and Morus notabilis, incorporating RNA‐Seq data for splice site prediction. For transcript‐based prediction, RNA‐Seq reads were aligned with Hisat2 v2.0.4 (Kim et al., 2015) and assembled using StringTie v2.2.1 (Pertea et al., 2015). Coding regions were refined with TransDecoder v5.7.1 (Haas, n.d.). EVidenceModeler v1.1.1 (Haas et al., 2008) consolidated the gene models, weighting RNA‐Seq data highest, followed by homology‐based models, and then de novo predictions. After predicting the gene approval rating, CPC2 v0.9‐r2 (Kang et al., 2017) analyzed the potential of the predicted genes, generating 46,357 gene models.

Functional annotation used BLAST+ v2.2.31 (Camacho et al., 2009) to align sequences against NR, NT, Pfam, eggNOG, and SwissProt databases (Supporting Information Text S2), providing insights into gene functions. InterProScan v5.67.99 (Jones et al., 2014) integrated KEGG, Gene Ontology (GO), and Gene3D databases (Supporting Information Text S2), further refining annotations.

Gene families and phylogenetic analysis

The genomes of U. minor and nine other plants (U. glabra, U. parvifolia, Humulus lupulus, C. sativa, Ziziphu jujuba, Prunus persica, Prunus dulcis, Malva sylvestris, and Corylus avellana) were analyzed for gene evolution (Table S3). To examine conservation and divergence among evolutionary lineages, GENESPACE (Lovell et al., 2022) utilized OrthoFinder v2.5.5 (Emms & Kelly, 2019) and MCScanX (Wang et al., 2012) to identify orthologs and paralogs, analyzing syntenic blocks to reveal chromosomal evolution and genome rearrangements. Annotations for U. glabra and U. parvifolia were generated de novo using GeMoMa.

All‐vs‐all alignments from OrthoFinder and species trees were processed with Tree2GD (Chen et al., 2022) to identify gene duplication and speciation events along the phylogenetic tree. Tree2GD includes five phases: (1) protein and coding DNA sequence comparisons with DIAMOND (Buchfink et al., 2015); (2) hierarchical orthologous groups identified using PhyloMCL (S. Zhou et al., 2020), classifying orthologs and paralogs; (3) multiple sequence alignments with MUSCLE (Edgar, 2004); (4) phylogenetic tree reconstruction with IQ‐TREE 2 (Minh et al., 2020) and ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017); and (5) identification of gene duplications and inference of whole‐genome duplications (WGDs). WGDs were distinguished from small‐scale duplications using Ks values and syntenic analysis with a support vector machine model.

This integrated approach provided insights into evolutionary history, gene retention after duplications, and genomic rearrangements across species, pinpointing both conservation and divergence in syntenic blocks and duplication events. Relative divergence times between species were inferred using fourfold degenerate transversion (4DTv) values, and absolute divergence times were validated using the TimeTree 5 tool (Kumar et al., 2022).

Hotspots of R genes in Ulmus species

Using the released genomes of subgenus Ulmus to date, we assessed the enrichment of genes possibly involved in DED‐resistance mechanisms in U. minor, U. glabra, and U. parvifolia (Table S3). We considered R genes as candidates likely involved in resistance to DED, given the occurrence of highly resistant cultivars (Martin et al., 2015). In addition, but in a separate analysis, we examined genes involved in biological processes previously suggested as sources of resistance to DED in the literature (Büchel et al., 2016). To analyze the genomic layout of these types of genes, we performed a window‐based enrichment analysis.

In first place, we identified and classified R genes in the three genomes and carried out a hotspot analysis to pinpoint clumps along the genome. R genes were classified into three main structural categories: (1) intracellular NLRs (NB‐LRR [nucleotide‐binding leucine‐rich repeats]; NB‐ARC [nucleotide‐binding adaptor shared by APAF‐1, R proteins, and CED‐4] domain‐containing); (2) transmembrane LRR‐RLKs (leucine‐rich repeat receptor‐like kinases) and LRR‐RLPs (LRR receptor‐like proteins); and (3) transmembrane LysM‐type RLKs and RLPs. Specifically, (1) NLRs were identified using NLR‐Annotator (Steuernagel et al., 2020), applied to each unmasked genome assembly in FASTA format. Candidate sequences were subtyped into TNL (Toll/Interleukin [TIR]‐NB‐LRR), CNL (Coiled‐Coil [CC]‐NB‐LRR), and NL (NB‐LRR). NL genes were defined as sequences containing a central NB‐ARC domain but lacking a recognizable N‐terminal domain such as TIR or CC. (2) LRR‐RLKs were predicted using iTAK (Zheng et al., 2016) in combination with the RLK classification scheme previously reported (Shiu et al., 2004). (3) LRR‐RLPs were identified by screening all predicted proteins with InterProScan, filtering out those shorter than 150 amino acids (Ngou et al., 2022). (4) LysM‐type R genes were subdivided into LysM‐RLKs (identified via iTAK) and LysM‐RLPs (proteins with LysM domains detected via HMMER and lacking PF00069.26; more details of these methods in Supporting Information Text S3). Transmembrane domain presence and orientation for the last three categories was verified using DeepTMHMM (Hallgren et al., 2022).

The final set of R genes was integrated and annotated by structural and functional category. Gene duplication patterns were analyzed using MCScanX (Wang et al., 2012) to determine the predominant mode of duplication within the R gene set in each genome.

To explore the genomic distribution of R genes and identify potential hotspots, each genome was segmented into 10‐Mb windows, within which we counted the number of R genes and of total genes. We then conducted 10,000 random permutations to redistribute the R genes among windows, with probabilities weighted by the total gene count in each window. This allowed us to compare the observed distribution of R genes with the randomized distributions. Statistical significance was determined using empirical p‐values, adjusted for multiple comparisons via false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Empirical p‐values were defined as the proportion of the number of permutation tests larger than the observed number of R genes. This comparison of the observed gene count with random distributions allowed us to statistically pinpoint significant deviations, deemed as R gene hotspots. For that, we calculated the median and 2.5%–97.5% quantiles of R gene counts across all permutations per window. Windows were considered R gene hotspots if they met two criteria: (1) an empirical p‐value below 0.01 after FDR correction, and (2) a strong enrichment in R gene count, defined as a fold change of at least four between the observed number of R genes and the corresponding median from the permuted distribution.

To evaluate the conservation of such hotspots, we studied the synteny among the three species, first, using GENESPACE we determined cognate chromosomes and, when necessary, inverted them to match orientations (Table S4). Afterward, we employed LASTZ to link R gene‐enriched windows across the different species.

Hotspots of genes involved in quantitative resistance in Ulmus species

In an analogous manner, we studied the clumping of several categories of genes involved in putative mechanisms of quantitative resistance to DED (Büchel et al., 2016). Based on literature, the categories of sources of resistance: (A) constitutive defense, mostly anatomical; (B) induced response, including anatomical structures and hypersensitive response and hormone‐mediated signaling pathways; (C) chemical defense compounds; (D) other plant–pathogen interaction proteins; (E) and transcriptomic response from published controlled experiments (Islam et al., 2022; Perdiguero et al., 2015; more detailed description in Supporting Information Text S4). Given that the genome of U. glabra had no available annotation and to ensure an unbiased comparison, we uniformly applied a de novo GeMoMa annotation process to the three Ulmus genomes, followed by functional annotations using eggNOG‐mapper v2.1.6 (Cantalapiedra et al., 2021). GO annotations were parsed to extract genes associated with a curated list of relevant GO terms associated with categories described above (A–E; Supporting Information File S1). Genes previously classified as R genes were excluded from this analysis. A window‐based enrichment analysis, identical to the described for R genes, was then independently conducted for each of the five major functional categories (A–E).

RESULTS

Sequencing coverage

The total coverage of whole‐genome (WG) sequencing techniques was over 265x of the estimated U. minor genome: 206 Gb of Illumina 250‐bp PE sequences resulting in 98x coverage; 292 Gb of PacBio subreads, therefore 139x coverage; and 61 Gb of PacBio HiFi reads tallying 29x coverage. PacBio sequencing subreads showed an N50 read length of 14.18 kb and an average subread length of 10.10 kb (Table S5), whereas PacBio HiFi reads showed an N50 read length of 13.76 kb and an average read length of 13.78 kb (Table S6). Chromatin conformation (Hi‐C) sequencing produced 158 Gb of Illumina 150‐bp PE sequences, achieving a coverage depth of 75x. Finally, sequencing of RNA transcripts from 19 different plant tissues summed 326.8 Gb (Table S7).

Genome size estimation and assembly

A k‐mer analysis using WG Illumina PE data and KMC 3 software (Kokot et al., 2017) estimated a genome size of 1.7 Gb for a haploid chromosome set, providing an approximation broadly consistent with Whittemore and Xia (2017), who reported U. minor’s genome size around 4.1 pg/2C (∼978 Mb/pg; ∼2.00 Gbp). Repetitive elements were estimated to account for 70.8% of the genome and the estimated heterozygosity was 3.59% (Table S8).

Regarding genome assembly, first, the outcome of Canu (Koren et al., 2017) software uniquely using PacBio subreads produced a preliminary genome of 6837 contigs with an N50 length of 1.82 Mb and a cumulative length of 3264.199 Mb. Filtered Illumina 250‐bp PE reads were incorporated into this assembly for polishing (Figure 1A), using two rounds of Polca module in MaSuRCA (Zimin et al., 2013), 60,031 substitution errors and 329,483 indel errors were corrected, achieving a remarkably high consensus quality of 99.98%. Second, long and accurate PacBio HiFi reads were assembled using HiCanu software (Nurk et al., 2020), resulting in an independent assembly comprising 4258 contigs, an N50 of 6.3 Mb, and a total length of 3477.199 Mb (Figure 1A).

(B) Circos plot of the 14 chromosomes displaying from the outermost to the innermost circle: chromosome ideograms with scale (Mb), histogram of gene density associated with quantitative defense responses (blue), and histogram of canonical resistance genes (R genes) (pink). The innermost links represent syntenic relationships between paralogous blocks identified by MCScanX. (C) Bar chart of the results obtained from the BUSCO analysis using the Viridiplantae and Embryophyta databases.

Subsequently, both assemblies were integrated using the MaSuRCA Samba module, resulting in an improved contig assembly comprising 3329 contigs with an N50 of 12.8 Mb and a cumulative length of 3489.273 Mb (Figure 1A). Since such excessive cumulative length pointed to the presence of homologous content, we applied Purge Haplotigs tool (Roach et al., 2018) to extract alternative haplotypes. This process identified 2065 contigs as alternative haplotypes and 966 as artifacts that were removed. Following the haplotype purge, a refined assembly emerged, composed of 298 contigs with an N50 of 14.48 Mb and a total length of 2105.458 Mb (Figure 1A).

Illumina reads from Hi‐C were rigorously trimmed, quality filtered, and deduplicated shrinking them into a 2.5‐Gb dataset. Along with the purged genome, they were submitted to a YaHS assembly process (C. Zhou et al., 2023). YaHS sequences were afterward manually oriented and positioned, resulting in a final assembly with a length of 2.1 Gb, an N50 of 133.76 Mb, consisting of 14 pseudochromosomes (90% of the total length) and 1227 scaffolds (10% of the total length; Table 1; Figure 1B). Visual inspection of the heatmap of the contig contact matrix generated using Hi‐C (Figure S1) confirmed the accuracy of the clustering, ordering, and orientation of the contigs.

Genome assembly quality check

BUSCO analyses tackle the quality of genome assemblies (Simão et al., 2015), specifically focusing on the completeness of conserved genes within the OrthoDB database (http://cegg.unige.ch/orthodb). For the Viridiplantae lineage, results indicated a high level of completeness with an overall score of 98.1%, categorized as follows: Complete genes were represented at 95.3%, with 77.4% being single‐copy and 20.7% duplicated. Fragmented genes accounted for only 0.2%, while missing genes tallied 1.7% (Figure 1C). The total number of genes assessed in this category was 425. Similarly, for the Embryophyta lineage, the overall completeness was 97.2%, comprising 75.6% single‐copy and 21.6% duplicated. Fragmented genes contribute 0.7% and missing genes accounted for 2.1% (Figure 1C). The total number of genes analyzed in this category is 1614. The low percentages of fragmented and missing genes underscore the completeness and reliability of the assembly for downstream genomic analyses (Figure 1C).

Absence of bacterial contamination was verified upon genome submission to NCBI, where Foreign Contamination Screen is performed as per protocol. As evidence, no genome‐wide significant differences were observed, and the points were concentrated around 35% (Figure S2). Finally, we evaluated the coverage and utilization of sequencing data aligning the trimmed WG Illumina PE reads to the final assembly, resulting in an incorporation of 98.48% of the reads, supporting the completeness of the assemblage.

Content of repetitive elements

The custom repeat library uncovered an intricate genomic landscape, revealing dynamism and a diverse composition of repetitive elements (Figure 2), nonredundant repeats encompassing a notable 81.45% of the total genome. Among the LTR elements, Gypsy elements constituted 44.10%, and Copia elements made up 12.65%, together representing 60.14% of the overall genomic landscape. DNA transposons accounted for 13.63% of the genome, including MULE‐MuDR at 4.01% and hobo‐Activator covering 1.41% of the genome. Moreover, rolling circles, covering 1.18% of the genome, increased genomic complexity. Unclassified elements occupied 2.78 %, highlighting the presence of diverse and yet‐to‐be‐characterized repetitive sequences.

Delimitation and functional annotation of genes

After validation and consolidation of genetic models from three approaches, a total of 46,357 coding genes were identified, with 95% exhibiting a complete open reading frame. The average length of these predicted genes was 2703.36 bp. On average, each gene contained four exons with an average length of 285 bp per exon, and three introns with an average length of 432 bp per intron. When comparing our predicted gene set to functional databases such as SwissProt, NR, NT, Pfam, KEGG, GO, and Gene3D 46,241 genes were annotated, covering 99.70% of the total gene set (Figure 3; Table 2). Prediction of noncoding RNAs through sequence comparison with the Rfam noncoding RNA library rendered 922 rRNAs, 3904 snoRNAs, and 12,959 miRNAs (Table S9). Additionally, tRNAs were predicted using tRNAscan‐SE (Chan & Lowe, 2019), which identified 855 tRNA sequences in the genome (Table S9). Verification of gene annotation was conducted by means of BUSCO evaluation using the embryophyta_odb10 database, producing a result of 97.2%, indicating that the annotation results were complete (Table S10).

Description and dynamics of gene families and phylogenetic analysis

The genomes of U. minor and nine plant taxa with increasing degrees of relatedness (U. glabra, U. parvifolia, H. lupulus, C. sativa, Z. jujuba, and four tree species comprising three Rosaceae spp. and one Fagales sp.; Table S3) were compared to investigate genome evolution. Results of OrthoFinder (Emms & Kelly, 2019) showed a total of 40,590 gene families, containing 42,230 genes, that were clustered in the U. minor genome together with the genomes of both closely related and remotely related species (Table S11). H. lupulus had the most unique families (791) and U. parvifolia the most unclustered genes (2709), while P. persica had the least unique families (54). U. minor showed more genes in common with Ulmus spp., hop (H. lupulus), jujube (Z. jujuba) and hemp (C. sativa) than with other species (Figure S3).

Through all‐versus‐all comparison of the abovementioned eight taxa using Tree2GD (Chen et al., 2022), we identified 14,467 gene families in the most recent common ancestor (MRCA; Figure 4A). Compared to the MRCA, C. avellana has expanded 1922 gene families, while other species have expanded 1478 gene families. Ulmus species shared 16,564 orthologs with H. lupulus and C. sativa, the two more related taxa. Since diverging from Z. jujuba, Ulmus species have gained 3651 gene families and lost 1241 gene families. Within the genus Ulmus, U. minor and U. glabra have each gained over 2400 families and lost around 1200, while U. parvifolia shows a contrasting pattern, with only 687 gains and 11,058 losses since its divergence from the other Ulmus spp. At the Ulmus divergence point from H. lupulus and C. sativa, 68% of gene duplications have retained all copies in Ulmus spp. (Figure 4A).

The distribution of 4DTv values across gene pairs in U. minor, U. glabra, and U. parvifolia showed a sharp peak near zero, consistent with recent duplications but lacked of secondary peaks on higher magnitudes of the statistic typically associated with ancient WGD events (Figure 4A), like the observed in Malus domestica at 4DTv∼0.1, consistent with a previously reported WGD (Velasco et al., 2010). This pattern is further supported by gene duplication ratios inferred with Tree2GD: M. domestica shows a high duplication retention (57.98%), while Ulmus species display markedly lower values (U. minor: 8.07%; U. glabra: 6.33%; U. parvifolia: 9.71%; Table S12). Intraspecific pairwise chromosome synteny‐based analysis in Ulmus using MCScanX did not support the occurrence of a recent WGD in the genus, but found some intrachromosomal duplications in U. minor (Figure 1), which were not apparent in U. glabra and U. parvifolia.

Phylogenetic reconstruction places C. avellana (order Fagales) as the earliest diverging lineage, followed by the Rosaceae clade (M. domestica and Prunus spp.), and subsequently Z. jujuba. The divergence between Ulmus species and the clade formed by C. sativa and H. lupulus occurred approximately 61.2 million years ago (MYA), while both lineages diverged from Z. jujuba around 74.6 MYA (Figure 4B).

Genomic synteny block analysis is a powerful tool for tracing the evolutionary origins of chromosomes across species. With this goal we utilized GENESPACE (Lovell et al., 2022) to examine the aligned protein sequences of U. minor and the nine species reported before. Concordantly with phylogenetics, genomes of Ulmus spp., H. lupulus, C. sativa, and Z. jujuba, exhibited large syntenic arrangements (Figure 5). Notably, the sex chromosomes of H. lupulus and C. sativa were orthologs of chromosome 7 in U. minor and chromosome 5 in Z. jujuba. This corroborates the thesis that the sex chromosomes in these two Cannabaceae likely emerged in a common ancestor after the split from the Ulmaceae lineage (Prentout et al., 2021). It is also worth noting that, at least visually, Ulmus genomes displayed higher synteny with Z. jujuba genome than with hemp or hop (Figure 5), despite being slightly less related. This may indicate differing rates of genome evolution among these taxa. Synteny analysis among the three Ulmus species revealed a broadly conserved chromosomal organization (Figure 5). Nevertheless, a few rearrangements were appreciable, which may reflect either true biological structural variation or technical inconsistencies due to the Hi‐C–based scaffolding.

Hotspots of R genes across genus Ulmus

Across all three Ulmus species, the R gene repertoire was dominated by NLR‐CNLs, followed by TM‐LRR‐RLKs, TM‐LRR‐RLPs, and to a lesser extent NLR‐TNLs, NLR‐NLs, and TM‐LysM‐RLKs. In U. minor, NLR‐CNLs were the most abundant class (n = 582), comprising nearly half of all predicted R genes (n = 1314), followed by TM‐LRR‐RLKs (337) and TM‐LRR‐RLPs (201; Table S13). A similar trend was observed in U. glabra (NLR‐CNLs = 469; total R genes = 1176) and U. parvifolia (NLR‐CNLs = 445; total R genes = 1099), with minor variation in the proportion of NLR‐TNLs and LysM‐RLKs. NLR‐NL‐type genes were few (<1% of the total set; Table S13) while no TM‐LysM‐RLPs was found.

Sliding‐window permutation tests (1 Mb step, 10 Mb window) identified a limited set of high‐confidence R‐gene hotspots in each species (Figure 6). U. minor exhibited eight significant hotspots (labeled a–m), being the most prominent on chr11 where three consecutive windows (k–m) collectively harbored 112 R genes versus a median expectation of 22 (Table S14). Two other relevant hotspots on chr02 (a) and chr07 (g) were identified, each containing more than four‐fold enrichments. U. glabra showed eight R hotspots distributed over six chromosomes, the largest on OZ067186.1 (chr02 cognate; a) where 43 R genes concentrated (∼7× enrichment). The adjacent windows k and l on OZ067189.1 (chr11 cognate) form a compound block analogous to the chr11 block in U. minor (Table S14). U. parvifolia presented seven hotspots, with the highest number of R genes on CM084350.1 (chr02 cognate; a, 55 R genes, 14×). A second high‐density region occurs on CM084354.1 (chr11 cognate; k–l, 95 R genes total), mirroring the tandem hotspot configuration observed in the cognate chromosome 11 of the other two genomes.

R gene hotspots appear to be syntenic and structurally conserved across Ulmus species, sharing region‐wise R‐gene‐type composition across species. For instance, the hotspot on U. minor’s chr02 and cognates is primarily composed of TM‐LRR‐RLKs, whereas NLR‐CNLs predominate in the hotspots located on chr11 and cognates (Figure 6).

Analysis of duplication modes revealed that tandem duplication is the predominant mechanism contributing to R gene expansion across all three Ulmus species. In U. glabra and U. parvifolia, tandemly duplicated R genes accounted for the majority of the repertoire, representing 69.1% and 67.3%, respectively. In U. minor, tandem duplicates comprised a substantial 52.9% of the total. This lower proportion in U. minor was partially compensated by a higher incidence of proximal duplications (21.0% in U. minor vs. 14.2% and 16.3% in U. glabra and U. parvifolia, respectively; Table S15), suggesting alternative modes of local gene amplification. In contrast, segmental duplications contributed marginally to R gene expansion, accounting for only 6.5% in U. minor and just over 1% in both U. glabra and U. parvifolia. A comparable proportion of R genes were classified as dispersed duplicates, with values ranging from 14.0% in U. glabra and U. parvifolia to 18.0% in U. minor. Singleton R genes were rare in all three species, consistently representing less than 2% of the total.

Genomic layout of genes involved in other putative mechanisms of quantitative defense

Functional analysis of quantitative genes revealed a high degree of conservation in the relative frequencies of GO terms across U. minor, U. glabra, and U. parvifolia. In U. minor, categories A, B, and C (constitutive mechanisms, induced response, and chemical composition, respectively) accounted for 8%–12% of GO hits each, while the transcriptome response category (E) accounted for 68.7% of total GO hits. Category D (other plant–pathogen interaction proteins; PPIP), largely composed of PR proteins, represented just 0.45% of hits.

Remarkably, neither categories B nor E (induced and experimental transcriptome responses) displayed any hotspot in any of the three species (Table S16). In contrast, categories C and D (chemical compounds and PPIP) presented a clumped distribution, especially the latter. Category A (constitutive mechanisms) displayed enriched windows but in a reduced number: only two genomic windows on chr05 and chr10 of U. minor, being the cognate of chr05 also detected in U. glabra, and no window found in U. parvifolia.

Gene category C (chemical defense compounds) presented five hotspots in U. parvifolia the largest number across the species, dropping to three in U. glabra and to two in U. minor. The hotspots detected in chr04 and chr08 (and cognates) were present in the three species. Isoprenoid metabolism, oxidoreductase activity, and sesquiterpene and terpenoid biosynthesis were the GO terms related to defense compound enriched in these regions.

Regarding category D of PPIP, U. glabra with eight enriched windows, presented the highest number, followed by U. minor with seven and U. parvifolia with five. Synteny was also observed, with windows in chromosomes 1, 5 and 11 (and cognates) shared among species. GO terms of systemic acquired resistance, serine‐type endopeptidase inhibitor activity, and defense response by callose deposition were remarkably abundant.

DISCUSSION

Field elm (U. minor) was once the most abundant elm species in Europe, ranging from North Maghreb to South Scandinavia, and from Great Britain to the Caucasus (EUFORGEN, 2016). It was a keystone species in riparian ecosystems and a relevant cultural element of many urban and rural landscapes. When DED ravaged the elm populations, and given the particular dynamics of these epidemics, U. minor was the most affected elm species, together with U. americana in North America. Even after many decades since its outbreak, DED still poses a significant challenge to the long‐term recovery and conservation of field elm (Martin et al., 2023). Here, we present the genome of V‐AD2 clone, a DED‐resistant wild tree (Martin et al., 2015), hoping it will serve as a reference for future research on this pathosystem.

Features of the genome assembly and annotation

The final assembly here presented has a length of 2.1 Gb distributed in 14 pseudomolecules, highly concordant with observations from flow cytometry (4.1 pg/2C ∼2.00 Gb; Whittemore & Xia, 2017). Our preassembly genome size estimation using KMC 3 software produced a slightly smaller value (1.7 Gb), probably due to heterozygosity and an elevated repetitive content, which hinder k‐mer‐based size estimations (Jaron, 2020). The improvement across the assembly process shown by statistics (Figure 1A) demonstrates the effectiveness of the strategy followed and the robustness of the final assembly in capturing continuity and gene completeness. Gene annotation through the consensus of three complementary approaches yielded 46,357 coding genes, with an average length of 2,703.36 bp, in line with other forest tree genomes (Table S17). The vast majority of these genes (99.70%) have been functionally annotated by homology, and BUSCO analyses reflected a high degree of completeness both in the assembly and the annotation. Final arrangement of the genome was done via proximity ligation, method comprising both automated and manual steps. We acknowledge that it may present some arrangement inaccuracies that should be evaluated in the future using complementary methodologies, such as linkage or optical mapping. Even though minor structural inexactness normally occurs and is generally innocuous for genomic studies such as genome‐wide association study (GWAS), it should be taken into account in other uses that rely on proper arrangement, such as synteny assessment.

Estimations of heterozygosity through k‐mer analyses on Illumina reads yielded a high value (3.59%). It is well established that forest trees present elevated genetic diversity, especially when compared to domesticated species, yet the values found here for field elm were four‐fold higher than for Salix purpurea, another riparian tree (Table S17). Given the experimental design, we cannot determine if this high heterozygosity value is common to the whole species, to the population of origin, or to the sequenced genotype (clone V‐AD2). However, ongoing genotyping studies of several U. minor wild trees points toward an individual‐specific characteristic (authors, unpublished results). Introgression from the allochthonous but currently sympatric U. pumila has been discarded by genotyping and admixture analysis (authors, unpublished results). A speculative explanation could be admixture between different intraspecific gene pools, leading to increased genetic diversity. Clone V‐AD2 genet origin is in the drainage divide between Mediterranean and Atlantic basins, that has been found to be an effective genetic barrier for other riparian trees (Macaya‐Sanz et al., 2012), and therefore a possible admixture zone.

Genome evolution: Phylogenetic signatures, richness of repetitive elements, and possible segmental duplications in subgenus Ulmus

Synteny within the clade shared by Z. jujuba, H. lupulus, C. sativa, and U. minor, roughly concurred with the already known phylogenetic relationships (Zhang et al., 2011). In disagreement with phylogeny, Ulmus genomes displayed more resemblance to Z. jujuba (Rhamnaceae) than to the two more closely related species in Cannabaceae, pointing to a faster rate of genomic evolution in the latter (Figure 5). Besides the currently undetermined and difficult‐to‐infer differences in selective pressures, including domestication of C. sativa and H. lupulus, shorter generation times of these two herbaceous species than of trees (Z. jujuba and U. minor) could underlie these results.

The analysis of repetitive content uncovered that the U. minor genome was highly enriched by nonredundant repeats (81.45%). U. minor genome also exhibited a higher proportion of segmental/WGD‐like duplications (18.65%) compared to U. glabra (5.84%) and U. parvifolia (7.11%). However, synteny analysis (Figure 1B) revealed that most duplicated blocks were located within the same chromosome—unlike the conserved interchromosomal patterns typically observed after WGD, like, for example, the reported in Malus (Daccord et al., 2017). 4DTv and Ks distributions showed sharp peaks near zero across all Ulmus species, consistent with recent local duplications rather than genome‐wide events. Phylogenomic analysis with Tree2GD initially suggested a high gene duplication ratio for U. minor (44.7%), but this value dropped to 8.07% when U. glabra and U. parvifolia were included, indicating that most of the duplicated gene families are shared among genera, rather than species‐specific. With the evidence currently at hand, the considerable higher genome size of U. minor compared to subgenus Oreoptelea (∼4.1 pg/2C vs. ∼3.1 pg/2C in Oreoptelea; Whittemore & Xia, 2017) might be a result from a combination of nonredundant repeat accumulation and scattered segmental duplications. Nevertheless, this hypothesis should be confirmed in future targeted studies.

The length of genomes assemblies within subgenus Ulmus was moderately different, being U. minor around 2.1 Gb, and U. glabra and U. parvifolia closer to 1.8 Gb. This discrepancy is likely due not only to biological differences, reflected in flow cytometry estimates sizes of 4.1, 4.0, and 3.9 pg/2C, respectively (Whittemore & Xia, 2017), but also to differences in technical approaches. On the one hand, the elevated BUSCO duplication rate in U. minor (21.6%) in comparison with U. glabra and U. parvifolia (4.2% and 2.1%, respectively; Table S18) appeared to result from the partial retention of redundant haplotigs in unplaced scaffolds. When we ran BUSCO on U. minor using only the 14 chromosome‐level scaffolds, the duplication rate decreased to 14.6%, but missing genes increased from 1.5% to 4.5%, suggesting that unplaced scaffolds harbored both redundant haplotigs and genuine gene content. Additionally, read coverage analysis identified 2551 10‐kb windows with ∼½X coverage in unplaced scaffolds (whereas only 609 in chromosomes), consistent with uncollapsed haplotigs. Inclusion of uncollapsed haplotigs would inflate both genome size and duplicated gene content, and potentially overestimating the extent of segmental duplications mentioned above. On the other hand, the chromosome assemblies of U. glabra and U. parvifolia may underrepresent actual genome lengths. Both assemblies contain unplaced scaffolds, though their total sizes are relatively modest (38 and 103 Mb, respectively). However, some of the most pronounced interspecific differences in chromosomal length (as displayed in Figure 6) may correspond to chromosomes where fragmented or missing regions were not successfully integrated. More importantly, certain genomic regions may not have been incorporated into the assemblies at all—neither as scaffolds nor as anchored sequences—potentially explaining the deviation from haploid genome sizes estimated by flow cytometry. Both assemblies are under 1.8 Gb (including scaffolds), with the U. glabra and U. parvifolia assemblies falling approximately 150 and 100 Mb short of their respective flow cytometry estimates. This discrepancy is likely due to the collapse or omission of repetitive, gene‐rich regions, which is a common challenge in large, repeat‐rich plant genomes. In conclusion, differences in the stringency of the approaches probably biased the three subgenus Ulmus genome assemblies in opposite directions, resulting from a combination of residual redundancy in U. minor and incomplete chromosomal reconstruction in U. glabra and U. parvifolia.

Syntenic R gene hotspots across genomes in subgenus Ulmus with traces of divergence

Our analyses revealed an uneven distribution of R genes within genomes but conserved across the three Ulmus species, with regions of low abundance interspersed with R‐gene‐rich hotspots. These hotspots exhibited synteny, although not all were consistently statistically significant across species. U. minor had the highest total number of R genes in assembled chromosomes (n = 1314), followed by U. glabra (n = 1176), with U. pumila trailing behind (n = 1099). Differences in the cumulative sizes of assembled chromosomes between species, as previously discussed, may partly explain the variation in R gene numbers. However, even after accounting for this factor, the species still show differences in R gene densities, following the same trend. We can conclude that R gene hotspots were surely present in their common ancestor and evolved through gains and losses of elements over time. Clustering of genes is uncommon in eukaryotic genomes but, conversely, is frequent in plant R genes (van Wersch & Li, 2019). Modern plant taxa benefit from these genomic arrangements, increasing the defense spectrum against pathogens due to synchronous activation. The variability in R gene copy numbers among the Ulmus species indicates distinct evolutionary trajectories under pathogen pressure, alike in animal immune systems (Nei & Rooney, 2005).

The number of R genes can greatly vary among related species and does not seem to correlate with major genomic events (expansion or polyploidization) (Borrelli et al., 2018), but with fine‐scale rearrangements driven by pathogen pressure, differentiating even ecotypes (van Wersch & Li, 2019). Of them, tandem duplications are the norm, especially for clusters of closely related R genes (Leister, 2004; Yi & Richards, 2007), but ectopic and segments duplications sometimes also operate the process. Our results about the type of duplication events shaping evolution of R genes in Ulmus concur with these previous studies, showing that most duplications (∼75%) are short range (either tandem or proximal). Although segmental duplications are present, they account for only about 6.5% in U. minor and even less in U. glabra and U. parvifolia. The profusion of tandem duplications and the clumped genomic arrangement of R genes provide a plausible explanation about the mechanisms underlying R gene cluster formation and expansion.

The richness of these arrays in U. minor is actually uncorrelated with resistance to DED, as only U. parvifolia shows species‐wide resistance, likely due to its coevolution with O. ulmi, which probably originated in East Asia (Masuya et al., 2010). U. minor and U. glabra may have experienced shorter periods of selective pressure, potentially influenced by geographic and climatic factors, leading to R gene expansions through processes such as duplication events (Leister, 2004). In contrast, U. parvifolia appears to have reached a more stable and optimal R gene copy number after prolonged coevolution with pathogens, avoiding the metabolic cost of maintaining excessive gene duplications (Ashfield et al., 2012; Shao et al., 2016).

We also encountered an uneven, clustered genome‐wide distribution of other genes related to DED resistance in the Ulmus genome, in particular those involved in defense chemical compounds, in plant–pathogen interaction proteins, and, to a lesser extent, in constitutive plant anatomy and phenology. DED resistance is influenced not only by molecular responses mediated by R genes, but also by constitutive anatomical traits and inducible physiological and chemical traits, all of which contribute quantitatively to limit pathogen damage (Büchel et al., 2016). For example, xylem vessel diameter is believed to strongly affect dispersion of the pathogen within the plant (Domínguez et al., 2024; Martin et al., 2021; Venturas et al., 2014). As shown here, clustered distribution patterns are common among many categories of genes involved in DED resistance, yet not among all. Genes associated with induced responses appear to be more evenly dispersed across the genome. All these categories of genes likely possess elements linked to overall resistance and should not be overlooked in future studies, especially those focused on breeding.

To better understand the mechanisms underlying DED resistance, comprehensive analyses comparing the sequences and structures of these clusters of R genes and other genic categories between resistant and susceptible genotypes are essential. These studies could reveal the dynamics of gene expansions and contractions and their association with resistance phenotypes. Additionally, hotspots of genes identified across species could serve as targets in genomic association studies such as GWAS or quantitative trait loci analyses, improving statistical power. These regions could also be tested in genomic prediction models using approaches such as weighted genomic best linear unbiased prediction to predict resistance‐related traits more accurately (Kainer et al., 2018).

CONCLUSION

Given the escalating threat posed by novel and more aggressive plant–pathogen interactions driven by global change, forest managers require innovative tools to respond swiftly and effectively. Deep exploration of molecular and genetic resistance mechanisms in elms will enhance our capacity to combat devastating diseases like DED. Integrating genomic insights into disease biology, coupled with conservation and management strategies, will be critical to maintain the resilience of forest ecosystems against invasive pathogens. Genomic assemblies and genomic arrangement studies, like the one presented here, will lay as foundation for these advances.
