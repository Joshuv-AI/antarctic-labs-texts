Augmenting fact and date of death in electronic health records using internet media sources: a validation study from two large healthcare systems
Authors: Michele LeNoue-Newton, Mohammed Al-Garadi, Kerry Ngan, Haritha Pillai, Ruth M Reeves, Daniel Park, Dax M Westerman, José J Hernández-Muñoz, Xi Wang, Aida Kuzucan, Shirley V Wang, Kueiyu Joshua Lin, Candace C Fuller, Melissa McPheeters, Michael E Matheny, Rishi J Desai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated death ascertainment from publicly available internet sources for patients in two large tertiary care US healthcare systems, Mass General Brigham (MGB) and Vanderbilt University Medical Center (VUMC), benchmarked against state and federal vital statistics data. Names, dates of birth, and dates of death were extracted from 8.1 million internet media records using previously developed natural language processing models. Internet records were matched to 78 848 deceased patients from MGB and VUMC on first name, last name, and date of birth. Dates of death were validated against state vital statistics databases or the National Death Index as reference standards. We calculated sensitivity and positive predicted values (PPV) of internet sources in identifying dates of death within 7 days of the reference standard. Exact matching of records between internet media and reference standards on first name, last name, and date of birth, resulted in 30 067 (38.8%) matches, which showed PPV for death identification (98.2%—MGB; 98.9%—VUMC) in internet media and increased sensitivity of death capture over EHR alone by 24% at MGB and 18% at VUMC. In conclusion, using internet sources to augment mortality data increased capture of death meaningfully over reliance on EHR records alone.Keywords: mortality rate, observational studies, sensitivity analysis, vital status, internet sources

Introduction

In safety and effectiveness evaluations of medications, mortality is an important outcome of interest to researchers, clinicians, and patients. However, information regarding deaths that occur outside of a clinical setting are frequently missing in real-world data sources such as electronic health records (EHRs) or insurance claims.1-3 Under-ascertainment of mortality can lead to bias in studies aimed at evaluating effectiveness or harms of given treatments or conditions.3-8 Thus, substantial effort has gone into identifying alternate sources for death data to augment information available in claims or EHRs, by linking these data to external sources of information.3,9-11

In the United States, deaths are tracked at the state and national levels through vital statistics registries.12,13 Reporting into electronic death registries occurs at the state-level usually within 24 to 48 hours, but the data do not become available for public use for 1-2 years. Currently, these data are not routinely linked between state death registries and EHRs or claims data. Several all-cause death databases exist, including the Social Security Death Index (SSDI), Census-Numident and CenSoc,14,15 the National Death Index (NDI), Veteran Administration data, and Center for Medicare Services (CMS) data16 but all have specific biases, delays in release, or are not readily available to researchers. CenSoc, Census-Numident, and SSDI data skew towards older populations and only contain individuals with social security numbers (SSN), which can lead to bias in racial-ethnic distributions.14,15,17 Missingness in SSDI has been exacerbated by regulatory changes made to the national SSDI in 2011 that prevents significant portions of state vital statistics data from being included.18,19 NDI remains a highly accurate source of information but has significant time delays, is prohibitively expensive for large-scale research studies, and has some bias in linkage towards individuals with SSNs.20,21

Internet based resources have been used for public health surveillance, including pharmacovigilance studies of adverse events, but generally on a population rather than an individual level.22-27 There are several publicly available data sources that could potentially be leveraged to augment death ascertainment. While cause of death information is largely unavailable outside of narrative notes, death certificates, and autopsy reports, date of death has the potential to be detected through several mechanisms. Obituaries and memorial websites offer near real-time updates on vital status,26,28-30 especially if posted electronically where they can be easily accessed and processed using natural language processing (NLP) tools. Other sources of social media information may also provide indications that an individual has died, including GoFundMe and similar sites on which family members or friends may share information.

Several prior studies used online obituary data to extract fact and date of death10,11,29-31; however, all of these studies focused on cohorts of patients with specific diagnoses, for instance cancer or scleroderma. Prior studies did not explore sources such as memorial websites and other social media in addition to obituaries. To that end, we aimed to provide health system level validation of death information retrieved using previously developed NLP models from various online sources. We linked NLP derived death information from online data sources to EHRs from two large tertiary care medical centers, Mass General Brigham (MGB) and Vanderbilt University Medical Center (VUMC), using probabilistic linkage methodology and benchmarked against >78 000 deaths identified from state and Federal vital statistics data as the gold standard information.

Methods

Study design

This multi-institutional study was conducted on patient populations from two large tertiary care medical centers: Mass General Brigham and Vanderbilt University Medical Center. Study populations between the two sites varied due to different operational constraints and available data resources. Mass General Brigham (MGB) utilized a linked EHR-Medicare Claims database and received access to three state vital statistics database (MA, VT, CT) to serve as the gold-standard for date of death. The MGB study population was limited to patients with at least one inpatient or outpatient visit between October 1, 2015 and January 1, 2020 and death date between October 1, 2015 and December 31, 2020 (Figure 1A and C). Vanderbilt University Medical Center (VUMC) is a comprehensive academic medical center located in Nashville, TN with nearly 2 million patient visits and 600 000 inpatient discharges annually. VUMC derived its cohort from an EHR-based research derivative and limited it to patients with at least one outpatient or inpatient visit between October 1, 2015 and January 1, 2020 and a last date of contact between January 1, 2019 and December 31, 2021 (Figure 1B). VUMC used a stratified random sampling of patients from its eligible population for submission to NDI to establish a “gold-standard” reference cohort (Figure 1B and C). The NDI submission process requires separate submission of patients known to be deceased from those with an unknown vital status and requires stratification of unknown patients on the year in which to start a search for records. Due to this process and the costs involved for NDI data, VUMC used a narrower final inclusion window and balanced maximizing patient inclusion with obtaining an adequate number of deceased patients for analysis and additional studies.

Data sources

Internet media data

Decedent names, birth dates, and dates of death were extracted from publicly available internet media posts within the United States between 2015 and 2022 from Obituary.com, GoFundMe, Everloved/TributeArchives. Data were compiled into a database to facilitate linkage with patient populations for analysis by both MGB and VUMC (Figure 2A). Data were extracted either from structured metadata or by using an XGBoost transformer model trained based on manual annotations of 3150 documents (1050 from each source) by trained nurse annotators. Details on the training and models for extraction of mortality data from social media are described elsewhere.32 The majority of 8 M+ social media records were derived from Obituary.com (91%) with some records from Everloved/TributeArchives (9%), a small amount from GoFundMe (<1%) (Figure 2B). All records in the final social media cohort were required to have complete entries for first name, last name, date of birth, and date of death, with no missing data.

Public internet media data. A) Internet media sources data extraction methods and B) distribution of records from specific sources.

MGB reference cohort

The MGB reference cohort consisted of 65 139 deceased individuals from an MGB EHR-Medicare claims linked data asset. All of these deaths were recorded between January 1, 2015, and Dec 31, 2020, in one of 3 state vital statistic database (MA, CT, VT) (Figure 1C). Demographics for the MGB reference cohort were 7.0% non-white, 93.5% > =65, and 51.1% male.

VUMC reference cohort

NDI submission cohort selection

It was not feasible to submit all VUMC patients within the eligibility cohort of individuals (over 1 M patients) to search for deaths records in NDI due to cost consideration, we submitted a subsample to NDI to obtain a “gold-standard” reference for deaths and allow for estimation across the larger cohort. Patient were initially stratified by availability of a recorded death date and year of last known contact in accordance with NDI submission protocols. We limited submission to those with last known contact dates or recorded death dates between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2021, and to patients with at least one encounter in the year prior to the last date of contact. Over 300 000 patients met these criteria, from these we selected a subsample of 35 015 individuals (Figure 1B). As the vast majority of patients of unknown status within the EHR were not expected to have a match in NDI, patients with recorded deaths were oversampled to ensure an adequate sample size of deceased patients for analysis of sensitivity and accuracy of date of death extraction, as well as for related studies using the cohort related to cause of death prediction. The subsample submitted to NDI was older, less racially diverse, and skewed slightly more male than the total patient population. (Table S1).

NDI matching process and final cohort determination

Of the 35, 015 patients submitted, 12 395 patients had a recorded death within the VUMC research derivative, while the remaining 22 620 patients had unknown vital status. Patients with unknown vital status had dates of last contact distributed roughly equally across the 3 submission years with approximately 7500 per year. Patients were selected randomly from each stratification grouping. NDI provided potential matches with probability scores, match category, and matched criteria from the national registry. We selected the highest probability match for each patient for assessment. We grouped potential matches based on the data elements matched (SSN, first name, last name, sex, and date of birth) and evaluated each group on concordance with recorded dates of death to establish deterministic match criteria resulting in the highest precision and recall. The following criteria were used to determine the final match cohort. Matches with a full SSN match and mismatches on a combination of two of the following elements: first name, last name, day of birth, month of birth, or year of birth. If DOB was the only mismatch, all 3 elements could be mismatched given that year of birth was within 10 years. If SSN was not a match, then all of the following elements needed to match exactly: first name, last name, and DOB. Determination of the final matched cohort aligned criteria between institutions and established similar criteria for matching between NDI and EHR as used for social media to EHR (Figure 1C).

Data linkage and validation

Linkage of internet media records to EHR records

Both MGB and VUMC utilized SPLINK33 to determine probabilistic matches between internet media records and their reference cohort. First name, last name, date of birth, and state of residence were used to match patient records between these sources. The algorithm was trained independently at each site with the full social media dataset and each institution’s reference cohort using identical parameters. The training determined weights for each variable and allowed for calculation of matching weights and probabilities for all matches within the dataset. Initial threshold for potential matching was set as a probability of 0.80, and the highest probability match for each reference patient was selected as the best match for further analysis.

Validating linkage of internet media records to EHR records

We manually reviewed the potential matches identified using the probabilistic matching algorithm for validity. We used stratified random sampling of three hundred matches grouped by probability scores separately at each institution. Accuracy for the probabilistic matching algorithm was defined as the proportion of records predicted by the algorithm as matches that were confirmed by manual reviewers. For the initial round of validation, we limited review to data elements available to the match algorithm and assessed whether the match was potentially the correct patient. The following criteria were used in the manual review for determination of a potential match: (1) Exact first name with DOB within 1-5 days, (2) Exact match on both an uncommon first name and uncommon last name with different DOB (eg, common names like John Smith would not match if DOB was not a match), (3) manual reviewers were free to use their discretion to account for nicknames (eg, Sue instead of Susan), typos, and misspellings. Determination of uncommon first and last names was informed by lists available from the US Census Bureau34 and the Social Security Administration.35 Given names not in the Top 1000 for any year or state and last names with a prevalence less than 10 per 100 000 in the 2010 Census were considered uncommon.

Next, we calculated positive predicted values (PPVs) using R36 based on alignment of date of death derived from the internet media sources and the date of death from the reference standard for each match. For these calculations, we considered cases where dates of death from internet media sources were within 7 days of the reference standard to be true positive cases. We also reported results based on exact match of date of death with the reference standard.

Ascertainment of augmentation

Augmentation rate for death ascertainment was defined by the increase in percentage of deaths identified by using social media compared to the percentage of deaths identified using the primary reference data source (EHR or EHR-Medicare Claims databases) alone. At MGB augmentation was calculated by reporting the sensitivity of capture of deaths from internet media records and EHRs as a proportion of total deaths identified in the reference cohort. To calculate comparable metrics at VUMC required adjustment for the oversampling of known deaths in the NDI reference cohort. Augmentation of death data by social media at VUMC required (1) determination of the percentage of patients with unknown vital status submitted to NDI with a matched death record, (2) identification of number of EHR recorded deaths, (3) estimation of total deaths, and (4) estimation of the number expected to have social media matches. To estimate unrecorded deaths within the VUMC 2019-2021 patient population, we multiplied the number of patients with unknown status by the percentage of unknown patients submitted to NDI with identified matches. We then calculated the sensitivity of deaths captured within the EHR (recorded deaths/total estimated deaths). To estimate the number of deaths potentially identifiable in social media from the VUMC 2019-2021 patient population, we multiplied the percentage of patients within the VUMC reference cohort identified in social media by the total number of deaths. We then used these figures to calculate the potential sensitivity of mortality capture using social media.

Validation results for linkage of internet media records to EHR records

Potential matches were stratified by match probability and manually reviewed to determine if likelihood of being a true match with an emphasis on reviewing matches ranked as non-exact matches. Both institutions found that matches with high probability (>0.99998) matched on names and DOB were deemed a “true” match, but that matches with lower probability only met the criteria for a “true” match in less than 50% of the reviewed pairs (Table 3). This is even with broad definition of match which only required first name match and DOB within 5 days or an uncommon full name match. The results indicated that to reduce levels of false positive matches, the match criteria required a full name and DOB match between social media and the EHR.

Predicted matches from the probabilistic matching procedure that matched exactly on the first name, last name, and date of birth showed PPV of 94%-99%. Predicted matches that did not exactly match on these variables showed PPV of <6%, suggesting that these matches were likely inaccurate matches. These values did not differ significantly amongst different demographic subgroups at MGB. PPV of online media was slightly lower amongst non-whites, women, and younger patients for exact date of death within the VUMC cohort (Table S2).

Augmentation and evaluation of death data

For analysis of augmentation of death both VUMC and MGB limited the social media matched cohort to those with exact matches on first name, last name, and date of birth, as these showed a high PPV value indicating low rates of inaccurate matches.

Augmentation of death data at MGB

Amongst the 65 139 deaths in the MGB reference cohort, the sensitivity of capture was 33% for EHR alone (n = 21 595) and 37% for internet media records (n = 24 228) when using exact match. Overall, internet media records using exact matching identified an additional 15 661 deaths compared to the EHR alone, increasing the sensitivity from 33% to 57% (Figure 3).

Augmentation of death data at VUMC

Within our reference cohort of 13 709 patients, 11 934 (87%) had a recorded date of death and 1775 (12.9%) had an unknown vital status in EHR records (Figure 1B). Of those with recorded deaths, 5629/11934 (47.2%) had internet media records and within the 1775 patients with unknown vital status 750 (42.3%) had matches in social media, increasing the capture of deaths from 87.1% to 92.5% in the reference cohort (Figure S1A and B).

As mentioned, the sample VUMC submitted to NDI was skewed for known deaths with 35% of the submission having recorded dates of the death within the EHR compared to 9.5% seen for the overall EHR cohort with matching selection criteria, a 3.6× enrichment. Given that the submission and subsequent reference cohort was not a random representative sample, we extrapolated the rate of augmentation in our total VUMC 2019-2021population based on the NDI results for comparison to MGB. To extrapolate the rate of augmentation, we used three data points (1) number of deaths recorded in the EHR, (2) estimate of the total deaths, and (3) estimate of the number of patients in social media. We found 29 223 patients with an EHR death record on the 2019-2021 VUMC cohort. To estimate unrecorded deaths, we used the rate of death amongst patients within unknown vital status in the NDI submitted cohort, 7.8%, multiplied by the total number of patients with unknown vital status to get 21 729 unrecorded deaths. This yielded an estimated total of 50, 952 deaths within the 2019-2021 VUMC patient population (Figure S1A and C). Based on that estimate and the actual recorded deaths found in the EHR, we calculated that 57.3% (29 223/50.952) of the 2019-2021 VUMC deaths were captured within the EHR. Using the rates of patients identified in social media from the reference cohort, we calculated the 22 984 potential VUMC patients in social media (13, 793 with recorded EHR death and 9191 with unknown status) (Figure S1D).Using these numbers increased the capture of deaths from 57.3% in the EHR alone to 75.4%, an 18% increase, comparable to the 24% increase at MGB (Figure 3).

Discussion

In this validation study conducted across two large healthcare systems from the US, we observed substantial missing data in the recording of deaths within EHRs. We further observed that leveraging NLP models to extract death information from publicly available internet media platforms can accurately identify an additional 18%-24% of deaths not captured in EHRs.

Mortality is a key outcome in many observational studies, making capture of accurate death information of high importance. Many observational research studies rely upon data collected during the course of standard clinical care and available within a subject’s EHR record, but these sources generally have missing mortality information.3,9 Missing mortality information leads to overestimates of survival with increasing levels of missingness relating to increased bias in survival estimates,3,5,37 with even small levels of missing data potentially having significant impact when assessing long-term survival and prevalence.38 Low sensitivity of mortality data must be addressed for data to be considered fit for purpose.4,5,8,39 Any study for which all-cause mortality is an outcome and where there is incomplete assessment of vital status amongst the patient population could benefit from augmentation of death data using online data sources. The greatest potential benefit is likely in studies requiring more real-time acquisition of data, such as active surveillance studies, or large population health studies where acquisition of vital status records from official reference sources may be cost-prohibitive. If specific cause of death outcomes are desired then online data sources have more limited utility as the most common online source for fact of death (Obituary.com) has limited information on cause of death.32

Standard sources for death data outside of the EHR include state vital statistics departments and NDI. NDI is often considered a “gold-standard” source for death information, but can face about 2 years lag before death data are made available.18 For state vital statistics, depending on the study question, investigators may need to petition for access to multiple state registries and some gaps in coverage may still exist if patients move between states. For these reasons, death information extracted using NLP from publicly available internet sources such as obituaries, memorial archives, and other social media sharing sites, like GoFundMe, may be a useful supplement to EHR recorded death information. These publicly available online media sources generally have minimal delays on the order of days to weeks compared to the 1-2 year delays for official data sources.30 Given this, these methods could be applied to more recent death data to augment dates of death in studies in close to real-time. To support others in utilizing these models, we made our NLP models publicly available.40

Prior studies attempted to use social media data to extract fact of death, but these primarily focused on obituary data in small cohorts.10,11,29 Our study expands on the literature by providing by far the largest validation of death information extracted from online sources including obituaries, memorial websites, and other social media, against gold standard information from state vital files or NDI. Importantly, our findings are likely more generalizable than prior studies as we conducted a population-based validation using deaths from two large healthcare systems in the United States without restricting to any specific subpopulation and our study populations between the two sites varied in selection methodology while obtaining similar results.

There are some caveats to the use of internet media sources for death ascertainment. Our results indicate that to avoid false positives, high levels of stringency need to be used to determine matches, in line with prior work.29,41 We acknowledge that even after successful extraction of information from social media and linkage with EHRs, the sensitivity of mortality capture remains suboptimal. We also note that MGB and VUMC cohorts had different selection criteria and source populations thus complicating direct comparison. MGB data were derived from a linked EHR-Medicare Claims database, whereas VUMC data were from a broad EHR sample with no Medicare linkage. Additionally, the VUMC data oversampled patients with EHR recorded deaths, which may not be representative of the broader patient population. Even with these differences both groups showed similar abilities of social media to increase the rate of death ascertainment within a cohort. Little literature exists on possible bias of using online memorial websites and obituaries for research purposes, but the possibility of non-representativeness in deaths recorded in internet media sources cannot be ruled out given the cost-related barriers associated with publishing obituaries and reliable internet access. Our results indicated similar racial, age, and gender demographics amongst those with online media records and reference cohorts, but other potential differences, such as urban vs rural population or socioeconomic status were not assessed. The study was unable to assess potential biases that may exist within the reference “gold-standards” due to the methods of data collection or matching algorithms.

Overall, internet media sources have the potential to increase coverage of mortality outcomes data by 18%-24% over EHR records alone and obtain this information in near real-time. Internet media sources offer a potential solution for timely capture of mortality information without the need for release of any PHI data outside of institutional firewalls, as public social media data can be scraped and brought into a secure environment for matching with EHRs.
