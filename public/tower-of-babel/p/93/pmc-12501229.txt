A comparative study on species composition and population dynamics characteristics of two Abies plants in the Pinaceae
Authors: Shanlin Yang, Xueying Yang, Renhang Zhang, Rongrong Cui, Kexin Sun, Yongchao Lv
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abies plants within the Pinaceae family represent foundational species of coniferous forests in the Northern Hemisphere. To investigate the ecological adaptability and limitations of plantation forests in urban settings, this study conducted a comparative analysis of species composition and population characteristics between Abies holophylla and Abies nephrolepis in Changchun City, employing TWINSPAN classification, dynamic population indices, static life tables, time-series models, and survival functions. Key findings include: (1) A. holophylla and A. nephrolepis communities were dominated by Pinus sylvestris and Betula platyphylla, respectively; (2) A. holophylla exhibited a fusiform age structure dominated by mature individuals, while A. nephrolepis displayed an inverse-J-shaped structure with predominantly juvenile individuals; (3) Both populations showed positive values for the intrinsic dynamic index (Vpi) and disturbance-mediated dynamic index (V’pi), indicating current growth trends; (4) Survival curves aligned with the Deevey-II, with mortality primarily driven by stochastic external disturbances. The convergence of Pmax toward zero revealed low resilience to environmental perturbations. This study reveals functional degradation in urban plantation ecosystems, demonstrating that both Abies populations face recruitment failure risks and exhibit weakened resilience to environmental disturbances. To address these challenges, targeted interventions-including artificial replanting, microhabitat reconstruction, and thinning of competing tree species to promote regeneration-are critically required. These findings offer theoretical underpinnings for adaptive management strategies in urban forest conservation.

Introduction

City forest parks have an obvious contributing role in purifying air quality, improving urban microclimate, creating urban leisure space, and enhancing the quality of life of urban residents1. As plant communities are important carriers of urban forest parks, plant community structure and species diversity are indicators reflecting the degree of variation in species composition, structure and dynamics within the community, and can assess key elements of its functional stability2,3. Species composition, as the basis for forming community structure, covers the variety and number of forest species and is the basis for recognizing the structure, ecology, and dynamics of plant communities, which can provide insights into the interactions between species, understand community stability and ecosystem functioning, reflect the degree of variation in species composition and structure and dynamics within plant communities, and parse ecological processes such as interrelationships and ecological niche allocation among species4–6. Research on community structure can reveal the degree of stabilization of the community, ecological characteristics and renewal of the population, and also has a guiding significance for the construction of plantation forest restoration models and the regulation of plantation forest community structure7,8.

Plant communities are built upon the foundation of populations. As a collection of individuals of the same species occupying a certain space at the same time, a population is the basic unit of a species’ existence in nature, assuming the key role of survival, reproduction and evolution of the species9. The dynamics of population structure and numbers is the core of population ecology research, including the age-class, size and number distribution of individuals within a population, reflecting the current status of the population and its natural survival potential10,11. The dynamics of a population shows the pattern of change in population size and number structure in time and space, and can further predict the trend of population development12,13. Reflecting the survival status of the population and the population’s adaptability to the environment through static life tables and dynamic indices can predict the future development trend of the population, understand the interactions within the population and between the population and the environment, and provide a scientific basis for the development of effective species conservation strategies14. Through intensive research on population structure and population dynamics, it can provide a strong scientific basis for the conservation of plant resources, and effectively plan the rational utilization of plant resources to ensure their sustainable development15.

Forest population dynamics are regulated by complex interactions between temporal population processes and spatial environmental gradients16. Traditional matrix models, while effective in projecting age-structured changes, often overlook spatiotemporal heterogeneities in mortality drivers-particularly for long-lived conifers like A. holophylla and A. nephrolepis. The population spatiotemporal sequence model can help us understand the diffusion, migration, and updating mechanisms of populations. Survival analysis studies the survival status and influencing factors of individuals or groups during a specific period of time, providing a scientific basis for evaluating the sustained survival ability of populations17,18. By combining these two methods, researchers can more comprehensively reveal the survival mechanisms, renewal patterns, and the impact of environmental factors on the development of populations, thereby providing scientific basis for population conservation, resource management, and sustainable development19,20.

Within this framework, the Abies genus constitutes a system of significant research value. Abies genus is a core establishment species of coniferous forests in the Northern Hemisphere, with rich understory vegetation diversity, high carbon sequestration, and a root system that is capable of effective soil sequestration and sensitive to low temperature and high humidity21–23. With obvious stratification phenomenon, including tree layer, shrub layer and herb layer, and developed moss layer in the understory, fir forests enrich biodiversity while playing an important role in ecosystem services such as soil and water conservation and carbon sink function24. As an important timber species in China, studying the population dynamics of Abies genus can provide a theoretical basis for optimizing forest management and harvesting strategies25. As a “sentinel species” of high-altitude ecosystems, Abies genus community dynamics directly reflect the 3 compound effects of climate change and human interference. Exploring the community characteristics and population dynamics of Abies genus can reveal the ecological adaptability of Pinaceae and support the formulation of ecological compensation policies.

This study focuses on two representative species within the Abies genus: A. holophylla and A. nephrolepis. With high adaptability to cold and wet climate and low temperature environment, A. holophylla significantly enhances forest carbon sink capacity through high biomass accumulation26,27. A. nephrolepis has a strong carbon sequestration capacity and can be used as an indicator species for the ecological effects of nitrogen deposition28,29. The research will investigate the A. holophylla and A. nephrolepis communities in the Nanhu Park area of Changchun City, Jilin Province, China. It aims to analyze their species composition and community dominance, characterize their community composition features and stability using TWINSPAN, and analyze the population dynamics of A. holophylla and A. nephrolepis through spatiotemporal sequence models and survival functions. The findings will provide a theoretical basis for regulating the community structure of Abies plantations in urban areas, including understory vegetation configuration, edge effect creation, thinning, and replanting. Based on the survey findings, the following hypotheses are proposed: (1) Low species richness in A. holophylla and A. nephrolepis communities due to the absence of shrub and herb layers; (2) Discontinuous age structures in A. holophylla and A. nephrolepis populations indicating potential decline risks; (3) Weak resistance to environmental disturbances in both fir populations likely stems from external pressures such as urban heat island effects.

Materials and methods

Study site

The study was conducted in Nanhu Park (41°51′04″ N, 125°18′23″ E) (Fig. 1), an urban forest ecosystem located in Changchun City, Jilin Province, northeastern China. The mean annual temperature in this region is approximately 5 °C, with an annual precipitation range of 500–700 mm. The average frost-free period lasts for approximately 140–150 d, and the maximum altitude is about 250 m above sea level. A comprehensive Analysis of the vegetation of Nanhu Park reveals the presence of over 580,000 plants belonging to 134 species. This vast array of plant life manifests in a discernible pattern of plant communities within the park, encompassing diverse categories such as coniferous forest communities, broad-leaved forest communities, and a mixture of both coniferous and broad-leaved forest communities. Further distinguishing this tapestry is the presence of open forest scrub communities, along with wetlands situated along lakeshores, collectively contributing to the park’s rich array of ecological plant communities. Nanhu Park was established in 1937. Both A. holophylla and A. nephrolepis were introduced as species during the initial park development phase. Having developed over 90 years, the investigated communities are located within designated conservation areas where human activities, forest thinning, and replanting are strictly prohibited. For the purpose of this study, prior authorization was obtained from the park administration permitting the survey activities. A. nephrolepis and A. holophylla are evergreen conifers and key protected plant species in China. Their populations are experiencing a gradual decline, primarily due to factors such as historical over-exploitation.

Sample plot

There are two types of plants, A. holophylla and A. nephrolepis. Four pure forest plots and eight mixed forest plots were selected for each plant, with an area of 20 m × 20 m. There are a total of 24 plots. The closure degree of the canopy within the sample plot is 61% −87%, with a mean ± standard deviation of 72.5% ± 7.6%.

TWINSPAN classification

The bidirectional indicator species analysis (TWINSPAN) method is modified based on indicator species analysis and can simultaneously complete species and plot classification30. The TWINSPAN principle is to first perform CA/RA sorting on the data, and obtain the first sorting axis for the types and plots, which are used for the classification of plot types31. The importance values of eight A. nephrolepis mixed forest samples and eight A. holophylla mixed forest samples were used to analyze the characteristics and stability of A. nephrolepis and A. holophylla communities in Nanhu Park32. The TWINSPAN and PCORD programs were utilized for two-way indicator species classification (TWINSPAN)33,34.

Population static life table

Using DBH instead of age to classify A. holophylla and A. nephropis is one of the methods for inferring age dynamics35. Trail-level groupings were used in place of age-classes for two populations of Abies. Based on Wang (2002) age-class classification criteria, a static life table was prepared and survival curves were plotted according to the method of Lin (2024)36,37. The survival curves of the populations were plotted using the age-classes corresponding to the trail levels as horizontal coordinates and the standardized survival numbers as vertical coordinates. In the actual analysis process, there is a negative mortality rate, which is inconsistent with the mathematical assumptions, so the data were processed using the even-slip technique. Survival curves depict age-specific survivorship patterns, with age-class on the x-axis and log-transformed standardized survivors [ln(lx)] on the y-axis38. Curve typology was assessed via the Hett & Loucks model39.

Survival analysis

To gain deeper insights into population dynamic patterns, this study introduces four key functions from survival analysis: Survival rate function (Sx), Cumulative mortality function (Fx), Mortality density function (fx), Hazard rate function (λx). These functions were calculated following the formulas established by Yang et al.(1991)42.

Data analysis

A comprehensive data Analysis was conducted to assess species composition, number, importance values, relative abundance, and relative dominance. This Analysis was performed using Excel 2019. Additionally, two-way indicator species classification (TWINSPAN) were performed with PCORD v5.0. Graphing was performed using Adobe Photoshop 2025 and Origin 2024.

Results

Comparison of community characteristics of two abies plants

Comparison of species composition of two abies plants communities

Interspecific comparisons showed higher taxonomic diversity in the Abies nephrolepis community (11 species, 6 families, 8 genera) than in Abies holophylla (10 species, 5 families, 7 genera), though both shared dominance by Pinaceae (4 species) and Aceraceae (2 species) among 13 total woody species (DBH ≥ 1 cm) (Table 1). Both communities showed pinaceae-dominated composition (40% in A. holophylla; 36.36% in A. nephrolepis).

In both communities, all 13 species exhibited importance values exceeding 1%. In A. holophylla community, A. holophylla had the highest importance value and relative abundance, while Pinus sylvestris showed the greatest basal area (DBH) and relative dominance, acting as the constructive and dominant species. In A. nephrolepis community, A. nephrolepis displayed the highest importance value and relative abundance, whereas Betula platyphylla dominated in basal area and relative dominance, serving as the constructive and dominant species (Table 2).

Discussion

Species composition is an important determinant of the nature of communities and an important way to understand the mechanisms that sustain ecosystems43,44. As the diversity of species composition affects the stability of the community and determines the ecological service function of the ecosystem, the study of the species composition of the community and the distribution characteristics of the dominant species can help to conserve biodiversity and maintain the stability of the ecosystem45. He (2021) and Zhou (2024) found Pinaceae to occupy the dominant species position in coniferous forest community studies46,47. Both Abies communities in this study were dominated by Pinaceae, with P. platyphylla and B. nephrolepis as the founding species of the A. holophylla and A. nephrolepis communities, respectively, and was replaced by key ecological niches, canopy shading may affect the growth of shrub and herb layers. In comparison with survey studies of forest communities in natural forests at the same latitude, such as secondary forests in the Changbai Mountains, the two Abies communities were not clearly stratified, with zero number of plants in the shrub and herb layers48. Comparing with studies on species composition of urban plantation forests such as Lanzhou Riverside Park49 the two Abies communities have fewer species families and lower community stability. While the Abies communities in the results of this study were ecologically stable, they were less so than forest communities in other parks, with Pinaceae plants playing a key role in maintaining the community structure of both Abies genera.

Height-class structure of a population reflects the spatial distributional characteristics and ecological niche differentiation of plants50,51. Research on the height structure of populations helps to understand plant growth strategies and community stability52. Population height structure of A. holophylla was fusiform, which was consistent with the findings of Yu (2019), indicating a declining population trend53. The height structure of A. nephrolepis population was inverted “J” shape, which was consistent with the results of the studies by Liu (2024), Zhu (2024), and Wu (2020), and the population had sufficient backup resources54–56. A majority of individuals in the A. holophylla and A. nephrolepis populations have already reached 15 m, indicating that these two species of Abies have strong growth capacity and competitiveness in their growing environments. Such structural features may be related to the growth characteristics and ecological needs of Abies plants, which typically have higher growth rates and greater photosynthetic capacity to capture more light resources in the taller canopy layers.

Age structure of plant populations can visualize the dynamics of the population and its adaptation to the environment57,58. Radial structure of xylophyta is closely related to their own ecological characteristics and external environmental competition59. Overall age-structural characteristics of the wild Shaniodendron subaequale population in the Dabie Mountains show a spindle-shaped age-class distribution pattern60. The age structure of the A. holophylla population in this study was nearly fusiform, lacking young individuals, and the population was weakly regenerated. Gui(2024) found that the age-class structure of Alseodaphne hainanensis population in primary and secondary forests was basically inverted “J” shape, and there was a problem of supplementation limitation in the process of transforming from seedlings to small trees61,62. The population of A. nephrolepis has the same age-class structure as that of natural Haloxylon ammodendron populations in the Lambuhe Desert63 with a strong capacity for population renewal, but only a small proportion of seedlings are able to make the transition to the juvenile stage successfully, which is a problem that leads to a weak foundation for maintaining the stability of the population. According to a previous study, a large number of young seedlings is the basis for the increase of individuals in each age-class, and the higher the proportion of young seedlings, the greater the potential for population growth64,65. Seedling replenishment of A. holophylla populations and low seedling-to-tree conversion rate of A. nephrolepis populations may lead to the transformation of two Abies populations into declining populations. By analyzing the vertical and age structures of the populations in this study, the future dynamics of the two Abies populations can be predicted, which can provide a scientific basis for the conservation and management of the populations. The absence of larger DBH individuals could stem from factors like the initial scarcity of large trees during planting, rather than solely implying poor survival as the quoted sentence suggests.

Analysis of population size dynamics can reflect the different dynamic features of the life history of a population and its interrelationships with the environment66,67. The analysis of the dynamic indices of population size showed that the dynamic indices (Vn) V2-V5 of A. holophylla populations between adjacent age-classes were less than 0, and the overall population size showed a fluctuating change of “growing-declining-growing”, and the V2 value was less than 0 for A. nephrolepis populations from age-class II to age-class III only. The V2 value of the stinking fir population was less than 0 only from age-class II to age-class III, and the number of individuals in the rest of the age-classes showed an increasing trend. It is evident that the A. nephrolepis population is the more stable of the two Abies populations. Quantitative Analysis of structural dynamics showed that both the population dynamics index and the random disturbance dynamics index were greater than 0, reflecting that the populations of the two Abies plants have the potential to grow under non-complete random disturbances, and are well adapted to the current growth environment of Nanhu Park in Changchun City. V’pi = 0.0138 for A. holophylla population and V’pi = 0.3348 for A. nephrolepis population, which were much smaller than the Vpi values, indicating that the dynamic indices of the age structure of the populations were unstable, and that the buffering capacity of the populations to the external disturbances was weak. Such instability may make populations more vulnerable to shocks in the face of environmental change or anthropogenic disturbances, thus affecting their long-term survival and reproduction68,69. The dynamic indices Vpi and V’pi are sensitive to sample size and age structure. In this study, both Abies plants have age-classes with Ax < 20 and age-class faults, which may lead to misjudgment of V’pi. Future research should incorporate long-term monitoring data to mitigate static sampling bias. Utilize time series prediction and survival function to assist analysis and avoid single indicator misjudgment.

As a key indicator for assessing the direction of plant population development, the static life table, in combination with the survival curve, reveals the current status and dynamic characteristics of the plant population and predicts the future growth trend of the plant population70. Life expectancy of A. holophylla and A. nephrolepis individuals was highest at age-class I and II, respectively, and showed a decreasing trend with increasing age-class, indicating that the individuals had high growth potential, which gradually decreased with increasing age-class. These results indicate stronger survival capacity in age-class I for A. holophylla and in age-class II for A. nephrolepis, with environmental adaptability declining as age-classes increased, leading to fewer individuals surviving to larger diameter classes. Survey data show that fewer young individuals of A. holophylla survived, which is consistent with the decline of the A. holophylla population between age-classes I-X. The survival curves of the two Abies populations in this study tended to be of Deevey-II, similar to the survival curves of the minimal population plant of Acer miaotaiense71,72 indicating that the two Abies populations were relatively stable to the environmental disturbances at each growth stage, and that individuals of all size classes of the population had relatively consistent mortality rates. The Deevey-II curve was proposed by Deevey (1947) and belongs to one of the three idealized survival curves. Its characteristic is that the mortality rate at each age stage is relatively constant, and the number of surviving individuals decreases approximately linearly with age, rather than sudden changes in early or late stages73. The population of A. holophylla needs to pay attention to the problem of replenishment of young individuals, and the population has a weak capacity for renewal and belongs to a declining type of population. A. nephrolepis population has more young individuals and has a certain capacity for renewal in the future, so it needs to replenish the loss of the number of individuals of larger diameter class in a timely manner.

The analysis integrating survival functions with time series forecasting models can reveal the mechanisms of population decline74. The primary mechanisms driving the decline of plant populations in urban plantations include habitat fragmentation, environmental changes, biotic homogenization, and alterations in microclimate and soil conditions, among other factors75. This study reveals differentiated decline mechanisms in two Abies populations within urban environments: time-series modeling projects localized declines in A. holophylla at age-classes II - III and A. nephrolepis at class II, while paradoxically, senile A. holophylla (> age-class VIII) and mid-aged A. nephrolepis (IV - VII) individuals exhibit counter-trend growth. This structural paradox-manifested as cumulative declines of 13.2% and 9.8% respectively-uncovers dual stressors in urban plantations: recruitment failure (impeded seedling replenishment) and resource competition (canopy dominance suppressing understory). Survival function analysis corroborates this mechanism: A. nephrolepis displays peak mortality density (> 40%) at class I and reaches demographic equilibrium as early as age-class V, reflecting a “high-risk/rapid-turnover” strategy; whereas A. holophylla peaks in hazard rate (λ = 0.467) at age-class V and delays equilibrium until age-class VIII, demonstrating “delayed-collapse” characteristics76–78. Such age-specific mortality patterns predict that A. nephrolepis enters decline 15–20 years earlier than A. holophylla, validating the universality of the stepwise collapse model (seedling deficit → resource competition → senile dependency) for urban conifers79,80.

Due to artificial introduction of fixed tree species, the shrub and herb layers are absent in the study area, resulting in reduction in niche diversity and weakening of the community’s vertical structure. This has led to loss of habitats for pollinators and seed dispersers. Concurrently, co-dominant tree species displace key ecological niches, intensifying interspecific competition. Their canopy shading may suppress the establishment of A. holophylla seedlings, while the rapid growth of Betula platyphylla likely competes for light resources. Consequently, the natural regeneration of A. holophylla is impeded, leaving the population dependent on existing mature trees. For A. nephrolepis, the transition rate from sapling to mature tree is low, exhibiting “demographic inertia” similar to desert vegetation. High visitor traffic in the park poses risks of tourism trampling suppressing seedlings. To address the challenges facing both fir populations, we advocate: Thinning competitive tree species (maintaining light transmittance above 45%) to create growth conditions for shrub/herb layers; Establishing disturbance buffer zones (over 100 m from roads); Blocking critical decline nodes (age-class I for A. nephrolepis/age-class V for A. holophylla).

Conclusion

This comparative study reveals significant functional degradation in Abies plantation ecosystems within urban environments, characterized by simplified biodiversity and Pinaceae dominance. While both populations exhibit positive intrinsic growth indices, critically low disturbance-response indices and near-zero resilience thresholds demonstrate severely compromised buffering capacity against anthropogenic pressures such as tourism trampling and urban heat island effects. The Deevey-II survival curves confirm mortality is primarily stochastic rather than age-dependent, aligning with external disturbance-driven collapse patterns observed in managed urban forests. Crucially, divergent decline mechanisms emerge: A. holophylla faces imminent recruitment failure due to suppressed regeneration, whereas A. nephrolepis exhibits a regeneration bottleneck resembling desert vegetation “demographic inertia”. These findings directly inform adaptive management-targeted replanting with microhabitat reconstruction for A. holophylla versus competitive thinning for A. nephrolepis - but underscore inherent limitations of static sampling in urban plantations. Future work must integrate longitudinal monitoring to validate time-series predictions, expand spatial replicates to address stability concerns, and experimentally test canopy-light interactions shaping understory regeneration. This study establishes a framework for conserving stress-sensitive conifers in rapidly urbanizing landscapes, though its localised context necessitates cautious extrapolation to natural forests.
