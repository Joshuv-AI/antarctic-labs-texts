Macroscopic modelling and analysis based on microscopic models for swarm systems
Authors: Quan Quan, Xinchen Yu, Yue Li, Guoyuan Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Swarm systems, whether biological or artificial, often exhibit higher efficiency, robustness, and flexibility than individual entities, enabling them to accomplish tasks that individuals cannot achieve. However, there is still no comprehensive and universal modelling and analysis methodology for swarm systems. This paper presents a bidirectional closed-loop modelling framework that combines microscopic models with macroscopic modelling. In this approach, a probabilistic finite state machine is first used to establish the microscopic model, and a macroscopic model is then constructed based on rate equations. Finally, the evolution of the macroscopic model provides feedback to refine the configuration of the microscopic model. Through a simulation case study of infectious disease transmission in a closed environment, the effectiveness of the macroscopic model is validated. Simulation results indicate that our method emphasizes the derivation process from microscopic to macroscopic modelling, overcoming the limitations of studies that focus solely on either the micro or macro level. The macroscopic model not only provides predictions of the long-term behavior of swarm systems but also facilitates optimization. Furthermore, optimization methods based on the macroscopic model can enhance system performance and decision-making efficiency without increasing system complexity. Our research offers a novel perspective on swarm system modelling and analysis, providing important theoretical support for the analysis and optimization of swarm behaviors in practical applications.

Introduction

Inspired by the collective behavior in nature, such as bird migration, ant colony foraging, bee nesting, and wolf hunting, artificial swarm systems have been developed. It is important to note that swarm systems, whether biological or artificial, do not seem to have a clear definition in terms of size. They are usually defined by their behavior, meaning that even a group of three members can show a collective behavior1. Similar to biological swarms, artificial swarm systems, which are mainly based on swarm robots, have the following five characteristics2: (i) individuals can move freely within the system; (ii) individuals can perceive and modify their environment; (iii) individuals have relatively simple functions, localized interaction range, and limited interaction capabilities; (iv) there is no centralized control within the system, and individuals do not have global information about the system; (v) individuals accomplish collective tasks through cooperation. These characteristics give swarm systems advantages such as robustness, flexibility, and scalability1,3,4. Therefore, studying swarm systems is of great interest from an engineering perspective because they can usually exhibit powerful capabilities that are not available to individuals and can accomplish complex tasks that individuals cannot.

Modelling and analyzing swarm systems typically involves two levels: the micro level and the macro level. The micro level corresponds to the microscopic model of individuals in the group, focusing on individual behavior within the overall collective task. It models a local view dominated by limited information, limited visibility, and uncertainty. The macro level refers to the macroscopic model, which describes the characteristics of the entire group exhibiting collective behavior from a holistic perspective, typically modeled under a global view that assumes full knowledge and overview information1. Military command and control in modern warfare can be seen as an example of the micro and macro levels of swarm systems. At the macro level, the military command and control system focuses on strategic planning and global command. Military operations planning involves the allocation of global resources and the distribution of unified tasks. At the micro level, the military command and control system focuses on individual soldier control and real-time feedback. The system provides detailed instructions and tasks for each soldier, monitoring their status and location in real-time through various sensors and communication equipment to ensure accurate task performance. In addition, large-scale gaming games based on deep reinforcement learning currently also have micro and macro level behavioral explanations. At the macro level, this manifests in the macroscopic strategies adopted by opposing parties, where aggregate metrics describe system states and learning cost functions model evolutionary processes. At the micro level, each agent operates within the game environment from a strictly limited perspective, focusing solely on individual tactical execution, immediate survival, and local advantages.

Designing swarm systems at the micro level typically follows a bottom-up paradigm5. Within this framework, agent-based modelling (ABM) serves as the dominant approach. Here, the system is modeled as a collection of autonomous interacting individuals, and the emergent properties of the overall system are studied by simulating their individual behaviors and local interactions6–8. In concrete implementation, individual design methods can broadly be categorized into behavior-based design and automatic design. Behavior-based design typically includes virtual physical force (VPF) design and finite state machines (FSM) design. VPF is defined as a method that abstracts a group of individuals into physical entities and considers the interaction forces of repulsion, attraction, and alignment with others to achieve collective behavior9–13. The most typical application of VPF is the use of the artificial potential field (APF) method and its improved algorithms for obstacle avoidance and path planning of swarm robots9–11. FSM is a mathematical model that describes the finite states of an object and the behaviors, such as transitions and actions, between these states. If the transitions or actions between states are described by probabilities, it is called a probabilistic finite state machine (PFSM)14–16. Automatic design methods generate behaviors without explicit developer intervention, primarily divided into reinforcement learning and evolutionary robotics. Reinforcement learning endows individuals in a swarm with learning capabilities. Each agent optimizes its decision strategy through repeated trial-and-error interactions with the environment or other agents, based on acquired reward or punishment signals, to maximize long-term cumulative gains17,18. Evolutionary robotics methods, inspired by natural evolution, transform the design problem of individual behavior controllers into an iterative optimization problem for a population of solutions. It does not rely on gradient information but instead employs genetic operations such as selection, crossover, and mutation to optimize more reasonable individual behaviors19.

Unlike microscopic models, macroscopic models treat the swarm system as a unified whole, disregarding individual components and focusing instead on high-level systemic descriptions of the aggregate. Macroscopic design methods based on differential equations are typically categorized into rate equations and differential equations that consider individual spatiality. The rate equation is usually an ordinary differential equation (ODE) that uses the number or density of swarm individuals as state variables. This type of equation does not consider the spatial location of individuals and belongs to a non-spatial class of probabilistic models20–22. Differential equations that take spatiality into account are typically represented by stochastic differential equations (SDE) and partial differential equations (PDE)23–26. In addition, there are network models based on random graphs27,28, adaptive network models that allow dynamic updating of the topology29,30, etc. Several studies investigate the micro-macro linkages of swarm systems by modelling individual microscopic models using the Langevin equation and then deriving the corresponding Fokker-Planck equation to model the entire population1,2,22,23. Quantitative links between micro and macro have also been studied in the literature. Reina et al.31 obtained a quantitative correspondence between the dynamics of their macroscopic model and micro implementations in a study of honey bee nest selection.

Related works

In recent years, numerous interesting methodologies have emerged in the field of swarm modelling and control. The continuous control approach proposed by Nikitin et al. transforms discrete individual dynamics into continuous field evolution equations32. To be specific, for microscopic models based on ODEs or SDEs, this method approximates the state differences between nodes as spatial partial derivatives via Taylor series expansion. This yields a macroscopic PDE model describing collective system behavior, which is then used to design continuous control laws. These control actions are subsequently mapped to individual agents through discretization. Density control represents a typical application scenario within the continuous control framework. Di Lorenzo et al. constructed a macroscopic density equation based on convection-diffusion equations, employing a continuity-based density control strategy to address large-scale grazing problems33. In traffic flow research, microscopic following models were continuous into macroscopic PDEs for density and velocity, enabling analysis and control of density instability phenomena at the macroscopic level34. Maffettone et al. employed continuation methods to establish a leader-follower swarm control framework that bridges microscopic stochastic models to macroscopic PDEs, with macroscopic density control feedback applied to individual agents35.

Furthermore, to more accurately capture the heterogeneity and dynamic evolution of individual behavior within swarms, modelling frameworks incorporating internal state variables have emerged as a significant development in this field. Such models break away from traditional homogeneity assumptions, treating individuals not only as simple particles with mechanical states like position and velocity, but also as carriers of internal variables such as emotion, stress, risk perception, or specific task states36,37. These variables evolve through individual interactions and environmental stimuli, while simultaneously influencing movement decisions, thereby giving rise to more complex macroscopic dynamic behaviors at the swarm level. For instance, in crowd dynamics, internal variables can describe panic propagation, adversarial interactions, and evacuation behaviors under infectious disease risk perception38. In theoretical swarm models, internal variables have been shown to modulate emergent patterns of alignment and aggregation39. A key challenge in such modelling lies in maintaining consistency between the microscopic description of internal variables and macroscopic continuous models, while establishing effective cross-scale parameter mapping and calibration methods.

The active particle method provides a unified and rigorous mathematical implementation for this modelling framework. Originating from generalized dynamical systems theory, this approach describes systems as collections of numerous “active particles”36,40. Beyond mechanical state variables, each particle possesses one or more behavioral state variables termed “activities,” representing heterogeneous internal characteristics such as driving habits41, infection levels42, immunity status43, opinions, or social attributes. By establishing evolutionary equations for particle distribution functions and employing moment methods (e.g., density, mean velocity), hydrodynamic or reaction-diffusion equations describing the system’s macroscopic behavior can be derived. This framework has been successfully applied across multiple domains: in traffic flow modelling, congestion formation and dissipation are reproduced using discrete velocity models and lane-changing game tables41; in epidemic dynamics, active variables characterize the continuous processes of viral mutation and immune competition42; and in multiscale epidemic models, individual infection dynamics are coupled with societal intervention strategies43. These studies demonstrate that the active particle method can systematically characterize the emergence process from heterogeneous microscopic behavior to macroscopic collective phenomena, providing a viable mathematical tool for integrating adaptive behavior with external control.

This stems from the fact that most analyses consider only the macro or micro level of abstraction, or employ solely bottom-up or top-down unidirectional modelling and analysis, failing to combine the two approaches. Methods

Each individual of the swarm system exhibits specific behavior during task execution. If we consider each member as a point in a two-dimensional space, defined by its position and speed, then each individual can be seen as a 4-dimensional system. By modelling the behavior of each individual, the entire swarm system can be viewed as a complex system with high dimensionality. Swarm systems should consider both micro and macro modelling in the process of completing tasks, since swarm tasks are defined at the macro level of the whole group, and the controllers that control the actions of individuals are represented at the micro level. The purpose of macro modelling for a swarm system is to simplify and formalize the system by reducing its dimensionality. This involves abstracting away micro details without losing the overall characteristics of the system, resulting in a low-dimensional system that is easier to understand and analyze. This section outlines the process of modelling a general swarm system from micro to macro levels and provides the steps for the bidirectional closed-loop modelling framework for swarm systems.

Microscopic modelling

The microscopic model describes the behavior of each individual member of the group, including their movement and interaction with others and the environment. During swarm task execution, individuals may experience various states, and both external input events (manual intervention control) and internal spontaneous events (autonomous interaction) can cause individuals to switch between states. Individuals may have different controllers assigned to them in different states, and their dynamic models may also vary, resulting in distinct continuous dynamic properties in each state. Therefore, a single individual can be considered as a hybrid dynamic system consisting of “dynamic continuous subsystems” and “discrete subsystems” that interact with each other45.

Macroscopic modelling

The macroscopic model directly describes the collective behavior of the entire swarm system from a macro perspective, unlike the microscopic model. The swarm system is considered as a whole, and some details of individuals, such as action trajectories and specific actions, are ignored. Instead, behaviors that represent average quantities, such as the number or density of individuals in each discrete state, are modeled. In fact, macroscopic models exist only in a phenomenological sense. Nevertheless, they remain useful tools for analyzing swarm systems due to their use of a limited number of variables and computational efficiency.

The rate equation is the most commonly used method to describe macroscopic models. It was first used to represent the dynamics of chemical reactant concentration. In swarm robotics, the rate equation describes the evolution of the number of individual robots (or the proportion of the total number of robots) in a discrete state over time. Additionally, the rate equation offers a systematic approach for translating microscopic models into macroscopic models.

Bidirectional closed-loop modelling framework for swarm systems

Outline

Fig. 3Bidirectional closed-loop modelling framework for swarm systems.

Bidirectional closed-loop modelling framework for swarm systems.

The proposed bidirectional closed-loop modelling framework for swarm systems is illustrated in Fig. 3. Once the collective task of the swarm is defined, the expected behavior of individual agents is typically specified in advance. Consequently, a microscopic model – often formulated as a probabilistic finite state machine – is initially constructed using prior domain knowledge, and its parameters are assigned baseline values. Subsequently, the macroscopic model is derived following the methodology in the previous section. However, since the derivation process from micro to macroscopic model ignores the detailed information of the microscopic model, the initially obtained macroscopic model may not capture the actual collective dynamics with sufficient accuracy. Therefore, a parameter identification step is required to calibrate the macroscopic model against observed or simulated data. Once calibrated, the macroscopic model enables the prediction of the swarm’s long-term evolution. The reliability of task completion can then be evaluated from these predictions. If the predicted reliability is unsatisfactory, the parameters of the macroscopic model are optimized with respect to relevant performance indices. The optimized parameters are subsequently mapped back to the microscopic model, thereby guiding its adjustment and improvement.

Thus, the framework follows a closed-loop sequence: starting from a microscopic model, a macroscopic model is derived; this macroscopic model is then identified, predicted, and optimized; finally, the results are feedback to refine the microscopic implementation. In this context, the macroscopic model serves as an approximate, computationally efficient representation for system-level analysis and optimization, whereas the microscopic model operates at the individual-agent level. Moreover, the same bidirectional design principle can be applied not only to the swarm as a whole, but also to hierarchical or modular sub-swarm systems.

Data-based parameter identification

To enhance macroscopic model flexibility and accuracy, correction parameters are often introduced. These are collectively denoted by a parameter vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon }$$\end{document} within the macroscopic model. After acquiring experimental or simulation data of the swarm performing the target task, system identification techniques are employed to estimate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon }$$\end{document}. The primary objective is to minimize a suitable cost function – typically the discrepancy between the model output and the measured data – so that the macroscopic model reproduces the results of collective dynamics as closely as possible. Classical methods such as least-squares estimation or evolutionary algorithms (e.g., genetic algorithms) are commonly used for this purpose. Alternatively, data-driven approaches including neural networks can also be applied for parameter fitting. Although a single identification run may yield parameters that reflect the individual state transitions under specific environmental conditions, such results can be sensitive to noise or particular operational contexts. Therefore, it is advisable to perform identification over multiple independent data sets, thereby obtaining a parameter set that possesses stronger generalization capability in a probabilistic sense.

Model-based macroscopic prediction

With the structure and parameters of the macroscopic model determined, model-based prediction serves as a powerful tool to forecast the long-term spatiotemporal evolution of the swarm system. This predictive capability is of central importance for both analysis and design. It allows designers to proactively anticipate the collective trajectory, assess the probability of successful task completion, and evaluate whether desired global outcomes (e.g., consensus, coverage, or target-reaching) are likely to be achieved within specified time horizons. The prediction process itself follows a systematic procedure: after defining the initial macro-state and setting the prediction time horizon, the macroscopic dynamical equation are solved either analytically (if tractable) or, more commonly, via numerical integration. The output is a time series or a spatial-temporal profile of the macro-state variables, quantitatively describing the system’s future tendency. Moreover, sensitivity analyses can be conducted atop this predictive framework to understand how uncertainties in parameters or initial conditions propagate and affect the collective outcome. Thus, macroscopic prediction not only offers a foresight into system behavior but also establishes an essential computational foundation for the subsequent step of systematic parameter optimization.

Index-based model optimization

Once a reliable macroscopic model is established and its predictive capability is verified, the model’s parameters can be systematically optimized to enhance the swarm’s performance with respect to predefined, quantifiable task criteria. This index-based optimization is formally posed as a numerical optimization problem over the model’s parameter space. A critical step is the careful definition of an objective (or cost) function that comprehensively encapsulates the relevant performance indices of the swarm system. These indices may include, but are not limited to, task completion time, success probability, energy efficiency, resource consumption, or stability metrics. The mathematical formulation of this function—whether it is a weighted sum, a multi-objective Pareto front, or a constraint-satisfaction measure—significantly influences the optimization outcome; it must be designed to align with the top-level task specifications while avoiding physi­cally unrealistic or extreme behaviors during the predicted evolution. In addition, the selection of an appropriate optimization algorithm is paramount and should be guided by the problem’s characteristics, such as the dimensionality of the parameter space, the likely presence of multiple local optima, and the computational cost of each model evaluation. Suitable algorithms range from gradient-based methods (for smooth, convex landscapes) to heuristic global optimizers like genetic algorithms, particle swarm optimization, or Bayesian optimization (for complex, non-convex problems). The optimization process iteratively queries the macroscopic model, adjusting parameters to extremize the objective function, thereby yielding a parameter set that promises improved swarm performance at the system level.

Microdata-based model validation

While prediction and optimization are conducted at the abstract macroscopic level, the actual execution and success of the swarm task are ultimately grounded in the interactions and states of individual agents. Therefore, microdata-based validation phase is indispensable to close the design loop and ensure consistency across modelling scales. After obtaining the optimized parameters from the macroscopic stage, these parameters are translated back into the microscopic simulation program—for instance, by adjusting the transition probabilities in the probabilistic finite state machines or modifying local interaction rules. The updated microscopic model is then executed under a variety of independent initial conditions and environmental settings to generate multiple new sets of high-fidelity, individual-level data. The emergent collective behavior from these microscopic runs is quantitatively compared against two benchmarks: first, the trajectories predicted by the calibrated macroscopic model; and second, the performance targets defined during the optimization stage. Statistical measures—such as mean squared error, confidence intervals, or hypothesis tests—are employed to assess the agreement. Consistency across multiple independent microscopic simulations confirms the predictive credibility of the macroscopic abstraction and, crucially, validates the effectiveness of the entire bidirectional design process.

To visually demonstrate the scalability and general applicability of the proposed bidirectional closed-loop modelling framework, Fig. 4 provides a schematic illustration of its application across different hierarchical levels of system organization. The figure is not intended to present a universal taxonomy of modelling abstraction; rather, its core purpose is to elucidate a fundamental strength of our approach: the consistent, recursive application of the same micro-macro bidirectional paradigm to swarm systems with nested structures. As shown in Fig. 4, we consider a complex swarm system with inherent hierarchy, such as a national economic system composed of individual agents, cities, and the nation as a whole. The framework’s operation begins at the foundational individual level, where the behavior of each entity is defined by a PFSM, constituting the microscopic model. When a subswarm level (e.g., a single city) is treated as an analytical unit, the framework’s recursive nature becomes evident. We consider all individuals within that city as a subswarm and, based on the micro-level rules of its internal individuals, derive a macroscopic model describing the collective dynamics of this subswarm. This subpopulation macroscopic model efficiently captures the aggregated behavior at the city level. Crucially, this process can be recursively applied upward. At higher levels encompassing entire groups (e.g., the national level), we study the macro-level behavior evolving from changes in individual states within the group. Building upon this, we can again apply our framework to aggregate the norms of these individuals, thereby deriving a higher-level macroscopic model describing the dynamics of the entire national system.

Case study: Infectious disease transmission

Current research on modelling methods for swarm systems focuses on collective behaviors of swarm robotic systems, such as aggregation, dispersion, foraging, and collective construction. However, the majority of group behavior in swarm robots is pre-programmed for each individual robot, and then studied to understand its swarm behavior. This can result in swarm characteristics being limited to the pre-programmed behavior, making the final macroscopic model less universal. Moreover, programming each robot inevitably increases the workload and is too costly for larger-scale swarm robot systems.

This section provides an example of how the bidirectional closed-loop modelling framework of the swarm system can be validated using a special swarm system. The swarm system is a self-built simulation system that models the spread of infectious diseases in a small, closed environment, such as an outbreak in a school or factory.

This simulation environment is designed based on the characteristics of infectious disease transmission. It progresses through discrete time steps, with the health status of each individual dynamically changing according to predefined transmission rules and probability models. Ultimately, the system evolves into a specific transmission pattern. To effectively control the epidemic and alter unfavorable transmission patterns, it is necessary to embed and evaluate different policy interventions within the simulation to influence disease transmission. However, directly setting policy interventions within the simulation model is a highly computationally intensive “trial-and-error” process. Assessing the effectiveness of a single policy combination under different parameters or random individual settings requires multiple simulation runs, with computational costs increasing exponentially as the number of individuals grows. This massive computational burden makes it extremely challenging to achieve the real-time nature of micro-level simulations and the rapidity and optimization required for policy-making. There is an urgent need to use bidirectional closed-loop modelling methods to elucidate the connection between the microscopic and macroscopic of infectious disease models, thereby leveraging macroscopic models to guide policy exploration at the micro-level.

Model prediction and optimization based on macroscopic model of infectious diseases transmission

Model prediction analysis

To make macroscopic model predictions of infectious disease transmission more realistic, we introduce the main index that measures the transmission dynamics of infectious disease: the effective reproduction number \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\textrm{e}}(t)$$\end{document}. We have previously studied the effective reproduction number46, and in this paper, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\textrm{e}}(t)$$\end{document} is expressed as23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} R_{\textrm{e}}(t) = \frac{-\Delta N_{\textrm{S}}(t)}{\Delta N_{\textrm{C}}(t)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta N_{\textrm{S}}(t)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta N_{\textrm{C}}(t)$$\end{document} denote the amount of change in the susceptible population and confirmed cases at moment t, respectively. The differential equation corresponding to confirmed cases \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{C}}(t)$$\end{document} with Eq. (21) is24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\textrm{d} {N}_{\textrm{C}}(t)}{\textrm{d} {t}} = \frac{k_3}{\tau _{\textrm{sym}}} N_{\textrm{I}}(t) + k_4 \frac{p_{\textrm{det}}}{\tau _{\textrm{det}}} N_{\textrm{I}}(t) . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Typically \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\textrm{e}}> 0$$\end{document}, and the controllability of infections is measured by comparing the magnitude of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\textrm{e}}$$\end{document} with 1. The relationship \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\textrm{e}}> 1$$\end{document} means that there are more net new infectious cases than net new confirmed quarantine cases in the long run, leading to an uncontrollable epidemic with a significant impact on society. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\textrm{e}} = 1$$\end{document} signifies a balance between the net new infectious cases and the net new confirmed quarantine cases, allowing all new infectious cases to be placed in confirmed quarantine. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\textrm{e}} < 1$$\end{document} indicates that there are more net new confirmed quarantine cases than net new infectious cases, meaning that the current level of medical quarantine is sufficient to handle the spread of the infectious disease, and the epidemic will be under control.

Model optimization analysis

The previous subsection’s prediction analysis was conducted using the initial parameter settings in Table 1, which are not fixed. Parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{\textrm{rw}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\textrm{det}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{\textrm{det}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{\textrm{rec}}$$\end{document} can be interpreted as adjustable parameters. The model optimization analysis in this subsection is essentially optimizing the policymaking parameters in the model to achieve the preset performance index of the model.

We selected the period of detection \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{\textrm{det}}$$\end{document} as the parameter to be optimized. On one hand, this is because \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{\textrm{det}}$$\end{document} serves as the direct link between the state transition of microscopic model and the macroscopic rate equation. It represents a clear and interpretable parameter mapping point between microscopic rules and macroscopic dynamics. In the microscopic model, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{\textrm{det}}$$\end{document} directly corresponds to the triggering condition for an individual transitioning from state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_{\textrm{I}}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_{\textrm{H}}$$\end{document}. In the macroscopic rate equation, its reciprocal is described as the detection rate, characterizing the rate at which infected individuals are identified and transferred into the hospitalized group. On the other hand, the detection period also plays a critical role in real-world public health interventions. In infectious disease control, adjusting detecting frequency is one of the core non-pharmaceutical interventions widely adopted by countries. Regarding other adjustable parameters: The random walk distance of individuals \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{\textrm{rw}}$$\end{document}, while reflecting social distancing policies, manifests in the macroscopic model as a complex function of the transmission rate. The detection probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\textrm{det}}$$\end{document} primarily depends on technical sensitivity, with limited scope for practical adjustment. The recovery period \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{\textrm{rec}}$$\end{document} is mainly determined by viral biology and individual immunity, and is not directly controllable by policy. Additionally, we select the economic status of this closed area as the index for model optimization. Other parameters remain unchanged, as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{\textrm{det}}$$\end{document} increases, the frequency of detection decreases, which reduces the cost of medical quarantine and increases the total gross product. However, this also leads to an increase in the number of infected people, which in turn increases the cost.

We choose the total economic loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O_{\textrm{el}}$$\end{document} during the epidemic as the objective function for parameter optimization, which is related to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{\textrm{rw}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{\textrm{det}}$$\end{document} and the duration of the epidemic \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{\textrm{dur}}$$\end{document}, as well as to each state variable (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{S}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} Results

Parameter identification results

The macroscopic model parameters were identified using data generated from the microscopic agent-based simulation. The simulation system was first executed once under the initial configuration detailed in Table 1. The time-series data of the compartment sizes (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{S}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{I}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{H}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{R}}$$\end{document}) obtained from this single run were used for the parameter identification of the macroscopic model. Fig. 8 shows a comparison between the output of the identified macroscopic model and the simulation data.

To enhance the robustness and generalizability of the parameter estimates, we employed a Monte Carlo approach to account for stochastic variability inherent in the microscopic simulations. The simulation system was randomly executed 200 independent times, each generating a distinct temporal trajectory for the compartment populations. Parameter identification was subsequently performed on each of these 200 individual datasets. The final, robust parameters for the macroscopic model were then obtained by calculating the arithmetic mean of the corresponding parameter values across all 200 identification rounds. This averaged parameter set is reported in Table 2.

In addition, we ran the simulation system 40 times randomly to generate 40 sets of data for the test data set, in order to verify the practicality of the macroscopic model. The test results are presented in Fig. 9, which shows that the macroscopic model curve is consistent with the trend of the test set data, which accurately reflects the spread of infectious diseases in this scenario. The goodness of fit between the macroscopic model and the mean of the test data set is shown in Table 3.

Conclusions

Swarm systems are typically analyzed at two distinct levels: micro (individual agents) and macro (collective behavior). Focusing solely on the micro level, the desired collective behavior can be achieved through the detailed design of individual rules, but this poses scalability challenges in larger-scale systems. Conversely, from a purely macro perspective, while macro-level objectives can be achieved independently, deriving the precise micro interactions that produce a given macro behavior is often impractical. Therefore, establishing connections between the micro and macro levels is crucial to comprehensive understanding of swarm system dynamics.

This paper proposes a universal bidirectional closed-loop modelling framework for swarm systems. The primary objective is to reveal the qualitative relationships between micro-level individuals and macro-level collectives in swarm systems, thereby guiding the formulation of micro-level rules through adjustments to the macroscopic model. At the micro level, individual agents are modeled as hybrid dynamical systems, with their state transitions formally described by PFSM. The macroscopic model abstracts away certain individual details and is represented by rate equations. Crucially, the macroscopic state flow diagram serves a functional purpose analogous to the microscopic PFSM, ensuring that each component of the macro model possesses a corresponding interpretation at the micro level. To enhance the generalizability and practicality of the macroscopic model, we refined its accuracy through a structured process involving: data-based parameter identification, model-based macroscopic prediction, and index-based model optimization. The validity of the macroscopic model’s predictions and optimized parameters was subsequently confirmed using micro-level data, enabling the feedback of these validated parameters into the actual swarm system configuration.

To demonstrate the applicability of our macroscopic modelling and analysis approach, we employed an infectious disease transmission scenario within a closed population as a case study. A corresponding simulation system was developed, where individual agents were programmed with microscopic behavioral rules, allowing the collection of macro-level system dynamics data from micro-level interactions. We constructed a macroscopic infectious disease model based on SIHR using the transitions of individual micro-level states, which is similar to the commonly used SIER model. Micro-level model data are crucial for parameter identification and validation of macroscopic model. The identification results and statistical metrics (e.g., coefficient of determination) indicate strong agreement between the macroscopic model and the simulation-derived data, with model trends accurately reflecting characteristic features of disease transmission dynamics. Finally, the validated macroscopic model was utilized for prediction and optimization studies, illustrating its potential to inform policy development for epidemic control. More importantly, macroscopic model are much more efficient than micro models without losing accuracy. Analysis based on macroscopic model can help improve the setup of swarm systems, which ultimately feeds back into the design of micro individual systems.

Discussion

This study presents a macroscopic state-based approach to bridge the micro-macro gap in swarm system modelling. By integrating PFSM at the micro level with rate equations and state flow diagrams at the macro level, we established a bidirectional closed-loop modelling framework. The methodology was further refined through data-based parameter identification, model-based macroscopic prediction, and index-based model optimization, demonstrating its utility in a simulated infectious disease transmission scenario. While these contributions offer a viable pathway for designing unidirectional swarm systems (whether bottom-up or top-down), several limitations warrant careful consideration.Neglect of Spatial Heterogeneity. The most significant constraint, as highlighted in this work, is the model’s inherent abstraction away from spatial information (e.g., agent position, velocity, local density gradients). Rate equations fundamentally assume spatial homogeneity or mean-field interactions. Consequently, when microscopic state transitions critically depend on spatial information (e.g., collision avoidance in robots, localized disease spread), the macroscopic state transition rates cannot be derived purely analytically from first principles. Instead, they require empirical calibration based on specific micro-behavior characteristics. Designing effective combinations of deterministic parameters and weight parameters often relies heavily on domain knowledge or simulation data, introducing subjectivity and limiting generalizability across spatially sensitive scenarios.Specificity of the Case Study. The primary validation and demonstration were conducted using a specific case study - infectious disease transmission in a closed population. While this serves as a proof-of-concept, the performance and accuracy of the proposed macroscopic modelling approach might vary significantly when applied to fundamentally different swarm systems, such as spatially coordinated collective transport, or systems with complex emergent patterns. Although the overall approach of this study can be applied to swarm systems, its generalizability across diverse swarm types requires further validation.Simulation Dependence and Reality Gap. Parameter identification and optimization were performed using data from programmed micro-simulations. While valuable for proof-of-concept, real-world applicability faces challenges including sensor noise, true individual heterogeneity, and environmental stochasticity not fully captured in simulations. The future application of this framework to physical swarm robotics requires validation with empirical data.The potential of macroscopic models extends beyond analysis to top-down control and decision-making in domains like swarm operations and autonomous systems. This contrasts with RL, which focuses solely on the micro level and may struggle to cope with stability issues that arise.

Specificity of the Case Study. The primary validation and demonstration were conducted using a specific case study - infectious disease transmission in a closed population.
