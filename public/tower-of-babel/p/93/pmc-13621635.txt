Expression and functional characterization of the TRPV3 channel in domestic cat (Felis catus) mature oocytes
Authors: Sebastián Vergara, José Duarte-Zúñiga, Claudio Hidalgo, Constanza Suárez-Suárez, Karen Castillo, Matthias Piesche, Felipe Aguilera, Rafael A. Fissore, Fernando Hinostroza, Daniel Veraguas-Dávila, Ingrid Carvacho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntracellular calcium (Ca2+) signaling is essential for oocyte maturation, activation, and fertilization, with repetitive Ca2+ transients elicited at fertilization being critical for egg activation and early embryonic development. Mouse oocytes express several non-selective cation channels to support these oscillations, including TRPV3, a member of the transient receptor potential (TRP) channel family. In addition to Ca2+, TRPV3 mediates zinc (Zn2+) influx, which is a modulator of cortical granules (CGs) distribution and actin organization. In mammals, CG exocytosis mediates the fertilization-induced block to polyspermy, and pharmacological activation of TRPV3 in mouse oocytes elicits Ca2+ influx sufficient to trigger activation and parthenogenesis. Despite these critical roles in mice, the expression and function of TRPV3 in other mammals remain unexplored. Here, we evaluate the functional expression of TRPV3 channels in cat oocytes.MethodsOvaries from domestic cats were obtained during ovariohysterectomies, and oocytes were isolated and matured in vitro. Trpv3 expression was assessed by RT-PCR from ovaries and germinal vesicle (GV) and metaphase II (MII) oocytes, while TRPV3 localization was evaluated by immunocytochemistry. Mouse WT and TRPV3-knockout oocytes were used as controls for antibody specificity. Functional channel activity was examined using Ca2+ imaging following addition of the TRPV3 agonist 2-APB. Three-dimensional modelling, molecular docking, and comparative sequence analysis of feline, mouse, and human TRPV3 proteins were aligned with MAFFT, focusing on identical and biochemically similar residues within the 2-APB-binding sites as well as pore-forming and temperature-sensing domains to assess potential species-specific functional differences.ResultsWe detected Trpv3 transcripts in cat ovaries and in GV and MII oocytes. Immunocytochemistry confirmed TRPV3 protein localization at the oocyte membrane in cats, consistent with reports in mouse oocytes. Additionally, addition of 2-APB elicited robust intracellular increase in Ca2+ in MII cat eggs, demonstrating functional TRPV3 channel activity. Comparative analyses revealed non-conservative substitutions in feline TRPV3 compared to mouse and human TRPV3, particularly within the pore-forming, channel gating, and temperature sensing regions, offering a molecular explanation for species-specific differences in TRPV3 function.ConclusionsOur results demonstrate functional TRPV3 expression in domestic cat oocytes. We find distinctive features in feline TRPV3 compared to rodent and human orthologs. These insights support the development of tailored artificial oocyte activation protocols in cats, with potential applications to Assisted Reproductive Technologies (ART) for endangered felids.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40659-026-00709-7.

Background

The conservation of endangered wild felids represents a critical challenge in wildlife biology, as nearly half of all felid species worldwide are currently threatened with extinction [1]. According to the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN), 14 felid species are currently classified as vulnerable and four as endangered (IUCN, 2025). The domestic cat (Felis silvestris catus) is widely used as a biomedical and reproductive model for developing assisted reproductive technologies (ARTs) in endangered felids [2]. In Chile, five native felid species inhabit the territory, among which the guiña or kodkod (Leopardus guigna) is of particular conservation concern. This species is listed as vulnerable by the IUCN due to its small and declining population, one of the most restricted geographic distributions among felids, and an estimated number of fewer than 100,000 reproductive-active adults [3]. Habitat fragmentation resulting from human activity, combined with direct persecution, is a major driver of this decline [4]. Preserving the genetic diversity of such endangered felids required innovative reproductive approaches to complement in situ conservation efforts.

ARTs, artificial insemination (AI), and in vitro embryo production (IVP) have become valuable ex situ tools for preserving the genetic diversity of endangered felids. IVP techniques, such as in vitro fertilization (IVF), intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), and interspecific somatic cell nuclear transfer (iSCNT), have successfully yielded live offspring in domestic cats and several endangered wild felid species [5, 6]. A notable example is the use of iSCNT, where enucleated domestic cat oocytes serves as recipients for nuclei from endangered felids. This approach has successfully produced live cloned offspring of the African wildcat (Felis lybica) and the sand cat (Felis margarita) [7, 8]. By leveraging the domestic cat as an oocyte donor, iSCNT circumvents the need to obtain gametes directly from endangered felids. Recent studies demonstrated that early stage kodkod embryos can be generated through iSCNT [9], and that kodkod oocytes can be matured in vitro and parthenogenetically activated to develop into blastocysts [10]. These approaches represents a potential alternative for preserving genetic diversity, given the challenges associated with gamete collection in this protected species. However, felid embryos generated by iSCNT often exhibit low developmental rates, poor blastocyst formation, and reduced implantation efficiency [11], partly due to the limited understanding of oocyte physiology and the fertilization signaling cascades in felines, highlighting the need for species-specific refinements of ART protocols. During fertilization, sperm-oocyte fusion triggers a signal transduction cascade, known as “egg activation”, that converts the oocyte into a diploid zygote, initiating embryo development [12]. Egg activation is triggered by the release of the sperm-specific phospholipase C ζ into the oocyte, and it involves a series of cellular processes that mostly depend on repeated increases in intracellular calcium (Ca2+) [13]. Importantly, Ca2+ influx is essential for both oocyte maturation and to support the oscillations of fertilization in mammals [12]. Moreover, an intracellular Ca2+ signal is the first signal of fertilization in all the species studied up to date [14]. Consequently, several experimental strategies have been developed to increase intracellular Ca2+ to activate eggs [15]. However, a limited number of studies have assessed the suitability of activation protocols for domestic cat oocytes, and most existing protocols were extrapolated from murine and bovine models [16, 20], yielding only modest success in felids. Contributing factors likely include an incomplete understanding of the molecular mechanisms that maintain metaphase II (MII) arrest and regulate fertilization in cats, as well as the reported frequent spontaneous activation of cat oocytes observed after in vitro maturation (IVM) and in vitro culture (IVC) [18]. These results emphasize the need to elucidate the signal pathway that sustains MII arrest and the Ca2+ influx channels that operate in feline gametes.

Transient Receptor Potential (TRP) channels are a family of non-selective cation channels that serve as cellular sensors for diverse physicochemical cues like temperature, pH, and osmolarity [21]. One family member, the TRP Vanilloid 3 (TRPV3) channel, is robustly and differentially expressed in mouse oocytes: its function increases during maturation and peaks at the MII stage [22]. Pharmacological TRPV3 activation in MII eggs induces robust Ca2+ entry sufficient to trigger egg activation and parthenogenesis (22). Consistently, TRPV3 also mediates strontium (Sr2+) influx, noteworthy because Sr2+ is a widely used common parthenogenetic agent, including in cats [16, 19, 23, 25]. These findings suggest that domestic cat oocytes may possess a functional TRPV3-like and/or TRPV3 channel, but its expression and functional activation remain unconfirmed in this species. In addition, beyond Ca2+, TRPV3 has recently been suggested in regulating zinc (Zn2+) dynamics in eggs, since TRPV3-null mouse eggs exhibit altered Zn2+ influx and concentration, leading to reduced egg developmental potential underscoring the broader significance of this channel in oocyte physiology [26]. Elucidating the function of TRPV3 in domestic cat oocytes could enhance the efficiency of in vitro maturation (IVM) and support the development of species-specific activation protocols for applications such as iSCNT.

This study aimed to characterize TRPV3 expression in germinal vesicle (GV) and metaphase MII oocytes (eggs) of domestic cats. To accomplish this, we combined molecular and functional approaches, including transcript and protein analysis to detect TRPV3 in cat oocytes, and live-cell Ca2+-imaging to evaluate TRPV3- mediated Ca2+ influx. We performed comparative sequence analyses to place feline TRPV3 structure and potential function in the context of mouse and human orthologs. Our results demonstrate that domestic cat oocytes express TRPV3 transcripts and protein, with TRPV3 localized in the plasma membrane.

Methods

Collection and in vitro maturation of cat oocytes

A total of 62 ovaries from 31 domestic cats (Felis catus, 4 months to 4 years old) were collected during routine ovariohysterectomies (OVH) performed in local veterinary clinics for clinical or reproductive control purposes and transported to the laboratory within 6 h of excision, where the oocyte collection was performed.

Germinal Vesicle (GV) oocytes were obtained by mechanically dissecting ovarian tissue in a handling medium consisting of M199 (Sigma, Cat. M4530) supplemented with 20 mM HEPES (Sigma, Cat. H3375) and 10% fetal FBS, pH 7.4. Metaphase II (MII) eggs were obtained by culturing GV-stage oocytes in maturation medium composed of M199 supplemented with 4 mM NaHCO3, 10 μg/ml estradiol (E2), 20 ng/ml epidermal growth factor (EGF), 10 μl/ml ITS, 0.5 IU/ml FSH, 1.0 IU/ml LH, 1.0 mM glutamine, 0.36 mM sodium pyruvate, 2.2 mM calcium lactate, and 0.3% BSA. Oocytes were incubated for 24 h at 38.5 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 [27]. Cumulus cells were removed by pipetting in the presence of 1 mg/ml hyaluronidase. For zona pellucida (ZP) removal, oocytes were initially exposed to 2 mg/ml pronase, followed by brief immersion in acid Tyrode’s solution until the ZP began to contract. Once shrinkage occurred, oocytes were immediately transferred to the handling medium, allowing the zona to re-expand and be removed easily. MII stage of the oocytes was assessed by visual confirmation of first polar body extrusion.

Superovulation and isolation of mouse eggs

Metaphase II (MII) oocytes (eggs) were isolated from 6- to 16-week-old wild-type (WT) and Trpv3 knockout (TRPV3KO, V3KO) female mice following standard hormonal stimulation. Mice received an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 5 IU of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG), followed 48 h later by 5 IU of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG). Ovulated MII-arrested eggs were collected from the oviductal ampullae 13–15 h after hCG administration and maintained in HEPES-buffered Tyrode lactate (TL-HEPES) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cumulus cells were removed by incubating the samples in TL-HEPES containing 2.6 mg/ml bovine testicular hyaluronidase (Sigma, H3506), followed by gentle pipetting.

Oocyte diameter and zona pellucida thickness measurements

Oocyte diameter measurement was performed using the ImageJ2 v1.54p software v.X (NIH), by tracing a line in the equatorial region of the oocyte. The mean cytoplasmic diameter of mouse oocytes was assessed using a Mann–Whitney test. The zona pellucida (ZP) thickness was measured by tracing a line through the ZP using ImageJ2 v1.54p (NIH); the oocyte was not included in the measurement. ZP thickness was determined by an unpaired t-test with Welch’s correction. A total of 21 mouse oocytes and 16 cat oocytes were used for both analyses.

TRPV3 Immunocytochemistry in mouse and cat oocytes

Immunocytochemistry was performed in WT and TRPV3KO mouse eggs based on the Neuromab Immunofluorescence protocol of Cultured Cells protocol with the modifications as detailed below. Isolated MII-arrested eggs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 15,710) for 20 min at room temperature (RT) under gentle agitation. The zona pellucida (ZP) was removed using Tyrode´s acid solution (pH 2.5), followed by three washes in PBS-T (150 mM NaCl and 0.1% Triton X-100 in 10 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.4).

Samples were blocked in 4% non-fat dry milk prepared in TBS-T (150 mM NaCl and 0.1% Triton X-100 in 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, “blocking solution”) for 1 h at RT with gentle agitation. Eggs were then incubated with anti-TRPV3 primary antibody (Neuromab, clone N15/4, 75–043, 10 µg/ml), diluted in blocking solution, for 1 h 30 min at RT. Excess antibody was removed by three washes (5 min each) in blocking solution.

Next, oocytes were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (Invitrogen, A32723), diluted in blocking solution, for 1 h at RT in the dark. After incubation, eggs were washed in PBS-T, protected from light.

Samples were mounted in µ-Slide 18 Well Glass Bottom chambers (Ibidi, Cat. No. 81817) using 100 µl of 1X PBS. Images were acquired using a Nikon AXR + NSPARC Confocal Laser Microscope System at the Institute for Applied Life Sciences, University of Massachusetts Amherst.

Following ZP removal, cat GV, and in vitro-matured MII oocytes were processed for immunocytochemistry using the protocol described for mouse oocytes above, unless otherwise indicated. Oocytes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, P6148) prepared in PBS containing 0.1% BSA for 20 min at RT under gentle agitation, followed by three washes in PBS-T. Blocking was performed for 1 h at RT with agitation.

GV and MII oocytes were incubated with the anti-TRPV3 primary antibody (20 µg/ml), diluted in blocking solution for 2 h at RT under gentle agitation. After three washes, samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam, ab150105; 1:500 dilution) and rhodamine phalloidin (Cytoskeleton Inc., Cat. #PHDR1; 70 nM), which binds actin filaments, both prepared in blocking solution. Incubations were performed for 1 h and 30 min, respectively, under gentle agitation and protected from light. Oocytes were then washed in PBS-T.

Oocytes (19 GV oocytes and 13 MII oocytes) were mounted on µ-Slide 18 Well Glass Bottom chambers using VECTASHIELD® PLUS Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories, H-2000). Confocal images were acquired on a Leica Stellaris 5 spectral confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems), and image analysis was performed using ImageJ2 v1.54p (NIH).

RNA isolation and cDNA synthesis

Ovaries (16 in total) were obtained as described above (OVH, see above “Collection and In Vitro Maturation of Cat Oocytes”). Tissue was then sectioned, transferred into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes containing TRIzol reagent (Ambion), and stored at − 20 °C until processing.

RNA was extracted from ovarian tissue using the TRIzol–chloroform method. Ovaries were manually dissected, and 100 mg tissue fragments were transferred into microtubes containing 1 ml of TRIzol reagent (Ambion), and homogenized. Metaphase II (MII, 10 ovaries) eggs were obtained by culturing GV-stage oocytes in maturation (as previously described). Subsequently, RNA was isolated from oocyte samples, including germinal vesicle (GV, isolated from 12 ovaries) oocytes and MII eggs, using approximately 180 eggs per extraction with the ReliaPrep™ RNA Cell Miniprep System (Promega). Homogenates of the ovarian tissue were mixed with 200 µl of chloroform per 1 ml of TRIzol for 3 min at RT, and then centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube, mixed with 500 µl isopropanol per 1 ml of TRIzol, incubated for 10 min at 4 °C, and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting RNA pellet was washed in 1 ml of 75% ethanol and centrifuged at 7,500 g for 5 min at 4 °C. After removing the supernatant, the pellet was air-dried for 5 min at 37 °C and resuspended in 50 µl nuclease-free water.

RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a Nano-400A spectrophotometer (ALLSHENG). cDNA synthesis was performed using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA products were stored at -20 °C until use.

Calcium imaging of cat MII eggs

Intracellular calcium concentration ([Ca2+]i) in ZP-free MII eggs was measured using Fura-Red-AM (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Eggs were incubated in 5 µM Fura Red-AM supplemented with 0.01% Pluronic F127 in high-glucose DMEM for 30 min at RT. After incubation, eggs were washed in the handling medium (Medium 199 supplemented with 18 mM Hepes and 10% FBS), and. transferred to an extracellular solution containing (in mM): 140 NaCl, 4 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, and 10 glucose, adjusted to pH 7.4 with NaOH. Calcium imaging was performed using a Leica Stellaris 5 spectral confocal microscope, acquiring images every 3 s for a total of 9 min.

Fura Red-loaded eggs were excited at 405 nm and 488 nm, corresponding to the Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound forms of the dye, respectively. The emission of Fura Red–AM in the Ca2+ bound state was measured at 610 nm for the Ca2+-bound state and 670 nm for the unbound state. Fluorescence intensities were quantified using LAS X software (v.4.3.0.24308), and Ca2+-responses were expressed as the ratio of 405/488 nm fluorescence.

To assess TRPV3 channel activity, 2-Aminoethyl diphenylborinate (2-APB, Sigma-Aldrich, D9754) was applied at final concentrations of 100 µM or 300 µM [28]. Eggs (6 eggs) imaged in the absence of 2-APB and in the extracellular solution were used as controls. All imaging experiments were conducted at RT.

Cat egg activation using 2-APB

GV oocytes were isolated and matured in vitro for 24–26 h at 38.5 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 as described above (“Collection and In Vitro maturation of Cat oocytes”). MII eggs exhibiting a visible first polar body were selected and incubated in maturation media to which 0.1% PVA was added instead of 0.3% BSA, supplemented with 300 µM 2-APB for 1 h at 38.5 °C and 5% CO2 to induce oocyte activation (24 eggs). After treatment, eggs were transferred to synthetic oviduct fluid (SOF) medium [29] containing 107.6 mM NaCl, 7.16 mM KCl, 1.19 mM KH2PO4, 2.23 mM CaCl2*2H2O, 0.472 mM MgCl2*6H2O, 25.07 mM NaHCO3, 0.251 mM sodium lactate, and phenol red. The SOF medium was further supplemented with 0.4 mM sodium pyruvate, 0.271 mM L-glutamine, 0.3% BSA, 1X MEM essential amino acids, 1X MEM non-essential amino acids, 10 ng/ml EGF, 0.1 mg/ml citric acid, 0.5 mg/ml Myo-inositol, and 2% FBS. Eggs were cultured in SOF for up to 40 h following 2-APB exposure. Control eggs (31 eggs) were processed identically, except for the absence of 2-APB treatment. Statistical differences between conditions were assessed using a one-way repeated measures ANOVA with Geisser–Greenhouse correction, followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test.

(2)-APB docking in the cat TRPV3 channel

The cat TRPV3 channel model and the computational docking of 2-APB to the cat TRPV3 (cTRPV3) channel were generated using Boltz-2 [30, 31]. The amino acid sequence of Felis catus TRPV3 (NCBI accession number: XP_019672883.1) was used as input to generate a structural model of the channel. To evaluate ligand-channel interactions, we used Visual Molecular Dynamics software v1.9.4 [32]. Boltz-2 software was also used to estimate the predicted EC50 for 2-APB binding to cTRPV3.

To estimate the volume and structural flexibility of the 2-APB binding sites in mouse, human, and cat TRPV3 channels, we used the FPocket software package [33]. For each species, binding-site volumes were calculated for individual subunits, and the average value was reported. For the mouse TRPV3 (mTRPV3; PDB ID: 6DVZ), we quantified the average pocket volume across three regions previously described as potential ligand-binding sites: the extracellular, transmembrane, and intracellular domains. For the human TRPV3 (hTRPV3; PDB ID: 8V6N), average pocket volumes were measured for the transmembrane and intracellular binding sites, since the 2-APB does not bind the extracellular binding site.

The molecular volume of 2-APB was determined using the Molecular Volume Calculator webserver (https://scfbio-iitd.res.in/Sanjeevini/Molecular-volume-calculator.php accessed on October 27th, 2025) following the procedure described by Jayaram et al., [34].

Three-dimensional structural visualization of mouse, human, and cat TRPV3 channels was performed using Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) v1.9.4 [32]. This software was used to inspect overall channel architecture, compare pore and binding-site regions among species, and generate molecular graphics for analysis and figure preparation.

TRPV3 comparative sequence analysis

A comparative sequence analysis of TRPV3 from mouse, human, and domestic cat was conducted to identify species-specific differences within functionally relevant domains. Protein sequences for Mus musculus (TRPV3_MOUSE), Homo sapiens (TRPV3_HUMAN), and Felis catus (XP_019672883.1) were retrieved from UniProt and NCBI. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using MAFFT under the auto strategy to generate a high-quality alignment. Pairwise sequence identity and similarity across full-length TRPV3 proteins were calculated using the EMBOSS Needle webserver (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/psa/emboss_needle).

Residue numbering was standardized to the mouse TRPV3 reference sequence to facilitate cross-species comparison of structural domains. Functional regions—including the ankyrin repeat domain (ARD), S1-S4 temperature-sensing helices, S4-S5 linker, pore-forming region (S5-pore helix-selectivity filter-S6), and TRP helix—were annotated based on published TRPV3 structural models and cryo-EM structures [35, 37]. For each domain, residue-by-residue comparisons were performed to identify conservative and non-conservative substitutions across species, with classification based on differences in physicochemical properties. Putative metal-coordinating residues (Cys, His, Asp, Glu) were specifically flagged due to their relevance in Ca2+ and Zn2+ permeation [38].

Domain-specific differences were organized into comparative matrices and subsequently used to interpret potential impacts on channel gating, thermal sensitivity, ion selectivity, and ligand binding. Structural alignments and domain visualizations were generated using Genious Prime.

Detailed inspection of the pore forming S6 transmembrane helix revealed four non-conservative amino acid substitutions in the cat TRPV3 channel relative to the mouse counterpart. The changes, L596V, K614S, K616N, and A628S (Fig. 7b; Supplementary Table 1) localize to the lower S6 gate, a critical region that couples ligand binding to the pore opening [35]. In mouse TRPV3, the adjacent lysines K614-K616 form a localized positive patch that likely stabilizes the open state and facilitates allosteric transitions induced by 2-APB. In contrast, the substitution of these residues by neutral amino acids S614 and N616 in cat TRPV3 sequence eliminated this electrostatic feature, likely altering local charge distribution, hydration, and conformational flexibility of the activation gate. In addition, the A628S substitution introduces increased polarity near the pore, which may further influence S6 stability and gating dynamics.

Beyond the pore region, multiple non-conservative substitutions were identified between mouse and cat TRPV3 within the domains involved in temperature-sensing and allosteric regulation, including the ankyrin repeat domain (ARD), the S1-S4 bundle, S4-S5 linker, and the TRP helix. Notable differences include Q114R, C131S, R136L, D198N, E207A, K246S, D468A, and S480G (Supplementary Table 2; Fig. 7). Substitutions within the ARD (Fig. 7d) may affect thermal priming, while changes within the S1-S4 bundle and S4-S5 linker (Fig. 7c) directly influence the gating apparatus that 2-APB stabilizes in mouse TRPV3. Together, the clustered substitutions in the S6 gate and in temperature-sensing modules of cat TRPV3 provide a plausible molecular basis for its reduced sensitivity to 2-APB compared with mouse and human orthologs.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate for the first time, that domestic cat oocytes express functional TRPV3 channel at both the mRNA and protein levels. Moreover, stimulation with the TRPV3 agonist 2-APB evokes robust Ca2+ influx in MII eggs, suggesting that TRPV3 protein is not only present but also functionally active at this developmental stage. This finding extends our previous work from mice [22, 26, 40] to a felid species.

In agreement with previous studies [1, 42], our quantitative analysis showed that cat MII eggs had a significantly larger average cytoplasm diameter (137 ± 1.7 µm) compared to mouse MII eggs (76.58 ± 0.4 µm; Fig. 1b). This ~ 1.8-fold increase in egg size was statistically significant, indicating a clear separation between the two populations. Feline MII eggs displayed larger diameters and thicker zona pellucida compared with mouse oocytes. These morphological differences likely reflect species-specific adaptations in feline reproductive physiology. The high lipid content characteristic of cat oocytes may contribute to energy storage and early embryonic development, both of which are associated with embryo competence and cryotolerance [43, 45]. A larger cytoplasmic volume may also imply greater Ca2+-buffering capacity and distinct organelle organization [46]. Such features could influence the amplitude and kinetics of Ca2+ signals during maturation and activation, helping explain why activation protocols developed for other species often perform sub optimally in felines. Consistent with this, conventional oocyte artificial activation treatments that are highly effective in mouse eggs have yielded only limited success in cat oocytes [19].These distinctive morphological features likely reflect species-specific reproductive adaptations in felids.

Calcium-permeable ion channels play essential roles in oocyte maturation and fertilization [12, 22, 40]. Our results on TRPV3 protein localization dynamics during cat oocyte maturation are similar with previous findings in mice, in which TRPV3 functional expression peaks at the MII stage [22]. TRPV3−/− mice exhibit somewhat reduced ER Ca2+ stores and lower levels of labile Zn2+ [26], underscoring the importance of this channel for divalent cation homeostasis and fertilization [47]. In cat oocytes, TRPV3 is localized to both the cytoplasm and plasma membrane in MII eggs, however, and different from mouse oocytes, this pattern becomes more prominently membrane-associated in GV oocytes (Fig. 3). Moreover, cat GV oocytes displayed a more sharply defined cortical enrichment compared to MII eggs, different that the TRPV3 functional pattern observed in mouse GV oocytes [22]. This pattern, and its colocalization with actin, suggests dynamic trafficking of TRPV3 during maturation, as it was reported in mouse [40], and supports a functional role at fertilization. It is important to note that the antibody used in this study has not been validated in cat; therefore, the possibility of non-specific binding cannot be excluded. This limitation may provide plausible explanation for the observed discrepancies in membrane-associated TRPV3 localization between mouse and cat GV oocytes.

We demonstrate that the TRPV3-agonist 2-APB induces physiologically relevant Ca2+ elevations sufficient to trigger parthenogenetic activation in a subset of cat oocytes. However, activation rates were modest compared with those reported in other models [22] (Figs. 4, 5). This discrepancy can be explained by two key factors. First, successful egg activation in mammals typically requires not a single sustained Ca2+ rise but a series of repetitive Ca2+ oscillations [48]. A limitation of using 2-APB is that it primarily induces a non-oscillatory Ca2+ increase, TRPV3 activation alone may be insufficient to support full embryonic activation in all oocytes. In addition, the total Ca2+ rise induced by 2-APB requires the contributions of intracellular channels, such as the IP3R1, to propagate the Ca2+ wave initiated by the Ca2+ influx. However, the sensitivity of this channel in cats is presently unknown. Similarly, the permeability ratios of feline TRPV3 remain unknown, particularly for Sr2+, whose influx through TRPV3 is known to trigger parthenogenetic activation in mouse eggs [22]. Second, our results suggest that species-specific structural differences in the TRPV3 protein may alter channel sensitivity to 2-APB [41, 49, 50]. Minor divergences in channel structure can substantially influence agonist responses. Thus, the comparatively limited response of feline vs. mouse eggs to 2-APB likely reflects an inherent pharmacological difference between species, on top of the suboptimal Ca2+ signal pattern. This interpretation agrees with prior reports that small structural changes can substantially alter TRPV3 sensitivity and efficacy to 2-APB [50]. These findings underscore the need to optimize agonist dose and exposure for each species and to seek TRPV3 modulators better matched to felid channels.

Our in-silico structural and docking analyses provided molecular basis for the reduced 2-APB sensitivity of cat TRPV3. As it was reported, our structural prediction of mouse TRPV3 contains three potential ligand-binding pockets: extracellular, transmembrane, and intracellular [41], whereas the cat TRPV3 structural model features a markedly smaller intracellular pocket relative to mouse TRPV3, and it is predicted to be inaccessible to 2-APB (Fig. 6). Consequently, the structural model predicts that cat TRPV3 has only two accessible 2-APB-binding pockets. Together these analyses suggest that while the transmembrane binding pocket is broadly conserved across species, and that species-specific structural differences, particularly within the intracellular cavity, may influence the accessibility and contribution of individual binding sites to 2-APB-mediated modulation of TRPV3. Indeed, cat TRPV3 likely has only two functional 2‑APB binding pockets per subunit (extracellular and transmembrane), and that modest changes in pocket volume and shape across orthologs can produce measurable differences in ligand sensitivity. This reduction in the binding-sites number or accessibility could diminish cooperative channel activation, effectively lowering the apparent agonist efficacy. Such replacements alter local charge distribution, hydration, and flexibility of the activation gate, offering a structural explanation for the reduced 2-APB sensitivity observed in cat oocytes. Together, these S6-specific substitutions support the notion that the 2-APB-stabilized conformation in mouse TRPV3 is not fully preserved in the cat ortholog, helping to explain differences in activation strength. In practical terms, feline TRPV3 might require higher 2-APB concentrations (or longer exposure) to achieve the same degree of channel opening and Ca2+ influx observed in the mouse with lower doses. Our results show that when designing artificial oocyte activation treatments for felids, simple adopting concentrations effective in mouse may be insufficient; instead, higher agonist doses or alternative TRPV3 modulators based in the particular structural characteristics of cat TRPV3, might be required. These findings underscore the need to optimize agonist dose and exposure for each species and to seek TRPV3 modulators better matched to felid channels.

Beyond ligand-binding sites, comparative sequence analysis of the domestic cat TRPV3 channel revealed several non-conservative amino acid substitutions that map to critical regions involved in channel gating and temperature sensing (Fig. 7). Several of these changes modify charge, polarity, or structural rigidity at positions previously implicated in heat sensitivity and allosteric coupling [36, 37], and could therefore modulate the energetics of temperature-dependent activation. Notably, four changes in the S6 helix of feline TRPV3 alter the electrostatic landscape of the lower pore domain. This region is essential for channel opening and for coupling ligand binding to pore dilation during activation [37]. Changes here could affect how the channel pore transitions from close to open states. We also identify unique substitutions across the ankyrin repeat domain, the S1-S4 transmembrane bundle (which acts as the temperature-sensing module), and the S4-S5 linker that connects the voltage-sensing like domain to the pore opening [51, 52]. The S4-S5 linker is often linkened to a mechanical “gearbox” in TRP channels translating conformational rearrangements in the sensor domain to the opening of the pore [53, 54], and upon activation by small-molecule agonists such as 2-APB [28, 50]. Therefore, the D468A substitution disrupts key electrostatic interactions in the linker region, which are required for efficient mechanical coupling between temperature-sensing helices and the S6 gate. Alterations of these domains in the cat TRPV3 likely engender subtle shifts in the energetics and kinetics and the stability of open or sensitized channel states. It is known from structural and biophysical studies that the heat activation of TRPV3 channel involve concerted conformational rearrangements in the S1-S6 transmembrane region [37, 52]. For example, TRPV1 (a heat-gated cousin of TRPV3) undergoes specific pore conformational changes upon heating and capsaicin binding [53, 54]. This molecular scenario provides a rational molecular explanation for the attenuated responsiveness to 2-APB observed in our Ca2+ imaging experiments. Collectively, these findings show that although TRPV3 is highly conserved across mammals, subtle yet strategically positioned sequence differences can significantly alter channel behavior in felids. This insight is particularly relevant for the development of TRPV3-based artificial oocyte activation strategies, emphasizing the need to consider species-specific pharmacodynamics when designing protocols for both domestic and endangered cats.

Collectively, these differences are predicted to alter thermal activation threshold, shift gating energetics, and generate species-specific conformational responses upon ligand binding.across species. Moreover, the substitutions we observe could bias permeation toward other divalent cations in addition to Ca2+, highlighting a potential role for ion-selectivity shifts in adaptive changes to mammalian physiology.

The broader implications of our work pertain to improving felid ARTs and acknowledging the current limitations in our understanding of felid egg activation. The efficiency of interspecific somatic cell nuclear transfer (iSCNT) in felids remains low, partly due to the limited understanding of domestic cat physiology and activation mechanisms [8]. Our study addresses one aspect of this knowledge gap by elucidating a potential Ca2+ signaling mechanism in cat oocytes. However, it also reveals limitations that need to be overcome. For instance, while triggering Ca2+ release via TRPV3 is a promising strategy (given TRPV3´s role during artificial egg activation, as shown here and in mice), it may need to be combined with techniques to mimic physiological Ca2+ oscillations [48] or supplemented by targeting other Ca2+ influx pathways to achieve full activation and embryonic development. Furthermore, oocytes from wild felids can differ in morphology and development responses, as demonstrated in the kodkod (Leopardus guigna), which produced smaller oocytes and exhibits species-specific sensitivities in activation protocols [10]. Such differences caution against directly extrapolating our results to all felids. A deeper understanding of Ca2+ signaling pathways, particularly the role of TRPV3 and how it structure varies between the species, will be essential for developing more effective and tailored activation protocols. Future work should build on this by exploring other Ca2+-permeable channels (e.g. TRPM7, Cav3.2) [55] that also are involved in egg activation, and by testing alternative TRPV3 agonist or modulators that might induce more favorable intracellular Ca2+ increases in felids eggs.

Our findings contribute to increasing the success rate of felid embryo production by identifying TRPV3 as viable target for artificial oocyte activation in felids and by illuminating the species-specific nuances that must be accounted for. By integrating molecular insights with reproductive technology, we move closer to establishing reliable assisted reproductive protocols not only for domestic cats but also as a model to safeguard the genetic future of endangered felines. This is particularly relevant for species such as the kodkod, for which gamete collection from live individuals is difficult and poses significant risks due to their small population size and protected status.

Conclusions

In this study, using a combination of mRNA expression, functional assays and comparative structural analysis, we found that the cat TRPV3 ortholog appears to contain only two accessible ligand-binding pockets per subunit (compared to three in mouse channels) and harbors unique amino acid substitutions in key pore-forming and regulatory domains. These structural differences correlate with a markedly reduced 2-APB responsiveness in the cat channel, providing a mechanistic explanation for its diminished activation relative to the highly conserved mouse TRPV3. Our findings demonstrated that subtle sequence variations can substantially alter ion channel gating and ligand sensitivity across species, representing the principal contribution of this work.

Together, these findings provide the first evidence for TRPV3 in felid oocytes and eggs, and lay the groundwork for developing more effective, field-specific oocyte activation strategies in the context of ART and biodiversity conservation.
