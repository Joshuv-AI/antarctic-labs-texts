Focal Constrictive Pericarditis, an Unusual Cause for Biventricular Failure
Authors: Farhan Parachikkottil, Harikishore Udhayan, Shijoy Parayil, Sajeer Kalathingathodika, Krishnakumar Prabhakaran, Kader Muneer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 34 year old male who had undergone surgical closure of a ventricular septal defect at the age of 4 years, after a long asymptomatic period, presented with heart failure and was diagnosed with atrial flutter and biventricular dysfunction. Chest X-ray showed a band-like calcification in the atrioventricular groove. Echocardiography revealed dilated atria, focal calcification with constriction at the atrioventricular groove, and biventricular systolic dysfunction. Focal constrictive pericarditis occurring in post-cardiac surgery patients is very rare. Here we report a case of focal variant of constrictive pericarditis manifesting in a young male after 3 decades of ventricular septal defect repair.Keywords: Focal constrictive pericarditis, Heart failure, Ventricular septal defect repair

Introduction

Normal pericardium is a thin sac, about 1–2 mm in thickness. In constrictive pericarditis [1], the chronic inflammatory process causes extensive calcification of the pericardium, leading to loss of elasticity, reduced intrapericardial volume, diastolic dysfunction, and myocardial deformation. Etiology [2] can be infective, connective tissue diseases, post-cardiac surgery [3], malignancy, radiation, and drug-induced. Postoperative constrictive pericarditis is rare and presents very late. The ideal treatment for constrictive pericarditis is total pericardiectomy, with a mortality risk of 5–10 %. Prognosis is generally poor with advanced heart failure, elderly, post-radiation, and associated myocardial disease. Our patient had associated biventricular dysfunction, making him a high-risk candidate for surgery.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old male patient, known case of congenital heart disease–ventricular septal defect, underwent repair at age 4, presented with complaints of dyspnoea over the past year. He also reported bilateral pedal edema and abdominal distension. Several months ago, he was hospitalized for sudden onset of palpitations accompanied by worsening dyspnoea. He was diagnosed with atrial flutter and managed with medications. During this admission, he was found to have moderate left ventricular systolic dysfunction. An abdominal ultrasound suggested early chronic liver disease. His pedal edema and abdominal distension resolved with treatment, and he was referred to us for further evaluation. On admission, his jugular venous pressure was elevated, vitals were stable, and he was in New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class II. ECG revealed sinus rhythm and an incomplete right bundle branch block (RBBB) pattern. Chest X-ray showed cardiomegaly with dense, band-like calcification in the left atrioventricular (AV) groove (Fig. 1A & B). The 2D transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated septal bounce, dilated left atrium, constriction at the AV groove without obstruction to the inflow (Fig. 4A & B), and annulus paradoxus (Fig. 2A & B). Doppler analysis showed LV diastolic dysfunction. Computed tomography (CT) demonstrated the extent of the pericardial calcification involving the AV groove and anterior pericardium (Fig. 3A). CT also showed bilateral mild pleural effusion and features of early cardiac cirrhosis. Ultrasound abdomen showed coarse parenchymal liver echotextue and splenomegaly. It was probably cardiac cirrhosis, as the workup for other causes of cirrhosis turned out to be negative.

A diagnosis of late-onset post-operative constrictive pericarditis was made. Cardiac catheterization data (Fig. 3B–D) showed elevated biventricular end diastolic pressures. Diastolic equalisation of ventricular filling pressures and ventricular interdependence during the phases of respiration were notably absent, because of focal involvement of the AV groove and anterior pericardium. He was having biventricular dysfunction, which was thought to be due to myocardial involvement or tachycardiomyopathy related myocardial dysfunction.

The pericardiectomy was performed through a midline sternotomy approach on cardiopulmonary bypass. The intraoperative assessment revealed thickened and calcified pericardium at multiple sites, especially denser in the posterior and inferior atrioventricular groove, and extending to the adjacent ventricular surface. Most of it had infiltrated the epicardium and myocardium. Pericardium was dissected from base to apex on the anterior, lateral and posterior aspects. Pericardial adhesion were stripped off, and most of the calcified pericardium was removed. The pleural cavity contained serous fluid, which was drained. He tolerated the procedure well. Post-operative X-ray showed that the thick, calcified pericardial band was removed (Fig. 1C & D). The pleural fluid was transudate, suggesting heart failure as the probable cause. He was discharged after a regular hospital stay.

1-year follow-up assessment revealed improved left ventricular function (Fig. 4C & D, Video 1C and D (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1463&window=additional_files&context=jsha)). The annulus paradoxus was no longer present (Fig. 2C & D). He did not develop any heart failure or arrhythmic events following the procedure and is in NYHA functional class I to date.

Discussion

Constrictive pericarditis is classified [4] into typical forms (subacute and chronic), effusive constrictive, localised, and occult. The incidence [5] of post-operative constrictive pericarditis is approximately 0.2 %. Depending on the area of pericardial involvement, patient symptoms and presentation can vary. Right ventricular outflow obstruction and pulmonary trunk stenosis have been reported in some case series [6]. The driving factor of focal pericardial thickening and calcification in a postoperative patient is the primary trauma and inflammation to the pericardium during the cardiac surgery itself. Patients develop systemic as well as local inflammation mediated by various cytokines and neutrophils. The most common areas of calcifications are anterior to the right ventricle, the AV groove, and the inferior diaphragmatic surface.

Literature [7] shows that the incidence of postoperative constrictive pericarditis is rising. The most common presentation is in elderly male patients with diabetes mellitus, and most had coronary artery bypass graft surgery. In contrast to other etiological groups, patients with postoperative constrictive pericarditis did not exhibit marked calcification. However, they exhibited higher average pulmonary artery, wedged capillary, and right heart pressures. In another study by Bertog et al., [8] off the 163 patients who underwent pericardiectomy for constrictive pericarditis, 37 % were having postoperative constrictive pericarditis. The perioperative mortality was highest for post radiation constriction (21.4 %), followed by postsurgical constriction (8.3 %) cases. Long-term (7 year) survival was excellent for idiopathic group (88 %), whereas, patients with postoperative constrictive pericarditis showed substantially reduced survival rate (66 %). They pointed out that pericardial calcification was more commonly associated with idiopathic constrictive pericarditis and it was not an independent predictor of survival. Recurrence following complete pericardiectomy is rare.

Usually, symptoms of pulmonary venous congestion are absent in patients with constrictive pericarditis, but patients with significant left atrioventricular groove calcification can have pulmonary venous and arterial hypertension. Biventricular dysfunction has been reported in chronic constriction due to scarring of the underlying epicardium/ myocardium, which usually results in higher perioperative mortality. Identification of pericardial calcification is very important in patients presenting with chronic systemic venous congestion. Chest X-ray lateral view clearly identifies calcification in most cases, whereas a CT scan is more sensitive to detect minor calcification.

Our patient had atrial flutter and displayed signs of biventricular failure. Tachycardiomyopathy can be diagnosed only if ventricular dysfunction improves after controlling the tachycardia rate. His rhythm was restored to sinus with oral antiarrhythmics; however, ventricular dysfunction remained. After pericardiectomy, he experienced significant symptom relief within a few weeks, indicating a substantial negative impact of constrictive pericarditis on myocardial function.

Learning objective

Heart failure caused by the rare, focal form of constrictive pericarditis is an extremely rare occurrence. It can be overlooked on echocardiography, necessitating a sharp clinical suspicion for proper diagnosis.
