Radiomics analysis of panoramic radiographs using machine learning for the detection of peri-implantitis
Authors: Serhat Efeoglu, Emre Karahan, S Tugce Gokdeniz, Burak Incebeyaz, Fehmi Gonuldas, Secil Aksoy, Kaan Orhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo develop and validate a deep-learning detection model (Mask R-CNN) and a complementary radiomics-based machine-learning analysis for peri-implantitis detection on panoramic radiographs.MethodsPanoramic radiographs from 144 patients (mean age 57.2 ± 11.7 years) were retrospectively collected. The peri-implantitis regions surrounding the implants of 144 patients were semi-automatically segmented by two dentomaxillofacial radiology residents. A total of 7,045 radiomic features peri-implant were extracted; 6 key features were selected via variance thresholding, SelectKBest, and LASSO regression. A Mask R-CNN (ResNet-50 backbone) was trained (80% train, 20% validation) with data augmentation. The best radiomics-based classifier (XGBoost) reached an F1-score of 0.84. Inter-observer ICC for segmentation was 0.97.ConclusionsRadiomics-enhanced deep learning can reliably detect peri-implantitis on panoramic radiographs. Background

Dental implants are a reliable option for rehabilitating edentulous areas caused by infection, trauma, neoplasia, or congenital defects [1]. Their success depends primarily on osseointegration, defined by Brånemark as the direct structural and functional connection between living bone and the implant surface under load-bearing conditions. This process relies on the balance between osteoclastic resorption and osteoblastic formation; disturbances in this balance can compromise osseointegration and lead to implant failure or peri-implant complications [2–6].

Implant dentistry has become a standard treatment method in oral rehabilitation [7]. However, as the use of dental implants becomes more prevalent, the incidence of complications that may jeopardize treatment success has also increased [8]. Among these, peri-implantitis has emerged as a significant clinical concern. It is characterized by inflammation of the peri-implant tissues and progressive marginal bone loss, which can compromise implant stability and longevity if not diagnosed and treated in a timely manner [6, 9, 10]. Although peri-implantitis shares certain clinical features with periodontitis, peri-implantitis is a distinct pathological entity limited to implant sites [11, 12]. Clinically, it is identified by increased probing pocket depth and bleeding on probing, along with radiographically detectable marginal bone loss (MBL) [10].

Given the necessity for early and accurate diagnosis, radiographic imaging plays a fundamental role in peri-implant disease detection. Among various modalities, panoramic radiographs (orthopantomograms; OPGs) are widely used due to their broad coverage of the maxillofacial region, low radiation dose, and ability to visualize multiple implants and surrounding bone structures in a single image. However, radiographic interpretation remains subject to examiner variability and limited sensitivity in early-stage disease detection [13].

In this context, artificial intelligence (AI) has gained significant attention for its potential to improve diagnostic accuracy and efficiency. AI-driven systems, particularly those based on deep learning algorithms, offer automated analysis capabilities and increased reproducibility compared to traditional methods [14, 15]. Recent advances in computing power and the availability of large annotated image datasets have enabled the successful implementation of AI in various domains of medical and dental radiology [16–18]. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), a subset of deep learning, have demonstrated robust performance in image recognition and classification tasks within medical diagnostics [19].

Despite this progress, the application of AI in the detection of peri-implantitis remains relatively underexplored [20–25]. Moreover, the relationship between the severity of peri-implantitis and the degree of radiographically detectable bone loss is not yet fully understood. This gap highlights the need for advanced image-based diagnostic methods that can extract deeper insights from routine radiographs. Radiomics, an emerging discipline that involves the high-throughput extraction of quantitative features from medical images, offers a promising approach to enhance AI performance [26].

It enables the identification of complex patterns and tissue characteristics that are not readily discernible by human observers [26]. The integration of radiomics with AI creates a powerful framework for data-driven diagnosis and risk stratification, as radiomics provides rich imaging features while AI enables accurate interpretation [27].

The primary aim of this study is to support clinical decision-making regarding the presence or absence of peri-implantitis on panoramic radiographs by combining Mask Region-based CNN (R-CNN) lesion localization/segmentation with radiomics feature extraction and conventional machine-learning classification. As a secondary aim, we generate pixel-wise lesion masks to visualize the location and extent of peri-implantitis.

Methods

Ethical approval and patient selection

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Ankara University (approval date: 10 March 2025, no:7). Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Panoramic radiographs were retrospectively collected from patients aged 18 years or older who visited the Ankara University Faculty of Dentistry. All radiographs were acquired using Planmeca ProMax 3D Max devices (Planmeca, Promax, Finland) and retrieved via the Fuji Synapse PACS system (Fujifilm, Japan). A total of 144 patients (52 males and 92 females; mean age: 57.24 ± 11.69 years; age range: 27–90 years) were included. Patients lacking peri-implant images were excluded from the study.

Overall, panoramic radiographs from 144 patients comprising 439 peri-implant regions were included in the radiomics and deep learning analyses. An a priori power analysis at the patient level (Cohen’s d = 0.5, α = 0.05, power = 0.80; equal allocation between peri-implantitis–positive and –negative groups) indicated a minimum required sample size of 64 patients per group (total n = 128), which was met by our cohort (n = 144). The dataset was randomly split at the patient level into training and validation sets using an 80:20 ratio (351 and 88 peri-implant regions, respectively; fixed random seed = 930). The validation set served as an internal test set for performance reporting, as no independent external test dataset was used.

Image processing and radiomics feature extraction

The dataset was transferred to the Radcloud platform (Huiying Medical Technology Inc., China) and processed for radiomics analysis at the Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, Faculty of Dentistry, Ankara University. Radiomics refers to the extraction of high-dimensional quantitative features from medical images using advanced algorithms [26]. In this study, the Radcloud platform (Huiying Medical Technology Co., Ltd., China) was used to perform radiomics feature extraction, data management, and statistical analyses. The platform enables quantification of multiple feature families (e.g., first-order intensity- and texture-based metrics, including GLSZM), supporting the identification of imaging biomarkers.

Features were extracted from mask-based ROIs derived from pixel-wise segmentation masks. Because the data consisted of 2D panoramic radiographs, extraction was conducted in the original in-plane resolution without resampling to isotropic spacing. Gray-level discretization and intensity normalization were applied using the platform’s standardized settings prior to texture computation to ensure consistent quantization across cases. Feature definitions followed the standardized implementations provided by Radcloud.

All images were acquired using the same panoramic unit under a standardized protocol to reduce distortion and magnification-related variability; however, residual magnification differences remain an inherent limitation of panoramic imaging.

This radiomics-based machine-learning analysis was included as an interpretable, feature-driven benchmark to complement the deep-learning approach. Radiomics features were extracted from the ROI masks and used only as inputs to the conventional classifiers (KNN, SVM, XGBoost, RF, LR, DT); they were not used as inputs to, nor embedded within, the Mask R-CNN architecture.

Segmentation and Labeling

Peri-implantitis (+) labeling was based on radiographic evidence of marginal bone loss (MBL) exceeding 2 mm relative to baseline radiographs, consistent with the radiographic component of the 2017 World Workshop criteria [5, 6]. Baseline bone levels were defined using the earliest available post-treatment baseline panoramic radiograph in the patient record, against which current bone loss was assessed. Because panoramic radiographs exhibit non-uniform magnification, linear measurements were calibrated using known implant dimensions (length and/or diameter obtained from manufacturer records) as an internal reference scale to convert pixel distances to millimeters. MBL was measured as the linear distance from the implant platform/shoulder to the most coronal bone-to-implant contact on the mesial and distal aspects, using the PACS measurement tool; the per-implant MBL value was defined as the maximum of the mesial and distal measurements. Clinical parameters (e.g., bleeding/suppuration on probing and probing depth) were not consistently available for standardized integration into the labeling process; therefore, labeling relied on the radiographic bone-loss criterion relative to baseline.

Regions of peri-implantitis were segmented on panoramic radiographs using a semi-automated tool within the LASSO framework, which allows manual refinement of object boundaries. The segmentation was performed by two dentomaxillofacial radiology residents with 3 and 4 years of experience, respectively, under the supervision of a senior specialist with 25 years of experience. Both residents independently annotated the regions of interest, and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion to ensure annotation accuracy. ROI definition and extraction. ROI was defined in a mask-based manner on panoramic radiographs. For peri-implantitis–positive cases, annotators delineated the radiographic marginal bone-loss region adjacent to the implant as a pixel-wise segmentation mask. For peri-implantitis–negative cases, an anatomically comparable peri-implant region around the implant was delineated to preserve contextual information. All subsequent analyses used these segmentation masks.

In the radiomics workflow, the ROI was defined in a mask-based manner on panoramic radiographs. For peri-implantitis–positive cases, annotators delineated the radiographic marginal bone-loss region adjacent to the implant as a pixel-wise segmentation mask. For peri-implantitis–negative cases, an anatomically comparable peri-implant region around the implant was delineated to preserve contextual information. Radiomics features were then extracted within these annotated ROI masks (mask-based radiomics), and the selected features were subsequently used for conventional machine-learning classification.

Inter-observer reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on ROI area (in pixels) derived from the independently generated masks (ICC = 0.97), indicating excellent agreement.

Deep learning classification model

Following segmentation, individual implants were cropped from the panoramic radiographs based on the annotated ROIs. Each implant was labeled as either exhibiting peri-implantitis or appearing normal.

To automate classification, a Mask Region-based CNN model was implemented using the pre-trained ImageNet ResNet-50 architecture. This model is known for its accuracy in object detection and feature extraction.

In this study, radiomics features were not incorporated as additional inputs or layers within the Mask R-CNN architecture. Instead, Mask R-CNN was used for ROI localization/detection, and radiomics features extracted from the corresponding ROI masks were used only in the downstream conventional machine-learning classifiers (KNN, SVM, XGBoost, RF, LR, DT) as a complementary benchmark.

The Mask R-CNN model was trained using specific hyperparameters to optimize detection performance. Data augmentation techniques such as random scaling and flipping were applied. The model was trained with a batch size of 1 for 2 epochs, using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) as the optimizer and a learning rate of 0.1. To mitigate overfitting, dropout layers were included. Training settings were selected considering the limited dataset size and the computational constraints of high-resolution panoramic images. A batch size of 1 was used due to GPU memory limitations typical for two-stage detectors. The Mask R-CNN model was initialized with pretrained weights (transfer learning), and training convergence was monitored using the training loss curve with best-checkpoint selection based on validation performance. Under these settings, model performance plateaued early and the final model was obtained after 2 epochs; longer training schedules will be explored in future work to further assess robustness.

Data augmentation was applied on-the-fly during training (random horizontal flipping and random scaling), and therefore no fixed number of stored augmented images was generated. Augmentation was applied uniformly during training to improve robustness to minor geometric variability.

Justification of IoU and NMS thresholds. The IoU threshold of 0.1 was selected for FROC-style evaluation because the target regions (peri-implant bone-loss areas) are small on panoramic radiographs and their boundaries are inherently uncertain due to projection effects and anatomical superimposition. Therefore, our primary goal was coarse localization and detection rather than precise box overlap, and a relaxed IoU criterion was used to avoid penalizing clinically acceptable detections that may not tightly match the annotated contour. In addition, an NMS threshold of 0.1 was used to suppress multiple highly overlapping boxes around the same finding and reduce duplicate detections; this strict NMS setting helps retain the highest-confidence box while limiting redundant outputs. These threshold choices are expected to influence the sensitivity–false-positive trade-off and were chosen to prioritize clinically plausible localization under annotation uncertainty.

During training, model optimization and convergence were monitored using the training loss curve (Fig. 5). Convergence was determined by stabilization of loss values across iterations, and the best-performing checkpoint was observed at iteration 221 (minimum loss = 1.028). To reduce overfitting, data augmentation (random scaling and flipping) and dropout layers were used. The final model was selected based on validation-set performance, and the reported results were obtained on the held-out internal test set.

Training loss curve over iterations. The model achieved optimal convergence at iteration 221 (loss = 1.028)

Inter-observer reliability for labeling was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), yielding 0.97 (excellent agreement). The dataset was split into training and validation subsets (80:20; fixed seed = 930) to ensure reproducibility. LASSO feature selection was performed using 5-fold cross-validation. In contrast, the Mask R-CNN model was trained using this fixed train/validation split (i.e., without k-fold cross-validation) due to computational constraints and the exploratory nature of model development. Model selection was based on validation-set performance, and final results were reported on the held-out internal test set. We acknowledge the lack of k-fold cross-validation for the deep-learning model as a limitation, and future work will include k-fold evaluation and external multi-center validation to better assess robustness and generalizability.

Results

Feature selection and model training

Following variance thresholding, SelectKBest, and LASSO regression, seven radiomics features were retained for model training. These comprised four first-order features (skewness, root mean square [RMS], minimum, and kurtosis extracted from two filtered image representations) and three GLSZM texture features (large area low gray level emphasis, gray level non-uniformity, and high gray level emphasis). To quantify the contribution of the proposed radiomics feature-selection strategy, we performed an ablation analysis comparing models trained on (i) the full radiomics feature set and (ii) the LASSO-selected 7-feature subset, using identical data splits and evaluation procedures. Models trained with the selected subset yielded higher performance (best AUC: full features = 0.74 vs. selected features = 0.83), supporting the added value of the feature-selection step within the radiomics pipeline.

Model convergence and loss analysis

The training process of the deep learning model was monitored via a loss curve, as shown in Fig. 5. The curve demonstrates a steady but non-linear decrease in training loss over the course of the iterations, indicating progressive model optimization. Despite minor fluctuations, the model achieved its best performance at iteration 221, with a minimum training loss of 1.028, suggesting that it had successfully converged without evidence of underfitting or overfitting.

Discussions

Early and accurate diagnosis of peri-implantitis is essential, given the invasive, costly, and technically demanding nature of implant replacement procedures. In this study, we proposed a deep learning model (Mask R-CNN) enhanced by radiomic feature selection to detect peri-implantitis on panoramic radiographs. The model achieved satisfactory diagnostic performance, with precision ranging from 0.82 to 0.89, sensitivity between 0.72 and 0.79, and F1-scores from 0.81 to 0.84 highlighting its potential as a clinical decision support tool [28, 29].

The low precision and recall observed in Table 2 reflect the strict detection thresholds and the inherent anatomical noise of panoramic imaging, particularly in the maxillary region. This is underscores the need for improved preprocessing and threshold tuning. From a clinical perspective, this balance between recall and precision indicates that the model prioritizes sensitivity (detecting as many true peri-implantitis cases as possible) at the expense of precision (increasing false positives). While this may be acceptable for screening tools aimed at minimizing missed cases, further refinement is needed to reduce unnecessary clinical follow-ups.

Previous studies predominantly focused on periapical radiographs for peri-implant disease detection. Vera et al. implemented a dual-stage system combining YOLOv3 and a rule-based intensity analysis, achieving mAP values up to 0.898 for implant and prosthesis detection [30]. Lee et al. employed YOLOv7 for periapical image analysis and reported outstanding performance with an F1-score of 97.1%. Although these results are highly promising, they utilized intraoral radiographs with limited fields of view [31, 32].

In contrast, our study focused on panoramic radiographs, which are routinely used in general dental practice due to their wide anatomical coverage and lower radiation dose. Nevertheless, panoramic imaging has well-known limitations including distortion, non-uniform magnification, and anatomical superimposition which may reduce diagnostic accuracy, especially in the maxillary region [33, 34]. Kıbcak et al. similarly observed reduced detection performance for maxillary implants, attributing this to anatomical noise such as airway overlap. The inherent limitations of panoramic imaging necessitate the use of advanced feature extraction methodologies, which formed the rationale for incorporating radiomics into the current study [35].

Radiomics was incorporated into our methodology to overcome the limitations of 2D panoramic imaging, enabling the extraction of high-dimensional features beyond visual inspection. This approach aligns with a recent study by Troiano et al. who successfully employed radiomics for early peri-implant bone remodeling prediction on intraoral radiographs (AUC = 0.751). In our study, radiomics-based conventional machine-learning modeling was included as a complementary, interpretable benchmark alongside the deep-learning approach, rather than as a replacement for it. In the present study, radiomics supported performance by identifying the most informative quantitative descriptors, including first-order and GLSZM-based texture features. This complementary analysis provides feature-level interpretability and can be advantageous under limited sample conditions, thereby improving generalizability and offering an efficient framework for computer-aided diagnosis [36].

From an interpretability perspective, the selected radiomics features are plausible in the context of marginal peri-implant bone loss. First-order statistics (e.g., minimum intensity, RMS, skewness, and kurtosis) describe shifts in the gray-level distribution within the peri-implant ROI, which may reflect increased radiolucency and altered trabecular density patterns associated with inflammatory bone destruction. GLSZM-derived texture metrics quantify spatial heterogeneity and the distribution of homogeneous intensity zones; for example, higher gray-level non-uniformity and large-area low gray-level emphasis can indicate more heterogeneous and/or more extensive low-intensity regions that may correspond to bone-loss areas on 2D radiographs [26].

In a recent study by Kıbcak et al. a deep learning-based framework utilizing U-Net for segmentation and AlexNet for classification was proposed for detecting peri-implantitis on orthopantomograms (OPGs). Their model achieved a high segmentation accuracy (DSC = 0.986) and robust classification performance (precision = 0.777, recall = 0.903, F1-score = 0.835), highlighting the viability of integrating AI tools into routine diagnostic workflows. Notably, their dataset included OPGs from multiple imaging devices, which enhances model generalizability a limitation acknowledged in our study. Moreover, the high recall values obtained in both studies emphasize the clinical relevance of minimizing false negatives in peri-implantitis detection. However, Kıbcak et al. also reported a relatively high number of false positives, consistent with our findings and underscoring the challenge of anatomical superimposition and radiographic artifacts in 2D imaging. These shared limitations further support the necessity of advanced preprocessing techniques, model refinement, and potential multimodal data integration to improve diagnostic specificity [35].

A study by Troiano et al. investigated the potential of radiomic features extracted from intraoral radiographs to predict physiological bone remodelling (PBR) following implant placement. Their machine learning-based approach, which employed a Naive Bayes classifier on radiomic features extracted with the LifeX platform, achieved moderate predictive performance (AUC = 0.751) in identifying sites at risk for greater PBR. Unlike the present study, which focuses on the detection of pathological peri-implant bone loss consistent with peri-implantitis, Troiano et al. aimed to characterize early, physiological changes in bone structure. Furthermore, while their study incorporated clinical variables such as tissue thickness and implant positioning depth into a multivariate analysis, our model was trained solely on radiographic data [36].

In addition, Troiano et al. utilized radiographs from multiple centres and addressed variability by applying ComBat harmonization, ensuring consistency across different imaging systems. In contrast, the present study relied on panoramic radiographs obtained from a single imaging device, which may limit the model’s external validity while enhancing internal consistency. Expanding the dataset to include images from different devices and clinical settings would be essential for improving model generalizability [36].

Zhang et al. explored implant failure prediction using both panoramic and periapical radiographs and demonstrated that combining both modalities improved diagnostic accuracy (87%) compared to either modality alone. While our model achieved comparable performance using only panoramic radiographs, expanding future datasets to include multiple image types could enhance robustness and generalizability. Furthermore, unlike Zhang et al., who used hybrid imaging inputs, our model is trained solely on a single modality, which may offer practical benefits in routine settings but also imposes limitations [33].

Although advanced imaging techniques such as cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) offers superior three-dimensional visualization and greater accuracy in detecting peri-implant bone defects compared to 2D imaging techniques, it also presents some limitations. Studies have shown that CBCT improves diagnostic reliability by enabling detailed assessment of buccal and lingual cortical bone loss, which often remains undetectable on 2D radiographs. However, its clinical use is challenged by beam-hardening artifacts caused by high-density materials such as metal implants and restorations. These artifacts may mimic or obscure peri-implant defects, leading to misdiagnosis or underestimation of bone loss. While CBCT remains valuable in complex diagnostic scenarios, panoramic radiography continues to be widely utilized in clinical practice due to its accessibility, lower radiation dose, and ability to capture the full maxillofacial region in a single scan [35].

Despite these promising results, the study has several limitations. The absence of implant-based metric calibration on panoramic radiographs may introduce measurement uncertainty due to non-uniform magnification. The AI model was not compared directly with the diagnostic performance of experienced clinicians. Prior research has shown high concordance between AI systems and expert assessments, suggesting that future studies should include such comparisons to validate clinical applicability. Peri-implantitis diagnosis in routine practice is not based on radiographic findings alone but also on clinical parameters (e.g., probing depth and bleeding/suppuration on probing). As this retrospective study relied on radiographic data, the absence of comprehensive clinical variables may introduce uncertainty in ground-truth labeling and could affect model training and real-world use if outputs are interpreted without clinical correlation. Therefore, the proposed system should be considered a clinical decision-support/triage tool rather than a standalone diagnostic method. The exclusive use of a single imaging modality and acquisition system may restrict model generalizability to other clinical environments. Incorporating data from multiple devices and institutions would improve robustness. Finally, the dataset size, while sufficient for initial modeling, may not capture the full spectrum of anatomical and pathological variability encountered in clinical practice [31, 37, 38]. Moreover, the use of a single-center cohort and a single panoramic imaging system may introduce domain-specific characteristics (scanner/protocol settings and population differences), and performance may decrease when applied to external institutions or devices due to domain shift. Therefore, multi-center, multi-device external validation and, where appropriate, harmonization strategies are needed to confirm robustness and generalizability. Because IoU and NMS settings influence the sensitivity–false positive trade-off in detection, future studies will examine robustness across alternative IoU/NMS thresholds, IoU matching criteria, and operating points on external datasets. Accordingly, the proposed approach should be interpreted as a radiograph-based decision-support tool rather than a standalone diagnostic method, and clinical confirmation remains essential. Finally, we note that inclusion of a dedicated non-radiomics (image-only) baseline would further strengthen conclusions regarding the incremental value of radiomics and is planned for future work.

Future research should aim to expand dataset diversity, integrate clinical variables into model training, and validate performance across external cohorts. The incorporation of 3D radiographic modalities, when justified by clear diagnostic gain, may further strengthen diagnostic accuracy, especially when combined with radiomic and deep learning methodologies.

Conclusions

Based on the findings of this study, the Mask R-CNN model demonstrated promising performance in detecting peri-implant bone loss on panoramic radiographs. The integration of artificial intelligence into peri-implantitis diagnostics may support clinicians by enabling earlier detection, timely initiation of regenerative therapies, and improved follow-up strategies. Such advancements have the potential to reduce implant failure rates, lower treatment costs, and enhance overall clinical outcomes in implant dentistry.
