Development of the Health Humanities Competence Scale for Multidisciplinary Healthcare Professional Students
Authors: Hsiang‐Chu Pai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHealthcare professionals often require a greater awareness of health and humanities than others. However, a Health Humanities Competence Scale (HHCS) for medical university healthcare professional students (HPS) has not been established yet.AimThis study aims to provide original, reliable, and valid measures for a HHCS for HPS that reflects the humanistic abilities of multidisciplinary healthcare professional students towards themselves, their families, and their professional relationships.MethodsAn observational design was adopted in this study. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to extract the factors of health humanities competence, and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to confirm reliability and validity, through a model fit assessment.ResultsPCA identified three factors in the HHCS for HPS: self, family, and profession‐related health and humanities, which explained 24.78%, 21.98%, and 18.67% of the variance, respectively. The final 19‐item instrument with the three factors explained 65.44% of the total variance. The Cronbach's alpha value was 0.92. Additionally, CFA identified a second‐order factor, HHCS for HPS. Overall, the goodness‐of‐fit indices indicated a satisfactory fit of the entire model (χ2/df = 1.31, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation = 0.069, Comparative Fit Index = 0.94, Tucker‐Lewis Index = 0.93). Additionally, the HHCS for HPS scores were significantly and positively correlated with the empathy subscale scores of the Communication Effectiveness Profile (r = 0.63, p < 0.01). Therefore, the results reveal that the HHCS for HPS demonstrates strong convergent validity.ConclusionThe original three factors (19 items) demonstrated support for use by university healthcare students. A larger sample could strengthen the reliability and validity of the scale. However, the three‐factor model is a useful measurement tool for assessing the demonstration of health humanities competencies by healthcare professional students.Keywords: competence, confirmatory factor analysis, health humanities, healthcare professional students, measurement, principal component analysis

Introduction

With generational transformation, advancement in medical technology, and improvement in people's knowledge and education, it appears that medical humanities cannot cover multidisciplinary humanities. Additionally, people who value health humanities are not only doctors, but also other personnel involved in medical care (e.g., nurses, speech‐language therapists, physiotherapists, and respiratory therapists), patients, and informal caregivers [1, 2]. Particularly in the context of the ongoing global pandemic, which places increasing demands on clinicians, health humanities can help in education and training, and promoting resilience and wellness [3]. While support for global attention to health humanities is rising [4], a Health Humanities Competence Scale (HHCS) for medical university healthcare professional students (HPS) has not been established yet.

Definition of Health Humanities and Its Domains

Health humanities is an interdisciplinary field concerned with understanding human health and disease conditions to prepare knowledgeable and responsive healthcare providers, patients, and family caregivers [5]. Crawford et al. [6] stated that health humanities are marked by championing increased sharing of the humanities capacities and resources of the professional health workforce, informal caregivers, and patients themselves in enhancing healthcare environments. The domain of health humanities encompasses self, family, and professional aspects. Health humanities does not replace medical humanities, but appears to be more inclusive, open, practical, and contemporary. Therefore, it is important for healthcare students to evaluate and cultivate health and humanities abilities for themselves and their familial and professional interactions.

Elements of Health Humanities

Previous research has used domains related to interpersonal skills (e.g., empathy, respect for patient autonomy, the ability to relate to others, and self‐awareness) to evaluate the effectiveness of health humanities courses in undergraduate interdisciplinary curricula [7]. George et al. [8] also stated that the humanities are necessary for the development of physician empathy and communication skills. Previous research demonstrates a positive correlation between the humanistic behaviour abilities and empathy levels of nurses [9]. Saffran and Doobay‐Persaud suggest that, currently, health humanities consider non‐clinical elements of culture that may influence perceptions of health, illness, and the provision of healthcare [10]. These findings indicate that health self‐awareness, culture, empathy, caring for others, and communication skills are important components of health humanities.

Recently, medicine, nursing, and psychological education have begun to emphasise the cultivation of health humanities [11, 12, 13, 14]. Nevertheless, Carr et al. [11] conducted a scoping review of health profession education and reported that the health humanities curriculum focuses on developing students' insight, self‐reflection, and person‐centred communication skills. However, the description of learning outcomes is inconsistent, indicating limited ability to compare health humanities curricula across different programmes and limiting the measurement of implementation effectiveness. Furthermore, Carr et al. [11] suggested that a set of clearly stated assessment tools for humanities competencies could facilitate comparisons of strategic interventions. Recently, Duarte et al. [12] conducted a health humanities seminar with a group of fourth‐year undergraduate nursing students. However, the method of narrative medicine technology was used to analyse its effectiveness. Clearly, there is a need to develop a set of quantitative scales to assess humanistic competencies in health. Thus, this study aims to provide original, reliable, and valid measures for an HHCS for HPS that reflect the humanistic abilities of multidisciplinary healthcare professional students towards themselves, their families, and their professional relationships.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

An observational design was adopted in this study. The sample for this study was recruited from 70 students taking an emotion management course at a medical university in Taiwan; they were studying at four different healthcare‐related colleges (e.g., medicine, medical science and technology, oral medicine, health care and management). Overall, 65 healthcare professional students completed the research tool successfully, which represented a response rate of 92.86%. Factor analysis is often thought to work best with large sample sizes. However, previous studies have found that a sample size of 50 is a reasonable absolute minimum [15, 16]. Additionally, Koran [17] suggested that if the index conditions are satisfied, the sample size should be deemed sufficient. As a test to see if the sample size is adequate, this study employs indices including Bartlett's test of Sphericity, the normed Chi‐square [NC = χ2/df], (Root Mean Square Error of Approximation [RMEA], Comparative Fit Index [CFI], and Tucker‐Lewis Index [TLI]).

Data Collection

Data were collected in March 2024 using a structured questionnaire that included the Health Humanities Competence Scale and the Communication Effectiveness Profile for empathising.

Health Humanities Competence Scale

Health Humanities Competence Scale (HHCS) for Healthcare Professional Students (HPS). This instrument was developed according to the four steps suggested by Davis [18]: (1) concept identification through a literature review; (2) item construction by determining the blueprint, item format, item writing, reading level, and scoring schema; (3) validity issues of the instrument through content validity (expert review), construct validity (factor analysis), and hypothesis testing; and (4) reliability analysis.

The literature review emphasises that medical universities should help students develop health humanities. Students as well as their families benefit from the fact that one day they and their loved ones might face disease themselves. Additionally, school curricula should cultivate humanities among healthcare professionals [11, 19]. Therefore, we designed items from the perspective of three domains: personal, family, and health professionals. Simultaneously, concepts, such as self‐health awareness [7], culture [10], empathy [7, 8], ability to relate to others [7], and communication skills [7, 8] were integrated into established items. Finally, a 26‐item HHCS for HPS was created using a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 0 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicated greater competence in health humanities. The items were reviewed by five healthcare professionals (one doctor, one psychologist, one speech‐language therapist, and two nurse educators) to ensure consistency with the study objective and research instrument.

Communication Effectiveness

The participants' communication effectiveness was measured using the Communication Effectiveness Profile developed by Warner [20]. The scale has 12 questions, and the scoring method uses a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = almost always to 5 = almost never). According to the original scoring method, higher scores indicate lower communication effectiveness. To facilitate readers' understanding, we inverted the scores to 5 = almost always, 4 = very frequently, 3 = frequently, 2 = occasionally, and 1 = almost never. Therefore, higher scores indicated better communication effectiveness.

Data Analysis

IBM SPSS version 22 and Mplus version 7.0 were used to examine data normal distribution, and for principal component analysis (PCA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). First, we examined the skewness and kurtosis of data; the normal distribution assumption will be satisfied when the skewness and kurtosis coefficients are in the range of ±2 and ± 7, respectively [21].

To determine the extraction of the factor phase, PCA, via varimax to rotate factors, was conducted to identify the number of factors [22]. Factors with an eigenvalue greater than one were retained in the model; the Kaiser‐Meyer‐Olkin (KMO) values were between 0.8 and 1.0, and Bartlett's test of sphericity was significant, indicating that the sampling was adequate. Items with loading values of less than 0.5 are excluded [15].

In the CFA phase, a second‐order CFA was performed to test the fit of the model, based on the data. The goodness of fit was determined using several metrics, including the normed Chi‐square (NC = χ 2/df), the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker‐Lewis Index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardised root‐mean‐square residual (SRMR). An NC < 3, and CFI and TLI value > 0.90 were expected, whereas values < 0.08 were regarded a satisfactory fit for the SRMR and RMSEA, respectively [23, 24].

Ethical Consideration

This study was approved by the institutional review board of Chung Shan Medical University Hospital (CSMUH No: CS1‐23126). During the free time in class, the investigator described the purpose of the study to the students, provided questionnaires after receiving their consent, and asked them to fill them anonymously. Students deposited the completed questionnaires in a box.

Results

Characteristics of Participants

Of the 65 participants involved in the study, 16.9% (n = 11) were male and 83.1% (n = 54), female. The mean age was 19.6 years (standard deviation [SD] = 1.36), ranging from 18 to 23 years. Most of the participants (n = 59, 90.8%) had siblings.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Result of PCA showed that the KMO was 0.86, and Bartlett's test of sphericity was significant (chi‐square = 783.508; df = 171; p < 0.001). These results demonstrated that the sample size was adequate for conducting factor analysis [15]. As shown in Table 2, three factors were obtained for the 19 items, and the coefficients for the items were presented by size. First factor: person‐related, with eight items, and factor loadings ranging from 0.65 to 0.78. Second factor: family‐related, with six items, and factor loadings from 0.68 to 0.84. Third factor: profession‐related, with five items, and factor loadings ranging from 0.56 to 0.83. Cronbach's alpha above 0.70 (First factor = 0.89; Second factor = 0.91; Third factor = 0.89) for all three factors, indicated good internal consistency and reliability [15].

Confirmatory Factor Analysis

By employing the maximum likelihood (ML) solution, a second‐order CFA was performed to evaluate the validity of the HHCS for HPS structure on the original PCA model (19 items). The research results showed that although most indices achieved good fitting effects (χ2/df = 214.70/149 = 1.44, CFI = 0.91, TLI = 0.90, and SRMR = 0.076), RMSEA = 0.082 did not, and the value should be lower than 0.080. Nonetheless, the model modification indices suggested that a correlation between the residuals of items 1 and 2, and 21 and 22 would improve the fit (χ2/df = 192.43/147 = 1.31, CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.069, and SRMR = 0.071). Figure 1 presents the model diagrammatically, using standardised estimates. The correlation analysis of the factors presented in Table 3 was significant (p < 0.01), suggesting that the second‐order CFA met the requirements for common factor extraction. Convergent Validity

This study used the Communication Effectiveness Profile subscale developed by Warner to measure students' communication effectiveness in empathy [20]. Prior studies have demonstrated that medical humanities can enhance communication abilities between physicians and patients [25]. Therefore, we conducted a Pearson correlation analysis between the Communication Effectiveness Profile subscale empathising and the HHCS for HPS to perform the convergent validity test. The HHCS for HPS scores were significantly and positively correlated with the Communication Effectiveness Profile subscale empathising scores (r = 0.63, p < 0.01). The three HHCS for HPS factors were also significantly positively correlated with the Communication Effectiveness Profile's subscale empathising scores (for self‐related, r = 0.55 p < 0.01; for family member‐related, r = 0.46, p < 0.01; and for profession‐related, r = 0.54, p < 0.01). Therefore, the results show that the HHCS for HPS has strong convergent validity (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to establish a Health Humanities Competence Scale (HHCS) for students (HSP). This scale can be used to evaluate the competence of students in health humanities. We first created a preliminary 26‐item HHCS for HPS through literature and expert panel reviews. Subsequently, we conducted PCA to extract the 19‐items and three factors for the HHCS for HPS. Seven items were deleted due to item loadings being less than 0.5 [15]. The three factors are personal (24.78%), family‐related (21.98%), and professional (18.67%). This three‐factor solution explained 65.44% of the total variation, which was higher than estimated by Streiner [26], who suggested that factors should explain at least 50% of the total variation. Additionally, it met the guideline established by Hair et al. [22], according to which “a factor solution should account for a minimum of 60% of the total variance” as the rule of thumb for this criterion. The overall explained variance of the HHCS for HPS was attained.

In this study, healthy humanistic competence was defined as an individual's ability to apply humanistic skills, including empathy, emotional intelligence, cultural awareness, communication effectiveness, and resilience, to healthcare. These competencies are also involved in the healthcare of self, family members, and professional clients. These factors are in line with previous research recommendations that healthy humanistic cultivation should benefit both individuals and their families, apart from the profession [11, 19]. These three factors are defined as follows. The term “Personal‐related health and humanities” was used to describe the capacity not only to respect one's own physical and mental well‐being and prospects for professional growth, but also to approach issues with optimism. The PCA conducted in this study showed that this factor accounted for the largest percentage of variation (24.78%), indicating that personal health and humanities are foundational to the whole. This finding also explains why an individual's good physical, mental, and social condition enables them to extend their health and humanities to their family and workplace. The term “family‐related health and humanities” refers to the capacity not only to take care for and support the physical and mental well‐being of family members, but also to recognise and address the challenges faced by one's family. This domain emphasises that individuals can observe, listen to, and reflect on family members' problems, fostering a care‐oriented perspective and building resilient family systems. Similarly, the term “profession‐related health and humanities” refers to the ability to engage with professional healthcare workers, attend to the health and well‐being concerns of service recipients, and perform professional duties in accordance with one's own professional code of conduct. This factor underscores the importance of professionals delivering human‐centred and evidence‐based care in complex clinical settings.

Additionally, Cronbach's alpha coefficients > 0.70 demonstrated sound reliability of the HHCS for HPS and its three subscales [15]. Bartlett's test of sphericity showed a significant result, with a KMO value of 0.86, indicating that the sample size was adequate [15].

CFA revealed An NC < 3, RMSEA, and SRMR < 0.08, and the CFI and TLI value > 0.90, indicating an adequate fit for the model [23, 24]. In other words, the data for the 19‐item HHCS for HPS were better fitted by the second‐order factor model. Furthermore, the results of the HHCS for HPS test validated our hypothesis of a positive link between communication efficacy and scores. This demonstrates the convergent validity of the HHCS for HPS and further suggests that communication efficacy increases with competence in health humanities. This finding is consistent with earlier research showing that medical humanities can improve doctor‐patient communication [25].

Strengths and Weaknesses

This study was conducted on a sample of medical university students from four healthcare‐related colleges, who jointly took a general education course (emotion management). Therefore, the sample size was restricted to 70, and the final number of completed questionnaires was 65; therefore, the small sample size inevitably limited its inference. However, we used a series of strategies to develop the items and conducted a robust statistical analysis. The results also echo Jung and Lee [16], who highlighted that high communality, a small number of factors, and factor analysis can yield stable estimates for small sample sizes. The inclusion of samples from medical universities in different countries will strengthen the reliability and validity of the HHCS for HPS.

Conclusion

The HHCS for HPS was established to assess the competencies of healthcare students in health humanities. We used a variety of methodological techniques, such as literature review, expert panel, PAC, and CFA. A three‐factor, 19‐item model was identified, which included self‐related, family‐related, and profession‐related health humanities competencies. This three‐factor model would be a useful measurement tool for assessing healthcare professional students towards the development of health humanities competencies. Future research could consider including samples from medical universities in different countries for generalizability of the findings.
