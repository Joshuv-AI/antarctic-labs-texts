Longitudinal assessment of cognitive function in patients with breast cancer and lymphoma receiving chemotherapy
Authors: Hongying Sun, Umang Gada, Riham Alieldin, Tianming Zhao, Hannah Teets, James D Bearden, Benjamin T Esparaz, Jamal Misleh, Kah Poh Loh, Allison Magnuson, Mostafa R Mohamed, Nikesha Gilmore, Timothy B Winslow, Annalynn M Williams, Chin-Shang Li, Michelle C Janelsins
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer-related cognitive impairment is a significant concern, yet data assessing long-term changes from pretreatment baselines are limited.MethodsIn this large nationwide study, patients with breast cancer and lymphoma and controls were assessed at pre-chemotherapy, post-chemotherapy, 6-month, 1-year, and 2-year follow-ups. Cognitive function was measured using self-reported and performance-based assessments. The analysis included 225 participants: breast cancer (41 patients/36 controls) and lymphoma (73 patients/75 controls). Cognitive trajectories were estimated using longitudinal linear mixed models, adjusting for demographic, clinical, and psychosocial factors. A minimal clinically important difference cutoff identified changes over time in perceived cognitive impairment.ResultsPatients with breast cancer reported greater cognitive complaints than controls, with declines in FACT-Cog Total (β = −17.17, P < .001), Perceived Cognitive Impairment (β = −11.09, P < .001), and Perceived Cognitive Abilities (β = −3.60, P = .03) from pre-chemotherapy to 2-year follow-up. Declines were observed in memory (Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test [RAVLT] Immediate Recall: β = −1.53, P = .04) and executive function (phone fluency: β = −1.53, P = .02) at 1 year. Patients with lymphoma also showed more complaints, with declines in Perceived Cognitive Impairment (β = −6.19, P = .01), poorer RAVLT Immediate (β = −1.62, P < .001), Delayed Recall (β = −1.72, P < .001), and phone fluency (β = −2.46, P < .001) from pre-chemotherapy to 1-year follow-up.ConclusionCompared with controls, patients with breast cancer and lymphoma showed persistent cognitive worsening up to 1-year posttreatment, and patients with breast cancer reported perceived impairment at 2 years compared with controls.

Introduction

Cancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI) is a common and significant clinical problem in patients with cancer. It is estimated to exist in 30%-40% of patients with cancer before treatment, up to 75% during treatment, and around 35% in the months to years following treatment1-16; these cognitive difficulties may persist or improve over time.17-23 CRCI is generally characterized as problems with memory, attention, executive function, and/or processing speed1 that reduce quality of life (QoL), including daily functioning, return to work, social well-being, and work performance.2,18,24-27

The effect of chemotherapy on cognitive function in patients with cancer has been extensively investigated. Nevertheless, research on the long-term impact of cancer and treatment is lacking.23 As systemic treatment combinations (including chemotherapy) become increasingly effective, the number of survivors of cancer will continue to increase, making the long-term impact of CRCI an increasing concern.

We previously conducted a nationwide longitudinal study through the URCC NCORP Research Base and 22 affiliates to examine cognitive changes in patients with breast cancer and lymphoma receiving chemotherapy (URCC10055; PI: Janelsins). In each cohort, cognitive changes from pre- to post-chemotherapy and 6 months later were assessed, with age- and sex-matched individuals without cancer as controls. Significant cognitive declines were observed in both patients with breast cancer and lymphoma compared with controls.28-30 These large, nationwide studies advanced the understanding of CRCI but were limited by short follow-up periods.

Understanding long-term cognitive trajectories after chemotherapy is critical for addressing impacts on survivors’ QoL and functioning. We conducted follow-up assessments in a subset of our original cohorts at 1- and 2-year post-chemotherapy, with matched controls evaluated at the same intervals. We hypothesized that survivors would manifest significant cognitive declines compared with controls.

Patients and methods

Participants and eligibility

The methods and primary outcomes of the parent study were described in detail elsewhere.28-30 Participants with breast cancer and lymphoma and controls were recruited from 16 US community oncology sites in the NCORP network. Eligible patients were scheduled for chemotherapy (details in Supplementary Methods). This study was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating NCORP sites and the University of Rochester Cancer Center NCORP Research Base, and all participants provided written informed consent. Up to 5 assessments were completed: pre-chemotherapy (A1), posttreatment (A2), 6 months (A3), 1 year (A4), and 2 years (A5; Functional Assessment of Cancer-Therapy–Cognitive Function (FACT-Cog) only). This analysis includes the 225 individuals who completed A4/A5, enabling evaluation of long-term cognitive changes relative to baseline.

Assessment time points

Cognitive assessments were conducted at 4 time points: A1, A2, A3, A4 for objective cognitive assessments and FACT-Cog (new data), and A5 for FACT-Cog only. Because the collection of A4 and A5 data was part of a substudy requiring a study amendment and new consent for participation of 225 individuals from the original cohort, and because accrual to the original breast cancer cohort occurred more quickly than the lymphoma cohort, by the time this amendment was approved, more patients with lymphoma were available within the time windows of study assessments, yielding more patients with lymphoma recruited to this substudy.

A1 represents a subset from our original cohort studies. For FACT-Cog analyses, we incorporated additional data points from participants in our prior study: A2 and A3. Control participants completed identical assessments on the same schedule. We maintained consistent testing paradigms as detailed in our previous publications,28-30 using outcomes that showed significant short-term changes for the current study. All assessments were conducted by formally trained staff using a standardized cognitive assessment manual, as detailed in our previous publications.28,29

FACT-Cog

The FACT-Cog Version 231 assessed perceived cognitive function via a 50-item questionnaire. The FACT-Cog has 4 subscales: Perceived Cognitive Impairment (PCI), Perceived Cognitive Abilities (PCA), the impact of PCI on QoL, and comments from others. Additionally, a total score was computed by summing all 4 subscales. Smaller values on these scales imply greater perceived cognitive difficulty.

We also calculated a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) as an indicator of potentially clinically meaningful change. The MCID was calculated for both a total score and the PCI score for multiple versions of the FACT-Cog (eg, version 2, 3).29,32-34 Because we used version 2 in our research (with 50 items), and to remain consistent with our prior publication,29 we used the MCID based on the ½ standard deviation change on the FACT-Cog total score. We calculated percentages of improvement (better), decline (worse), or no change (same) based on a ½ standard deviation MCID over time: A1 to A2, A1 to A3, A1 to A4, and A1 to A5. In this sub-study, the MCID was a decrease of ≥7.98 points for the breast cohort and a decrease of ≥10.75 points for the lymphoma cohort in FACT-Cog total score using the ½ standard deviation for controls at baseline.

Computerized neuropsychological assessments

Computerized tests from the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB) were used including the Delayed Match to Sample test (12-second delay), Verbal Recognition Memory, Rapid Visual Processing, and One Touch Stockings of Cambridge.35

Paper-based neuropsychological assessments

Paper-based assessments included the Hopkins Verbal Learning and Memory Test–Revised (HVLT-R),35-37 the Trail Making Test (TMT) A (CTMT 1) and B (CTMT 5),38-40 and the Controlled Oral Word Association test.41,42

Phone-based cognitive assessments

The Brief Test of Adult Cognition by Telephone included the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) immediate and delayed recall (based on 1 list learning trial), Digits Backward, Category Fluency, and Backward Counting.43

Covariates

Covariates including patients’ self-identified age, sex, and race were obtained from the clinical record. Participants were categorized as White, Black, or Other, with Other being anyone who was neither White nor Black. Patient medical and treatment information was obtained from the medical record. Baseline reading ability, a proxy for cognitive reserve, was assessed with the Wide Range Assessment Test–4th Edition (WRAT-4) Reading subscale. Anxiety was assessed with the Spielberger Trait Anxiety Inventory,44 and depressive symptoms were measured by an item from the Multidimensional Fatigue Symptom Inventory.45

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristic comparison

Baseline characteristics were compared between patients with cancer and controls using t tests for continuous variables, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for ordinal variables, and χ2 and Fisher exact tests for nominal variables.

Cognitive trajectory analyses

We used longitudinal linear mixed modeling (LMM) to assess cognitive function trajectories from A1 to A4 and A1 to A5, adjusting for baseline covariates. For the breast cohort LMM, fixed effects included time, group, age, education, race, cognitive reserve (WRAT), anxiety, depression, and group-by-time interaction. For the lymphoma cohort, we added sex as a fixed effect along with related interactions (group by time, time by sex, group by sex, group by time by sex). Both models used subject-specific time-point means as random effects with unstructured covariance matrices and restricted maximum likelihood estimation.

We also evaluated group differences in lymphoma type among patients with lymphoma and hormonal therapy usage among patients with breast cancer using LMM with time, group (Hodgkin vs non-Hodgkin or hormonal therapy yes/no), demographic covariates, and group-by-time interaction as fixed effects. Unadjusted data and analysis results appear in Tables S1, S2, S7, and S8.

Sensitivity analyses comparing pre-chemotherapy factors in the subgroups compared with the remainder of the original cohorts

We compared each study subgroup—patients with breast cancer, their matched controls, patients with lymphoma, and their controls—to the rest of their original cohorts at baseline. Sample sizes were breast cancer (n = 41 vs 465), breast cancer controls (n = 36 vs 297), lymphoma (n = 73 vs 119), and lymphoma controls (n = 75 vs 108). Baseline comparisons included LMM factors, hormonal therapy (breast cancer), lymphoma type, and all cognitive measures. Independent t tests were used for continuous, Wilcoxon rank-sum for ordinal, and χ2 or Fisher exact tests for nominal categorical variables.

All analyses were conducted using R and SAS software, with statistical significance set at P < .05, and all tests of statistical significance were 2-sided.

Baseline characteristics

The breast cancer and control groups were balanced for age, race, ethnicity, education, menopausal status, reading scores, and depression (Table 1). Controls showed lower anxiety scores (P = .04).

For the lymphoma cohort with a total sample size of 148, there were 73 patients and 75 controls who completed A1, A2, A3, A4, and/or A5 assessments (Figure 1). The lymphoma patient and control groups were balanced for age, sex, race, ethnicity, education, and reading scores (Table 1). Controls showed lower anxiety scores (P < .001) and lower depression scores (P = .04).

Breast cancer cohort

Sensitivity analyses comparing pre-chemotherapy factors in breast cancer subgroups

To assess the baseline characteristics of the current long-term cohort group compared with the original cohort, we compared the current long-term analysis group to the remainder of the cohort. No statistically significant differences in demographic factors were observed between the included participants and the remainder in the breast cancer cohort. However, statistically significant cognitive differences were observed in A1. Patients with breast cancer included in this study, compared with the remainder, performed significantly better on HVLT-R Immediate Recall (P = .01), HVLT-R Delayed Recall (P = .01), CANTAB One Touch Stockings of Cambridge Mean Choice to Correct Response (P < .001), and CANTAB Verbal Recognition Memory Immediate Recall (P = .03). Similarly, controls paired with patients with breast cancer included in this study had significantly better performance on CTMT 1 (P = .047), CTMT 5 (P = .03), and Phone-based Category Fluency Total Correct (P < .001) compared with the remaining controls.

We performed the same analysis comparing the long-term follow-up cohort with those who did not participate at A2, and the results are consistent with the sensitivity analysis at A1. No significant differences in demographic factors or cognitive measures were observed between included participants and nonparticipants in either the breast cancer or control groups.

Baseline cognitive function

At A1, there were no significant differences between patients with breast cancer and controls on any cognitive outcomes, either before (Table S1) or after adjusting for age, education, race, cognitive reserve (WRAT-4), anxiety, and depression (Table 2).

Longitudinal changes in cognitive function

FACT-Cog

Patients with breast cancer reported significantly greater cognitive complaints from A1 to A5 compared with controls, represented by declines in FACT-Cog Total Score (β = −17.17, 95% CI = −26.79 to −7.54; P < .001) and subscale scores of PCI (β = −11.09, 95% CI = −17.34 to −4.84; P < .001) and PCA (β = −3.60, 95% CI = −6.73 to −0.47; P = .03) (Table 2).

Prevalence of perceived cognitive impairment over time

In patients with breast cancer, 63.4% reported a perceived decline in FACT-Cog scores compared with 8.3% of controls from A1 to A2 (P < .001; Figure 3). From A1 to A2, 46.3% of patients with breast cancer reported a decline in FACT-Cog total scores compared with 13.9% of controls (P = .003). From A1 to A4, 43.9% of patients with breast cancer reported a decline in FACT-Cog scores compared with 22.2% of controls. From A1 to A5, 47.4% of patients with breast cancer reported a decline in FACT-Cog scores compared with 8.6% of controls (P < .001). A similar MCID analysis using FACT-Cog PCI scores yielded comparable proportions of participants classified as better, worse, or same as FACT-Cog total scores, with an MCID threshold of 5.48 for FACT-Cog PCI.

Objective measures of memory, attention, and executive function

Compared with controls, patients with breast cancer showed significantly less improvement in RAVLT immediate recall from A1 to A4 (β = −1.53, 95% CI = −3.00 to −0.08; P = .04) (Table 2 and Table S5). There were no significant differences between the patients with breast cancer and controls in the assessments of attention. Patients with breast cancer showed a significant decline in phone category fluency tests, a measure of executive function, from A1 to A4 (β = −1.53, 95% CI = −2.80 to −0.25; P = .02), compared with controls.

Predictors of cognitive decline

LMM analyses revealed that older age, lower WRAT-4 reading scores, higher baseline anxiety scores, and higher baseline depression scores were all significant predictors of cognitive decline for both self-report and objective assessments, but variations existed from test to test (all P < .05; Table S3).

We also assessed the cognitive impact of hormone therapy exposure over 2 years in the breast cancer group. Patients with breast cancer not receiving hormonal therapy had better perceived cognitive performance, with higher FACT-Cog PCI scores (P = .02) than those patients on hormonal therapy. However, no significant differences were observed in cognitive changes over time or in other measures assessed (RAVLT Immediate Recall and Phone-based Category Fluency).

Lymphoma cohort

Sensitivity analyses comparing pre-chemotherapy factors in lymphoma subgroups

No significant demographic differences were found between patients with lymphoma included and those not included in this study at A1. However, controls paired with patients with lymphoma included in the follow-up had significantly higher education levels (P = .02) and lower depression scores (P = .009) compared with the remaining controls. Patients with lymphoma included in this study demonstrated significantly better baseline cognitive performance in RAVLT Immediate Recall (P = .04), CANTAB Rapid Visual Processing Total Correct (P = .006), Phone-based Backward Counting Final Number (P = .005), Digits Backward Total Correct (P = .02), and Phone-based Category Fluency Total Correct (P = .04) compared with the remainder. Additionally, Hodgkin lymphoma was more prevalent among patients with lymphoma included in this subgroup (P = .04) compared with the remainder. No significant cognitive differences were found between lymphoma controls included and those not included in the follow-up.

We also completed a sensitivity analysis at A2 and came to the same conclusion: the participants in the follow-up cohort performed better than those in the original cohort.

Baseline cognitive function

At A1, patients with lymphoma reported significantly greater cognitive difficulties than controls on self-report measures, specifically Perceived Cognitive Abilities (PCA; β = −3.93, 95% CI = −6.52 to −1.48; P = .003) and FACT-Cog Total Score (β = −8.11, 95% CI = −16.71 to −1.65; P = .04) after adjusting for age, education, race, cognitive reserve (WRAT-4), anxiety, depression, and sex (Table 3 and Table S6). However, no significant baseline differences were found between patients and controls on objective cognitive measures in the lymphoma cohort after similar adjustments (Table 3).

FACT-Cog

Patients with lymphoma reported significantly greater cognitive complaints from A1 to A4 compared with controls, as indicated by declines in the FACT-Cog subscale score of Perceived Cognitive Impairment (PCI; β = −6.19, 95% CI = −11.10 to −1.30, P = .01) (Table 3).

Prevalence of perceived cognitive impairment over time

In patients with lymphoma, 33.3% reported a perceived decline in FACT-Cog scores compared with 14.7% of controls from A1 to A2 (P = .011; Figure 4). From A1 to A3, 35.6% of patients with lymphoma reported a decline in FACT-Cog scores compared with 8.0% of controls (P < .001). From A1 to A4, 35.2% of patients with breast cancer reported a decline in FACT-Cog scores compared with 27.0% of controls. From A1 to A5, 31.3% of patients with breast cancer reported a decline in FACT-Cog scores compared with 16.9% of controls. When applied to FACT-Cog PCI scores, the MCID approach produced a similar pattern of classification (better, worse, or same) as total FACT-Cog, yielding an MCID cutoff of 6.56 for PCI.

Objective measures of memory, attention, and executive function

Compared with controls, patients with lymphoma experienced significant declines from A1 to A4 in telephone-based RAVLT immediate recall (β = −1.62, 95% CI = −2.33 to −0.91, P < .001) and delayed recall (β = −1.72, 95% CI = −2.62 to −0.82, P < .001) (Table 3). There were no significant differences between patients with lymphoma and controls in assessments of attention. Patients with lymphoma also had a significant decline in phone-based category fluency tests, a measure of executive function, from A1 to A4 (β = −2.46, 95% CI = −3.55 to −1.37, P < .001).

Predictors of cognitive decline

LMM analyses reveal that older age, Black and Other races (compared with White), lower education level (compared with college or higher), lower WRAT-4 reading score, higher baseline anxiety score, and higher baseline depression score were all significant predictors of cognitive decline, but variations existed from test to test (all P < .05; Table S4). We also observed that sex significantly predicted cognitive decline, depending on the tests.

Patients with Hodgkin lymphoma (n = 20, median age = 38 years) were significantly younger than patients with non-Hodgkin lymphoma (n = 53, median age = 60 years; P < .001). Analyses comparing cognitive outcomes between patients with Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphoma revealed no significant differences between the 2 groups in cognitive measures from A1 to A4 and A5, including FACT-Cog PCI, RAVLT Immediate Recall, and phone-based Category Fluency.

Discussion

Whereas our prior work has shown that patients with breast cancer and lymphoma experience short-term cognitive declines for up to 6 months post-chemotherapy,28-30 this study examined long-term perceived cognitive function for 2 years and objective neurocognitive performance for 1 year. Results demonstrated that patients with breast cancer reported significantly greater cognitive complaints, with perceived declines persisting for 2 years posttreatment and measurable objective declines observed at 1 year. Patients with lymphoma also demonstrated significant memory declines for 1 year post-chemotherapy. This research highlights distinct differences in cognitive trajectories between patients with breast cancer and lymphoma (Figure 2). These findings suggest the need for long-term monitoring of subjective complaints in women diagnosed with breast cancer, particularly those receiving hormonal therapy.46,47

To our knowledge, this study is among the few studies20,28,48-54 evaluating 2-year cognitive changes in patients with breast cancer and lymphoma in a national cohort. Longitudinal studies in patients with breast cancer have typically conducted only limited follow-up, rarely extending beyond 6 months after completion of chemotherapy, whereas this study focused on long-term effects.48 Several longitudinal studies found that cognitive impairments observed immediately following chemotherapy improved within short-term follow-ups; however, some of these studies reveal that patients do not return to their baseline level of function.20,28,49-51 Our research is consistent with other longitudinal studies, showing cognitive impairments persisted in breast cancer survivors for more than 1 year.52-54 Similarly, our findings in lymphoma survivors align with prior research reporting persistent cognitive impairments.55-58 Additional cohort studies further demonstrated long-term cognitive difficulties in lymphoma survivors across multiple domains.59-62

This study has several notable strengths. It is a nationwide study involving community oncology clinics across diverse geographic regions, enhancing its generalizability. The study employed a rigorous design with age-matched controls for patients with breast cancer and both age-matched and sex-matched controls for patients with lymphoma. Integrating subjective (FACT-Cog) and objective cognitive measures provides a comprehensive view of cognitive function. Interestingly, phone-based assessments revealed the most significant findings, similar to our prior studies.28,30 Our focus on all adults aged 21 years or older allowed us to study cognitive changes across the age continuum.

A key limitation of this study was the relatively small sample size. Whereas sensitivity analyses showed minimal sociodemographic differences between groups, substudy participants demonstrated statistically higher baseline cognitive function than the larger original cohort, indicating possible selection bias, with “healthier” patients more likely to participate in the follow-up assessments. Nevertheless, we observed significant cognitive performance differences over time, with patients with breast cancer and lymphoma performed worse than controls. Thus, these data suggest that the burden of cognitive decline may be greater than what we observed. Because this was a substudy to inform future research, we did not adjust for multiplicity, increasing the risk of type I error.

Studies revealing persistent cognitive complaints and performance-based cognitive decline in cancer patients underscore the need for clinicians to integrate cognitive screening into care, monitor cognitive changes during treatment, and implement interventions when necessary. Future research should focus on developing interventions to mitigate the effects of CRCI.63
