The Emotional and Cognitive Effects of Synchronizing to Music During Acute Exercise: An fNIRS Study
Authors: Yixue Quan, Kirk N Olsen, William Forde Thompson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSynchronizing movements to music enhances exercise performance and enjoyment, yet its short‐term effects on attention and cognition remain underexplored. This study examined the influence of synchronous music, asynchronous music, and a no‐music control condition on emotional responses, perceived exertion, attentional focus, and executive function during moderate‐intensity cycling. Twenty‐seven healthy young adults completed three cycling sessions under different conditions in a within‐subjects design. Measures included perceived valence, perceived activation, ratings of perceived exertion (RPE), state attention, remembered pleasure, intrinsic motivation, Flanker Squared Task (FST) performance, and changes in cerebral oxygenation in the left prefrontal cortex (PFC) using functional near‐infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS). Compared to the asynchronous and control conditions, synchronizing to music during exercise significantly enhanced perceived valence, perceived activation, intrinsic motivation, and external focus while reducing RPE. When the central arrow in the target stimulus conflicted with the surrounding flanker arrows in the FST (i.e., stimulus incongruent trials), synchronization tended to improve performance, an effect fully mediated by reductions in RPE. External focus during exercise was positively associated with FST performance, partially mediated by reduced cerebral oxygenation in the PFC. The findings suggest that synchronizing movements to music enhances the psychological experience of exercise and may support executive function by increasing external focus and reducing RPE.Keywords: aerobic exercise, executive function, left prefrontal cortex, music, synchronization

Introduction

Music is often used to accompany exercise and can evoke more positive hedonic experiences compared to exercising in silence [1, 2]. Music can be used synchronously or asynchronously with exercise. When used asynchronously, music serves as a background stimulus, acting as a distraction or pleasurable accompaniment in the absence of conscious synchronization of movements to the music. In contrast, synchronization involves conscious timing and regulation of movement patterns to align them with the musical tempo [3, 4].

Synchronizing to music is known to benefit exercise involving repetitive movement patterns, such as walking, running, and cycling. Compared to exercising without music, exercising while synchronizing to music reduces muscle activation, improves walking/running speed and economy, extends endurance time, enhances mood, and lowers perceived exertion [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. These benefits may stem from the manipulation of attention away from bodily sensations of fatigue and towards the rhythmic structure in music [1, 11]. Additionally, synchronization requires less energy to replicate rhythmic movements, promoting neuromuscular efficiency and decreasing the metabolic cost of exercise [4, 12].

Conversely, asynchronization of exercise and music may increase metabolic demand arising from inefficiencies in movement [13]. Compared to asynchronous music, synchronous music has been shown to improve running performance [14], reduce limb discomfort [2], extend cycling endurance [15], lower oxygen consumption [13], enhance arousal and mood [16], and reduce ratings of perceived exertion (RPE) [17]. A meta‐analysis also reports that synchronization has a larger effect size on endurance performance than asynchronization [3]. These benefits may stem from more efficient attentional engagement: synchronous music encourages an active focus on coordinating movement with rhythmic cues, which can facilitate motor efficiency. In contrast, asynchronous music may divert attention passively toward musical elements such as melody or harmony, leading to reduced sensorimotor coupling and less efficient movement [2, 9].

Despite the established physical, psychological, and psychophysical benefits of synchronous music in exercise, limited research has explored its cognitive impact. Aside from its value for physical fitness, many people engage in exercise for its benefits to mental and brain health. Meta‐analyses have demonstrated that acute moderate‐intensity exercise improves executive function in young adults [18, 19, 20], especially for exercise shorter than 20 min [21]. Executive function refers to a set of higher‐order cognitive processes required for focused attention and concentration, such as planning, action sequencing, inhibition of competing impulses, and goal‐conflict resolution [22, 23]. This benefit may occur because acute exercise increases cerebral oxygenation in brain regions associated with executive functioning on the prefrontal cortex (PFC), especially the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), which mediates the improvements in executive function [24, 25]. The left DLPFC plays a key role in emotional regulation, RPE, endurance performance, and executive function [26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. Observed correlations between emotion (i.e., arousal and mood), DLPFC activation, and executive function performance suggest that exercise‐induced positive emotions may enhance executive performance via DLPFC activation [31, 32]. Despite this correlational evidence, this potential mechanism is not strongly supported by causal statistical analysis.

Several studies have explored the cognitive benefits of music‐supported exercise in healthy young adults. Research using asynchronous music during moderate‐intensity exercise has yielded inconsistent findings. Some studies have found that exercising with asynchronous music leads to significant improvements in tasks such as the Sternberg [33] and Stroop [34] tasks, compared to exercising without music. Other studies report no significant differences in Stroop task performance when comparing asynchronous music with silence [35] or a metronome sound [32]. Fukuie et al. [36] showed that exercise with groove rhythm improved executive function more than a white‐noise metronome, but only among participants who enjoyed audio‐motor entrainment, and this was limited to very light‐intensity exercise. Chen et al. [16] examined the impact of music tempo matching and found improvements in Stroop task performance when exercise heart rate matched the music tempo, compared to slower‐ or faster‐mismatched exercise heart rates. However, their approach focused on differences in tempo rather than comparing exercise conditions in which movements were either synchronous or asynchronous with the musical rhythm. They matched musical tempo to the exercise heart rate rather than cadence, which does not align with the definition of synchronization.

The aim of the current investigation was to compare the effects of synchronous music, asynchronous music, and no‐music control on emotional responses, state attention, intrinsic motivation, RPE, executive function, and prefrontal oxygenation during moderate‐intensity aerobic exercise in young adults (18–35 years). Consistent with previous research, our first hypothesis was that synchronizing with music would have a stronger effect on improving positive emotions and reducing RPE compared to exercise with asynchronous music or no music. Our second hypothesis was that synchronization would lead to greater improvements in executive function via increased prefrontal oxygenation. This hypothesis follows from the assumption that enhanced positive emotions improve executive function through increasing oxygenation of the PFC [31, 32, 36].

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited through social media and flyers posted at Macquarie University. Eligibility criteria included: (1) age between 18 and 35 years; (2) no history of major health issues, including cardiovascular disease (e.g., heart attack and stroke), respiratory disease (e.g., asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), cardiopulmonary disease, metabolic disorders (e.g., diabetes), joint problems or musculoskeletal disorders, psychiatric or neurological disorders, or other condition that prevents engagement in exercise; (3) no history of alcohol or drug abuse; (4) normal weight (18.5 < BMI < 25); (5) not pregnant or recently given birth; (6) regular physical activity (> 75 min per week) for the past 6 months; (7) normal hearing; (8) not a professional athlete or professional musician; (9) classification as low risk according to the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire [37], with “no” responses to all seven questions; (10) right‐handedness, which was determined by answering the question “Which hand do you prefer to use for daily activities, such as writing, throwing, using a toothbrush, and using a spoon?”.

The optimal sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1 [38]. The calculation employed the “ANOVA: Repeated measures, within factors” test with the following parameters: power = 0.8, α = 0.05, f = 0.31 (converted from partial Ƞ2 = 0.09 as reported in Chen et al. [16]), number of groups = 1, and number of measurements = 3. It indicated a minimum requirement of 18 participants. The final sample comprised 27 participants (22 females, four males, and one non‐binary), aged 20–35 years (M = 26.67, SD = 5.22). All participants reported regular aerobic exercise, averaging 199 min per week. Twenty participants regularly exercised with music. No participants withdrew from the study. All participants signed the informed consent form. This study was approved by the Macquarie University Human Ethics Committee (Reference No. 520231289053954).

Experimental Design

This was a within‐subjects experiment. Participants visited the laboratory in four sessions that were scheduled at the same time of day, with intervals of 48 h to 1 week between visits. Participants were instructed to (1) avoid vigorous exercise for 24 h presession; (2) abstain from caffeine and alcohol for 24 h presession; (3) not eat within 2 h presession; and (4) arrive hydrated. A cycle ergometer (Kickr smart bike, Wahoo Fitness, Atlanta, USA) was used for cycling, with saddle height adjusted based on participant's height. Heart rate was monitored throughout each visit using Polar H10 (Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland). Music was played through external speakers (Sterling TX930). Inquisit 6 [39] was employed to display instructions and administer measures and the Flanker Squared Task (FST).

On the first visit, participants were informed of the procedure, practiced the FST, and performed a progressive maximal exercise test to determine the maximal heart rate (HRmax). For the exercise test, participants were required to maintain a cadence of 60 revolutions per minute (RPM), monitored via a digital display screen connected to the Wahoo application. Following a 2‐min warm‐up at 15 watts (W), resistance increased incrementally (male: 20 W; female: 15 W) per minute until the termination criteria specified in the American College of Sports Medicine guidelines [40]. All participants terminated the test upon reaching an RPE of 19 or 20. The mean resistance and maximal heart rate during the test were 143.3 W (SD = 33.7) and 155.7 BPM (SD = 12.06), respectively.

The second, third, and fourth visits were the experimental phases. Participants completed three 15‐min moderate‐intensity cycling bouts under three conditions: synchronization with music (130 BPM), asynchronization with music (110 or 150 BPM), and no‐music control. In the asynchronization and control conditions, participants were instructed to maintain a 65 RPM cycling cadence using a visual display connected to the cycle ergometer APP (Wahoo: Ride, Run, Train, Wahoo Fitness). In the synchronization condition, participants were instructed to synchronize their pedaling cadence with the musical beats. With each half‐revolution of the pedals matching each beat of the music [2], the music was set at 130 BPM, which is twice the instructed cycling cadence of 65 RPM in the other two conditions, ensuring a consistent cadence across conditions. In the asynchronization condition, participants were randomly assigned to listen to either 110 BPM or 150 BPM music, to minimize tempo‐related effects on exercise performance [3, 13, 41]. The selection of 110 BPM and 150 BPM was intended to prevent unintentional synchronization by creating a ± 20 BPM temporal mismatch from 130 BPM. The order of the conditions was counterbalanced using the Latin Square to reduce the impact of learning or carryover effects.

Each experimental session followed a structured sequence. Participants first watched a 5‐min guided box breathing meditation video to assess their resting heart rates (HRrest). This was followed by a pre‐test of the FST. Upon mounting the bike, participants completed a 30‐s baseline task, during which they sat with eyes closed and counted from 1 to 50 to prevent mind‐wandering [42]. They then rated their pre‐cycling perceived valence and perceived activation. Following this, participants completed a 3‐min warm‐up, during which the experimenter increased resistance to reach the target heart rate, set to 50% of the heart rate reserve for moderate‐intensity exercise [43]. It was calculated using the Karvonen formula: Target HR = [(HRmax − HRrest) × 50%] + HRrest. Throughout the 15‐min cycling, the researcher monitored heart rates and adjusted resistance to maintain the rate within ± 2 BPM of the target. Participants rated their perceived valence, perceived activation, RPE, and state attention every 3 min using the Cedrus Response Pad (RB‐840) attached to a tablet holder on the bike. A 2‐min cool‐down period followed, with resistance set to 0 W and participants pedaling at their comfortable speed. After dismounting, participants had a 5‐min interval before the post‐test of the FST. During this time, they rated remembered pleasure, intrinsic motivation, music preference, and familiarity, and could then either stretch or sit quietly.

Music Stimuli

Three pieces of music lasting approximately 15 min or longer were chosen so they could be played continuously throughout each bout. Different music tracks were used in the music conditions to reduce the impact of repeated exposure [44, 45]. For the synchronization condition, Santa Esmeralda's Don't let me be misunderstood (119 BPM) was used and adjusted to 130 BPM. For the asynchronization condition, I fly in my dreams (156 BPM) by Andrew Batterham and The house of the rising sun (120 BPM) by Santa Esmeralda and Leroy Gomez were used and adjusted to 150 BPM and 110 BPM, respectively. These pieces of music were processed using Audacity [46] to normalize perceived loudness to −23 loudness units full scale and to adjust tempi to their corresponding values.

Measures

During the 15‐min cycling bouts, participants provided ratings at 3‐min intervals for the following measures. Perceived valence and perceived activation were assessed using the Feeling Scale [47] and the Felt Arousal Scale [48], respectively. RPE was measured using the Borg's Perceived Exertion Scale [49], which ranges from 6 (no exertion) to 20 (maximal exertion). State attention was assessed using a single item, “Please rate your current focus of attention,” on a 10‐point scale ranging from 0 (external thoughts) to 10 (internal thoughts) [50, 51].

Immediately following the exercise, participants completed additional measures. They rated the remembered pleasure of the exercise session on an integer between −100 (very unpleasant) and +100 (very pleasant), responding to the question “How did the exercise session make you feel?” [52, 53]. Intrinsic motivation was assessed using the 7‐item interest/enjoyment subscale of the Intrinsic Motivation Inventory [54] that is used to evaluate the subjective experience associated with a target activity (e.g., “I enjoyed doing this activity very much”, “I thought this was a boring activity” (reverse‐coded), “This activity was fun to do”). Music preference and familiarity were assessed by the questions, “Please rate your preference for the music” and “Please rate your familiarity with the music”, each rated on a 7‐point Likert scale. A 7‐point commitment question asked, “To what extent did you adhere to the instruction?”. Additionally, a 7‐point synchronization perception question “To what extent did you perceive yourself synchronize with the music?” was included in the synchronization condition.

Flanker Squared Task

Given the multilingual nature of the participant pool, the Stroop Task, which is commonly used to assess executive function, was not employed in this experiment due to its reliance on language proficiency [55]. To avoid imposing additional cognitive demands with language differences, executive function was assessed using the FST, which is a language‐independent measure [56]. It measures a specific aspect of executive function known as attention control, also referred to as central executive or interference control [21]. It involves attending to task‐relevant information while attempting to ignore task‐irrelevant external events and internal thoughts. Unlike the traditional Flanker Task, it incorporates an additional level of conflict by including multiple distractors in varying spatial arrangements. Participants need to earn as many points as possible within a set time frame. Points are awarded for accurate responses and deducted for incorrect responses. The test demonstrates high internal consistency reliability (α = 0.94), test–retest reliability (r = 0.74), and convergent validity with similar measures, and it shows resistance to practice effects. During the first visit, participants completed a 60‐s practice trial, which was repeated until they made no more than two errors and explicitly indicated that they understood the task. In the experimental phases, both pre‐ and post‐tests consisted of a 10‐s practice phase and a 60‐s test phase.

In each trial, participants are presented with a target stimulus and two response options, each consisting of one central arrow and four flanking arrows. Participants need to match the direction of the flanking arrows of the target stimulus with the direction of the central arrow in one of the two response options. A detailed task description can be found in Burgoyne et al. [56]. The task includes four trial types: (1) fully congruent, where both the target stimulus and response options are congruent; (2) stimulus congruent and response options incongruent, where the target stimulus is congruent while the response options are incongruent; (3) stimulus incongruent and response options congruent, where the target stimulus is incongruent while the response options are congruent; and (4) fully incongruent, where both the target stimulus and response options are incongruent. Performance was evaluated using the total points earned and points earned for each test trial type. For each condition, change scores were calculated by subtracting the score during the pre‐test from the score during the post‐test: ΔFST_total for the total score and ΔFST_fullcon, ΔFST_congruent, ΔFST_incongruent, and ΔFST_fullincon for the change score in the fully congruent trial, stimulus congruent–response incongruent trial, stimulus incongruent–response congruent trial, and fully incongruent trial, respectively.

NIRS Data Acquisition and Analysis

Left PFC oxygenation was measured using the PortaLite NIRS device (Artinis Medical Systems, Gelderland, The Netherlands). It has one receiver and three transmitters emitting near‐infrared light at wavelengths of 750 nm and 840 nm. It was positioned at Fp1 according to the international 10–20 system [57], secured with double‐sided adhesive tapes, and shielded from ambient light interference using a headband. It remained on the head throughout each session. Data were collected at a sampling frequency of 10 Hz, and an age‐based differential path length factor was applied. OxySoft v3.5.15.4 was used to preprocess the data with a moving Gaussian filter to reduce high‐frequency motion artifacts. This approach has been shown to offer better noise reduction accuracy over Butterworth bandpass filtering in prefrontal and motor execution tasks [58]. The average concentrations of O2Hb and HHb were calculated for the pre‐test, 30‐s baseline, 15‐min exercise, and post‐test phases of each exercise bout. Hemoglobin difference (HbDiff = O2Hb − HHb) was used as the indicator of cerebral oxygenation, as it is sensitive to changes in regional cerebral blood flow [59, 60]. To calculate the change in cerebral oxygenation (ΔHbDiff) for cycling, the mean values during the 30‐s baseline were subtracted from the mean values during the 15‐min exercise. ΔHbDiff for the FST was calculated by subtracting the mean values during the post‐test from the pre‐test.

Statistical Analyses

One‐sample t‐tests were conducted on ΔHbDiff to assess whether task‐relevant brain oxygenation in the left PFC significantly differed from zero, indicating a significant change in neural activity in each condition. To assess differences between the three experimental conditions, linear mixed‐effect models were performed using the lme4 package [61] in R [62], with participant as a random factor and the order of conditions as a covariate. For the four measures assessed during the 15‐min cycling (i.e., perceived valence, perceived activation, RPE, and state attention), the fixed factor was Condition × Time Intervals. For other measures (i.e., remembered pleasure, intrinsic motivation, performance on the FST, ΔHbDiff for the FST, and ΔHbDiff for cycling), Condition was used as the fixed factor. The statistical significance of main effects and/or interaction effects were determined using likelihood ratio tests, comparing models with and without the factor of interest using the anova() function [63, 64]. Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustments were performed to identify significant differences between conditions using the emmeans() function. Spearman's correlations were applied to explore associations between prefrontal oxygenation, executive function task performance, and psychological responses, as most variables were not normally distributed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Manipulation Checks

There was no significant difference between Conditions for average HR (χ2 (2) = 3.92, p = 0.14), suggesting exercise intensity remained consistent across Conditions. Pre‐cycling perceived valence (χ2 (2) = 0.46, p = 0.79) and perceived activation (χ2 (2) = 0.28, p = 0.87) were also similar between Conditions. Music preference (F(2) = 1.35, p = 0.27) and music familiarity (F(2) = 0.95, p = 0.40) did not differ significantly between the three music tracks, suggesting the tracks used in the two music conditions were comparable in musical factors that could influence enjoyment [66] and RPE [67] during exercise. Independent t‐tests did not reveal a significant difference between the 110 BPM and 150 BPM music tracks in the asynchronization condition on any outcome. The average RPMs were 66.04 (SD = 1.72), 65.52 (SD = 1.87), and 65.26 (SD = 1.16) for synchronization, asynchronization, and control conditions, respectively, confirming consistent cycling cadence across conditions. In the asynchronization condition, the average RPM of 65.5 corresponded to approximately 131 BPM, further confirming that participants did not synchronize with the music at either 110 BPM or 150 BPM. Ratings of perceived synchronization to the music in the synchronization condition ranged from 4 to 6 (M = 5.33, SD = 0.62).

Psychological and Psychophysical Outcomes

Table 2 shows the linear mixed‐effect model results for psychological and psychophysical outcomes. Significant main effects of Condition were found in all outcomes. Pairwise comparisons (Table 3) indicated that the synchronization condition was associated with significantly higher perceived valence, perceived activation, intrinsic motivation, and attention toward external thoughts compared to both asynchronization and control conditions. Intrinsic motivation was also significantly higher in the asynchronization condition than in the control. RPE was significantly lower in the synchronization condition compared to asynchronization. Although there was a significant main effect of condition on remembered pleasure, the Bonferroni‐adjusted pairwise comparisons did not reveal significant differences between conditions.

Prefrontal Oxygenation

During the FST, a significant change of oxygenation in the left PFC (ΔHbDiff_FST) was observed in the synchronization condition (t(26) = 2.34, p = 0.027), while asynchronization (t(26) = 1.63, p = 0.12) and control (t(26) = 0.64, p = 0.53) conditions showed no significant change of oxygenation. No significant differences were found between Conditions for ΔHbDiff_FST (χ2 (2) = 0.89, p = 0.64). The trends showed greater improvements in HbDiff after synchronization than the other two conditions.

During the 15‐min cycling period, the changes in prefrontal oxygenation (ΔHbDiff_cycling) were significant in all conditions: synchronization (t(26) = 4.7, p < 0.001), asynchronization (t(26) = 6.99, p < 0.001), and control (t(26) = 5.86, p < 0.001). ΔHbDiff_cycling did not significantly differ between conditions (χ2 (2) = 3.14, p = 0.21). The raw ΔHbDiff_cycling means were the lowest under the synchronization condition, followed by the asynchronization and control conditions.

Performance on the FST

Table S2 presents the raw means and SD values for pre‐test, post‐test, and change scores on the FST trials by condition. Significant differences in pre‐test scores were observed across Conditions, likely due to random variation. To account for the baseline variability and to directly assess improvements in executive function, we focused our analysis on change scores, allowing direct interpretation of the effect of experimental conditions on performance improvement. A marginal difference between Conditions was observed on ΔFST_incongruent (χ2 (2) = 5.33, p = 0.07), with the synchronization condition showing a marginally significant trend toward better performance than the asynchronization condition (t = 2.27, p = 0.08). The other trials did not differ significantly across Conditions. Nevertheless, trends indicated higher improvements under the synchronization condition compared to the asynchronization and control conditions for ΔFST_total, ΔFST_congruent, and ΔFST_fullincon.

Associations Between Prefrontal Oxygenation, FST Performance, and Psychological Outcomes

The correlation matrix (Table S3) revealed that state attention at 15‐min was positively correlated with ΔHbDiff_FST (ρ = 0.32, p = 0.003) and negatively correlated with ΔFST_total (ρ = −0.28, p = 0.01) and ΔFST_congruent (ρ = −0.31, p = 0.005). Perceived activation at 15‐min (ρ = 0.26, p = 0.02) and intrinsic motivation (ρ = 0.25, p = 0.03) were also positively correlated with ΔHbDiff_FST. ΔFST_incongruent was positively correlated with perceived valence at 15‐min (ρ = 0.26, p = 0.02) and negatively correlated with RPE at 15‐min (ρ = 0.32, p = 0.004). ΔHbDiff_FST was negatively correlated with all performance changes in FST trials except for ΔFST_congruent. ΔHbDiff_cycling showed no significant associations with psychological outcomes.

Mediation Analyses

Baron and Kenny [68] outlined three prerequisites to establish a mediation: the predictor affects (1) the dependent variable and (2) the mediator, and (3) the mediator affects the dependent variable. The above correlations appear to suggest that ΔHbDiff_FST may mediate the relationship between state attention and ΔFST_total. Linear mixed‐effect models were conducted with variables of interest, Condition, and the order of conditions as fixed factors and participants as the random factor. Results indicated that state attention at 15‐min significantly predicted both ΔHbDiff_FST (β = 0.15, t = 2.55, p = 0.013) and ΔFST_total (β = −0.5, t = −2.71, p = 0.008). Since ΔHbDiff_FST also significantly predicted ΔFST_total (β = −0.83, t = −2.33, p = 0.02), the criteria for mediation were met. After adding ΔHbDiff_FST to the model, state attention remained a significant predictor of ΔFST_total, though its effect was reduced (β = −0.42, t = −2.17, p = 0.03). This suggests that, regardless of experimental conditions, exercise‐induced attention to external thoughts improved FST performance, partially mediated by decreased prefrontal oxygenation.

We next explored whether psychological or psychophysical factors mediated the effect of experimental conditions on FST performance. The only condition‐related difference was observed in the incongruent FST trial, where the synchronization condition marginally outperformed the asynchronization condition. The synchronization condition was dummy‐coded (1 = synchronization, 0 = asynchronization or control) as the predictor. Correlational results showed that ΔFST_incongruent was significantly correlated with RPE and perceived valence at 15‐minutes. With the order of condition as the covariate and participants as the random factor, ΔFST_incongruent was predicted by RPE (β = −0.33, t = −3.7, p < 0.001) but not by perceived valence (β = 0.25, t = 1.95, p = 0.057). The mediation effect of RPE was then analyzed. Linear mixed‐effect models revealed that the synchronization condition significantly predicted both ΔFST_incongruent (β = 0.67, t = 2.05, p = 0.045) and RPE (β = −0.53, t = −2.01, p = 0.049), meeting the mediation criteria. When RPE was added to the model, the effect of the synchronization condition on ΔFST_incongruent became nonsignificant (β = 0.44, t = 1.34, p = 0.19). This finding indicates that synchronization enhances performance on incongruent FST trials indirectly by reducing RPE.

Discussion

Participants completed three 15‐min moderate‐intensity cycling sessions in a control condition (silence), an asynchronization condition (with background music), and a synchronization condition (intentional cadence‐matching with music). The first hypothesis was supported—synchronization significantly enhanced perceived valence, perceived activation, and intrinsic motivation, while reducing RPE compared to the other conditions. These findings align with prior research [2, 4, 9, 10] and highlight the unique benefits of rhythmic motor entrainment for maximizing hedonic responses and motivation for exercise, beyond the mere presence of music [1]. However, the mechanisms responsible for the affective and motivational benefits of synchronization over asynchronization are not well understood. We propose two mechanisms.

First, synchronization is less cognitively demanding. Humans have an innate predisposition to synchronize movement with rhythmic auditory stimuli. The brain might have an internal clock that can coordinate afferent nerve signals (e.g., auditory temporal signals from music) with the efferent nerve signals that control rhythmic movements [12]. Indeed, individuals can spontaneously adjust their cadence to match changes in music tempo without awareness and explicit instruction [69]. Moreover, music rhythm provides precise temporal cues that facilitate movement planning within the motor system. Aligning movements to these rhythms, in turn, offers immediate feedback on the accuracy of rhythmic anticipations, which creates a feedforward‐feedback loop for rhythmic entrainment [70, 71]. This auditory–motor synchronization also enhances cadence consistency, reducing energy loss caused by frequent changes in movement rates and improving movement economy [8]. Together, these benefits of synchronization suggest it can improve movement efficiency, reduce self‐regulation depletion, and enhance relaxation, ultimately making exercise more enjoyable [5, 72, 73]. In contrast, asynchronization imposes a cognitive burden by requiring participants to compete with their innate tendency to synchronize, so they have to allocate greater attentional demands in overriding their preferred movement rhythms [69, 74, 75]. It also disrupts movement planning and accurate rhythmic anticipation, which may lead to greater variability in movement rates. This increased cognitive burden can be reflected by higher left PFC oxygenation during cycling in asynchronization compared to control and synchronization conditions. Increased prefrontal oxygenation indicated reduced exercise automaticity and greater mental effort [76]. These results suggest that asynchronization may require a greater need for cognitive control, even compared to the no‐music control that did not involve conscious mental competition. However, this interpretation should be considered with caution, as differences in PFC oxygenation during cycling were not significant across conditions.

Second, synchronization more effectively redirects attention from internal physiological sensations to external task‐unrelated signals (i.e., music). The parallel processing model posits that dissociative strategies occupy limited attentional capacity, preventing fatigue and discomfort from dominating focal awareness during low‐ and moderate‐intensity exercise [77]. Music serves as such a dissociative strategy by engaging auditory pathways, thereby reducing RPE and increasing positive affective responses [71, 78, 79]. In synchronization, individuals were more likely to focus on music, as they needed to pay attention to both the musical tempo and whether their movements matched the tempo. In contrast, in the asynchronization condition, music primarily acted as a background stimulus, attracting attention through its inherent properties (e.g., harmony and melody) but not to the same degree as synchronization [2]. Indeed, our findings show significantly higher external focus during synchronization compared to asynchronization. Nevertheless, in our experiment, participants in the asynchronization condition were instructed to maintain a certain cadence, which might direct their attention internally. It is recommended that in future studies, music with tempi that do not permit synchronization during cycling (e.g., 90 and 180 BPM) can be used to examine differences in attention allocation between conditions. Furthermore, EEG could be used to provide neurobiological evidence of attentional switching, indicated by increased beta frequencies, suppressed low‐frequency waves, and enhanced frontal–central communication [78, 79, 80].

The second hypothesis was not entirely supported because no statistically significant differences between conditions were observed in the FST scores. However, synchronization led to a trend toward higher improvements in the stimulus incongruent–response congruent trials compared to asynchronization. Dummy‐coding the synchronization condition revealed that it significantly predicted improvements in the incongruent FST trial, and this effect was fully mediated by reductions in RPE. Xie and Zhang [81] found that physical exertion can impair concurrent working memory performance when both tasks demand high perceived effort. This suggests a shared mental representation of perceived effort across cognitive and physical tasks, meaning that resources engaged in one modality will affect another. To account for our findings, synchronization makes participants experience less exertion, freeing resources for engaging in cognitively demanding trials that require greater inhibition and attentional control to suppress conflicting stimuli (i.e., the stimulus incongruent trial). Together with the nonsignificant differences in prefrontal oxygenation across conditions, this result suggests that synchronization enhances executive function by reducing perceived exertion of exercise rather than directly influencing prefrontal activity.

The other mediation analysis showed that regardless of conditions, a greater tendency to focus on external thoughts improves executive function, mediated by a decrease in change in prefrontal oxygenation. The negative correlation between change in PFC oxygenation and FST performance might indicate an improvement in neural efficiency, with less cognitive workload required for task execution [82]. Although this relationship was not condition‐specific, it underscores the importance of external focus during exercise in priming the PFC for improved executive function. A moderate‐intensity exercise naturally shifts attention between internal physiological sensations and external signals [77]. Focusing on external cues like music may serve as a beneficial strategy to reduce fatigue‐related sensations while priming the PFC for tasks that require inhibition and attentional control. Given that synchronization significantly increased external focus, it may have greater potential than the other conditions to improve executive function and neural efficiency. However, it may not be sufficiently effective in distracting attention from internal sensations to significantly impact executive function or PFC oxygenation, resulting in non‐significant trends toward higher FST performance and PFC oxygenation, rather than statistically significant differences compared to the other two conditions. Future studies could enhance its attentional effects by explicitly instructing participants to focus on the music and/or the interaction between the music and their movements.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, in the synchronization condition, participants may not have consistently synchronized with the music, as indicated by the average pedaling cadence (66 RPM), which deviated by one RPM from half of the 130 BPM. This minor discrepancy may result from natural fluctuations in motor control, occasional mind‐wandering, or individual differences in beat perception ability [83], but it falls within an acceptable range for real‐world exercise settings. Moreover, prior research [84] indicates that perceived synchrony—rather than precise temporal alignment—is more strongly associated with affective responses such as groove. Given participants’ high reported levels of perceived synchrony (M = 5.33 out of 7), we consider that synchrony in the synchronization condition was effectively manipulated and achieved. To enhance the validity of synchronization, future studies could implement objective measures of synchronization and quantify phase coupling between music tempo and pedaling cadence [85], allowing for a more precise evaluation of its relationship with cognitive outcomes. Second, different music tracks were used in the two music conditions. Although participants rated them similarly in terms of preference and familiarity, the intrinsic musical features (e.g., harmony, timbre, and dynamics) may still have elicited varied emotional responses. Future research can consider adjusting the tempo of a single music track or using music with comparable musical features across conditions. Third, this study used a single‐channel functional near‐infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) device, which limited the ability to include a control region for comparison, making it difficult to determine whether observed changes were specific to the PFC or part of a general hemodynamic response. Portable multichannel fNIRS systems are recommended for future studies to enable examination of wider brain regions and better differentiation between task‐relevant regions and the control region. Fourth, this study focused on healthy young adults who exercise recreationally. Future research can examine the effects of synchronization in professionally trained exercisers who have distinct attentional strategies during music‐based exercise [12] and older adults who receive greater improvements in executive function from acute aerobic exercise [20, 21].

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that, compared to exercise without music or with asynchronous music, synchronizing movements to music reduces perceived exertion and increases perceived valence, perceived activation, and intrinsic motivation. These benefits likely arise because synchronization is less cognitively demanding and more efficient at diverting attention from interoceptive perceptions. To enhance motivation, adherence, and long‐term participation in exercise [1, 2], instructors or recreational exercisers can incorporate rhythmic music into aerobic sessions and encourage synchronized movements with the music. To maximize executive function benefits, exercisers should focus on external cues like music during the workout, as this may promote neural efficiency during subsequent cognitive tasks. While synchronization is an efficient strategy to promote external focus, its benefit on executive function did not differ significantly from those of asynchronous background music or silence in this study. Its cognitive benefits might be improved with training in musical beat perception and instructions that emphasize focusing on the interaction between music and movement.
