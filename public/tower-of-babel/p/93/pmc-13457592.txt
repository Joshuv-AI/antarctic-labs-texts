Nur77 agonism invigorates Natural Killer cell immunity against hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Shi Yong Neo, Yu Mei, Joni Chong, Kang Yi Lee, Jyue Yuan Lim, Menaka P Rajapakse, Timothy Wai Ho Shuen, Junzhe Zhao, Le Tong, Tasneem Kajiji, Hui Shi Cheong, Charis Toh, Zhen Wei Neo, Li Yen Chong, Wei Lin Tang, Nicholas Ang, Alicia Tay, Xinsong Chen, Hartman Johan, Shanshan Wu Howland, Joe Poh Sheng Yeong, Subhra Kumar Biswas, Elaine Lim, Hanry Yu, Shengli Xu, Han Chong Toh, Haiyan Liu, Kong-Peng Lam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite promising development as emerging “off-the-shelf” therapeutics against cancer, natural killer (NK) cells still faced considerable challenges in the solid tumor microenvironment (TME), including poor penetrance and immuno-suppression. Here, we employ spatial and single-cell transcriptomics to reveal a role for Nur77 in NK cell-mediated immunity against hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Orthogonal analysis of human and mouse HCC tumors indicate that the expression of NR4A1, encoding Nur77, is associated with NK cell proliferation, activation of the immunostimulatory AP-1 gene regulons, and better disease-free survival in HCC. Conditional ablation of Nr4a1 in NK cells perturbs their homeostasis and accelerates tumor progression in multiple tumor models. Conversely, the agonistic activation of Nur77 in NK cells ex-vivo or in-vivo enhances their anti-tumor functions. Mechanistically, downstream functional assays confirm that Nur77 activation attenuates CD36 expression in NK cells and confers resistance against oxLDL-mediated immunosuppression in the TME. Collectively, our findings highlight the potential of harnessing Nur77 agonism in improving NK cell-based immunotherapy against HCC.Subject terms: Tumour immunology, Cancer microenvironment, NK cells

Introduction

Natural Killer (NK) cells are cytotoxic lymphocytes with innate features, and their activation is orchestrated by a repertoire of activating and inhibitory receptors rather than antigenic stimulation1,2. In recent years, NK cells have garnered much interest for their “off-the-shelf” potential for allogenic cell-based therapeutics in cancer treatment3. However, the success of adoptive NK cell therapy remains largely limited to the hematological cancers. NK cells, like T cells, face challenges in the immunosuppressive solid tumor microenvironment (TME), which affect their infiltration, persistence and functions4. Furthermore, NK cell activity can be suppressed by immune checkpoints such as PD-1, TIGIT and LAG-35,6. Since the liver harbors a large reservoir of NK cells within the body7–9, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) can be an ideal model to address the need for the discovery of molecular pathways that potentially reinforce NK cell immunity within the TME.

Amongst various molecules known to affect immune cell functions, Nur77 stood out as an intriguing protein with critical roles in regulating various cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, metabolism, inflammation and apoptosis10–13. Nur77, encoded by NR4A1, is an orphan nuclear receptor that is widely recognised as a negative transcriptional regulator of T and B cells during immune tolerance14–17. Several studies also outlined the role of Nur77 agonism in B cells and macrophages to inhibit atherosclerosis beyond the context of cancer18–20. It was recently reported that Nur77 is upregulated upon antigenic stimulation of NK cells during murine cytomegalovirus (MCMV) infection21; however, Nur77 influence on NK cell-mediated anti-tumor responses is largely unknown. In tumors, the role of Nur77 is seemingly complex and dependent on the type of cancer and cellular context. Nur77 was earlier identified to be essential for the activation of patrolling monocytes to prevent tumor invasion in lung cancer22. Furthermore, agonistic activation of Nur77 using the naturally occurring octaketide Cytosporone B, elicited direct anti-tumor effects and even promoted CD8 T cell-dependent immunity in combination with anti-PD1 therapy13,23. While Nur77 has been recognised as a tumor suppressor gene in HCC10,11, its potential to enhance innate immunity may represent a potential therapeutic avenue to improve adoptive NK cell therapy for cancer.

Our present study outlines a critical role of Nur77 as a transcriptional regulator that enhances NK cell-mediated immunity against HCC. Notably, in a conditional knockout mouse model, Nr4a1 deficiency in NK cells implicates maturation states and impairs their ability to control tumor growth in vivo. Furthermore, activation of Nur77 triggers AP-1 gene regulatory networks and downregulates the expression of CD36 on NK cells to reduce the accumulation of lipid peroxides, thereby preventing their immune suppression. By demonstrating how Nur77 agonism improves antitumor efficacy of adoptive NK cell therapy, we have identified Nur77 as a molecular driver of NK cell activation, with potential application in enhancing NK cell therapy against solid tumors.

Results

Spatially identified Nur77 immune gene signature associates with NK cell immunity and is prognostic for improved survival of HCC patients

To uncover the possible regulatory role of Nur77 in the immune biology of HCC, we examined a cohort of treatment-naïve HCC tumors (Cohort detailed in Supplementary Table 1) within tissue microarrays using Digital Spatial Profiling (DSP), stratifying regions of interest (ROI) based on CD45 and Nur77 expression for subsequent transcriptomics analysis (Fig. 1A–C). We focused on the top differentially expressed genes (DEG) enriched in the Nur77+ immune regions, interestingly identifying KLRK1 (encoding NKG2D) among the top 10 DEG expressed (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that Nur77+ immune regions are positively enriched for NK cell pathways from both gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopaedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) curated databases (Fig. 1E, F). Conversely, we observed consistent negative enrichment scores (NES < −2) for several lipid metabolism gene sets in these Nur77+ immune regions (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). Additional GO analysis also uncovered significant enrichments in other immune ontologies related to inflammatory response and lymphocyte activation within these Nur77+ immune regions (Fig. 1G). Using a curated NK cell gene signature (M45040, MSigDB)24 and CIBERSORT, further immune deconvolution was performed. Though not significant, we observed that Nur77+ immune regions to have the highest NK signature score (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Notably, CIBERSORT revealed that no other immune cell types were differentially enriched when comparing Nur77+ to Nur77− immune regions (Supplementary Fig. 1D).

To determine the prognostic value of those top 10 DEGs as a Nur77-associated immune gene signature, we subsequently interrogated the publicly available HCC bulk transcriptomics dataset from TCGA-LIHC and found that this gene signature is significantly associated with better overall survival (p = 0.0247, Hazard ratio = 0.64) as well as disease-free survival of HCC patients (p = 0.0067, Hazard ratio = 0.63) (Fig. 1H). Notably, despite NR4A1 being previously identified as a tumor suppressor gene in HCC10,11, its gene expression alone was not significantly associated with better overall survival (p value = 0.11, Hazard ratio = 0.7297) even though disease-free survival was similarly favored (p = 0.031, Hazard ratio = 0.6981) (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). Next, using top 10 DEG derived from Nur77+ non-immune regions, we further tested that the “Nur77 non-immune” signature was not prognostic to HCC patient survival outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 2C, D). This observation was suggestive that the prognosis benefits of Nur77-associated transcriptional programme is mainly driven by immune lineages. Considering how Nur77 immune regions are transcriptionally enriched for NK cell-related genes (Fig. 1E, F), we subsequently benchmark our “Nur77 Immune” signature to the NK cell gene signature, observing the NK cell signature to give a comparable prognostic value (Hazard ratio = 0.5992 for overall survival and 0.6004 for disease-free survival) in the TCGA cohort (Supplementary Fig. 2E, F). Importantly, our “Nur77 Immune” signature strongly correlated with the NK cell gene signature (Supplementary Fig. 2G), and in contrast, only a weak positive association was seen between “Nur77 Non-Immune” signature with NK cell signature (Supplementary Fig. 2H). Further multiplex immunohistochemistry revealed punctate Nur77 expression observed within the nucleus of CD45+ immune cells (particularly CD3 + T cells and GZMB+, CD56+, CD16 + NK cells) but not in non-immune cells (CD45-) (Fig. 1I and Supplementary Fig. 3A). Together, our analysis here uncovered an association between Nur77+ immune gene signature in HCC tumors and NK cell immunity as well as the favourable prognostic value of this signature in HCC patients.

Nur77-expressing NK cells are transcriptionally enriched for immune activation and AP-1 gene regulatory networks

Given Nur77 can be possibly expressed by most immune cells and even tumor cells10,15,25, there is a need for cell type-resolved approaches to further investigate how Nur77 specifically regulates NK cell functionality within the HCC microenvironment. To do so, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing from a separate patient cohort (Supplementary Table 2) of which cells were isolated from paired tumor and non-tumor tissue specimens (Fig. 2A). Here, unsupervised clustering uncovered the majority of NK cells to be either liver resident NK cells (CXCR6+, CD160+) or canonical cytotoxic NK cells (FCGR3A+, GZMB+), while proliferative and transitional NK cells constituted minor clusters (Fig. 2A, B). Of note, all 4 subclusters of NK cells expressed PRF1, NKG7, TYROBP and PTPRC (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Interestingly, we observed a small subset of proliferative NK cells (MKI67+, GMNN+) that is only present in virus-associated HCC tissues (Supplementary Fig. 3C, D) and a considerably higher proportion of these proliferative NK cells were NR4A1+, co-expressing NR4A2 and NR4A3 (Fig. 2C). Moreover, most NR4A1 + NK cells were found in non-tumor tissues of HBV-HCC and HCV-HCC (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. 3E). Re-investigating the top 10 DEGs that were spatially identified in the Nur77+ immune regions of our tissue microarrays (Fig. 1D), we observed 8 of 10 genes to be upregulated in NR4A1 + NK cells (Fig. 2E) and that the expression of these DEGs varies across different NK subsets (Supplementary Fig. 3F). Next, we performed differential gene expression and generated a GO enrichment network to compare NR4A1+ to NR4A1- NK cells, identifying significant enrichment in several ontologies regulating immune activation (Fig. 2F) and other pathways related to response to cellular stress (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Importantly, NR4A1 + NK cells displayed higher expression of AP-1 subunits (Fig. 2G) which was similarly observed in three other publicly available HCC single- cell transcriptomics datasets (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Likewise, when looking into NR4A2+ or NR4A3 + NK cells, common upregulated DEGs were largely overlapped with those of NR4A1 + NK cells, including AP-1 subunits (JUNB, FOS, and FOSB) (Fig. 2H). As AP-1 is a well-established regulator downstream of T and NK cell activation26, we further investigated the differential gene regulatory network using SCENIC method (single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering)27, revealing most AP-1 subunits to be amongst the top 50 putative regulons in NR4A1+ NK cells in contrast to NR4A1− NK cells in which most AP-1 regulons were not within the top 100 in rank (Fig. 2I, J). While NR4A1 was broadly associated with SP128,29, we did not find NR4A1 + NK cells to display any enrichment for SP1 gene regulatory networks (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Additionally, NR4A1 + NK cells did not express higher levels of inhibitory receptors across all 4 datasets and, therefore, unlikely to be associated with immune exhaustion (Supplementary Fig. 4D–G). Together, our single-cell sequencing data supported the validation of the previous spatial transcriptomics findings (Fig. 1) and further reveal an association between Nur77 and AP-1 transcriptional programs during NK cell activation.

Tumor-liver organoids recapitulate the loss of Nur77 expression in NK cells in HCC

To substantiate the findings from the transcriptome data from our first two patient cohorts, we next performed flow cytometric profiling of NK cell phenotypes in a third HCC cohort (Supplementary Table 3). We first observed that the proportion of Nur77+ NK cells was significantly reduced in both non-tumor and tumor tissues as compared to the peripheral blood of HCC patients (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 5A). With reference to known NK cell subsets (Supplementary Fig. 5B), Nur77 was only downregulated in the immature (CD57low/neg, NKG2A+, CD56dim) and canonical (CD56dim, CD57+, NKG2Alow/neg) NK subsets (Fig. 3B). More importantly, we observed higher expression of CD16, NKG2C, Tbet, perforin, granzyme B and Ki-67 in Nur77+ NK cells (Fig. 3C–L and Supplementary Fig. 5C–E). To determine if similar NK phenotypes can be observed beyond HCC, we explored a cohort of breast cancer (BrCa) patients (Supplementary Table 4) to find increased expression of all six of the markers mentioned above (Supplementary Fig. 5F–K). These observations further substantiated the link between Nur77 and NK cell effector functions.

To further investigate our findings in HCC (Fig. 3A), we next postulated if HCC tumor-derived cues can mediate the downregulation of Nur77 in NK cells as a form of immunosuppression. Here, we incorporated differentiated HepaRG hepatocytes (CYP3A4+) together with iPSC-derived Kupffer (CD45 + ) and stellate cells (SMA + ) to form 3D organoids with or without the presence of a HCC cell line, SNU-475 (GPC3 + ) (Fig. 3M and Supplementary Fig. 6A). The presence of SNU-475 tumor cells in these 3D cultures resulted in the reduction of Nur77+ NK cells (Fig. 3N, O) as well as the frequencies of CD49a+ NK cells (Fig. 3N and P). Within these cultures, we also observed similar Nur77-associated activation phenotypes particularly the increased expression of CD16 and Granzyme B (Fig. 3P). While TGFβ is one of the most widely known immune suppressors in HCC30,31, we further demonstrated here that TGFβ potently induced Nur77 downregulation in isolated NK cells (Supplementary Fig. 6B, C). In contrast, the addition of exogenous oxidised low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL), another relevant metabolic immune suppressor32,33, did not have any clear impact on Nur77 expression. Collectively, our phenotypic data consistently highlighted the potential role of Nur77 as a driver for NK cell anti-tumor immunity, which we have successfully modelled using 3D in vitro organoids.

Conditional gene knockout of Nr4a1 affects NK cell maturation and activity in tumor-bearing mice

Previous studies of Nur77 relied heavily on Nr4a1−/− total knockout mouse models, which are known to have defects in monocytic cell differentiation and T cell dysregulation25,34. Here, we generate a conditional knockout of Nur77 in murine NK cells using the cre-loxP system (referred to as Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice). We first confirmed that NK cell frequencies and maturation were minimally affected by Nr4a1 deletion in non-tumor bearing mice prior to further in vivo tumor studies (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Fig. 4A, B). However, there were reduced proportions of Perforin+ and Ki-67 + NK cells in the liver of Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice (Fig. 4C, D). Next, using a MHCI-deficient MC38 tumor model which was previously found susceptible to NK-mediated killing and desensitized to T cell surveillance35, we observed here that tumor growth was accelerated in Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice as compared to the control group (Fig. 4E). Reduced NK cell infiltration into these tumors and Perforin+ (Prf+) NK cells were observed Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice despite no differences being observed in the spleens (Fig. 4F–H). While NKG2D expression was similarly low compared to tumor-infiltrating NK cells in both groups of mice, NKG2D was slightly reduced in splenic NK cells of Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice (Fig. 4I). Importantly, the proportion of mature NK cells (CD11b+, CD27−) in the spleens and tumors was greatly reduced in Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice (Fig. 4J–L). Cross-validated with the widely used RMAS tumor model (a MHCI-deficient lymphoma highly susceptible to NK-mediated killing)36, we further confirmed that Nur77 deficiency in NK cells led to accelerated tumor growth and reduced infiltration of NK cells into the tumor (Fig. 4M, N). Collectively, our data demonstrated a critical role for Nur77 in the regulation of NK cell responses in tumor-bearing mice.

Nr4a1 deficiency impairs NK cell immunity in the liver and escalates HCC progression

To assess the role of Nur77 in NK cell responses against HCC, we established an orthotopic HCC tumor model using Hepa1–6 cells and observed greater tumor growth within the livers of Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice (Fig. 5A–C). To validate these findings in a clinically relevant setting, we employed a hydrodynamic tail vein injection (HTVi)–based HCC model (Fig. 5A). In this model, we co-delivered CRISPR–Cas9 and Sleeping Beauty transposon systems to delete Tp53 and overexpress c-Myc in the livers of immunocompetent mice, thereby inducing Tp53KO, c-MycOE HCC tumors37. Consistent with our previous observations, Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice developed a significantly higher tumor burden in this induced-HCC model (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. 8A). Transcriptome profiling of the liver immune landscape within these HCC-bearing Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice revealed reduced proportion of tumor-infiltrating NK cells and at the same time, a slight reduction of T and B cells. On the other hand, frequencies of neutrophils and subsets of hepatocytes were increased in Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice (Fig. 5E and Supplementary Fig. 8B). Compared to the control group, the downregulated DEGs in the Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice included several key immune effector genes such as Tyrobp (encoding DAP10), Prf1 (encoding perforin) and subunits of IL-2/IL-15 receptors (Il2rg and il2rb) which are involved in IL-15 signalling (Fig. 5F). We next used CellphoneDB to predict putative ligand-receptor interactions that could be significant between tumor cells and NK cells38. With Nr4a1-deficient NK cells, we observed lower interaction scores involving CRTAM, TNF, LTB, IFNG, CD226 and CD96 receptors. On the other hand, there was a higher score for FASLG-FAS interaction in the Nr4a1-deficient NK cells, suggesting that these NK cells could perhaps switch to tumor killing via apoptotic ligand interactions (Fig. 5G). Strikingly, a large variety of interactions involving adhesion molecules were also found to be reduced in Nr4a1-deficient NK cells (Supplementary Fig. 8C). Compared to Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice, NK cells from the control group were also indeed enriched for immune activation and NK cell cytotoxicity gene sets, which could be partly explained by the co-enrichment of IL-2 and IL-15 receptor signalling (Fig. 5H and Supplementary Fig. 8D–F).

Within the HTVi-HCC model, we confirmed by flow cytometry that the frequencies of NK cells were indeed lower in the livers and spleens of Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl HCC-bearing mice (Fig. 5I, J). As similarly observed in our subcutaneous MC38 tumor model, the proportion of mature (CD11b+, CD27−) NK cells was significantly reduced in the liver, blood and spleen of Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 5K). Notably, while NKG2A expression is higher in control NK cells as compared to Nur77-deficient NK cells within naïve mice, these differences were abrogated in HCC tumor-bearing mice (Supplementary Fig. 8G, H). Ki-67 expression was not different between the two groups of tumor-bearing mice (Supplementary Fig. 8I) observed in naïve mice (Fig. 4D). We next tested the differential cytokine response to in vitro stimulation and found impaired production of granzyme B, IFNγ, perforin and TNF, particularly in the CD49a+ liver resident NK (LrNK) subset of HCC-bearing Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice (Fig. 5L–O). Taken together, our findings here confirmed Nur77 to be a potent driver of NK cell immunity in the liver to dampen HCC tumor growth.

Cytosporone B mobilises Nur77 binding and confers resistance to oxLDL-mediated immune suppression in NK cells

It was previously reported that Nur77 represses CD36 (a scavenger receptor for oxLDL and long chained fatty acids) in myeloid and tumor cells12,39. Of note, CD36 is considered a metabolic immune checkpoint upregulated by tumor-infiltrating T cells and NK cells in HCC40. Here, we found that a drastic increase in CD36+ LrNK cells in HCC-bearing Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, these Nr4a1-deficient hepatic NK cells had higher accumulation of lipid peroxides than control NK cells (Fig. 6B). Next, we explored the use of Cytosporone B (CsnB), a known potent small molecule agonist of Nur7723, resulted in CD36 downregulation in human NK cells and their reduced accumulation of lipid peroxides in the presence of exogenous oxLDL (Fig. 6C, D). Likewise, CsnB pre-treated NK cells were also impaired in their ability to uptake long chained fatty acids (Supplementary Fig. 9A, B). CsnB also restored the killing capacities of NK cells in the presence of palmitate-mediated immunosuppression (Supplementary Fig. 9C). Having found Camk1d to be differentially expressed in our mouse transcriptome data (Fig. 5F), we further demonstrated here that oxLDL can suppress activation-induced calcium flux signalling, which is in turn, can be rescued by CsnB pre-treatment (Fig. 6E, F). Importantly, CsnB also restored the expression of CD16 and the production of perforin and granzyme B, which were all suppressed in the presence of oxLDL (Fig. 6G–I).

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms of CsnB-Nur77 agonism during NK cell activation, we first demonstrated how CsnB enhances Nur77-JUN interaction but not to JunD (Fig. 6J). Subsequent CHIP-sequencing also confirmed CsnB to enhance Nur77 binding to DNA (Fig. 6K). As previously reported in other cell types41,42, we similarly found that Nur77 not only binds the promoter regions but also to gene bodies with the latter enhanced by CsnB treatment (Supplementary Fig. 9D–G). Both de novo and known binding motifs were identified in which we observed a particular increase in the binding of AP-1 motifs (Fig. 6L and Supplementary Fig. 9H). Interestingly, reduced binding to the known Nur77 (NR4A1) motif was seen upon CsnB treatment and suggested that the agonistic role of CsnB mobilises Nur77 to bind alternative DNA sites. Furthermore, we also observed increased binding to known Tbet, STAT5B, NFKB and Ikaros motifs, supporting the notion that CsnB enhances general immune cell activation (Fig. 6L). Importantly, we have cross-validated that CsnB enhances activation of AP-1 response element in a luciferase reporter assay (Fig. 6M).

To determine the therapeutic potential of CsnB in vivo, we directly treated HCC-bearing wild- type mice with CsnB 5 to 14 days post-HCC induction and found reduced tumor burden in the livers of these mice (Fig. 6N, O). As expected, IFNγ production in response to in vitro stimulation was heightened in liver and splenic NK cells isolated from CsnB-treated mice (Fig. 6N and Supplementary Fig. 9I). Furthermore, increased IL-2 and TNF production was observed during in vitro stimulation of CD49a+ LrNK cells (Fig. 6P), suggesting a potential role of Nur77-activated LrNK in modulating the liver immune landscape shown earlier (Fig. 5E). Additionally, we confirmed that CsnB specifically enhances killing capacity of control NK cells and does not benefit Nr4a1-deficient NK cells (Fig. 6Q). Also observed in human NK cells, CsnB only enhances perforin and Ki67 expression in Nur77+ NK cells but not in Nur77− NK cells (Supplementary Fig. 9J, K). While the use of bis-indole derivatives (e.g. DIM-C-pPhOH) as a Nur77 inverse agonist could improve T cell survival and functions43, we reported here that both CsnB and DIM-C-pPhOH does not have any impact on the viability of NK cells (Supplementary Fig. 10A, B). In fact, the Nur77 inhibitor, DIM-C-pPhOH downregulated the expression of CD16 and phosphorylated form of JUN, while Nur77 agonist enhances the phosphorylation of FOS (Supplementary Fig. 10C–E).

Further in vivo validation using MHCI-deficient RMAS lymphoma and MHCIlow B16F10 tumor models also confirmed the enhanced NK cell-mediated immunity driven by CsnB treatment (Supplementary Fig. 11A, B). Next, we further investigated if CsnB treatment implicates T cell activation and phenotype in vivo. Looking first into the CD4 T cell compartment within the tumor-bearing liver, there is a slight increase in the effector subset and CD69 + T cells (Supplementary Fig. 11C, D). There were no differences in the expression of PD-1 and TIM3, but interestingly, we observed a significant increase in IL-2 + CD4 T cells (Supplementary Fig. 11E–G). Significant shifts in proportion of effector and naïve CD8 T cells were observed, but there was no difference in the expression of CD69 (Supplementary Fig. 11H, I). Similarly, no significant changes in PD1, TIM3 or cytokine production were observed (Supplementary Fig. 11J–L). Taken together, our collective data provided substantial mechanistic insights on how Nur77 agonism improves NK cell activation. While one could expect T cell immunity to decline with systemic CsnB treatment, our findings here had indicated otherwise and thus is in line with previously reported benefits of combining CsnB with anti-PD1 therapy13.

Nur77 agonism improves the efficacy of adoptive NK cell therapy to attenuate HCC tumor progression

To determine if Nur77 agonism can yield therapeutic benefits in adoptive cell therapy settings, we pre-treated CD45.1 murine NK cells with CsnB ex vivo prior to infusion into Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice bearing HCC (Fig. 7A). Prior to the intended experimental endpoint, HCC-specific fatalities were observed in the control (2 out of 9) and untreated NK (2 out of 6) groups, while no mice died in the group in which CsnB pre-treated NK cells were administered. Moreover, tumor formation in the livers was indeed reduced in the group infused with CsnB pre-treated NK cells (Fig. 7B). At experimental endpoint, FACS profiling further showed differential phenotypes of the infused CD45.1 NK cells, particularly in the liver of the tumor-bearing host, whereby the frequencies of CD11b+ and perforin+ NK cells were higher in CsnB pre-treated cells as compared to untreated NK cells (Fig. 7C–E).

To explore if similar efficacies can be observed with human NK cells, we first subcutaneously engrafted Huh7 human HCC cells into NOD SCID Gamma (NSG) mice over a duration of 25 days for tumor progression (Fig. 7F). Similarly, only the infusion of CsnB pre-treated NK cells was able to dampen tumor growth (Fig. 7G). Importantly, CsnB pre-treated NK cells were found to better infiltrate these subcutaneous xenografts (Fig. 7H). As expected, Nur77 was downregulated together with Tbet and other activation markers while the expression of CD56 and CD36 was increased when comparing intratumoral NK cells to peripheral blood NK cells (Fig. 7I). In contrast to untreated NK cells, the expression of Nur77, CD16 and perforin was better sustained in CsnB pre-treated NK cells within the tumors (Fig. 7J–L). Altogether, our findings here suggested that CsnB pre-treatment can increase the efficacy of NK cells and highlighted the potential of Nur77 agonism as means of improving the therapeutic outcomes of adoptive NK cell therapy.

Discussion

The molecular basis of Nur77 activity broadly varies, leading to diverse and complex regulatory outcomes depending on cellular context. Canonically, Nur77 binds gene promoters to exert its gene regulatory function, but it was recently demonstrated to bind gene bodies to stall the transcription of stress-induced genes for enhanced survival41. In the context of HCC, Nur77 expression can be associated with hepatitis-driven aetiology44 and is profoundly recognised as a tumor suppressor gene10,11,45,46. Moreover, it was demonstrated that hepatitis C viral core protein can downregulate Nur77 as means of driving tumor progression47. While systemic Nur77 agonism can be rationalised to treat viral-driven HCC16,48,49, most T cell-centric studies elucidated a critical involvement of Nur77 in immunotolerance. Subsets of HBV-specific T cells, particularly regulatory T cells and exhausted CD8 T cells, exhibited higher Nur77 activities accompanied by a TCR-activated epigenome profile50. Mechanistically, Nur77 can antagonize AP-1 and enhance H3K27 acetylation at the epigenome level42,51,52. While these studies interrogated NR4A-mediated immunotolerance, its role in innate immunity, particularly in the context of NK cells, remained largely unclear. Here, our present study uncovered an axis in which Nur77 confers NK cells superior effector functions and the resistance to metabolic stress.

Similar to T and B cells, whereby Nur77 is an indicator of antigen signalling15, Grassman et al. interestingly revealed the upregulation of Nur77 during early antigenic stimulation of adaptive NK cells that is, however, associated with enhanced clonal expansion in response to MCMV21. Likewise, we observed Nur77 expression particularly in the proliferative NK cells in both human and murine livers. Further transcriptomics and CHIP sequencing revealed how Nur77 is mobilised to bind immunostimulatory motifs, including AP-1, STAT5, TBET and NF-kB. Importantly, our present study did not find any clear associations of Nur77 with any potential signs of NK cell exhaustion. Moreover, Nur77 inhibition resulted in a marked downregulation of CD16. These findings collectively indicated that one should not extrapolate and assume NK cells to share common exhaustion programs as CD8 T cells.

Rather than a uniform regulator of immune activation, the role of Nur77 arguably follows a context-dependent model that aligns better with the broader literature describing NR4A family members as signal-integrating transcription factors whose output is dictated by co-factor availability, chromatin landscape, and ligand-dependent conformational states. Although Yu et al. demonstrated how Nur77 dampens STAT1/IRF1 signalling and potentially drives exhaustion53, our experimental approach substantially differs by using an immunocompetent in vivo model to isolate lineage-restricted effects. Here, the conditional gene ablation of Nr4a1 (encoding Nur77) impaired maturation and proliferation, suggesting that NR4A1 fulfils a distinct, non-redundant role in NK cell homeostasis and tumor surveillance. We also found that TGFβ downregulates Nur77 in NK cells, which can be a result of immunosuppression, aligning with previous reports on how TGFβ potently suppresses NK cells and even drive their conversion to a ILC-like regulatory phenotype in the TME54–56. Moreover, hepatic NK cell states show broad effector suppression with prominent TGF-β–linked signatures in end-stage NASH cirrhosis, further indicating how NK cell activation may be constrained by the metabolic and suppressive cytokine milieu57. Interestingly, there is also an essential role for Nur77 during monocyte differentiation and proliferation25 which may be driven by similar molecular associations between Nur77 with AP-1 or NF-kB activity previously reported20,58. And in the context of cancer, patrolling monocytes were also shown to require Nur77 activation to prevent metastasis in the lung22. Taken together, these collective insights support the notion that Nur77 reinforces innate immune control against tumor progression.

Importantly, we demonstrated how Nur77 agonism can improve adoptive NK cell therapy. Mechanistically, Nr4a1-deficient NK cells expressed higher levels of CD36, while Nur77 activation confers NK cells the resistance to lipid-mediated immunosuppression. Notably, CD36 was found to be a druggable metabolic immune checkpoint associated with HCC and obesity40,59, Consistently, previous studies reported Nur77 to repress CD36 expression in tumor cells and macrophages12,39. We proposed that Nur77 agonism can be applied as a purposeful ex vivo, wash-out preconditioning step integrated into existing GMP-compliant manufacturing workflows60 for NK cell products to improve persistence and function. Such similar usage of small-molecule additives like nicotinamide61 and GSK3 inhibitor62 can “pre-set” NK cell states for enhanced maturation and effector functions, demonstrating that relatively brief chemical conditioning during manufacturing can translate into durable functional consequences.

Nevertheless, our present study is limited in scope. Considering the pleotropic complexity of Nur77 ligands as agonists or inverse agonists43,63, follow-up studies can address the translational gap with lead optimisation of the CsnB scaffold64, alongside PK/ADME investigations to address pharmacological feasibility65,66. Given that Nur77high NK cells were shown to have a distinct epigenetic profile21, a separate study can also be designed to look deeper into Nur77-dependent epigenetic modulation of NK cells, which may provide further insights into memory-like NK cell responses to chronic hepatitis B that is also relevant to the broader understanding of innate anti-viral immunity.

To summarise, the present study provided cross-validated findings to establish how targeting Nur77 will elevate the basal activity of NK cells and invigorate their anti-tumor responses in HCC (Graphical summary in Supplementary Fig. 12). On a translational front, we highlighted the broad therapeutic potential of Nur77 agonism to improve NK cell immunity either as a monotherapy or integrated in adoptive cell therapies for solid tumor treatments.

Methods

Study approval

Treatment naïve, resectable liver cancer (HCC) and BrCa samples were obtained from patients prospectively recruited from National Cancer Centre of Singapore with written informed consent and approval from the Central Institution Review Board (ref. 2019/2303 and 2015/2976, respectively). Experimental procedures and protocols were approved by the regional ethics review board (Etikprövningsnämnden, ref. 2016/957-31 and 2017/742-32) to collect treatment-naive tumor samples from surgically resected BrCa specimens from the Department of Clinical Pathology and Cancer Diagnostics at Karolinska University Hospital and Södersjukhuset, Stockholm, Sweden with informed consent. All clinicopathological features of surgical resections were confirmed by the pathology department prior to the release of samples for research purposes (Summarised in Supplementary Tables 1–4). Collection of residual blood components from de-identified healthy donors was approved by the Singapore’s Health Sciences Authority (HSA Ref No. 201306-04 and SIgN Ref No. 2024-133). All donors had provided consent via the HSA donor declaration process for the use of residual blood components and samples for medical- or health-related research purposes. Approved protocols for animal experimentation were granted by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (2023-01483, 221714, and 241896) at A*STAR. Experimental and control mice were co-housed under specific pathogen-free conditions maintained at 23 °C and 40 to 60% humidity on a 12-h light/dark cycle at A*STAR Biological Resource Centre. Criteria for early termination, also known as humane endpoints include any signs of moribund (not able to eat, or showing impaired ambulation, or hunched posture), significant loss of body weight (is more than 20 or 10% within 24 h), tissue necrosis or when tumor volume reaches 2000 mm3.

GeoMx spatial transcriptional profiling

TMA Slides were processed and profiled on the GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiler (DSP) as per manufacturer’s protocol (Nanostring GeoMx manuals). Briefly, FFPE slides were baked on a heating pad at 60 °C for 30 min and deparaffinized sequentially in xylene, 100% ethanol, 95% ethanol and PBS. Antigen retrieval was performed at 99 °C in a steamer, followed by incubation with Proteinase K (1 µg/mL, Ambion, Austin, Tx, USA) at 37 °C. Samples were washed and fixed in 10% Neutral Buffered Formalin (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, Richard-Allan Scientific 5700, San Diego, CA, USA). In situ hybridization with the human WTA probes from the GeoMx assay kit was set up overnight in the hybridization chamber at 37 °C as per manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were immunostained with a cocktail of morphology markers that included CD45 AF532 (2B11 + PD7/26, Novus Biologicals (Novus), Littleton, CO, USA, 19.25 µg/mL), CK8/18 AF594 (K8.8 + DC10, Novus, 10 µg/mL) Nur77 AF647 (Novus, 34 µg/ml) and counter-stained with Syto 13 (50 µM) for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were profiled on the Digital Spatial Profiler with an exposure time of 300 msecs for CD45 and CK8/18 and 200 msec for Nur77 and nuclear stain. ROI including immune and non-immune compartment were defined and collected based on unbiased threshold method based on CD45 immunostain. Taking into account of the high background signal of Nur77 immunostain, Nur77 positivity was manually defined into either Nur77+ or Nur77− TMA cores. Both CD45+ immune regions and CD45- non-immune regions from Nur77+ and Nur77− TMA cores were used for downstream transcriptome profiling. Using the output count matrix file generated from GeoMx DSP analysis suite, analysis was carried out in Partek Flow software for subsequent log2 normalisation, PCA and differential gene expression. GSEA was performed using the GSEA software (available from https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea) while pathway enrichment was analysed using ShinyGO v0.82 (available from https://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/). Further immune deconvolution using CIBERSORT67 was performed as described. The publicly available HCC bulk transcriptomics TCGA (the cancer genome atlas) cohort was accessed from (https://xena.ucsc.edu/). The curated NK cell gene signature was previously identified by Cursons et al24 and obtained from MSigDB (M4050).

Multiplex immunohistochemistry

Hyperplex Immunohistochemistry staining and imaging was performed on 4-µm thick formalin-fixed paraffin embedded (FFPE) tissue sections using COMET platform (Lunaphore Technologies SA, Switzerland). In brief, FFPE tissue sections were deparaffinised and subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval with the PT module (Epredia, Kalamazoo, MI, USA). The slides were then mounted with microfluidic chips for the spatial detection of 40 markers. This was followed by sequential immunofluorescence staining and image acquisition using off-the-shelf primary antibodies, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies, and DAPI as the nuclear counterstain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on the COMET platform (Lunaphore Technologies SA, Switzerland) (Antibody panel listed as Supplementary Table 5)68,69.

Tissue dissociation and single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis

Sample preparation and sequencing were similarly prepared as per our previous study70. In brief, treatment-naïve HCC tissues were digested in buffer, containing DMEM, Collagenase P (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), and RNase-free DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C with agitation for 10 min. For mouse HCC-bearing livers, tissues were minced into small pieces (approximately 1 mm) before incubating with enzyme cocktail of mouse tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany) for one hour at 37 °C. For both human and mice tissues, each sample was then suspended in PBS + 1% BSA before loading for droplet encapsulation using the 10× Genomics Chromium Controller or iX at a targeted cell recovery of about 5000 cells per sample. Single cell gene expression libraries were prepared using the 10× Genomics Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ v3.1 Library & Gel Bead Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting libraries were subjected to 2 × 151 cycle sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 targeting 50,000 read-pairs/cell.

CellRanger v6.0 (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA) analysis pipeline was used to filter and generate gene expression matrix output files for subsequent gene expression analysis in Partek Flow software. Standard analysis pipeline includes single cell QA/QC filtering, log2 normalisation, PCA, UMAP, hierarchical heatmap and Louvain clustering. All differential gene expression analysis were tested using the Hurdle model71. Pathway enrichment was analysed using metascape (available from https://metascape.org). To identify gene regulatory networks (GRN) from single cell RNA-seq data with annotated and normalized count matrix, we used the SCENIC R package (version 1.3.1)27. Putative cell-cell interaction was analysed using CellphoneDb method within Partek Flow38.

Tumor cell lines and iPSC-derived cell cultures

B16F10 (ATCC), B2m-KO MC38 (Provided by Nutsa Burduli and Arnika Wagner, Karolinska Institute), and Hepa1–6 (ATCC) cell lines were maintained in DMEM medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific), whereas RMA-S (Karolinska Institute), SNU-475 (ATCC), and Huh7 (JCRB) cell lines were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Both media were supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 100 U/mL Pen/Strep (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell culture supernatants were confirmed to be mycoplasma-negative using MycoAlert PLUS Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza) prior to inoculation into mice. HepaRG was cultured according to the supplier’s protocol. Briefly, undifferentiated HepaRG cells (Biopredic International, Rennes, Frances, #HPR101) were maintained in HepaRG Growth Media (William’s E media supplemented with Growth Medium Supplement [Thermo Fisher Scientific]) for 2 weeks, before being maintained in HepaRG Growth Media supplemented with 1.7% DMSO for 2 weeks for differentiation into a mixture of non-tumorigenic fetal-hepatocyte-like cells and cholangiocyte-like cells.

IMR90-iPSCs (WiCell) were maintained under feeder-free conditions on 6-well plates coated with Matrigel (Corning, Corning, NY, USA), using mTeSR1 media (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada). Cells were passaged weekly and only the first 10 passages after thawing were used for experiments. iPSC-Kupffer cells (iKC) were derived from IMR90-iPSCs (WiCell) as previously described72. Briefly, confluent iPSCs were detached and aggregated into embryoid bodies (EB), which were cultured for 4 days in mTeSR1 supplemented with recombinant human BMP4 (50 ng/mL), VEGF-A (50 ng/mL), SCF (20 ng/mL) and Y-27632 (10 μM). Subsequently, these EBs were cultured in X-VIVO 15 media (Lonza) with 0.055 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 100 U/mL Pen/Strep, and recombinant human IL-3 (25 ng/mL) and mCSF (100 ng/mL). After 3–4 weeks, macrophage precursors were harvested every week from half of the total media and were subsequently differentiated into iKCs for 7 days. The differentiation media contains 50 ng/mL recombinant human mCSF, and 50% primary human hepatocyte conditioned media with William’s E + 50% KC maintenance media with Advanced DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific). iPSC-hepatic stellate cells (iHSC) were derived from IMR90-iPSCs directly following an established protocol73. Briefly, the differentiation is divided into four stages with stage-specific factors added to a basal medium: Day 1–4: BMP4 (20 ng/mL), Day 5–6: BMP4 (20 ng/mL), FGF1 (20 ng/mL) and FGF3 (20 ng/mL), Day 7–8: FGF1 (20 ng/mL), FGF3 (20 ng/mL), Retinoic acid (5 µM) and Palmitate (100 µM), Day 9–12: Retinoic acid (5 µM) and Palmitate (100 µM). The medium changes were performed every day. The mature iHSCs were harvested on Day 13 and used for further experiments. All cell culture-related reagents are listed in supplementary Table 6.

Isolation of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells

From healthy donor apheresis cone blood, residual blood was diluted 1:2 with Wash Buffer [1× PBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 2 mM EDTA (1st Base, Singapore)] and layered on Ficoll-Paque Plus (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA), 2:1 ratio in a 50 mL falcon tube. Blood was processed using Ficoll-based density gradient centrifugation (500 × g for 30 min at 4 °C) without acceleration and deceleration. The PBMC layer was transferred to a fresh 50 mL falcon tube and washed once with wash buffer. Red blood cell (RBC) lysis was performed using ACK lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Purified PBMCs were re-suspended in MACS buffer [1× PBS (Gibco) with 2 mM EDTA (1st Base) and 0.5% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific)].

Isolation and expansion of human Natural Killer cells from PBMCs

NK cell isolation was done with the human NK cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec) according to manufacturer’s protocol. Purified NK cells were placed in AIM-V media (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 5% Human Serum (Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA), with an addition of 300 IU/ml recombinant IL-2 (Miltenyi Biotec) and 5 µL/mL of pre-prepared loaded MACS Anti-Biotin MACSiBeads Particles, prepared according to Human NK cell Activation/Expansion kit manufacturer’s protocol (Miltenyi Biotec). Final working concentration of 2 µM of Cytosporone B (CsnB; Merck, KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) was added for NK cell treatment for 3–4 days before in vitro or in vivo xenograft tumor experiments.

Generation of HepaRG-derived liver organoids and downstream co-culture assay

Differentiated HepaRG, iKCs and iHSCs were detached, pelleted and mixed at a ratio of HepaRG: iKC: iHSC = 10:4:174 before embedded in domes of Geltrex Reduced Growth Factor Basement Membrane Matrix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After the gel solidified, organoid media was added. Adopted from a previous study75, this media contained Advanced DMEM/F12 supplemented with 100 U/mL Pen/Strep, 1% GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 10 mM HEPES (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× B27 supplement minus vitamin A (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× N2 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1.25 mM N-acetylcysteine, 10 mM nicotinamide, 10 ng/mL recombinant human R-spondin 1, 10 nM recombinant human gastrin, 50 ng/mL recombinant human EGF, 100 ng/mL recombinant human FGF10, 25 ng/mL recombinant human HGF, 10 μM forskolin, 5 μM A83-01, 25 ng/mL Noggin and 10 μM Y-27632. The organoids emerged after 2–3 weeks of initiation phase and were further expanded for 1 week before experiments. To produce SNU475-infiltrated organoid, the HCC cell lines were detached and mixed with the other cell types at a ratio of HCC: HepaRG: iKC: iHSC = 5:5:4:1. All other steps remained identical.

For co-culture experiments involving NK cells and organoids, organoid culture media was replaced with complete RPMI media (supplemented with 10% FBS). Human PBMC-derived NK cells (pre-stimulated with 300 IU/mL of IL-2 for 48 h) were directly added to either HepaRG-derived organoids or SNU-475 infiltrated organoids as described above. After 4 days of in vitro co-culture, all experimental wells are subjected to enzymatic dissociation using TrypLE (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for up to 10 min to obtain single cell suspension for downstream flow cytometry analysis.

Generation of conditional Nr4a1 knockout mice model

NK lineage Cre-expressing mice (C57BL/6Smoc-Ncr1em2(2A-iCre) Smoc, Cat no. NM-KI-190027) and Nr4a1fl/fl mice (C57BL/6Smoc-Nr4a1em1Smoc, Cat no. NM-CKO-205045) were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center Inc. Genotypes were confirmed by PCR using primers (Supplementary Table 7) and protocols from the supplier. Conditional knockout mice (Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl) were generated by crossing Ncr1iCre mice with Nr4a1fl/fl mice, and subsequent generations were maintained. Homozygous floxed mice lacking Cre (Ncr1WT, Nr4a1fl/fl) were used as controls. By Flow cytometry, NK cell phenotyping was done prior to syngeneic tumor studies.

Syngeneic tumor models

For subcutaneous tumor models, either B16F10, B2m-KO MC38 or RMAS was used in in-house bred C57BL/6 mice (males, 6–8 weeks old). B16F10, B2m-KO MC38, and RMA-S were resuspended to 1 × 107cells/mL and for each of these lines, 1 × 106 cells were mixed with Geltrex (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio and injected subcutaneously into the right flank of a mouse under anaesthesia with 3% isoflurane. Mice were monitored at least once every 2 days following tumor inoculation. Tumor size was monitored using an electronic caliper every 2–4 days from the point of tumour palpation. Tumor volume was calculated as ½ × length × width2. Mice were euthanized before tumor volume reached 2000 mm3 (maximum limit approved for humane endpoint) or other criteria for early termination as described above. Blood, spleens and tumors were harvested and processed (as described below) for downstream analysis.

To generate orthotopic HCC, 1 × 10⁶ Hepa1–6 cells were surgically implanted into the left liver lobe of C57BL/6 mice (males, 7–8 weeks old). Mice were monitored daily for at least 3 days following tumor implantation and at least once every 2 days thereafter. Tumors were allowed to develop for two weeks before mice were euthanized for analysis. For CsnB treatment, mice were injected with CsnB via i.p. at the dose of 13 mg/kg b.w. per mice one day before the surgery, as well as every 3 days after the first injection until the endpoint. Control groups were injected with PBS. Two weeks after tumor implantation, mice were euthanized for analysis. For the spontaneous HCC model, hydrodynamic tail vein injection (HTVi) was performed in 8–10-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (HTVi-HCC model). A sterile saline-plasmid solution, with a total volume equivalent to 10% of the animal’s body weight, was rapidly injected (within 6–8 s) into the lateral tail vein. The injected DNA mixture contained 30 µg total plasmid, comprising CRISPR-Cas9 vectors carrying single guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting Trp53, a transposon vector encoding c-Myc, and CMV-SB13 transposase in a 2:1:0.2 ratio (kindly provided by Prof. Carmen Chak-Lui Wong, the University of Hong Kong). Mice were monitored at least once every 2 days after the HTVi procedure. As experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized 4–5 weeks post-injection for downstream analysis of liver, spleen and peripheral blood.

Mouse tissue/organs harvesting and processing

Animals were euthanized using CO2 in accordance with approved IACUC ethical protocols. Briefly, mice were euthanized by exposure to increasing concentrations of CO2 over 6 min, and death was confirmed by the absence of withdrawal reflexes to toe pinching. Blood was collected via terminal cardiac puncture using a 25 × g needle and 1 mL syringe, directly into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube containing 20 µL of 0.5 M EDTA (1st Base). Blood was centrifuged down to remove plasma before being lysed twice with ACK lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and cell pellet re-suspended MACs buffer for antibody staining and flow cytometry analysis. Spleen was harvested, mechanically dissociated and filtered through 70 µm nylon cell strainer to obtain single cell suspensions. RBC lysis was done with ACK lysis buffer, and the cell pellet was re-suspended in MACs buffer for downstream analysis or processing. For bone marrow, both hind legs of the mouse were harvested and excess flesh removed to expose the femur and tibia bones. Tibia and femur were then processed aseptically to flush out bone marrow. Bone marrow cells collected were RBC lysed with ACK lysis buffer, and the cell pellet was re-suspended in MACs buffer to obtain primary bone marrow cell suspensions. Both healthy and tumor-bearing livers were carefully resected to ensure all lobes were removed. Tissues were minced and enzymatically digested with Mouse Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec), then passed through a 70 µm cell strainer to dissociate single cells from residual tissues. RBCs were lysed with ACK lysis buffer, and cell pellet was re-suspended in either PBS supplemented with 5% FBS for downstream flow cytometry or in 1% BSA in PBS for single-cell sequencing. Subcutaneously implanted tumors were surgically removed, minced and enzymatically digested with Mouse Tumor Dissociation Kit, then passed through a 70 µm cell strainer to dissociate single cells from residual tissues. RBCs were lysed with ACK lysis buffer, and cell pellet was re-suspended in PBS supplemented with 5% FBS for downstream flow cytometry.

Adoptive NK cell transfer in syngeneic HTVi-HCC model

To obtain healthy NK cells from CD45.1 mice, spleen and bone marrows were collected and processed (as described above) to obtain single cell suspension. NK cells isolation was done using mouse NK cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec) according to manufacturer’s protocol. Purified mouse NK cells were placed in AIM-V media (Gibco) supplemented with 5% Human Serum (Sigma), with an addition of 300 IU/mL recombinant IL-2 (Miltenyi Biotec). Final working concentration of 2 µM of CsnB (Merck) was added for NK cell treatment for 3–4 days before injection into mice. Subsequently, CsnB-treated and untreated mouse CD45.1 NK cells (2 × 106 NK cells, re-suspended in 200 µL PBS per mouse) were intravenously injected via tail vein on day 14 and day 21 post oncogenic plasmid inoculation in Ncr1iCre, Nr4a1fl/fl mice within the HTVi-HCC model as described above. Mice were euthanized on day 31 post-plasmid inoculation unless humane endpoint was reached earlier. Blood, spleen and tumor were collected and processed (as described above) for downstream analysis.

Adoptive NK cell transfer in a human cell line-derived xenograft tumor model

5 × 106 of Huh7 tumor cells suspended in PBS were subcutaneously injected with Geltrex (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a 1:1 ratio into the right flank of the mouse (NOD scid gamma mice, 6–8 weeks old males) under anaesthesia with 3% isoflurane. Tumor size was recorded with an electronic calliper every 2–3 days from the point of tumor palpation. Tumor volume was calculated with the following formula: ½ × length × width2. CsnB-treated and untreated human NK cells (2 × 106 NK cells, re-suspended in 200 µL PBS per mouse) were intravenously injected via tail vein on day 15 (when average tumor volume >200 mm3) and day 18 post tumor inoculation. In accordance with humane endpoints defined in the approved ethical permits, mice were euthanized on day 25 post tumor inoculation. Blood and tumors were collected and processed (as described above) for downstream analysis.

Cytosporone B treatment and ChIP-seq DNA extraction

NK cells isolated from healthy donor PBMC were seeded into non-treated 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 106 cells/mL in AIM-V media (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 100 IU/mL of IL-2 (Miltenyi Biotec). CsnB was added to the treatment wells at a final concentration of 2 µM. After incubation for 4 days at 37 °C in a humidified chamber, untreated and CsnB-treated cells were processed for ChIP-seq using the Pierce Magnetic ChIP Kit (Cat no. 26157, Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Anti-Nur77 antibody (Novus) was used to immunoprecipitate Nur77. Purified DNA was stored in −20 °C prior to downstream library preparation and sequencing (Novogene, Beijing, China). In brief, DNA fragments were repaired, A-tailed and further ligated with Illumina adapter. The final DNA library was obtained by size selection and PCR amplification. The library was checked with Qubit and real-time PCR for quantification and bioanalyzer for size distribution detection. Quantified libraries will be pooled and sequenced on Illumina platforms, according to effective library concentration and data amount required. Raw reads were filtered to remove adapters, reads containing N > 10% (N represents the base cannot be determined) or low-quality reads (Q score ≤ 5, base which is over 50% of the total base).

Output data were further subjected to the quality control process with FastQC prior to alignment with hg19 genome using BWA-MEM (version 0.7.10-r789)76. This was followed by sorting the alignment for deduplication. The deduplicated bam alignments were then processed with BEDTools software (v2.21.0)77 to remove black-listed regions which contain satellite repeats located in hard-masked telomeric and pericentromeric regions. This was facilitated by the list of black-listed regions published in a previous report78. SAMtools suite (v1.13)79 was used to index the black-listed regions filtered bam files. Additional checks on the behaviour of data were performed by analysing Fingerprint plots and cross-correlation plots with deepTools (v2.0)80 prior to peak calling with MACS2 (v2.2.9.1)81.

MACS2 was run with default parameters and QC was performed with ChIPQC (v1.30.0)82 where input parameters comprise of hg19 annotation and the value of summit equals to 250 in order to include 250 base pairs upstream and downstream of the peak summit for all chromosomes. BEDTools’ multiinter was used to extract the overlapping peaks among each type of biological replicates. Differential binding analysis of the ChIP-seq peaks between treated and untreated groups obtained from the above steps was carried out with the R package, DiffBind (v.3.4.11). In addition to DiffBind package, we also used ChIPSeeker (v.1.40)83 to generate visualizations to observe the distribution of peaks of treated and untreated samples from 3 kb upstream and 3 kb downstream of the TSS site. We used GREAT online tool84 to evaluate the distribution of peak sites away from the transcription start site (TSS). Known and De-novo motif finding was performed with MEME-ChIP in MEME Suite (v.5.5.8)85. E-values are computed by adjusting p-value of a region for the number of regions tested with Bonferroni correction followed by multiplying the adjusted p-values by the number of motifs input to CentriMo.

Flow cytometry

Cells were stained with Live/Dead Fixable Viability dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific) diluted in 1 × PBS for 10 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by washing with FACS buffer (5% FBS in 1 × PBS). For mice samples, the cells were incubated with anti-CD16/32 (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) for 15 min at 4 °C, followed by washing with FACS buffer. For surface labelling, cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies diluted in Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and washed with FACS buffer. Cells were permeabilized and washed using either eBioscience Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or Transcription Factor Buffer Set (BD Biosciences). For intracellular labelling, cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies diluted in Permeabilization wash buffer. For intracellular cytokine detection, cells were stimulated separately with Cell Stimulation Cocktail (Invitrogen) in the presence of 10 µg/mL Brefeldin A (BioLegend) for 4 h prior to staining. Cells were washed and resuspended in 300 µL FACS buffer for acquisition flow cytometer. All antibody staining and incubation were performed at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark unless stated otherwise. All FACS data were acquired on BD FACSymphony A3 Cell Analyzer or BD Fortessa X20 (BD Biosciences) or Cytek Aurora 5 L Spectral Analyzer (Cytek Biosciences, Fremont, CA, USA) and analyzed using FlowJo V10.10.0 software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR, USA). (Flow cytometry panel, information of antibodies and other reagents are listed in Supplementary Tables 8–10).

To measure the uptake of long-chained fatty acids, NK cells were incubated with 1μM of BODIPY FL C12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 20 min in normal cell culture incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2) prior to routine FACS staining with viability dye. As for the detection of lipid peroxides, NK cells were incubated with 800 nM of BOPIDY665/676 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min in normal cell culture incubator. FACS readout for lipid peroxidation is the ratio of PE: APC mean fluorescence intensities. For calcium flux assay, cells were resuspended to a concentration of 1 × 107 cells/mL in RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS and stained with 20 µM Fluo4-AM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 40 min at room temperature in the dark. Next, cells were labelled with viability dye as described above, then resuspended to a concentration of 3 × 106 cells/mL in FACS buffer and rested at room temperature in the dark for 30–60 min to allow dye de-esterification. Cells were pre-warmed to 37 °C prior to acquisition and baseline reading was recorded for 30 s. The sample was removed and ionomycin (Sigma Aldrich) was promptly added to a final concentration of 20 µg/mL, briefly vortexed and immediately returned for acquisition and recording for up to 6 min.

Immunoprecipitation and western blot

Human NK cells purified from healthy donors were harvested after 2 days of CsnB treatment in the presence of 300IU/mL of IL-2 and NK cell activation beads (Miltenyi Biotec). Cell pellets were processed with 1× cell lysis buffer (CLB) (Cell Signaling Technologies), supplemented with 1× protease/phosphatase inhibitor (Cell Signaling Technologies) at a concentration of 100 µL per 1 × 106 cells. Samples were briefly sonicated and incubated at 4 °C with gentle mixing for 30 min. After 30 min, samples were centrifuged and supernatant was transferred into a fresh tube. Protein quantification was measured with Bradford assay (Bio-Rad). Whole cell lysates (WCL) were prepared with 0.2 mg of protein lysate, topped up with Laemmlli sample buffer (Bio-Rad, supplemented with β-Mercaptoethanol (Sigma Aldrich) in a ratio of 20:1) to 50 µL.

Immuno-precipitate (IP) samples were prepared with 0.5 mg of protein lysate and incubated with Nur77 antibody (Cell-Signaling Technologies) at a dilution of 1:100, overnight with gentle mixing at 4 °C. Protein A/G beads (Santa Cruz) was washed once with CLB and added directly into the samples after overnight incubation. Samples were then further incubated at 4 °C for an hour with gentle mixing. After beads incubation, samples were washed 3 times with 500 µL of CLB each, discarding supernatant. After final wash, discard supernatant and re-suspend pellet in 40 µL of Laemmlli sample buffer. WCL and IP samples were placed in a heating block at 95 °C for a minimum of 5 min. After which, samples were removed from the heat block and centrifuged. 10 µL aliquots of samples (equivalent of ~20% of IP lysate and ~40 µg WCL) were used per run for western blot analysis.

Westen blotting was ran on Bio-Rad Mini-PROTEAN Tetra Vertical electrophoresis system (with Mini-Trans-Blot Module and PowerPac HC Power Supply). Using a 10% acrylamide gel (Bio-rad) with 1× Tris-Glycine SDS Buffer (1st Base), SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis was performed by running the gel for 15 min at 80 V, followed by an additional 45 min at 150 V. Next, protein is transferred from gel to PVDF membrane (Cytiva) in a sandwich transfer assembly at 100 V for 1 h at 4 °C (according to Bio-Rad manufacturer’s recommendations). Prior to staining, membrane blot was blocked with 5% non-fat milk powder for 1 h at room temperature (Cell Signaling Technologies) in TBST (1× Tris Buffered Saline (TBS) buffer (1st Base) with 0.1% Tween-20 (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, USA)). Membrane blot was stained with primary antibodies for c-Jun, Jun-D or GAPDH (See Supplementary Table 12) overnight at 4 °C. After overnight primary antibody incubation, membrane blot was rinsed thrice with TBST followed by secondary staining with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (See Supplementary Table 12) diluted 1:1000 in 5% non-fat milk powder in TBST for 1 h at RT. For blot imaging, chemiluminescent substrate (Advansta Inc, San Jose, CA, USA) was applied according to manufacturer’s protocol and acquired using Bio-Rad ChemiDoc Imaging System. Details to all reagents used here is listed in Supplementary Table 11.

AP-1 response element reporter assay

1 × 105 HEK293 cells were plated on a 12-well plate and rested for at least 4 h before pre-treated with CsnB. After 48 h of CsnB pre-treatment, 900 ng of AP-1 response element plasmid (Cat no. E4111, Promega) and 100 ng of Renilla plasmid (for a total concentration of 1000 ng plasmid per well, in a ratio of 10:1) was added into each well with Lipofectamine 3000 Reagent (Cat no. L3000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific), prepared according to manufacturer’s recommendations. Using Dual-Glo luciferase assay reagent and Stop & Glo reagent with manufacturer’s protocol (Cat no. E2940, Promega), luminescence was measured using Agilent BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader and software.

Cytotoxicity assay

Murine spleens were mechanically dissociated by gentle pressing through a 70-µm strainer into cold RPMI-1640 media. Cells were washed and RBCs lysed using ACK lysis buffer. NK cells were isolated using MojoSort Mouse NK Cell Isolation Kit (Cat no. 480050, BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified NK cells were then treated with either 1% oxLDL or 2 µM CsnB or both overnight in complete RPMI-1640 media supplemented with mouse IL-2 (100 IU/mL), IL-12 (10 ng/mL), and IL-15 (10 ng/mL) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After treatment, cells were washed twice with PBS to remove residual compounds before cytotoxicity assays.

The CytoTox96 nonradioactive cytotoxicity assay (Cat no. G1780, Promega) was used to measure the cytotoxic activity of the NK cells, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, target cells Hepa1–6 (1 × 104 cells/well) were plated on 96-well U-bottom plates (Corning), and the NK cells (effectors) were added to a final volume of 200 µL at the Effector to Target ratio (E:T) of 1:2. The plates were then incubated for 6 h in a humidified 5% CO2 chamber at 37 °C and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min. Aliquots (50 µL) were transferred from each well to fresh 96-well flat-bottom plates, and an equal volume of reconstituted substrate mix was added into each well. The plates were then incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Stop solution (50 µL) was added, and the absorbance was measured at 492 nm. The percentage of killing was calculated using the following formula: (A492nm [experimental]–A492 nm [effector spontaneous]–A492 nm [target spontaneous]) × 100/(A492nm [target maximum]–A492 nm [target spontaneous]).

Statistics analysis

With regards to experimental design, Sample sizes were adopted to achieve greater than 95% probability of identifying differences, by appropriate statistical analysis. All experiments are repeated at least twice independently. Data exclusion criteria include obvious outliers, poor tissue viability and mice with abnormal or failure in tumor engraftment. Unless stated otherwise, all statistical analysis were performed using Prism (Graphpad Software, LLC, San Diego, CA, USA) for two-sided significance testing, including t-test, ANOVA and other non-parametric tests such as Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis test. All sample sizes, n, represent biological replicates and error bars represent standard deviation of the mean. Flow cytometry data were analyzed with FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC). Western blot images were captured using Bio-Rad Chemi-Doc Imaging System (Bio-Rad) and luminescence from reporter assay was measured using Agilent Biotek Synergy H1 (Agilent Biotek, Winooski, VT, USA).
