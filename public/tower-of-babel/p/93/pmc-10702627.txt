Efficacious Primary Pasireotide Therapy in a Case of a Large Invasive Adrenocorticotropin-secreting Pituitary Tumor
Authors: Yuko Tamaki, Junko Nakaue, Fumihiko Nishimura, Yutaka Takahashi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 41-year-old woman presented with a headache, diplopia, weight gain, moon face, and central obesity. Her plasma adrenocorticotropin (ACTH) level was 25.5 pmol/L (116 pg/mL) (normal range, 1.6-13.9 pmol/L [7.2-63.3 pg/mL]), serum cortisol level was 397.3 nmol/L (14.4 µg/dL) (normal range, 195.1-540.7 nmol/L [7.1-19.6 µg/dL]), and urinary free cortisol was 413.9 nmol/day (150.3 µg/day) (normal range, <221.5 mmol/day [<80.3 µg/day]). ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism was present, with cortisol suppression using a high-dose dexamethasone suppression test. Cushing disease was diagnosed and a contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging scan demonstrated a 36-mm pituitary tumor with right cavernous sinus invasion. Before surgery, 20 mg pasireotide long-acting-release was initiated, and her symptoms rapidly improved. After 1 month, obvious tumor shrinkage was observed, ACTH and cortisol levels decreased, and diplopia resolved; therefore, we continued medical therapy. After 11 months, her ACTH and cortisol levels normalized, and most of the tumor had disappeared. The clinical course in this case suggests that pasireotide may be useful for preoperative treatment and primary medical therapy, at least in some patients with Cushing disease caused by a large tumor predicted to have difficulty achieving remission by surgery.

Introduction

Macroadenomas are observed in approximately 10% of Cushing disease cases. They are relatively aggressive and recur frequently after surgery (1), and it is difficult to achieve surgical remission owing to invasion of the cavernous sinus (2). Medical therapy is generally used to normalize cortisol levels in cases of postoperative nonremission, recurrence, or inoperable cases. Pasireotide, a pituitary-directed therapy, acts mainly on the somatostatin subtype receptor 5, suppresses adrenocorticotropin (ACTH) secretion, and shrinks tumors (3). Although it has reportedly been useful for the treatment of postoperative Cushing disease, whether it is useful for preoperative or primary medical therapy remains unclear. In this study, we report a case of Cushing disease with a large invasive tumor, in which surgical remission was predicted to be difficult to achieve. As treatment with pasireotide long-acting-release (LAR) as a preoperative medical therapy showed remarkable efficacy, we finally administered it as a primary medical therapy.

Case Presentation

A 41-year-old woman with headaches and diplopia was referred to our hospital. A contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan revealed a 36 × 24 × 33-mm pituitary tumor of Knosp grade 4, encasing the right internal carotid artery (Fig. 1A). She was admitted to the hospital for further evaluation. At age 39 years, she was diagnosed with pregnancy-induced hypertension and had gained 15 kg in weight over the past 2 years. Her height and weight were 163 cm and 90 kg (body mass index, 34), respectively, and blood pressure was 134/78 mm Hg. Physical examination revealed a moon face, central obesity, buffalo hump, and red abdominal striae.

Diagnostic Assessment

The patient's plasma ACTH level was 25.5 pmol/L (116 pg/mL), serum cortisol level was 397.3 nmol/L (14.4 µg/dL), and urinary free cortisol (UFC) was 413.9 nmol/day (150.3 µg/day) (1.9 times the upper limit of normal range). She showed a 1.5-fold ACTH response in a corticotropin-releasing hormone test and cortisol suppression by high-dose but not low-dose dexamethasone (Table 1). Based on these biochemical data, the presence of a pituitary tumor, and clinical features, the patient was diagnosed with Cushing disease. Interestingly, although growth hormone (GH) levels were suppressed (0.3 ng/mL) during the oral glucose tolerance test, insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) levels were slightly elevated (SD score of 2.5), suggesting the possibility of GH co-secretion.

Treatment

Because the patient manifested oculomotor nerve palsy associated with cavernous sinus invasion, decompression surgery was planned. Before the surgery, 20 mg pasireotide LAR was administered as a preoperative medical therapy. Interestingly, after the first dose of pasireotide LAR, symptoms of headache and diplopia rapidly improved, and ACTH and cortisol levels decreased. Therefore, we decided to continue the medical therapy in close collaboration with the neurosurgeon in preparation for emergency surgery if symptoms worsened. MRI showed that the tumor had shrunk (Fig. 1B), and the diplopia resolved after 1 month. Two months later, there was a transient increase in the ACTH and cortisol levels (Fig. 2), but the tumor had not enlarged (Fig. 1C); therefore, pasireotide LAR was continued at the same dose. After the transient increase, a drastic decrease in ACTH to 2.1 pmol/L (9.5 pg/mL) and cortisol to 77.3 nmol/L, (2.8 µg/dL) and adrenal insufficiency (general malaise) were observed; hydrocortisone replacement therapy was then administered. IGF-1 levels also decreased during medical therapy.

Clinical course of the patient. A transient increase in adrenocorticotropin (ACTH) and cortisol levels at 2 months and thereafter a marked decrease in ACTH and cortisol levels were observed. Hydrocortisone replacement therapy was performed 2 months after pasireotide long-acting-release treatment.

Outcome and Follow-up

After 6 months of treatment with pasireotide LAR, the patient's ACTH levels was less than 1.5 pg/mL, cortisol level was 179.3 nmol/L (6.5 μg/dL), and IGF-I was 12.3 nmol/L (94 ng/mL) (normal range, 12.4-31.4 nmol/L [95-240 ng/mL]) (−2 SDS) (see Fig. 2). The tumor size remarkably decreased to 15 × 10 × 10 mm (Fig. 1D) with a change into a low-intensity signal on T1-weighted imaging MRI, and the symptoms resolved. The glycated hemoglobin level slightly increased from 37 to 45 mmol/mol after 2 months of treatment but decreased to 37 mmol/mol after 6 months without any antidiabetic agents. Owing to the good response to pasireotide LAR, the patient did not undergo surgery and continued medical therapy. After 11 months of treatment, the tumor shrank further and most of it had disappeared (Fig. 1E). Because complete surgical removal of the residual tumor, especially in the lateral cavernous sinus, is difficult, pasireotide LAR has been continued as a primary medical therapy.

Discussion

In this case, Cushing disease with a large invasive tumor showed a drastic response to pasireotide LAR, with obvious tumor shrinkage, normalization of ACTH and cortisol levels, and symptom improvement. Pasireotide LAR was first used as preoperative therapy; however, based on these effects, we found it to be an efficacious primary medical therapy in this case.

In a recent meta-analysis, the remission rates after primary and revision transsphenoidal surgeries for Cushing disease were 80% and 58%, respectively (4). The early postoperative remission rates were reportedly 77% for Knosp grade 0 to 2 but 53% for grade 3 to 4 (5) and cavernous sinus invasion was a significant risk factor hampering remission (6), suggesting that the tumor in this case was less likely to achieve remission by surgery, although debulking may be possible. Pasireotide LAR targets pituitary tumors and has been reported to be efficacious in terms of biochemical control and tumor shrinkage; 40% of patients treated with pasireotide LAR showed normalization of UFC at 7 months (5), and 46% of macrotumors showed a tumor volume reduction of more than 20% by 24 months, with a stable tumor size thereafter. Among the patients with measurable tumors at 24 months (n = 35), 85.7% showed a reduction in tumor volume (7). Considering the drastic effect in this case, preoperative pasireotide therapy may be considered an option for some macrotumors. For GH-secreting tumors, pasireotide as preoperative therapy has been reported to be effective (8). In addition, this case suggests that, at least in part, a large invasive ACTH-secreting tumor can be treated using pasireotide as a primary medical therapy.

Transient elevations in ACTH and cortisol levels were observed after pasireotide treatment. Interestingly, T2-weighted imaging MRI showed high signal intensity in part of the tumor just before the transient elevations (see Fig. 1B, arrowhead); thereafter, the tumor drastically shrank. A similar phenomenon has been reported in GH-secreting tumors, which may reflect tumor necrosis in the tumor (9). In addition, a similar case of Cushing disease showing a transient increase followed by a decrease in ACTH after the use of pasireotide with tumor shrinkage has been reported (10). These results, including ours, suggest a cell-killing effect of pasireotide and that the transient elevation of ACTH may be a result of cell death and leakage from these cells.

In this case, elevated IGF-1 and a subsequent decrease in IGF-1 levels after pasireotide treatment suggest the possibility of GH co-secretion, and the drastic effect of pasireotide may be related to such characteristics; however, GH was suppressed normally during the oral glucose tolerance test; therefore, it was difficult to diagnose without pathological findings in this case.

In conclusion, we report a successful case of Cushing disease caused by a Knosp grade 4, large invasive tumor treated with pasireotide LAR as the primary medical therapy. Although surgery is generally the first-line therapy for Cushing disease, pasireotide may be useful as a preoperative or primary medical therapy, at least in part, for patients with large tumors predicted to have difficulty achieving remission by surgery.
