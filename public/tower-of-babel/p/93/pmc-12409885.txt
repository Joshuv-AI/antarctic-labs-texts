Exploring the Integration of Patient Perspectives in Surgeon Education: Awareness, Engagement and Barriers to Implementation
Authors: Hazal Fiskeci Vardar, Manoj Khatri, Claire Thornber, Kokeb Andenmatten, Jane Thorley Wiedler, Monica Ghidinelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntegrating patient perspectives in medical education is increasingly recognised as critical for patient-centred care. However, many continuing professional development (CPD) programmes – particularly in surgical education – lack a structured approach to involve the patient perspective. This study explored faculty awareness, exposure, engagement and perceived barriers to integrating patient perspectives in surgeon education. We conducted an initial interview with a surgeon educator and a patient advocate to promote awareness and inform the design of an online questionnaire to be distributed to surgeon faculty. The questionnaire assessed familiarity with patient inclusion in education, frequency of involvement in educational activities, collaboration with patient organisations and perceived importance and barriers. We received 68 responses from surgeon faculty across 32 countries. While 77.7% were familiar with the concept of patient inclusion, only 54.4% had attended education that included patient perspectives in the past 5 years and 44.1% had involved patients in designing or delivering education. The most cited barriers for patient inclusion were lack of awareness (64.7%), institutional culture (45.6%), time constraints (42.7%), and concerns about liability or ethics (36.8%). Despite perception of low current levels of integration by 85.3% of respondents, the overall importance of incorporating the patient perspective in surgical education was rated high (average 3.57/5). Suggestions for improvement included partnerships with advocacy groups, use of patient stories, and involving patients in curriculum development. We can conclude that faculty recognise the value of integrating patient perspectives in surgical education, but practical implementation in CPD remains limited. Addressing cultural, ethical and institutional barriers and promoting collaboration with patient organisations may support more meaningful and sustained integration of patient perspectives in surgical education.KEYWORDS: Patient involvement, patient perspectives, surgical education, CPD, CME

Introduction

Traditionally, medical education has reduced the role of patients to recipients of care, excluding their perspectives from teaching and curriculum development. This is changing: the patient role is increasingly recognised as a valuable contributor to medical training [1,2] particularly in continuing medical education (CME) and continuing professional development (CPD).

Involving patients as educators can improve communication, give patients space to express concerns, enhance empathy and deepen understanding of patient needs [1,3–5]. This collaborative approach leads to more compassionate patient-clinician communications and relationships [6,7] offers diagnostic insights, reveals the broader impact, reduces provider anxiety and reveals systemic gaps [3,4,8,9].

Patients with long-term illness, hospitalisation or intensive healthcare experiences often face mental-health challenges such as low mood, health condition-related anxiety, depression, isolation and adjustment difficulties that go beyond their physical symptoms. As experts in their own lived experiences, patients offer critical insights often overlooked by clinicians. Studies show that many patients feel their concerns and personal narratives are not heard or genuinely acknowledged by their healthcare professionals. These challenges can seriously impact quality of life and mental health, of which physicians be unaware [3,10,11]. This disconnect underscores the need for more empathetic holistic care [4,7,12,13]. Recognising this, organisations like the Accreditation Council for Continuing Medical Education (ACCME) and the National Health Service (NHS) actively promote meaningful partnerships between patients and education providers through policies and practical resources [14,15].

Patient perspectives can be integrated into education through seminars, workshops, feedback loops and collaborative curriculum development. Open communication channels further ensure that the patient’s voice is consistently incorporated during the medical education process. Many patients are willing to contribute to such efforts out of a desire to support healthcare professionals, contribute to their communities and help others, providing a unique and valuable perspective in medical education [5,12,16–18].

The AO Foundation known for its global leadership in surgical education and research across different clinical specialities including AO Trauma, AO Spine, AO CMF, AO Recon and AO Sports has historically had limited direct patient involvement in shaping their educational programmes. This study investigated faculty awareness, exposure, engagement and perceived barriers related to the integration of patient perspective in surgical education.

Method

Study Design

The initial draft of the questionnaire was developed by cross-referencing existing questionnaires and through an interview with a surgeon educator and a patient advocate who have collaborated for many years. The questionnaire underwent two stages of revisions and was pilot tested with five surgeons before being administered. It was distributed via SurveyMonkey (Momentive Inc., San Mateo, CA, USA).

Population

The study targeted 8867 AO faculty worldwide, defined as surgeons with at least one teaching assignment in our courses in the last 5 years.

Data Collection

The interview with the surgeon educator and the patient advocate informed an article raising awareness about patient engagement in surgical education (Supplementary material 1). The questionnaire (Supplementary material 2) was distributed by email in association with the article. The questionnaire included 16 multiple-choice questions and one optional open-ended question allowing participants to share personal experiences. Seven of the multiple-choice questions included an “other” option, for respondents to expand on their responses with further insights. A 5-point rating scale was chosen to enable meaningful analysis while minimising the burden on respondents, helping to increase the response rate [19–21].

Methods of Collecting Data

The questionnaire link was embedded in a news item to 8867 AO faculty members. A total of 71 surgeons responded, of whom 68 completed the full questionnaire (completion rate of 96%). The average completion time for the questionnaire was 4 min and 49 s. Only completed responses were included in the data analysis resulting in a response rate of 0.8%.

Data Analysis

Descriptive analysis was conducted on data collected from 68 responses using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, version 16.0, Redmond, USA). Tables and figures display percentages for selected responses to clearly represent key trends and insights. Open-text responses under “other” were categorised, with certain answers grouped into identifiable categories and included in the results section. The remaining responses that did not fit into common categories were included directly in the results section as individual responses.

Ethical Approval

According to the Ethics Committee of the Canton of Zurich, this study did not require ethical committee approval (Req-2025–00606). On the questionnaire, we included the following statement of purpose, which disclosed our intended use of the data: “The information you provide will be anonymised and made available to the study group in aggregate form. The data will be used for research purposes”.

Results

The survey was emailed to 8867 AO faculty members worldwide. Of these, 3862 opened the email, 187 clicked on the questionnaire link and 68 individuals completed the questionnaire, resulting in a response rate of 0.8%. Most respondents were from AO Trauma (55.9%, n = 38), followed by AO CMF (25%, n = 17), AO Spine (16.2%, n = 11), AO Recon (1.5%, n = 1), and AO Sport (1.5%, n = 1). Respondents practiced in 32 countries, with the largest proportion in the USA (14.7%, n = 10), followed by India (10.3%, n = 7) and Egypt (5.9%, n = 4) (Table 1), reflecting the geographical diversity of our organisation.

Overall, 77.7% of surgeons (n = 47) were familiar with the concept incorporating the patient perspective in education (range: “slightly familiar” to “very familiar”), while 32.3% (n = 21) reported being “not familiar at all” (Figure 1).

To evaluate AO faculty exposure to education that included the patient perspective, we asked how often they had participated in such programmes over the past 5 years. A considerable proportion (45.6%, n = 31) reported never attending such an education, 39.7% (n = 27) had low exposure (1–5 times), and only 4.4% (n = 3) had high exposure (more than 50 times) (Figure 2(a)).

The education was provided by their hospital in 20 cases (29.4%), by AO in 6 (8.8%), by other medical societies in 9 cases, by patient associations in 2 cases, medical device industry in 2 cases and 1 by a non-government organisation (NGO). The most common methods to involve patients were patient stories and testimonials (36.8%, n = 25), followed by patients as guest speakers (22.1%, n = 15) (Figure 2(b)). Less common methods included patients creating content (n = 3), participating in curriculum development (n = 3), patients reviewing content (n = 2), and in the “other” category, patient reported outcome measures (PROMs) (n = 1), and webinars (n = 1) (Figure 2(b)).

We then asked faculty about their direct experience in incorporating patient perspectives in designing or delivering education. The majority reported never including the patient’s perspective over the past 5 years (55.9%, n = 38), while 44.1% had some level of experience (range 1–5 times (N = 19) to more than 50 times (N = 4)) (Figure 2(c)). The primary providers of the education were their hospital (n = 14), followed by AO (n = 6), industry (n = 2), their university (n = 1), and a speciality organisation called COTI (n = 1). Among those who included the patient’s perspective in designing and delivering education, 27.7% (n = 18) used recorded patient stories or testimonials, 15.4% (n = 10) involved patients in the needs assessment and 7.8% (n = 5) invited patients as guest speakers. Less common approaches included patients reviewing content (n = 3), participating in curriculum development (n = 3) and creating content (n = 2) (Figure 2(d)). Additionally, six respondents reported other methods such as PROMs, discussing treatment options and consent with a patient within lectures and case presentation, as well as patient-related research.

Overall, these findings highlight high variability in exposure and direct involvement to educational programmes including patients’ perspective across AO faculty.

We additionally asked the faculty about their collaboration with patient organisations and/or advocacy groups over the past 5 years. The majority (67.7%, n = 46) had “never” collaborated, and 32.4% had some level of collaboration (range 1), ranging from 1 to 5 times (n = 13) to 26–50 times (n = 1) (Figure 3(a)). The most common collaboration methods included providing patient counselling (n = 14), conducting joint educational events (n = 11), and participating in quality improvement projects (n = 10) (Figure 3(b)). Co-designing educational materials (n = 4) and conducting collaborative research projects (n = 4) were less frequent (Figure 3(b)). In the “other” category, examples included discussion panels, a presentation at patient congresses, and the development of educational materials for patients. These findings indicate that our faculty had limited collaboration with patient organisations and advocacy groups.

Faculty also rated the current general level of inclusion of patient perspectives in education and their perceived importance of it. The rating of current level of inclusion varied from very low (58.8%, n = 40) to very high (2.9%, n = 2) (Figure 4(a)). The importance was rated between moderately to extremely important by most responders (88.2%), with six respondents rating it as “low important” and two as “not important at all” (Figure 4(b)).

The main perceived barriers to including patient perspectives in education were lack of awareness (64.7%, n = 44), institutional culture (45.6%, n = 31), time constraints (42.7%, n = 29), fear of liability or ethical concerns (36.8%, n = 31) and scepticism about the value of patient input (30.9%, n = 21) (Figure 4(c)). Respondents also noted various challenges under “other”, including the need for high compliance and validated outcome measures, patients’ limited education, a focus among surgeons on surgical techniques over patient perspectives, the necessity for patient education before implementing initiatives, concerns about patient over-empowerment and systemic pressures prioritising economic incentives over comprehensive care. Regarding steps to try increase patient involvement in education, respondents provided various suggestions (Figure 5).

Suggested steps to increase patient involvement in surgical education. N=67.

The most common suggestions were encouraging patients to share their experiences and perspectives (73.1%, n = 49), followed by establishing partnerships with patient organisations (59.7%, n = 40), and actively seeking patient input in curriculum development (40.3%, n = 27). Additionally, seven respondents provided suggestions under the “other” category, such as incorporating education about PROMs into AO activities, raising awareness, acknowledging the need for and value of patient perspectives, conducting studies and encouraging surgeons to share patient input.

Discussion

The study evaluated faculty awareness, exposure and perceived importance of incorporating patient perspectives in surgical education across an international cohort of faculty affiliated with our global organisation. Despite our organisation’s commitment to improving patient care, patient involvement in the design or delivery of educational content remains limited. Our findings also indicate that most faculty consider the integration of patient perspectives in surgical education of value, regardless of their personal experience with it. Barriers identified included limited awareness, institutional culture, time constraints and concerns around ethics and liability.

Nearly half of the respondents had not engaged in educational activities that included patient perspectives in the past 5 years, and more than half had never involved patients in the design or delivery of educational content. Conversely, over three-quarters of respondents acknowledged at least some degree of familiarity with the concept. This gap between awareness and implementation reflects a broader trend in medical education, where conceptual endorsement frequently does not lead to sustained behavioural change or institutional practice [5,22]. This emphasises the need for structured opportunities and institutional support to promote patient involvement in medical education [23].

Our findings also demonstrate that the inclusion of patient perspectives remains largely passive, typically limited to patient testimonials and guest speaker roles. Less frequent were collaborative or co-creative approaches, such as involving patients in curriculum design, content review or as a faculty. This is consistent with existing literature showing that most programmes rely on storytelling and providing feedback rather than active collaboration [2,8,9,24]. This hierarchical pattern of engagement has been previously described in frameworks categorising patient involvement levels, from passive receipt of care narratives to active partnership in educational strategy and governance [25] with higher levels such as co-design offering improved relevance, authenticity and learner engagement [1,23].

Questionnaire results also indicated that collaboration with patient organisations and advocacy groups is underused. Two-thirds of respondents reported no such collaboration in the past 5 years. When collaborations occurred, they tended to be counselling or ad-hoc events rather than ongoing partnerships or content development. These represent missed opportunities to build equitable and trusted structured relationships and address strategic, ethical and policy concerns [26,27].

In addition to structural barriers like lack of awareness, time constraints and institutional culture, respondents identified other forms of resistance to patient inclusion, such as prioritisation of teaching technical skills, clinical expertise regarded as the most accurate understanding of patient needs, and a general undervaluation of patients’ input. Some faculty feared patients becoming “too empowered”. This reflects traditional views of medical professionalism and scepticism about patient involvement [9,16,23,24]. Other limiting factors, such as patients’ physical pain, discomfort or emotional conditions also merit consideration [2,12,28,29]. Addressing barriers and fostering a supportive environment are essential for promoting greater awareness and advancing patient-centred medical education [5,6,15]. Following ACCME’s Patient Partner Roadmap, establishing clear written agreements with patients and patient organisations can further reduce barriers to collaboration by clarifying roles, addressing legal and ethical considerations and fostering mutual trust and accountability [30].

These findings have implications for the development of future educational activities in surgical CME/CPD. First, educational organisations should consider creating mechanisms that promote co-design and participatory curriculum development with patients. Second, establishing partnerships with patient advocacy groups can facilitate sustained involvement and mutual learning. Third, faculty development initiatives should provide tools and trainings to integrate patient perspectives effectively. Finally, further research is needed to evaluate the impact of patient-inclusive educational models on learner outcomes, clinical decision-making and patient care. Building on these general recommendations, the AO has identified the need for a structured approach to incorporating patient perspectives into surgical education. The AO is exploring an initiative designed to raise awareness of patient involvement’s value and to consider ways of systematically integrating patient input into curriculum planning processes, beginning with the needs assessment phase.

Limitations

While this study offers valuable insights into the current state of integrating patient perspectives among our faculty members, several limitations must be acknowledged. Although the respondents represented a diverse sample, potential participation bias may have influenced the results; those who chose to participate may have a stronger interest in patient involvement, leading to an overrepresentation of certain viewpoints. The use of self-reported data introduces the possibility of response bias, with respondents potentially over- or underestimating their engagement in patient involvement activities. Despite the low response rate of 0.8%, which is not uncommon in web-based surveys, the data offer valuable initial insights to the research questions [31,32]. However, the findings may not fully represent the views of all faculty members, which could limit the generalisability of our results.

Conclusion

While faculty show keen interest in incorporating patient perspectives into surgical education, actual implementation remains limited and inconsistent. Bridging this gap requires cultural change within institutions, targeted faculty development and structured collaboration with patient communities. Stronger partnerships between surgeons, patients and education specialists are necessary to ensure that CME/CPD initiatives address the real needs and priorities of patients and healthcare professionals. Future educational efforts should adopt strategies that support meaningful patient involvement, thereby enhancing the relevance, impact and ethical foundation of surgical education.
