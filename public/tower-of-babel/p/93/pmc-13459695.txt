Intrauterine Pregnancy in a Uterus Didelphys Mimicking an Ectopic Pregnancy
Authors: Catarina Ferreira, Cláudia Pinto, Ana Teixeira-Castro, Adriano Soares, Elsa Pereira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Uterus didelphys is a rare congenital uterine anomaly resulting from the complete failure of fusion of the Müllerian ducts. Early pregnancy in a duplicated uterus can present a significant diagnostic challenge, as the gravid lateralized hemi uterus may be mistaken for an adnexal mass. A 31-year-old woman presented at six weeks of gestation with mild abdominal cramping. A two-dimensional transvaginal ultrasound revealed an empty main uterine cavity and a gestational sac adjacent to the right ovary. Given a serum beta-hCG level of 4,200 mIU/mL and concern for an ectopic pregnancy, urgent diagnostic laparoscopy was performed. Surgery revealed a completely duplicated uterus, with the pregnancy confirmed to be intrauterine within the right hemi-uterus. A subsequent three-dimensional transvaginal ultrasound confirmed the diagnosis of uterus didelphys. The pregnancy progressed without complications, resulting in a planned cesarean delivery at 39 weeks due to breech presentation. Uterus didelphys can closely mimic an ectopic gestation during early ultrasound assessment. Early integration of three-dimensional transvaginal ultrasound in stable patients with suspected ectopic pregnancies is an invaluable tool to accurately identify congenital anomalies and prevent unnecessary surgical interventions.

Introduction

Congenital uterine anomalies (CUAs) often remain asymptomatic and are typically discovered incidentally during infertility investigations or routine obstetric ultrasounds. Uterus didelphys, classified as U3b/C2 according to the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology/European Society for Gynaecological Endoscopy (ESHRE/ESGE) consensus system, results from the complete failure of fusion of the Müllerian ducts [1,2]. This structural anomaly is characterized by two completely separate hemi-uteri and duplicated cervices, occasionally presenting alongside a longitudinal vaginal septum [1,2].

The estimated prevalence of uterus didelphys in the general population is around 0.1%-0.5% and accounts for approximately 5-11% of all Müllerian anomalies [3]. While female reproductive potential is generally preserved, early pregnancy assessment in these patients can be highly complex [3,4]. Specifically, the non-gravid uterus may be preferentially visualized, while the gravid hemi-uterus could appear lateralized, causing the pregnancy to simulate an adnexal mass and potentially leading to a misdiagnosis of ectopic pregnancy [4].

We report a case of an early intrauterine pregnancy in a previously undiagnosed uterus didelphys that strictly mimicked a tubal ectopic gestation. To our knowledge, only a limited number of cases describing uterus didelphys initially misdiagnosed as an ectopic pregnancy have been reported in the literature.

Case presentation

A 31-year-old woman presented to the emergency department at six weeks of gestation (based on last menstrual period), complaining of mild, intermittent lower abdominal cramping. Her obstetric history included one prior full-term vaginal delivery at another institution, with no reported complications. On physical examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable. The abdomen was soft, with no adnexal tenderness, and there was no evidence of vaginal bleeding.

Initial imaging via two-dimensional transvaginal ultrasound (2D-TVUS, Figures 1-2) demonstrated an anteverted uterus with an endometrial thickness of 14 mm; however, no definitive intrauterine gestational sac was identified. Both ovaries appeared normal. Further exploration visualized a gestational sac containing a live embryo (crown-rump length of 2.3 mm, consistent with five weeks and five days of gestation) located adjacent to the right ovary.

Laboratory results showed a serum beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (beta-hCG) level of 4,200 mIU/mL (non-pregnant reference level: <5 mIU/mL; discriminatory zone typically 1,500-2,000 mIU/mL).

Given the unavailability of immediate advanced imaging and the unacceptable risk of delaying treatment for a potentially ruptured ectopic pregnancy, the patient underwent urgent exploratory laparoscopy.

Intraoperatively, the laparoscopy revealed a complete uterine duplication (Figure 3). Two separate uterine bodies were identified, each associated with its own distinct fallopian tube and ovary.

Intraoperative laparoscopic view showing two distinct uterine bodies, each with its own fallopian tube and ovary.

Arrow A indicates the left uterine cavity and its ipsilateral fallopian tube and ovary; Arrow B indicates the right uterine cavity and its ipsilateral fallopian tube and ovary.

The gestational sac was found to be distending the right hemi-uterus, confirming a legitimate intrauterine pregnancy. No tubal pathology was observed.

The patient's postoperative recovery was unremarkable. To further characterize the anatomical anomaly, a three-dimensional transvaginal ultrasound (3D-TVUS, Figure 4) was performed, showing two divergent uterine horns separated by a deep external fundal cleft, alongside two distinct cervices, confirming the diagnosis of uterus didelphys.

Arrow A indicates the left uterine body and cervix; Arrow B indicates the right gravid uterine body and its corresponding cervix.

Following diagnosis, the patient was managed expectantly, and the pregnancy progressed without further complications. Due to persistent breech presentation, a planned cesarean section was successfully performed at 39 weeks of gestation, resulting in the delivery of a healthy newborn, weighing 2,890 g and measuring 48 cm.

Discussion

In routine clinical practice, the absence of an intrauterine gestational sac when beta-hCG levels exceed the "discriminatory zone" (typically 1,500-2,000 mIU/mL) is considered highly suggestive of an extrauterine gestation [5]. In this patient, the markedly elevated beta-hCG levels combined with the apparent adnexal location of the gestational sac on 2D-TVUS strongly supported a presumptive diagnosis of a tubal ectopic pregnancy.

The primary hallmark and diagnostic pitfall of this case was the lateralization of the gravid hemi-uterus, which effectively simulated an adnexal mass. While 2D-TVUS is the standard first-line imaging modality in early pregnancy, its sensitivity for accurately diagnosing CUAs is limited. The non-gravid hemi-uterus is often mistaken for a normal, empty uterus, while the gravid horn is misinterpreted as an ectopic structure.

The subsequent use of 3D-TVUS proved crucial for the non-invasive characterization of this patient's anomaly. 3D-TVUS is now considered the gold standard for evaluation of uterine morphology [6-8]. Unlike 2D imaging, it allows for a comprehensive assessment of the external fundal contour - necessary to distinguish between septate, bicornuate, and didelphys uteri - as well as the internal anatomical relationships between the endometrial cavities [6-8]. Earlier utilization of 3D-TVUS in the diagnostic workup might have prevented the need for surgical exploration. However, given the potentially life-threatening nature of a ruptured ectopic pregnancy, prompt surgical management in the emergency setting was completely justified to ensure patient safety.

Furthermore, recognizing uterus didelphys early is critical for tailoring prenatal care. Although reproductive potential is preserved, patients face increased obstetric risks, including miscarriage (reported rates up to 30%), preterm birth (ranging from 20% to 40%), fetal malpresentation (particularly breech presentation), and cervical insufficiency [9-11]. Despite these risks, favourable term outcomes can be achieved with appropriate obstetric surveillance [12].

Conclusions

Uterus didelphys represents a significant diagnostic challenge during early pregnancy and can closely mimic an ectopic gestation. Early consideration of congenital uterine anomalies - and the proactive incorporation of 3D-TVUS into the diagnostic workup for suspected ectopic pregnancies in hemodynamically stable patients - is essential. These practices help minimize diagnostic uncertainty, avoid potentially dangerous misdiagnoses, and optimize pre-operative planning. While acute clinical presentation may still warrant surgical exploration, this case highlights a critical pitfall in early gestational assessment and emphasizes the role of advanced 3D imaging in refining modern obstetric diagnostics.
