MED15-TFE3 rearranged renal cell carcinoma: a subtype of TFE3-rearranged renal cell carcinoma with unique clinicopathologic features and better prognosis
Authors: Zhenggen Deng, Yanhao Xu, Enjia Duan, Xiaopo Zhang, Xiang Dong, Hongqian Guo, Weidong Gan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMED15-TFE3 rearranged renal cell carcinoma (MED15-TFE3 rRCC) is a rare subtype of TFE3-rearranged renal cell carcinoma (TFE3 rRCC). To date, only 47 cases of MED15-TFE3 rRCC (including 2 cases from our institution) have been reported worldwide.MethodsUsing immunohistochemistry (IHC), fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and RNA sequencing, we aimed to identify whether TFE3 rRCC patients at our institution harbor the MED15-TFE3 gene fusion. We will perform a statistical analysis of the pathological features, imaging findings, and clinical outcomes of all published cases of MED 15-TFE rRCC.ResultsMED15-TFE3 rRCC typically has a cystic and solid (predominantly cystic) architecture. The tumor cells feature round nuclei with inconspicuous nucleoli, abundant cytoplasm, and clear to eosinophilic staining. Immunohistochemically, 38 of 39 patients had positive nuclear staining for TFE3, and 37 of 38 patients were positive for PAX8. The FISH assay was positive for rearrangement (red–green signal separation). RNA sequencing identified the MED15-TFE3 fusion gene. One pediatric patient presented with preoperative hilar lymph node metastasis. During follow-up, only one patient developed metastatic disease.ConclusionsIn contrast to traditional TFE3 rRCCs, MED15-TFE3 rRCCs may have distinctive clinicopathological features and a better prognosis. These findings enhance our understanding of the heterogeneity of TFE3 rRCCs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04048-x.Keywords: MED15-TFE3, rRCC (rearranged renal cell carcinoma), clinicopathologic, TFE3, prognosis

Simple Summary

Located at Xp11.2, the TFE3 gene can form fusion genes through chromosomal translocation, leading to TFE3 protein overexpression and ultimately tumor development. While TFE3 rearranged renal cell carcinoma is rare, our study focused on the MED15-TFE3 variant, an exceptionally uncommon subtype. By analyzing two cases from our institution alongside all reported cases, we aim to characterize its distinct clinical profile and identify unique diagnostic features. Its specific management of TFE3 rearranged renal cell carcinoma, including its surgical strategy and lack of need for adjuvant therapy, suggests that it may have the most favorable prognosis.

Introduction

TFE3 rRCC was classified as an independent subtype of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) within the MiT (microphthalmia-associated transcription factor) family of translocated RCCs in 2016 and was classified as a molecularly defined renal cell carcinoma according to the latest WHO genitourinary tumor classification in 2022 [1, 2]. The TFE3 gene is located on chromosome Xp11.2 and can be fused with other genes via chromosome translocation. In turn, TFE3 fusion genes are formed, resulting in the overexpression of the TFE3 protein, which ultimately leads to tumorigenesis and progression and represents only 1%−2% of all RCC cases [3]. Common TFE3 fusion gene partners include ASPL, PRCC, SFPQ, and NONO, and these four isoform fusion isoforms account for approximately 80% of all TFE3 rRCCs [4–7]. In addition, various fusion genes have been reported, including but not limited to NONO-TFE3, ASPL-TFE3, PARP14-TFE3, PRCC-TFE3, ARID1B-TFE3, RBM10-TFE3 and MED15-TFE3 rRCC [8–15].Previous studies have identified solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) as potential biomarkers for the diagnosis and prognosis of renal cell carcinoma [16]. However, in TFE3 rRCCs, the type and number of genes fused in the real world for TFE3 rRCC are not yet known. The presence of different fusion partners contributes to TFE3 rRCC being a highly heterogeneous tumor [17]. MED15-TFE3 rRCC is extremely rare among reported TFE3 rRCC cases, with only 47 (including 2 cases from our institution) documented to date.

Morphologically, TFE3 rRCC is characterized by a papillary structure with clear or eosinophilic cytoplasm and psammoma bodies [4, 18]. Immunohistochemically, tumor cells poorly express epithelial markers. Conversely, melanocytic markers (Melan A and HMB45) may often be positively expressed in tumors [19, 20]. The clinical behavior of TFE3 rRCC varies widely in the literature, with tumors often having an indolent course in children under 16 years of age and an aggressive course being more common in adult patients, who often present with regional lymph nodes and distant metastases [17, 21–23]. The expression of Dickkopf-1 (DKK1) and low-density lipoprotein related receptor protein 5 (LRP5) influences the clinical outcomes of renal cell carcinoma, whereas kidney injury molecule 1 (KIM-1) plays an immunomodulatory role in clear cell RCC. Considering the heterogeneity of TFE3 rRCC, different genotypes of tumors may have various biological behaviors and clinical prognoses [20, 24–27]. We found that not all TFE3 rRCCs have the same dismal prognosis. MED15-TFE3 rRCC represents a unique exception among renal tumors. The MED15 gene (Mediator Complex Subunit 15), located on chromosome 22q11.21, is a component of the Mediator complex, a multiprotein complex that serves as a bridge in RNA polymerase II-mediated transcription. This subtype arises from a translocation between the TFE3 gene on chromosome Xp11.2 and the MED15 gene on chromosome 22q11.21, resulting in a MED15-TFE3 fusion gene [28, 29]. Because the imaging characteristics of MED15-TFE3 rRCC closely resemble those of simple renal cysts, clinicians lacking experience with this rare entity may inappropriately perform cyst decortication, which is a potentially detrimental management error with irreversible consequences. Here, we report two cases of MED15-TFE3 rRCC accompanied by special morphologic features and an indolent clinical course and review the relevant literature to deepen the understanding of MED15-TFE3 rRCC.

Methods

Literature search and case collection

This study retrieved literature from the EMBASE, PubMed, MEDLINE, Cochrane, and DARE databases via the search keywords “Xp11.2 translocation”, “MED15”, “TFE3”, and “renal cell carcinoma”. After a full-text review, cases that met the criteria were identified. Since MED15-TFE3 rRCC was first reported by Classe et al. in 2017, studies published between January 1, 2017, and May 31, 2024, were included. All identified articles were carefully reviewed to avoid duplication and to ensure that no omissions occurred in cases of overlapping datasets. (1) Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) literature reporting MED15-TFE3 rRCC confirmed by TFE3 immunohistochemistry or break-apart FISH assay; (2) the MED15-TFE3 fusion subtype was ultimately verified by molecular diagnostic techniques such as RT‒PCR, fusion-specific FISH probes, or next-generation sequencing; (3) included cases containing corresponding clinicopathological and survival data, including age, sex, tumor size, clinical stage, follow-up duration, histopathological features, and immunohistochemical results; and (4) for cases from the same center or with overlapping subjects, the most recently published or most comprehensively documented case was selected. (2) The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) positive for TFE3 immunohistochemical staining or break-apart FISH but without further molecular testing; (2) confirmed as MED15-TFE3 rRCC but lacking any clinicopathological data or follow-up records; (3) duplicated cases from the same center or study cohort; and (4) pathological types not classified as RCC.

Immunohistochemistry

The tumor samples were fixed with formalin (10% solution) and embedded in paraffin. The primary antibodies used for immunohistochemistry were as follows: TFE3 (ab179804, 1:250; Abcam), PAX8 (ab181054, 1:500; Abcam), CK7 (OV-TL 12/30, 1:800; Dako), CAIX (ab108351, 1:150; Abcam), CD10 (ab951, 1:25; Abcam), Cathepsin K (3F9, 1:300; Abcam), CD117 (2E4, 1:1000; Dako), and P504s (13H4, 1:200; Dako). The assessment of immunoreactivity was performed as described previously [30].

Fluorescence in situ hybridization

Four-micron-thick formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections were obtained for fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). The rearrangement of the TFE3 gene was detected via a dual-color break-apart TFE3 probe (Anbiping Company, Guangzhou, China). The detailed steps of FISH were described in a previous study [30]. A minimum of 100 tumor nuclei were analyzed via fluorescence microscopy, and the split signal was defined as a distance >2 signal diameters. A positive FISH result was defined as more than 10% of tumor nuclei with a split signal.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from FFPE tissue sections via an RNeasy Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality was assessed on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent, USA). Ribosomal RNA was then removed via the RNase H enzyme, and libraries were constructed via the KAPA Stranded RNA-Seq Kit with RiboErase (HMR) (KAPA Biosystems). Library quality was evaluated on the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system to ensure RNA integrity. The data output per sample should be >10 Gb, and the expected alignment rate should exceed 70%, with an RNA integrity number (RIN) >2.0 and a DV200 value (percentage of RNA fragments >200 nucleotides) >30%. High-throughput RNA sequencing was performed by GloriousMed Technology (Beijing, China) on an Illumina HiSeqTM 2000 platform (California, USA), with a sequencing depth ≥ 100 million reads and a Q30 base ratio ≥ 90%. Base calling was conducted via bcl2fastq (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) to generate raw sequencing reads in FASTQ format. The quality-pass reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) via STAR aligner (v2.5.3a) to identify exon, intron, and intergenic features [31]. The average coverage of mapped reads across feature coordinates was calculated. Gene fusions were identified and visualized via the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) [32]. The research ethics committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital approved this retrospective study and granted a waiver of informed consent for the included patients.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed via SPSS statistical software version 26.0. Statistical analysis was performed on data such as age, sex, tumor size, clinical stage, follow-up time, histopathological features, and immunohistochemistry results, among other variables. Categorical data are presented as numbers (%), and continuous data are reported as the means.

Clinical characteristics

Patient 1. The patient was a 27-year-old female whose renal tumors were found during a physical examination without any symptoms. The patient had no underlying disease or family history of tumors. A reverse computed tomography (CT) enhanced scan revealed a cystic solid mass (predominantly a cystic lesion, 6.8*5.2 cm) in the right kidney. The solid part of the mass showed slightly circular enhancement during the corticomedullary phase (Fig. 1A-B). Robot-assisted laparoscopic partial nephrectomy was ultimately performed. According to the TNM staging, the tumor was in the T1bN0M0 stage. The patient recovered well postoperatively without any adjuvant therapy. No recurrence or metastasis was found during the 71-month follow-up (Fig. 1C: 3-month post-operative CT scan; Fig. 1D: Most recent CT scan).

Patient 2. The patient was a 17-year-old girl who visited a local hospital due to nausea and vomiting. The girl was first misdiagnosed with gastroenteritis and was managed symptomatically. However, the symptoms did not improve, so the patient further underwent an abdominal ultrasound examination. Ultrasound imaging revealed inhomogeneous echogenicity, with a large abdominal mass. An abdominal contrast CT scan was subsequently performed. The cystic solid lesion (16*15.5 cm) contained a greater proportion of the cystic component. The solid component was moderately enhanced in the corticomedullary phase. In addition to the primary tumor, an enlarged lymph node was also observed at the right renal hilum (Fig. 2A-B). Finally, the patient underwent open radical nephrectomy and lymphadenectomy at our hospital. The pathological TNM stage was T2bN1M0. At follow-up 47 months after surgery, the girl exhibited no recurrence after surgery (Fig. 2C: 3-month post-operative CT scan; Fig. 2D: Most recent CT scan).

According to previous reports, the majority of cases involved adult patients with large, incidentally discovered cystic and solid masses on imaging who underwent partial nephrectomy (with enucleation) or radical nephrectomy. No additional adjuvant therapy was administered to any patients postoperatively. One case reported pulmonary metastasis after 15 years, but the patient declined a lung biopsy to confirm the origin of the metastatic lesion, representing only an isolated case of very late metastasis. The remaining 46 patients showed no evidence of metastasis or recurrence during long-term follow-up.

Pathological findings

Microscopically, most parts of the tumor were cystic, and solid structures were visible in some areas. The tumor cells were arranged in cords or nests and contained abundant eosinophilic or clear cytoplasm. Psammoma bodies were randomly distributed within the stroma (Fig. 3). Among the total cases, psammoma bodies were documented in 37 evaluated cases, with 27 cases (27/37, 73.0%) showing the presence of psammoma bodies. CA-IX, CD10, CD117, CK7, cathepsin K and P504s were negative in both MED15-TFE3 rRCCs. In contrast, positive expression of TFE3 and PAX8 was detected in both cases (Table 2) (Fig. 4).

Compared with TFE3 immunohistochemical staining, the FISH assay had greater accuracy. Since males had only one X chromosome, a pair of separated (red and green) signals was considered positive FISH results. Because one of the two X chromosomes was inactive in females, the positive results included a pair of separated (green and red) signals and a fused (yellow) signal. Both of our female patients presented these features (Fig. 4). Considering the influence of section truncation, a single red or green single in males was also regarded as a positive result. The same reasoning applied to female patients.

On the basis of the integrated analysis of pathological features and corresponding immunohistochemical results in all patients with MED15-TFE3 rRCC, the vast majority exhibited cystic architecture. Furthermore, 37 out of 38 patients (97.4%) demonstrated immunoreactivity for PAX8, whereas variable expression patterns were observed for CA-IX, CD10, CD117, CK7, cathepsin K, and P504s.

Discussion

Since the discovery of PRCC-TFE3 rRCC as the first fusion partner in TFE3 rRCC, various partners have been reported in TFE3 rRCCs [5, 33]. Previous studies have indicated that the different molecular subtypes of TFE3 rRCC may contribute to different clinicopathological features [17, 20, 25, 30, 34, 35]. For example, ASPL-TFE3 rRCCs may have the most typical morphologic structure of TFE3 rRCCs, including papillary or nested structures, alveolar patterns, and abundant psammoma bodies. In contrast, PRCC-TFE3 rRCC is characterized by a compact architecture, scant cytoplasm, and tightly arranged tumor cells, with psammoma bodies less frequently observed [4]. These pathological features differ significantly from those of MED15-TFE3 rRCC [35]. Cathepsin K is often expressed in PRCC-TFE3 rRCCs but is not expressed in ASPL-TFE3 rRCC or SFPQ-TFE3 rRCCs [25]. Compared with PRCC-TFE3 rRCCs, ASPL-TFE3 rRCCs tend to present with rapid progression and an advanced stage [34].

As a part of the multiprotein mediator complex, MED15 is involved in the RNA polymerase II-dependent transcription process. It has been reported that MED15 is an essential promoter of tumor initiation and progression in RCCs [36]. The first case of MED15-TFE3 gene fusion was reported by Classe et al. [37]. To date, only 47 cases of MED15-TFE3 rRCC have been reported worldwide. Detailed clinicopathological information is summarized in Table 1. According to the available studies, MED15-TFE3 rRCCs are more common in young females (35/47, 74.5%), with an average age at onset of 40 years, which is consistent with reports on TFE3 rRCCs [12, 38–44]. Similarly, a majority of patients are asymptomatic and are often incidentally observed during physical examination. Although the prognosis of TFE3-related rRCC remains controversial, most studies still consider that the tumor is an aggressive disease with a poor prognosis [12, 40, 45]. Among the 194 TFE3 rRCC patients whose complete follow-up information was available, nearly half (91 patients) experienced aggressive behavior [12]. However, TFE3 rRCC is more common in children and adolescents, accounts for up to 50% of renal cell carcinomas in children, and has a better prognosis than adult renal cell carcinomas [46–50]. For the first time, we report a MED15-TFE3 gene fusion in a pediatric patient with TFE3 rRCC. Moreover, the patient is the only one with lymph node metastasis within MED15-TFE3 rRCCs. However, even pediatric patients with lymph node metastasis also have a more favorable prognosis than adult patients do [21, 23, 51]. The total follow-up time was 47 months, and no recurrence or metastasis was observed; long-term follow-up is still needed. In the present study of MED15-TFE3 rRCC, the follow-up ranged from 6 to 180 months. Apart from one case of pulmonary metastasis that occurred 15 years post-operatively, no metastases or recurrences were observed in the remaining patients. As mentioned, this singular case of very late metastasis—particularly without confirmatory biopsy—is viewed with caution, and it remains the only reported instance of metastasis or recurrence across all cases. Although one patient presented with preoperative lymph node metastasis (case 2) and another developed postoperative distant metastasis (a patient from the literature), all patients ultimately survived, either disease free or with stable disease. Overall, MED15-TFE3 rRCC has a favorable prognosis, which stands in stark contrast to the highly aggressive behavior and high mortality rate typically associated with conventional TFE3 rRCC subtypes such as ASPL-TFE3 rRCC [30, 34]. On the basis of these findings, it is plausible that this fusion variant may represent the most prognostically favorable subtype among TFE3 rRCCs.

On imaging, a complex cystic component is common in TFE3 rRCCs [42, 52, 53]. This feature is particularly prominent in MED15-TFE3 rRCCs [14, 41]. Our patients also presented with mixed solid‒cystic components (mainly cystic) on CT images. We report a pediatric patient with MED15-TFE3 rRCC with lymph node metastasis. The enlarged lymph node could be visualized before the preoperative CT. Considering the tendency of TFE3 rRCC to undergo lymph node metastasis, the possibility of TFE3 rRCC should be considered for RCC with enlarged lymph nodes in young patients. Some previous studies on CT imaging in MED15-TFE3 rRCC also revealed a complex cystic mass [20, 38, 54]. For MED15-TFE3 rRCC, a mixed cystic-solid lesion on CT may be the characteristic imaging feature. Therefore, CT should be distinguished from simple renal cysts at the time of initial diagnosis, and inappropriate cyst decompression should be performed in cases of cystic lesions [55].

Morphologically, most MED15-TFE3 rRCCs have extensive cystic structures, and a small portion of them accompanied by fibrotic septa are similar to multilocular cystic renal neoplasms of low malignant potential. The tumor cells had round nuclei with inconspicuous nucleoli and abundant clear or eosinophilic cytoplasm. Psammoma bodies are frequently found in MED15-TFE3 rRCCs [20, 30, 37, 54]. Interestingly, in the only previous case of MED15-TFE3 rRCC with disease progression, tubular, papillary and solid morphological structures, rather than typical cystic‒solid features, were observed [30]. We consider that the simple structure of MED15-TFE3 rRCCs may be associated with a favorable prognosis. Immunohistochemically, TFE3 showed varying degrees of positive expression in almost all MED15-TFE3 rRCCs, except for one case that was considered TFE3 negative because of weak nuclear staining [54]. Like other studies on MED15-TFE3 rRCC, PAX8 expression was positive in our patients. Interestingly, melanotic Xp11 neoplasms harboring the MED15-TFE3 fusion gene have been reported to have negative expression for PAX8 and positive expression for cathepsin K and HMB45 [30]. Similar to previous studies on MED15-TFE3 rRCC, we found that CK7 and CA-IX were frequently negatively expressed in tumor cells. Cathepsin K is considered the transcriptional target of the MiT family. The expression of cathepsin K varies in different subtypes of TFE3 rRCCs. This marker is often positively expressed in PRCC-TFE3 rRCCs but negatively expressed in SFPQ-TFE3 rRCCs, ASPL-TFE3 rRCCs and NONO-TFE3 rRCCs [56]. Among all studies on MED15-TFE3 rRCC, positive and negative expression of cathepsin K was observed in 24 and 3 patients (including our 2 patients), respectively. Hence, the expression of cathepsin K in MED15-TFE3 rRCC is uncertain, and further reports with large sample sizes are needed.

However, positive staining for TFE3 is not a specific manifestation of TFE3 rRCC, which can also be found in other types of RCCs [57]. To confirm the existence of TFE3 translocation in our cases, a TFE3 break-apart FISH assay was used. This assay has been regarded as the gold standard for the diagnosis of TFE3-related rRCC in recent years [12, 35]. Unlike the negative FISH result induced by intrachromosomal inversion, the MED15-TFE3 rRCC shows typical positive FISH results in our cases. Considering the unusual morphological features and imaging findings of TFE3 rRCC in our cases, we carried out RNA sequencing to conclusively confirm the presence of the MED15-TFE3 fusion gene.

Until now, there has been no specific drug for treating TFE3 rRCCs, and surgery has remained the first-line treatment for patients with early-stage disease [12]. Considering the aggressive behavior and poor prognosis of TFE3 rRCCs, radical nephrectomy is performed in most cases [34]. Regardless of which surgical method is selected, the prognosis of patients with MED15-TFE3 rRCC is excellent. In our previous study of TFE3 rRCC, we demonstrated that nephron-sparing surgery should be considered for patients with cT1a tumors. However, for patients with cT1b tumors, radical nephrectomy may be the preferred choice for surgery [46]. This finding may not be applicable to patients with MED15-TFE3 rRCC. Owing to the excellent prognosis of patients, we speculate that nephron-sparing surgery could still be recommended as the preferred surgical method, even for patients with cT1b tumors.

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size limits the authenticity of our conclusions. Second, because high-throughput RNA sequencing has not been routinely conducted in clinical work, most studies have not categorized TFE3 rRCCs according to their fusion partner. Therefore, the misdiagnosis of MED15-TFE3 rRCC may limit the generalizability of the conclusions. The cystic structure, psammoma body, and TFE3 break-apart FISH assay may help in the diagnosis of MED15-TFE3 rRCC. On the basis of the distinctive clinical, radiological, pathological and biological characteristics of MED15-TFE3 rRCC, the TFE 3 rRCC isoforms can be identified without reliance on high-throughput RNA sequencing. We will actively pursue academic collaborations to collect more case samples. Our team will intensify the screening of historical renal cystic-solid lesions and enhance MED15-TFE3 rRCC-specific diagnostic protocols. By accumulating this specialized experience, we aim to reduce the reliance on costly high-throughput RNA sequencing while improving the clinical diagnostic accuracy for MED15-TFE3 rRCC.

Conclusion

In this study, we reported two cases of TFE3 rRCC with MED15-TFE3 gene fusion confirmed by RNA sequencing. One of these cases was the first reported pediatric patient with renal hilar lymph node metastasis. MED15-TFE3 rRCC is morphologically distinct from typical TFE3 rRCC, and a representative cystic‒solid structure is common in such tumors. Unlike the traditional poor prognosis of patients with TFE3 rRCC, combined with all previously reported cases of MED15-TFE3 rRCC, patients with MED15-TFE3 rRCC have better prognosis among the various fusion subtypes. The distinctive features of MED15-TFE3 rRCCs increase our understanding of the heterogeneity of TFE3 rRCCs.
