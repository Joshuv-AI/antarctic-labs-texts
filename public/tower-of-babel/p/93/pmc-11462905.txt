Detection and characterization of Campylobacter in air samples from poultry houses using shot-gun metagenomics – a pilot study
Authors: Thomas H A Haverkamp, Bjørn Spilsberg, Gro S Johannessen, Mona Torp, Camilla Sekse
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFoodborne pathogens such as Campylobacter jejuni are responsible for a large proportion of the gastrointestinal infections worldwide associated with poultry meat. Campylobacter spp. can be found in the chicken fecal microbiome and can contaminate poultry meat during the slaughter process. Commonly used sampling methods to detect Campylobacter spp. at poultry farms use fecal droppings or boot swabs in combination with conventional culture techniques or PCR. In this pilot study, we have used air filtering and filters spiked with mock communities in combination with shotgun metagenomics to detect Campylobacter and test the applicability of this approach for the detection and characterization of foodborne pathogens. To the best of our knowledge is this the first study that combines air filtering with shotgun metagenomic sequencing for detection and characterization of Campylobacter.ResultsAnalysis of air filters spiked with different levels of Campylobacter, into a background of mock or poultry house communities, indicated that we could detect as little as 200 colony forming units (CFU) Campylobacter per sample using our protocols. The results indicate that even with limited sequencing effort we could detect Campylobacter in the samples analysed in this study. We observed significant amounts of Campylobacter in real-life samples from poultry houses using both real-time PCR as well as shotgun metagenomics, suggesting that the flocks in both houses were infected with Campylobacter spp. Interestingly, in both houses we find diverse microbial communities present in the indoor air which reflect the fecal microbiome of poultry. Some of the identified genera such as Staphylococcus, Escherichia and Pseudomonas are known to contain opportunistic pathogenic species.ConclusionsThese results show that air sampling of poultry houses in combination with shotgun metagenomics can detect and identify Campylobacter spp. present at low levels. This is important since early detection of Campylobacter enables measures to be put in place to ensure the safety of broiler products, animal health and public health. This approach has the potential to detect any pathogen present in poultry house air. Background

Infections caused by Campylobacter spp. are among the most frequently reported gastrointestinal bacterial infections in Europe and worldwide [1, 2]. Campylobacteriosis present symptoms ranging from mild gastroenteritis to severe diarrhea. Complications can lead to a variety of diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease, reactive arthritis and Guillain-Barré syndrome [1]. For the genus Campylobacter more than 30 species and subspecies have been described and for many of these species the role as a human pathogenic bacteria is unclear [3]. Thermotolerant Campylobacter species, such as C. jejuni and C. coli, are most commonly associated with human infection and are often isolated from poultry and poultry products [4, 5]. Campylobacter has been isolated from the environment and a range of wild and domesticated animals, but poultry, especially broilers and laying hens, is considered the main reservoir [6, 7]. In the EU, monitoring of Campylobacter is mandatory [8], and from 2018 mandatory monitoring of Campylobacter on broiler carcasses has been implemented in the member states [9, 10]. In addition, to ensure a whole chain approach as recommended by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), control measures should also be implemented at farm level [11]. At present, on-farm sampling of poultry is carried out by sampling fecal droppings or using boot swabs, which are also widely used for Salmonella monitoring [12]. After Campylobacter colonize a flock, it is not only detectable in fecal droppings and the litter, but also in the air inside the house [13, 14]. This knowledge was recently used to show that air sampling can be used as an alternative strategy for screening of Campylobacter in broiler flocks [15, 16]. Air sampling was demonstrated to detect the presence of Campylobacter and in some cases even earlier than the current conventional methods [13–15].

In many air filter experiments, the collected airborne material is used for conventional culture techniques or specific PCR-based methods such as denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis (DGGE) or real-time PCR [13, 15, 17, 18]. The efficiency of conventional culture techniques is however suboptimal for specific organisms. A study by Johannessen et al. [15], indicated that cultivation of air filters often failed to detect Campylobacter spp. as compared to boot swabs. A follow-up study by Hoorfar et al. [16] supported this observation. This is in contrast to a higher Campylobacter detection rate when real-time PCR is used directly on the air filters compared to the same assay used material collected from boot swabs [15, 16]. The reason for the latter difference might be that the material on air filter has less biomass and therefore contains less inhibitors affecting PCR reactions.

Air filtering can collect all the microorganisms present in the enclosed air of buildings, which makes it suitable for diversity studies applying either amplicon or shotgun sequencing. Several studies describing poultry farm air samples indicate presence of a diverse community present in the poultry house air. By analyzing particle size fractions between 0.4 and 20 µm at broiler and layer farms Bai et al., showed that microbial species can show different abundances in different size fractions between these farm types [19]. This shows that air particle size composition is a factor that could influence community composition. In another study, Dai et al., showed that a range of potentially pathogenic bacterial genera, such as Corynebacterium, Escherichia and Staphylococcus are present in Chinese poultry house air [20]. Most of these organisms are associated with particles that are present in the air and have a particle size diameter smaller than 2.5 µm [20]. Other studies using 16S rRNA DGGE profiling [17], shotgun sequencing [21], or conventional culture techniques [18] from material collected on air filters showed similar organisms being present in Canadian or Polish poultry houses. All these studies indicate an airborne community in the poultry house dominated by Firmicute and Actinobacterial species [21]. In addition, it is also shown that airborne communities have a higher abundance of antibiotic resistance genes than found in the fecal material communities from which the farm air dust is originating [21]. As such, the presence of potential pathogens and antimicrobial resistant bacteria in the air of poultry houses might be a possible health risk for both animals and workers although there is currently insufficient data to support this.

The chicken fecal material deposited on the farm floor is partly responsible, via aerosol formation, for the bacterial community present in the poultry farm air. Therefore, the chicken fecal microbiome has an influence on the air microbiome. The chicken fecal microbiome consist of a variety of bacteria belonging to the phyla Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes and Proteobacteria [22–25]. At a finer taxonomic scale, the dominant taxa are Lactobacillaceae, Ruminicoccaceae and Enterobacteriaceae in broiler fecal samples [25–27].

Pathogenic bacterial species in the genera Campylobacter, Listeria, and Salmonella, seem only present at low abundances or are absent in microbiomes from fecal material of healthy broiler flocks [25]. These taxa can be present on shells of eggs from the same animals that had a negative fecal sample [25]. Although fecal fluid can contaminate the eggshell, it is also possible that other sources, such as animal feathers, parental care, or even the environment could be responsible for the presence of pathogenic microbes [28]. As such, sampling of fecal material might not give an adequate view of the presence of pathogens.

Here we compared real-time PCR detection with shotgun metagenomics to detect and characterize Campylobacter via air sampling. We used two different approaches for our test: 1) We analyzed air filters spiked with mock communities and different concentrations of C. jejuni to determine the detection limit of the method used; 2) We studied the microbial communities of air filter samples from two Norwegian poultry houses as real test for the combined usage of air filters and shotgun metagenomics.

Results

Four gelatin air filters were split into halves and spiked with either 75 or 125 µl of a mock community consisting of cells from 10 different species in a log abundance distribution (Fig. 1, Table 1). Two of the half filters spiked with mock community were further spiked with 200 CFU (colony forming units) / half filter of C. jejuni CCUG 11284T and another two with 20,000 CFU / half filter, while the remaining four halves were not spiked and used as controls. The samples spiked with the ZymoBiomics log distributed standard communities are called “MOCK” in the rest of the text to distinguish them from general discussion of mock communities. One air sample was collected from each of two poultry houses on the same farm (samples called “HOUSE”). Both filters were split in four pieces. Three pieces from each filter were spiked with three different concentrations of C. jejuni 927 (Fig. 1, Table 1), while the last piece from each filter was not spiked. All filter pieces were treated as separate samples.

Real-time PCR was used to detect Campylobacter spp. in all samples. Campylobacter was detected at approximately Cq 29 in the samples spiked with 20,000 CFU and at approximately Cq 36 in the samples spiked with 200 CFU (Table 1). The MOCK samples that were not spiked with Campylobacter were all negative (two “No Cq” and two above our standard hard cutoff Cq value of 40, Table 1). Campylobacter was detected in the HOUSE samples including the non-spiked samples, demonstrating that both poultry houses harboured Campylobacter spp.

Microbial community composition

Microbial community composition of the air filter communities was determined using metagenomic shotgun sequencing (Table 2). In order to assess which fraction of the microbial communities was captured using shotgun sequencing we used Nonpareil3 to calculate the metagenome coverage [29]. The coverage was defined as: the fraction of the total DNA extracted from a sample that is represented in the corresponding metagenomic sequence data [29]. Our results indicated that for the MOCK samples we had reached sufficient coverage (> 0.97), while the HOUSE samples with a chicken house background had an average coverage of 0.76 (Supplementary materials figure S1). To obtain a metagenome coverage for the HOUSE samples of 0.95, 10 times more reads would have been required, which was out of the scope of this pilot study. This result was expected as the mock communities consisted of 10 species, while the HOUSE communities were natural samples with an unknown number of species present. Thus, in line with this, we find that the HOUSE samples showed a higher Nonpareil index of sequence diversity (Nd) ( 19.2 ± 0.1) than the MOCK samples ( 15.9 ± 0.2) [29]. This index describes the community diversity in sequence space using a natural logarithm and is highly correlated with the Shannon diversity index [29]. The Nd found for our MOCK samples falls in the same range as mock community samples tested by the authors of Nonpareil3. Most host-associated communities tested in Rodriquez-R et al., show a Nd range between 17 and 22, and our HOUSE samples show similar Nd values [29].

The taxonomic community composition of the air filter communities was analyzed using Kraken 2 and Bracken [30, 31]. For the MOCK samples, we found that on average 10.5% of the reads were unclassified, while for the HOUSE samples this was 41.8%. When combining both sample types, we detected 20 taxa at the phylum level with a relative abundance > = 0.01% (Supplementary materials figure S2). As expected, Firmicutes and Proteobacteria dominated the MOCK samples. For the HOUSE samples we found that the phyla Actinobacteria and Firmicutes dominated, followed by the Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria. The other sixteen phyla were all present in the HOUSE samples at relative abundances below 0.1%, except the eukaryotic taxon Streptophyta (average relative abundance: 1.6% ± 0.027) and the viral taxon Uroviricota ( 1.4% ± 0.071). A close inspection of the species classification for Streptophyta indicated reads were assigned to a variety of plants such as wheat (Triticum aestivum), barley (Hordeum vulgare) and maize (Zea mays), which are all used as feed ingredients for poultry. Reads assigned to the Uroviricota matches a wide variety bacteriophage genera related to Staphylococcus infecting phages.

The proportion of Listeria and Pseudomonas in the MOCK communities were given to be 89.9% and 8.9%, respectively (ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standard II). However, the average measured relative abundance were 38.0% (± 13.4) and 42.1% (± 14.5) for the two taxa (Fig. 2). In this pilot experiment, we were primarily interested in detecting Campylobacter. As such, we choose a DNA extraction protocol optimized for Gram-negative bacteria. As expected Gram-positive bacteria were underrepresented.

In the HOUSE samples we found a group of different genera (relative abundance > 0.5%) and some of these are known to contain pathogenic species (Fig. 2). The dominant taxa (average relative abundance > 3%) were Brevibacterium, Corynebacterium, Staphylococcus, Brachybacterium, and Mycobacterium. The samples in each house showed a high reproducibility with similar abundances for most genera except for Klebsiella and Mycobacterium, where some samples had a clearly higher abundance in HOUSE 1. For HOUSE 2 samples, we found several other taxa with higher abundances compared to the HOUSE 1 samples (Fig. 2), including Alistipes, Phocaeicola and Megamonas. The identification of the genus Triticum (Wheat) in both houses indicates the presence of feed particles in the house air.

Campylobacter detection in communities spiked with C. jejuni

To detect and identify Campylobacter we spiked MOCK and HOUSE samples with C. jejuni CCUG 11284T and C. jejuni 927 respectively. These two isolates were sequenced with Illumina HiSeq 3000 and the resulting assemblies were submitted to ENA (WGS project numbers: CAJPVE01; CAJQFQ01) and have a genome size of 1.59 Mbp and 1.73 Mbp respectively (Supplementary Table 1).

We used both Kraken 2 taxonomic classification with the Kraken PlusPFP database as well as mapping with BBsplit with a collection of finished Campylobacter genomes, in addition to the isolate genomes to identify Campylobacter spiked into MOCK and HOUSE samples (Fig. 3) [30, 32]. Both methods identified reads matching to C. jejuni. We used the Tetra correlation search at the JspeciesWS webserver [33] to identify the taxonomically closest known isolates for our strains. These isolates are C. jejuni subsp. jejuni 327 for 927 (Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI): 100.00) and C. jejuni subsp. jejuni ATCC33560 for CCUG 11284T (ANI: 99.66). The match for CCUG 11284T indicates the same strain but with a different culture collection identifier. The ANI value between 927 and CCUG 11284T is 97.5%.

We used the two sequenced C. jejuni genomes in our BBsplit mapping approach together with many complete reference genomes in order to determine how many reads match the two spiked genomes (Fig. 3B, Supplemental figure S3). Strain CCUG 11284T was spiked into the MOCK samples, while C. jejuni 927 was spiked into the HOUSE samples. Our mapping results capture clearly which strain was spiked in which sample, although reads matching the genome of the other strain were also identified. Interestingly, in the MOCK samples we found, at low levels, reads uniquely matching C. jejuni 927, although this was not spiked into the samples (Fig. 3B). Likewise, reads with a unique match to CCUG 11284T were found in the HOUSE samples that were spiked with C. jejuni 927. In addition, in the HOUSE samples we found more reads in the metagenomes that map to other Campylobacter spp. genomes as well, which could be expected as this is a real sample (Supplemental figure S3).

The taxonomic classification with Kraken 2 did detect Campylobacter spp. in all sequenced samples (Fig. 3A). In the unspiked MOCK samples, we consistently detected C. hominis using Kraken 2 with an average classified read count of 62.8. After Bracken, we detected 224 reads classified to C. hominis, which is a human commensal, in sample 7-MOCK2-B-a.

The approach where we mapped the metagenomic reads against a collection of Campylobacter genomes and the two genomes of our isolates used for spiking (Fig. 3B, Supplemental figure S3) followed a similar patterns as the Kraken 2 classifications. This indicates a consistent detection irrespective of the method used for detection. For the MOCK samples spiked with 200 or 20,000 CFUs of C. jejuni CCUG 11284T we could classify on average 153 or 7,615 reads respectively to Campylobacter spp. Interestingly, in sample 3-MOCK1-S1-a, we detect 154 reads assigned to C. canadensis instead of C. jejuni after the Bracken classification correction. These results indicate that species detected using Kraken 2 and Bracken with abundances close to the used minimum read abundance (here 50 reads) should be carefully evaluated and can still show misclassifications.

Discussion

Campylobacter detection using metagenomics

The prevention of gastrointestinal disease due to foodborne pathogens requires early and sensitive detection of pathogens along the food production chain. For pathogens such as Campylobacter spp., it is important to identify the presence at the farm to prevent (or limit) further contamination. The current method for on-farm sampling of poultry is by boot swabs of the floor environment, including fecal droppings, analyzed by either conventional culture techniques or real-time PCR. These methods may have limitations in their application due to cultivation bias or PCR limitation to one target per assay. In recent years, shotgun metagenomics has been shown to be able to detect a large variety of microbes without conventional culture techniques and as such, it can be used to identify multiple pathogens simultaneously. Furthermore, air sampling at poultry farms combined with PCR detection indicated earlier detection of pathogens than conventional sampling [15, 16]. In addition, DNA extracted from the air samples apparently contained less PCR inhibitors compared to DNA extracted from boot swabs or fecal samples [15, 16]. Thus, combining metagenomics with air sampling could help to identify a variety of pathogens without being affected by cultivation biases.

Here, we used real-time PCR and metagenomic shotgun sequencing to analyze microbial communities present in MOCK and HOUSE samples with a specific focus on the detection of Campylobacter.

In both HOUSE samples we obtained positive PCR results in the non-spiked filters (Table 1), demonstrating that both poultry houses were infected with Campylobacter spp. These results were also confirmed by conventional culture techniques of boot swabs sampled simultaneously with the air samples from the two houses [15]. At the time of spiking of the air filters, we did not have these results and were not aware of the presence of Campylobacter. However, we still decided to include these HOUSE samples as they harboured a realistic background of Campylobacter and composition of microbiota. The prevalence of Campylobacter in broiler flocks in Norway is low (5 – 8%) compared to other European countries and there is generally not a lot of houses/farms that are continuously positive [34]. As such, we did not expect that that the houses on the farm we randomly selected were positive.

The MOCK samples were spiked with Campylobacter in an aimed ratio of 100 between the two levels. The sequencing data showed a ratio of 180 and the PCR results a ratio of 130 (Cq difference 7.05) between the two levels. The experiment was not intended as a quantitative study, but both sequencing and PCR data clearly reflect the spiked quantities. As both poultry houses sampled were infected with Campylobacter, we were not able to calculate ratios for those.

With the shotgun data we used two different approaches to identify Campylobacter spp. in our samples (Fig. 3). First we used classification of reads with Kraken 2, which showed that the HOUSE samples had between 100 and 300 reads / million reads that could be classified to Campylobacter spp. The second approach was to map the sequences with BBsplit to a variety of Campylobacter spp. genomes, and it only counts reads mapping unambiguously to a single genome. When a read would map equally well to multiple genomes it was not counted. In principle, the mapping method can be used to identify specific strains of Campylobacter, which further can be used in an outbreak situation where the outbreak strain is known. However, in this study we have focused on identifying the different species. Here we find in the HOUSE samples that BBsplit mapped between 16 and 147 reads / million reads to both of the spike genomes of C. jejuni CCUG 11284T and 927, which is a slightly smaller fraction than when using Kraken 2. The latter was spiked into these samples, while C. jejuni CCUG 11284T was used in the MOCK samples as a spike. This suggests the presence of C. jejuni strains sharing genomic similarity with CCUG 11284T in the HOUSE samples. An additional 0.03% of reads mapped to a variety of other Campylobacter spp. including a third C. jejuni (NCTC 11168) reference genome (Supplementary materials Figure S3). In contrast, we only find few reads (< 1 read / million reads) in the MOCK samples that were assigned to C. jejuni 927, while 3 (200 CFU) to 330 (20,000 CFU) reads / million reads were assigned to C. jejuni CCUG 11284T in the spiked samples.

Our results suggest that both Kraken 2 and BBsplit can be used to identify Campylobacter spp. in the air of houses as can real-time-PCR [15]. The added value of using shotgun metagenomics for detection is the possibility to identify other bacterial species of interest, including the variety of Campylobacter species present at a farm, which is not possible with single 16S rRNA based real-time PCR, nor 16S amplicon sequencing. However, our metagenomic results also indicate that interpretation of such data should be carried out with caution. For instance, the BBsplit results suggests the presence of a large number of different species (supplementary materials Figure S3), while Kraken 2 predominantly identified C. jejuni (Fig. 3). Some of the species genomes used in the BBsplit mapping approach have very distinct host ranges. For instance, the species C. fetus, C. hyointestinalis and C. iguaniorum are associated with other hosts [35–37] and it seems unlikely that they should be present in our samples. Others like C. jejuni are known to be genomically versatile with broad ecological/ host ranges, including wild birds, livestock and humans [38, 39]. In addition, Campylobacter spp. are known for their high rate of horizontal gene transfer making it likely that environmental isolates from different species might contain genomic regions shared by multiple species [40, 41]. Finally, the small number of reads assigned to different Campylobacter species compared to the typical genome size found in this genus (e.g. ≈ 2.0 Mbp), makes it unlikely that we can actually have so many species in the samples as found with BBsplit. All these factors make it difficult to identify these different species unambiguously and caution is needed for the interpretation. Nonetheless, by using two different approaches our results suggest that Campylobacter can be detected in the air of our HOUSE samples albeit at low abundance.

Air sample microbiomes vs broiler fecal microbiomes

The microbiome composition of the air in poultry houses is closely related, but not identical, to the fecal microbiome composition. After deposition of the feces on the litter layer in the house the microbiome composition likely changes in community composition and only a part of the community becomes airborne. In our analysis, we find a highly diverse community present in the air, with several genera also known to be present in the broiler feces. Typical genera found in broiler feces are Bacteriodes, Brevibacterium, Corynebacterium, Lactobacillus and Staphylococcus [24–26, 42, 43]. These taxa were also identified in our air sample microbiomes of the poultry houses as well as in other studies using different detection methods [17, 18, 20]. This suggests that the air microbiome in poultry houses can be used as a proxy for the fecal microbiome. It also implies that the poultry house air contains many of the microbes associated with the birds and as such gives a good overview of the microbiome of the entire flock. Interestingly, many of the genera identified in our analysis are known gut microbiome species, but not all of them. Brachybacterium spp. for instance has been isolated from a wide range of sources including poultry litter, Gouda cheese, oil brine or via air sampling [44, 45]. Not unexpectedly, Brachybacterium spp. have also been identified in the dust from poultry farms [20]. They have been rarely identified as pathogens for humans [46]. The same is true for the genera Dietzia and Pseudomonas, which are both widely distributed bacteria that can be opportunistic pathogens [47]. Pseudomonas spp. can regularly be found in chicken fecal material as well as the air of poultry houses and are of concern for food production [18, 25, 48, 49]. These results therefore indicate that shotgun metagenomics of air samples provide a suitable approach to monitor poultry farms for a wide variety of pathogens.

Sensitivity of air sample metagenomics

The use of air sampling combined with real-time PCR or sequencing to detect pathogens at poultry farms has clear benefits over the gold standard with boot swabs with respect to the higher sensitivity and faster throughput [15, 16]. An additional advantage is the potential to use the same samples to study other microbial species present and characterize the microbiome with metagenomic approaches. However, there are some pitfalls for these approaches. The first factor is the low input amount of DNA collected by this technique. Such samples are highly sensitive to contaminants from kits and reagents themselves, in addition to laboratory contamination [50, 51].

Secondly, the DNA extraction method can introduce bias. In our study, we selected a method aimed at Gram-negative bacteria and specifically at lysing the cells from Campylobacter spp. To analyze the DNA extraction bias in our experiment we used mock communities. As expected, we did find a difference between the expected and observed mock community composition of the samples. This was especially clear for the abundances of L. monocytogenes and P. aeruginosa, which had a predicted relative abundance of 89 vs 8.9%, but instead showed an almost equal abundance in this experiment (Fig. 2, supplementary figure S4). This was most likely due to the Gram-positive L. monocytogenes DNA being extracted with lower efficiency than the Gram-negative P. aeruginosa. Nonetheless, in the HOUSE samples, we do find many Gram-positive bacterial genera, but the relative abundance of these taxa is likely underestimated relative to Gram-negative bacteria. This underscores the importance of including mock communities in microbiome studies, in order to understand the DNA extraction bias for particular communities. If the aim of the analysis had been e.g., L. monocytogenes or the entire microbiome, it would be appropriate to select a DNA extraction method aimed at such organisms.

As with any sensitive molecular method there is the risk of sample contamination leading to false positive results. For instance, we find contaminating genera such as Aeribacillus, Limosilactobacillus and Mycobacterium in the airfilter samples spiked with the MOCK community (Fig. 2). The contamination could come from the gelatin air filters, the kitome [50], cross contamination in the lab or from cross-talk between samples during sequencing. The production of gelatin is a harsh, but non-sterile process, which requires sterilization before shipment of such filters. Thus, such filters can contain DNA despite the fact that these filters are gamma sterilized [52]. Furthermore, the literature on gelatin production indicates that common contaminants belong to thermotolerant, aerobic, endospore-forming bacteria [52]. Many library preparation methods like the one used here, include pipetting steps of PCR products in open plates and negative controls in sequencing experiments are in contrast to PCR experiments normally not without false positive reads [53, 54].

We used air filters spiked with a mock community to identify if species were derived from the kitome, the gelatin matrix or laboratory contamination. The air filters spiked with mock community should only contain the mock community species. At the genus level, Bracken identified, besides the 10 MOCK community taxa, 882 genera with a minimum of 50 reads per taxon. Most of these genera have a low abundance with Mycobacterium, Aeribacillus, Meiothermus and Neobacillus making up around 2% of the total MOCK sample reads and the other 878 taxa another 2% on average. Among these 878 taxa we find genera that belong to various bacterial, eukaryotic and euryarchaeota phyla which were not part of the MOCK communities (Supplementary Materials figure S2). Many of the low abundant taxa are likely false positives, since we did not use the confidence set-off in Kraken 2. Another source for contamination might be cross-talk between the samples during sequencing. For instance, the genus Triticum (wheat) is highly abundant in the HOUSE samples (1% of the reads), and is found in all the MOCK samples with 0.01% of the reads.

What is clear from our analysis, is that contaminating species in air filter experiments are present and easily detected when using mock communities. This underlines the need to include mock communities and extraction controls with blank air filters in all microbiome experiments using air sampling, to be able to filter out contamination from a variety of sources.

Application of air sampling

The results from our study indicate that sampling indoor air of poultry houses together with shotgun metagenomic sequencing can be used to detect and monitor pathogenic microorganisms, such as Campylobacter spp. This approach has the potential to study a variety of pathogens, moulds, antimicrobial resistance genes or other genes of interest in environments where a high hygienic standard is required [21, 55]. These findings indicate that pathogens can be found in the air of animal production facilities, which might cause health concerns for the humans working in such spaces [18, 56]. Likewise, the animals in these facilities are also exposed to the same potential pathogens that are present in the air [20, 57]. With the increasing availability of shotgun metagenomics, it becomes possible to monitor animal house facilities for a wide variety of pathogens without the limitations in detection when using conventional culture techniques or PCR approaches. This enables the industry not only to monitor for pathogens that can cause foodborne outbreaks at an early stage, but it could also stimulate the development of intervention methods to reduce the proliferation of such pathogens at the farm level. In addition, these approaches can help to improve the animal health by developing measures to reduce the exposure to a wide variety of potential pathogens in the long term. Such measures could include better regulation of the indoor climate of poultry houses. The extra benefit of such developments is that the working conditions for humans at those farms might improve as well.

Nonetheless, to exploit fully the power of metagenomics in an industrial monitoring set-up, a further reduction of the sequencing cost, the development of user friendly analysis pipelines as well as shorter time from sampling to finalized analysis and interpretation are needed. Finding potential pathogens in the air of poultry farms might cause concern. While some of the organisms identified are also part of the healthy fecal microbiome of chicken, it is a challenge to identify at what level pathogen detection might require intervention. This pilot study laid the basis for a follow-up study using the same methodology, where we compare the airborne communities in poultry farms from different European countries. We expect that the follow-up study can provide some answers to some of the above questions on pathogen presence and sensitivity.

Conclusions

In this pilot study, we have used shotgun metagenomics to detect Campylobacter spp. in air samples from poultry houses. Our results indicate the presence of a diverse community that contains many genera that are also found in the chicken fecal microbiome. This shows that air sampling of poultry house air combined with shotgun metagenomics can be used to investigate the presence of fecal microbiome members in a poultry flock. Shotgun metagenomics allowed us to identify Campylobacter spp. present at low levels, and the results could be used to identify other pathogenic microbes of health concern. By using mock communities, we address some technical limitations that can aid data analysis and interpretation of data generated with the air sampling method. These include DNA extraction biases due to the extraction method chosen, as well as the background contamination of the samples due to laboratory handling and/or background DNA present in the gelatin matrix of the filters.

By combining air sampling with shotgun metagenomics it will be possible to study the microbial communities associated with animal production and food industry with emphasis on the presence of microbes considered as foodborne pathogens. Understanding the dynamics of microbial communities present in poultry houses will help to identify processes to reduce to the introduction of pathogens in the poultry production as well to improve poultry and human health.

Methods

Sample collection

Air filters from two Norwegian broiler houses on the same farm were collected using an AirPort MD8 device (Sartorious Stedim Biotech, France) with disposable gelatin filters (80 mm diameter; Sartorius, 17,528-80ACD) as described by Johannessen and coworkers [15], one from each house. The filters collected were used for spiking with a Campylobacter isolate as described below.

Preparation of artificially contaminated air filters

One air filter collected from each of two broiler houses (see above) were divided into quarters and spiked with three different concentrations of C. jejuni 927 (poultry) (WGS project number CAJPVE01) (5600, 560 and 56 cfu/quarter filter, respectively) (Table 1). One quarter of each filter was not inoculated with C. jejuni 927 and will reflect the content of the real sample (Fig. 1). The spiking of the filters was performed directly in an Eppendorf tube to ensure all bacteria were included in the further step of the DNA extraction.

Four gelatin membrane filters were divided into two pieces each (Table 1, Fig. 1). Six half filters were spiked with 75 µl of microbial community standard (MOCK), and two half filters were spiked with 125 µl of the same standard. The microbial community standard (MOCK) used in this study has a log abundance distribution of different bacterial species and fungi ranging from 108 to 102 cells (ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standard II, ZYMO RESEARCH EUROPE GMBH, Freiburg, Germany) and is well suited for assessing the accuracy of composition and ideal for the quality control of microbiome measurements. In addition, we spiked four of the half filters already spiked with mock communities, with two different concentrations of C. jejuni subsp. jejuni CCUG 11284T (bovine) (WGS project number CAJQFQ01) (200 and 20,000 cfu /half filter, respectively) (Table 1).

The bacterial inocula were prepared from frozen glycerol stocks. A loopful of the frozen stock was streaked onto blood agar (Oxoid Blood agar base no.2, with bovine blood) and incubated in a microaerophilic atmosphere (Genbox microaerofil, bioMérieux, Marcy-I’Etoile, France) at 41.5°C for approx. 48 h. After incubation, a single colony was transferred to 10 ml Brain Heart Infusion broth (Difco) and incubated as described above except it was incubated for 24 h. The numbers of Campylobacter in the broths were estimated by making tenfold dilution series in Buffered Peptone Water (OXOID) and plating 100 µl of the appropriate dilutions in triplicate on blood agar. An aliquot of 250 µl from the appropriate dilution was used for spiking to obtain the desired number of cells.

The DNA extraction was performed as previously described in Hoorfar et al., 2020 [16]. Briefly, air filters were pretreated for DNA extraction as follows; half a filter was dissolved in 3.5 ml ddH2O and 100 µl alkaline protease (Protex 6L, Genencor International BA, The Netherlands) was added. The samples were mixed by vortexing until the filters were dissolved. The samples were then incubated at 37°C in a thermal mixer for 6 min at 1000 rpm, centrifuged at 4°C for 5 min at 8000 × g and the supernatant discarded. The pellet was used for DNA extraction using the Gram-negative protocol for DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen) with some minor modifications described briefly below. The incubation period at 56°C was set to one hour for all samples and all samples were treated with 4 µl RNase A (100 mg/ml, Qiagen). DNA was eluted in 100 µl TE buffer with 0.1 mM EDTA. The DNA quantity was determined by using the high sensitivity DNA Qubit assay (ThermoFisher Scientific) and DNA quality was assessed, if enough DNA, by using Nanodrop spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific).

DNA from the two C. jejuni strains used for spiking were extracted using QIAamp DNA Mini kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quantity and quality was determined by using the broad range DNA Qubit assay and Nanodrop spectrophotometer. The DNA from the two C. jejuni isolates was whole genome sequenced using the same protocols as for the metagenomic samples, for details see below under metagenomic library preparation and sequencing.

Real-time PCR for Campylobacter

Real-time PCR was performed with forward primer 5’-CTGCTTAACACAAGTTGAGTAGG-3’ and reverse primer TTCCTTAGGTACCGTCAGAA as combined and optimized by Lübeck et al. (2003) with LNA probe 5’-6FAM CA[T]CC[T]CCACGCGGCG[T]TGC BHQ1-3’ [58–61]. Shortly, 500 nM primers and 300 nM probe were added to Brilliant III Ultra-Fast QPCR mastermix (Agilent) for analysis of HOUSE samples or Tth DNA polymerase mastermix (containing: 1 × PCR buffer for Tth DNA polymerase, 25 mM MgCl2, 7% glycerol, 0.6 mM dNTPs, 0,2 mg BSA and 1 U Tth polymerase) for the MOCK samples. For the Brilliant mastermix was the cycling conditions were 95 °C for 3 min followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 15 s. For the Tth DNA polymerase mastermix was the cycling conditions were 95 °C for 3 min followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 60 s and 72 °C for 30 s. In both cases, a CFX96 instrument (Bio-Rad) was used. C. jejuni was used as positive template control (PTC) and molecular grade water was used as no template control (NTC). One extraction blank was included in each round of DNA extraction. The samples were only ran once in the real-time PCR assays.

Metagenomic library preparation & metagenomics shotgun sequencing

Metagenomic shotgun libraries were prepared at the Norwegian Sequencing Centre (Oslo, Norway) from the air filter DNA extracts and the two C. jejuni isolates. DNA was mechanically fragmented using sonication to 350 bp (Covaris) and Dual indexed libraries were generated using SMARTer ThruPLEX (Takara) adapted to low-input DNA. PhiX DNA was spiked into the libraries to improve the separation of the sequencing clusters on the Illumina flow cells. The libraries were sequenced on a HiSeq 3000 (Illumina) generating 150 bp Paired end reads.

Genome assembly of Campylobacter genomes.

The Campylobacter sequence data was processed with the Bifrost pipeline [62] ("https://github.com/NorwegianVeterinaryInstitute/Bifrost") with modifications. In brief, PhiX sequences were removed using BBmap (v 38.76) [32], adapter sequences and low quality bases were removed using Trimmomatic (v 0.39) [63]. Assembly was performed with Skesa (v 2.3.0) [64], using default settings. Due to the very high coverage (> 2000 X, we screened the contigs for contamination using Kraken 2 (v 2.0.8_beta)[30] and a Minikraken database containing Refseq microbial genomes. We identified a contaminant Staphylococcus epidermis genome (accession number: CAJPVF01) in the C. jejuni 927 assembly that was present as ≈ 1% of the reads. All non-Campylobacter contigs were removed from the C. jejuni 927 assembly. Next, we use Pilon (v 1.23) [65] to correct the assemblies of both isolates and Prokka (v 1.14.5) [66] was used to annotate both genomes.

Metagenomic data quality control and classification

Metagenome sequence data quality control was performed using the Sunbeam pipeline (v 2.1.0) [67] (Supplementary materials figure S5). In brief, the pipeline runs Cutadapt (v 2.8) and Trimmomatic (v 0.36) to remove adapters and low quality bases. Next, low-complexity regions are masked using Komplexity (v 0.3.6) and subsequently host contamination filtering was performed to remove read matching both the PhiX (NC_001422.1) and a masked version of the human genome (Hg19, https://drive.google.com/file/d/0B3llHR93L14wd0pSSnFULUlhcUk/edit?usp=sharing). The Gallus gallus reference genome (GCA_016699485.1_bGalGal1.mat.broiler.GRCg7b) was downloaded from NCBI and masked with BBmask (BBmap version 38.86) with entropy set to 0.7. This genome was used to remove reads with BBmap (minid = 0.95, maxindel = 3, bwr = 0.16, bw = 12, minhits = 2) present in the metagenomics samples which match the chicken reference genome. The sequence depth for the individual cleaned samples was estimated using Nonpareil (version3) [29]. The Average Genome Size of the samples was calculated with MicrobeCensus [68]. Taxonomic classification was performed using Kraken 2 using the PlusPFP database downloaded on 22–02-2022 (v 2.1.2)[30]. The Kraken 2 PlusPFP database contains Archaea, Bacteria, viral, plasmid, human, UniVec_Core, UniVec, fungi, plant and protozoa. Species abundances were corrected using Bracken (v2.6.1) with a kmer length of 150 and a threshold of 50 [31]. The threshold was set at 50, due to the presence of less than 50 reads in the Kraken 2 classification results of Campylobacter spp. reads in MOCK samples not spiked with Campylobacter. We assume that any reads classified as Campylobacter in non-spiked samples are likely misclassifications.

Mapping of metagenome sequences

Species abundances based on reads were also determined in the following way: a collection of fasta sequences was downloaded that included the mock community species genomes (https://s3.amazonaws.com/zymo-files/BioPool/ZymoBIOMICS.STD.refseq.v2.zip), 29 Complete Campylobacter spp. reference genomes from Refseq (downloaded 25–11-2020, Supplementary table S2), and the Gallus gallus reference genome version 6 (GCA_000002315.5). We also added the genome of C. coli NTICC13 (GCF_009756375.1) as well as the genomes from C. jejuni subsp. jejuni CCUG 11284T and C. jejuni 927. All genomes were masked using BBmask (BBTools version 38.86), with entropy set to 0.7 [32]. Metagenomic reads were mapped against the masked genomes using BBsplit with default settings and discarding reads with ambigious mapping within a genome or between genomes. A summary of the mapped reads per genome was used for visualization.

Visualization of the results

The figures were created using R-studio (version 1.3.959) with R (version 4.0.0). The following R-packages were used to process data tables and generate figures: dplyr (v1.0.2), forcats (v 0.5.0), ggplot2 (v3.3.2), purr (v 0.3.4), readr (v1.4.0), tidyr (v1.1.2).
