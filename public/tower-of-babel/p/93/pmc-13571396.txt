Pleiotropic Phenotype of RYR2 Exon 3 Deletion
Authors: Loïc Faucher, Thomas Cardi, Simon Fitouchi, Clément Baldacini, Baudouin Koenig, Alexandre Schatz, Mohamad Kanso, Olivier Morel, Halim Marzak, Laurence Jesel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPathogenic RYR2 variants cause a spectrum of inherited arrhythmia syndromes. The exon 3 deletion (E3DS) is a rare ryanodinopathy that combines arrhythmias and structural cardiomyopathy.Case SummaryA 33-year-old woman was incidentally found to have premature ventricular contractions during pregnancy. Further evaluation revealed biventricular noncompaction, sinus node dysfunction, and nonsustained polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. In the postpartum, she developed persistent bradyarrhythmia. Electrophysiological study demonstrated complete supranodal atrioventricular block and extensive right atrial low-voltage areas. Cardiac magnetic resonance confirmed biventricular noncompaction. A dual-chamber implantable cardioverter-defibrillator was inserted; 3 months later, she experienced ventricular fibrillation terminated by appropriate implantable cardioverter-defibrillator shocks. Genetic testing identified a pathogenic RYR2 E3D (class 5) and a variant of uncertain significance in FLNC.DiscussionTo our knowledge, this is the first reported case to demonstrate the complete phenotypic spectrum associated with RYR2 E3D.Take-Home MessagesE3DS should be suspected in patients presenting with a combination of conduction disease, atrial pathology, and left ventricular noncompaction.Key Words: cardiac magnetic resonance, cardiomyopathy, echocardiography, electroanatomical mapping, electrocardiogram, electrophysiology, genetic disorders, supraventricular arrhythmias, treatment, ventricular fibrillation

History of Presentation

A 33-year-old woman, gravida 1 para 0, at 32 weeks of gestation, was referred after incidental detection of frequent premature ventricular contractions during routine obstetric ultrasound. She denied palpitations, chest pain, syncope, exercise intolerance, or heart failure symptoms, and pregnancy had been uneventful. Examination was normal, with blood pressure 110/60 mm Hg, heart rate 58 beats/min, and oxygen saturation 99% on room air. Fetal assessment was reassuring.

Past Medical History

She had no prior cardiovascular disease, chronic illness, known arrhythmia, toxic exposure, or family history of sudden cardiac death, cardiomyopathy, or arrhythmia.

Investigations

The resting 12-lead electrocardiogram showed an irregular rhythm with absent sinus P waves, junctional escape rhythm at 60 beats/min, narrow QRS complexes, anterior T-wave inversion, frequent ventricular bigeminy, and a corrected QT interval of 400 ms (Figure 1).

Resting 12-Lead Electrocardiogram

Junctional rhythm at 60 beats/min, absent sinus P waves and ventricular bigeminy.

A 24-hour Holter monitor recorded a mean heart rate of 47 beats/min, with a daytime average of 59 beats/min and nighttime minimum of 38 beats/min. More than 10,000 polymorphic premature ventricular contractions were recorded, accounting for 12% of total beats, with couplets, triplets, and several runs of nonsustained polymorphic ventricular tachycardia lasting up to 4 beats. Junctional escape predominated at night.

Transthoracic echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (62%) with prominent left ventricular trabeculations and a noncompacted/compacted ratio >2:1 in apical and mid-ventricular segments. The right ventricle was enlarged and markedly trabeculated, consistent with biventricular noncompaction. Both atria were dilated, right ventricular systolic function was preserved, and there was no significant valvular disease or pulmonary hypertension (Figure 2).

Echocardiography and Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Imaging

(A and B) Apical 4-chamber and 3-chamber views in transthoracic echocardiography showing prominent trabeculations and deep intertrabecular recesses. (C and D) Cardiac magnetic resonance (steady-state free precession sequences) short and long axis confirming marked left ventricular dilation with extensive apical and mid-ventricular trabeculation, as well as right ventricular involvement.

Given the high arrhythmic burden and nonsustained ventricular tachycardia during pregnancy, a LifeVest wearable cardioverter-defibrillator was prescribed until delivery. She remained asymptomatic and underwent elective cesarean delivery at 39 weeks of gestation. Postpartum evaluation confirmed persistent sinus node dysfunction with junctional rhythm at 44 beats/min. Repeat echocardiography showed preserved systolic function, persistent biventricular trabeculation, and significant biatrial enlargement, whereas the premature ventricular contraction burden appeared to have decreased.

Exercise testing showed no sinus rhythm recovery, with frequent premature ventricular contractions and multiple runs of polymorphic nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (Figure 3). Electrophysiological study 2 months after delivery (Figure 4) showed junctional rhythm at 50 beats/min with frequent premature ventricular contractions. Atrial pacing failed to conduct to the ventricles even at high output, confirming complete supranodal atrioventricular block. Right atrial voltage mapping demonstrated extensive low-voltage areas, consistent with advanced atrial myopathy and diffuse atrial disease.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging at 3 months postpartum confirmed biventricular noncompaction (Figure 2), with left ventricular dilation, indexed end-diastolic volume of 136 mL/m2, noncompacted/compacted ratio >2.3 in multiple segments, preserved systolic function, right ventricular trabeculation, biatrial dilation, and no late gadolinium enhancement. Genetic testing identified a pathogenic heterozygous RYR2 exon 3 deletion and a heterozygous FLNC p.Arg1982His variant of uncertain significance.

Management

Postpartum, despite preserved ventricular function, the combination of biventricular noncompaction, supranodal atrioventricular block, atrial myopathy, and high arrhythmic burden was considered high risk. Six months after delivery, a dual-chamber implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was implanted through the left subclavian vein with active fixation atrial and ventricular leads. Although extensive right atrial low-voltage areas had been documented, the diagnosis of permanent atrial standstill had not yet been definitively established, raising the possibility of subsequent electrical reverse remodeling. A dual-chamber system was therefore selected to preserve potential atrial sensing and pacing, atrioventricular synchrony if atrial capture was achievable, and rhythm discrimination.

Conduction system pacing, including left bundle branch area pacing, was considered. However, at implantation, systolic function was preserved, QRS complexes were narrow, and permanent ventricular pacing dependency was not yet established. Moreover, left bundle branch area pacing would have required an additional transvenous lead, increasing lead burden in this young patient. It remains a follow-up consideration in case of pacing-induced ventricular dysfunction or future device revision.

Atenolol 50 mg daily was poorly tolerated and switched to bisoprolol 2.5 mg daily, with dapagliflozin 10 mg daily. Postpartum counseling included avoidance of competitive sports, strenuous exercise, and high-adrenergic situations; low- to moderate-intensity physical activity was permitted under beta-blocker therapy and close follow-up.

Three months later, the patient experienced syncope while traveling. ICD interrogation revealed ventricular fibrillation appropriately treated by 2 shocks (Figure 5). Because of absent atrial capture and sensing related to advanced atrial myopathy, the device was reprogrammed to VVIR mode at 65 to 130 beats/min, with lower hysteresis rate of 60 beats/min, and bisoprolol was increased to 5 mg daily. Three months after ICD insertion, apixaban 5 mg twice daily was initiated for atrial standstill, permanent ventricular pacing, and atrial high-rate events during device follow-up.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At 12 months of follow-up, the patient remained stable and asymptomatic, with good functional capacity. ICD interrogation showed no recurrence of ventricular arrhythmias since the ventricular fibrillation episode. Echocardiography confirmed preserved ejection fraction (50% to 55%), persistent biventricular noncompaction, and biatrial dilation. She was entirely ICD-dependent. Genetic counseling was provided, and cascade screening of first-degree relatives was initiated. Her daughter exhibited a normal electrocardiogram; however, neonatal echocardiography revealed features consistent with left ventricular noncompaction, and she is currently under cardiogenetic evaluation.

Discussion

This case highlights the pleiotropic nature of RYR2 exon 3 deletion syndrome (E3DS), a rare ryanodinopathy that is phenotypically distinct from classical catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT). E3DS accounts for approximately 1% of pathogenic RYR2 variants and has been reported in fewer than 50 cases worldwide.1

An important differential diagnosis in this postpartum setting is pregnancy-related ventricular hypertrabeculation or trabeculation secondary to cardiomyopathic remodeling. Pregnancy-related loading conditions may induce or unmask ventricular hypertrabeculation overlapping with morphologic criteria for left ventricular noncompaction. In this case, true disease-associated biventricular noncompaction was supported by biventricular involvement, persistence after delivery, left ventricular dilation, biatrial enlargement with atrial myopathy, exercise-induced polymorphic ventricular arrhythmias followed by ventricular fibrillation, and a pathogenic RYR2 exon 3 deletion previously associated with noncompaction phenotypes.

In contrast to CPVT, which manifests as adrenergically triggered ventricular arrhythmias in structurally normal hearts, exon 3 deletion presents with a striking overlap of arrhythmic and structural features.2 Sinus node dysfunction has been reported in more than half of patients, atrioventricular block in about 20%, atrial arrhythmias in up to 50%, and ventricular arrhythmias in more than 50%. Structural abnormalities are frequent, with left ventricular noncompaction in nearly one-third and dilated cardiomyopathy in about 20%.1 Our patient demonstrated nearly the full clinical spectrum, combining sinus node dysfunction, complete supranodal atrioventricular block, atrial myopathy with standstill, biventricular noncompaction, and malignant ventricular arrhythmias including ventricular fibrillation.

Pathophysiologically, the exon 3 deletion results in the loss of 35 amino acids in the N-terminal domain of RyR2, leading to impaired channel gating.3,4 This abnormality reduces the threshold for store overload–induced calcium release and impairs the termination of calcium release, causing spontaneous calcium leak, delayed afterdepolarizations, and triggered activity.5,6 These mechanisms not only predispose to malignant arrhythmias but also promote structural remodeling, providing an explanation for the overlap of arrhythmia and cardiomyopathy observed in this syndrome.

Management of E3DS is complex. Although beta-blockers are the cornerstone of CPVT treatment, they can exacerbate bradyarrhythmias in this context, and must be used with caution.7,8 Our patient required ICD insertion despite preserved ventricular function, justified by the occurrence of life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias. This strategy aligns with the 2022 European Society of Cardiology guidelines on ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death, which recommend device therapy in inherited arrhythmia syndromes with high arrhythmic risk.9

The genetic findings are also of interest. The pathogenic RYR2 exon 3 deletion provides the most plausible unifying explanation for the patient's pleiotropic phenotype. The concomitant FLNC p.Arg1982His variant of uncertain significance was also considered when interpreting the structural findings. FLNC pathogenic variants have been associated with arrhythmogenic and dilated cardiomyopathies. Although the FLNC variant is currently classified as a variant of uncertain significance, oligogenic inheritance is increasingly recognized in inherited arrhythmia and cardiomyopathy syndromes.10 The coexistence of variants affecting complementary pathways involved in excitation-contraction coupling and myocardial structural integrity may contribute to phenotypic severity beyond that expected from either variant alone. Therefore, its potential modifier role in association with the RYR2 variant cannot be excluded.

Finally, this case underscores the importance of family screening. Exon 3 deletion exhibits high penetrance but variable expressivity, and relatives may be asymptomatic carriers at risk of arrhythmia or cardiomyopathy.11, 12, 13 Early identification of affected family members enables surveillance, preventive strategies, and personalized management. Educationally, this case illustrates the integration of multimodality imaging, invasive electrophysiology, and genetic testing into clinical decision-making, in accordance with contemporary guideline-directed practice.

According to our knowledge, this is the first reported case in the literature that fully encompasses the full phenotypic spectrum of E3DS, including sinus node dysfunction, complete atrioventricular block, atrial standstill, biventricular noncompaction, and malignant ventricular arrhythmias.

Conclusions

E3DS represents a unique overlap between channelopathy and cardiomyopathy. Recognition of this entity has crucial implications for diagnosis, ICD insertion, anticoagulation, and family screening.
