Intracameral pressure during capsulorhexis in cataract surgery using cohesive versus dispersive ophthalmic viscosurgical devices: a randomised trial
Authors: Daniel M Handzel, Walter Sekundo, Chiraz Ben Abdallah, Markus S Ladewig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo study the properties of different ophthalmic viscosurgical devices (OVDs) with respect to their ability to create and maintain high intraocular pressure (IOP) during the creation of a continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis (CCC) by comparing the drop in IOP before and after creation of the CCC.Methods and analysisThe study included 174 eyes in four groups using two different OVDs (hyaluronic acid (HA) and hydroxypropylmethylcellulose (HPMC)) and two different instruments (utrata forceps (UF) and 26G cystotome (RN)). IOP was measured using rebound tonometry with sterilised probes immediately before the beginning of the CCC and immediately afterwards during cataract surgery.ResultsThe drop in IOP during capsulorhexis was measured at 52.1±10.7 mm Hg with the combination HA/RN, 62.4±11.5 mm Hg with HA/UF, 60.5±10.0 mm Hg with HPMC/RN and 68.7±14.1 mm Hg with HPMC/UF. The variance in IOP reduction between HA and HPMC did not reach statistical significance (p=0.100).ConclusionThe lack of a noticeable difference in IOP levels, despite the use of different OVDs with unique properties, indicates that IOP may not be the main factor influencing the notable differences in tactile feedback perceived by surgeons during capsulorhexis procedures conducted with varying OVDs. To elucidate these sensations, additional characteristics such as viscosity, elasticity, pseudoplasticity and cohesion will have to be explored.Keywords: Anterior chamber, Intraocular pressure, Lens and zonules, Prospective Studies, Treatment Surgery

Introduction

The use of ophthalmic viscosurgical devices (OVDs) has markedly increased cataract surgery safety.1 2 Given that cataract procedures are among the most common surgeries,3 improvements in this area have global implications. An OVD is injected into the anterior chamber (AC) to create and maintain space between the lens and the corneal endothelium2 4 during surgery. Hyaluronic acid (HA) and hydroxypropylmethylcellulose (HPMC) are the principal OVDs developed.2 4 Rheological properties—viscosity, elasticity, pseudoplasticity and cohesion—affect OVD function and performance. Based on rheology, OVDs are generally classified as cohesive or dispersive, and products are not interchangeable due to unique characteristics.1 5 Prolonged endothelial adhesion enhances protection.2 4 6 A stable AC and higher pressure on the anterior lens capsule facilitate the formation of a curvilinear circular capsulorhexis (CCC),1 7 with high anterior-chamber pressure flattening the anterior capsule and reducing rhexis-tear risk.8 9 The notion that high-molecular-weight OVDs enable such effects is common in the literature, yet a significant link between capsule flattening and specific OVD features remains unproven.

HA is highly viscous and maintains anterior-chamber space, supporting critical steps like capsulorhexis and lens emulsification. However, cohesive properties may lead to faster drainage from the chamber and potentially less endothelial protection than HPMC.1 4 HPMC is a dispersive, lower-viscosity OVD that adheres well to intraocular structures, protecting the corneal endothelium during surgery.10 11 It is suggested that HPMC does not remain in the AC as long as HA and may be less effective at maintaining chamber depth.2 4 10 11

Several intraoperative intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement techniques exist. Indentation tonometry originated in the 19th century,12 but modern studies often rely on experimental or invasive setups.13,16 Some methods use covers for existing tonometers to maintain sterility, raising questions about reliability.17 18 No described method is commercially available.

Although high intracameral pressure is thought to be advantageous during CCC creation,19 20 data on the actual required ‘favourable’ pressure are limited. Khng et al reported vitreous pressures up to 163 mm Hg after OVD instillation and up to 400 mm Hg during hydrodelineation in three cadaver eyes.15 Kreutzer et al observed IOP up to 80 mm Hg during phacoemulsification in 10 patients; capsulorhexis-tonometry data were not provided.16

Rebound tonometry has emerged as a reliable extraocular method, comparable to other devices.21,23 New devices enable supine-position tonometry (eg, iCare systems).24,27 Although probes are individually packed and not sterile, intraoperative use of sterilised probes has been demonstrated as feasible and yields results comparable to non-sterile probes.24 28 29 The iCare device and its probes are commercially available and can be sterilised in standard surgical sterilisation processes.

An ex vivo porcine eye study found no statistically significant IOP differences between different OVDs during cataract surgery.29 Data on IOP changes during the critical capsulorhexis phase from larger studies are lacking. This study aims to assess anterior-chamber stability during CCC creation with various OVDs, using the IOP drop as an index of stable conditions. A better understanding and optimised use of suitable OVDs may further enhance the safety of cataract surgery.

Materials and methods

This is a randomised, single-blinded, prospective interventional investigation involving 174 eyes of 104 patients scheduled for routine cataract surgery. Inclusion criteria consisted of age 40–100 years and significant lens opacification justifying cataract surgery. The participants were included consecutively as long as they met the inclusion criteria. Exclusion criteria encompassed prior ocular surgeries, except for laser therapy targeting the trabecular meshwork or retina, particularly excluding vitreoretinal, glaucoma or corneal procedures. Patients with uncontrolled open-angle glaucoma or acute angle-closure glaucoma were also ineligible for the study.

In this study, we actively involved patients and members of the public throughout the research process by involving patient representatives, getting feedback on study materials, disseminating results and building a long-term partnership according to the principles of patient and public involvement.30 These measures were taken to ensure that our research questions and outcomes are relevant, meaningful and patient-centred. Through these measures, we aim to ensure that patient perspectives and needs are fully integrated into our research, thereby enhancing its relevance and impact on patient care.

The estimation of the sample size was conducted based on the analysis of variance (ANOVA) with main effects and interactions using the software G*Power V.3.1. Based on empirical data, a medium- to large-effect size (f=0.35) was assumed, with a power (1−Type II error) set at 90% and an alpha error of 0.05. Based on these parameters, the total sample size was determined to be n=171.

Each eye underwent routine cataract surgery using either HA (PolyHyl 1.4%, ALBOMED GmbH, Schwarzenbruck, Germany; OVD1) or HPMC (PolyVisc 2.0%, ALBOMED GmbH; OVD2) as the OVD (table 1). 90 eyes received HA and 84 eyes received HPMC. Patients had been allocated randomly by the study nurse when surgery was scheduled with an octagonal dice with 1, 2, 3 and 4 leading to the use of HPMC and 5, 6, 7 and 8 to HA. The instrument used for the CCC was again determined randomly with an octagonal dice with 1, 2, 3 and 4 leading to the use of a cystotome (RN) and 5, 6, 7 and 8 to the use of utrata forceps (UF). During the preoperative examination, the AC dimensions were measured using Scheimpflug imaging (Sirius, Costruzione Strumenti Oftalmici, Scandicci, Italy). The values for anterior chamber volume (ACV) and anterior chamber depth (ACD) were recorded. Analysis divided eyes with a shallower or ‘smaller’ AC from eyes with a larger AC. The criteria to use an ACV of 100 µL and ACD of 2.5 mm as a cut-off between ‘small’ and ‘large’ AC were derived from classifications of angle closure glaucoma.8 31 32

Surgery was carried out by a single experienced surgeon (DMH). The exclusion criteria encompassed any prior ocular procedures with the exception of laser treatments targeting the trabecular meshwork or retina, specifically excluding vitreoretinal, glaucoma or corneal surgeries. Patients with uncontrolled open-angle glaucoma or acute angle-closure glaucoma were also not considered for inclusion.

IOP was measured using the rebound tonometer iCare 200 (iCare Finland Oy, Vantaa/Finland) at three different points in time during the surgery: T1 (before the start of the procedure), T2 (immediately prior to the creation of the capsulorhexis after instillation of the OVD) and T3 (immediately after the creation of the capsulorhexis). The probes for the tonometer had been sterilised according to the surgical unit’s protocol (autoclave with 134°C for 5 min). This sterilisation procedure has been shown to be safe for the probes and not to alter their function.24

The individual steps of the surgery were carried out according to national and international guidelines,33 34 with the addition of IOP measurements, which were conducted as described previously.28 35

Different configurations were investigated in the following groups:

Use of a cohesive OVD (OVD1) and creation of the capsulorhexis with UF through the main incision.

Use of a cohesive OVD (OVD1) and creation of the capsulorhexis with a 26G bent needle through the sideport.

Use of a cohesive OVD (OVD1) and creation of the capsulorhexis with a 26G bent needle through the sideport.

Use of a dispersive OVD (OVD2), creation of the capsulorhexis with a 26G bent needle through the sideport.

The primary endpoint of the study was the IOP drop during the creation of the capsulorhexis. This drop was described as the difference in IOP between T2 and T3. A lesser decrease in IOP can be regarded as an indicator of a more stable situation during this manoeuvre.

IOP was measured intraoperatively, and the results were recorded in an Excel sheet immediately.

The analysis of the pressure drop was performed using an ANOVA with the main effect of OVD (HA/HPMC) as well as interactions regarding the instruments used, ACV and ACD.

Results

Reliability of the iCare200

All measurements were successfully recorded for 174 eyes at T1, T2 and T3, resulting in a 100% success rate. No dropouts occurred due to technical or other issues, confirming the reliability of the measurement technique. Two initial measurement attempts at T1 were unsuccessful (failure rate 1.14%), with failures divided between HA (1.11%) and HPMC (1.19%). In both cases, tonometry was successfully performed immediately after probe replacement and subsequent measurements proceeded without difficulties.

Results of intraoperative tonometry

IOP measurement showed comparable values at T1 (HA 24.5±6.7 mm Hg vs HPMC 23.6±6.2 mm Hg); the hypothesis of differing means could be rejected for T1 (p=0.332). This could also be shown for T2 (HA 73.8±11.6 mm Hg vs HPMC 79.3±12.0 mm Hg) and T3 (HA 16.8±8.6 mm Hg vs HPMC 15.5±8.2 mm Hg).

IOP drop during capsulorhexis

IOP drop during capsulorhexis was 57.0±12.2 mm Hg in eyes operated using HA and 63.8±12.4 mm Hg using HPMC. The difference in IOP drop between HA and HPMC was not statistically significant (p=0.100) (tables25).

As different surgical instruments showed a significant influence on IOP drop in contrast to different OVDs, an interdependency between the two variables could be a reason for bias. A two-factor variance analysis was carried out to eliminate such an error. The analysis could rule out any interdependency; the result of this analysis was highly significant. Therefore, it can be concluded that the reduction in IOP during the creation of the capsulorhexis is only related to the surgical approach and not the use of a certain OVD or any OVD/instrument combination.

Discussion

Intraoperative tonometry provides valuable insights into the dynamic changes in IOP that occur during cataract surgery. IOP is crucial for the execution of surgical manoeuvres and plays a significant role when intraocular agents such as gases, fluids, OVDs or oils are used to facilitate surgical steps or stabilise the globe intraoperatively or postoperatively.

Rebound tonometry with sterilised probes was employed to measure IOP intraoperatively in a larger cohort of eyes, demonstrating the device’s reliability with successful readings in 100% of cases at T1, T2 and T3. These three measurements mark important steps during the surgical process when trying to understand the dynamics of cataract surgery. However, it would be desirable to apply continuous measurement to gain even more insight. Alas, continuous measurement is not commercially available, and measuring probes applied at the same time as surgery takes place bear problems in terms of sterility and clarity of the operating field.

At T1, IOP levels were often markedly elevated, potentially indicating latent angle closure in patients with cataracts with thick lenses, likely due to age-related crystalline lens changes and medically induced mydriasis,36 compounded by a supine position prior to surgery.37 Immediately before capsulorhexis (T2), IOP significantly increased when the AC was filled with OVD, a desired effect to facilitate the procedure by flattening the anterior capsule.19 20 Following capsulorhexis creation (T3), IOP decreased during intraocular manipulation, primarily due to reflux of OVD through the incisions. The extent of IOP reduction from T2 to T3 varied depending on the instrument used; for instance, the UF introduced through the larger main incision resulted in greater reflux and a more pronounced IOP decrease compared with the 26G cannula through the side port.

Few studies have been published regarding the topic of IOP changes during the course of cataract surgery. In a study by Khng et al, three cadaver eyes underwent a cataract surgery procedure while tonometry was carried out using a pressure transducer placed in the vitreous cavity.15 IOP after the instillation of the OVD (resembling T2 in our study) was measured between 42.7 mm Hg and 163.0 mm Hg. The next value in the course of surgery is given ‘during capsulorhexis’ and varies between 38.1 mm Hg and 107.2 mm Hg. While the vast variation of IOP at T2 is explained by the use of different approaches (paracentesis and main incision)—a correlation also found in our study—the term ‘during capsulorhexis’ leaves much room to speculate, as our study has shown a significant drop between T2 (before CCC) and T3 (after CCC), so the term ‘during’ describes a dynamic situation rather than a defined point of time in this case. Kreutzer et al also used intravitreal probes in 10 eyes scheduled for combined cataract and vitreoretinal surgery.16 T1 showed comparable results (15.9±9.5 mm Hg and 17.0±13.5 mm Hg in the two groups of this study); the next measurement was taken at ‘lens removal’, which is after T2 and T3 in our study. Javrishvili et al measured IOP with a TonoPen in nine eyes during cataract surgery.38 While the average IOP (18 mm Hg) before the start of the procedure was comparable to the findings in our study, after the instillation of the OVD, the average IOP averaged 16 mm Hg, so IOP was lower after the instillation in eight out of nine cases described. These results seem questionable, as IOP is not supposed to sink after OVD instillation. Hejsek et al reported values resembling T1 of 18.0 mm Hg, T2 of 15.0 mm Hg and T3 of 8 mm Hg using the TonoPen.39 Again, there was a lower IOP recorded after the OVD was instilled. One can speculate that in the studies by Hejsek39 and Javrishvilli,38 the contact of the TonoPen may have caused OVD leakage through corneal incisions, leading to artificially lower IOP readings, rather than reflecting the true IOP after OVD instillation. The touch of the iCare probe is much softer, making leakage unlikely. The single study using rebound tonometry intraoperatively was published by Davis et al concerning the results of 42 eyes of 30 paediatric patients.14 Tonometry was performed at the start and at the end of the surgical intervention. IOP at the beginning (resembling T1 in our study) was measured at 23.52±9.76 mm Hg, with glaucomatous eyes being part of the cohort. In summary, these studies report highly variable IOP measurements obtained using different tonometry methods, surgical techniques and materials, making a comparison near impossible. Another reason for the great variation may be low case numbers in these studies, which in many cases were more of a case series than a study meeting the demands for statistical significance and making errors go by unnoticed.

Our findings indicate that the IOP increase following OVD instillation prior to capsulorhexis and the subsequent decrease during the procedure are consistent across different OVD types. The differences between T2 and T3 within each group were not statistically significant, suggesting that the physical properties of the OVDs—such as viscosity, elasticity, pseudoplasticity and cohesion—may have limited influence on intraoperative IOP changes. Instead, other factors may play a more prominent role in intraoperative tactile feedback and IOP dynamics.

In contrast to that, there were statistically significant differences depending on the instrument used. This could lead to the preference of the cystotome or 26G microforceps, especially in situations where a prolonged maintenance of high IOP is desirable, for example, elevated vitreous pressure.

Using the findings of this study

This study presents data on IOP fluctuations during cataract surgery. While the results may reinforce observations long recognised by seasoned surgeons in routine practice, this work is the first to provide qualitative data on this topic. Importantly, the results establish a basis for addressing meaningful questions that could further enhance cataract-surgery safety. Potential avenues include the following.

Clinical significance of IOP differences

What magnitude of IOP change is clinically meaningful, and how might such differences translate into reduced complications, improved control of the capsular bag or decreased need for reinjection of viscoelastic devices (OVDs)?

The data report IOP at three time points and emphasise the magnitude of the IOP drop during capsulorhexis (T2→T3). The study found no statistically significant differences in absolute IOP levels across stages between OVD types and, specifically, no significant difference in IOP drop attributable to OVD type (p>0.05). Therefore, no explicit clinical threshold can be defined by our findings alone.

Clinically meaningful implications depend on stability

A smaller IOP decline during capsulorhexis (ie, greater AC stability) could facilitate cleaner, more controllable CCC geometry and reduce capsule tears. Conversely, larger reflux-induced IOP reductions might correspond to greater instrument-induced instability or inconsistent CCC contours.

Within the presented data, instrument-related differences (port location and instrument type) can modulate IOP dynamics and may impact CCC control more than OVD type per se. A clinically meaningful magnitude would reliably shift the IOP trajectory to enhance chamber stability and reduce tactile variability or tears, but the study cannot quantify a specific mm Hg cut-off. Future work should empirically define such thresholds.

Defining pressure thresholds

Which IOP levels during CCC constitute a safe operating window across diverse anterior-chamber morphologies and patient factors? Establishing thresholds linked to complication risk would enable predefined intraoperative targets.

Predictors of instability during CCC

Which preoperative or intraoperative parameters (eg, ACV, ACD, lens vault, zonular integrity and corneal biomechanics) predict greater instability or excessive reflux? Quantification may enable risk stratification and tailored strategies and allow definition of an IOP range (or rate of change) associated with optimal CCC stability versus tearing risk. Establishing thresholds would require systematic variation and larger datasets.

Quantified data could identify which variables (eg, larger incision, specific instrument through main vs side port and certain OVD properties) independently predict greater IOP decline or fluctuations, informing preoperative planning.

Relationship between capsule flattening and OVD characteristics

Correlating IOP changes with direct measures of capsule flattening or contour during CCC would clarify whether high-MW OVDs confer measurable mechanical advantages.

Instrument selection and incision design

The data indicate that instrument choice and port placement significantly influence IOP drop during CCC (p<0.01). Therefore, in difficult CCC cases, selecting an instrument and entry port that minimises disruptive reflux could promote more stable conditions and improve CCC control. Prefer configurations with a favourable IOP trajectory (ie, smaller drop) when stability is critical. Given that incision size and port location modulate reflux and IOP dynamics, incision planning could aim to optimise OVD reflux, balancing stability with OVD egress, potentially coordinating main incision size and side-port position to achieve controlled reflux at CCC.

Standardising reporting

Incorporating intraoperative IOP time-course data (T1, T2, T3 and IOP drop) into study protocols would enable cross-study comparisons and meta-analyses of OVD rheology, instrument effects and technique-related stability.

Modelling geometry–dynamics relationships

How do incision size, instrument diameter and AC dimensions interact to determine IOP trajectories during CCC? Developing predictive models could inform instrument choice and incision planning in real time.

Multivariate modelling of IOP dynamics

With precise measurements of OVD type, viscosity, elasticity, incision size, instrument diameter and port location, one can model how these factors jointly shape IOP trajectories during CCC, enabling the prediction of instability risk.

Training and standardisation

Reported T1–T3 IOP values and the quantified IOP decrease can provide objective benchmarks for trainee performance. Trainees could compare their intraoperative IOP profiles to reference trajectories to evaluate stability and reproducibility across different viscoelastic device (OVD) regimens and instrument configurations. When paired with quantitative CCC (capsulotomy/curative) outcome metrics (eg, regression lines, curvature and tear rate), quantified IOP dynamics may yield objective performance indicators.

Similarities between ex vivo and in vivo results

The results of this in vivo study confirm the results of an ex vivo study on porcine eyes.29 The similarity of results suggests that the ex vivo model may be reliably used to investigate other aspects of the dynamics of cataract surgery.

Future OVD research

Data showing no differential IOP effect between cohesive and short-term cohesive regimens in this context imply that rheological properties affecting tactile feedback may be more relevant than gross IOP differences. Quantified IOP dynamics can assist in modelling how OVD viscosity, elasticity and cohesion contribute to chamber stability, thereby guiding future comparative trials.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that the increase in IOP following the instillation of OVDs prior to capsulorhexis, as well as the subsequent decrease during the procedure, is consistent across different types of OVDs. The lack of significant differences between the groups suggests that factors other than the physical properties of the OVDs—such as viscosity, elasticity, pseudoplasticity and cohesion—may play a more critical role in influencing tactile feedback during surgery. These results highlight the complexity of intraoperative IOP dynamics and underscore the need for further research to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. To explain these sensations, other features like viscosity, elasticity, pseudoplasticity and cohesion will have to be investigated. A better understanding of these factors could improve surgical techniques and patient outcomes in cataract surgery.
