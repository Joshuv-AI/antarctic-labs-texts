Constructing A Core Competency Evaluation Index System for Incontinence Specialist Nurses: A Delphi Study
Authors: Xiao Miao Tian, Yu Zhen Chen, Su Mei Xie, Han Mei Shao, Yan Yi Li, Jian Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study aims to construct a core competency evaluation index system for incontinence specialist nurses; clarify the core competency elements required for incontinence nursing work; and provide a theoretical foundation for a comprehensive, objective and scientific evaluation of incontinence specialist nurses.DesignA Delphi study design was adopted.MethodsBased on the theoretical framework of the Competency Framework for Registered Nurses in China and related literature, a preliminary core competency evaluation index system for continence specialist nurses was determined by conducting semi‐structured interviews. Subsequently, through two rounds of the Delphi method, 27 medical experts, nursing educators and managers, and clinical nursing experts from 16 provinces, including Beijing, Shanghai, Guangdong, Zhejiang, Jiangsu, Anhui and Chongqing, and two special administrative regions, including Hong Kong and Macao and Singapore, were consulted by letter. Indicators with a mean importance score ≥ 4.00, full score rate ≥ 20% and coefficient of variation ≤ 0.25 were retained.ResultsThe effective recovery rates of the expert letter inquiries' two rounds were 90% and 100%. The Kendall's coefficient of agreement (W) values obtained from the two rounds of expert inquiries in this study were 0.150–0.244 and 0.129–0.173, respectively, (p < 0.001). Their Kendall coefficients were 0.152 and 0.128, respectively, with an expert authority coefficient of 0.930 (p < 0.001). The final evaluation index system included 6, 17 and 70 first‐, second‐ and third‐level indicators, respectively.ConclusionsOur study constructed a core competency evaluation index system for incontinence specialist nurses. The construction process—though scientific—must be verified in practice.Public ContributionThe construction of a core competency evaluation index system for incontinence specialist nurses may help develop training programmes for them, facilitate the comprehensive evaluation of their abilities and act as a theoretical and practical reference in this regard. This study fills the research gap vis‐à‐vis constructing a core competency evaluation index system for incontinence specialist nurses.

Introduction

Urinary incontinence (UI) refers to involuntary urine outflow from the urethral opening, while faecal incontinence refers to the involuntary loss of solid or liquid faeces. Faecal incontinence is typically accompanied by UI—both of which are collectively referred to as incontinence (Luo and Shen 2017; Woodley et al. 2017). Reportedly, among hospitalized patients, the prevalence of faecal incontinence ranges from 6% to 16.3%, while that of UI ranges from 13% to 26% (Koloms et al. 2022). Notably, UI is a problem affecting millions of people worldwide, precipitating an economic burden of billions of dollars, and negatively impacting patients' quality of life (Game et al. 2023).

Services provided by incontinence specialist nurses—compared with those provided by general practitioners—precipitate greater satisfaction among patients with UI (Albers‐Heitner et al. 2013). Moreover, lifestyle and behavioural interventions for patients with UI led by incontinence counsellors effectively improve symptoms (Borrie et al. 2002). Hong Kong Queen Elizabeth Hospital is among the earliest hospitals in China to provide outpatient care for incontinence; its incontinence nurses have obtained specialist training certificates through incontinence specialist courses organized by either the Nursing Institute of the Hong Kong Hospital Authority or other countries (Bo et al. 2017). China's first international incontinence specialist nurse training class was held in Shenzhen, Guangdong Province, in 2019 (Zhang and Xiaoping 2021). With incontinence‐specialized nursing's development, incontinence‐specialized nurses play an increasingly important role in improving outcomes and saving treatment costs among patients with incontinence (Franken et al. 2018). However, research on developing a core competency evaluation index system for incontinence nurses is lacking.

Background

On March 8, 2011, in China, nursing was elevated from a second‐level discipline of clinical medicine (100209) to a first‐level discipline (1011); the formation and establishment of specialized nurses is an important symbol of the nursing specialization's development (Han 2020; Wang and Zhang 2013). The National Nursing Development Plan (2016–2020) proposed to systematically train a group of specialized nurses to satisfy clinical nursing needs—aiming to improve nurses' service capability (National Health and Family Planning Commission 2016). The American Nurses Association defines a specialist nurse as a registered nurse who holds a master's or doctoral degree, possesses rich clinical practice experience, is proficient in the knowledge and skills of a special field of a clinical specialty, and has a specialist nurse licence (Li et al. 2015). In China, a clinical nurse specialist refers to registered nurses who have standardized training and qualification certificates, possess solid theoretical knowledge, rich clinical experience, and excellent operational skills in a certain field of clinical nursing, and are skilled in providing specialized services to patients (Cao et al. 2015; Li and Xi 2019).

Incontinence specialist nurses refer to registered nurses who possess skilled nursing skills and knowledge in the field of incontinence and have completed the necessary education courses, passed the examination and obtained the corresponding qualification certification (Zhang and Xiaoping 2021). Currently, incontinence specialist nurses are predominantly developed by wound and ostomy therapists, while patients with incontinence are increasing daily. The existing ET simply cannot fulfil the growing needs of patients with incontinence; hence, developing incontinence specialist nurses independently is necessary. However, the incontinence specialty's development began late. Incontinence specialty nurses' training lacks corresponding management, and no uniform setting of teaching materials, courses, training institutions and certification centres exists.

In China, the concept of nurse core competency was first proposed in 2003 (Zhao 2019). Core competency refers to the sum of employees' knowledge, skills, abilities, and traits that enable them to be competent for their job position; possessing these characteristics precipitates excellent job performance (Liu et al. 2023). Establishing an evaluation system for nurses' core competency can provide a scientific foundation and guidance for specialist nurses' selection, admission, training, assessment, and evaluation and is conducive to training more professional and efficient specialist nurses (Duan et al. 2020). Most existing core competency evaluation index systems for specialist nurses are focused on wound and ostomy incontinence specialist nurses; further, specialist incontinence nurses' selection, admission, training, assessment and evaluation lack pertinence. To clarify the core competency elements required for incontinence nursing work, this study adopts Liu et al.'s (2006, 2008) Competency Framework for Registered Nurses in China as the theoretical framework, refers to relevant literature, employs semi‐structured interviews and Delphi expert letter consultations, and constructs a core competency evaluation index system for incontinence specialist nurses, thus providing a theoretical foundation for a comprehensive, objective and scientific evaluation of incontinence nurses.

Research Method

Research Design

Considering the Competency Framework for Registered Nurses in China as the theoretical framework, semi‐structured interviews were performed to construct a preliminary index system with reference to relevant literature. A Delphi study was conducted to investigate 27 experts' attitudes toward the core competency evaluation index system for incontinence specialist nurses.

Evaluation Index System's Construction

Research Group Establishment

The research team comprised 11 individuals with rich experience in incontinence treatment, nursing and management, including one director of the nursing department (chief nurse), two deputy directors of the nursing department (chief nurses), one director of urology surgery (one chief nurse and one deputy chief nurse), one specialist incontinence nurse (chief nurse), one urological nurse (nurse), one nursing master (nurse) and two urological surgeons (associate chief physicians).

Document Research

Utilizing CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, China Medical Journal Database, PubMed, JBI and EBSCO as search databases, and using ‘incontinence, core competence, competency, specialist nurses, clinical experts, advanced nurse practitioners’ as search terms, we conducted a literature search to collect data regarding the core competency of incontinence specialist nurses. Subsequently, the search results were classified, discussed and analyzed.

Semi‐Structured Interviews

Based on our literature research and the Competency Framework for Registered Nurses in China, we interviewed eight medical experts and nursing managers—including two clinical medical experts and six incontinence specialist nurses or incontinence‐related nursing managers—from five cities in three provinces, namely, Beijing, Shenzhen, Guangzhou, Jiangmen and Kunming. The interview outline was as follows: (1) What is your understanding of specialist nurses and their core competencies? (2) What abilities do you think incontinence nurses should possess? The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) clinical nursing staff, nursing managers, medical experts, or professors engaged in the field of incontinence; ≥ 5 years of related work experience; Bachelor's degree or above; and informed consent and voluntary participation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (2) those who could not—or refused to—participate.

Proposed Entry Pool

The research team collectively discussed the literature research and interview results and constructed a preliminary core competency evaluation index system for incontinence specialist nurses, including 6, 21 and 83 primary, secondary, and tertiary indicators, respectively.

Delphi Implementation

Expert Inclusion Criteria

Medical experts, nursing managers, and clinical nursing experts who possessed rich clinical knowledge and experience, had worked in a specialized field for 10 years or more (for those with a Master's degree, this criterion was relaxed to 5 years or more), had a Bachelor's degree or above (with an intermediate title or above), provided their informed consent, participated voluntarily in this study and could cooperate with two–three rounds of correspondence. Overall, this study included 27 experts.

Preliminary Draft of Expert Consultation Questionnaire

The letter questionnaire comprised three components. (1) The letter's purpose and significance and the questionnaire itself were introduced, and notes for completing it were provided. (2) The experts' general characteristics were collected, including their age, years of work experience, professional title, position, educational background, whether Master's supervisor, research direction and familiarity with and judgement basis vis‐à‐vis the letter questionnaire items. The judgement basis comprised clinical practice experience, theoretical analysis, reference to domestic and foreign literature, and intuitive feeling. Each factor's influence on judgement was divided into three levels—namely, large, medium and small. Each of the five familiarity levels was assigned a value—specifically, very familiar = 1.0, familiar = 0.8, fairly familiar = 0.5, not very familiar = 0.2 and not familiar = 0.0. (3) Finally, six dimensions—including clinical nursing knowledge and skills, scientific research and its application ability, management ability, educational ability, professional development ability and ethical and legal knowledge—were scored using a 5‐point Likert‐type scale to rate each consultation item's importance (5 = very important, 4 = relatively important, 3 = moderately important, 2 = not very important, and 1 = not important). The ‘Supplementary Comments’ column is placed after each indicator level, and the ‘Modify or Delete Comments’ column is placed after each indicator level to collect the experts' opinions.

Delphi Consulting and Feedback Cycle

Electronic questionnaires were issued by email or WeChat from June to October 2023, and experts were required to reply within 2 weeks. In the first round, indicators that fulfilled an average importance score ≥ 4.00, full score rate ≥ 20%, and coefficient of variation ≤ 0.25 were retained (Yu et al. 2020). Based on the expert opinions and statistical analysis results, the research team discussed whether to delete, modify or supplement the indicators, thus forming the basis for the second round of letter questionnaires, which were distributed and sorted following the same aforementioned method until the expert opinions reached a consensus.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Excel 2019 and SPSS 22.0. Experts' activeness degree was represented by the questionnaire's effective recovery rate, and the experts' authority degree was represented by the authority coefficient (Cr)—determined by the expert's judgement basis (Ca) and familiarity (Cs): Cr = (Ca + Cs)/2. The concentration degree of the expert opinions is expressed using the mean, coefficient of variation, and full score rate. The degree of expert opinion coordination is represented using the Kendall harmony coefficient. Finally, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Experts' General Information

Overall, 27 medical experts, nursing educators, nursing administrators and clinical nursing experts from 16 provinces—namely, Beijing, Shanghai, Guangdong, Zhejiang, Jiangsu, Chongqing, Jilin, Shandong, Sichuan, Anhui, Hunan, Hubei, Jiangxi, Henan, Yunnan and Shaanxi—and two special administrative regions of Hong Kong and Macao and Singapore were invited for two rounds of correspondence. Among them, 13, 3 and 1 had a Bachelor's, Master's and Doctoral degree, respectively, while 7 were Master's supervisors; further, 7, 10, 7 and 3 had senior professional titles, associate senior professional titles, intermediate professional titles and other professional titles (Hong Kong and Macau and Singapore), respectively. Their work experience ranged from 8 to 49 (27.74 ± 8.97) years, while their ages ranged from 34 to 60 (48.52 ± 7.19) years.

Expert Activeness Degree

In the first round, 30 questionnaires were distributed, of which 27 valid questionnaires were recovered, indicating an effective recovery rate of 90%. In the second round, 27 questionnaires were distributed, all of which were recovered with valid responses, indicating an effective recovery rate of 100%. This suggests that the experts were highly enthusiastic about this study.

Expert Authority Degree

The expert authority degree was represented by the authority coefficient (Cr)—determined by the expert's judgment basis (Ca) and familiarity (Cs) with the index: Cr = (Ca + Cs)/2. The greater the coefficient of authority, the higher the authority degree, generally requiring Cr > 0.7 (Ang et al. 2021). In this study, Ca, Cs and Cr were 0.956, 0.904 and 0.930, respectively, indicating a high expert authority degree.

Degree of Coordination of Expert Opinions

The degree of coordination of expert opinions was expressed using the Kendall harmony coefficient (W)—ranging from 0 to 1. The larger the value, the higher the expert coordination degree. Overall agreement (consensus) between expert panel members was estimated using Kendall's coefficient of concordance (Kendall's W). There are 5 levels of agreement: slight (W ≤ 0.3), fair (W = 0.31–0.50), moderate (W = 0.51–0.70), substantial (W = 0.71–0.90) and almost perfect (W > 0.90) (Qiao‐Yan et al. 2018). The Kendall's coefficient of agreement (W) values obtained from the two rounds of expert inquiries in this study were 0.150–0.244 and 0.129–0.173, respectively. The differences were statistically significant (p < 0.001). The results suggest that the overall coordination of experts' opinions on the evaluation indicators of the core competencies of incontinence specialist nurses is moderate, and there is some heterogeneity in the judgements of the importance of the indicators by different experts. Discussion

Reliability Analysis of Research Results

The Competency Framework for Registered Nurses in China is a self‐assessment scale that comprehensively evaluates registered nurses' core competency and can also be used for other related assessments. It comprises seven dimensions—namely, critical thinking; scientific research; clinical nursing; leadership; interpersonal relationships; law and ethical practice; professional development, education, and consulting ability. Incontinence nurses' training in our country focuses on clinical practice and develops the core abilities of scientific research, teaching and management concerning clinical practice ability. Considering actual clinical needs, our study adopted the Competency Framework for Registered Nurses in China as the theoretical framework, referred to relevant literature, conducted semi‐structured interviews with eight experts in the field of incontinence, extracted the core competency index of incontinence nurses, and formulated a more reasonable and comprehensive expert consultation questionnaire.

The Delphi method is a widely used methodological tool for achieving group consensus (Yan et al. 2024). This study strictly consulted experts in incontinence fields by letter following the Delphi method. Overall, 24 incontinence experts from 16 provinces in China—along with 2 experts from Hong Kong and Macao and 1 from Singapore in Southeast Asia—were consulted. The sample of experts exhibits regional representativeness. Simultaneously, the experts ranged across multiple varied areas in the field of incontinence, such as incontinence medicine, incontinence nursing education, incontinence nursing management and incontinence clinical nursing. The authority coefficient of experts was 0.930, indicating that the experts possess rich experience in incontinence treatment, nursing, management and education; can deeply and comprehensively comprehend the evaluation of the core competency elements of incontinence specialist nurses, and propose constructive suggestions for constructing a core competency evaluation index system for them.

The effective recovery rates of the expert letter questionnaires in rounds one and two were 90% and 100%, respectively. The experts provided positive, authentic and scientific feedback for each item and exhibited high enthusiasm. The Kendall coefficients of the two rounds of inquiries were 0.150–0.244 and 0.129–0.173 (p < 0.001). The overall coordination degree of experts' opinions on the evaluation indicators of the core competencies of incontinence specialist nurses was moderate. There was some heterogeneity in the judgement of the importance of the indicators by different experts, which might be related to the fact that the experts covered multiple fields such as incontinence medicine, incontinence nursing education, incontinence nursing management and incontinence clinical nursing. Based on a literature review and semi‐structured interviews, this study employed the Delphi method to integrate experts' subjective judgement with quantitative analysis. In sum, this study's research method is scientific, its process is rigorous and its results are scientific and reliable.

The Establishment of Core Competence Evaluation Index System for Incontinence Specialist Nurses Has Clinical Practicability

Different countries and regions have different definitions of nurses' core competency; however, it predominantly includes, among others, the abilities to directly provide clinical care, lead and make decisions, provide education and guidance, and conduct scientific research (Xiaoping et al. 2011). After two rounds of Delphi consultation, the core competency evaluation index system for incontinence specialist nurses was established, including 6 first‐level, 17 second‐level and 70 third‐level indicators.

The six first‐level indicators included the following six dimensions: clinical nursing knowledge and skills, scientific research and application ability, management ability, education ability, professional development ability and ethics and legal knowledge. Of these, the highest importance score was assigned to ‘clinical nursing knowledge and skills’ (5.00 ± 0.00), which covers the physiological mechanism of normal urination and defecation; pathogenesis and clinical diagnosis and classification of UI and faecal incontinence; medication treatment, nursing care, observation and treatment of complications vis‐à‐vis UI and faecal incontinence, and so on. ‘Clinical nursing knowledge and skills’ included 2 second‐level indicators—namely, ‘theoretical knowledge of incontinence nursing’ and ‘skill level of incontinence nursing’—and 29 third‐level indicators. Among all the secondary indicators of the core competency evaluation index system, the importance average scores of ‘theoretical knowledge of incontinence nursing’ and ‘skill level of incontinence nursing’ were the highest (4.93 ± 0.39), indicating that clinical nursing knowledge and skills are the most fundamental and critical elements for incontinence specialist nurses. Theoretical knowledge and skill levels concerning incontinence complement each other and collectively contribute to the development of advanced incontinence nursing practice—consistent with most prior findings (Chao et al. 2021; Jieli et al. 2023).

The first‐level indicators ‘educational ability’ and ‘professional development ability’ were both the second‐highest among the 6 first‐level indicators. Among the third‐level indicators of ‘education ability’ and ‘professional development ability’, ‘ability to provide health education to patients with incontinence’ and ‘ability to update new knowledge, new technology, new business and new progress of incontinence’ ranked high. Therefore, constantly exploring, innovating and cultivating themselves as experts in the field of incontinence is extremely important for incontinence specialist nurses. Simultaneously, incontinence specialist nurses should possess—and continuously improve—the ability to provide high‐quality health education. The first‐level indicator of ‘scientific research and application ability’ ranked fourth among the 6 first‐level indicators, which can provide novel ideas for developing specialized nursing talents. With the development of the economy, science, and technology, and medical technology's continuous upgrading, the nursing discipline's development is also highly rapid. This necessitates incontinence specialist nurses to promptly acquaint themselves with the latest progress in incontinence nursing, possess the ability to synthesize high‐quality evidence to implement evidence‐based nursing and publish research findings to facilitate peer learning and communication. Although the first‐level indicators ‘ethical and legal knowledge’ and ‘management ability’ had low importance scores, they were considered the core competency of incontinence specialist nurses and an important condition to ensure incontinence nursing quality; hence, the role of these indicators cannot be ignored.

The core competency evaluation index system for incontinence specialist nurses is comprehensive—considering the clinical, educational and professional development and research capabilities of continence specialist nurses. This is conducive to ensuring quality service focused on the safety of patients with incontinence and an operational and quantifiable evaluation tool for evaluating incontinence specialist nurses' core competency. This system not only aligns with the current situation of incontinence nursing education in China but also reflects relevant competency requirements, aiming to promote the specialized development of incontinence nursing. Therefore, the core competency evaluation index system for incontinence specialist nurses developed herein exhibits satisfactory clinical practicability and provides important guidance for developing incontinence specialist nursing.

Pre‐Survey and Formal Testing Plan for the Core Competence Evaluation Index System of Incontinence Specialist Nurses

Although the Delphi expert consultation has established the core competence evaluation index system for incontinence specialist nurses, the expert consensus cannot replace the measurement attributes of the tools. Therefore, a systematic pre‐survey and formal testing will be carried out in the future. Local city‐based incontinence specialist nurses will be selected for the pre‐survey, with a focus on testing the clarity and operability of the items. The item analysis method (critical ratio, homogeneity test) will be used to screen or modify the items with poor discrimination; at the same time, exploratory factor analysis will be conducted to preliminarily determine whether the dimension division is reasonable and to explore whether the model is consistent with the actual situation, thereby forming the testing version of the incontinence specialist nurse core competence evaluation index system. In the formal testing stage, cross‐sectional surveys will be conducted on incontinence specialist nurses in each province by cluster sampling. Reliability: internal consistency reliability (Cronbach's α coefficient) and inter‐rater consistency (independent scoring by two researchers, calculating the intraclass correlation coefficient ICC) will be calculated. Validity: confirmatory factor analysis will be used to test the structural validity (model fitting indicators); if there are recognized criteria (such as nursing quality scores, patient satisfaction and etc.), the standard correlation validity will also be tested, thereby forming the application version of the incontinence specialist nurse core competence evaluation index system.

Strategies for Implementing the Core Competency Evaluation Index System for Incontinence Specialized Nurses

The core competency evaluation index system for incontinence specialized nurses adopts the Likert 5‐point rating method, with scores ranging from ‘Very inconsistent’ to ‘Very consistent’ assigned as 1 to 5 respectively. By assigning scores to the indicators, the core competency scores of incontinence specialized nurses can be calculated, which are highly quantifiable and evaluable (Yilan et al. 2024). The core competency evaluation index system for incontinence specialized nurses is specifically designed for the incontinence care work of clinical nurses and defines the capabilities that incontinence specialized nurses should achieve. Studies have shown that training based on core competencies can improve the clinical practice ability of specialized nurses (Liu et al. 2021). However, the value of the evaluation tool ultimately depends on whether it can smoothly transform from the theoretical framework to clinical practice. To avoid the disconnection between this index system and clinical training practice, subsequent research will advance its implementation in three stages. The first stage: phased testing in different clinical fields. Given that this index system is mainly constructed based on the background of the Chinese mainland and Hong Kong and Macao regions, it is necessary to first conduct cross‐cultural adaptation and measurement equivalence tests in a multi‐medical system to assess its applicability. The first (standardized inpatient scenario): select representative tertiary general hospitals, regional medical centres, and university‐affiliated hospitals, covering their neurology departments, geriatric medicine departments, intensive care units, rehabilitation medicine departments and surgical wards. The incontinence care processes in these institutions are relatively standardized, making it convenient to conduct small‐sample pre‐tests to examine the operability of the indicators in the standardized nursing pathway and cross‐cultural equivalence. Second (Specialized Institution): Expanding to specialized institutions focusing on incontinence care, including pelvic floor rehabilitation and postpartum recovery centres, spinal cord injury rehabilitation specialized hospitals, etc. The key evaluation indicators are focused on the sensitivity and discrimination under different disease spectra, age groups and specialized care models. Third (Primary Care and Long‐Term Care Institutions): Conducting external validity verification in scenarios such as primary hospitals, community health service centres, nursing homes and elderly care institutions, to test the applicability and reliability of the indicator system in environments with relatively limited resources and stronger continuity of care. Considering the functional positioning differences of different‐level medical institutions, dynamic adjustment of indicator weights or hierarchical evaluation schemes can be explored to enhance the adaptability of the tool. The second stage: Conducting a national cross‐sectional survey to understand the current status of core competencies of incontinence specialist nurses and identify weaknesses, providing a basis for training. The key point of this stage lies in the representativeness of the sample and the standardization of the survey implementation. The third stage: Based on the indicator system, optimizing training and clinical transformation. Relying on the incontinence specialist nurse training base and the Guangdong‐Hong Kong‐Macao Incontinence Care Alliance platform, optimize the existing training courses and practical goals based on the core competency evaluation indicators. The division of responsibilities is as follows: Medical institutions provide time and resource support (specialized study hours, simulation equipment and teaching personnel), and incorporate capability evaluation into performance assessment; Educators receive specialized training on the indicator system to ensure the homogeneity of evaluation standards and are responsible for the design of modular courses and the development of assessment tools; Nursing managers coordinate the training plan, coordinate shifts to ensure training time, and establish a regular review and feedback mechanism. The training adopts a ‘theory + workshop + clinical shadowing’ mixed model, with a total of 40–60 h of study, completed in 4–6 weeks, and equipped with special funds. The quality supervision group is jointly established by the nursing department and the training base, through classroom observation, skill tests and collection of anonymous questionnaires to collect process feedback, hold teaching review meetings every 2 weeks, and establish a digital two‐way feedback channel to achieve real‐time communication between trainees and the teaching team. By embedding the evaluation indicators into the ‘need assessment – process implementation – effect evaluation’ full chain, a closed‐loop management system combining formative evaluation and summative evaluation is constructed.

Limitations

The core competence evaluation index system for incontinence specialist nurses constructed in this study has a relatively good scientific basis and clinical practicability. However, it has the following limitations: First, the reliance of the research method on expert consensus. The Delphi method is fundamentally based on expert subjective judgement. Although the experts in this study were highly authoritative, differences in clinical background, management or educational experience among different experts may lead to deviations in the importance scores of the items. Second, the Kendall's coefficient of agreement is low. The Kendall W values of the two rounds of inquiries were 0.150–0.244 and 0.129–0.173 (p < 0.001); although they were statistically significant, the W values were at a relatively low level, indicating that experts did not have a high degree of consistency in the ranking of the importance of indicators. This reflects that there are reasonable differences in the priority levels of the core competencies of incontinence specialist nurses among experts from different fields. Third, there is a lack of sufficient empirical verification. Currently, the index system has not undergone reliability and validity tests with a large sample and multi‐center, and its measurement attributes are not yet clear. Fourth, the ability priority ranking in different clinical situations has not been fully considered. The average importance scores of the indicators in this study reflect the overall evaluation of the expert group (‘clinical nursing knowledge and skills’ is the highest), but in different clinical settings such as primary medical institutions, community care or specialized outpatient clinics of tertiary hospitals, the actual weights of management ability, research ability or educational ability may be significantly different. Not conducting contextualized weight stratification may affect the targeted nature of the index system. Fifth, the necessity of cross‐medical system testing. The experts consulted mainly come from the Chinese mainland, Hong Kong, Macao and Singapore and the index system is embedded in the practice scope of Chinese incontinence specialist nurses (such as without independent prescription rights). Before promoting it to other medical systems (such as in Europe and America) or different levels and regions of hospitals within China, cross‐cultural adaptation and measurement equivalence tests must be conducted for cross‐cultural adjustment and measurement. Subsequent research should focus on: ① Conducting large‐sample empirical investigations to comprehensively test the reliability and validity of the index system; ② Exploring contextualized weight adjustment schemes or stratified evaluation strategies; ③ Conducting cross‐regional and cross‐cultural validations in multiple medical systems to enhance the external validity and promotion value of the tool.
