Divergent responses of rhizosphere microbial diversity and co-occurrence patterns to elevation and season in Quercus franchetii from the Yuanmou dry‑hot valley, Southwest China
Authors: Xuedan Xie, Guanping Li, Jiao Wu, Cailan Liu, Haitao Yue, Dacai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDry-hot valleys suffer from severe ecological stress, with the rhizosphere being an essential microhabitat for plant adaptation. Elevation and seasonal changes are key drivers shaping rhizosphere microbial diversity and community composition. However, their impacts on rhizosphere bacteria and fungi associated with Quercus franchetii in dry-hot valleys remain poorly understood.ResultBacterial α-diversity exhibited a V-shaped pattern along the elevational gradient, whereas fungal α-diversity generally declined with increasing elevation. Seasonal effects were pronounced, with both bacterial and fungal diversity higher in the rainy season. Soil pH had strong positive correlations with bacterial α-diversity, and both soil pH and water content were associated with variations in bacterial and fungal community composition. Bacterial co-occurrence networks were more complex than fungal networks. Rainy-season networks had higher natural connectivity, and higher random and targeted robustness AUC. Actinobacteriota, Acidobacteriota, and Ascomycota acted as keystone taxa stabilizing network interactions. Functional predictions indicated that bacterial communities were predominantly chemoheterotrophic, whereas fungal communities were dominated by symbiotrophic guilds.ConclusionsElevational gradients exert a stronger influence than seasonal variation on rhizosphere microbial diversity and composition. Soil pH and water content are key environmental filters shaping microbial assemblages. Microbial networks maintain ecosystem functions through seasonally modulated connectivity and cooperation. Keystone taxa may mediate network stability and functional resilience under spatiotemporal environmental variation. These results provide a basis for predicting microbial responses to environmental change in dry-hot valleys.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05364-2.Keywords: Quercus franchetii, Dry-hot valley, Rhizosphere microbiome, Elevation, Season, Diversity, Co-occurrence network, Functional prediction

Introduction

The rhizosphere is a highly dynamic interface between plant roots and soil, where microenvironments (e.g., temperature, soil moisture, plant root exudates) selectively recruit microbial taxa and regulate microbial diversity and ecosystem functions [1, 2]. Rhizosphere microbiome plays essential roles in nutrient cycling, plant health, and resilience to abiotic stress, especially in habitats with drought and nutrient limitation [1, 3]. Understanding and predicting the spatial distribution patterns of rhizosphere microbiome are crucial for assessing plant health and anticipating ecosystem responses to global climate changes [1–3]. The rhizosphere microbial abundance, community structure, co-occurrence complexity and trophic compositions vary along environmental gradients, driven by the interactions of both biotic and abiotic [4–6].

Spatial gradients, especially elevation, recognized as natural experimental systems for examining microbial responses to long-term climatic and edaphic variations [2]. Elevational gradients simultaneously alter multiple environmental factors, including temperature, moisture, nutrient availability, and vegetation composition, thereby imposing selective pressures that shape microbial community diversity and structure [7]. Previous studies have reported that microbial diversity and community composition shift along elevational gradients, with exhibiting various nonlinear patterns (e.g., monotonic, U-shaped, hump-shaped, or no trend) and different structures [7–10]. their responses to elevation reflect complex ecological interactions, and their complexity leads to numerous exceptions [2].

Temporal factors, particularly seasonal variation, represents another critical determinant of microbial dynamics [11]. Seasonal variation in precipitation and soil moisture, particularly in regions subject to pronounced dry and rainy periods, exerts a strong influence on microbial activity and community composition [12]. Previous studies have reported that the diversity and abundance patterns of fungi and bacteria may synchronize or diverge under seasonal variation, and distinct processes generally drive these seasonal dynamics [13–15]. Therefore, examining the response characteristics of rhizosphere microbiome to seasonal drought is essential for understanding these spatial distribution patterns.

Indeed, elevation and season does not directly cause microbial diversity and co-occurrence patterns. Both direct climatic factors and indirect ones, such as soil properties, have been hypothesized to affect soil microbial diversity [14, 16]. At local spatial scales, indirect factors, such as soil physicochemical properties become significant drivers determining microbial distributions [17]. Soil physicochemical factors, particularly pH, water content, nutrient availability, and organic carbon, strongly drive spatial variations in microbial diversity and community composition [18]. Local environmental conditions such as soil pH are critical for structuring bacterial communities [19], whereas soil pH, total nitrogen (TN), and soil organic carbon (SOC) acted as the key factors influencing the fungal community composition [18, 20]. However, these effects vary across spatial scales and ecosystems [21].

The dry-hot valleys in southwestern China, characterized by extreme seasonal droughts [22], support a set of characteristic species and floristic indicators that are well acclimated to dry and hot conditions, such as Quercus franchetii [23]. However, few studies have investigated the relationships between microbial communities and their environmental drivers in dry-hot valley ecosystems [24]. To address this knowledge gap, this study aimed to understand the responses of rhizosphere microbial diversity and co-occurrence patterns to elevation and seasonal drought in Quercus franchetii from the Yuanmou dry‑hot valley. We hypothesized that: (i) elevation and season both influence rhizosphere microbial diversity and co-occurrence patterns. (ii) Bacteria and fungi show different responses owing to their different tolerances to soil physicochemical conditions.

Materials and methods

The study sites were situated in the Yuanmou dry-hot valley, Southwestern China. Based on the natural distribution range of Q. franchetii, five elevation gradients (1200 m, 1400 m, 1600 m, 1800 m, 2100 m) were selected (Fig. 1). Field sampling was conducted in January (dry season) and August (rainy season) of 2024. Voucher specimens of Q. franchetii were deposited at the Herbarium of Southwest Forestry University and were taxonomically identified by Prof. Dacai Zhang of the same university. Their accession numbers range from SWFC0072428 to SWFC0072432. Soil and plant sampling was performed in accordance with relevant institutional, national and international ethical guidelines.

Map of sampling sites across the Yuanmou dry-hot valley, Yunnan Province, Southwest China. The base map was sourced from the Standard Map Service (GS (2024) 0650), and all provincial and county administrative boundaries remain unmodified

Three 10 × 10 m quadrats were established within each plot (Table 1), spaced at least 5 m apart from one another. Five trees with comparable diameter at breast height (DBH) were selected as target individuals inside every quadrat. As the ages of the trees could not be accurately determined in the field, DBH was used as a proxy for tree age during individual selection. Rhizosphere soil was collected from the 5–20 cm soil layer within a 50 cm radius around each sampled tree trunk. Owing to the typical rocky, sandy soils and low ambient soil moisture prevailing in the dry‑hot valley, soil particles barely adhered to fine roots, making it impossible to collect strictly root-adhering soil using the root-shaking method. Therefore, soil within 1 cm of the roots was defined as rhizosphere soil in this study, and a standardized root-proximal sampling volume was used throughout to ensure comparability across elevations and seasons. The soil samples were sealed in zip-lock bags and promptly transported to the laboratory, followed by sieving through a 40-mesh screen to eliminate fine root debris and gravel. The Soil subsamples from each quadrat were thoroughly homogenized by mass and split into two portions, yielding 60 soil samples in total. Thirty samples were stored in liquid nitrogen for high-throughput sequencing, and the remaining 30 were air‑dried prior to soil physicochemical measurement.

Determination of rhizosphere soil physicochemical properties

Soil water content (WC) was measured by the oven-drying method (105 ℃ to constant weight). Soil pH value was determined potentiometrically using a glass electrode (soil-to-water ratio of 1:2.5). SOC content was quantified via potassium dichromic oxidation-external heating method. TN was digested with sulfuric acid-accelerator followed by Kjeldahl distillation. Total phosphorus (TP) was analyzed by NaOH fusion and molybdenum-antimony anti-spectrophotometry. Available Nitrogen (AN) was measured using alkali-hydrolyzable diffusion method. Available Phosphorus (AP) was extracted with ammonium fluoride-hydrochloric acid solution and determined by Mo-Sb anti-colorimetry.

Sequencing of rhizosphere soil microbial communities

Protocols for DNA extraction, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification, and high‑throughput sequencing are provided in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Statistical analysis

The main and interactive effects of season and elevation on microbial α-diversity (Chao1 and Shannon indices) were analyzed using nonparametric ANOVA based on Aligned Rank Transform (ART). Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distances was performed to visualize shifts in microbial β-diversity across season and elevation gradients, while permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was adopted to test the statistical significance of community divergence across these two factors.

Seasonal variations in soil physicochemical properties were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and inter‑elevation variations were examined using the Kruskal‑Wallis H test, followed by Dunn’s post‑hoc test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to assess relationships between microbial α-diversity and edaphic variables, with partial correlations controlling for elevation and season. Distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities was conducted to evaluate the relationships between soil physicochemical properties and microbial community composition. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) detected in fewer than 5% of samples were discarded, and the remaining ASV data were subjected to Hellinger transformation. To mitigate multicollinearity, environmental variables with a variance inflation factor (VIF) > 10 were excluded. The significance levels of the overall model, individual explanatory variables, and constrained axes were determined using permutation tests.

Network analysis was performed to construct microbial co-occurrence networks based on the top 500 most abundant ASVs. Low-abundance ASVs (mean relative abundance < 0.01%), ASVs with zero variance, and those detected in < 20% of samples were filtered out prior to network construction. Pairwise Spearman’s correlations with |r| ≥ 0.6 and false discovery rate (FDR) -adjusted P < 0.05 were retained as edges. Topological properties, including nodes, edges, average degree, clustering coefficient, graph density, modularity, global efficiency, and natural connectivity, were calculated. Network robustness was assessed via simulated random and targeted node removal, with the relative size of the largest component recorded and the area under the curve (AUC) computed to quantify stability. Zi-Pi metrics were calculated to classify nodes as module hubs, network hubs, connectors, or peripheral nodes.

Functional prediction of bacterial communities was conducted via the Functional Annotation of Prokaryotic Taxa (FAPROTAX) database using 16 S rRNA gene sequences. Fungal ecological functions were inferred from ITS1 sequences based on the FUNGuild database, with only ASVs assigned with “probable” or “highly probable” confidence rankings retained for subsequent functional analyses.

Results

Taxonomic composition and diversity of bacterial and fungal communities

Taxonomic classification of ASVs indicated that the bacterial community was affiliated to 42 phyla, 135 classes, 321 orders, 544 families, 1109 genera and 3107 species, while the fungal community spanned 13 phyla, 56 classes, 146 orders, 381 families, 801 genera and 1288 species. The dominant bacterial phyla were Actinobacteriota, Proteobacteria, Acidobacteriota and Chloroflexi, collectively contributing more than 75% of total bacterial relative abundance across all samples (Fig. 2A). Ascomycota and Basidiomycota dominated the fungal community, accounting for over 90% of total fungal reads (Fig. 2B).

Taxonomic composition of dominant microbial taxa in the rhizosphere soil of Q. franchetii. A Relative abundance of dominant bacterial phyla. B Relative abundance of dominant fungal phyla

The ART nonparametric ANOVA indicated that the α-diversity of bacterial and fungal communities varied with elevation and season (Supplementary Table S2). Both bacterial and fungal α-diversity was consistently higher in the rainy season than in the dry season (Fig. 3). Bacterial α-diversity exhibited an approximately V-shaped pattern along the elevational gradient (Fig. 3A-B). Conversely, fungal α‑diversity generally declined with increasing elevation, apart from a minor rebound at the 2100 m site during the rainy season (Fig. 3C-D).

Responses of bacterial and fungal α-diversity to seasonal and elevational variations

PCoA revealed the distinct separation in bacterial and fungal community compositions across elevation gradients (Fig. 4). PERMANOVA further corroborated this pattern: elevation imposed a significant influence on the community assembly of both bacteria and fungi (P < 0.05), while seasonal variation exerted no statistically significant impacts (P > 0.05; Supplementary Table S3).

Bacterial (A) and fungal (B) β-diversity visualized by PCoA based on Bray-Curtis distances

Relationships between soil physicochemical properties and microbial diversity

All measured soil physicochemical properties exhibited variable across seasons and elevation gradients. WC showed a consistent trend of higher values in the rainy season across elevations. In contrast, the remaining soil variables displayed site-specific patterns along the elevational gradient (Supplementary Fig. S1; Supplementary Table S1).

Spearman’s correlation revealed that bacterial α-diversity had strong positive correlations with soil pH, whereas fungal α-diversity had mostly weak relationships with the measured soil variables (Fig. 5A-B). Correlations between soil physicochemical traits and microbial α‑diversity were generally weaker in the rainy season than in the dry season. Partial Spearman’s correlation further confirmed that pH was the dominant soil factor associated with bacterial α-diversity (Fig. 5C). By comparison, fungal α-diversity displayed weaker partial correlations with soil properties.

Relationships between soil physicochemical properties and microbial α-diversity by Spearman’s correlation (A-B) and Partial Spearman’s correlation analysis (C)

db-RDA indicated that the screened low-collinearity edaphic variables (pH and WC) collectively accounted for a fraction of the compositional variation in both bacterial and fungal communities (Fig. 6). The first two db-RDA axes explained 17.14% and 3.68% of total variation for bacteria, and 14.06% and 3.50% for fungi, respectively. Sample clustering was primarily structured by elevation, while seasonal groups exhibited partial overlap within identical elevation sites.

Dashed ellipses represent 95% confidence intervals for elevation groups. Arrows indicate the direction and relative strength of soil variables retained after VIF screening

Co-occurrence networks of bacterial and fungal communities

Co-occurrence network analysis revealed that bacterial networks were more complex than fungal counterparts across elevation gradients and seasons (Fig. 7; Supplementary Table S4). In the dry season, all networks had fewer nodes, edges, and lower average degree, yet featured a higher proportion of positive edges and longer average path lengths. Bacterial networks reached the maximum connectivity at 1600 m, while fungal and cross-kingdom bacterial-fungal networks fluctuated irregularly along the elevation gradient. Bacterial networks showed highest connectivity at 1600 m, whereas fungal and bacterial-fungal networks displayed irregular fluctuations among elevations.

Robustness analysis indicated that the rainy season networks displayed higher natural connectivity, and greater random and targeted robustness AUC values (Supplementary Fig. S3). Zi-Pi classification further illustrated that Actinobacteriota functioned as module hubs and connectors within bacterial-fungal networks in the dry season; Ascomycota served as module hubs and connectors in fungal networks, and Acidobacteriota acted as connectors in bacterial networks.

Functional prediction of rhizosphere bacterial and fungal communities

Functional annotation based on FAPROTAX revealed that chemoheterotrophy, aerobic chemoheterotrophy and cellulolysis constituted the predominant functional groups of bacterial communities (Fig. 8A; Supplementary Fig. S4). Among the predicted carbon-cycling functions, cellulolysis exhibited significantly higher predicted abundance in the dry season relative to the rainy season (P < 0.01). Conversely, two key nitrogen-cycling processes (nitrate reduction and ureolysis) were enriched in rainy-season samples (P < 0.001). According to FUNGuild classification, fungal communities were dominated by four trophic modes: symbiotroph, saprotroph-symbiotroph, pathotroph-saprotroph, and pathotroph-saprotroph-symbiotroph (Fig. 8B). The relative abundance of symbiotrophs was significantly higher in the dry season than in the rainy season (P < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. S4).

Discussion

Microbial diversity patterns across seasonal and elevational gradients

We verified that microbial diversity exhibited directional changes along the elevational gradient, and that season also played a significant role. Bacterial α-diversity exhibited an approximately V-shaped pattern along the elevational gradient. The lower values at 1600 m sites suggested that some specific environmental constraints affected by elevation strongly suppress bacterial diversity [13]. Because bacterial α-diversity was strongly correlated with pH (Fig. 5), this suggests that pH may be the primary inhibitory factor limiting bacterial richness [8]. In contrast, fungal α-diversity generally declined with increasing elevation, apart from a minor upturn at the 2100 m site. Field surveys revealed remarkable increases in plant height and species richness at the 2100 m site in this study. Accordingly, we infer that this elevated fungal diversity at high elevation is likely attributable to higher plant richness and enhanced root exudate inputs at 2100 m, which provide additional resources that facilitate fungal recruitment and symbiosis [16]. Consistently, soil physicochemical measurements further validated this speculation: the contents of SOC, AP, TP, AN, as well as the soil C/N ratio, were markedly higher at the 2100 m site than at other elevational sites (Supplementary Fig. S1). High elevation is associated with lower temperatures, but due to the strong environmental tolerance, under conditions of relatively sufficient nutrient resources, the fungal diversity does not decline but rises instead [25]. PCoA ordination further revealed clear clustering of both bacterial and fungal communities by elevation. As an integrative ecological filter, elevation selects for distinct microbial taxa through changes in soil physicochemical properties [13, 16, 26].

Seasonal fluctuation represents a critical temporal factor shaping microbial community assembly by altering soil moisture, temperature, nutrient availability, nutrient cycling rates, and plant root metabolic activity [11, 27]. Increased soil water content and higher plant root exudation in the rainy season may enhance the availability of labile carbon and nitrogen, promoting proliferation of chemoheterotrophic bacteria and symbiotrophic fungi [28, 29]. Additionally, higher soil moisture and temperature may break dormancy in quiescent fungal spores that are otherwise metabolically inactive under prolonged drought stress conditions, further facilitating fungal colonization and symbiosis [30, 31]. However, inconsistent seasonal diversity trends have been documented across tropical and ecosystems, where both bacterial and fungal richness was higher during dry seasons, suggesting that local climate and vegetation context modulate seasonal effects [8, 15].

Soil physicochemical properties effect microbial diversity and composition

Soil microbial community has developed diverse strategies to adapt to varying soil physicochemical properties, resulting in strong correlations between diversity and environmental variables [14, 32]. Among the measured factors, soil pH showed the most consistent relationship with bacterial α-diversity, even after controlling for elevation and seasonal effects. This result supports the notion that pH is a major environmental filter for bacterial community composition [19, 33]. Bacterial diversity and structure were particularly sensitive to pH fluctuations, likely due to the narrow pH ranges required for optimal growth of specific taxa. For example, Acidobacteria and Actinobacteria, as typical dominant taxa in our dataset, are classified as acid-tolerant taxa [14, 34, 35]. In contrast, fungal α-diversity weakly responsive to pH fluctuations owing to their wider pH adaptation [36], yet displayed relatively consistent covariation with TN, AP, SOC and WC. These results suggest that fungal community may be more strongly influenced by other environmental factors, including soil temperature, soil depth, vegetation, plant root exudates, microclimatic conditions, or biotic interactions [8, 14].

db-RDA further revealed that pH and WC were the key factors structuring microbial community composition, explaining a significant portion of the variation in both bacterial and fungal communities. As an ecological filter, soil pH may indirectly alter microbial functional potential by modulating community composition [37], whereas WC influences both the structure and metabolic activity of soil microbial assemblages [38, 39]. In turn, microbial communities can actively modify pH through their involvement in fundamental biogeochemical processes, such as ammonification and denitrification [21, 32]. Despite these patterns, a substantial fraction of the variation remained unexplained in the constrained ordination, indicating that unmeasured environmental variables, spatial processes, host-related effects, or complex biotic interactions also contribute to microbial community assembly.

Co-occurrence network complexity and robustness across seasonal and elevational gradients

Co-occurrence network analysis revealed that bacterial, fungal, and bacterial-fungal networks showed different complexity and robustness across seasonal and elevational gradients. At the 1600 m site, bacterial α-diversity was lowest, yet the co-occurrence network exhibited higher complexity and robustness. This phenomenon implies that, even with depleted species richness, the surviving taxa assemble into a densely interconnected, highly interactive community. Such tight associations likely arise from functional redundancy or closely coupled metabolic cooperation, which buffer community stability against environmental filtering and selective pressure [40]. The mild rise in fungal α-diversity at the 2100 m site was accompanied by increases in positive edge proportion, clustering coefficient, and average degree, along with a decrease in modularity. These features point to intensive intramodular cooperation, efficient resource partitioning and stable local subcommunity structure.

Dry-season networks had fewer nodes, edges, and lower average degree but possessed higher proportions of positive edges and longer path lengths. Seasonal drought triggers a series of environmental changes, and these environmental factors act as filters that may selectively remove taxa poorly adapted to arid conditions [41]. In contrast, drought-tolerant species tend to form selective cooperative interactions and assemble stable functional modules to sustain network integrity and functionality [42, 43]. Such structural characteristics compensate for the ecological functions lost by sensitive taxa, thereby enhancing community resistance to random environmental disturbances [40]. Robustness analysis further indicated that rainy-season networks exhibited higher natural connectivity, random and targeted robustness AUC. Adequate soil moisture and nutrient availability in the rainy season provided a favorable environment for microbial survival [14], leading to denser interspecific connections and higher network connectivity.

The keystone taxa and the main functional predictions

Zi-Pi analysis revealed that the dominant phyla Actinobacteriota, Acidobacteriota, and Ascomycota function as keystone species. These keystone taxa and their unique functional traits may underpin the patterns of network complexity and robustness across the seasonal and elevational gradients, thereby stabilizing microbial interactive networks [13, 40]. As keystone mediators of soil biogeochemical cycling, bacterial and fungal communities collectively drive nutrient mineralization, with fungi specializing in lignin degradation and bacteria in cellulose decomposition [44, 45]. Functional predictions further revealed that symbiotrophic fungal guilds and cellulolytic bacteria were enriched in the dry season. Although the overall fungal abundance declined in the dry conditions, the relative abundance of symbiotrophic fungi increased significantly. This variation can be attributed to the capacity of drought-tolerant fungal species to sustain hyphal growth, which facilitates efficient water and nutrient acquisition [46, 47], and improves local microenvironmental conditions, ultimately enhancing the functional resilience of fungal communities against drought stress [48].

In this study, we focused on divergent responses of rhizosphere microbial diversity and co-occurrence patterns to seasonal and elevational variation, as well as the regulatory mechanisms of soil physicochemical properties driving microbial community diversity and structure. We further analyzed trends in community network complexity and stability, and predicted the key taxa and their ecological functions. Nevertheless, the limited sampling scale in this study constrains the reliability and universality of the observed microbial distribution patterns and seasonal variations, which requires further validation. Moreover, climatic factors, which act as key drivers of microbial distribution, and plant root exudates, which play a central role in shaping the rhizosphere microenvironment, represent important variables that should not be overlooked. Future research should prioritize these factors, expand sampling coverage and biological replicates, and implement long-term fixed-site monitoring to comprehensively elucidate the dynamic assembly of microbial communities.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that rhizosphere bacterial and fungal communities exhibited differences in diversity, taxonomic composition, network complexity, and stability across seasonal and elevational gradients. Elevational gradients exerted a stronger influence than seasonal fluctuation in shaping the diversity and composition of rhizosphere microbial communities. Bacterial diversity was sensitive to elevation-induced environmental heterogeneity, with soil pH identified as the predominant driver governing bacterial spatial distribution. Microbial network structure displayed divergent adaptive strategies along spatiotemporal gradients: dry-season communities maintained ecological functionality through enhanced intramodular cooperation, whereas rainy-season communities relied on higher network connectivity to sustain community stability. These results provide a basis for understanding of the divergent responses of rhizosphere microbial diversity and co-occurrence patterns to elevation and season.
