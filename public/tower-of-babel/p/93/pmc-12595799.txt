Diabetes management at the grass-root level without gatekeeping: exploring the association between primary care seeking and health outcomes
Authors: Jiaqian Sun, Sumit Kane, Weijun Zhang, Gaofeng Zhang, Yongsong Luo, Jiayan Huang, Yin Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn countries without a gatekeeping system, including China, promoting primary healthcare (PHC) utilization can improve system efficiency and reduce healthcare burden. However, evidence on diabetes patients’ PHC utilization and its impact on health outcomes remains limited. This study employs continuity of care (COC) to examine care-seeking behavior at PHC institutions in China and assess its association with clinical outcomes and healthcare costs.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study in Yuhuan, Zhejiang Province, China, including 3,672 patients newly diagnosed with diabetes between 2016 and 2019. Data from chronic disease management records, follow-up service records, and electronic medical records from 2020 to 2023 were linked. COC was assessed from 2020 to 2022 using standard measures including the Continuity of Care Index (COCI), Usual Provider of Care (UPC), and Sequential Continuity (SECON), as well as PHC-specific measures: the Primary Healthcare Index (PHCI) and a binary indicator for having the primary healthcare institution as the usual provider of care (PHC-UPC). We evaluated clinical outcomes (hospitalization, glycemic control) and healthcare costs in 2023. Logistic and linear regression models were used to assess association, adjusting for patient demographics and clinical characteristics.ResultsThe mean PHCI was 0.73. Higher PHCI was significantly associated with decreased outpatient costs (P < 0.001), decreased inpatient costs (P < 0.001), and a lower likelihood of hospitalization (OR = 0.503, P < 0.001). Similarly, having used a PHC-UPC was associated with reduced outpatient and inpatient costs (P < 0.001) and a lower likelihood of hospitalization (OR = 0.708, P < 0.001). However, among patients with poorly controlled diabetes, neither PHCI nor having used a PHC-UPC showed a positive association with glycemic control (OR = 0.496, P = 0.003; OR = 0.629, P = 0.002).ConclusionsContinuity at PHC significantly lowers hospitalization and healthcare costs in China’s non-gatekeeping system. However, no significant improvement in glycemic control among poorly controlled diabetes was observed. Strengthening PHC capacity for personalized diabetes management and timely hospital referrals is essential to optimize outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-025-03058-8.

Introduction

The global prevalence of diabetes continues to rise, posing one of the most significant public health challenges of our time. As of 2022, an estimated 828 million adults aged 18 and older were living with diabetes—nearly double the number reported in 1990 [1]. Faced with such a growing burden of diabetes, healthcare systems around the world are under tremendous pressure to respond effectively. To address this challenge, the World Health Organization (WHO) has emphasized Primary Health Care (PHC) as the most appropriate and equitable framework for delivering personalized healthcare interventions for diabetes care [2].

Guiding diabetes patients to seek care at the PHC level has thus become a widely accepted global strategy. In countries with established gatekeeping systems, the majority of basic healthcare needs of patients with diabetes can typically be addressed at the PHC level [3]. Gatekeeping systems require patients to initially seek care at PHC institutions, where general practitioners (GPs) or family physicians provide initial diagnosis and treatment [4, 5] and refer patients to high-level facilities, such as hospitals for more advanced care only when necessary [6]. Often integrated with a family doctor model, this approach facilitates continuity of care (COC), in which family physicians deliver basic medical care and follow-up services while also monitoring patients’ health status and lifestyle behaviors [7].

However, many low- and middle-income countries have not yet established a fully implemented gatekeeping system [8]. For example, in China, although the basic medical insurance system has extensive coverage, ensuring that most residents have access to basic healthcare services, a nationwide gatekeeping system has not been implemented [9, 10]. Patients are generally free to bypass PHC institutions and seek care directly at secondary or tertiary hospitals without undergoing initial screening by PHC providers [11]. In this context, strengthening the capacity and attractiveness of PHC institutions has become a key policy lever to guide patients, particularly those with chronic diseases, towards local care. Recent reform, such as the hierarchical healthcare system and expanded family doctor contract services, has gradually enhanced PHC service capabilities [12, 13]. Yet, it remains unclear whether these efforts have effectively redirected patients with diabetes to PHC institutions.

Existing studies have examined the healthcare-seeking behaviors among diabetes patients in China, often using the Continuity of Care (COC) index as an evaluation metric [14, 15]. COC refers to the experience where a series of discrete healthcare seeking events are treated as being coherent, connected, and consistent with the patient’s medical needs and personal context [16]. While these studies have found that higher COC is associated with improved health outcomes, few studies have specifically assessed the role of PHC institutions in ensuring continuity or explored patients’ actual care preference at the primary level. Given the central role of PHC in chronic disease management, evaluating continuity specifically at the PHC level can better reflect the effectiveness of primary care-oriented interventions.

This study was conducted in Yuhuan, a county-level city in southeastern Zhejiang Province, China. In 2024, Yuhuan had a population of approximately 631,000 residents and a per capita GDP of 17,546 USD, placing its economic development above the national average. Yuhuan city comprises economically advanced urban areas, mountain-rural areas, and relatively remote island areas. This geographical situation limits some residents’ access to high-level hospitals. Given these geographical and service disparities, strengthening primary health care capacity is essential to ensure that all residents can receive basic medical services.

To support coordinated primary care across the city, Yuhuan has established an electronic health record (EHR) platform. The platform captures all residents’ healthcare encounters, whether reimbursed by insurance or paid out-of-pocket. It integrates clinical records from all medical institutions and links them with population registration data from the public security system. Information from the platform can be shared across different levels of healthcare institutions.

Against this backdrop, Yuhuan provides an ideal setting to examine how diabetes patients seek care at PHC institutions. This study aims to fill that gap by analyzing whether diabetes patients in China are seeking care at PHC institutions and examining whether their healthcare-seeking behavior at the primary level affects clinical outcomes and healthcare costs. Using COC as a centric metric, this study also provides policy implications for improving the appeal and function of PHC institutions for diabetes patients in the absence of formal gatekeeping systems.

Methods

Sample and data source

Data were primarily drawn from three sources within the electronic health record (EHR) system in Yuhuan City: the chronic disease management database, chronic disease follow-up records, and electronic medical records. Patients newly diagnosed with diabetes between 2016 and 2019 were first identified through the chronic disease management database based on their recorded diagnosis dates. The chronic disease management system in Yuhuan covers all residents diagnosed with diabetes, ensuring complete population capture. Using unique national identification numbers, patients’ follow-up information from 2020 to 2023 was retrieved by linking to the chronic disease follow-up records. In addition, electronic medical records were collected to obtain detailed information on patients’ demographics, outpatient and inpatient visits, and healthcare costs. Importantly, the medical record system encompasses all visits regardless of insurance reimbursement status, and includes out-of-pocket claims from insured patients.

Measurement

Independent variables

To assess whether patients with diabetes consistently sought care within PHC institutions, we combined commonly used COC indicators with refined measures specific to PHC. COC was assessed over a three-year period from 2020 to 2022. Three widely used COC indicators were selected to measure patients’ overall continuity of care: the Continuity of Care Index (COCI), which measures the dispersion of visits across providers; the Usual Provider of Care (UPC), reflecting the proportion of visits to the main provider; and Sequential Continuity (SECON), which considers the sequence of consecutive visits to the same provider [17, 18].

Building on UPC, we introduced two additional indicators focused on continuity within PHC. The Primary Healthcare Index (PHCI) is a continuous variable representing the proportion of a patient’s healthcare visits occurring at PHC institutions. (Table 1) In this study, PHC institutions were defined as 10 township health centers and 175 village clinics in Yuhuan City, excluding 4 public hospitals. The second indicator, PHC-UPC (Primary Healthcare Institutions as Usual Provider of Care), is a binary variable indicating whether the healthcare institution most frequently visited by the patient was a PHC institution. The binary PHC-UPC captures patients’ preference or reliance on PHC as their usual provider, while the continuous PHCI captures the degree of engagement with PHC services. Together, these complementary indicators allow a nuanced assessment of PHC-specific continuity of care.

All continuous COC indicators range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating better continuity of care. The PHC-UPC variable is binary, coded as 1 if the usual provider is a PHC institution, and 0 otherwise.

Outcome variables

To assess whether patients’ consistent use of PHC institutions was associated with positive health outcomes, clinical outcomes and healthcare costs for patients in 2023 were measured. Clinical outcomes were represented by two binary variables: hospitalization and glycemic control. The hospitalization variable was coded as 1 if the patient experienced at least one all-purpose hospitalization during the year. The patient’s average fasting blood glucose (FBG) in 2023 was calculated from all follow-up visits during that year. Each patient underwent at least one routine annual health examination, and additional follow-up visits were scheduled according to disease severity. The glycemic control variable was coded as 1 if this mean FBG fell within the normal range of 4–7 mmol/L. Values outside this range were coded as 0, which was defined as poorly controlled diabetes. Healthcare costs were divided into outpatient and inpatient costs, which were further subdivided into insurance re-imbursement costs and out-of-pocket costs. All costs were converted from Chinese Yuan (CNY) to US dollars (USD) using the 2023 average official exchange rate of 1 USD = 7.08 CNY, based on World Bank data (Indicator code: PA.NUS.FCRF).

Covariates

We addressed confounding by incorporating patient-level characteristics measured between 2020 and 2022. These included characteristics such as age at the end of 2022, sex, type of resident, type of medical insurance, number of outpatient encounters, diabetes duration, and glycemic control. Residents of Yuhuan were classified by residence type: those living in Yucheng, Damaiyu, and Kanmen sub-districts were defined as urban, while residents of rural towns or remote island areas were classified as rural. In China, individuals with stable employment are usually covered by the Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance. Other groups, such as children, the elderly, and unemployed residents, are covered by the Resident Basic Medical Insurance.

The annual average number of all-level outpatient encounters was calculated for the period from 2020 to 2022. Outpatient visits were categorized into three groups. The cut-off of 12 visits per year corresponds to the minimum frequency implied by the local health insurance policy, which typically allows a one-month prescription supply. To further distinguish high utilizers, we defined ≥ 21 visits per year based on the 75th percentile of the study population. Patients with 12–20 visits were grouped as an intermediate category.

In the absence of HbA1c data, which is a more standard indicator of glycemic control, poorly controlled diabetes was defined using average FBG values. The average FBG values were calculated as the mean of all FBG measurements recorded for each patient between 2020 and 2022. Patients with average FBG > 7 mmol/L or < 4 mmol/L were categorized as poorly controlled. Diabetes duration was defined as the time from the initial diagnosis of diabetes (between 2016 and 2019) to the year 2023.

Statistical analysis

We conducted descriptive and bivariate analyses to assess patients’ COC and individual characteristics from 2020 to 2022. Continuous COC variables were compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the binary PHC-UPC variable was compared using the chi-square test. P-values for multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni procedure. To assess the temporality within the relationship, we then examined the association between COC over these three years and patient outcomes in 2023.

Logistic regression was used to evaluate the associations between COC and health outcomes: hospitalization and glycemic control. Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression was applied to assess the relationship between COC and healthcare costs.

We also conducted subgroup analyses to examine whether these associations varied across different population groups. Subgroups were defined based on age (< 60, 60–69, ≥ 70), sex (male, female), residence type (urban, rural), medical insurance type (Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance, Resident Basic Medical Insurance), number of overall outpatient encounters (< 12, 12–20, ≥ 21), and glycemic control (good, poor). All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 17.0.

Results

Population statistics and bivariate analysis results

A total of 3672 patients were included in the cohort, with an average age of 65.4 years. Of the patients, 1,808 (49.2%) lived in urban areas and 1,864 (50.8%) lived in rural areas. The distribution of outpatient encounters was as follows: 1012 patients (27.6%) had < 12 encounters, 1729 patients (47.1%) had 12 ~ 20 encounters, and 932 patients (25.4%) had ≥ 21 encounters. The average diabetes duration for patients was 5.41 years. (Table 2) From 2020 to 2022, 1848 patients (50.3%) had poor glycemic control. The mean values of COCI, SECON, UPC, and PHCI were 0.54, 0.63, 0.67 and 0.73. A majority of patients (79.5%) most frequently visited PHC institutions. In 2023, 842 patients (22.9%) were hospitalized, and 1989 patients (54.2%) had poor glycemic control. The mean outpatient costs for all patients were 473.95 USD, while the mean inpatient costs were 313.00 USD.

Overall, continuity of care varied significantly across demographic subgroups. Younger patients consistently demonstrated lower continuity across all measures, and female patients also exhibited lower COCI (P = 0.001), SECON (P = 0.001), and UPC (P = 0.009). High outpatient encounters were associated with lower continuity (P < 0.001). Notable patterns emerged in PHC–related continuity. Female patients, residents covered by Resident Basic Medical Insurance, and those living in rural areas all showed higher PHCI (P = 0.011 and both P < 0.001) and were more likely to use PHC-UPC (P = 0.020 and both P < 0.001). In addition, patients with poor glycemic control demonstrated higher PHCI (P = 0.009) and more frequent use of PHC-UPC (P < 0.001), suggesting stronger engagement with primary care among these groups. The association between continuity and clinical outcomes

Based on the logistic regression analysis, higher continuity of care in the first three years was associated with reduced risk of hospitalization in the fourth year. Both COCI (OR = 0.653, P = 0.039) and PHCI (OR = 0.503, P < 0.001) were significantly linked to this reduced risk. In addition, patients who consistently used a PHC-UPC during the first three years were also less likely to be hospitalized in the fourth year (OR = 0.708, P < 0.001), suggesting that maintaining continuity of PHC may be linked to reduced hospital admissions. The association between continuity and healthcare costs

Higher continuity of care at the primary health care level was associated with lower outpatient costs in the fourth year (Table 6). Specifically, PHCI (P < 0.001) and consistent use of a PHC-UPC (P < 0.001) were linked to reductions in total outpatient and out-of-pocket costs, while no significant association was observed for reimbursed costs. Every 0.1 unit increase in PHCI corresponded to a reduction of 57.21 USD in total outpatient costs and 55.04 USD in out-of-pocket costs. Similarly, patients who used a PHC-UPC had 299.08 USD lower total outpatient costs and 300.02 USD lower out-of-pocket costs compared with those who did not. (Table 6) These patterns were generally consistent in subgroup analyses, indicating that higher continuity of primary health care was associated with lower overall and out-of-pocket outpatient costs.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that in Yuhuan, patients with diabetes are actively utilizing PHC institutions for care. While previous studies have largely focused on the benefits of continuity across all levels of care [19–23], our study differentiates continuity across levels of care, and examines the use of services at PHC level for its impact on clinical outcomes and healthcare costs. Notably, patients who consistently used/received care at PHC institutions experienced fewer hospitalizations and lower healthcare costs. However, this pattern was not consistently associated with improved glycemic control.

The Yuhuan model emphasizes enhancing PHC capacity to attract diabetic patients

Globally, various initiatives have been implemented to strengthen PHC services. For example, Thailand has promoted primary care through its Universal Health Coverage (UHC) strategy, which emphasizes access and financial protection [24]. Brazil has expanded primary health care via the Family Health Strategy, focusing on community-based teams [25]. In contrast, the Yuhuan model is novel in the sense that unlike traditional models of gatekeeping which tend to exclusively rely on mandates, rationing, and restriction of services by level of care, the model explicitly focuses on enhancing the attractiveness of primary care level services.

In Yuhuan, the most effective capacity-building measure has been the development of an EHR-based information platform. The information platform enables real-time sharing of patient records, clinical guidance, and technical support across hospitals and primary care facilities, thereby enhancing the service capacity and continuity of care at the primary level [26, 27]. Actually, existing studies have implied that prioritizing the development of information platforms can strengthen primary care [28, 29]. Our study highlights a similar finding. Moreover, digital health has been the priority development even in the developing countries, like Malawi, Tanzania, Zimbabwe [30–32], especially after the COVID pandemic. By leveraging information technology, resource-limited regions can access external expertise and clinical guidance [33]. This helps compensate for limited human resources by improving the efficiency of healthcare staff [34]. Such initiatives, whether locally driven or externally supported, provide a feasible pathway to strengthen primary care even under resource constraints [35].

Higher continuity at PHC is linked to lower costs and fewer hospitalizations

Our findings suggest that higher overall COC at the PHC level is associated with reduced outpatient and inpatient costs, as well as lower hospitalization rates. PHC institutions in Yuhuan focus on the basic management of chronic diseases and offer higher medical insurance reimbursement rates compared to higher-level hospitals [36, 37]. Therefore, seeking care at PHC institutions can significantly reduce healthcare costs, particularly the out-of-pocket costs, thereby directly lowering the financial burden on patients [38].

In addition, family doctors at PHC institutions can continuously monitor the health status of chronic disease patients over the long term. Compared to hospital doctors, family doctors often develop closer relationships with their patients, enabling them to detect changes in health conditions more promptly [39]. This timely identification helps reduce the risk of disease deterioration, such as lowering the likelihood of hospitalization. Since hospitalization is a common indicator of worsening health, reducing hospital admissions reflects improved patient outcomes [40].

Continuity at PHC has limited impact on glycemic control among patients with poorly controlled diabetes

Although seeking care at PHC institutions was associated with several benefits, consistent improvements in glycemic control were not observed, particularly among patients with poorly controlled diabetes. Previous longitudinal studies have reported that fewer than 10% of patients with baseline HbA1c ≥ 10% reach target levels even after three months of intensive treatment. This suggests that clinical complexity, such as impaired β-cell function and severe insulin resistance, may limit treatment effectiveness regardless of care continuity [41]. Together, these challenges may create a “ceiling effect”, whereby improvements from PHC interventions alone are insufficient to reverse glycemic dysregulation in this subgroup.

In addition, patients with more severe or unstable diabetes are more likely to seek frequent care at PHC institutions, which is reflected in a high PHCI. These patients often have various complications in addition to poor glycemic control, leading them to visit PHC repeatedly. Because of the complexity of their diabetes, they require more targeted follow-up services [42]. Regular access to PHC allows family physicians to closely monitor their condition and provide timely referrals to support treatment [43]. Building on these services, family physicians could also adopt more personalized approaches to better address the needs of patients with complex diabetes.

Limitation

This study has the following limitations. First, due to data constraints, the calculation of care continuity was done at the institutional level rather than the physician level, which may have led to an overestimation of continuity. Second, poorly controlled diabetes is typically defined based on HbA1c, as it provides a more stable and authoritative measure of long-term glycemic control compared to average FBG levels. However, due to limitations in data availability and testing convenience, most Yuhuan patients’ electronic health records lacked HbA1c measurements. Using the annual mean FBG in 2023 may have misclassified some patients’ glycemic status. Consequently, patients labeled as ‘poorly controlled’ based on FBG might actually have had stable long-term control, potentially obscuring associations between PHC continuity and glycemic outcomes.

Conclusion

This study highlights the critical role of PHC services in managing chronic conditions such as diabetes. Using the Yuhuan City as a case study, our findings demonstrate that strengthening PHC capacity can increase patient engagement at the primary care level, reduce hospitalization rates, and lower healthcare costs. These results underscore the potential of investment in PHC - not through restrictive gatekeeping, but by enhancing service quality, accessibility, and COC- to improve health system performance in settings without formal gatekeeping mechanisms.
