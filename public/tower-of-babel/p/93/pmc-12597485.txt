Revisiting the Role of Interleukin-6 in Predicting Stroke and Cardiovascular Events
Authors: Kaori Miwa
Published: 2025

See article vol. 32: 1390-1399

Despite advances in contemporary preventive therapies, cardiovascular events remain the leading cause of mortality and morbidity. Therefore, effective strategies to reduce this residual risk are urgently needed. There is growing evidence that inflammation plays a key role in the development of atherosclerosis, thrombosis, and cerebral small-vessel disease (SVD), contributing to both initial and recurrent cardiovascular events 1 , 2) .

Observational studies have shown that blood inflammatory markers, such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), are associated with cardiovascular risk 3 , 4) . CRP is a nonspecific acute-phase reactant that is released mainly from hepatocytes in response to the expression of IL-6 1) . While genetic studies suggest CRP is unlikely to play a causal role in atherogenesis and should be considered a marker of inflammation, IL-6 appears to play a more direct pathogenic role. Mendelian randomization studies of a functional polymorphism (Asp358Ala) in the IL-6 receptor (IL6-R) have demonstrated that reduced IL-6 signaling, through increased shedding of membrane-bound IL6-R and consequent attenuation of IL-6 activity on leukocytes and hepatocytes, is associated with a decreased risk of coronary artery disease 5) . In two large consortia, each inherited copy of the 358Ala allele was associated with a 3.4–5% lower risk of coronary events 5) . Similarly, the MEGASTROKE consortium reported that genetic variants associated with reduced IL-6 signaling were linked to an 11% lower risk of all ischemic stroke, a 24% lower risk of large artery stroke, and 29% lower risk of lacunar stroke, but not cardioembolic stroke 6) . These findings support the hypothesis that lifelong reduction of IL-6 signaling confers protection against cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events.

Kitagawa has conducted extensive research on the clinical significance of inflammatory markers in relation to cerebrovascular disease over the years. Data remain limited in patients with established vascular risk or covert vascular brain injury, such as SVD or intra-/extracranial arterial stenosis. In this issue of JAT, Kitagawa et al. provide important insights into the prognostic significance of serum IL-6 levels in patients with vascular risk or SVD (n = 471) 7) . This study provided important validation of the prognostic value of IL-6. Patients in the highest IL-6 tertile (＞2.5 pg/mL) had significantly higher risks of stroke (adjusted hazard ratio [HR], 4.84; 95% CI, 1.02–23.05) and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) (HR, 3.68; 95% CI, 1.01–13.51) than those in the lowest tertile, independent of vascular risk factors and SVD-findings. In contrast, IL-6 levels were not associated with the risk of Alzheimer disease dementia.

These findings reinforce the role of inflammation as a critical risk factor for vascular events in this population. Although this study did not stratify IL-6 associations by specific subtypes of MACE or stroke, previous studies have shown that inflammation contributes to the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis, atrial fibrillation, coagulation activation, and vascular recurrence after lacunar stroke 1) . A recent meta-analysis demonstrated that IL-6, but not hsCRP, is associated with recurrent stroke due to large artery atherosclerosis, small vessel occlusion, and cryptogenic stroke 8) .

Clarifying the prognostic value of inflammatory biomarkers is essential for identifying high-risk patients in both clinical practice and randomized trials. Although current stroke guidelines do not recommend routine measurement of inflammatory markers, this study supports the notion that IL-6 measurement may help identify individuals at the highest risk of vascular recurrence. Furthermore, the findings support the rationale for clinical trials of anti-inflammatory therapies to prevent MACE. Recent randomized controlled trials in coronary artery disease have shown that anti-inflammatory agents such as canakinumab (an IL-1 antagonist) and colchicine reduce residual vascular risk 9 , 10) . The Colchicine for Prevention of Vascular Inflammation in Non-Cardioembolic Stroke (CONVINCE) trial did not demonstrate a statistically significant benefit for the primary outcome (HR 0.84; 0.68–1.05) ; however, in a prespecified on-treatment analysis, low-dose colchicine reduced the risk of MACE by 20% (HR 0.80; 0.63–0.99) 10) . These results suggest that future trials should adopt more selective eligibility criteria to target patients most likely to benefit from anti-inflammatory therapies. In this context, IL-6 may serve as a promising biomarker for guiding patient selection.

In conclusion, given the established prognostic relevance of IL-6, further efforts should focus on its clinical implementation to enhance risk stratification and guide individualized anti-inflammatory strategies for the secondary prevention of stroke and cardiovascular disease.
