Microbiological profile of sterile site organisms and culture positivity rates from a decade of paediatric admissions at a tertiary hospital in South Africa: A lower and middle-income country perspective
Authors: Prenika Jaglal, Khine Swe-Swe Han, Colin Menezes, Sithembiso Velaphi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSterile site (SS (blood and cerebrospinal fluid)) infections are formidable causes of childhood morbidity and fatality. This study aimed to determine aetiological organisms, antimicrobial resistance patterns and sepsis trend rates in paediatric inpatients from a low-and middle-income country.MethodsA retrospective review of SS culture results of inpatients from the neonatal unit (NNU), general paediatric (GPW) and paediatric haematology-oncology wards (PHOW) at Chris Hani Baragwanath Academic Hospital, South Africa, from January 2013 to December 2022.ResultsA total of 58 053 SS specimens were submitted for analysis. Culture-positivity rate was 36.0 %, comprising 22.8 % and 13.2 % for pathogens and contaminants. Pathogen culture-positivity rate was higher in the NNU when compared to GPW (35.5 % vs 17.6 %; OR–5.38; 95 % CI 4.99–5.81) but lower than PHOW (35.5 % vs 46.6 %; OR-0.47; 95 % CI 0.39–0.52). Infection rates per 1000 patient-days increased from 1.74 in 2013 to 3.11 in 2022. Gram-negative bacteria (GNB) (66.0 % vs 18.7 %, OR-5.70; 95 % CI 5.23–6.19) and yeast (12.4 % vs 8.4 %, OR-7.66; 95 % CI 6.13–9.57) were more common in NNU than GPW. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogen prevalence was 35.1 % with extended-spectrum beta-lactamase Enterobacterales contributing 40–50 %. Carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter species increased from 40 % to 60 % from 2013 to 2022 (p = 0.004). MRSA prevalence decreased from 30 % to 10 % from 2013 to 2022 (p = 0.004).ConclusionThe burden of paediatric MDR gram-negative SS infection was relatively high requiring ongoing surveillance, judicious antimicrobial use, while reinforcing infection control contact-based transmission precautions. Further studies on drivers of antimicrobial resistance such as unit specific antimicrobial consumption rates and prescriber practices, should be explored.Keywords: Microbiological profile, Multidrug-resistant organisms, Sterile site pathogens, Paediatric population, Low and middle-income country

Introduction

Worldwide, sepsis is a prominent cause of neonatal and childhood ailment [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) report in 2020, almost half of all sepsis occurred in children younger than 5 years of age with an incidence of 1.2 million cases annually and an associated mortality rate of approximately 20 % [2,3]. Pathogen prevalence differs with gram-negative bacteria (GNB) being predominant in low and middle-income countries (LMIC), as compared to a gram-positive predominance in high-income countries [4]. Aetiological organisms of sepsis tend to vary geographically and temporally [2]. An international review article focusing on neonatal and paediatric sepsis expressed the burden of Group B Streptococcus (GBS), Staphylococcus aureus, Enterobacterales and Candida species (spp) as frequently encountered pathogens [5]. At a large South African quaternary public hospital's paediatric unit, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter spp and Escherichia coli were the common GNB isolated, while coagulase negative staphylococci (CoNS), S. aureus, Enterococcus faecalis and GBS were the common gram-positive bacteria (GPB) cultured [6].

Global sepsis surveillance has noted a significant multidrug resistant organism (MDRO) uprise [7]. A prospective multi-country observational study revealed multidrug-resistant (MDR) Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae as well as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) as major pathogens in neonatal sepsis [8]. European MDR paediatric infection cases represent 30 % of their total sepsis cases [9]. More than half of the neonatal sepsis and meningitis reported in Sub-Saharan Africa have been attributed to antimicrobial resistant bacteria, a critical health threat in LMICs with a formidable pathogen predominance of MDR Acinetobacter spp, MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa, extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) producing and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), however, a relatively high incidence of MRSA and vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) still exist in paediatric oncology units and intensive care units (ICUs) [4,5,[9], [10], [11]]. The WHO has published a “priority status'’ pathogen list for which new antimicrobial development is urgently needed comprising Enterococcus faecium, S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp (ESKAPE) pathogens [12]. The National Institute for Communicable Diseases published a review in 2021 on antimicrobial resistance (AMR) surveillance highlighting the rise in ESBL producing K. pneumoniae (70 %), carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (40 %), as well as carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (80 %) from bloodstream infection (BSI) in South Africa [13].

Research on the burden of sepsis amongst the general paediatric, neonatal and paediatric haematology-oncology units from LMICs is crucial to inform on prevention, diagnosis and management strategies [7]. Combatting multidrug-resistant (MDR) infections in pediatric intensive care units (PICUs) in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in South Africa remains a challenge [14,15]. This study aimed to determine organisms isolated in blood and cerebrospinal (CSF) samples and their AMR profile in children admitted to a public tertiary hospital over a ten-year period, and to assess the contribution of different paediatric populations to the overall affliction of sepsis.

Methodology

Study population and design

This was a descriptive retrospective review of blood and CSF culture results from paediatric inpatients at the neonatal unit (NNU), general paediatric wards (GPW) and paediatric haematology-oncology wards (PHOW) from January 2013 to December 2022. All paediatric patients who had blood and CSF culture results recorded in the National Health Laboratory Services (NHLS) database with ages 14 years and below, were included in this study.

Study setting

This study was conducted in the Department of Paediatrics at Chris Hani Baragwanath Academic Hospital (CHBAH), the largest public tertiary healthcare facility in Africa situated in Soweto, Johannesburg, South Africa. The Department of Paediatrics is divided into GPW comprising of four wards, two PHOW, and a NNU comprising of five neonatal wards. There are 241 beds in GPW, 47 in PHOW and 185 in the NNU. There are approximately 10 500 children (including neonates admitted from the community) hospitalized annually in GPW, 9000 in PHOW, and about 4000 (mainly preterm infants and those requiring admission soon after delivery) in the NNU.

All patients admitted to paediatric wards with symptoms and/or signs suggestive of infection have blood and/or CSF specimens taken and sent to the NHLS Microbiology laboratory for microscopy, culture and susceptibility testing as part of sepsis screen. In the laboratory, blood culture (BC) specimens are loaded into an automated continuous monitoring system (BacT/Alert® system, bioMerieux, Marcy l'Etoile, France). Once flagging positive, the BC is then processed according to standard operating procedures (SOPs). BC and CSF specimens are processed 24 h a day. All cultures are processed using the Vitek 2 system® (bioMérieux) and API® (bioMérieux) according to SOPs for microorganism identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing as recommended by the current version of the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [16].

Data collection and analysis

Data on all blood and CSF samples sent for microbiological analysis from January 2013 to December 2022 was retrieved from the NHLS database, the Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW). These included both negative and positive cultures, types and nomenclature of organisms as well as their AMR patterns. All positive cultures isolating the same pathogen (bacteria or yeast) from the same patient, within 14 days of each other, were considered as a single episode of infection. We had not analysed as to how many blood cultures had indeed the same organism isolated repeatedly within the 14-day period. None of the records were excluded as all the data that was requested from the CDW had admission wards. Annual paediatric admission data was obtained from the Department of Paediatrics.

Organisms were considered to be MDR if resistant to at least one antimicrobial agent in three or more antibiotic categories [17]. Enterobacterales resistant to third-generation cephalosporins were described as ESBL-producing, while those GNBs intermediate or resistant to any of the carbapenems were described as carbapenem-resistant (CR) i.e. CR Acinetobacter spp (CRAS), CR Enterobacterales (CRE) and CR P. aeruginosa (CRPA). For GPB, staphylococci and enterococci were grouped as MRSA and VRE based on the positive cefoxitin screen (inferring resistance to cloxacillin) and vancomycin resistance. Bacteria were categorized as contaminants/commensals using the Centers for Disease Control (CDC)/National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) criteria [18]. Thus the following organisms: diphtheroid (Corynebacterium spp, not C. diphtheria), Bacillus spp (not B. anthracis), CoNS, Viridans group streptococci and Micrococcus spp were considered contaminants or common commensal microorganisms in general [18]. According to the CDC/NHSN Bloodstream Infection Event document, a commensal may be a cause of bloodstream infection if the following apply: A patient of any age exhibits at least one of the following signs or symptoms: fever (greater than 38.0 °C), chills, or hypotension, and the organism(s) identified in their blood culture are not related to an infection at another site. Additionally, the same NHSN-defined common commensal organism must be identified by culture from at least two separate blood specimens collected on different occasions. The criteria elements must occur within a seven-day Infection Window Period (IWP), which includes the date of the positive blood specimen, the three calendar days before, and the three days after. Importantly, at least one clinical sign or symptom (fever, chills, or hypotension) must be present to meet the criterion and occurs within the IWP. In our study, unfortunately clinical parameters were not analysed hence it is not known if the commensals were true pathogens [18].

Organisms were grouped into GNB, GPB and yeast for the individual paediatric units (GPW, NNU and PHOW). The annual sepsis rates were estimates of frequency and proportions with results stratified according to the paediatric units. Pathogen and contaminant culture positivity rates were calculated by dividing pathogen and contaminants by the total number of paediatric BC or CSF isolates per year. BSI and meningitis rates were calculated by dividing the total number of paediatric BC or CSF isolates per year by the annual patient admission days. Sterile site (SS) organisms isolated annually over the decade from all paediatric wards were summarized using frequency and percentages. The sepsis trend rates and MDRO trends were displayed using line graphs for each unit over time. Stata® Statistical Software version 18.0 IC (College Station, TX: StataCorp LP) and Microsoft® Excel 2016 were used for statistical analysis. Comparison of organism (GNB, GPB, yeast) culture positivity rates amongst the different unit types were performed using logistic regression. Results were reported as odd ratio and 95 % confidence interval. The Z-proportion test was used to compare proportions with statistical significance set at 5 %.

Ethical considerations

Ethics approval to conduct the study was obtained from the University of the Witwatersrand Human Research Ethics Committee, clearance certificate number: M220718. Patient consent was waived as all patient identifiers were removed and data was anonymised.

Results

Paediatric sterile site culture positivity rates

A total of 58 053 BC and CSF culture results were retrieved from the NHLS microbiology database for the ten-year period with 46 % (n = 26877) from the NNU, 42 % (n = 24483) from GPW and 12 % (n = 6693) from PHOW. Overall, the culture positivity rate from all SS was 36 % (n = 20923), with contribution of 22.8 % from pathogens and 13.2 % from contaminants (Fig. 1). BC specimens accounted for the majority of the samples (61.6 %; 35694/58053) with CSF accounting for 38.4 % (n = 22295). For all wards combined, the BC positivity rate due to pathogens was 28.3 % and that due to contaminants was 21.3 %, and in CSF it was 14.0 % and 0.3 % due to pathogens and contaminants, respectively. GPW culture positivity rate due to pathogens and contaminants in blood was 17.6 % and 18.5 % and in the CSF was 24.3 % and 0.3 %, respectively (Fig. S2). Neonatal culture positivity rate from blood due to pathogens and contaminants was 18.4 % and 35.5 %, while in CSF it was 8.5 % and 0.3 %, respectively (Fig. S3). PHOW culture positivity rate from blood due to pathogens and contaminants was 46.5 % and 28.8 %, and in CSF was 5.8 % and 0.1 %, respectively (Fig. S4). The contaminants from BC and CSF were excluded from further analysis.

Culture positivity rates in sterile sites from admissions in all Paediatric Wards.

The incidence of infection in paediatric patients from all wards combined, based on number of isolates per 1000 patient-days increased from 1.74 in 2013 to a peak of 4.09 in 2019 with a slight decrease to 3.11 in 2022 (Table 1, Fig. 5). Overall, the increase in the trend of incidence of sepsis was statistically significant for all paediatric wards combined (p < 0.0001), for GPW (p = 0.021) and PHOW (p = 0.033), except for NNU (p = 0.110). The incidence of infection in the NNU was relatively higher at 7.68–10.70 isolates per 1000 patient-days when compared to GPW at 0.84–3.07 isolates per 1000 patient-days, and PHOW at 1.42–2.49 isolates per 1000 patient-days.

Predominant pathogens from paediatric sterile sites

Predominant bacteria and yeast isolated from blood and CSF included GNB (Enterobacterales and non-Enterobacterales GNB), GPB (staphylococci and enterococci) as well as yeast (Candida spp). Pathogenic SS GNB, GPB and yeast contributed to 49 % (6496/13239), 43 % (5693/13239) and 8 % (1050/13239) during the study period (Table 3). K. pneumoniae (26 %; 1685/6496) (increase in 0.04 [95 % CI, 0.02–0.06]; p < 0.0001) bloodstream isolates yearly), Acinetobacter spp (25 %; 1597/6496) (increase in 0.06 [95 % CI, 03–0.08]; p < 0.0001) bloodstream isolates yearly) and S. aureus (27 %; 1531/5693) were the predominant bacterial pathogens isolated, with C. parapsilosis (41 %; 433/1050) and C. albicans (32 %; 333/1050) representing the predominant yeast cultured (Table S4).

S. aureus, Enterococcus faecium and Enterococcus faecalis were the predominant GPB observed in all units contributing 27 %, 18 % and 13 % (Table 5). The majority of GBS isolated were from GPW (63 %; 163/257) when compared to NNU (36 %; 92/257) and PHOW (1 %; 2/257). C. parapsilosis represented the predominant yeast in the NNU (44 %; 356/811) and PHOW (41 %; 62/151), whereas C. albicans dominated in the GPW (59 %; 52/88). Antimicrobial resistance spectrum of organisms isolated from paediatric sterile sites

Among the paediatric GNB MDROs; ESBL, CRE, CRPA and CRAS dominated during the study period (Fig. 6, Figs. S7–S9). Ten-year trend analysis revealed >50 % increase in ESBLs (p < 0.0001) from all paediatric wards. CRAS was isolated consistently at a yearly rate of ≥95 % from the NNU. High rates of CRE have been observed in the PHOW at a frequency of 15 %–20 % per year. General paediatric and neonatal CRPA rates fluctuated throughout the decade with ≤50 % of P. aeruginosa being reported as CR annually.

MDRO trend analysis, all Paediatric Wards.

Ten-year pooled MRSA and VRE rates were 33 % (235/713) (p = 0.00) and 2 % (9/468) (p = 0.31) in the NNU, with 17 % (26/154) (p < 0.0001) and 21 % (79/375) p = 0.31) in the PHOW. A significant downward MRSA trend was noted in the NNU (annual decrease of 0.6 [95 % CI, 0.39–00.06]; p = 0.01). No VRE was isolated in the GPW, however, MRSA rates were 11 % (73/664).

C. albicans displayed 100 % susceptibility to fluconazole, voriconazole and amphotericin B (pooled analysis, 2013–2022), however, C. parapsilosis fluconazole susceptibility was reduced (30 %; 129/433). Incidence rates of C. auris (100 % resistance to fluconazole and voriconazole) were high in the NNU owing to an outbreak that commenced mid-year 2019.

Discussion

In this retrospective analysis of paediatric SS data at a large tertiary healthcare facility, we have noted high culture positivity rates with almost half contributed by the NNU. A predominance of GNBs (49 %) prevailed during the study period as compared to the GPBs (43 %) with NNU as the highest contributor (66 %). The leading GNBs and GPBs cultured were the notorious ESKAPE pathogens displaying an overall MDRO prevalence of 35.1 % with CRAS and ESBL Enterobacterales dominating the study period. At least half of all paediatric Enterobacterales produced ESBLs (p < 0.0001), respectively. Alarming neonatal CRAS rates of ≥95 % detected annually had added to the overwhelming paediatric GNB MDRO burden. We found an appreciable decrease of MRSA from 30 % in 2013 to 10 % in 2022 (p = 0.004) amongst all paediatric units with a significant annual decrease (0.6 [95 % CI, 0.39–00.06]; p = 0.01) observed in the NNU. The ongoing predicament of common neonatal and childhood pathogens such as GBS, H. influenzae and L. monocytogenes were mainly observed in the NNU (36 %; 92/257, 76 %; 112/148, 85 %; 17/20) and GPW (63 %; 163/257, 22 % 33/148, 15 %; 3/20). Of concern was the ten year pooled BC contamination rates that were far above the CLSI acceptable criteria of ≤3 %.

A predominance of K. pneumoniae (26 %) and Acinetobacter spp (25 %) highlighted the study period which is on par with the global epidemiology of SS infections in LMIC accounting for >80 % of sepsis cases and sepsis related deaths [2,[19], [20], [21]]. In our study, Acinetobacter spp was the predominant GNB from NNU displaying more than double the culture positivity rate of 31 % (1350/4290) when compared to a previous study conducted from October 1, 2007 to 31st October 2011 in the same unit [22]. An epidemiological shift of SS gram-positive to gram-negative bacterial predominance was evidenced in the NNU. Enterobacterales remained the predominant GNB amongst the GPW and PHOW featuring E. coli (28 %) and K. pneumoniae (29 %) in keeping with sepsis aetiologies from other general paediatric and oncology units in developing countries [23,24]. The South African listeriosis foodborne outbreak between June 2017 and April 2018 saw seventeen case isolates (85 %) from only the NNU, representing a significant incidence of congenital listeriosis (p < 0.0001) [25].

Interestingly, our rates of BSI (between 1.7 and 4.09 per 1000 inpatient days) were comparatively lower than other LMIC such as India (17.0 per 1000 inpatient days) and Cameroon (11.6 per 1000 inpatient days), whereas a local tertiary institute in the Western Cape reported BSI rates similar to our study (3.8 per 1000 inpatient days) [21,26]. During the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic (2020–2022), a decrease in sepsis rates was observed amongst all paediatric wards with a noticeable decrease from a peak of 4.09 per 1000 patient-days in 2019 to 3.11 per 1000 patient-days in 2022 most likely due to heightened infection prevention and control (IPC) practices.

Internationally, MDRO trends have highlighted a rise in ESBL, CR GNBs and MRSA in the majority of paediatric units, with incredibly high VRE bloodstream rates (40 %) observed in a multi-institutional paediatric oncology study [8,[27], [28], [29], [30]]. A five-year resistance trend analysis study in Kwazulu-Natal demonstrated an appreciable increase in ESBL Enterobacterales from clinical specimens of ≥54 % [20]. Nosocomially acquired CR non-Enterobacterales GNBs have been steadily increasing with reports of exceptional prevalence rates of ≥70 %. from paediatric ICUs and cancer units globally [8]. South African surveillance has reported a predominance of GNB MDROs, specifically CRAS, implicated in multiple outbreaks in keeping with our study. This would require initiation of broader spectrum empiric antimicrobials to reduce the high mortality associated with delayed time to targeted therapy, a vicious cycle of selective pressure and further MDRO propagation. Elevated CRE rates evidenced in the PHOW (15–20 %) may be due to additional risk factors of gastrointestinal mucositis and chemotherapy-induced neutropenia leading to numerous fatalities [30]. The decline in neonatal MRSA was reassuring and most likely due to the introduction of daily 0.2 % chlorhexidine baths from 2014. Our low paediatric VRE rates (≤21 %) reflected low burden in South Africa.

Yeasts contributed the least to the total bloodstream isolates (8 %) with the majority being C. parapsilosis (NNU (44 %) and PHOW (41 %)) and C. albicans (GPW (59 %)) both regarded widely as common causes of paediatric candidaemia. A total of 169 bloodstream MDR C. auris isolates were cultured from neonatal specimens during the study period. In 2019, when the CHBAH neonatal outbreak had begun, antibiogram analysis revealed only seventeen C. auris patient bloodstream isolates with a subsequent increase in cases resulting in a protracted outbreak.

The ten-year pooled contamination rates (GPW 18.5 %, NNU 18.4 %, PHOW 28.8 %) were significantly high (p < 0.001) with CoNS (75.9 %), a well-known skin coloniser, being the largest contributor. This highlights the criticality of aseptic BC collection practices to reduce laboratory diagnostic costs and labour, as well as mitigate pseudo-bacteraemias.

Our study was limited by possible pre-analytical, analytical and post-analytical challenges. Isolates may have reflected colonisation/contamination as we were unaware if aseptic techniques were employed during microbiological sampling, correct specimen labelling or possible laboratory database transcription errors. Information on the laboratory request forms may have been incomplete to discern organism significance with primary infection site remaining unknown. Patients may have been on antimicrobials influencing microbiological culture yield. As oncology patients have implanted ports through which surveillance BCs are taken, the high rates of CoNS may reflect colonisation instead of a true pathogen [11]. It is unknown if the commensals were true BSI pathogens according to the CDC/NHSN criteria, as clinical signs and symptoms were not evaluated in the study. Our study describes the magnitude of drug resistant pathogens among septic children from Soweto illustrating a LMIC perspective on paediatric MDR pathogens rates. This is an eye opener signaling early sepsis detection in addition to strengthening existing antimicrobial stewardship and IPC practices.

To our knowledge, this study is representative of the largest paediatric population at a tertiary academic institute in Africa, and a means to benchmark further studies.

Conclusion

This study reports the unprecedented rise of paediatric MDROs at CHBAH, specifically ESBL Enterobacterales and CRAS, which may delay the initiation of appropriate antimicrobial therapy and contact-based transmission precautions. Ongoing microbiological MDRO surveillance, strict infection control interventions and rational antimicrobial use are paramount in curbing the spread of drug resistant pathogens needing greater efforts to alleviate this. BC contamination rates were profound requiring periodic evaluation and ongoing training on aseptic sampling techniques. Methods to optimize environmental cleaning and disinfection should be sought as the hospital microbiome is a nidus for MDR pathogens. Future studies reviewing unit specific antibiotic consumption rates and clinician prescriber practices as potential drivers of MDR nosocomial infection, as well as clinical and microbiology collaboration to determine paediatric SS organism significance, are duly warranted.
