Gut function among children treated for severe acute malnutrition: A cohort study in Uganda
Authors: Betty Lanyero, Nicolette Nabukeera–Barungi, Ezekiel Mupere, Bikila S Megersa, Iris Weidema, Pingping Jiang, Maria Wiese, Musemma K Muhammed, Henrik Friis, Hanne Frøkiær, Dennis S Nielsen, Vibeke Brix Christensen, Benedikte Grenov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesImpaired gut function in children with severe acute malnutrition (SAM) is associated with morbidity. We aimed to assess changes in six biomarkers representing different domains of gut function among children with complicated SAM during nutritional rehabilitation, and to identify predictors of these changes.MethodsWe followed 108 children aged 6–59 months with complicated SAM who participated in a probiotic trial (ISRCTN16454889). Plasma citrulline, a marker of enterocyte mass, fecal myeloperoxidase and neopterin, markers of intestinal inflammation and other gut function biomarkers were assessed at admission, discharge from inpatient therapeutic care (ITC), and after 8 weeks of outpatient therapeutic care (OTC). We assessed changes in these biomarkers and compared OTC values with 30 healthy community reference children.ResultsDuring ITC, plasma citrulline increased from very low to near normal values (geometric mean: 5.69–21.4 µmol/L, p < 0.001). The number of days with diarrhea predicted a higher increase in plasma citrulline (p = 0.020) during ITC, while children with human immunodeficiency virus and diarrhea on admission had a lower change in plasma citrulline in OTC. Fecal myeloperoxidase decreased (geometric mean: 16,546–3781 ng/mL, p < 0.001), while fecal neopterin increased (582–923 nmol/L, p < 0.01) during ITC; both were higher than normal level cut‐offs of 2000 ng/mL and 70 nmol/L. During OTC, no biomarker differences were observed between children with SAM and community reference children. Probiotics did not influence the changes in any biomarker.ConclusionsInpatient treatment of children with complicated SAM may improve gut function; however, intestinal inflammation seems to persist even after 8 weeks in OTC. Trial Registration: www.isrctn.com: ISRCTN16454889.Keywords: citrulline, environmental enteric dysfunction, gut inflammation, myeloperoxidase, neopterin

INTRODUCTION

Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) annually affects more than 19 million children under 5 years worldwide. 1 It is associated with physiological and metabolic changes in the gastrointestinal system, such as villous atrophy, infiltration of the lamina propria with inflammatory cells, and increased permeability, 2 , 3 that is, environmental enteric dysfunction (EED). These changes have detrimental effects on digestion, nutrient absorption, and gut barrier function. 3 , 4

Interventions improving gut status and function may be important for the management of children with SAM. Studies have evaluated the use of zinc, 5 mesalazine, 6 and glutamine 7 to improve the intestinal mucosal function in children with SAM with varied results. Probiotics have also been suggested as a treatment to improve intestinal barrier function. 8

Recent studies have used noninvasive biomarkers to evaluate gut health. 9 , 10 Fecal myeloperoxidase (MPO) and neopterin (NEO) are commonly used biomarkers of intestinal inflammation, 9 , 11 , 12 while plasma citrulline, a nonprotein amino acid mainly produced by the enterocytes in the small intestine has been used as a marker of enterocyte mass and function. 10 , 13 , 14 Fecal α‐1 antitrypsin (AAT) is used as an indicator of intestinal permeability, 10 , 15 , 16 while microbial translocation has been evaluated using polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) to detect DNA of microorganisms in blood. 17 , 18 The use and interpretations of these biomarkers in children with complicated SAM remains to be understood. In this study, we assessed changes in six biomarkers representing different domains of gut function in children with complicated SAM during inpatient therapeutic care (ITC) and at completion of 8 weeks of nutritional rehabilitation in outpatient therapeutic care (OTC), and examined predictors of these changes including the use of probiotics during nutritional rehabilitation. In addition, we compared gut function biomarker values at the end of 8 weeks of OTC with healthy community reference children.

METHODS

Ethics statement

Study approval was given by the Makerere University, School of Medicine Research and Ethics Committee (# REF REC 2013 132), Uganda National Council of Science and Technology, and the Uganda National Drug Authority. Consultative approval was also given by the Danish National Council of Health Research Ethics. Caregivers were informed about the study verbally and in writing, and written informed consent was obtained from all caregivers of the children before enrollment.

Study design, population, and intervention

This was a prospective cohort study nested within a randomized probiotic trial (www.isrctn.com, ISRCTN16454889) investigating the effect of B. animalis subsp. lactis BB‐12 and L. rhamnosus LGG among children with SAM. Days with diarrhea during ITC and OTC were primary and secondary outcomes. As described elsewhere, 19 the study was conducted in Mwanamugimu Nutrition Rehabilitation Unit, Mulago National Referral Hospital, Uganda, between 2014 and 2015. In brief, children aged 6–59 months with complicated SAM were enrolled into a probiotic trial. Children with admission weight less than 4.0 kg, severe respiratory distress or in shock at admission, or with obvious congenital abnormalities were excluded. Of 400 children enrolled into the probiotic trial, 108 with blood and fecal samples available at hospital admission, ITC discharge, and completion of 8 weeks of follow‐up were included in this substudy. Thirty unmatched children aged 6–59 months with a weight‐for‐height Z (WHZ) > −1 selected from a similar community as children with SAM were enrolled as community reference children.

This substudy, with approximately 100 participants enrolled, allowed detection of a standardized effect size of around 0.3 SD with 80% power (assuming a significance level of 5%), corresponding to a medium effect size.

Treatment of all children followed the national protocol of management of SAM 20 adopted to the 2013 World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations for management of children with SAM. 21 In addition, each patient received a daily dose of 10 billion colony‐forming units/day of a combination of Bifidobacterium animalis subsp lactis BB‐12 and Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG) or placebo throughout ITC and OTC. Further description is reported elsewhere. 19

Data collection

Data on sociodemographic characteristics, medical history, and feeding history were collected using a case report form. Physical examinations including grade of oedema, temperature, respiratory rate, pulse rate, dehydration status, and chest auscultations were conducted at admission, daily during hospitalization, at discharge from ITC, and every 2 weeks during OTC. Caregivers also rated the child′s general status on a visual analog scale (VAS) with a score ranging from 0 to 10.

Weight, length/height, and mid‐upper arm circumference (MUAC) were measured in triplicate using a digital weighing scale (Seca 813; Seca GmbH & Co.), a length board (Infant/Child Shorr Board®), and a nonelastic tape (MUAC Child Red/Pac‐50), respectively, at admission, discharge from ITC, and completion of 8 weeks in OTC. Precision of the measurements were 0.1 kg, 0.1 cm, and 0.1 cm, respectively. WHZ and height‐for‐age Z scores (HAZ) were obtained using WHO Anthro software.

Blood and stool samples were collected at hospital admission, discharge from ITC, and at 8 weeks in OTC for children with SAM and once at first encounter for community reference children.

Blood sampling and analysis

Venous blood was collected into heparinized evacuated tubes (Becton Dickinson) and used to determine Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) sero‐status at the Mwanamugimu mini lab. Detection of plasma citrulline by liquid chromatography mass spectrometry from venous blood and bacterial 16S rRNA and Candida albicans internal transcribed spacer (ITS) gene fragments from dry blood spots (DBS) by real‐time qPCR was performed as described elsewhere. 22 To assess microbial contamination, seven 3‐mm diameter spots punched outside of the DBS spots of random DBS cards were analyzed. Negative controls were also included for assessment of possible carryover contamination. C‐reactive protein (CRP) was analyzed using Pentra 400 (Horiba ABX). Blood sample processing, storage, and analysis is further detailed in the Supporting Information: Digital Content #S1.

Stool sample collection and analysis

For stool samples obtained at admission and discharge from ITC, stool was collected from study patients in a plastic bag and emptied directly into cryo tubes. For the 8‐week follow‐up and community reference children, caregivers were provided with a stool kit to collect stool samples at home. This was introduced because the children infrequently passed stool during follow‐up visits. The stool kit was prepared by adding 5 mL RNA‐later to a sterile 10 mL tube, and a ring mark equivalent to 6 mL was made on the tube. On the appointment date, the caregiver added an adequate volume (~1 mL) of stool from the study participant to fill up to the 6 mL ring mark. However, we noted that some caregivers added more than 1 mL of stool, hence affecting the stool sample dilutions. At the University of Copenhagen, stool samples were analyzed for MPO, NEO, and AAT by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The protocols of DuoSet ELISA Human MPO (R&D Systems), ready‐to‐use ELISA kit RE59321 NEO ELISA, IBL (Salzburg, Austria), and Human Serpin A1 DuoSet ELISA (R&D systems) for MPO, NEO, and AAT analysis, respectively were followed as per the manufacturer′s instructions. Further details are described in Supporting Information: Digital Content, methods. An overview of EED blood and stool biomarkers with reference ranges is shown in Supporting Information: Digital Content #S2.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 16.0. Socio‐demographics, clinical characteristics, and laboratory data were summarized as means ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages (%) for categorical variables. The blood and fecal biomarker measurements were skewed and hence presented as geometric means with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Change in biomarker levels during ITC was calculated by subtracting the admission value from the discharge value. For OTC, change was calculated by subtracting the discharge value from the value measured at 8 weeks postdischarge. To determine the predictors of change of biomarkers during ITC and OTC, linear regression analysis was done adjusting for age and sex, duration of hospitalization (or duration of OTC), and the level of the analyzed biomarker at admission (or discharge) from ITC. Changes in MPO, NEO, 16S rRNA gene copies, and Candida ITS gene fragments were skewed; therefore, data were log10‐transformed before regression analysis. The results were then back‐transformed and reported as ratios. p‐value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Missing data were not imputed, and adjustment for multiplicity was not applied.

RESULTS

Of 400 children with SAM enrolled in the original probiotic trial, 108 were included in the cohort study (Supporting Information: Digital Content #S3). Children in the cohort study had similar characteristics as those not included (Supporting Information: Digital Content #S4). The average duration of the ITC and OTC phases of nutrition rehabilitation was 19.8 ± 9.4 days and 57.1 ± 2.5 days, respectively. Children with SAM were on average 9 months younger than the community reference children (17 vs. 26 months, p < 0.001), and 42% were females (Table 1). The prevalence of HIV among the children with SAM was 11%. Up to half of the children with SAM presented with symptoms of cough, diarrhea, or fever during follow‐up in OTC, 30% had CRP > 10 mg/L, and the mean HAZ was −3.43 ± 1.35 at 8 weeks follow‐up in OTC (Supporting Information: Digital Content #S5).

Plasma citrulline

The geometric mean plasma citrulline increased during ITC from 5.69 to 21.4 μmol/L, (p < 0.001) but there was no difference in plasma citrulline levels among the children treated for SAM at 8‐week follow‐up and community reference children (p = 0.330) (Table 2). Pneumonia at admission was associated with 6.85 μmol/L (95% CI: 0.30; 13.4) higher change in plasma citrulline during ITC (Table 3 and Supporting Information: Digital Content #S6), and the number of days with diarrhea during hospitalization was associated with 0.51 µmol/L (95% CI: 0.08; 0.94) higher increase in plasma citrulline per day with diarrhea. One unit increase in WHZ score during OTC was associated with a 2.55 μmol/L (95% CI: 0.63; 4.48) increase in plasma citrulline. Conversely, HIV and diarrhea at admission were associated with a lower change in plasma citrulline in OTC. The change in plasma citrulline was 8.17 μmol/L (95% CI: 0.86; 15.5) lower in HIV positive children compared to HIV negative children (Table 3).

Fecal MPO, NEO, and AAT

The geometric mean of fecal MPO decreased during ITC from 16546 to 3781 ng/mL, (p < 0.001) while that of fecal NEO increased from 582 to 923 nmol/L, (p < 0.001). The mean fecal AAT did not change significantly during ITC (Table 2). There was no difference in fecal MPO, NEO or AAT among the children treated for SAM at completion of 8 weeks follow‐up in OTC and the reference children. HIV‐positive children had a 79% (expβ = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.04; 3.08) higher change in fecal NEO compared to HIV negative children (Table 4 and Supporting Information: Digital Contents #S7, #S8, and #S9). Compared to children without edema at admission, those with edema had a 37.7 µg/mL (95% CI: 4.35; 71.1) lower fecal AAT change during ITC (Table 4).

Blood spot bacterial 16S rRNA gene copies and C. albicans ITS fragments

Overall, geometric mean blood concentrations of bacterial 16S rRNA gene and C. albicans ITS gene fragments did not change during ITC and did not differ between children with SAM in OTC and reference children (Table 2). Interestingly, one unit increase in MUAC was associated with a 26% decrease in C. albicans ITS change during ITC (expβ = 0.74, 95%CI: 0.58; 0.94). Children with grade 1 edema showed a five times greater increase in C. albicans ITS change during ITC compared to those with no edema (expβ = 5.12, 1.14; 23.4) (Supporting Information: Digital Content #S10).

Probiotics

The two probiotic strains, BB‐12 and LGG, did not affect changes in any of the six biomarkers of gut function assessed during nutritional rehabilitation in ITC or OTC (Tables 3 and 4, Supporting Information: Content #S10).

DISCUSSION

In this nested cohort study, plasma citrulline increased from very low to near‐normal levels during ITC. The number of days with diarrhoea predicted a greater increase in plasma citrulline during ITC, whereas children living with HIV and those presenting with diarrhoea at admission showed lower changes in plasma citrulline during OTC. Fecal MPO levels decreased while fecal NEO levels increased in ITC. Children with edema at admission had a lower change in fecal AAT during ITC compared to those without edema. We observed no differences in biomarker levels between children with SAM and community reference children in OTC.

We observed a marked increase in plasma citrulline during ITC, from a geometric mean of 5.69 µmol/L at admission to 21.4 µmol/L at hospital discharge. The admission level was substantially below the 10 µmol/L threshold used in adults to define intestinal failure due to diffuse villous atrophy. 14 , 23 For children, no definitive cut‐offs have been established; however, median concentrations of 28 24 and 31.2 µmol/L 25 have been reported in healthy children from high‐income countries.

Several factors may have contributed to the increase in citrulline levels during ITC. Children with complicated SAM often suffer from EED (malnutrition enteropathy), which involves villous atrophy, increased gut permeability, and malabsorption. 26 This damage results in a decreased number and/or impaired function of enterocytes, which may have led to markedly low plasma citrulline levels at admission. 13 Treatment with nutrient‐dense therapeutic feeds and medical support in ITC may have resulted in proliferation of new enterocytes from crypts, which in turn increased citrulline production. 27 , 28

The increase in citrulline may also reflect reduced gut inflammation, as SAM is characterized by systemic and intestinal inflammation that impairs gut function and damages enterocytes. 29 , 30 Infection treatment and therapeutic feeding help resolve inflammation, supporting enterocyte recovery. 14 , 23 We have earlier reported a strong negative association of elevated CRP > 10 mg/L, an established marker of systemic inflammation, with citrulline at admission in the same population. 22 Others have also reported similar findings. 31 , 32 In critical illness, low plasma citrulline levels may reflect both impaired intestinal synthesis and diversion toward arginine and nitric oxide production under inflammatory stress. 33 , 34 The rise in citrulline observed during ITC may therefore reflect reduced demand for arginine and nitric oxide alongside declining systemic inflammation. Furthermore, the rise in citrulline may result from improved nutrient absorption and metabolism, including increased availability of glutamine, a key precursor for citrulline synthesis. 35

Both pneumonia at admission and more days with diarrhea during ITC were associated with a higher increase in plasma citrulline. This may result from initial enterocyte loss during acute illness, followed by compensatory enterocyte regeneration and enhanced citrulline synthesis during recovery and nutritional rehabilitation. 13 , 23 A gain in WHZ during OTC was associated with a greater increase in plasma citrulline, suggesting that improved nutritional status may enhance gut function and thereby promote citrulline production. 36 This finding is consistent with the results of a study conducted among underweight children in Bangladesh, where weight gain was associated with improved intestinal function assessed using the lactulose‐mannitol test. 37 In contrast, diarrhea and HIV at admission were negatively associated with changes in citrulline during OTC. Children admitted with diarrhea may have a lower increase in citrulline due to continued exposure to enteropathogens in the environment when the children return from hospitalization. HIV infection is known to accelerate epithelial apoptosis, impair epithelial cell differentiation, and downregulate genes involved in intestinal growth. 38 , 39 Additionally, HIV‐positive children often experience persistent diarrhea, further compromising the integrity of the intestinal mucosa. 40

The intestinal inflammation marker fecal MPO decreased during ITC, while fecal NEO slightly increased. The observed decrease in MPO, a marker of neutrophil activity, may relate to resolved acute infections and intestinal injury. 41 , 42 In contrast, the observed increase in NEO, a marker of Th1‐driven macrophage activation suggests persistent Th1 immune activity linked to prolonged systemic and intestinal inflammation after nutritional recovery or, simply, that macrophages are active in both inflammation and in restoring tissue and immune function afterwards. 9 , 29

We observed no significant differences in fecal MPO and NEO levels at completion of 8 weeks of follow‐up after discharge compared with community reference children. However, the measured levels of MPO (9068 ng/mL) and NEO (308 nmol/L) were markedly higher than the respective reference values of 2000 ng/mL and 70 nmol/L reported from high‐income countries. 43 , 44 This may indicate that persistent intestinal inflammation likely due to continued exposure to enteropathogens contributes to the increased risks of mortality, morbidity, and stunting observed among children who initially recovered from SAM. 45 The malnutrition and enteric disease study cohort study conducted across eight sites in low‐ and middle‐income countries, 16 the SHINE interventional study in Zimbabwe, 10 and the HOPE­SAM longitudinal study among children with complicated SAM in Zimbabwe and Zambia 41 also reported elevated MPO and NEO levels.

The higher increase in NEO during ITC among HIV positive children may result from both direct HIV‐induced cellular damage across multiple organs and indirect effects, such as chronic immune activation and opportunistic infections due to immunosuppression. 46 Accordingly, the HOPE‐SAM study reported elevated markers of systemic inflammation and endothelial activation in HIV‐infected children with SAM compared to those without HIV infection. 41

The lower change in fecal AAT observed during ITC among children with edema at admission may indicate persistent intestinal permeability and protein‐losing enteropathy, possibly due to immune suppression, dilution of fecal AAT related to extracellular fluid accumulation, or delayed gut recovery, which are commonly observed in children with kwashiorkor. 47 , 48 Similarly, a study from Malawi indicates that children with kwashiorkor showed persistently elevated lactulose‐rhamnose ratios, a marker of intestinal permeability reflecting delayed gut barrier function restoration. 4 Additionally, the observed increase in C. albicans ITS gene fragments among children with Grade 1 edema during ITC strongly suggests that immune suppression, increased intestinal permeability, and/or microbial imbalance. 49 In the current study, AAT values among both children with SAM and community reference children did not exceed the suggested reference value of 270 µg/g. 9

Probiotics may improve intestinal barrier function. 8 However, the probiotics, BB‐12 and LGG did not predict change in any of the biomarkers of intestinal function used, even though these probiotics reduced diarrhea during OTC, but not ITC, in the original trial where this cohort was nested. 19 This is consistent with findings of a study conducted in Malawi that found no effect of LGG on intestinal permeability among children with a high prevalence of elevated lactulose: mannitol ratio. 50

The study had some limitations; first, participants with SAM were younger than reference children, thereby affecting biomarker concentrations as some vary with age. 9 , 10 Nonetheless, age was adjusted for during analysis. Secondly, children in the cohort study may be different from the original population. However, comparison of socio‐demographic characteristics between children included in the cohort study and those not included showed no differences (Supporting Information: Digital Content Table S3). Thirdly, as fecal samples collected in the community were processed differently from hospital samples, absolute changes of MPO, NEO, and AAT during outpatient treatment could not be calculated. Finally, we did not measure or adjust for differences in dry matter of fecal samples, and there was a possibility of contamination of filter papers as the DBS were air‐dried in a room for several hours. Nevertheless, we assessed contamination on filter papers without blood and found levels below the detection limits.

CONCLUSION

Based on the EED biomarkers studied, intestinal function in children with complicated SAM improved during ITC. However, intestinal inflammation remained above clinical cut‐offs in both children with SAM and community reference children. HIV negatively influenced changes in multiple biomarkers, while diarrhea, pneumonia, elevated CRP, edema, and increases in WHZ were associated with changes in specific biomarkers only. Probiotic intake was not associated with changes in gut function markers. Persistent intestinal inflammation may contribute to an increased risk of recurrent morbidity or relapse of SAM. Further research is needed on biomarkers of gut function in children with SAM and on interventions to minimize intestinal inflammation after recovery.
