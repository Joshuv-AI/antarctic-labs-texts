Exploring the dark side of the moon: diagnostic errors in critically ill patients
Authors: Andreas Valentin, Hans Flaatten, Martin W. Dünser
Published: 2025

A diagnostic error is defined as the missed opportunity to arrive at a correct or timely diagnosis [1]. In critically ill patients, this process requires close consideration of multiple differential diagnoses while simultaneously assessing both chronic and evolving conditions. Unsurprisingly, this process is highly error-prone and does not only refers to delayed, missed, or incorrect diagnoses but also to their consequences leading to patient harm. Focusing on the later we need to consider three facts. First, critical illness is an extremely time-sensitive condition with diagnostic delays delicately affecting patient outcomes [2]. Second, critical illness is not limited to intensive care units (ICUs) but is also commonly encountered outside of ICUs [3]. Third, critical illness itself can be the result of diagnostic errors occurring in previously non-critically ill patients. Diagnostic errors in critical are, therefore, particularly detrimental and crucial to prevent. Of note, diagnostic errors must not be regarded as the result of human limitations alone but system weaknesses, as well.

Incidence and impact of diagnostic errors

In 2,428 adults hospitalized with general medical conditions, who were either transferred to an ICU or died, diagnostic errors were identified in 550 patients (23%) and judged to have contributed to temporary harm, permanent harm, or death in 436 subjects (17.8%) [4]. A recent systematic review of discrepancies between clinical and autopsy-based diagnoses in critically ill patients reported a 19% pooled estimate of major missed diagnoses, one third of which may have altered survival if diagnosed pre-mortem [5]. In contrast, meta-analyses of general medical inpatients reported harmful diagnostic error rates closer to 0.7% [6]. The higher rate of diagnostic errors in critically ill patients is attributed to the higher complexity and acuity of critical illness, limitations in history-taking, non-specific clinical findings, and the rapid evolution of disease processes. No data have so far been published on differences in the frequency of diagnostic errors between general and specialised ICUs. While specialist ICUs might be highly accurate in diagnosing conditions within their area of expertise, they may, on the other hand, face important challenges when diagnosing patients with multiple diseases and symptoms outside of this field. Conversely, the same may apply to general ICUs. Unexpectedly, despite advances in monitoring, imaging and laboratory technologies over the past decades, the rate of diagnostic errors remained unchanged. Diagnostic errors in critical patients can lead to overuse of resources, increased risk of death and delayed recovery. Such errors can also give rise to chronic critical illnesses [5, 7].

Risk factors for and causes of diagnostic errors

The greatest risk of error occurs in critically ill patients with a high disease severity, atypical symptoms, non-specific presentations, and those unable to give a medical history due to an altered mental state or need for mechanical ventilation [7]. Socioeconomic disadvantages and low health literacy further increase the risk of delayed or missed diagnoses [7, 8]. In addition to patient-related risks, cognitive and system-related factors contribute to diagnostic errors. Common cognitive errors include anchoring bias (overreliance on initial impressions), availability bias (favouring diagnoses that easily come to mind), premature closure (ending diagnostic reasoning too early), and confirmation bias (seeking information supporting a suspected diagnosis while discounting contradictory findings) [9, 10]. At an individual level, overconfidence or unrecognized knowledge gaps may also play a role. Moreover, invasive procedures and machine-based therapies as required by many critically ill patients carry the potential to draw the clinician’s focus away from traditional but still highly effective methods to identify the correct diagnosis, such as history taking (both including patients and their families), physical examination and the complex challenge of diagnostic reasoning and uncertainty. The same risk applies to overreliance on the results of laboratory or diagnostic investigations without putting them into the right clinical context. All biases are intensified by negative influences such as high stress levels, overwork, and fatigue. Environmental or system-related factors include failures in communication, assessment follow-up, coordination, and sub-optimal use or design of electronic health records [10]. Another important cause of diagnostic error in critically ill patients is the syndromic approach to critical care. Common syndromes (e.g. sepsis, ARDS, delirium) lack sensitivity and specificity. They group heterogeneous patients with diverse underlying pathologies, reducing diagnostic and therapeutic precision.

Preventive strategies to reduce diagnostic errors

While not all diagnostic errors in intensive care medicine are preventable, many are. Strategies to prevent diagnostic errors are not exclusively about finding solutions to eliminate inherent human weaknesses, but rather about risk minimisation by designing training programmes, optimizing working conditions and other system-related factors. Another fundamental aspect in this regard is the need for sufficient ICU staffing in view of the rising demands on critical care due to demographic changes, particularly in countries with a lower gross national product or lower health expenditures [11]. Accordingly, effective strategies to prevent diagnostic errors in critical care involve a combination of cognitive and system-based interventions (Fig. 1). Understanding and avoiding biases are crucial to minimise cognitive errors [10]. Recommended approaches are teaching and modelling of critical thinking, explicitly discussing cognitive biases, and encouraging a symptom-based diagnostic work-up that relies on broad differential diagnoses. Structured changes of ICU processes, such as implementation of diagnostic checklists, standardised handoff protocols and multi-disciplinary patient review strategies (e.g. team-based case reviews or second opinions by specialists) are all effective in reducing the rate of diagnostic errors [12, 13]. Post-ICU follow-up clinics may provide another valuable opportunity to identify diagnostic discrepancies and offer feedback to ICU teams, ultimately supporting both quality improvement and education. Additionally, standardized review strategies, for example, applying the Revised Safer Dx Instrument [1] or modified Diagnostic Error Evaluation and Research (DEER) taxonomy [14], can improve objective measurement and reduction of diagnostic errors. Although important limitations remain, artificial intelligence-driven clinical decision support systems have the potential to reduce diagnostic errors and improve patient safety by offering real-time insights, reducing cognitive biases, and prioritising differential diagnostic thinking [15].
