Engagement with gestational diabetes self-management: a qualitative study from the UK
Authors: Amanda P Moore, Nerys Astbury, Shoba Poduval, Jamie Ross
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOptimal management of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is essential to reduce risks for mother and child. Women with GDM have a tenfold higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes (T2D), particularly those from ethnically diverse or socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. This can be reduced through lifestyle change and annual screening but there is a paucity of data from the UK exploring engagement. Aims were to (1) explore GDM experiences among a diverse sample of UK women and (2) identify factors which could help women engage with GDM self-management guidance during pregnancy and with T2D risk prevention strategies postpartum.MethodsWe recruited a diverse sample of South Asian, black African and white British women from the community and primary and secondary care settings to take part in an online or face-to-face interview. Community recruitment was prioritised to engage women who may not normally take part in research. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed. An inductive thematic content analysis was conducted.Results35 women participated (66% from ethnically diverse groups; 43% first-generation immigrants). Engagement with GDM guidance was shaped by five themes: the psychological burden of diagnosis; challenges balancing everyday life with glucose monitoring and dietary changes; building resilience through personal strategies; healthcare relational support influencing confidence and adherence; and one-size-fits-all guidance being inadequate. Postpartum, women described being ‘left at sea’, reporting limited follow-up and unclear pathways for ongoing diabetes prevention and competing pressures of motherhood.ConclusionsApps, online support groups, personally tailored lifestyle advice and empathetic online or face-to-face healthcare interactions supported GDM self-management. Postpartum knowledge about the risks and useful steps for diabetes prevention was low, and confusion was exacerbated by mixed messages from healthcare teams. Engagement was harder for women facing socioeconomic adversity and for those for whom cultural norms conflict with lifestyle guidance.

Background

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a form of glucose intolerance that can occur during pregnancy.1 Prevalence is increasing globally and in the UK GDM is diagnosed in 23% of pregnancies.2 The burden falls disproportionately on women from minoritised ethnicities.35 Risk also increases with maternal age, elevated body mass index1 6 7 and socioeconomic adversity.8 The first line strategy for GDM self-management is lifestyle change (diet and exercise), with supplementary use of metformin and insulin therapy, if necessary.9 Blood glucose levels above the recommended range are associated with increased risk of antenatal depression, pre-eclampsia, preterm birth and birth intervention and the baby is vulnerable to macrosomia, shoulder dystocia, stillbirth and hypoglycaemia at birth.1 Both mother and child are also at increased risk of progression to type 2 diabetes (T2D) compared with those with a normoglycaemic pregnancy.7 Estimates across Europe suggest up to 50% of women will develop T2D following GDM.71012 GDM self-management guidance includes testing blood glucose in the morning and after each meal, restricting carbohydrate portion size, keeping active and taking medication, as required.9 Blood glucose readings need to be recorded manually and uploaded periodically to healthcare teams or can be monitored via a GDM-specific app, depending on approved clinical use by the relevant healthcare trust. Engagement with self-management guidance and lifestyle change can help improve GDM outcomes13 and can reduce the risk of subsequent development of T2D.14 15

Existing reviews have explored the lived experience of gestational diabetes1618 and highlight mental distress, challenges with dietary advice and a financial burden associated with GDM. A recent review among minority ethnic groups (primarily Hispanic, Aboriginal and First Nation communities and African Americans) living in high-income countries indicated there may be culturally-specific barriers associated with normative beliefs, socioeconomic status and cultural-appropriateness of healthcare.19 However, there is a paucity of studies from the UK setting. Furthermore, the need to urgently address maternal and diabetes-related health inequalities among the UK’s growing South Asian and black African communities is becoming increasingly apparent.20 21 There is a need to understand how we may improve engagement with pregnancy and postpartum self-management guidance. Furthermore, this research is timely as the use of mobile apps in maternity care has increased,22 remote care delivery has become more commonplace,23 and there has also been an update to the clinical guidance since the data in the most recent UK study was collected in 2015.24

The aim of our study was to explore experiences and identify factors which could help women engage with GDM self-management guidance during pregnancy and with T2D risk prevention strategies postpartum, among a diverse sample of UK women.

Methods

We conducted a qualitative interview study and aimed to recruit a sample of women representative of those with GDM in the UK, particularly in terms of ethnic diversity and socioeconomic status. The inclusion criteria were aged 18–45 years, currently pregnant and diagnosed with GDM or having had a diagnosis of GDM within the last 24 months. We made provision for non-English speakers to take part. Women were excluded if they had had a diagnosis of T2D before their pregnancy. We targeted three ethnic groups to allow comparison—black African, South Asian and white European/British but women from other ethnicities were eligible to take part.25

Recruitment

We recruited primarily from community services supporting women facing hardship in boroughs with high ethnic diversity and deprivation (via charities, Children’s Centres and National Health Service (NHS) community services), and from eight primary care practices and one secondary care maternity setting. Recruitment took place between November 2022 and July 2023. We built relationships with local charities supporting women and young families and asked them to share details of the study using flyers, posters and WhatsApp. One of the research team (AM) was also temporarily stationed within one of the local children’s centres for the recruitment phase. She took part in mother and baby activities and provided some diabetes education sessions to raise awareness of the study and build trust. Women who responded to the recruitment activities were sent an information sheet and consent form. Some women had further questions and these were answered over the telephone, by a member of the research team. We aimed to recruit 20–30 women in line with guidance for conducting qualitative thematic analysis.26 Consent forms were signed via secure electronic signing software or in-person and an opportunity for audio-recorded verbal consent (in English or native language for interviews with interpreters) was also offered to address language and literacy barriers. We also collected basic demographic data.

Data collection

The topic guide was developed in partnership with members of our patient and public involvement (PPI) team. It covered questions related to experiences of GDM healthcare guidance, behaviour during pregnancy and readiness for and attitude towards lifestyle change and screening to reduce later risk of T2D. Data saturation was determined when no further themes were being added. Interviews were audio-recorded and professionally transcribed.

Data analysis

An inductive thematic analysis was conducted, according to reported processes.27 This involved reading the transcripts multiple times and illustrating the emerging themes in hand-drawn mind maps on an iPad.

Results

35 women took part in a semistructured interview (60 min duration), at which point data saturation was determined. 32% of women were recruited from community routes. The interviews were conducted online or face-to-face in community venues or the home. Translation services were offered, and one interview was conducted with an interpreter. The interviews were conducted in November 2022 to March 2023 (by AM, a female postdoctoral research fellow and experienced qualitative researcher). 66% of participants were from diverse ethnicities (see table 1) and 43% were first-generation migrants.

GDM self-management in pregnancy

Most women wanted to do the best for their baby and the diagnosis of GDM meant their pregnancy became medicalised. The challenge for women is encapsulated in the two themes, The burden of diagnosis and Coping with the everyday. The theme Building self-empowerment explains some of the strategies women engage in to balance these challenges. Women’s frustration with the generic nature of GDM care is summarised in the theme Universal guidance for a personal challenge and reflections on the value of positive relationships are summarised in the theme, The power of a supportive healthcare interaction (see figure 1).

The burden of diagnosis

Cognitive burden

Remembering to check blood glucose, reporting the results, understanding what foods triggered glucose spikes, medication management, as well as the behaviour-regulation processes associated with avoiding temptation and changing the diet were cognitively challenging—‘always having to think about food, it becomes completely invasive…set an alarm test your sugars, you’ve got to time everything’ (14-White British). In addition, women felt added anxiety because not following the guidance had the potential to harm their unborn child—‘it scares you, they say there’s a high risk of still-born, baby being overweight, so needing a C. Section…just all these different things’ (16-Black African). The cognitive burden could be overwhelming for some women, limiting their adherence to recommendations. This was particularly the case for those who entered pregnancy with health issues such as depression and those facing social and economic adversity.

Guilt and culpability

A third of women felt some sense of culpability in the GDM diagnosis. Women felt guilt about eating sugar and carbohydrate in the period before diagnosis and suggested that dietary information related to glucose control should be part of basic pregnancy dietary guidance given at conception, especially for those with risk factors.

20 weeks into your pregnancy, it’s pretty late, it brings anxiety and worry. When you have your first midwife appointment there should be more information on it. They test ethnic minorities. Had I’d known earlier I probably would have cut carbs. (10-South Asian)

Feelings of guilt and shame could be exacerbated by the perceived judgement of others, for example friends and family thinking GDM was related to not eating well—’people were asking me, did you do something to cause it?’ (5-South-East Asian (SE.A))—but also healthcare staff when their reflections on blood glucose control were seen as judgemental (3-White British). First generation, East African women in the sample (n=3) reported not to talk to others about their diagnosis because of this guilt and shame. This led to a sense of isolation but also limited opportunities to develop knowledge via peer interaction, which was a common adaptive strategy to improve engagement among other women, highlighting an important need for culturally specific support for some women.

Loss of control

Empowerment is seen as vital in self-management for T2D28. Yet women reported feeling disempowered over GDM self-management and a loss of control over the perceived ‘medicalisation’ (20-Mixed race (MR)) of their pregnancy—‘[we are] treated like a vessel. The vessel must do everything the vessel is told’ (11-White British).

Coping with the everyday

Managing GDM took place in the context of everyday normative influences on food intake and physical activity and pressures of everyday life.

Social influences

Normative dietary influences were difficult to navigate. For all women, eating out and socialising was challenging and some adjustment needed to be made to family meals at home. Women felt pressure from family to follow expectations for a normal pregnancy diet, making behaviour change harder. Many women from minoritised ethnicities (eg, Indian and Caribbean) ate a mixture of Western and traditional foods but for those following their cultural dietary patterns more consistently, dietary change was especially difficult. These tended to be first-generation migrants. For these women, their diet needed significant adjustment compared to European women because of the heavy rice or starchy carbohydrate bias and because of habitual practices of eating the family meal late in the evening which contradicted GDM advice. Furthermore, within these cultural traditions, food was symbolic and central to social and family interaction and offering food to pregnant women was the cultural norm. This could create tension when trying to make dietary change—‘My uncles, uncle’s wife, cousins we always get together and they used to tell me ‘oh my God you’re refusing food’. They were a bit upset because I wasn’t eating’ (34-South Asian). The women from these minoritised ethnicities suggested that including the wider family GDM education may be helpful because of this social pressure and because often they themselves were not doing all the food preparation.

When guidance clashed directly with cultural beliefs about what was best for the baby, it could result in poorer engagement. For example, some black African and South Asian women held cultural beliefs about the safety of exercising in pregnancy and needing to feel full for the baby, which created conflict in the minds of mothers and could reduce their intention to exercise and restrict carbohydrate portions. A strong attachment to traditional cultural beliefs was particularly noted in first-generation migrants.

External pressures

Beyond these normative influences on lifestyle behaviour, there were other practical challenges women faced. Living in difficult social environments could contribute to stress and depression, which made the burden of GDM management difficult to bear. These women described being quite isolated and they wanted peer support as well as empathy from their healthcare team. Work pressures added to the load, especially when women also had other children. This made planning food, glucose monitoring and physical activity difficult—‘they said ‘go for a walk after lunch’ – I’d say ‘I need to earn money. I can’t do that!’ (11-White British).’ Women, across all ethnicities, who described socioeconomic challenges were also more likely to describe periodic disengagement with GDM self-management. For example, they may stop monitoring or reporting blood glucose for periods of time or would partially follow the guidance, such as taking medication but not altering their diet.

I stopped recording, stopped checking all the time… they said you don’t do it this way. Why did you stop recording? That’s not how we can follow good treatment. I did have quite a lot on my mind at that time, that was the least thing on my mind. (26-Black African)

Pregnancy-related challenges

Some women faced pregnancy-related issues, particularly in the last trimester, which influenced the kinds of food they could eat and their ability to engage in exercise and control their glucose levels—‘Once the SPD (symphysis pubis dysfunction) kicked in I could no longer walk so it (blood glucose) did go high… then they considered insulin.’ (16-Black African)

Building self-empowerment

Striving for control

Women employed various strategies to help them retain control of their pregnancy. Most of these strategies positively influenced self-management. Some women (with sufficient digital literacy and access) used their GDM/pregnancy apps and other technology to plan their meals and glucose management with precision—‘I’ve made a spreadsheet, I make little plots it has the date the timepoint, what I’ve eaten…’ (5-South-East Asian). Others sought out information to fill gaps in their knowledge, for example by engaging with GDM management websites. They also sought reassurance over GDM risks and medication choices from people they knew who were medically qualified. It was easier for the more health literate and those confident within the healthcare interaction to advocate for themselves and seek out the information they needed. Equally, it was easier for women who were more digitally literate to access information online.

Support seeking

Whilst feeling they were in capable clinical hands, women still felt they needed more emotional support, practical day-to-day guidance. This led to seeking support from peers and social networks. Online support groups, for example Facebook or other mums’ networks, were valued for their immediate two-way interaction and for the emotional support they provided. Women also looked to existing social networks for support, finding peer support from other women with lived experience of GDM particularly valuable—‘my friend had her GDM diagnosed a few weeks before me. I was lucky I had close, very informed support. She had lots of useful tips for me to try.’ (6-White British). Some women from minoritised ethnicities, particularly first-generation migrants of African heritage, were less likely to report access to support networks, possibly due to stigma associated with GDM.

Universal guidance for a personal challenge

Women were frustrated with a perceived one-size-fits-all approach to lifestyle education and treatment and wanted more individualised care.

Generic dietary advice

With regard to diet, the advice was perceived as generic and not tailored to personal dietary habits. The group that struggled most with this were those who already ate quite healthily and wanted more nuanced information and had low faith in the NHS advice.

I thought it was really poor…the kind of NHS typical eat a low fat, healthy plate diet. […] I always remember them saying for a snack you might want to eat two satsumas… I think I switched off at that point, it was like ok you’re talking nonsense. (14–White British)

Most women supplemented the dietary guidance with their own research from a range of resources beyond NHS-endorsed sources. Women from diverse ethnicities generally felt equipped to adapt the advice to their own culture. They would of course still benefit from signposting and more tailored support as they needed to do their own research—‘I would say I didn’t have much information on the Indian stuff, I had to go to YouTube.’ (4-South Asian) For recent, first generation migrants, however, this lack of tailored dietary advice was a more significant barrier.

Vague physical activity guidance

Despite the importance of physical activity in glucose management29 30, exercise recommendations were either not tailored for pregnancy-related symptoms or were not a key focus of the guidance: ‘vague’ (13-White British), ‘they didn’t particularly talk about it’ (30-South Asian), ‘most of the advice was diet’ (5-South-East Asian). This left women unsure about the detail of the activity they should do and they often picked up advice from peers.

Un-contextualised personal risk

Women felt once they were diagnosed they were treated as high risk and lost control over considerations around the type of birth they could have for example, as this participant described—‘Why are you treating me as someone who’s baby is highly at risk when I’ve shown you that I’ve been keeping my blood sugar low. No-one was able to discuss that with me on a case-by-case basis.’ (ID12 – S.E Asian)

The power of a supportive healthcare interaction

Empathetic support versus judgement

Some from minoritised ethnicities, particularly black African, reported being treated disrespectfully by staff historically. Given this context, they remarked upon the value positive relational interactions during their GDM care. If women did not feel the teams were supportive and engaged it created anxiety and disengagement in some, particularly those who were less health literate, coping with additional psychosocial burden or less driven to avoid risks. Use of pressure and negative language created resistance—‘I’ll be honest I lied about my numbers sometimes, I’m not going to lie. Because I thought they didn’t give me that care and so why should I?’ (34-South Asian). Language used to create fear had a negative effect and participants reported being made to feel guilty over some food choices in a way they felt unnecessary. On the other hand, when teams were supportive, empathetic and non-judgemental, women found this reassuring and motivating. They responded better to positive reinforcement than pressure. For example, responding to a lack of engagement with empathy was encouraging—‘[the midwife] called me twice because I cancelled my appointment 7 times, ‘we need to make sure you and your baby are ok’. So, there’s a lot of empathy and sympathy, she made adjustment for me to come and see her even when not scheduled’ (33-South Asian). Feeling that their opinions were considered and that they were treated as an individual also helped.

Supportive use of technology

For all participants some of the elements of their GDM care were offered remotely and, overall, it was well-accepted. Group education sessions for lifestyle management had advantages of being easy to attend and having less barriers to access than online resources, although it could limit the opportunity to ask questions, especially in larger groups (group sizes of 2–30 people were reported)—‘I guess you are less likely to ask questions and talk about your individual issues. [The technology] doesn’t invite that’ (17-White European). Other barriers to digital tools for women facing socioeconomic adversity were lack of privacy and unstable connections. Approximately 37% of women used the mobile GDm-Health app, linking them to their care teams31. Women who used the GDm-Health app, across ethnicities, reported great benefit in the management and reporting of their glucose figures ‘a major help’ (4-South Asian), ‘really clever’ (6-White British), ‘an efficient way to communicate’ (19-Black African). They enjoyed the opportunity the technology provided for information exchange and contact with their teams. Furthermore, women found the monitoring the trends supported self-management. Ongoing clinical support for glucose monitoring was primarily via telephone with the diabetic midwife, with occasional face-to-face hospital visits. Women enjoyed the convenience of a telephone call but they did value occasional face-to-face meetings—‘it’s just nice to sit down in person’ (11-White British). Furthermore, three women with poorer control said they did not report their readings accurately over the telephone, for fear of judgment by the healthcare staff, suggesting at least some face-to-face appointments are needed.

Engagement with postpartum T2D prevention guidance

Two themes reflected the tension between women struggling with lack of continuity of care between secondary and primary care and the practical burden of motherhood. Both of which negatively influence postpartum T2D prevention engagement: Left at sea—you are on your own and Moving forward: Life gets in the way.

Left at sea—you are on your own

Withdrawal of healthcare support

Women who were worried about T2D risk felt a sense of abandonment postpartum, after the intense monitoring of pregnancy—‘I feel like once the hospital has discharged you, that’s the end of the diabetes, go back to your normal life’ (5-South East Asian).

Uncertainty about personal risk and actions to take

The messaging from healthcare teams often conflicted leaving women uncertain of their own risk and whether they needed to take action.—‘[I’m] trying to paint my own picture. It would be nice to have a concrete picture rather than one I’m piecing together that could well be wrong’ (19-Black African). Some women felt they were not personally at future risk, or that they needed a clearer picture of predication of future risk for them personally, to motivate behaviour change. Almost half the women interviewed (46%) were not aware of annual HbA1C screening.

GP as a necessary partner

Other than awareness the key barrier to attending screening was remembering and universally women wanted their general practice (GP) to send a routine text reminder, as well as give more information about what the test meant and why it was important—‘I will always go if I get a reminder. It would be nice if my GP knows and they remind me annually. It shouldn’t be up to me. I’m not a health professional.’ (3-White British)

Moving forward: life gets in the way

Competing responsibilities

Postpartum pressures related to looking after the baby, mothering other children led to sleep deprivation, which in turn impacted lifestyle choices negatively. Participants described difficulty in getting any structure or control of your life—‘juggling responsibilities’, ‘trying to survive’—so their own health was not their priority.

Health-related challenges

Some women had health challenges postpartum related to their baby’s health, the birth or their own health. This participant explained, illness culminating in a pulmonary embolism which led to weeks of tests—‘babies can’t come to the blood clinic, so like up and down, in and out every week to do your blood clot tests […] now having to look for someone to look after the kids’ (ID23—Black African). These factors reduced any focus on their own health.

Social and cultural issues

Postpartum challenges were accentuated amongst some patients for social or cultural reasons. First-generation immigrant women lamented their lack of support compared to being in their own country, where their family would support them for 40 days. Our data and other reports acknowledge that postpartum women have many competing priorities related our data suggest this may be accentuated amongst those facing psychosocial adversity. This participant describes poor housing and anti-social neighbours limiting her sleep—‘I’ve got crippling post-natal depression, crippling anxiety. I’m not sleeping. It’s overwhelming.’ (15-White British)

Discussion

GDM management guidance was perceived as burdensome and challenging in the context of everyday life, despite a motivation to do the best for your baby. Engagement was positively influenced by peer support, the use of mobile apps and online information provision for women with digital literacy and access, and by positive relational interactions with healthcare teams. On the other hand, the clarity and personal appropriateness of lifestyle guidance and unsupportive healthcare interactions added to the self-management challenge and increased anxiety. This overwhelmed some women dealing with other psychosocial pressures associated with adversity and could lead to disengagement with self-management guidance. This was evident across the sample, but was more common among minoritised ethnicities. For first-generation immigrants from minoritised ethnicities, lifestyle change was made more difficult by normative dietary patterns in the community. Postpartum women felt unsupported in understanding future risk, received mixed messages from healthcare professionals and lacked detailed knowledge about diabetes prevention strategies. Without this clarity, changing lifestyle was not a priority, given the pressures of life postpartum.

Women finding the GDM dietary advice generic, echoes other reports across different settings.28 32 33 To compensate, while women were happy to seek further information from online resources, this advice was not usually NHS endorsed and searching for trustworthy information added to stress. This offers an opportunity to signpost women to endorsed educational sources online, which can be adapted to individual dietary norms and cultural preferences and which can provide more in-depth support for those who want it. However, previous research suggests that only a small minority of GDM digital interventions are culturally appropriate or adequately match digital literacy levels, suggesting a risk of excluding women who need culturally tailored diet advice, language support or simpler interfaces.34 Our data differed slightly from other studies among diverse ethnicities, where the lack of culturally tailored support was a significant barrier.19 32 33 In our sample, women from minoritised ethnicities that had reasonable health literacy, were able to translate the advice to their cultural diet through their own research but barriers were more evident for women with lower health literacy or who closely adhered to cultural dietary traditions. Similar findings have been reported for women from minoritised ethnicities living in other high-income settings.22 The strong influence of sociocultural barriers is recognised in T2D self-management and there is strong evidence to support culturally-tailored interventions.35 36 Recent reviews into maternal inequalities in the UK have suggested that community-level interventions offer promise to support these women.36 37 We recommend that these culturally tailored approaches should be further researched. We also recommend that women are signposted to online tailored dietary resources developed by, or endorsed by, NHS sources. Additionally, given the importance of physical activity in glucose management,29 30 our findings suggest more comprehensive advice on physical activity may be warranted. The need for social support, given the psychological burden of GDM, has been previously reported.17 24 Women sought out social support from others with lived experience within their communities and online. Our study is the first to report the value women with GDM found within online communities but there is a developing literature exploring the value of online peer support in other health and maternity-related conditions,3840 suggesting it can provide psychosocial support,39 41 although the mechanisms for the effectiveness are uncertain.38 Our findings suggest women from some ethnic groups faced specific cultural barriers associated with lifestyle change and were less likely to access peer support—this was particularly apparent for some black African, Bangladeshi and Indian participants. The provision of culturally salient social support has been identified as a potentially successful strategy to improve engagement in lifestyle-related behaviour change,42 43 and merits investigation.

Technology-driven healthcare delivery in the form of wearable devices, mobile apps, text messaging support and live online consultations has increased over recent years, although there are gaps in our evidence for the value and impact in maternity care.44 The increased provision of telehealth as part of general maternity care, as a result of COVID-19, has been reasonably well-accepted by women as being time-saving and convenient.23 Furthermore, the effectiveness of remotely delivered GDM blood glucose monitoring support from the diabetes teams is suggested from clinical trial data.45 Our findings support the use of glucose monitoring apps for GDM and suggest a role for remote care delivery. With respect to the latter, however, it remains important to offer some in-person support, particularly for those who are struggling to manage GDM or who face barriers to remote or digital access.

A lack of shared understanding with healthcare teams for women with GDM has been previously reported in the UK by Parsons et al.24 46 Healthcare teams are currently under considerable pressure. Nevertheless, our data suggests women’s experiences and engagement can be improved by subtle shifts within existing relational interactions. To improve trust, risk presentation should be balanced, reflecting that the likelihood of serious consequences is limited if glucose levels are well-controlled. Furthermore, choice of language is impactful.47 There is some debate in the literature about the effectiveness of positive compared with negative framing, in supporting behaviour change.48 Nevertheless, the value of positive framing, suggested by our data, has been echoed in other studies supporting behaviour change for T2D.49 Furthermore, a person-centred approach to decision making has potential to reduce the psychological burden and improve trust engagement, echoing the recommendations for T2D care.50

Postpartum, our data suggest women want to understand their future personal T2D risk and to be signposted to reliable information about recommended postpartum lifestyle change, echoing recommendations from a recent systematic review.51 Although, evaluations of postpartum interventions to reduce risk of T2D suggest that more directed behaviour change support is needed for interventions to be effective.52 In this study, the knowledge and understanding of the role of recommended annual HbA1C testing was relatively low, reflecting UK national data.53 Women requested reminders of their screening appointments from their GPs, an approach which has proven merit in T2D management,49 but more than this, they want to be told what the results mean and the importance of the screening, as well as understanding their own personal risk profile. Our data suggest there is immediate justification for clarity of postpartum guidance reminders to be part of routine primary care and potential value in considering the development of a postpartum risk stratification calculator to identify those at highest risk.

Our data have a London-centric bias where ethnic diversity is high, reducing the direct transferability to other UK geographies. Our sample is diverse, providing a comparative overview of issues facing different communities but it does not provide a detailed culturally specific picture which could be provided by sampling more narrowly by ethnic group. Similarly, the sample though inclusive of many women facing adversity was overall well-educated and therefore likely to have relatively high health literacy. We also collected limited data about socioeconomic status and information about receipt of benefits or other socioeconomic indicators would have been helpful to add nuance to the data. There is potential for recall bias of antenatal experience among those who were postpartum at the time of interview. Similarly, inclusion of women currently pregnant may have underrepresented their experiences of the burden of GDM, which is likely to build over time. We only conducted one interpreted interview, so our data cannot describe the experiences of women with less proficiency in spoken English, despite the fact they are likely to experience significant challenge. We could also have expanded coverage of postpartum experiences in the topic guide. Strengths of this study include the strong focus on community recruitment which engaged participants who would not generally access the research process.

Conclusions

This study highlights the importance of pertinent lifestyle knowledge, emotional support and person-centred interactions in engagement with GDM management. The use of tech and online resources is an acceptable way to support behaviour change and engagement, while reducing burden on healthcare teams. The psychosocial burden of GDM self-management is high and can be overwhelming for those dealing with adversity and other social challenges. In the presence of cultural barriers and lack of social support, community networks may offer promise to support women from minoritised ethnicities to engage with GDM guidance. Postpartum women are unlikely to engage in postpartum T2D risk prevention as they are unclear of their personal risk, feel unsure of what changes they should make and feel unsupported. Offering consistent messaging, supporting women to understand their personal profile and signposting to further support and resources would be a start to support engagement.
