Head and Neck Cancer Incidence, Mortality, and Disability-Adjusted Life Years From 1990 to 2021-A Systematic Analysis for the Global Burden of Disease Study
Authors: Yue Wang, Jingjing Zheng, Wenhao Ren, Shaoming Li, Keqian Zhi, Ling Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study evaluates the global burden of head and neck cancers, analyzing incidence, mortality, and DALYs from 1990 to 2021, across age groups and regions with varying SDI levels.Materials and MethodsData from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database (1990-2021) was utilized to examine incidence, mortality, and DALYs for head and neck cancers. Regional disease trends were analyzed using age-standardized rates (ASR), estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), Joinpoint regression, and the Age-Period-Cohort model. A global map depicted disease burden across 204 countries and territories, 21 GBD regions, and 5 SDI regions.ResultsIn 2021, there were 421,577 new lip and oral cavity cancer cases and 208,379 deaths, with DALYs totaling 5,874,070 years. EAPC results indicated rising incidence for lip and oral cavity cancers (EAPC: 0.4% [95% CI: 0.33-0.46]) and other pharyngeal cancers (EAPC: 0.68% [95% CI: 0.6-0.75]). The AAPC model showed an overall increase in incidence for lip and oral cavity cancers (AAPC: 0.442 [0.374-0.511]) and other pharyngeal cancers (AAPC: 0.716 [0.607-0.826]). While mortality rates remained stable or declined. Lip and oral cavity cancer incidence rose most in the middle SDI region, followed by low-middle and low SDI regions, while other pharyngeal cancers saw the highest increase in high, low-middle, and low SDI regions. The AAPC model indicated a declining incidence for lip and oral cavity cancers in all regions except the high SDI region (AAPC: -0.012 [-0.17-0.147]), where other pharyngeal cancers had the highest increase (AAPC: 1.034 [0.741-1.329]). Mortality for lip and oral cavity cancers rose in low-middle SDI regions, while other pharyngeal cancers showed increased mortality in low-middle and low SDI regions. South Asia reported the highest incidence, mortality, and DALYs for lip, oral cavity, and oropharyngeal cancers in 2021. Age-cohort analysis showed that incidence rose until age 55, stabilizing or declining afterward, while the burden of lip and oral cavity cancers increased after 55, with mortality highest over 80.ConclusionHead and neck cancer burden is concentrated in low- and middle-income countries, highlighting the need for prevention, early screening, risk factor control, and improved data monitoring in low-income regions.

Introduction

Cancer is a major contributor to the global disease burden, a challenge expected to intensify over the next 2 decades.1,2 In 2019, approximately 10 million cancer-related deaths occurred worldwide.3 While head and neck cancers represent a relatively small percentage of overall cancer cases, their mortality rates are alarmingly high, with oral cancer, a subtype of head and neck cancers, causing around 199,000 deaths that same year.3 This complexity arises due to various factors, including environmental influences, behavioral patterns, and genetic predispositions.4,5 Significant risk factors include smoking and alcohol consumption, with viral infections also contributing to cancer development in some regions. The interplay of these factors results in diverse epidemiological profiles across regions. Additionally, surgical interventions can have a profound impact on facial appearance, severely affecting patients' quality of life.6 Despite the high burden of head and neck cancers, research on their global impact remains relatively limited.

Monitoring the extent of the burden and its demographic, spatial, and temporal variations, is crucial for informed health planning and setting clinical and research priorities.7 The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database serves as a comprehensive public health resource, offering vital indicators like incidence, mortality, and Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) for various diseases.8 The GBD database not only provides essential data on health conditions across regions but also serves as a foundation for policymakers and public health researchers.9 By analyzing data over time and across geographic and demographic segments, researchers can identify trends in disease burdens to inform health policy development. This approach helps decision-makers allocate resources effectively, target high-burden diseases, and enhance the specificity of clinical care and research.

This study aims to assess the global burden of head and neck cancers by analyzing incidence, mortality, and DALYs. Utilizing GBD data, we will examine regional, age-based, and demographic variations to clarify trends and impacts associated with head and neck cancers worldwide.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

This study uses data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database (1990-2021) to assess the burden of head and neck cancers: lip and oral cavity (ICD10:C00-C07, C08-C08.9), laryngeal (ICD10:C32-C32.9), other pharyngeal (ICD10:C09-C10.9, C12-C13.9), and nasopharyngeal cancers(ICD10:C11-C11.9), as provided by the GBD Results Tool.10 The dataset provides detailed estimates of incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) across 204 countries and territories, categorized into 5 socio-demographic index (SDI) regions: low, lower-middle, middle, high-middle, and high-income.11 This categorization, along with the division into 21 geographic regions, supports a comprehensive analysis of trends across 3 decades, offering valuable insights into the evolving burden of head and neck cancers.

Analytical methods

The Age-Standardized Rate (ASR, per 100,000 population) and Estimated Annual Percentage Change (EAPC) were employed to examine trends in head and neck cancers.12 ASR allows for comparison of incidence and mortality across populations by adjusting for age structure differences, thus providing a clearer picture of disease burden. The EAPC quantifies the magnitude of change over time as an annual percentage, highlighting the average annual shifts in incidence or mortality. It is calculated through regression analysis, represented by the equation y=α+βx+ε, where y=ln(ASR) and x is the calendar year. An EAPC greater than 0 with a 95% UI above 0 signals an upward trend, whereas an EAPC below 0 indicates a decline. Stability in ASR is noted when the EAPC lies between the upper and lower limits of the 95% UI.

Joinpoint analysis

The Joinpoint model, widely used in epidemiology and cancer research, detects "join points" in time series data where trends change. By estimating the Annual Percent Change (APC) across different intervals, it identifies accelerations or decelerations in trends. The Average Annual Percent Change (AAPC) further summarizes the trend over the full observation period. Using Joinpoint software (version 5.2.0) (https:// surveillance.cancer.gov/joinpoint/), the AAPC’s statistical significance is tested, with a p-value below 0.05 indicating significant differences in trends across regions.13

Geospatial mapping

This study generated geospatial maps illustrating global head and neck cancer burden, stratified by age-standardized incidence rates, mortality, and DALYs across 204 countries and territories, 21 GBD regions, and 5 SDI regions.14 These maps visually represent geographic disparities in disease burden, using color coding to indicate varying levels of burden, facilitating clear comparisons between regions.

Age-period-cohort (APC) model

The Age-Period-Cohort model decomposes influences on disease trends into age, period, and cohort effects.15 The age effect captures variations in prevalence across age groups; the period effect reflects the impact of specific timeframes on population health; and the cohort effect accounts for differences in exposure among birth cohorts. Ages range from 20 to 95 years, with 5-year intervals for period effects (1992-2021).

An additional analysis using the average annual percentage change (AAPC) was conducted to examine shifts in disease incidence across different periods since 1990. The AAPC results were largely consistent with the EAPC findings, with some variations. Specifically, incidence rates for lip and oral cavity cancer (AAPC: 0.442 [0.374 - 0.511]) and other pharyngeal cancers (AAPC: 0.716 [0.607-0.826]) increased, while incidence rates for other cancers decreased. Global trends by SDI

We performed a comprehensive analysis of global trends across the 5 Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) regions. In the EAPC analysis of incidence, age-standardized rates (ASR) for lip and oral cavity cancer and other pharyngeal cancers showed an upward trend in the 5 SDI regions. Notably, lip and oral cavity cancer had the most significant increase in the middle SDI region [EAPC: 0.89 (0.79-0.99)], followed by the low-middle SDI [EAPC: 0.64 (0.56-0.73)] and low SDI [EAPC: 0.33 (0.23-0.43)] regions. Meanwhile, the largest increases for other pharyngeal cancers were seen in the high SDI [EAPC: 1.05 (0.98-1.13)], low-middle SDI [EAPC: 0.99 (0.92-1.06)], and low SDI [EAPC: 0.73 (0.62-0.85)] regions. The incidence of oropharyngeal cancer is linked to HPV infections, potentially explaining these unexpected trends (eTable 2, Figure 2).

For Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs), most types of head and neck cancers displayed a downward trend across SDI regions. Exceptions included lip and oral cavity cancer in the low-middle SDI region [EAPC: 0.17 (0.12-0.22)], and other pharyngeal cancers in both the low-middle SDI [EAPC: 0.58 (0.54-0.63)] and low SDI [EAPC: 0.34 (0.26-0.42)] regions, where DALYs showed an increasing trend. In the low SDI region, lip and oral cavity cancer did not show significant changes (eTable 3, Figure 2). Mortality trends were consistent with those observed in DALYs (eTable 4, Figure 2).

In the AAPC incidence analysis, lip and oral cavity cancer showed an upward trend across all regions except for the high SDI region [AAPC: -0.012 (-0.17-0.147)], with the highest increase observed in the middle SDI region [AAPC: 0.936 (0.879-0.992)]. Similarly, other pharyngeal cancers displayed a general increase, especially in the high SDI region [AAPC: 1.034 (0.741-1.329)]. Other head and neck cancers generally showed a declining trend (eTable 5, eFigure 1).

For AAPC mortality, lip and oral cavity cancer exhibited either an upward trend or no significant change in the low-middle SDI, low SDI, and middle SDI regions, while high and high-middle SDI regions displayed a downward trend. Mortality rates for other pharyngeal cancers rose in low-middle and low SDI regions, while other head and neck cancers showed a general decline (eTable 6, eFigure 1). The AAPC results for DALYs closely mirrored those for AAPC mortality (eTable 7, eFigure 1).

Age-period-cohort model analysis

Our analysis of age-specific incidence rates across different birth cohorts revealed that globally, the incidence of head and neck cancers generally increases with age up to 55. After 55, while lip and oral cavity cancer continues to rise, other head and neck cancers peak and begin to decline. Over successive birth cohorts, the overall incidence of head and neck cancers has shown a downward trend, with the smallest decline in nasopharyngeal cancer and the largest in laryngeal cancers. Over time, nasopharyngeal cancer incidence remains relatively stable, whereas other cancers generally show an upward trend, with the most significant increases in lip and oral cavity cancer and other pharyngeal cancers (Figure 4A).

For mortality, the death rate from lip and oral cavity cancer has shown a steady increase, with a moderate rise between ages 55 and 80 and a more pronounced increase after 80. The cohort effects on mortality mirror those seen in incidence trends. Regarding the temporal effect, mortality trends closely follow incidence trends, with the largest increases observed in lip and oral cavity cancer and other pharyngeal cancers (Figure 4B).

In terms of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), the trends align with mortality patterns. However, for lip and oral cavity cancer, there is an unexpected declining trend in the age effect within the 50 to 80 age range. Temporally, DALYs for nasopharyngeal cancer show a downward trend (Figure 4C).

We also analyzed incidence trends across different Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) regions. The findings indicate that in high and high-middle SDI regions, the incidence rate of laryngeal cancer is decreasing, while trends for other cancer types generally align with global trends (eTable 12).

Discussion

Cancer remains a major public health challenge worldwide. Although head and neck cancers account for a relatively small proportion of the overall cancer burden, their unique anatomical locations often lead to disfigurement and significantly impair patients' quality of life.3 While previous studies have primarily focused on the pathogenesis and clinical treatment of HNCs, epidemiological analysis is equally crucial. It enables us to understand how disease burden evolves across different economic settings and historical periods. In regions where the disease burden is particularly high, such analysis can inform improved health policies and guide targeted interventions.2

In addition to disparities in healthcare accessibility and disease burden across regions, socioeconomic status may also influence individual health behaviors. People living in environments with lower economic pressure are often more likely to adopt healthier lifestyles, such as reducing tobacco and alcohol consumption—both known risk factors for cancer. This raises an important hypothesis: could socioeconomic development indirectly reduce cancer incidence by improving behavioral health patterns?16 To explore this question at both the population and regional levels, we conducted a comprehensive epidemiological analysis of head and neck cancers across different SDI levels and geographic regions using data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database.10

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of global trends in the burden of head and neck cancers from 1990 to 2021, leveraging data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database to examine variations across Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) levels and GBD regions.17 Our findings reveal that lip and oral cavity carries the heaviest burden among head and neck cancers, with the highest incidence, mortality, and Dalys' rate, among subtypes. Furthermore, substantial variation in cancer burden exists across SDI levels: although many regions have seen a general decline, lip and oral cavity cancers and pharyngeal cancers have notably increased, particularly in low- to middle-SDI regions. This trend is closely linked to high-risk behaviors like tobacco use, alcohol consumption, and areca nut chewing, which are more common in these areas and often lack robust preventive measures.18,19 In high-SDI regions, other pharyngeal cancers have increased, likely due to HPV infections, suggesting that dominant risk factors vary by SDI level.20

The geographic distribution analysis reveals significant increases in lip and oral cavity cancers and pharyngeal cancers in South Asia, while the burden is comparatively low in North Africa and the Middle East. This disparity may stem from lifestyle differences, levels of economic development, and healthcare resources. Additionally, the widespread habit of areca nut chewing in Southeast and South Asia is a known contributor to pharyngeal cancer in these regions.21,22

The age-period-cohort (APC) model analysis reveals that incidence rates for head and neck cancers rise with age until 55, after which they stabilize or decline, except for lip and oral cavity cancers, which continue to increase in older populations. This trend highlights both age dependence and cohort influences, consistent with other findings showing a heavier burden among older individuals. Mortality rates for lip and oral cavity cancers and other pharyngeal cancers have also risen, with these cancers bearing a greater burden than other subtypes. Among those aged 50-80, mortality rates for lip and oral cavity cancers have stabilized, and improvements in treatment and early diagnosis have reduced disability, contributing to a downward trend in disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). However, most studies focus on mortality trends alone, often overlooking the effects of treatment on DALYs.23,24

This study also emphasizes that, while head and neck cancer burdens have stabilized in high-income countries, the incidence of lip and oral cavity cancers continues to rise in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in South Asia, East Asia, and low-SDI regions. This calls for urgent measures to enhance early screening and address high-risk behaviors to counter rising incidence rates.25 Although mortality rates have stabilized or decreased in some regions, changes in DALYs reflect the effects of disease management and quality of life improvements, especially in high-income countries. These findings align with studies suggesting that improved cancer treatment and supportive care can reduce disability burden.26, 27, 28 Due to the lack of HPV infection data in the GBD database, this study could not fully assess the impact of HPV on the burden of lip and oral cavity cancers in high-SDI regions.29

Variations in data sources, analytical models, and research focuses across studies may result in inconsistencies in DALY trends and the burden of specific cancer types. This underscores the need for careful data selection and analytical approaches in understanding head and neck cancer burdens, suggesting that future research should emphasize data standardization. Further investigation of SDI levels, high-risk factors, and specific cancer types is essential to provide a foundation for global prevention and control strategies.30,31

This study highlights notable socioeconomic and regional disparities in head and neck cancer burdens, with a sustained increase in low-income countries, indicating an urgent need for public health interventions.32 Reducing high-risk behaviors (e.g., smoking and alcohol consumption) and promoting health education and early screening are critical strategies.32 In high-income countries, widespread screening has reduced cancer burden, suggesting that implementing cost-effective screening in high-burden areas could improve early diagnosis and survival rates.33 This study also underscores the importance of optimizing resource allocation and enforcing tobacco and alcohol control policies to benefit high-risk populations and support regional prevention and treatment strategies.

Compared to previous studies, this research adopts a multi-level global analysis approach, revealing distribution characteristics of head and neck cancers and capturing their trends over time. The multi-level model and advanced statistical methods applied here improve result accuracy, providing a reliable framework for future research. Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations should be noted. First, the GBD database lacks certain key risk factor data, such as HPV infection status, which restricts our ability to fully assess virus-associated cancer burden, particularly for pharyngeal cancers in high-SDI regions. Second, the analysis does not incorporate treatment-related factors, such as access to care or therapeutic advancements, which may influence DALY estimates and mortality trends. Third, the quality and completeness of cancer registry data vary considerably across regions, especially in low-income countries, which may introduce bias or underestimation. Fourth, the statistical models used may not fully capture rapid socioeconomic or healthcare transitions in some settings, limiting their sensitivity to real-time changes. Lastly, while our study evaluated long-term trends, some indicators remain cross-sectional in nature and may not reflect the short-term effects of public health interventions or behavioral shifts.34

Conclusion

This study highlights regional disparities in the global burden of head and neck cancers, with a decline in high-income countries but a rising trend in low-income areas, especially those lacking resources, screening, and preventive measures. Through systematic analysis, we identify key risk factors across specific populations and regions. These findings emphasize the need to prioritize screening and health education in high-burden regions to promote more balanced global health outcomes.
