A small nucleolar RNA dictates the structure and function of translating ribosomes in Leishmania
Authors: K Shanmugha Rajan, Saurav Aryal, Sharanya Murugeshan, Yinzhou Zhu, Anat Bashan, Mika Olami, Hava Madmoni, Yuko Nobe, Tirza Doniger, Smadar Cohen-Chalamish, Eric Prina, Pascale Pescher, Tom Beneke, Masato Taoka, Christopher L Holley, Ron Unger, Gerald F Späth, Ada Yonath, Shulamit Michaeli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe most common rRNA modification is 2’-O-methylation. Here, we determine the landscape of 2’-O-methylation in Leishmania, a parasite that cycles between two different hosts, insect and mammalian. We find two 2’-O-methylated positions that are differentially modified during the parasite’s two life stages. The deposition of these modifications is guided by snoRNAs. When we perform cytosine base-editing of the snoRNA responsible for guiding one of the two stage-regulated modifications, Am479, we fail to detect ribosomes lacking this modification, suggesting that it is essential. To better understand the role of the snoRNA and its guided modification, we determine the cryo-EM structures of ribosomes from cells overexpressing the guiding snoRNA and compare them to ribosomes from the parental strain. We do not find structural changes around Am479 or in the small subunit rRNA, but observe a difference in H68 of the large subunit rRNA due to a second base-pairing interaction, suggesting a potential chaperone activity for the snoRNA. Based on these results, translatome and tRNA analysis, we propose a mechanism whereby changes in ribosome structure affect the release of specific tRNAs, which correlate with changes in translation of only a subset of mRNAs.Subject terms: Transcriptomics, RNA modification, Proteomics, Ribosome

Introduction

The ribosome is a large macromolecule composed of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and ribosomal proteins (RP)1. Alterations in RP have been described in congenital diseases and in cancer2. Little is known about how changes in rRNA affect ribosome function. One of the key features of rRNA is that it undergoes massive post-transcriptional chemical modification during its biogenesis3. The most abundant modification on rRNA is 2′-O-methylation (Nm), which is guided by C/D small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs). C/D snoRNAs carry two conserved boxes, the C box (5′-RUGAUGA-3′) and the D box (5′-CUGA-3′)4 whereas many snoRNAs carry additional boxes (C’ and D’ boxes). The rRNA modifications are introduced co-transcriptionally in the nucleolus, concomitantly with pre-rRNA processing. The C/D snoRNA are bound in a complex with the proteins SNU13, NOP56, NOP58, and the methyltransferase Fibrillarin (NOP1)4. The snoRNA interacts with its target substrate via 9-20 Watson–Crick (WC) base-pairs and adds Nm 5 nt upstream to the D or D’ box (+5 rule). Each snoRNA can potentially guide at least two Nm modifications5. In addition, several snoRNAs, especially those involved in rRNA processing, were shown to interact with pre-rRNA domains and serve as chaperones for the proper rRNA folding6,7. rRNA methylation is known to fine-tune translation fidelity and efficiency, since Nm modifications optimize rRNA folding and affect their interactions with proteins3,6.

The snoRNA repertoire and its guided Nm sites range from 55 Nms in yeast to 112 Nms in humans. The Nm sites conserved among all eukaryotes are located in functional domains of the rRNA, such as the peptidyl transferase center (PTC), helix 69 (H69), and the A-site finger (ASF) of the large subunit (LSU), and the decoding center of the small subunit (SSU). Many of these rRNA modification sites are almost fully modified8–11. Depleting individual Nm modifications has little effect on translation, and only depleting multiple Nms in yeast was shown to affect translation12,13. In cancer cells, rRNA Nm sites that exhibit altered levels are primarily located on the ribosomal surface14–16. CRISPR knock-out of the snoRNA guiding Cm174 (SNORD45) affected the translation of distinct mRNAs. These affected mRNAs have a codon bias, suggesting the contribution of snoRNA to the formation of specialized ribosomes17.

The protozoan parasite Leishmania affects millions of people in ~99 countries18. These parasites cycle between two hosts. The promastigotes are the extracellular form that propagates in the mid-gut of the sand fly, whereas the amastigotes are the intracellular form that proliferates inside mammalian macrophages18. Regulation of gene expression in Leishmania is unique since these parasites lack transcriptional regulation of protein-coding genes, and gene expression is regulated mostly post-transcriptionally by RNA-binding proteins19 and antisense regulators20. In trypanosomatids, including trypanosomes and Leishmania species, most of the snoRNA genes exist in clusters that typically encode for C/D and H/ACA snoRNAs, but a few snoRNAs also exist as solitary genes21–23. We previously mapped the Nm landscape in trypanosomes in the two life stages of the parasite, and eight out of 99 Nms positions, which are located near the functional sites of the ribosome24 were shown to be differentially regulated in the two life stages24. Furthermore, changes in levels of pseudouridine (Ψ), the second most abundant rRNA modification, were found to differ between the two life stages of trypanosomes and Leishmania25,26.

Here, we determined the Nm landscape on rRNA of three Leishmania species, L. major, L. donovani, and L. amazonensis, revealing more than a hundred Nms on rRNA. Species-specific changes in the level of the Nm were found between the promastigote and amastigote stages, but only two alterations are shared among the three species. One of these modifications, in the SSU rRNA at nucleotide Am479, is guided by the single-copy snoRNA, LD/LM32Cs1C1, that is elevated in the amastigote stage. Cytosine base editing of LD32Cs1C1, by altering its D box, almost completely abolished its expression. However, cryoEM analysis of ribosomes from the snoRNA-depleted cells did not detect ribosomes lacking the modification, suggesting that it is essential. Cells overexpressing (OE) the snoRNA compromised growth and translation capacity. However, surprisingly, OE of a mutant that cannot guide the modification also had a similar effect. Interestingly, a mutation in a second rRNA interaction domain of the snoRNA partially eliminated the OE phenotype. The cryo-EM structure of the ribosome from cells overexpressing the snoRNA exhibited structural changes at LSU H68, which forms the intersubunit B7a bridge, but no structural changes were found at the SSU rRNA Am479 site carrying the Nm. RNA-seq analysis identified specific tRNAs that are differentially associated with the ribosomes of cells overexpressing the snoRNA, leading to a codon bias. Our results support a role of the C/D snoRNA as a chaperone for fine-tuning translation, suggesting a function of snoRNAs in shaping ribosome structure and function.

Results

Heterogeneity in the rRNA Nm landscape of different Leishmania species

The most reliable method to determine the landscape of Nm modification is RibOxi-seq24. This strategy enriches sequences with 3ʹ-terminal Nm sites, leading to highly Nm-specific, low complexity cDNA libraries analyzed by next-generation sequencing24. A schematic overview of the experimental workflow followed in this study is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. The results are presented in Supplementary Data 1 and 2, summarizing the Nm positions, as identified by RibOxi-seq and mass-spectrometry (LC-MS). We used RNA-seq data derived from libraries that were prepared from small RNAs affinity-purified from L. major cells overexpressing the C/D protein SNU1321 to identify the snoRNA that guides each Nm modification. Similar libraries were also prepared from L. donovani SNU13. The “reads per kilo million” of each C/D snoRNA in the library is presented in Supplementary Data 3. A representative RibOxi-seq dataset of L. donovani is presented in Supplementary Fig. 2A, showing the score of all 111 Nms. The 14 species-specific Nms present in L. donovani are depicted in red on the 3D structure of Leishmania ribosome27. More than half of these modifications are located around the PTC and entrance tunnel (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Since RibOxi-seq data are not quantitative, in order to assess changes in Nm level during the developmental cycle, the Nm modification level was determined by RiboMeth-seq24,28. The RMS (relative methylation score: score C) was calculated for each Nm site detected by RibOxi-seq. The mapping was performed in promastigotes and amastigotes isolated from mammalian hosts as described in the “Materials and Methods.” The results (Supplementary Data 4) indicate that not all of the Nm sites are equally modified along the rRNA in the two stages (Fig. 1A). Six Nm sites were hypermodified, and four sites were hypomodified in L. major amastigotes compared to promastigotes (Supplementary Data 4). In L. donovani, four sites were hypermodified, and eleven sites were hypomodified when comparing these two stages, whereas in L. amazonensis, only one site was hypermodified and one was hypomodified in these stages (Supplementary Data 4).

Localizing the Nm modifications on the 3D structure of L. major25 (Fig. 1B-i) and L. donovani27 ribosomes (Fig. 1B-ii) demonstrated that two of the differentially regulated Nm in L. major are located around the protein exit tunnel, and four such Nm also exist in L. donovani. The other modifications are not located near any known functional domains. Interestingly, only Am479 on SSU rRNA was found to be hypermodified in amastigotes of all Leishmania species examined. The hypermodification of Am479 was further verified by LC-MS in both L. donovani and L. amazonensis (Fig. 1C). To understand the mechanism by which Am479 is hypermodified in amastigotes, we checked the level of the corresponding guide snoRNA (LM/LD/LA32Cs1C1) using Northern analysis. The result (Fig. 1D) suggests that the snoRNA level is indeed increased in amastigotes compared to promastigotes.

Cytosine base editing depletion of LD32Cs1C1 demonstrates the importance of Am479

The Nm modification Am479 is located in the SSU near the uS12 ribosomal protein, which contacts the incoming mRNA (Fig. 2A). We previously demonstrated that the CRISPR-Cas9-based knockout (KO) of a single copy H/ACA snoRNA can be achieved in Leishmania25. To deplete L. donovani cells for single-copy C/D snoRNA (LD32Cs1C1) guiding Am479, we decided to use the recently described cytosine base-editing (CBE) approach29. The CBE system is comprised of cytidine deaminase domain (APOBEC-1), Cas9-D10A nickase, and uracil glycosylase inhibitors (UGI). The guide RNA (gRNA) directs the CBE system to introduce a C-U mutation in the genome. A single gRNA can guide multiple C-U editing events in nucleotides 4–12 on the complementary strand to which the gRNA binds. The mutated uridine is then repaired to thymidine during the DNA repair processes29. To eliminate the function of the C/D snoRNA, we decided to mutate the D-box in both copies of the gene encoding this species. Studies on yeast30 and vertebrates31 demonstrate that a mutation in the D-box interferes with the binding of the core proteins that stabilize the snoRNP complex, since these proteins make direct contact with the D-box32. We anticipated that editing the D box would result in unstable snoRNP. We designed a gRNA that obeys the conventional CRISPR rule (20 nt upstream from the PAM sequence that guides Cas9 binding) with a D-box motif (CUGA) within the 4–12 editing window and no predicted off-target site. The gRNA was cloned into plasmids containing a ribosomal promoter, hepatitis delta virus ribozyme sequence for proper termination, and all the protein components necessary for the CBE approach29. The proposed secondary structure of LD32Cs1C1 is presented (Fig. 2B-i), and the sequences that were used to design the guide RNA and the PAM sequence are marked (Fig. 2B-i). We transfected to L. donovani the plasmid carrying the guide RNA and the CBE enzymes described above. Clones were obtained, and the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used to amplify the region surrounding the edited site in both edited and un-edited cells. As expected, all three cytosines in and around the D box were edited (Fig. 2B-ii). After observing that the mutations were correctly introduced into both snoRNA genes, the RNA from the transgenic parasites was subjected to Northern blot analysis, demonstrating near-complete elimination of the snoRNA (Fig. 2A-iii). However, minute amounts of snoRNA were still detectable, suggesting that complete elimination of the snoRNA could not be achieved. The level of Nm on Am479 guided by the snoRNA was determined by RiboMeth-seq, and the results (Fig. 2C-i) showed only a ~29% reduction in the Am479 level. Similar results were also obtained by LC-MS (Fig. 2C-ii), suggesting that either another snoRNA guides the same position, or that the residual amounts of the snoRNA can still guide the Nm. Indeed, we searched for additional snoRNAs that could potentially guide this modification and found that three snoRNAs (LD27Cs1C3, LD36Cs4C1a, and LD26Cs1C1) that could base pair with 8–10 nt base-pairing and guide Am479 (Supplementary Fig. 3). However, even though the rRNA was only partially modified on Am479 compared to the parental strain (PS), the growth of the transgenic parasites was compromised (Fig. 2D). To examine if the inhibition in growth was due to the reduction in the snoRNA level, and to identify the snoRNA function that contributes to the effect on growth, the snoRNA-edited parasites were transfected with an addback construct encoding the wild-type snoRNA (LM32Cs1C1) from L. major, or its mutant derivatives that cannot guide the modification (M2) using the pX-vector system25. The interaction domain of LD/LM32Cs1C1 and SSU rRNA is depicted in Fig. 2B-iv, highlighting the positions used to introduce mutations and disrupt the perfect base-pairing. The L. major and L. donovani snoRNA are 96.4% similar. However, as the region around the PAM sequence differs, the gRNA directed to L. donovani snoRNA (LD32Cs1C1) gene cannot edit the incoming L. major gene (LM32Cs1C1). The addback of the wild-type gene expressed from the episomal pX vector increased the level of Nm to above its normal level (Fig. 2C). Adding back the wild-type snoRNA gene substantially improved growth, as compared to the CBE edited parasites, and to cells carrying the M2 addback (Fig. 2D). Since several of the snoRNAs that function in rRNA processing, such as U333 and snR3034 exhibit chaperone activity in addition to a methylation function, we examined whether the CBE editing of LD32Cs1C1 affected the level of rRNA precursors (pre-rRNA). Accordingly, total RNA from edited and un-edited cells was hybridized with ITS1 and ITS3 antisense probes35 (Fig. 2E). ITS1 is located between the SSU and 5.8S rRNA and can report defects in pre-SSU rRNA levels, while ITS3 is located between LSUα rRNA and SR1 rRNA and reports defects in the pre-LSU rRNA level. The results (Fig. 2E) show no defect in rRNA processing, since the pattern of the SSU and LSU pre-rRNA precursors and their levels was the same as those resulting from snoRNA-editing, as well as observed in the different addbacks. To evaluate potential defects in translation, we fractionated extracts from PS- and snoRNA-edited cells on a sucrose gradient and observed minor changes in polysome patterns (Fig. 2F).

To examine global translation, we used the SUnSET (Surface SEnsing of Translation) assay36. The results (Fig. 2G) suggest that LD32Cs1C1 edited cells have severe defects in protein synthesis, and wild-type snoRNA addback was able to reverse the phenotype. Interestingly, the addback of the M2 mutant could not reverse the phenotype, suggesting that the Nm guided by this snoRNA is essential for global translation (Fig. 2G). To understand the mechanism of the effect on growth, the transcriptome of the LD32Cs1C1 snoRNA-edited parasites and of the edited cells expressing the different addbacks was determined (Supplementary Data 5). The results (Fig. 2H) show that 1024 mRNAs (681 down and 343 up-regulated) were differentially regulated (p-adj < 0.01, fold change >/<2-fold) in the snoRNA edited cells, and only 232 mRNAs were differentially expressed in wild-type addback cells, suggesting that most (77%) of the effect on the transcriptome was abolished upon wild-type snoRNA addback. Whereas, in the M2 addback cells, 508 mRNAs (49%) remained differentially expressed, due to the lack of Am479 modification. Gene ontology (GO) analysis of the 1024 differentially expressed mRNAs suggests that genes involved in microtubule-based movements and protein phosphorylation are significantly affected in the edited cells (Fig. 2I).

To gain further insights into the effect of snoRNA depletion on ribosome structure, we purified polysomes, reconstituted the post-decoding pretranslocation state (classical-PRE) complex in vitro25 using pre-programmed mRNA and tRNAs, and subjected these complexes to cryo-EM single particle analysis (SPA). Following several rounds of 2D and 3D classifications, we obtained five different EM maps ranging from 2.1 to 2.3 Å resolution. Local inspection of the SSU rRNA region carrying Am479 in the resulting maps indicated that the apparent density representing the 2′-O-methyl group was present in all the reconstructed maps (Fig. 2J), suggesting that rRNA lacking Am479 is not incorporated into the polysomes. Thus, the lack of Am479 in LSU rRNA results in a reduced number of polysomes, leading to a global reduction in translation.

Overexpression of LM32Cs1C1 compromised growth, affecting the translation of a subset of proteins

Since complete depletion of the snoRNA could not be achieved, suggesting its essentiality and/or the modification it guides, we sought to study its function by overexpression (OE). Interestingly, our analysis also suggested that the level of snoRNA is increased in the amastigote form compared to the promastigote stage (Fig. 1D). To this end, LM32Cs1C1 wild-type and M2 mutant carrying mutations in the interaction domain to guide Am479, as depicted in Fig. 2A-iv, were expressed from the pX-expression vector in promastigote-stage L. major25. The results (Fig. 3A) show a dramatic increase in overexpression of the wild-type (WT) and of its M2-mutant (Fig. 3B). RiboMeth-seq and LC-MS analysis showed an increase in the level of Am479, and a slight but non-significant decrease in the level of Am479 in the cell line overexpressing the wild-type and the mutated M2 snoRNA, respectively (Fig. 3B). Next, the growth of the transgenic parasites was compared to the parental strain OE pX-empty vector, and growth inhibition was observed in both the wild-type and the mutant cells overexpressing the snoRNAs (Fig. 3C). To examine whether cells OE the WT snoRNA and its M2 derivative affect rRNA processing, total RNA from OE cells was hybridized with ITS1 and ITS3 antisense probes35 (Fig. 3D). The results (Fig. 3D) indicate that the levels of SSU and LSU precursors were unchanged between the parental strain and cells OE the wild-type or its mutant M2 derivative, suggesting that LM32Cs1C1 OE does not affect rRNA processing. To examine the translation capacity of cells overexpressing the snoRNA, global translation was assessed by measuring the incorporation of puromycin using the SUnSET assay36 (Fig. 3E). The results demonstrate no significant reduction in protein synthesis in either WT-OE or M2-OE cells. Interestingly, minor defects in the polysomal pattern of the OE cells (Fig. 3F) were observed, and no significant difference was observed in the composition of the 80S ribosomal protein as determined by mass-spectrometry (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Data 6).

To better understand the impact of LM32Cs1C1 OE and mutation on translation, the poly-A mRNA from whole-cell extracts and those associated with translating ribosomes were isolated and sequenced (Fig. 3H, I, and Supplementary Data 7, 8). The levels of only 9 and 18 mRNAs were significantly (p-adj < 0.01, fold change >/<2-fold) downregulated upon WT-OE and M2-OE in the whole-cell extracts (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Data 8), which is consistent with the specific effect on translation. A scatter plot illustrating the pattern of ribosome-associated mRNA levels in cells overexpressing WT and M2, presented in Fig. 3I, shows that 1165 (944 down-regulated and 221 up-regulated) and 1573 (1149 down-regulated and 424 up-regulated) mRNAs (p-adj < 0.01, fold change >/<2-fold) changed their translation pattern upon WT and M2 snoRNA overexpression. Comparison of both patterns suggests that the M2 mutant was able to reverse the translation pattern of only 213 out of 1165 mRNAs, suggesting that 952 mRNAs are differentially translated in both WT and M2 overexpressing cells, and indicating that the domain responsible for guiding Nm is essential for the translation of only a small subset (18%) of mRNAs. The major effect on translation may therefore stem from an additional function of the snoRNA. GO analysis of down-regulated mRNAs in the WT-OE translatome suggests that genes associated with ribosomes and protein heterodimerization are significantly affected in OE cells (Fig. 3J). Whereas genes involved in protein phosphorylation are enriched in the mRNAs upregulated in the OE translatome. Comparison of the WT-OE translatome with the previously published transcriptome of L. major amastigote vs. promastigote37 suggests that 52 mRNAs that are down-regulated in our translatome were also down-regulated significantly in the amastigote transcriptome. Some of these genes encode for proteins involved in flagella formation, which is known to be down-regulated in amastigote, suggesting that the overexpression of the snoRNA contributes to this phenomenon during differentiation37.

Cryo-EM analysis of ribosomes from cells overexpressing LM32Cs1C1 snoRNA

To further investigate the role of the snoRNA and its guided Nm in translation regulations, we determined the 3D structure of 80S ribosomes from L. major cells overexpressing LM32Cs1C1 wild-type gene (LM80S-WT-OE) and its mutated version (LM80S-M2-OE) using single particle (SPA) cryo-EM techniques. 80S ribosomes in their native state were purified from log-phase cells and subjected to SPA cryo-EM data collection. The initial dataset included 134493 and 714733 ribosome particles for LM80S-WT-OE and LM80S-M2-OE, respectively. Upon several rounds of 2D and 3D classifications, 3.0 and 2.73 Å cryo-EM maps were obtained, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4). The maps were further improved by Bayesian polishing and refinement, resulting in 2.72 Å (PDB ID: 8QHU) and 2.38 Å (PDB ID: 8QIE) resolution, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Data 9). The resolution of the head and body regions of SSU was further improved by applying multibody refinement (Supplementary Fig. 3). The final composite EM map enabled us to model and refine all nine rRNA species (SSU rRNA, the LSU rRNA, 5.8S rRNA, 5S rRNA, four srRNAs (sR1, 2, 4, 6), E-site tRNA, and 73 RPs) (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Data 10). Local inspection of the region carrying Am479 in the resulting maps indicated that this region is clearly resolved in both maps (Fig. 4B). In addition, the high-resolution EM maps enabled us to model ~150 rRNA modifications, including 2′-O-methylation sites, pseudouridines, and base modifications, such as m1A, m62A, m5C, m7G, and m1acpΨ (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Data 1). Together with our previously published cryo-EM structures of the L. major 80S ribosome (PDB ID: 8A3W, 8A98, 8OVJ), our cryo-EM data verified the existence of ~103 2′-O-methylation modifications in L. major 80S ribosomes, as initially indicated by RibOxi-seq.

Comparison of the structures of LM80S-WT-OE and LM80S-M2-OE, with our previously reported cryo-EM structure of the parental strain (LM80S) (PDB ID: 8OVJ), revealed a highly similar overall architecture. All RPs modeled in LM80S were present in the LM80S-OE and LM80S-M2-OE ribosomes. We compared the three ribosome structures to reveal the structural significance of snoRNA overexpression targeting Am479 and its neighboring residues. We found almost identical structural features of rRNA nucleotides and RPs in this region (Fig. 4C), suggesting that overexpression of LM32Cs1C1 has very limited or no significant impact on the structure of SSU rRNA in the region containing Am479. We then searched for any additional site(s) that may have been affected by the OE of the snoRNA (Fig. 5A) based on its WC base-pairing interactions. Indeed, this search revealed structural changes in the LSU part of the B7a intersubunit bridge (Fig. 5). This bridge links the platform domain of SSU with the base of L1 stalk adjacent to the E-site in the LSU38. In the high-resolution 1.98 Å cryo-EM structure of the E. coli 70S ribosome39 (PDB ID: 7K00), the B7a bridge is formed by A702 (A1160 in L. major) that flips out of the h23 helical axis and interacts with A1848 (A453 in L. major) via a stacking interaction in the minor groove of H68 (Supplementary Fig. 5). Similarly, in the high-resolution 1.67 Å cryo-EM structure of the human 80S ribosome40 (PDB ID: 8GLP), the B7a bridge is formed by SSU-G970 (A1160 in L. major) stacking with LSU-A3712 (A454 in L. major) (Supplementary Fig. 5). Inspection of the high-resolution 2.4 Å cryo-EM structure of the L. major parental strain ribosome (LM80S-PRE, PDB ID: 8RXH) revealed a similar interaction, wherein the SSU-A1160 of h23 interacts with the sugar moiety of LSUβ-G452 in H68 (Fig. 5B). Indeed, by comparing LM80S-WT-OE and LM80S-M2-OE ribosome structures with the LM80S ribosome structure, we found that in both LM80S-WT-OE and LM80S-M2-OE ribosome structures, the B7a bridge is formed by the SSU rRNA residue A1160, which is stacked with LSU rRNA residue A453, instead of the A1160–G452 interaction in the LM80S ribosome structure (Supplementary Fig. 6). Since a similar bridge-forming interaction was observed in both LM80S-WT-OE and LM80S-M2-OE ribosome structures (Supplementary Fig. 6), it seems that the mutation abolishing the ability of snoRNA to guide Am479 does not hamper its ability to interact with H68 rRNA.

Since the EM density of H68 nucleotides was of lower quality in the vacant 80S ribosome structures, we sought to determine the structure of LM80S-M2-OE ribosome in the post-decoding pre-translocation state (classical-PRE)25 using pre-programmed mRNA and tRNAs, compared to parental strain (LM80S-PRE) PRE-ribosomes (PDB ID: 8RXH). Thus, we obtained a high-resolution cryo-EM map of ~2.25 Å using the LM80S-M2-PRE-ribosome complex. The EM density allowed us to model all RPs, the rRNAs that were modeled in the LM80S-M2-OE ribosomes, and P-E tRNAs (Supplementary Data 10). The high-resolution EM density of LM80S-M2-PRE ribosome enabled us to better model H68 and the B7a intersubunit bridge (Fig. 5C). Comparison of the LM80S-M2-PRE ribosome structure (PDB ID: 9FXO) with LM80S-PRE (PDB ID: 8RXH) suggested that U451 is “flipped out” of the H68 helical axis and forms a stacking interaction with uL2-W256, whereas in the LM80S-PRE structure U451 is located within the helical axis (Fig. 5C). This flipping out movement enables LSUβ-A453 to form a B7a bridge with SSU-A1160, as was seen in LM80S-M2-PRE structure (Fig. 5C). Comparison of human (PDB ID: 8GLP) and E. coli (PDB ID: 7K00) B7a bridge with LM80S-PRE and LM80S-M2-PRE suggest that U451 is located within the helical axis of human (U3709, human rRNA numbering) and E.coli (G1846, E.coli rRNA numbering) ribosomes and similar B7a intersubunit bridge is formed in the OE cells albeit difference in the nucleotide composition (Fig. 5D). Interestingly such flipping out movement was not observed in the ribosomes purified from LD32Cs1C1 edited cells (Supplementary Fig. 7) suggesting that only overexpression of the snoRNA induces such movement. Since the polysomal pattern of the OE cells did not reveal accumulation of 40S and 60S subunits (Fig. 3F), and no significant changes in pre-rRNA levels were observed (Fig. 3D), the altered nucleotides in B7a bridge did not significantly affect overall 80S formation, suggesting that no major defects in ribosome formation and assembly from its subunits is present, which could explain the defects observed in translation (Fig. 3I), suggesting a direct effect on translation of only subset of mRNAs.

LM32Cs1C1 acts as a chaperone, affecting the structure and function of mature ribosomes

The effect of OE partially emerges from its effect on Am479 modification. However, the structural change observed in (Fig. 5) directed us to investigate the possible interaction of the snoRNA with the affected LSU domain. Indeed, bioinformatic analysis indicates that LM32Cs1C1 has the potential to interact by perfect base-pairing of 12 nt with H68-LSUβ rRNA in the B7a bridge (Fig. 6Ai), alongside the structural changes that were observed in ribosomes from cells that overexpressed the snoRNA (Fig. 5C). These structural changes are near the interaction domain of the snoRNA with LSUβ rRNA, which is located 17nt upstream of the D’-box (Supplementary Fig. 8). To validate this interaction domain of the snoRNA with LSUβ rRNA we performed in vivo UV crosslinking by treating cells with 4′-aminomethyl-trioxsalen hydrochloride (AMT) and irradiation at 365 nm20. The purified RNA was randomly fragmented, neighboring 5′–3′ ends were ligated, and crosslinking was reversed at 254 nm. The RNA was subjected to cDNA synthesis, and PCR analysis was conducted with oligonucleotides specific to the snoRNA, around the region of Am479 in SSU rRNA and the H68 region of LSUβ rRNA. As expected, our approach detected chimeric RNA molecules carrying both the snoRNA and the SSU rRNA region around Am479 modification (Supplementary Fig. 9). In addition, we also detected chimeric RNAs carrying the snoRNA and LSUβ rRNA region around H68, suggesting that the snoRNA interacts with both SSU and LSUβ rRNA in the cell. To further examine the role of this interaction domain, a mutation, termed M6-OE, was introduced to disrupt this interaction (mutations are highlighted in green (Fig. 6A-i)). The mutant snoRNA was cloned and overexpressed, and its expression was compared to that of the cells overexpressing the wild-type gene (Fig. 6A-ii). A significant increase in snoRNA level in the OE cells was observed compared to parental cells expressing the empty vector. RiboMeth-seq and LC-MS analysis of M6-OE cells indicate that the level of Am479 is increased similarly to the WT-OE, suggesting that the M6 mutation does not affect the methylation level (Fig. 6B). Monitoring the growth of this M6-OE alongside the parental strain overexpressing pX-empty vector, WT-OE, and M2-OE, which fails to guide the Am479 modification, shows that M6-OE grows similarly to the parental strain (Fig. 6C), suggesting that the dominant effect on growth stems from the chaperone function of the overexpressed snoRNA.

To monitor the effect of the M6-OE on the structure of H68-LSUβ rRNA in the B7a bridge, we purified the 80S subunit from these cells, reconstituted the classical-PRE complex, and subjected it to cryo-EM SPA analysis. After several rounds of classification, Bayesian polishing and refinement, we obtained a high-resolution EM map (LM80S-M6-PRE) of ~1.96 Å nominal resolution. EM density of H68 in this EM map suggested that U451 is located within the helical axis (Fig. 6D), similar to ribosomes from parental cells (LM80S-PRE, Fig. 5C), suggesting that the structural changes in the OE were elicited as a result of snoRNA base-pairing interactions with the predicted LSU region.

To assess whether M6-OE could revert the effect of WT-OE, ribosomes were fractionated, and the transcriptome of the translating mRNA was determined (Fig. 6E and Supplementary Data 7, 8). The results in Fig. 6E suggest that only 581 mRNAs were differentially translated (501 down-regulated and 80 up-regulated) (p-adj < 0.01) in M6-OE cells. Interestingly, comparison of differentially translated mRNAs in M6-OE and WT-OE cells indicates that the translation of 63% of the altered mRNAs was recovered in M6-OE cells (732 out of 1165 differentially altered in translation) (Fig. 6F). To further validate the defects in translation, we performed label-free quantitative analysis of whole-cell lysates prepared from WT-OE and M6-OE cells (Supplementary Data 11). The analysis was able to quantify more than 7358 proteins. The results (Fig. 6G-i) suggest that 216 proteins (139 upregulated and 77 downregulated) were differentially expressed (p < 0.05, FC > 1.4) in the WT-OE cells compared to the parental strain. Comparison of the WT-OE and M6-OE proteome suggests that the level of 144 (67%) proteins was recovered by M6-OE, suggesting that the M6-mutation partially recovers (~63–67%) the translation phenotype (Fig. 6G-ii), as was seen in the analysis of ribosome-bound mRNAs (Fig. 6E, F). By applying a strict multiple-testing correction (q < 0.05, fold change >1.4), we identified only 12 significant protein changes that are changed upon WT-OE. Among these, the differential levels of 9 proteins were reversed upon M6-mutation (Supplementary Data 11). GO analysis of 139 proteins downregulated in the proteome suggested that genes involved in flagella and axonemes are enriched upon snoRNA overexpression (Fig. 5G-iii). No enrichment was found for the 77 proteins upregulated in the proteome. Note, the number of mRNAs that were differentially expressed was greater in the ribosome-associated mRNA than those seen in the proteome. Previous studies from our group25 and others17 have observed such a difference when RNA-seq experiments were compared to proteome results. One potential explanation could be the increased sensitivity of RNA-seq in quantifying translating mRNAs on the ribosomes compared to the proteome, which can also be influenced by factors such as protein folding and stability. To better understand the features of the ribosome-associated mRNAs that are affected in OE cells, we analysed the codon usage of the differentially translated mRNAs (Fig. 6H). In the mRNAs that are up-regulated in the ribosome fractions, only two codons (Gln-CAA and Glu-GAA) were enriched significantly (p-adj < 0.001). Whereas, in mRNAs that were down-regulated, 3 codons were enriched (Lys-AAG, Arg-CGC, and Ile-ATC).

A mechanistic model for the preferential translation of mRNAs carrying codon bias mediated by H68 and E-site tRNA

Previous studies from our group suggested that H68 is a dynamic helix that moves during various stages of mRNA translocation in coordination with the E-site tRNA and the L1 stalk41. Interestingly, we previously showed that antisense PNA oligos targeting the stem-loop regions of H68 (orthologous to the binding site of LD/LM32Cs1C1) affect the ability of ribosomes to translate protein in vitro41, suggesting that perturbation of the helix could potentially affect its interaction with E site-tRNA and the L1 stalk. Analysis of the LM80S-PRE ribosomes suggests that H68 nucleotides (G456-C458) are in close proximity to the acceptor stem of the E-site tRNA (Fig. 7A). Since the ribosome purification protocol for cryo-EM yielded vacant ribosomes bound to only endogenous E-site tRNAs, and do not contain A/P-site tRNAs, as seen in Fig. 4A, we sought to purify and quantify E-tRNA from monosomes and polysomes preparations. Accordingly, we purified polysomes and subjected them to SPA analysis and found that only 3D classes representing ribosomes with E-site tRNA are present in our polysome preparation (Supplementary Fig. 10). Two replicates of pooled monosomes and polysomes were purified from PS cells carrying empty-vector (pX-empty) and wild-type LM32Cs1C1-OE cells, and E-site tRNA was extracted and subjected to tRNA-seq. Since tRNAs are rich in RNA modifications, we used the thermostable group II intron reverse transcriptase that introduces mutations in nucleotides carrying specific modifications42. Long read sequencing was used to capture full-length tRNAs. Quantification of tRNAs encoding 42 unique codons is presented in Supplementary Data 12. The DESeq2 analysis of tRNAs from two biological replicates suggested that only four tRNAs encoding Tyr-UAC, Ser-UCG, Pro-CCA, and Glu-GAA were significantly (p-adj < 0.05) downregulated, and two tRNAs encoding Leu-UUG and Leu-CUA codons were upregulated (Fig. 7B). In addition, we also performed tRNA-seq of A-site and P-site tRNA from the pX-empty and LM32Cs1C1-OE cells upon treatment with cycloheximide that prevents translocation by blocking the E-site. Note, this preparation contains A-site, P-site tRNAs and also tRNAs in their hybrid state translocating between A- and P-sites during translation. The results (Fig. 7B) suggest that only a single tRNA encoding for the Glu-GAA codon was significantly reduced in the A-site and P-site pool. Interestingly, the Glu-GAA codon is among the two most significantly enriched codons in the ribosome-bound mRNAs that are upregulated upon snoRNA overexpression (Fig. 6H). The additional E-site tRNAs that are affected according to the DESeq2 analysis may result from differences in binding to the H68, but their deregulated level do not contribute to codon bias and translation. To understand how changes in H68 could affect A-site and P-site tRNA levels, we compared ribosome structures in the L.major classical PRE-state (PDB ID: 8RXH) and also the human hybrid-PRE state (PDB ID: 6Y57) (Fig. 7C). Interestingly, H68 interacts with P-site tRNA in the hybrid P/E state, suggesting that in cells overexpressing LM32Cs1C1 snoRNA, the tRNA encoding for the Glu-GAA codon exits the E-site and P-site relatively faster than other tRNAs (Fig. 7D).

Our previous study suggested that loss of a single pseudouridine in H69 affected its local structure, altered its interaction with A-site tRNA, and affected the translation of a subset of mRNAs carrying specific codons25. Previous analysis of tRNAs that differentially interact with H69 suggested that tRNAs carrying specific RNA modifications in the H69-tRNA interaction region are preferentially selected by the ribosomes lacking a single pseudouridine25. The complete repertoire of Leishmania tRNA modification is unknown, especially in the acceptor stem that interacts with H68. It is currently not clear how flipping of U451 in the H68 in the OE cells affects the interaction with specific tRNAs. The data therefore support the notion that overexpression of LM32Cs1C1 resulted in structural differences in H68 of LSU rRNA affecting the efficient binding of specific tRNAs.

Discussion

In this study, we mapped the Nm on rRNA of both life stages of three Leishmania species. Our study identified more than a hundred Nm sites with high confidence. Using RiboMeth-seq, we identified several Nm sites that are differentially modified between amastigote and promastigote. We focused on the role of Am479, guided by LD/LM32Cs1C1, which was found to exhibit elevated modification levels in amastigotes due to increased snoRNA levels. Depletion of the snoRNA by CBE suggested that Am479 is essential for ribosome formation, as ribosome subunits lacking the Nm are not incorporated into translating ribosomes. Overexpression of this snoRNA resulted in a specific effect on translation, affecting over a thousand mRNAs. However, this major impact on translation observed in the OE strain is mostly not because of the Nm (Am479) guided by the snoRNA, but rather due to its function as a chaperone for properly shaping the structure of the ribosome.

Since the discovery that C/D snoRNAs guide Nm modifications, their role as chaperones in ribosome biogenesis was neglected, apart from that of a small number of snoRNAs that are directly involved in rRNA processing, by removing pre-rRNA spacers (ITS/ETS). Here, we show that the chaperone function of a snoRNA dictates ribosome structure. Overexpression of a single snoRNA resulted in structural perturbation, affecting the nucleotides in H68, as observed using cryo-EM techniques. Accordingly, this study revives an old concept regarding the chaperone function of C/D snoRNAs.

In addition, we show that Nm levels are not homogeneous across rRNA throughout the parasite’s life cycle. Variation in the levels of some Nm modifications was observed in mammalian cells, whereas most Nm sites are nearly fully modified. Notably, the modification level of a subset of sites varies only during specific developmental stages at the tissue level43. In cancer cells, 56–90% of the differential Nms are located on the surface of the ribosome16. It was previously demonstrated that in yeast, certain Nms in rRNA are only partially methylated over the population44. This also seems to be the case in T. brucei24,26 and Leishmania species (Fig. 1A). We suggest that the low level of modification on specific sites is due to the large number of sites available for Nm modification, and limiting levels of core proteins to assemble a large number of each snoRNP, thereby restricting the modification at each position24. Alternatively, the proximity of the Nm sites, each with its own snoRNP, in the rRNA results in competition between snoRNAs for access to neighboring nucleotides. The Nm guided by LD/LM32Cs1C1 is developmentally regulated, and it correlates with an increase in the level of its guiding snoRNA. The presence of low-level Nm on certain sites may enable regulation by increasing these modifications under various stress conditions. More importantly, the elevated level of the snoRNA may have an impact on ribosome structure and affect the translation of a subset of proteins, as seen by overexpression of the snoRNA (Figs. 3I and 6G). Indeed, Grumpy snoRNA is dramatically elevated during the transition in the mammalian host of the bloodstream form Trypanosoma brucei (T. brucei)45. The effect of grumpy OE seems to result from the direct effect of snoRNA on mRNA expression. In this study, we demonstrate that LD/LM32Cs1C1 primarily affects ribosome structure and function. However, we cannot exclude its role as an antisense regulator of mRNA or as a guide for Nm on mRNAs.

CRISPR knockout of a single snoRNA guiding Nm in rRNA in human cancer cells affected the translation of distinct mRNAs with codon bias due to their GC/AU content, suggesting that specific Nm (Cm174) modification contributes to the formation of specialized ribosomes17. We recently described the presence of specialized ribosomes in Leishmania25 and trypanosomes26. In Leishmania, a single knockout (sKO) of a snoRNA guiding Ψ in helix 69 (H69) affected ribosome function. Cryo-EM analysis of ribosomes lacking a single Ψ in H69 revealed altered interactions of H69 with helix 44 (h44), which affected the ability of the ribosomes to interact with specific tRNAs25. In addition, translational defects of mRNAs carrying codon bias with specific codon compositions due to selective accommodation of tRNAs by the ribosome was observed25. In T. brucei, sKO of the homologous snoRNA also resulted in a specific defect in translation due to the altered composition of the 80S monosome26. These changes specifically affected the translation of only a subset of proteins, and this was correlated with changes in the ribosomal protein, eS12, as shown by cryo-EM structure. Thus, rRNA modifications could potentially generate ribosomes that preferentially translate state-beneficial proteins. In both Leishmania and T. brucei, the sKO phenotype could be compensated by the addback of the wild-type snoRNA, but not the addback of a mutant defective in guiding the modification25,26. In this study, we show that the effect on translation due to overexpression of LM32Cs1C1 is more dramatic than elimination or overexpression of a single Ψ in these parasites. The impact on translation was clearly seen in cells overexpressing LM32Cs1C1 by monosome-polysome profiling and proteome analysis, mostly due to the C/D snoRNA chaperone activity. However, the CBE showed a clear effect on translation attributed to guiding the Nm modification, since ribosomes lacking this modification were not observed in the CBE cells by cryo-EM SPA analysis. Moreover, the impact on translation in the cells overexpressing the snoRNA is mainly attributed to the chaperone function of the snoRNA in its interaction with LSU rRNA, since mutating this domain (M6-mutation) substantially abolished the effect on translation (Fig. 5E–G).

The dual function of LM/LD32Cs1C1 in guiding modification and acting as a chaperone was further supported by CBE of the snoRNA. This powerful approach for functional analysis of snoRNA is well suited for trypanosomatids, in which most of their snoRNAs are present in repeated clusters in the genome21. Interestingly, despite the presence of 70% Nm modification on rRNA at Am479, growth of the parasite was compromised due to a reduction in this rRNA modification (Fig. 2). The addback experiments with M2 mutation allowed us to identify the specific defects emerging from the reduction in Nm. The addback of wild-type snoRNA was not able to completely compensate for the defects in the snoRNA-depleted cells, suggesting that this snoRNA may have additional functions, such as guiding Nm on mRNAs46 or other small RNAs, or acting as an anti-sense regulator on mRNA, as was recently shown by our group for Grumpy snoRNA45. Indeed, mature translating ribosomes lacking Am479 on rRNA, based on cryoEM, were not observed. Despite almost complete depletion of the snoRNA, Am479 was observed in ~71% of the rRNA, possibly because other snoRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 3) can potentially guide this modification, suggesting that there is a backup system to assure the modification of very essential sites. Interestingly, depletion of LD32Cs1C1 significantly reduced global translation and affected the levels of hundreds of mRNAs (Fig. 2G, H). Indeed, recent studies have suggested that the ribosome is a master regulator of the mRNA degradation pathway, in which ribosome transit rate is a major determinant of mRNA half-life47.

This study, along with our previous study on the effect of Ψ on ribosomes25, suggests that the rRNA modification (Nm and Ψ) as well as the guiding snoRNA, affect the ribosome structure, leading to differential association of tRNAs, thus creating codon bias in the translating mRNAs, contributing to translation regulation.

Prior to the finding that C/D snoRNA guides Nm, it was proposed that snoRNA could function as chaperones by assisting the intricate folding process of the huge pre-rRNA into productive domains. Currently, the only snoRNAs implicated in chaperone activity are snoRNAs involved in rRNA processing, which include mainly U3, U14, and snR30. These essential snoRNAs are directly involved as chaperones in the cleavage of the pre-rRNA to release SSU rRNA6. The function of LM32Cs1C1 seems different from that of these snoRNAs, as it does not interact with pre-rRNA spacers, but its presence affects the structure of the mature ribosomes, with no effect on rRNA processing. Indeed, the snoRNA-edited cells had no rRNA processing defects. Thus, this study suggests that snoRNA has a chaperone function in dictating ribosome structure, unrelated to rRNA processing or RNA modification. An analogous chaperone function of snoRNAs may exist in humans, since recent studies using in vivo cross-linking identified snoRNAs that are associated with rRNA, but not in regions known for carrying Nm modification; thus, these interactions may potentially reflect chaperone activities of the snoRNA48.

We previously reported the chaperone function of a small RNA on the spliced leader-associated (SLA1) RNA49. We showed that although SLA1 RNA guides Ψ on the trypanosome spliced leader (SL) RNA, its primary function is to fold the SL RNA into an active conformation that is needed for its interaction with the capping machinery50. Thus, additional chaperone functions may exist for other ncRNAs, including long non-coding RNAs.

In summary, Leishmania ribosomes are modified with over a hundred Nms, comparable to the numbers found in humans. These Nms are predominantly located within functional sites of the ribosome and are guided by more than eighty snoRNAs. We identified two Nms that are commonly regulated between the two life stages of three distinct Leishmania species. SnoRNA guiding such conserved modifications possesses a dual role, both guiding methylation and acting as a chaperone to ensure proper folding of H68. Overexpression of these snoRNA species changes the structure and function of the ribosomes. This suggests that the chaperone function of snoRNA is not solely tied to rRNA processing or modification, and underscores its critical role in translation regulation.

Methods

Animals, parasites and cell lines

Female Golden Syrian hamsters (Mesocricetus auratus RjHan:AURA) weighing 60–70 g, and 6-week-old NMRI-nu female mice were purchased from Janvier Laboratories (Le Genest-Saint-Isle, France), and handled under specific pathogen-free conditions in A3 animal facilities accredited by the French Ministry of Agriculture for performing experiments on live rodents (agreement A 75-15-01-2). L. donovani strain Ld1S (MHOM/SD/62/1S-CL2D) was propagated in hamsters, L. major strain Friedlin FV1 (MHOM/IL/80/ Friedlin) and L. amazonensis strain LV79 (MPRO/BR/1972/M1841) expressing mCherry were propagated in NMRI-nu mice. Amastigotes were purified from cutaneous lesions (LV79 and FV1) and livers (Ld1S) of infected animals.

L. donovani and L. amazonensis promastigotes were differentiated from animal-derived amastigotes and maintained in M199 complete medium (M199, 10% FBS, 25 mM HEPES; 100 µM adenine, 2 mM L-glutamine, 10 µg/ml folic acid, 13.7 µM hemin, 4.2 mM NaHCO3, 1xRPMI1640 vitamins, 8 µM 6-biopterin, 100 units penicillin and 100 µg/ml streptomycin, pH 7.4). L. major promastigotes were also differentiated from animal-derived amastigotes and maintained in M199, 10% FBS, 25 mM HEPES; 100 µM adenine, 2 mM L-glutamine, biotin 1 µg/ml, 13.7 µM hemin, 4.2 mM NaHCO3, 100 units penicillin and 100 µg/ml streptomycin, pH 7.4.

Generation of transgenic L. major parasites overexpressing snoRNA

To generate L. major (Friedlin strain) overexpressing LM32Cs1C1 snoRNA, the regions flanking the snoRNA were cloned into pX-Neo plasmid51 using the primers listed in Supplementary Data 13. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed to introduce mutations in the rRNA interaction domains. The template plasmid was digested with DpnI (NEB), and clones were isolated.

Editing of the snoRNA gene

Guide RNA (gRNA) targeting the LD32Cs1C1 gene was designed using BE-Designer (http://www.rgenome.net/be-designer/) and EuPaGDT (http://grna.ctegd.uga.edu/). Since CBE takes place within the 4–12 nt window of gRNA, the gRNA was designed such that the cytosine in the D-box motif (CUGA) is included in this window. The gRNA was cloned into the pLdCH-hyBE4max-LmjFDBD29 plasmid between BbsI restriction sites. The primers are listed in Supplementary Data 13. To generate LD32Cs1C1-edited L. donovani cells, the plasmid was transfected into Ld1S (MHOM/SD/62/1S-CL2D) with or without gRNA, selected using Hygromycin, and cloned by serial dilution. Clonal population was derived and verified using Sanger sequencing and Northern analysis. All primers used in this study were purchased by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT).

Preparation of the C/D snoRNome

snoRNAs associated with the SNU13 (a C/D snoRNP) were isolated using the tandem affinity purification (TAP) method21. To this end, L. donovani cells (2 × 1011 cells) overexpressing SNU13-TAP was washed twice with PBS and once with buffer-I (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.7), 150 mM KCl, and 3 mM MgCl2). The cells were resuspended in 15 ml of buffer-II (buffer-I with 1 mM DTT and 10 μg/ml leupeptin), equilibrated by nitrogen cavitation (Parr Instruments Co.) at 750 p.s.i. N2 for 1 h at 4 °C, and disrupted by pressure release. After release of the pressure, the protease inhibitor mixture (Roche Applied Science) was added, and the extract was treated with 0.5% Triton X-100. The extract was incubated at 4 °C for 15 min, passed through a syringe, cleared of cell debris by centrifugation (15,000 × g), and the supernatant was incubated with rabbit IgG-agarose beads (200 μl) (Sigma) while rotating for 2 h. The beads were washed 5 times with TEV-buffer (buffer-I with 0.5 mM DTT, 0.5 mM EDTA) and incubated overnight in 1.5 ml of TEV-buffer with 200 units of tobacco etch virus protease (Promega). After centrifugation, the supernatant was incubated with 50 μl of Strep-T actin-Sepharose beads (IBA) for 1 h. The beads were washed with buffer-III (TEV-buffer with 2 mM CHAPS (GE Healthcare)), and the complexes were eluted with elution buffer (100 mM Tris-Cl (pH 8), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA) containing 2.5 mM d-desthiobiotin (Sigma). The RNA associated with NHP2 was recovered by phenol: chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation.

RNA extracted from the elution was used for library preparation, essentially as described below. Briefly, 800 ng of RNA was dephosphorylated with FastAP Thermosensitive Alkaline Phosphatase (Thermo Scientific), cleaned by Agencourt RNA clean XP beads (Beckman Coulter) and ligated to a 3′ linker using a high concentration T4 RNA Ligase 1 (NEB) in a buffer containing DMSO, ATP, PEG 8000 and RNase inhibitor (NEB). The ligated RNA was cleaned from excess linker using Dynabeads® MyOne™ SILANE beads (Thermo Scientific), and first-strand cDNA was prepared using the AffinityScript Reverse Transcriptase enzyme (Agilent). The RNA was subsequently degraded using 2 μl of 1 M NaOH, and the cDNA was cleaned using Dynabeads® MyOne™ SILANE beads (Thermo Scientific). The cDNA was further ligated to the 3′ adapter using high-concentration T4 RNA Ligase 1 (NEB) and cleaned of excess adapter by using Dynabeads® MyOne™ SILANE beads (Thermo Scientific). The adapter ligated cDNA was PCR enriched using NEBNext® High-Fidelity (NEB) polymerase (9 PCR cycles), separated on an E-Gel EX agarose gel (Invitrogen) and size selected at the range of 150–300 bp (containing ~30–180nt corresponding to RNA). The amplicons were gel-purified using NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up kit (Macherey-Nagel), and sequenced in a Nextseq system (Illumina) in paired-end mode (40 million reads for each sample). The L. major SNU13 TAP data were derived from our previous study21.

Northern analysis

For Northern analysis, total RNA was extracted and separated on a 10% acrylamide denaturing gel. Antisense RNA probes were prepared by in vitro transcription using α-32P-UTP52,53. Primers used for in vitro transcription are listed in Supplementary Data 13.

RibOxi-seq library preparation and data analysis

Total RNA samples were initially assessed by Bioanalyzer (Agilent) to ensure sample quality. To prepare RibOxi-seq libraries, total RNA was denatured for 3 min at 90 °C and fragmented by Benzonase (final RNA concentration: 100 ng/µL, enzyme 0.5 units/100 µL reaction) on ice for 90 min. Following extraction with acidic phenol-chloroform, the fragmented samples underwent one round oxidation-elimination cycle. The oxidized RNA was then ligated to the NEB Universal miRNA cloning linker using T4 RNA ligase 2, truncated KQ (NE Biolabs, M0373S) at 16 °C overnight. Next, 25 µM RibOxi RT primer was annealed to the reaction before ligation of 5′ RNA linker using T4 RNA ligase I at room temperature for 1 h and subsequent reverse-transcription using Protoscript II (NE Biolabs, M0368S). cDNA was then purified with AmpureXP beads at a 1:1.8 ratio. Illumina compatible forward and reverse PCR primers (10 µM) were added together with Q5 master mix (NE Biolabs, M0544S) followed by Ampure XP purification with 1:1 ratio. A custom cycling program was used to amplify the final PE libraries for 5 cycles at an annealing temperature of 55 °C, and the remaining 23 cycles at an annealing temperature of 62 °C. Library QC was performed with an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2200 and DNA1000 chips and reagents. Three biological replicates were used for each Leishmania species. Our RibOxi-seq data analysis pipeline is available as a Jupyter notebook and R Markdown at the GitHub repository (https://github.com/yz201906/LM_rRNA_RibOxi), where Leishmania major rRNA was used as a sample dataset.

Alkaline hydrolysis and RiboMeth-seq library preparation

Total RNA (5–10 μg) was denatured at 90 °C for 2 min in a thermocycler. Then, an equal volume of buffer (NaHCO3/Na2CO3, pH 9.9) was added, and RNA samples were incubated at 90 °C for 20 min24. The hydrolyzed RNA was then used for library preparation. Briefly, 800 ng of the RNA was dephosphorylated with FastAP Thermosensitive Alkaline Phosphatase (Thermo Scientific) and cleaned by Agencourt RNA clean XP beads (Beckman Coulter). The RNA was then ligated to a 3′ linker using high-concentration T4 RNA Ligase 1 (NEB) in a buffer containing DMSO, ATP, PEG 8000 and RNase inhibitor (NEB) for 1.5 h at 22 °C. The ligated RNA was purified from excess linker using Dynabeads® MyOne™ SILANE beads (Thermo Scientific), and first-strand cDNA was prepared using the AffinityScript Reverse Transcriptase enzyme (Agilent) at 55 °C for 45 min. Next, the RNA was degraded using 2 μl of 1 M NaOH, and the cDNA was cleaned using Dynabeads® MyOne™ SILANE beads (Thermo Scientific). The cDNA was further ligated to 3′ adapters using a high concentration T4 RNA Ligase 1 (NEB) overnight at 22 °C and cleaned of excess adapter by using Dynabeads® MyOne™ SILANE beads (Thermo Scientific). The adapter-ligated cDNA was PCR-enriched using NEBNext® High-Fidelity (NEB) polymerase (9 PCR cycles), separated on an E-Gel EX agarose gel (Invitrogen), and size selected for the range of 150–300 bp (containing ~30–180 nt corresponding to RNA). The amplicons were gel-purified using NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up kit (Macherey-Nagel) and sequenced in a Nextseq system (Illumina) in paired-end mode (20-40 million reads for each sample).

RiboMeth-seq data analysis

To analyze the RiboMeth-seq libraries, the reads were initially trimmed of adapter sequences using Trim Galore version 0.4.4 (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore) with the following parameters:--stringency 4 --length 30 --paired --retain_unpaired. The alignment to the reference rRNA sequence was done by STAR (ver 2.0.6) (https://github.com/alexdobin/STAR/releases)54, mapped, and properly paired reads were converted to *.bed using BEDtools v2.26.0 Suite55. The Leishmania genomes used in this study are the Sudanese L. donovani strain 1S2D (Ld1S) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA396645), TriTrypDB–47_Lamazonensis MHOMBR71973M2269_Genome (https://tritrypdb.org/tritrypdb/app/downloads/release-47/LamazonensisMHOMBR71973M2269/fasta/data/), TriTrypDB9.0_LmajorFriedlin_Genome (https://tritrypdb.org/tritrypdb/app/downloads/release-9.0/LmajorFriedlin/fasta/data/).

5′- and 3′-end counting was done directly on the *.bed file using a dedicated Unix script. Analysis was performed by calculation of the MAX score for the detection of Nm residues and the RMS score (score C) for their quantification. To calculate the MAX score, the relative change of end coverage position by position was calculated in both the 5′ → 3′ and reverse directions. The relative change was normalized to average values spanning −6 and +6 nucleotides. The normalized relative change for the 5′ → 3′ and reverse direction was averaged, and the maximal value between the average and normalized relative change was retained (score MAX). RMS score was calculated for score C using the relative impact of neighboring nucleotides by RiboMeth-seq24,28. The Leishmania rRNA sequence and scripts used to analyse RiboMeth-seq are available at the GitHub repository (https://github.com/michaelilab/Leishmania_Nm_Paper/).

Proteolysis, dimethyl labeling and mass spectrometry analysis of proteins

Purified ribosome samples were precipitated in 80% ethanol, and the protein pellets were dissolved in (8.5 M urea, 10 mM DTT, 400 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC), sonicated twice (90%, 10–10 cycles, 5′), and centrifuged. The proteins in the solution were reduced (60 °C for 30 min), modified with 35.2 mM iodoacetamide in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate (in the dark, at room temperature for 30 min) and digested in 1.5 M Urea, 70 mM ABC with modified trypsin (Promega) at a 1:50 enzyme-to-substrate ratio, overnight at 37 °C. A second digestion was done with modified trypsin at a concentration of 1:100 (enzyme to substrate) for 4 h at 37 °C. The resulting peptides were desalted using C18 Top tip columns (Glygen), dried, and re-suspended in 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.9). Labeling by dimethylation was done in the presence of 100 mM NaCBH3 (sterogene, 1 M), by adding light formaldehyde (35% frutarom, 12.3 M) to one of the samples, and heavy formaldehyde (20% w/w, Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, 6.5 M) to the other sample to a final concentration of 200 mM. The samples were incubated for 1 h. Samples were neutralized with 1 M ABC for 30 min, and equal amounts of the light and heavy peptides (determined using Bradford assay) were mixed, cleaned on C18 Top tip columns, and re-suspended in 0.1% formic acid.

The peptides were resolved by reverse-phase chromatography on 0.075 × 180-mm fused silica capillaries (J&W) packed with Reprosil reversed-phase material (Dr Maisch GmbH, Germany). The peptides were eluted with a linear 180 min gradient of 5–28%, a 15 min gradient of 28–95%, and 25 min at 95% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid in water, at a flow rate of 0.15 μl/min. Mass spectrometry was performed by a Q-Exactive HFX mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) in positive mode using repetitive full MS scan followed by collision to induce dissociation (HCD) of the 30 most dominant ions selected from the first MS scan. The mass spectrometry data were analyzed using the Proteome Discoverer 1.4 (Thermo Scientific) software, searching against the L. major from the TriTrypDB v.35 database (https://tritrypdb.org). Results were filtered with rank 1 peptides and 1% false discovery rate. The ratios were normalized according to the protein’s median ratio. Perseus software (https://maxquant.net/perseus/) was used for statistical analysis of the data. Significant outliers relative to a given population were calculated using intensity-dependent calculation. The truncation was based on the Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple hypothesis testing (SignificanceB)56. For the combined analysis of different replicates, a one-sample t-test was used to determine if the mean is significantly different from a fixed value (0). All MS analyses were performed at the Smoler Proteomics Center, Technion, Israel, and raw data are available via ProteomeXchange, with identifier PXD052150. The identity of heavy and light isotope labeled samples is listed in Supplementary Data 14.

Label-free quantitative proteome: proteolysis, mass spectrometry analysis, and data analysis

Cell pellets were resuspended in SDS buffer (2% SDS, 10mM DTT, 100 mM Tris pH 8), boiled at 95oC for 10 minutes and then sonicated twice. The samples were then precipitated in ice-cold acetone (80% final concentration) for 1.5 h at -20 °C, and washed three times with cold 80% acetone. The protein pellets were dissolved in 8.5 M urea, 400 mM ammonium bicarbonate, and 10 mM DTT. Protein amount was estimated using the Bradford assay. The samples were reduced (60 °C for 30 min), modified with 35.2 mM iodoacetamide in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate (room temperature for 30 min in the dark), and digested in 1.5 M urea, 66 mM ammonium bicarbonate with modified trypsin (Promega), overnight at 37 °C at a 1:50 (M/M) enzyme-to-substrate ratio. An additional second trypsinization was done for 4 h at a 1:100 (M/M) enzyme-to-substrate ratio. The tryptic peptides were desalted using an Oasis HLB 96-well µElution Plate (Waters), dried, and re-suspended in 0.1% formic acid in 2% acetonitrile.

The resulting peptides were analyzed by LC-MS/MS using an Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) fitted with a capillary HPLC (Vanquish, Thermo Scientific). The peptides were loaded in solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) on an Ionoptics, AUR3-25075C18-XT, 25 cm × 75 μm ID, 1.7 μm C18 reversed phase analytical column. The peptide mixture was resolved with a 6–34% linear gradient of solvent B (80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid) for 120 min, followed by a 0.1-min increase to 34–99%, and 11 min at 99% solvent B at a flow rate of 0.15 μl/min. Mass spectrometry was performed repetitively in positive mode, using a full MS scan (m/z 380–985, resolution 120,000) followed by DIA scans (10 Da isolation windows with 1 m/z overlap, and resolution 30,000).

The mass spectrometry data was analyzed using the DIA-NN software version 1.9.257,58 against the Leishmania major proteome from the TriTrypDB (https://tritrypdb.org/tritrypdb/), with min peptide length set to 7, a maximum number of missed cleavages set to 1, cysteine carbamidomethylation enabled as a fixed modification, and protein N-term acetylation enabled as a variable modification. Peptide- and protein-level false discovery rates were filtered to 1%. Statistical analysis of the identification and quantitation results was done using Perseus 1.6.7.0 software. All MS analyses were performed at the Smoler Proteomics Center, Technion, Israel, and raw data are available via ProteomeXchange, with identifier PXD068423.

Surface sensing of translation (SUnSET) assay

To measure global translation, mid-log phase L. major cells (20 × 107) were incubated with Puromycin (10 μg/ml) (Invivogen) in fresh M199 media and were incubated for 60 min at 27 °C. Cells were pelleted and washed twice using PBS. Whole cell lysates were prepared and subjected to Western blot analysis using mouse monoclonal anti-puromycin antibody, clone 12D10 ZooMAb® (Sigma, ZMS1016) (1:2000 dilution). Cells treated with cycloheximide before treatment with puromycin were used as a negative control.

Poly (A) RNA sequencing and analysis

Poly (A) RNA sequencing reads were aligned to the L. major Friedlin (v9) genome using SMALT (v0.7.5). Raw read counts were quantified using HTSeq-count59. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.38.3)60. Significance was calculated if the fold change was greater than or equal to two-fold and p-adj < 0.01. Only mRNAs having at least one read in all libraries were used for differential analysis. UTR lengths for L. major mRNAs were calculated based on the dominant 5′ SL sites and 3′ poly (A) sites61. Codon usage analysis was determined using the R package, Cordon (v1.6.0) (https://github.com/BioinfoHR/coRdon). Briefly, this software calculates the synonymous codon usage order, the codon usage bias in a set of coding sequences by assigning a codon usage order score to each synonymous codon. The codon usage order score represents the frequency of each codon in a sequence compared to the expected frequency based on the overall codon usage bias in the sequence. Significant differences between differentially translated mRNA species were determined using the Wilcoxon test. The volcano plots, boxplots, and other plots were created in R (v4.2.2) using the ggplot2 package (https://github.com/tidyverse/ggplot2).

Fractionation of ribosomes by sucrose gradient

Whole cell extracts were prepared from 5 × 109 L. major cells in a buffer containing 150 mM KCl, 20 mM Tris-pH 7.6, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.5 M DTT, and 0.1% NP-40. In addition, 1 µl leupeptin (10 mg/ml) protease inhibitor, and 1 µl of Rnasin (Thermo Scientific) were added to the lysate. Cells were lysed using nitrogen cavitation (90 bar, 45 min, 4 °C), and cell debris was removed by centrifugation (30 min at 8000 rpm) at 4 °C. The lysate (400 μL) was fractionated on a 10–30% or 15–50% (w/v) sucrose gradient by centrifugation at 210,000 × g (35,000 rpm) for 3 h or 288,000 × g (70,000 rpm) for 1.5 h, respectively, in a Beckman SW41 rotor at 4 °C. The monosome fractions were ethanol precipitated, and ribosomes were resuspended in buffer-150 (150 mM KCl, 20 mM Tris-pH 7.6, 10 mM MgCl2) and used for mass-spectrometry analysis. For the preparation of polysome fractions, the cells were incubated with 50 μg/ml cycloheximide for 15 min prior to cell lysis, and 100 μg/ml cycloheximide was added to all buffers mentioned above. RNA from monosome and polysome fractions was isolated using the standard Trizol/chloroform method.

Purification of 80S ribosomes for cryo-EM

Mid-log phase L. major cells (1.5–2 × 107 cells/ml, ~1 g pellet) were washed three times in resuspension buffer (45 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.6, 100 mM KOAc, 10 mM Mg(Oac)2 and 250 mM sucrose) and then suspended in cold buffer-A (45 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.6, 100 mM K(Oac), 10 mM Mg(Oac)2, 250 mM sucrose, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and a 1:40 dilution of RNasin® ribonuclease inhibitor (Promega)). Cells were lysed using nitrogen cavitation (750 p.s.i. N2, 45 min, 4 °C), and cell debris were removed by centrifugation (30 min at 21,000 × g) at 4 °C. The lysate was gently loaded onto a 1.1 M sucrose cushion in cold buffer-B (20 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.6, 150 mM KOAc, 10 mM Mg(Oac)2, 1.1 M sucrose and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol) and centrifuged at 310,000 × g at 4 °C for 15 h (Ti70 rotor, Beckman). Following centrifugation, the pellet was resuspended in cold buffer-C (20 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.6, 150 mM KOAc, 10 mM Mg(Oac)2 and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol) and loaded onto a 10–40% (w/v) sucrose gradient in buffer-C for centrifugation (88,500 × g, 11 h, on SW28 rotor, Beckman). The peak corresponding to 80S ribosomes was collected, balanced with buffer-C and centrifuged at 310,000 × g for 12 h at 4 °C (Ti70 rotor, Beckman). The resulting pellet was suspended in buffer-D (20 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.6, 100 mM KOAc, 10 mM Mg(Oac)2, 10 mM NH4Oac and 1 mM DTT) and centrifuged for 90 min at 245,000 × g (TLA-100 rotor, Beckman). The final ribosome pellet was gently resuspended in buffer-D overnight at 4 °C, and the OD260 was measured. Aliquots were flash-frozen and stored at −80 °C until further use. The identity of the 80S fraction was further confirmed by mass-spectrometry analysis.

Purification of E-site tRNA from ribosomes

Mid-log phase L. major cells (~4 × 1010 cells) were washed three times in resuspension buffer (45 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.6, 100 mM KOAc, 10 mM Mg(Oac)2 and 250 mM sucrose) and then suspended in cold buffer-A (45 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.6, 100 mM K(Oac), 10 mM Mg(Oac)2, 250 mM sucrose, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and a 1:40 dilution of RNasin® ribonuclease inhibitor (Promega). Cells were lysed using nitrogen cavitation (750 p.s.i. N2, 45 min, 4 °C), and cell debris were removed by centrifugation (30 min at 9000 rpm) at 4 °C. The lysate was gently loaded onto a 1.1 M sucrose cushion in cold buffer-B (20 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.6, 150 mM KOAc, 10 mM Mg(Oac)2, 1.1 M sucrose and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol) and centrifuged at 55,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 h (Ti70 rotor, Beckman). Following centrifugation, the pellet was resuspended in cold buffer-C (20 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.6, 150 mM KOAc, 10 mM Mg(Oac)2 and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol) and loaded onto a 10–50% (w/v) sucrose gradient in buffer-C for centrifugation (22,000 rpm, 11 h, on SW28 rotor, Beckman). The peak corresponding to monosomes and polysomes were collected, balanced with buffer-C and centrifuged at 55,000 rpm for 12 h at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was gently suspended in buffer-D (20 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.6, 100 mM KOAc, 10 mM Mg(Oac)2, 10 mM NH4Oac and 1 mM DTT) and centrifuged for 90 min at 75,000 rpm (TLA-100 rotor, Beckman). The final ribosome pellet was gently resuspended in 50 μL buffer-D overnight at 4 °C, and the OD260 was measured. A small aliquot of the polysomes fraction was subjected to cryo-EM SPA analysis (Supplementary Fig. 10). To extract the bound E-site tRNA, 50 μl ribosomes were incubated in 500 μl ribosome-splitting buffer (20 mM Tris-pH 7.5, 300 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT and 2 mM puromycin) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The disassociated ribosome subunits (60S and 40S) were centrifuged for 90 min at 90,000 rpm (TLA-100 rotor, Beckman), and the supernatant was used for E-site tRNA isolation using the standard Trizol/chloroform method.

Purification of A/P-site tRNA from ribosomes

Mid-log phase L. major cells (~4 × 1010 cells) were incubated with 50 μg/ml cycloheximide for 15 min, washed three times in cold buffer-C (20 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.6, 150 mM KOAc, 10 mM Mg(Oac)2, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol and 100 μg/ml cycloheximide) and resuspended in buffer-C containing 100 μg/ml cycloheximide, 1:40 dilution of RNasin® ribonuclease inhibitor (Promega) and protease inhibitor (Roche). Cells were lysed using nitrogen cavitation (750 p.s.i. N2, 45 min, 4 °C), and cell debris were removed by centrifugation (30 min at 14,000 rpm) at 4 °C. The supernatant was loaded onto a 10–50% (w/v) sucrose gradient in buffer-C containing 100 μg/ml cycloheximide for centrifugation (28,000 rpm, 4 h, on SW28 rotor, Beckman). The peak corresponding to monosomes and polysomes were collected, balanced with buffer-C containing 100 μg/ml cycloheximide and centrifuged at 55,000 rpm for 12 h at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was slowly suspended in ribosome-splitting buffer (20 mM Tris-pH 7.5, 300 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT and 2 mM puromycin). The disassociated ribosome subunits (60S and 40S) were centrifuged for 90 min at 90,000 rpm (TLA-100 rotor, Beckman), and the supernatant was used for A/P-site tRNA isolation using the standard Trizol/chloroform method. Since A/P site tRNAs are charged, the tRNAs were further deacetylated in 0.5 M Tris-HCl, pH 9, for 4 h at 25 °C. RNA was recovered by ethanol precipitation, and 800 ng RNA was used for library preparation.

Ternary 80S complex formation with mRNA and tRNAs

To reconstitute the classical-PRE-ribosome complex with mRNA and three tRNA molecules, we sequentially mixed vacant 80S ribosomes with an mRNA fragment coding for MET (AUG) and PHE (UUC) codons (CACCAUGUUCAAA, Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc.), a P-site tRNAfMet (E. coli, Sigma), and an A-site tRNAPhe (E. coli, Sigma) at a 1:100:5:5 stoichiometric ratio. The ternary complex was later incubated with paromomycin (Sigma). The complex assembly was performed at 37 °C in buffer-D (20 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.6, 100 mM KOAc, 10 mM Mg(OAc)2, 10 mM NH4OAc, and 1 mM DTT) with a relaxation time of 15 min after the addition of each component.

Cryo-EM data collection and refinement

To prepare cryo-EM grids, 3.5 μl of ribosome suspension (~10 A of A260) was applied onto glow-discharged holey carbon grids (Quantifoil R2/2) coated with a continuous thin carbon film. The grids were blotted and plunge-frozen using Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fischer Scientific). A Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fischer Scientific) operating at 300 kV equipped with a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan Inc.) was used for collecting cryo-EM micrographs at liquid nitrogen temperature at a nominal magnification of ×105,000, with a pixel size of 0.85 Å/pixel and a dose rate of ~1 electron/Å2/frame. Defocus values ranged from −0.5 to −1.5 μm. Relion 4 and 5 were used for data processing62. Motion correction and contrast transfer function parameters were estimated using Motioncor263 and CTFFIND-364, respectively. The extracted particles were subjected to several rounds of unsupervised 3D classification using a low-pass filtered cryo-EM density map. 3D classes similar to 80S particles were selected and subjected to auto-refinement in Relion 4 and 5. Following initial refinement, particles were subjected to CTF refinement, Bayesian polishing, and further refinement. The resulting high-resolution 3D density map was then subjected to a cycle of multibody refinement using separate masks for the LSU, the head, and body regions of SSU65. The gold standard Fourier shell correlation value criterion of 0.143 was used to determine averaged map resolutions, as implemented in Relion 4 and 5. Local resolution was estimated using Resmap66.

Model building and refinement

rRNA and ribosomal protein models were built using template-guided model building in COOT67. The coordinates of the L. major ribosome (PDB ID: 8OVJ and 8RXH) were used as a template for model building and were docked onto density maps using UCSF ChimeraX68. RNA modifications were manually modeled based on RibOxi-seq, RiboMeth-seq, Ψ-seq, HydraPsiSeq, and mass-spectrometry data. The Mg2+, Zn2+, Na+, and K+ ion compositions were modeled according to the recently described criteria69,70. Model refinement was performed using an iterative approach, including real-space refinement and geometry regularization in COOT67, followed by real-space refinement using the PHENIX suite71. The final model was validated using MolProbity72.

Purification of rRNA subunits for detection of 2′-O-methylation by tandem LC-MS

L. major rRNAs were purified by using reversed-phase LC through a PLRP-S 4000 Å column (4.6 × 150 mm, 8 μm, Agilent Technologies) from the total RNA. The rRNAs were eluted with a 120-min gradient of 10.8–13.6% (10.8–12.4% for the first 20 min, and 12.4–13.6% for the next 100 min) (v/v) acetonitrile in 100 mM TEAA (pH 7.0) and 0.1 mM diammonium phosphate from the column at a flow rate of 200 μl/min at 60 °C, while monitoring the eluate at A26073.

LC-MS and MS/MS analysis and database search of RNA fragments

Purified rRNA was digested with RNase T174. The RNA fragments were analyzed with a direct nanoflow LC-MS system75. The column was prepared with a fused-silica capillary (150-μm i.d. × 240 mm in length) packed with a reversed-phase material (Develosil C30-UG-3, 3-μm particle size; Nomura Chemical). The LC mobile phases were as follows: solvent A was 10% methanol containing 9 mM triethylamine acetate (pH 7.0), and solvent B was 45% acetonitrile containing 5.5 mM triethylamine acetate (pH 7.0). The LC was performed at a flow rate of 200 nl/min using a 120-min linear gradient from 0% B to 18% B at room temperature. The eluate was sprayed online at −1.3 kV with the aid of a spray-assisting device to a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in negative ion mode76. Ariadne77 was used for database searches and assignment of MS/MS RNA spectra. The composite of L. major rRNA sequences was used as a database. The following default search parameters for Ariadne were used: maximum number of missed cleavages, 1; variable modification parameters, two modifications including methylation for any residue per RNA fragment; RNA mass tolerance, ±5 ppm, and MS/MS tolerance, ±20 ppm.

In vivo psoralen UV cross-linking and ligation to generate chimeric RNA

Mid-log phase L. major cells were harvested and resuspended at 5 × 107 cells/ml and washed twice with PBS. Cells (5 × 109) were concentrated and incubated on ice. 4′-aminomethyltrioxsalen hydrochloride (AMT) (Sigma) was added to the cells at a concentration of 0.2 mg/ml for 30–40 min on ice. Cells treated with AMT were kept on ice and irradiated using a UV lamp at 365 nm, at a light intensity of 10 mW/cm2 for 30 min. Cells were washed once with PBS, resuspended in PBS, deproteinized by digestion with proteinase K (Roche) (100 μg/ml in 1% SDS for 30 min), and RNA was prepared using TRIzol (Sigma) reagent. Five micrograms of cross-linked RNA was fragmented by mild alkaline hydrolysis using FastAP buffer (Thermo Scientific), dephosphorylated using FastAP Thermosensitive Alkaline Phosphatase (Thermo Scientific), cleaned by TRIzol-chloroform method and the RNA-RNA hybrid was ligated using high concentration T4 RNA Ligase 1 (NEB) in a buffer containing DMSO, ATP, PEG 8000 and RNase inhibitor (NEB). The cross-linking between the ligated RNAs was reversed by irradiation at 254 nm. The recovered RNA was then used for cDNA preparation and PCR analysis.

Statistics and reproducibility

All next-generation sequencing and proteomics experiments were repeated at least two times. Tandem LC-MS for rRNA modification was done only once. Statistical analysis and bar-graphs were prepared using Microsoft Excel, Origin (OriginLab Corporation) and DESeq2 (v1.38.3)60. ImageJ (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij) software was used to analyze Western and Northern blots. Canvas X (Canvas GFX, Inc.) was used to arrange and prepare the images and figures.
