Qualitative Analysis of User Experiences of a mHealth Self-Care Intervention for Care Partners of Individuals with Traumatic Brain Injury
Authors: Rongqi Bei, Christopher M. Graves, Sung Won Choi, Angelle M. Sander, Madison J. Fansher, Jennifer A. Miner, Zhenke Wu, Predrag Klasnja, Mark W. Newman, Noelle E. Carlozzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify challenges and solutions for conducting mobile health (mHealth) research in a rehabilitation care partner population that involved personalized self-care push notifications and self-monitoring data collection.DesignQualitative semistructured debriefing interviews with participants who completed a 6-month app-based self-care intervention involving self-monitoring alone or self-monitoring plus personalized push notifications. Interviews were guided by a data-prompted approach to aid participants’ recall.SettingTwo academic medical centers.ParticipantsThirty-six care partners (N=36) of individuals with complicated mild to severe traumatic brain injury requiring assistance with daily activities.InterventionsThe CareQOL app is a mHealth intervention that delivers personalized self-care push notifications based on daily self-monitoring data, including self-reported caregiver strain, depression, and anxiety, plus Fitbit-tracked sleep and step data.Main Outcome MeasuresQualitative interview data about participants’ experiences with, impressions of, and suggestions for the CareQOL app: (1) self-monitoring components (ie, daily questions, health data dashboard); (2) personalized self-care notifications, including overall experience and perceptions of selected messages.ResultsDeductive thematic analysis was used to uncover participants’ experiences with the CareQOL app and identify considerations across intervention and study design. Intervention-wise, push notifications personalized solely based on passive sensing and self-report can be misaligned with participants’ needs, reducing support relevance; limited contextual cues in dashboard displays constrained meaningful self-reflection. Study-wise, technical breakdowns disrupted engagement during the extended study duration; data-prompted interviews effectively supported recall, with participant preferences informing methodological considerations.ConclusionsFindings identified critical considerations and practical recommendations for designing personalized mHealth interventions for self-care and evaluating them in longitudinal randomized controlled trials.

Informal care partners of individuals with traumatic brain injury (TBI) often experience considerable mental strain, manifesting in increased anxiety, depression, fatigue, and social isolation.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Care partners often prioritize their care recipients’ needs over their own health, creating a cycle where declining caregiver health-related quality of life (HRQOL) ultimately impacts both the caregiver and the individual receiving care.6,7

Recognizing that mobile health (mHealth) interventions offer greater accessibility, flexibility, and scalability than traditional in-person medical care for self-care improvement, we designed the CareQOL app to provide low-burden, personalized self-management support for care partners.8, 9, 10 The app has 2 key intervention components: (1) self-monitoring through daily ecological momentary assessments (EMAs) of caregiver strain, anxiety, and depression, and Fitbit-tracked sleep and step data (fig 1A), with all data visualized on a dashboard (fig 1B); and (2) personalized self-care push notifications tailored to users’ self-monitoring data (fig 1C).Fig. 1(A) The CareQOL app Activities page showing the completion status of the daily self-monitoring and Fitbit syncing tasks and the resulting self-care push notifications. The CareQOL app’s efficacy was evaluated through a 6-month randomized controlled trial (RCT) with 254 care partners of individuals living with TBI. Participants were randomized to study arm 1, self-monitoring only (no notifications), or study arm 2, self-monitoring plus personalized notifications (receiving tailored push notifications with a 50% daily probability). We assessed participants’ HRQOL outcomes monthly, as well as at 3 and 6 months postintervention (see11 for methodological details). In total, 244 out of 254 participants completed the 6-month follow-up.11 An interim analysis (February 2023) of the data suggested the possibility of null findings which prompted the current study to collect qualitative data on participants’ experiences with the CareQOL app.

We adopted the user-centered approach and conducted qualitative semistructured debriefing interviews with a subset of 36 participants (16 from study arm 2) who completed the 6-month RCT.12 We explored participants’ overall impression of the CareQOL app, experiences with specific app components (eg, self-monitoring features, self-care push notifications), and suggestions for improvement.

Methods

Participants

We conducted semistructured debriefing interviews with 36 care partners of individuals living with TBI to better understand the null results suggested by the February 2023 interim analysis. Eligible participants included those who completed the RCT either within the preceding 6 months as of March 1, 2023, or after that date (through August 2023). Recruitment sources and eligibility criteria for the original CareQOL app RCT are documented in prior work.11 All interview participants provided independent informed consent prior to participation. All study activities were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the University of Michigan, the lead site, which served as the single IRB of record (IRBMED HUM00181282).

Intervention

The CareQOL app is an adaptive mHealth intervention aimed at providing personalized, low-burden self-management support for care partners. Each day, users completed an EMA consisting of single-item measures of caregiver strain, depression, and anxiety administered through a computer adaptive test (CAT) whose score reset each week.13, 14, 15 Participants also wore a Fitbit to track their sleep and steps. The app used these data to classify users’ mental health, sleep, and activity into below, at, or above average performance and provided data visualizations for self-monitoring (fig 1B).10 The app randomized the delivery of a tailored push notification to participants in study arm 2 (fig 1C), with a 50% chance of delivery daily. These notifications targeted 1of 6 health aspects (ie, caregiver strain, depression, anxiety, sleep, mood and mindfulness, and physical activity) and were tailored based on whether the user’s score for that category was above average, average, or below average. Figure 1D shows the workflow from data collection to notification delivery. Figure 1E provides sample scenarios illustrating how users received personalized notifications.

Interview design

We conducted semistructured debriefing interviews to explore participants’ experiences with the CareQOL app and RCT participation. Interviews were conducted by research staff at each data collection site; the same staff member worked with each participant throughout the duration of the study. Interviewers encouraged candid feedback, emphasizing that responses would be deidentified and used to improve the app for future caregivers and that the interviewer was not involved in the app design and development. To support recall and facilitate discussion, we employed a data-prompted interview approach, using each participant’s own trial data to “stimulate discussion during the interview”.16 The interview covered the following: (1) overall experience with the app, including its perceived impact on caregiving and quality of life; (2) experiences with self-monitoring features (daily EMAs and the health data dashboard); (3) experiences with the personalized self-care push notifications (study arm 2 only), including overall impressions and reactions to selected push notifications; and (4) suggestions for app improvement. See supplemental appendix S1 (available online only at http://www.archives-pmr.org/) for the complete interview guide.

Study arm 2 participants were shown personal examples of message notifications, delivery timestamps, and relevant health data displays (fig 2) as memory cues to prompt detailed recall and reflection.16 Notifications were selected for presentation and discussion if they (1) were delivered during periods of consistently high or low symptom levels (eg, persistently high caregiver strain); (2) coincided with noticeable changes in relevant conditions (eg, a reduction in depression levels after delivery); and (3) were proximate to the interview date. The slides included 2 data presentation formats: (1) a visualization with granular, raw data (fig 2A) and (2) a text summary presenting descriptive statistics (fig 2B). We also collected feedback from study arm 2 participants on whether these data visualization slides were effective memory cues. Biased visualization patterns in the memory cues could influence participants’ recall because individuals tend to “[relate] recognizable features of the data to remembered experience, routines, and known facts” to “tell a cohesive story”.17 To facilitate accurate recall, we avoided misleading design choices documented in prior work18 and prevented showing potentially suggestive trends before participants shared their own perceptions.Fig. 2Sample slides used during the follow-up data-prompted interview as memory cues. Data analysis

All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. We adopted a hybrid approach to thematic analysis in which initial coding was guided by interview domains (ie, notifications, dashboard, self-monitoring, and interview materials) and experience valence (ie, positive, negative).19,20 Then, we inductively identified specific codes as they emerged from participants’ descriptions (eg, increased self-awareness, inapplicable suggestions, confusion about T scores). Themes were developed by connecting related codes and interpreting underlying issues that explained participants’ experiences. The first author conducted the primary coding, creating transcript excerpts, codes, and analytic notes that were discussed weekly with 3 additional authors. These discussions informed revisions to the codebook, exploration of emergent ideas, and iterative refinement of themes. To help contextualize individual experiences and support interpretation of the themes, the first author also wrote an analytic memo for each participant summarizing demographic details, caregiving context, and key app usage patterns, preferences, and experiences.21

Through deductive thematic analysis, we identified 4 themes from the interview data relevant to intervention design and trial procedures. These themes captured participants’ experiences with the CareQOL app and study participation, particularly in areas where the intervention or study processes posed challenges or opportunities for refinement, including (1) personalization misalignments reduced the perceived relevance of support; (2) limited contextual cues in dashboard displays constrained meaningful self-reflection; (3) app and Fitbit technical breakdowns disrupted engagement; and (4) data-prompted interviews supported contextualized recall. Themes 1 and 4 emerged only among participants in study arm 2 because study arm 1 did not receive personalized push notifications or review notification-related interview materials. Data saturation was reached after the 16th interview, when no new codes or conceptually meaningful insights emerged; subsequent interviews confirmed existing themes.

Personalization misalignments reduced the perceived relevance of support

Many participants found the personalized push notifications helpful for increasing self-awareness, prompting self-care, and encouraging them to act on the suggestions provided. However, participants reported challenges with notification relevance (table 2). Some found suggestions unrealistic given their intensive caregiving demands, whereas others received recommendations at inappropriate times (eg, at work, during severe weather) when they could not act on them. Such misaligned timing reduced notification effectiveness as one participant noted the suggestion “is not something that kind of stayed with me all day”. From our conversations with participants, we observed that more engaged participants developed their own workarounds: intentionally delaying adherence until a more convenient time, taking actions when they later recalled the message, or writing down personal lists of helpful messages to revisit during moments of need. However, requiring users to independently develop these strategies places an additional burden on this already-strained population, suggesting that additional app features are warranted.

Limited contextual cues in dashboard displays constrained meaningful self-reflection

The CareQOL app’s dashboard presented participants’ caregiver strain, depression, and anxiety levels as standardized T scores, along with sleep hours and step counts, in weekly, monthly, or yearly views (fig 1B). Around two-thirds of participants reported that these visualizations increased their health awareness and encouraged them to reflect on changes in their data over time. For some, these self-monitoring practices also prompted behavioral changes, such as maintaining step count goals, getting more sleep, and analyzing their feelings introspectively.

However, the dashboard offered limited support for deeper reflection. Participants expressed greater interest in understanding the “why” behind their past feelings rather than simply reviewing scores. As one participant noted, “I was more concerned about why I answered [the EMA questions] the way I did... I try to remember what happened during that previous time period...” Recalling how one felt at a given moment became harder over time, especially when viewing T scores. Several participants found these scores confusing, with questions like “I’m not really sure what the numbers are… I didn’t know if this was like a scale or...” and “The number on the side… is that a percentage?”.

Several participants proactively mentioned that they would have benefited from documenting the underlying causes of their feelings alongside daily EMAs, “If you had a stressful day, you might make a note of why it was, just for future reference… if it’s specifically about caregiving duties, or there are other things going on…”. They expressed willingness to invest additional effort to document underlying causes when they deemed it necessary.

Intervention’s technical breakdowns disrupted engagement

Several participants mentioned technical issues that interfered with their app experience (table 3). During this multiyear trial (December 2020 to February 2024), our app occasionally failed because of practical issues, like operating system updates (eg, iOS, Android). When these breakdowns were not immediately resolved, they created inconsistent participant experiences, including missed personalized notifications, unsynced Fitbit data, duplicated prompts for self-monitoring, and lost data because of application programming interface (API) integration issues. Data-prompted interviews supported contextualized recall

Participants found the data prompts (fig 2) helpful for recalling their experiences with intervention messages. They appreciated the contextual information shown in the slides (eg, dates), helping them connect data patterns with personal events or routines to interpret experiences more meaningfully.

Regarding presentation format preferences, most participants preferred graph-based data presentations over text summaries. This was often attributed to personal learning styles, with several identifying as “visual learners” or a “graph person”. Participants appreciated graphs that offered quick visual reference, allowing intuitive understanding of trends. However, considerations emerged regarding graph presentation: (1) 2 participants found that gaps in the bar charts, which reflected days without self-monitoring data, made the visualizations harder to interpret, and sometimes triggered feelings of guilt over missed data collection; (2) although graphs provide granularity, participants with stable data patterns found them less informative; (3) 2 participants suggested allowing personal notetaking during the trial, which could later be integrated into interview materials to further enhance contextualized recall.

Discussion

In this section, we contextualize our findings within existing literature, then derive 4 practical considerations for designing and implementing adaptive mHealth interventions for self-care in longitudinal studies.

First, findings indicated that push notifications’ effectiveness was reduced when timing and content was misaligned with users’ contextual needs, even when delivery was adapted to self-monitoring data. This is consistent with findings from the adaptive mHealth interventions literature: users find poorly timed or misaligned notifications disruptive and are less willing to follow them.22, 23, 24 To address this, prior work has incorporated microinteractions (eg, snoozing) and proposed treating user preference expression as an ongoing human-in-the-loop mechanism that allows users to shape notification delivery.25, 26, 27 Our findings found that engaged participants spontaneously developed informal strategies to navigate mismatched notifications by delaying actions or curating personal notification lists.

We therefore recommend allowing users to shape notification delivery through lightweight interactions. “Snoozing” or “microplanning” features may help users reschedule suggestions to address later. Additionally, allowing users to “opt out” of certain content categories or “favorite” useful notifications for future reference may help curate content relevance. Beyond immediate adjustments, users’ explicit feedback (eg, marking notifications as favorites or rescheduling suggestions) may inform the system’s future personalization decisions, creating feedback loops where user preferences continuously refine the intervention’s relevance and timing.

Second, findings indicated that participants wanted to engage in deeper reflection about emotional patterns yet found it difficult to recall what influenced their emotional states over time. Without contextual cues, participants’ reflections were descriptive (ie, revisiting what happened) rather than explanatory or interpretive.28,29 As such, prior work recommends capturing context through optional free text or photo logging.30 In our study, we not only used visual cues to elicit context, we showed that, within a longitudinal setting, participants were willing to selectively document contextual explanations within EMA responses as memory cues to support later dashboard interpretations.

We therefore recommend contextualizing self-reflections in the dashboard by enabling optional, low-friction annotations within EMA workflows (eg, brief text or tags) and surfacing these annotations alongside dashboard to support meaningful retrospective reflection.

Third, findings revealed that technical breakdowns substantially impacted participants’ experiences. Long-term mHealth deployments face inevitable technical challenges including system “bugs and glitches” that occur midstudy.31,32 These technical failures directly threaten implementation fidelity, the degree to which an intervention is delivered as intended.32 Prior work has emphasized preventive strategies to minimize technical disruptions, including internal testing and close communication between technical and clinical teams.31 However, technical breakdowns may still occur in long-term deployments because of external dependencies beyond researchers’ control (eg, operating system updates, API changes).

We therefore recommend batch enrollment and fallback planning to manage inevitable technical failures. Batch enrollment (ie, enrolling participants in waves)33 can mitigate the impact of midstudy technical issues. When participants begin the study simultaneously, system failures affect them equally, simplifying troubleshooting and allowing batch identifiers to serve as covariates. This is particularly valuable in RCTs where technical inconsistencies complicate interpretation. Batch enrollment also enables recruitment pauses for scheduled updates. In addition, proactive fallback planning helps ensure interventions remain meaningful when technology malfunctions, including alternative delivery methods and graceful degradation capabilities.

Fourth, findings indicated that presenting participants’ own data during poststudy interviews (eg, fig 2) facilitates recall and elaboration of experiences over an extended trial period. Data-prompted interview techniques have been used to strengthen recall and stimulate discussions about participants’ past experiences, yet practical considerations for large-scale, long-term trials remain underspecified to our understanding.16,34,35

Our findings reveal three considerations for applying data-prompted interviews to facilitate recall. First, using temporal markers such as specific dates or trial-recorded context (eg, participants’ self-report data) helped participants connect data patterns with personal life events. Second, tailoring presentation formats to individual preferences and data characteristics may improve recall and reduce negative emotional responses. Although many participants preferred visual presentations for intuitive pattern recognition, format effectiveness depends on data variability and completeness. Third, automating memory cues preparation is critical for scaling. Although creating individualized prompts (eg, fig 2) can be time-consuming, automated approaches like end-to-end Python scripts make this technique feasible for larger samples and balance personalization with preparation burden.

Study limitations

Although this study offers several strengths, it is important to consider its limitations. First, our convenience sampling approach may introduce self-selection bias. The 53% interview response rate (36 of 68 invited) raises concerns about participation bias, though our interview sample’s HRQOL change patterns closely mirrored the full RCT sample’s trimodal distribution (supplemental appendix S2) and interview participants were nearly evenly distributed across both study sites (table 1). However, responders may still vary in other characteristics such as motivation to provide feedback or availability. Second, as interviews were initiated after interim analyses that revealed no between-arm differences, the sample skewed toward participants who completed the RCT more recently to ensure better recall of app experiences. These later participants may have had different experiences than earlier participants, as technical issues with the app became more prevalent toward the end of the trial. Third, the sample was predominantly women (29 of 36 participants), which may limit generalizability to male care partners. Regardless, this sex distribution aligns with the broader caregiving population (ie, >75% are women)2 and our RCT sample. Future work is needed to determine whether these findings are generalizable to male care partners and other care partner populations. Fourth, because interviews were conducted by research staff with whom participants had established rapport, social desirability bias may have influenced response candidness. However, participants did share critical feedback, including experiences where the app mismatched their needs, suggesting the interviewers were able to foster honest conversations. Finally, while interviews were coded by a single researcher, 3 additional authors met regularly to discuss and refine codes, themes, and interpretations collaboratively.

Conclusions

Care partners are at risk of overlooking their own well-being given caregiving demands, and mHealth interventions have been a proposed solution to support self-care activities. In this critical reflection paper, we draw on our experience with the CareQOL app to share practical considerations and recommendations for designing personalized self-care mHealth interventions and evaluating them in longitudinal RCTs. Future studies would benefit from referencing these design and methodological recommendations when developing personalized mHealth interventions for rehabilitation care partner populations.
