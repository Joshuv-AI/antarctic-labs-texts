Serological evidence of bluetongue virus exposure in domestic ruminants at altitudes above 4500 m (Ali Prefecture, Tibetan Plateau, China)
Authors: Yuqing Song, Nan Wang, Xinjuan He, Fanhua Meng, Zhancheng Tian, Youjun Shang, Jijun He, Guiquan Guan, Ou Zuo, Hong Yin, Junzheng Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBluetongue virus (BTV) causes a vector-borne disease of considerable veterinary and economic importance. The virus is spread by biting midges (family Ceratopogonidae). BTV exposure has been reported in a wide range of ecological settings, but evidence from very high-altitude areas remains limited. To our knowledge, this is the first report of serological evidence of BTV exposure of domestic ruminants at extremely high altitudes (>4500 m) in Ali Prefecture, western Tibetan Plateau, China. Testing with a competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay revealed that 31.1% of 633 blood samples collected from sheep, goats, yaks, cattle, and yak–cattle hybrids in Ali Prefecture in 2023 were positive for BTV-specific antibodies, with seroprevalence varying from 90.7% in goats to only 0.4% in yaks, confirming significant geographical and interspecies differences (p < 0.001). BTV-specific antibodies were detected in animals from locations above 4500 m, indicating previous exposure at elevations where evidence remains scarce. These findings provide baseline seroepidemiological data relevant to livestock health in alpine ecosystems, and support the need for strengthened entomological surveillance in high-altitude regions.Keywords: Bluetongue virus, Seroprevalence, Tibetan plateau, Climate change, Domestic ruminants

Introduction

Bluetongue virus (BTV) causes an arthropod-borne disease in wild and domestic ruminants characterized by vascular damage, resulting in congestion, edema, and hemorrhage (DeMaula et al., 2001). Clinical signs of BTV infection include fever, nasal discharge, dyspnea, oral ulceration, lameness, and coronitis (Herder et al., 2024). It also can result in reproductive pathology, especially early embryonic death, stillbirth, and congenital malformations (Saminathan et al., 2020b). As a notifiable disease, the World Organization for Animal Health reports that BTV infection reduces livestock productivity and increases mortality, potentially resulting in trade restrictions on animals and animal products, thereby further contributing to substantial economic losses to local economies (Santman-Berends et al., 2024).

The occurrence and persistence of BTV are influenced by climatic conditions, the availability of susceptible hosts, and the presence of competent vectors (genus Culicoides) (Wilson & Mellor, 2009). In China, among approximately 20 Culicoides species reported to transmit or potentially transmit animal viruses, BTV is associated with at least 18 competent or suspected vectors widely distributed across the country (Su-sheng & Jing-lin, 2022). Experimental studies indicate that Culicoides sonorensis exhibits increased transmission efficiency for orbiviruses at elevated temperatures, whereas viral replication within Culicoides is markedly reduced below approximately 15 °C (Mellor et al., 1998; Wittmann et al., 2002). Since the late 20th century, climate has been considered one of several factors contributing to the substantially expanded geographic range of BTV, including northward spread in Europe (Mellor & Wittmann, 2002; Purse et al., 2005; Samy & Peterson, 2016; Wilson & Mellor, 2009). Increasing evidence suggests that BTV can infect or be transmitted to a broader range of hosts, including wild ruminants, camelids, and non-traditional species, underscoring the epidemiological importance of wildlife reservoirs and spillover events (Ruiz-Fons et al., 2014; Ul-Rahman & Nasir, 2026; Ul-Rahman et al., 2025).

China maintains large populations of cattle and sheep, and 17 BTV serotypes have been isolated, with seropositive animals reported in most regions of the country, including sheep from Nyingchi Prefecture in the Tibet Autonomous Region (Ma et al., 2017; Xin et al., 2026). Nyingchi Prefecture is located at a relatively lower altitude (approximately 3100 m) and is characterized by a warmer and more humid climate with greater vegetation coverage. Ali Prefecture (29°41′N–35°52′N), with an average altitude exceeding 4500 m, is among the highest inhabited regions on the Tibetan Plateau. However, the epidemiological status of BTV exposure in domestic ruminants in this region has not been systematically investigated. Accordingly, this study aimed to determine the seroprevalence of BTV in domestic ruminants in Ali Prefecture, Tibet.

Materials and methods

Investigation sites

BTV seroprevalence in domestic ruminants was monitored in six counties of Ali Prefecture, western Tibet—Pulan, Zada, Gaer, Ritu, Gaize, and Geji—located at 78°24′E–86°20′E longitude and 29°41′N–35°52′N latitude. The region has an average altitude > 4500 m and a typical plateau climate, with an annual mean temperature of −2 °C to 0 °C. Meteorological data from 2023 showed seasonal temperature fluctuations from the highest recorded temperature of 20 °C in August (mean monthly minimum, 5 °C; mean monthly maximum, 16 °C) to the lowest of −26 °C in February (mean monthly minimum, −21 °C; mean monthly maximum, −10 °C).

Serum samples

A multistage cluster sampling approach was used to randomly select herder households from each township within the six counties. All clinically healthy domestic ruminants (sheep, goats, yaks, cattle, and yak–cattle hybrids) present in the selected households at the time of sampling were enrolled. In total, 633 blood samples were collected between June and August 2023, comprising 161 sheep, 151 goats, 252 yaks, 17 yak–cattle hybrids, and 52 cattle. Samples were transported to the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute laboratory and serum was separated by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 5 min and then stored at −20 °C until analysis.

Serological assay

Serum samples were tested for BTV-specific antibodies using a commercial competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (ID Screen® Bluetongue Competition; Innovative Diagnostics, Montpellier, France), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples with optical density (OD) values ≤40 % of the mean negative control value were considered positive, while those with positive or borderline results were retested for confirmation.

Data analysis

Regional differences in the seroprevalence of BTV were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test. Fisher’s exact test was used to validate the chi-square results. A Firth logistic regression model was fitted with Ritu County as the reference to estimate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) for the other counties. Species-specific differences in BTV seroprevalence were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square test and confirmed with Fisher’s exact test. Pairwise interspecies comparisons were performed using Fisher’s exact test with Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Results

All sampled animals were managed under extensive grazing systems with seasonal transhumance, involving regular movement between pastures for rotational grazing. No BTV vaccines had been administered in Ali Prefecture, indicating that all detected antibodies resulted from natural exposure. Among the 633 serum samples tested, 31.1 % (n = 197) were positive for BTV antibodies by competitive ELISA (Table 1). Seroprevalence differed across the six counties: Ritu, 36.6 %; Gaer, 37.9 %; Geji, 50.7 %; Gaize, 20.9 %; Zada, 0 %; and Pulan, 0 % (Fig. 1; Table 2). Pearson’s chi-squared test indicated significant differences among counties (χ² = 96.46, df = 5, p < 0.001) from 50.7 % (104/205) in Geji to 0 % in Zada (0/47) and Pulan (0/81).

Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction at the county level indicated that seroprevalence was significantly higher in Geji than Ritu, Gaer, and Gaize (p < 0.01), while no difference was observed between Ritu and Gaer (p = 0.909). Fisher’s exact test indicated significant interspecies differences (p < 0.001). Post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction showed that seroprevalence was higher in goats than sheep (p < 0.001) and higher in sheep than yaks (p < 0.001), indicating pronounced interspecies variation in the seroprevalence of BTV antibodies.

Discussion

This study provides the first serological evidence of BTV exposure of domestic ruminants in Ali Prefecture, Tibet, China, and adds unique baseline data from a very high-altitude region. Sampled animals were managed under seasonal transhumance with rotational grazing, which may have influenced exposure patterns through contact between herds and seasonal movement. However, because this study was based on serological detection of antibodies, the results indicate previous exposure rather than active virus circulation or confirmed local transmission.

The annual mean temperature (−2 °C to 0 °C) and large diurnal fluctuations of Ali Prefecture are likely challenging to the survival and activities of many Culicoides species, which may limit the transmission of BTV. Since different Culicoides species exhibit varying environmental tolerances (Purse et al., 2005; Wilson & Mellor, 2009), sampling was conducted from June to August, when temperatures are relatively warmest. However, no entomological data were collected in the present study, suggesting that any interpretation regarding seasonal vector activity, vector species composition, or environmentally driven changes in transmission potential remains speculative. Currently, no data are available on the distribution, species composition, or abundance of Culicoides species in Ali Prefecture, highlighting the need for entomological surveys using light traps combined with morphological and molecular identification. Ali Prefecture also hosts several rare wild ruminants, including the Tibetan antelope (Pantholops hodgsonii), Tibetan gazelle (Procapra picticaudata), wild yak (Bos mutus), and blue sheep (Pseudois nayaur), and the potential relevance of BTV exposure to wildlife health in this region warrants further investigation.

In Yunnan Province (adjacent to Tibet), the seroprevalence of BTV is reportedly 30 %–50 % in cattle and goats, and several newly identified Culicoides species have been recognized as potential BTV vectors (Duan et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023, 2025). BTV-29 was isolated from asymptomatically infected goats in Xinjiang, China (Yang et al., 2021). During surveillance conducted between 2013 and 2014 in Nyingchi, Tibet, a BTV seroprevalence rate of 19.1 % was detected among Tibetan sheep populations (Ma et al., 2017). The surrounding regions exhibit established BTV activity, suggesting that Ali Prefecture may form part of a broader ecological continuum rather than an isolated focus. In Himachal Pradesh, India, the BTV seroprevalence rates were as follows: goats, 67.34 %; sheep, 7.14 %; and cattle, 52.17 % (Ayanur et al., 2016; Saminathan et al., 2020a). In Nepal, the prevalence rates were: sheep, 17.82 %; goats, 47.50 %; cattle, 53.05 %; buffaloes, 58.05 %; and yaks, 7.62 % (Khanal et al., 2016). In Bangladesh, prevalence rates were: goats, 78.1 %; and sheep, 41.9 % (Islam et al., 2023). The high seroprevalence observed in Ali Prefecture aligns with broader patterns across the Qinghai–Tibetan Plateau and adjacent regions, with consistently high prevalence in goats and comparatively low prevalence in yaks.

Yaks are susceptible to BTV and may develop severe clinical signs (Li et al., 2015; Mauroy et al., 2008). In this study, yaks in Ali Prefecture had very low seroprevalence (0.4 %, 1/252) as compared to sheep (36.6 %) and goats (90.7 %). This pattern aligns with previous surveys in northern China, where seroprevalence was low in cattle and consistently exceeded 30 % in sheep (Gong et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2019). These interspecies differences may reflect variation in husbandry, animal movement, and exposure opportunities. Hence, these results should be interpreted with caution because practical constraints at extreme altitudes limited fully random sampling and no entomological data were collected to assess vector-related factors. Notably, the absence of seropositive animals in Zada (0/47) and Pulan (0/81) may be explained by true absence or low exposure, limited sample size, differences in grazing or livestock movement, ecological or geographic barriers, or sampling bias. Nevertheless, these findings provide useful baseline seroepidemiological data for future studies in this region.

Conclusion

Since BTV is transmitted by biting midges (Culicoides spp.), low temperatures may limit vector survival and activity. Evidence of BTV exposure from very high-altitude regions such as the Tibetan Plateau has been limited. In this study, we investigated BTV exposure in domestic ruminants in Ali Prefecture, a high-altitude region (>4500 m) in western Tibet. Serological testing showed that 31.1 % of 633 animals were positive for BTV antibodies. These findings provide baseline evidence of BTV exposure in domestic ruminants from this high-altitude region and may have implications for livestock and wildlife health in alpine ecosystems. However, the absence of viral isolation, molecular confirmation, and entomological data limits conclusions regarding active virus circulation, local transmission, and vector involvement. Longitudinal virological and entomological surveillance will be necessary to clarify the epidemiology of BTV in high-altitude regions.
