Nutritional status and attitudes toward overweight and obesity among health-science students in Vietnam
Authors: Ngo Xuan Khoa, Vo Tien Huy, Nguyen Xuan Tuan, Ngo Thi Tam, Tran Linh Cham, Hoang Duc Hanh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo assess nutritional status across different health-professional disciplines and to examine attitudes toward overweight and obesity and their associated factors among undergraduate health-science students in Vietnam.MethodsA cross-sectional analytical study was conducted among 764 undergraduate students from medicine, nursing, pharmacy, and other health programs at two universities in Hanoi. Stratified random sampling ensured representation by major and academic year. Nutritional status was classified by BMI using WHO Asian cutoffs. Attitudes toward obesity were measured using the 20-item Attitudes Toward Obese Persons Scale and analyzed in three domains identified by factor analysis. Physical activity, emotional well-being, and resilience were assessed using validated scales. Ordered logistic regression identified predictors of higher BMI category, and Tobit regression examined factors associated with attitude domain scores.ResultsMost students had normal BMI, while overweight and obesity were more common in males, in certain living situations, and in general medicine students. Female gender and pharmacy major were associated with lower odds of higher BMI, whereas higher depression scores were associated with higher BMI category. Attitudes toward obesity showed moderate negative stereotypes and relatively positive beliefs. BMI category was associated only with the psychological inferiority domain, with higher scores among students with overweight. Female gender, lower depression, higher happiness and resilience, and moderate physical activity were associated with more favorable attitude profiles.ConclusionAmong Vietnamese health-science students, nutritional status and attitudes toward obesity varied across demographic and psychosocial characteristics. Excess weight and weight-related attitudes appear partly independent but influenced by mental well-being and training context. Integrated health promotion and stigma-reduction strategies in health-professional education may improve both student health and future obesity care.

Introduction

Medical and health-professional students are expected to model healthy lifestyles and provide competent care for people with obesity. However, many studies show that excess weight is already present in this population. The global prevalence of excess weight among medical students is substantial, with a 2024 meta-analysis reporting 18% overweight and 9% obesity (24% overall), with higher rates in males and an increasing trend over time across regions [1]. Prevalence varies markedly by region, ranging from approximately 11–12% overweight and 2–4% obesity in European cohorts to over 20% overweight and up to nearly 30% obesity in several Asian and African settings [2–6]. Many factors beyond knowledge contribute to students’ nutritional status. Male sex and later stages of training are associated with higher odds of excess weight, while recent weight gain strongly predicts overweight and mindful eating behaviors are protective [7]. Unhealthy dietary habits, such as frequent fast-food intake, sugary drinks, and eating while using screens, are also linked to higher BMI [8–10]. In addition, low physical activity, smoking, and poor diet quality increase cardiometabolic risk [11, 12]. Psychological factors play an important role as well. Emotional eating, stress, and depressive symptoms are consistently associated with higher BMI in students [12, 13]. Importantly, nutrition knowledge alone may not predict BMI because knowledge does not always translate into actual dietary practices, and is often outweighed by socioeconomic conditions, environmental constraints, motivation, and established habits; consequently, students may demonstrate adequate knowledge while still maintaining unhealthy eating behaviors and experiencing excess weight [14–17].

Alongside nutritional issues, attitudes toward people with obesity among health-professional students are a growing concern. An experimental study in Germany showed that almost all medical students expressed negative attitudes toward an obese patient vignette, compared with about half toward a normal-weight vignette [18]. More recent studies in European health-related students also found that weight stigma remains common, although lower among females and physically active students [19]. Stigmatizing beliefs are stronger when obesity is attributed to personal failings such as overeating or lack of willpower rather than biological or social causes [20, 21]. Other associated factors include male gender, lower empathy, limited contact with people living with obesity, and exposure to stigmatizing role models in training [22, 23]. Educational programs can improve knowledge and counseling confidence, but their impact on attitudes and implicit bias is often limited [24, 25].

Overall, existing evidence shows that both nutritional status and attitudes toward obesity are important issues among health-professional students, and they are shaped by multiple behavioral and psychosocial factors. However, most studies focus only on medical students and are conducted in high-income settings. Research comparing different health-professional disciplines, such as medicine, nursing, pharmacy, and other health sciences, is still scarce. Differences in curriculum, clinical exposure, and professional culture may influence both BMI patterns and attitudes toward overweight and obesity. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the nutritional status of students across different health-professional programs and to examine their attitudes toward overweight and obesity. This integrated approach can provide evidence to guide education and stigma-reduction strategies in health-professional training.

Materials and methods

Study design, settings and participants

This study used a cross-sectional analytical design to assess nutritional status and attitudes toward overweight and obesity among undergraduate health-professional students.

The study was conducted at two major medical universities in Hanoi, Vietnam: Hanoi Medical University and the School of Medicine and Pharmacy, Vietnam National University. Recruitment and survey administration were performed between March and May 2025 using institutional online communication platforms.

Eligible participants were full-time undergraduate students aged ≥ 18 years enrolled in health science programs, including General Medicine, Nursing, Pharmacy, and other health-related majors. Students were excluded if they were not currently enrolled, were < 18 years of age, or provided incomplete or inconsistent responses on key study variables. Students were selected using stratified random sampling across six academic years (Year 1–Year 6) to ensure proportional representation by training level and major. Strata were defined by the combination of academic year (six strata) and major (four groups), and proportional allocation was applied based on the official student distribution at each institution. Within each stratum, participants were randomly selected using institutional student lists. Of 765 selected students, 764 provided complete responses and were included in the final analysis. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained electronically before survey completion. The questionnaire was administered online, and responses were automatically recorded; incomplete submissions were excluded, and missing data were minimal (< 1%), therefore complete-case analysis was applied.

Variables and measurements

Demographic and academic characteristics

Demographic and academic information included gender, current residence (living with family, rented housing, dormitory, or other), academic year (Year 1–Year 6), clinical training stage (no clinical exposure, preclinical, clinical), and major (general medicine, nursing, pharmacy, and other health-related programs). Academic performance was assessed using grade point average (GPA) and categorized into four levels commonly used in Vietnamese universities: excellent (≥ 8.0), good (7.0–<8.0), fair (6.0–<7.0), and poor (≤ 6.0). All variables were self-reported in the online questionnaire and used as independent predictors in analyses.

Nutritional status

Nutritional status was determined using body mass index (BMI), calculated from self-reported weight (kg) and height (m) as BMI = kg/m². BMI was classified according to WHO Asian cut-off values into four ordered categories: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–22.9 kg/m²), overweight (23.0–24.9 kg/m²), and obesity (≥ 25.0 kg/m²). BMI category was treated as the primary nutritional outcome in regression analyses.

Attitudes Toward Obese Persons Scale (ATOP)

Attitudes toward people with overweight and obesity were assessed using the 20-item Attitudes Toward Obese Persons Scale (ATOP), which measures stereotypical beliefs and evaluative attitudes toward obese individuals. In the original instrument, items are rated from − 3 to + 3 and summed to yield scores from 0 to 120, with higher scores reflecting more positive attitudes [26]. In the present study, items were adapted to a 6-point Likert format and scored so that higher values indicated more negative attitudes. This adaptation was conducted to improve respondent comprehension and consistency in an online survey context. Prior to the main survey, the adapted instrument was pilot-tested on a small group of students to assess clarity and internal consistency, with minor wording refinements applied. Exploratory factor analysis identified three domains: negative stereotypes, positive beliefs, and psychological inferiority. Factor analysis was conducted using principal component extraction with varimax rotation, and factors were retained based on eigenvalues > 1 and interpretability. Internal consistency was acceptable for all domains (Cronbach’s α = 0.893, 0.746, and 0.716, respectively). Domain scores were calculated by summing item responses and were standardized to a 0–100 scale to enable comparison across domains.

Physical activity (IPAQ-SF)

Physical activity was assessed using the short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF). Participants reported frequency and duration of vigorous, moderate, and walking activities during the previous 7 days. Weekly energy expenditure was calculated in MET-minutes per week using standard coefficients (8.0 for vigorous, 4.0 for moderate, and 3.3 for walking activities). Based on total MET-minutes, participants were categorized into three levels: low (< 600 MET-min/week), moderate (600–2999), and high (≥ 3000), following established IPAQ scoring guidelines [27].

Emotional well-being (SDHS)

Emotional well-being was evaluated using the Short Depression–Happiness Scale (SDHS), which includes six depression items and three happiness items rated on a 4-point scale (0–3). Higher depression subscale scores indicated greater negative affect, while higher happiness scores indicated greater positive affect. The SDHS showed satisfactory internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.7896 for depression and 0.7490 for happiness). Subscale scores were analyzed as continuous variables [28].

Resilience (Brief Resilience Scale, BRS)

Psychological resilience was measured using the 6-item Brief Resilience Scale (BRS), which assesses the ability to recover from stress or adversity. Items were rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with negatively worded items reverse-scored. Total scores ranged from 6 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater resilience. The BRS demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in this sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.71) and was analyzed as a continuous variable [29].

Bias

Several steps were taken to reduce bias. Stratified sampling ensured balanced representation across majors and academic years. Standardized validated instruments were used to reduce measurement bias. The anonymous online survey minimized social desirability bias in reporting attitudes and behaviors. Only complete questionnaires were included in analysis to avoid information bias from missing responses.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Stata version 17.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics across BMI categories. Differences between BMI groups were examined using chi-square tests for categorical variables and Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables. Ordered logistic regression models were applied to identify independent predictors of higher BMI category. The proportional odds assumption was assessed before model application. The proportional odds assumption was assessed using the Brant test and was not violated, supporting the appropriateness of the ordered logistic regression model (overall p > 0.05). Tobit regression models were used to examine associations between explanatory variables and ATOP domain scores, which were bounded between 0 and 100. This approach was selected to account for the censored nature of the outcome variables. Results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) or regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

ATOP domain comparisons were stratified by BMI category. All regression models included academic year and major to account for the stratified sampling structure. Only participants with complete data on variables of interest were included, as missing values were minimal (< 1%); complete-case analysis was therefore applied. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using both raw BAT domain scores and standardized 0–100 scores, and results were consistent; standardized scores are reported for clarity and comparability.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Thang Long Institute of Medicine and Pharmacy Research Ethics Committee (Approval code: 01.23/GCN-HDDDNCYSH-IMP). Participants received study information and provided electronic informed consent before participation. Data were collected anonymously and stored securely to ensure confidentiality.

Results

Among 764 students, overweight and obesity were markedly more common among male students (25.7%) than females (4.5%). Differences in BMI distribution were also observed across residence (p = 0.015) and clinical training stage (p = 0.021), indicating statistically significant variation across these groups. Higher proportions of excess weight were observed among students living with family (16.9%) and in other housing (21.4%) compared with rented or dormitory housing. By training stage, excess weight was more frequent in preclinical (14.4%) and clinical students (11.7%) than in those without clinical exposure (10.8%). Across majors, general medicine had the highest combined overweight/obesity prevalence (16.3%), whereas nursing and pharmacy had lower levels (3.0% and 5.4%, respectively). Physical activity showed a gradient, with excess weight increasing from low (9.0%) to moderate (13.1%) and high activity (17.0%) groups. Academic year and GPA categories displayed broadly similar BMI distributions (Table 1).

Exploratory factor analysis of the ATOP identified three domains with acceptable internal consistency: negative stereotypes (11 items; Cronbach’s α = 0.893), positive beliefs (5 items; α = 0.746), and psychological inferiority (4 items; α = 0.716). The three-factor solution explained 53.7% of the total variance (Factor 1: 26.1%, Factor 2: 14.3%, Factor 3: 13.4%), supporting the adequacy of the model. Factor loadings were highest for ATOP16 (“should not lead normal life,” loading 0.839), ATOP4 (“workers not successful,” 0.835), and ATOP10 (“others uncomfortable,” 0.830) within the negative stereotypes domain; ATOP18 (“sexually attractive,” 0.729), ATOP1 (“as happy as others,” 0.715), and ATOP9 (“self-confident,” 0.714) within positive beliefs; and ATOP2 (“not as good as others,” 0.738) and ATOP8 (“not satisfied self,” 0.735) within psychological inferiority. Mean standardized domain scores (0–100) were 33.29 (17.53) for negative stereotypes, 56.08 (17.85) for positive beliefs, and 47.79 (18.30) for psychological inferiority (Table 2).

Ordered logistic regression showed that female gender was associated with lower odds of being in a higher BMI category (OR = 0.25, 95% CI 0.18–0.36, p < 0.001). Compared with living with family, students living in rented housing had lower odds (OR = 0.64, p = 0.012), whereas those in other residence types had higher odds (OR = 3.04, p = 0.046). Pharmacy students had lower odds of higher BMI compared with general medicine students (OR = 0.56, p = 0.014). Higher depression scores were positively associated with higher BMI category (OR = 1.09 per unit, p = 0.032). No significant associations were observed for academic year, GPA level, clinical training stage, physical activity level, happiness, or resilience (all p > 0.05) (Table 4).

SDHS Short Depression–Happiness Scale

In the Tobit models for ATOP domain scores, BMI category showed a significant association only with the psychological inferiority domain. Regression coefficients (β) represent the expected change in standardized ATOP domain scores (0–100) associated with a one-unit change in the predictor or compared with the reference group. Compared with underweight students, those with overweight had higher psychological inferiority scores (β = 8.40, p = 0.002), while normal BMI and obesity were not significantly different. BMI category was not significantly associated with negative stereotypes or positive beliefs (all p > 0.05).

Gender and psychological variables were associated with several ATOP domains. Female students had lower negative stereotype scores than males (β = −5.32, p < 0.001). Higher depression scores were inversely associated with both negative stereotypes (β = −1.90, p < 0.001) and psychological inferiority (β = −1.70, p < 0.001). Happiness was positively associated with positive beliefs (β = 1.39, p < 0.001), and resilience was also positively associated with positive beliefs (β = 0.47, p = 0.036). No significant associations were observed for residence, academic year, GPA, clinical training, or major across domains.

Lifestyle factors showed limited associations. Compared with low physical activity, moderate activity was associated with lower negative stereotype scores (β = −3.79, p = 0.006), whereas high activity showed no significant effects. Physical activity was not associated with positive beliefs or psychological inferiority (p > 0.05). Overall, significant predictors varied by domain, with negative stereotypes mainly related to gender, depression, and moderate activity; positive beliefs to happiness and resilience; and psychological inferiority to BMI and depression (Table 6).

Discussion

This study provides an integrated assessment of nutritional status and attitudes toward overweight and obesity among health-science students in Vietnam. Overall, excess weight was unevenly distributed across demographic and training characteristics, and attitudes toward obesity clustered into three interpretable domains. Distinct psychosocial correlates emerged for BMI category and for attitude domains, indicating that nutritional status and weight-related attitudes represent related but partly independent constructs among future health professionals. However, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships.

The BMI distribution in this study shows that most health science students were in the normal range, with smaller groups underweight and overweight or obese. This pattern is similar to international evidence in health related students. The HOLISTic multicenter study of 6,222 students from seven countries reported median BMI values between 21.1 and 23.2 kg/m², indicating that most students had normal weight but with signs of increasing overweight in some settings [30, 31]. Compared with other regions, the prevalence of excess weight in the present sample appears lower. For example, Saudi health science students had overweight 20.4% and obesity 14.9%, and Puerto Rican nutrition and culinary students had 27% overweight and 27% obesity [8, 32]. The strong gender difference observed here, with females having lower odds of higher BMI, is consistent with many university studies showing more overweight among male students, although some countries report different patterns [8, 33]. Differences by major were also observed, with pharmacy students having lower BMI risk than general medicine students. Previous research suggests that lifestyle patterns and academic routines within majors may influence diet and weight more than nutrition knowledge itself [32, 34].

The regression analysis also highlights the importance of living situation and mental health. Students living in rented housing had lower odds of higher BMI than those living with family, while those in other housing types had higher odds. Studies have shown that residence conditions affect food access, meal regularity, and diet quality among university students, which in turn influence body weight [8, 31, 34]. Depression was positively associated with higher BMI category in this study. Similar associations have been reported in several student populations, where poorer diet quality and higher body fat were linked with depressive symptoms and psychological distress [33, 35]. Possible mechanisms include emotional eating, reduced physical activity, and changes in appetite regulation. In contrast, academic year, GPA, clinical training, physical activity, happiness, and resilience were not associated with BMI. This suggests that living context and depression may play a more central role in student nutritional status than academic or general well being factors. Nonetheless, these findings suggest associations rather than causal effects, and alternative explanations cannot be excluded.

The present findings show that attitudes toward people with overweight and obesity among health science students were generally moderate, with domain scores indicating more positive beliefs than negative stereotypes. This pattern is broadly consistent with international evidence showing that health professional students often report mixed or neutral attitudes rather than uniformly positive views. For example, European studies in Italy and Spain found that only about half of healthcare and nutrition students had low stigma on the ATOP, while the remainder showed moderate to high stigma [19]. Similarly, UK nursing students reported overall neutral attitudes but still demonstrated gaps in knowledge and lifestyle practices related to obesity [36]. The relatively moderate negative stereotype scores in the current study therefore align with the global picture that explicit stigma exists in health students but is not uniformly extreme across all individuals or domains.

BMI category was associated only with the psychological inferiority domain. Students with overweight had significantly higher psychological inferiority scores than underweight students, whereas other BMI groups did not differ. This finding is compatible with literature suggesting that personal weight status may influence internalized beliefs about obesity more than explicit stereotypes. Some European studies report that individuals with overweight or obesity tend to show slightly lower external stigma but may experience more internalized weight concerns [18, 19]. The absence of BMI differences in negative stereotypes and positive beliefs is also consistent with mixed international evidence, where own BMI has weak and inconsistent associations with explicit attitudes [19]. Overall, the results suggest that personal weight status mainly relates to self directed perceptions rather than general beliefs about people with obesity.

Gender, psychological well being, and lifestyle factors showed clearer associations with attitudes. Consistent with the regression results, female students had lower negative stereotype scores than males, indicating more favorable attitudes. This finding is supported by multiple studies across different settings showing that female students tend to report more positive explicit attitudes toward individuals with obesity, whereas male students are more likely to endorse negative stereotypes [37–39]. This difference may be explained by higher levels of empathy, greater health awareness, and stronger social sensitivity among female students, as well as greater exposure to patient-centered communication and caregiving roles in health-related training. In contrast, male students may be more influenced by sociocultural norms that emphasize personal responsibility and control over body weight, which can contribute to stronger stigmatizing beliefs [37–39]. Depression was inversely associated with both negative stereotypes and psychological inferiority, while happiness and resilience were positively associated with positive beliefs. Broader evidence indicates that empathy and psychological well being are linked with lower weight bias and more supportive attitudes toward people with obesity [22, 40, 41]. Moderate physical activity was also associated with lower negative stereotypes, consistent with European findings that physically active health students show less stigma [19]. Together, these results indicate that attitudes toward obesity among health science students are shaped more by psychosocial and behavioral factors than by academic or demographic characteristics.

These findings have implications for practice and policy. Universities may consider integrating structured nutrition education, mental health support, and stigma-reduction training into health-science curricula. Educational strategies focusing on the biopsychosocial determinants of obesity, communication skills, and bias awareness may help improve both student health behaviors and professional attitudes. Institutional actions such as promoting healthy campus environments and accessible counseling services may further support these goals.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causality between psychosocial factors, BMI, and attitudes, and the observed relationships should be interpreted as associations only. Second, BMI and other variables were based on self-reported data, which may introduce measurement bias due to potential underreporting or overreporting. All measures were self-reported, which may introduce reporting bias, particularly for weight-related attitudes and psychological scales. The sample was drawn from two universities in Hanoi, which may limit generalizability to other regions or health-training contexts. In addition, unmeasured lifestyle factors such as dietary intake or sleep patterns were not included, and implicit attitudes toward obesity were not assessed.

Conclusion

Among Vietnamese health-science students, nutritional status and attitudes toward obesity varied across demographic, academic, and psychological characteristics. Excess weight was more common in males and certain training contexts, while attitudes toward obesity comprised distinct domains associated with emotional well-being and BMI. These findings underscore the importance of integrating student health promotion with stigma-reduction education to foster both healthier future professionals and more compassionate care for individuals with overweight and obesity. Targeted curriculum reforms and institutional policies addressing both health behaviors and weight stigma should be prioritized by universities and educators.
