Symptomatic manifestation of a cauda equina arteriovenous fistula after resection of a coexisting spinal schwannoma: illustrative case
Authors: Akira Ito, Takuya Yamaguchi, Yoshiharu Takahashi, Samuel Willyarto Anugerah, Taketo Nishizawa, Tatsuya Sasaki, Toshiki Endo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDSpinal arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) arising below the conus medullaris, particularly those involving the cauda equina or filum terminale, are rare. The coexistence of spinal AVFs and spinal tumors is exceedingly uncommon. The authors report a case of a cauda equina AVF that became clinically evident after resection of a coexisting cauda equina schwannoma.OBSERVATIONSA 72-year-old man presented with progressive bilateral lower extremity weakness, sensory disturbance, gait impairment, and constipation. MRI demonstrated multiple spinal schwannomas, and the symptoms were attributed to a cauda equina schwannoma at the L5–S1 level. Resection of the schwannoma resulted in neurological improvement. However, 17 months later, the patient developed gradual neurological deterioration without tumor recurrence. Further evaluation revealed a cauda equina AVF, which was retrospectively identifiable on the initial MRI examination. Microsurgical interruption of the AVF led to neurological improvement, with no recurrence during follow-up.LESSONSThis rare case of a cauda equina AVF concomitant with a schwannoma emphasizes two key considerations. Careful preoperative imaging evaluation is essential to identify occult vascular lesions. Additionally, resection of a spinal tumor can potentially alter local hemodynamics and render a previously silent AVF symptomatic, underscoring the importance of comprehensive imaging interpretation and surgical planning. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE251017

Among spinal vascular malformations, arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) arising below the conus medullaris differ from those of other regions in terms of embryonic origin and anatomical and imaging features.1 AVFs arising below the conus medullaris are classified into three types based on feeding arteries: filum terminale AVFs, fed by the artery of the filum terminale; dural AVFs, fed by dural arteries; and cauda equina AVFs, fed by radicular arteries.2 Dural AVFs in this region account for 23% of all spinal dural AVFs; hence, they are not considered rare.3 Cauda equina AVFs with fistulas located on a nerve root are quite rare compared with filum terminale AVFs.2,4 Herein, we report an extremely rare case of cauda equina AVF concomitant with spinal schwannoma, wherein the cauda equina AVF became symptomatic after spinal schwannoma resection.

Illustrative Case

A 72-year-old man presented with progressive bilateral leg weakness and sensory disturbance. The patient’s lower limb motor strength (based on manual muscle testing) was 4/5 for the iliopsoas and quadriceps muscles. Sensory testing showed decreased pinprick sensation in both legs (8/10) and diminished vibration sensation predominantly on the right side. The patellar reflex test revealed hyperreflexia. The patient also exhibited bladder dysfunction and constipation. MRI revealed multiple spinal schwannomas. The symptomatic mass lesion at the L5–S1 level was hyperintense on T2-weighted imaging and hypointense on T1-weighted imaging (Fig. 1A and B). Flow voids and abnormal intramedullary T2 hyperintense signals were not clearly detectable. Although serpiginous vascular structures were retrospectively identifiable on T2-weighted images, they were not sufficiently prominent to raise suspicion for a spinal AVF (Fig. 1A). Resection of the L5–S1 tumor was performed, with pathological examination confirming a World Health Organization grade I schwannoma (MIB-1 index 1%–2%). At 17 months after the surgery, the patient experienced recurrent gait instability and frequent falls. MRI revealed no evidence of tumor recurrence. However, T2-weighted MRI showed hyperintensity of the lower thoracic spinal cord with flow voids in the spinal canal, indicating a spinal AVF (Fig. 2A). T1-weighted sagittal MRI with contrast demonstrated enhancement of the draining veins (Fig. 2B). Spinal digital subtraction angiography revealed a cauda equina AVF fed by the proximal radicular artery. The anterior spinal artery (ASA), originating from the left T9 segmental artery (the Adamkiewicz artery), showed changes in caliber and continued to the proximal radicular artery, which served as the feeder to the cauda equina AVF. The radicular artery was directly connected to the radicular vein at the L3 level. The draining vein exhibited a wavy running pattern and ascended to the posterior spinal vein (Fig. 2C–E). After providing consent, the patient underwent L3 osteoplastic laminotomy. The filum terminale and cauda equina nerve roots were identified after durotomy. The fistula was located on the left cauda equina nerve root, supplied by the proximal radicular artery with cranial drainage via an enlarged radicular vein (Fig. 3A). Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring with motor and sensory evoked potentials was conducted. Indocyanine green video angiography confirmed the feeder, shunt point, and draining vein (Fig. 3B). Shunt flow cessation was validated via temporary interruption of the feeder using a surgical clip. After verifying the absence of changes in motor and sensory evoked potentials, the feeder was coagulated and cut immediately proximal to the fistulous point (Fig. 3C). Indocyanine green video angiography confirmed the disappearance of early venous filling (Fig. 3D). Intraoperative findings were consistent with cauda equina AVF (Video 1). Discussion

Observations

As highlighted in the present case, several clinical and radiographic findings should diagnostically raise suspicion for a spinal AVF. Clinically, spinal AVFs typically present with slowly progressive myelopathy characterized by gait disturbances, sensory deficits, and bladder or bowel dysfunction due to chronic venous congestion of the spinal cord. Radiographically, the characteristic MRI findings include intramedullary T2-weighted hyperintensity reflecting venous hypertensive myelopathy and serpiginous perimedullary flow voids representing dilated draining veins. In the present case, the diagnosis of AVF was not suspected at the initial evaluation, and preoperative MRI did not demonstrate typical imaging features of venous congestive myelopathy. Moreover, multiple spinal schwannomas were present, and the large L5–S1 lesion was considered responsible for the patient’s neurological symptoms. These factors likely contributed to the initial diagnostic focus on the spinal tumor and oversight of the underlying vascular lesion. Therefore, the present case highlights the importance of carefully evaluating subtle vascular findings on spinal MRI and maintaining a broad differential diagnosis during the assessment of patients with progressive neurological symptoms. Even subtle serpiginous vascular structures along the cauda equina should prompt consideration of an underlying AVF and further vascular evaluation.

Cauda Equina AVF

Spinal AVFs arising below the conus medullaris affect the cauda equina or filum terminale. Despite being distant from the spinal cord, these lesions typically cause progressive myelopathy rather than hemorrhage.1,5 To appropriately treat these lesions, obtaining an accurate diagnosis is important.6,7 Namba et al. reported the precise angioarchitecture of cauda equina AVFs.4 Nevertheless, to reassess the detailed angioarchitecture of cauda equina AVF, a literature review was performed. A PubMed search was conducted using the following terms: “cauda equina AND arteriovenous fistula” or “cauda equina AND arteriovenous malformation.” After performing the literature review, 13 cases, including ours, were identified (Table 1).2,4,8–12 According to these reports, the cauda equina is supplied by the segmental radicular arteries. The proximal one-third of the radicular nerve is supplied by the radicular artery from the vasa corona, ASA, and posterior spinal artery. Meanwhile, the distal two-thirds are supplied by the radicular artery from the lumbar or sacral segmental artery.13 Cauda equina AVFs fed by the proximal radicular arteries are less common.8 Nevertheless, our literature review showed that cauda equina AVFs supplied by the proximal radicular artery were more common than those supplied by the distal radicular artery (Table 1). In our case, the cauda equina AVF was fed by the proximal radicular artery from the ASA, and the ASA exhibited caliber and course changes at the conus medullaris apex level, which may reflect the transition of the ASA into the radicular artery. These angiographic characteristics were consistent with those of previous reports.4 In our review, the radicular vein had a wavy draining pattern in 8 of the 10 cases, which may be a diagnostic finding for cauda equina AVF, as shown in the study by Namba et al.4 Treatment options for cauda equina AVFs include direct surgery, endovascular embolization, or a combination of both. Eight patients underwent direct surgery and 2 underwent embolization, with generally favorable outcomes (Table 1). Coexistence of Spinal Tumor and Spinal AVF

The coexistence of spinal AVFs and spinal lipoma is common due to the association of lipoma with spinal dysraphic malformations.2,14–26 To identify the coexistence of spinal AVFs and spinal tumor other than lipoma, a literature review was performed using the following PubMed search terms: “spinal arteriovenous fistula AND spinal tumor” or “spinal arteriovenous malformation AND spinal tumor.” This search revealed only 2 cases. One case involved a cauda equina schwannoma with an incorporated direct arteriovenous shunt fed by a small arterial branch of the median sacral artery, which was identified preoperatively.27 The fistula was confirmed on the tumor capsule and cauterized during schwannoma resection. The other case involved a spinal dural AVF adjacent to a thoracic neurofibroma.28 Preoperative diagnostic modalities could detect only the neurofibroma. They could not identify the tumor intraoperatively. However, they found the dural AVF and cauterized it. The patient did not recover, and postoperative MRI showed a residual spinal tumor. The tumor was successfully resected during the second surgery.

In our case, the patient initially presented with neurological improvement; however, his symptoms gradually recurred 17 months after the surgery. Follow-up MRI showed newly apparent perispinal flow voids indicative of an arteriovenous shunt. Subsequent spinal digital subtraction angiography identified a cauda equina AVF at the L3 level. Direct microsurgical interruption of the intradural draining vein resulted in significant symptomatic improvement.

We hypothesized that resection of the schwannoma altered the venous drainage of the preexisting but clinically silent cauda equina AVF. This then led to postoperative hemodynamic changes and subsequent symptom manifestation. This hypothesis is supported by the findings of Sato et al., who compared symptomatic and asymptomatic spinal dural AVFs and showed that obstruction or alteration of radicular venous outflow to the extradural venous plexus plays an important role in the development of venous congestion, spinal cord hypoperfusion, and neurological deterioration.29

In the current case, laminotomy at a different spinal level may have altered the venous drainage pathways of the cauda equina AVF, unmasking its hemodynamic impact and resulting in delayed symptom progression. This case emphasizes that even if a spinal AVF is not apparent on initial imaging, surgical intervention for a coexisting spinal pathology may precipitate clinical manifestation through changes in venous outflow dynamics.

Lessons

Herein, we report a rare case of concomitant spinal schwannoma and cauda equina AVF that became symptomatic after schwannoma resection. Cauda equina AVF is a rare vascular pathology that can cause congestive neurological deterioration and may coexist with other spinal disorders. Hence, it is challenging to diagnose. In the current case, the AVF was overlooked on the initial evaluation. Moreover, treatment focused solely on the spinal schwannoma, resulting in delayed identification of the underlying vascular lesion and subsequent symptom recurrence. This case underscores the importance of accurate diagnosis in cases of coexisting multiple spinal pathologies, including rare lesions such as cauda equina AVFs. Multiple sporadic schwannomas may obscure subtle radiological findings of an AVF, thereby emphasizing the need for comprehensive preoperative image interpretation with a broad differential diagnosis. Furthermore, this case highlights that a clinically silent spinal AVF may become symptomatic after surgical intervention for another spinal pathology, presumably due to alterations in local hemodynamics. Awareness of this potential interaction during initial diagnostic assessment and surgical planning is essential to prevent delayed diagnosis and neurological deterioration. Ultimately, cautious assessment and appropriate management of coexisting spinal pathologies may contribute to improved patient outcomes.
