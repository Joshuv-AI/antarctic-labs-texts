A novel synthetic MRI for discrimination of oral cavity and oropharynx malignancy
Authors: Rui Wang, Qifan Ma, Yong Zhang, Jie Shi, Hongbo Zhao, Ying Yuan, Xiaofeng Tao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the discriminative performance of morphologic and quantitative images derived from synthetic MRI in oral cavity and oropharynx lesions.MethodsA total of 65 patients with 69 lesions and 5 healthy volunteers underwent preoperative MRI scans using the pre-contrast synthetic MRI sequence called MAGiC, which generates morphologic and quantitative images. The repeatability of the quantitative values was assessed in healthy volunteers using the coefficient of variation (CV) and Bland-Altman plots. The quantitative values were measured, and subsequent histogram analysis was conducted on the images obtained by MAGiC to differentiate between benign and malignant lesions. Logistic regression calculated prediction probabilities for each index, combining multiple indices to generate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, which were compared using the Delong test.ResultsThe quantitative values derived from the MAGiC sequence exhibited high repeatability with CV below 15%. The directly measured T1 and T2 values within the lesions exhibited significant differences (P < 0.01). Moreover, notable differences were observed in the histogram features of Energy and Total Energy for differentiation (P < 0.05). The area under the curve (AUC) for histogram features extracted from synthetic morphologic images was 0.826, while for synthetic quantitative images was 0.833. The diagnostic accuracy was further enhanced by integrating synthetic morphologic and quantitative images, resulting in an optimal AUC of 0.936.ConclusionAs a time-efficient synthetic sequence, MAGiC offers an alternative approach for preoperative differentiation of oral cavity and oropharynx lesions with enhanced diagnostic efficacy in the absence of contrast.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Synthetic MRI, Histogram features, Oral cavity and oropharynx

Introduction

Malignancies originating from the mucosa of the oral cavity and oropharynx are widely acknowledged as one of the most prevalent types of malignancies globally, with an estimated 470,000 new cases and 225,000 deaths annually [1]. Accurate differentiation between benign and malignant pathologies can help minimize unnecessary biopsies and inform the selection of personalized preoperative treatment strategies, thereby guiding the decision between conservative management and aggressive surgical resection.

Conventional Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with contrast enhancement preforms well in differentiating malignancies in the oral cavity and oropharynx regions, whereas the administration of contrast medium potentially lead to the nephrogenic systemic sclerosis in patients with renal failure and gadolinium deposition concerning in the brain parenchyma as well as other sites [2]. Additionally, prolonged scanning is much more likely to cause unintentional swallowing and movement contributing to motion artifacts that affect the image quality of the oral cavity and oropharynx. Synthetic MRI (SyMRI) allows for the acquisition of various MR contrasts prior to contrast injection, significantly reducing examination time compared to standard MRI sequences, thereby can effectively address these issues [3, 4].

Magnetic Resonance Image Compilation (MAGiC), a type of SyMRI, enables the quantification of relaxation times and proton density through a multi-echo acquisition technique known as saturation-recovery using turbo spin-echo readout (QRAPMASTER) within a significantly shortened examination time. MAGiC facilitates the simultaneous acquisition of multiple parametric images including morphologic and quantitative images within a single scanning session, reducing scan time and improving quantitative reproducibility [4]. Quantitative longitudinal relaxation time (T1), transverse relaxation time (T2), and proton density (PD) values are intrinsic magnetic properties of the tissue and independent from the MRI scanners or scanning parameters at a given field strength. Quantitative images with relaxation values revealed different physiological information by providing extracellular level information on tumors, as widely used for myocardial and other diseases [5–8]. Previous studies have reported the promising results in differentiating various pathologies through quantitative images derived from SyMRI [7, 9–12]. However, there have been few reports on the diagnostic efficiency in distinguishing lesions of the oral cavity and oropharynx by synthetic morphological and quantitative images.

The histogram feature provides quantitative parameters of the pixel distribution of gray level in an original image. Recent studies have conducted histogram analysis based on SyMRI for tumor differentiation and correlation with prognostic factors in breast cancer and head and neck tumors [12–14]. However, limited studies have focused on the oral cavity and oropharynx with quantitative analysis. Thus, the current study aims to evaluate the performance of the MAGiC technique with histogram analysis and quantitative relaxation values in differentiating benign and malignant lesions within the oral cavity and oropharynx regions.

Methods and materials

Study participants

This retrospective study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of our hospital. For this study, 112 patients with lesions in oral cavity and oropharynx were conducted MRI scans with MAGiC sequence at our institution between April and November 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients underwent MRI of MAGiC sequence preoperatively; (2) pathological diagnosis was confirmed; (3) no previous treatments for lesions before MRI scan. Exclusion criteria included (1) metastatic or recurrent lesions; (2) poor image quality with motion artifacts; (3) some benign lesions exhibited a significant presence of vascular and water components, including vascular malformation and inflammation; (4) failure reconstruction from the dedicated workstation. In total, this study included 65 patients with 69 lesions, and an additional 5 healthy volunteers were recruited to validate the stability of quantitative measurements. MR data acquisition

The examinations were conducted on a 3.0-T MR scanner (Premier, GE Healthcare, WI) with a 21-channel head and neck coil. We used a parallel imaging method called ASSET (Autocalibrating Signal for Sensitivity Encoding Technique) by GE Healthcare to accelerate scan times while maintaining image quality. The ASSET technique uses multiple receiver coils (phased-array coils) to acquire signal simultaneously. Each coil has a different spatial sensitivity profile, allowing partial data (under-sampled k-space) to be acquired to reconstruct a full image. To minimize motion artifacts, prior to the MAGiC sequence scan, we request that the patient reduce and minimize the amplitude of swallowing and breathing as much as possible via a microphone. MAGiC-derived images were obtained covering the oral cavity for each subject using multiple-delay-multiple-echo (MDME) sequence with two echo times (14/92 msec) and four saturation delay times (170/670/1840/3840 msec). The scan parameters of the MAGiC sequence were: FOV, 240 × 192 mm2; acquisition matrix, 320*256; TR, 4648 ms; echo-train length (ETL), 16; slice thickness/gap, 3.00/0.3 mm; NEX, 1.00; scan time, 3 min 42s. For T1-weighted images derived from MAGIC, the nominal TR was 650 ms and the TE was 10 ms. For T2 STIR images derived from MAGIC, the nominal TR was 15,000 ms, the TE was 100 ms, and the inversion time was 300 ms. The healthy volunteers were included without any diseases in oral cavity and oropharynx, and did not have any alcohol or smoking habits. All participants underwent three MAGiC scans within a two-week period, with a minimum of one hour fasting prior to each MR examination.

The MAGIC sequence has been approved by FDA and can be used as a clinical protocol. However, the MAGIC sequence is used as a research protocol in our hospital because we would like to further validate the clinical significance of quantitative imaging in discrimination of oral cavity and oropharynx malignancy.

Image processing

The synthetic MR images were reconstructed off-line using the dedicated workstation. We included the T1-weighted (T1W) and T2 short-tau inversion recovery (T2-STIR) images from all synthetic morphological images, as well as T1, T2, and proton density (PD) mappings from all synthetic quantitative images. Regions of interests (ROIs) were manually delineated on synthetic T2-STIR images with 3D slicer (download.slicer.org, version 5.2.2) by referring to conventional T2-weighted fat-saturation (T2-FS) images by an experienced radiologist with 5 years of diagnostic experience. Subsequently, one experienced radiologist with over 15 years of expertise in head and neck imaging reviewed and modified the ROIs in agreement with the first radiologist. The conventional T2-FS images were registered onto synthetic T2-STIR images using the “Registration” module in 3D slicer software. The purpose of registration was two-fold. In addition to spatial alignment for head motion correction between different sequences, the slice displacement due to swallowing and breathing could also be minimized. Due to the convenience of formation of consistent images derived from MAGiC, the delineated ROIs were copied to other synthetic morphologic and quantitative images. The ROIs were defined along the periphery of the lesions at the largest slice, excluding any vessels and adjacent structures. The quantitative T1, T2, and PD values were measured within the same ROIs.

We conducted the position correction of three volunteer scans when measured ROIs. For volunteers, we measured values in the oral cavity and surrounding tissues bilaterally using the same rectangular shape at the same slices across the subjects. The average values obtained from three consecutive slices centered on the first visible level of uvula were considered as the final measurements. The measurements were independently conducted by two experienced radiologists who were blinded to each other’s assessments. Specifically, the tongue was measured by dividing it into the anterior 2/3 and posterior 1/3, referred to as the body of tongue and the root of tongue respectively. For patients, the final measurement was also defined as the average of the quantitative values obtained from three consecutive slices along the lesion periphery, with the central slice selected based on the largest lesion area. This central slice was also used as the reference for ROI delineation. Then, histogram features were extracted from the delineated ROIs of both morphologic and quantitative images by PyRadiomics (version 3.0.1). The features and measured values exhibiting significant differences were selected for constructing the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Graphpad Prism, Medcalc and SPSS software. We performed a post-hoc power analysis based on the observed effect sizes evaluate the sample size. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis was used to assess the agreement of quantitative parameters between two experienced radiologists. Bland-Altman plots were generated, and the coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated to assess the repeatability of the quantitative values obtained from mappings.

The differences of measured quantitative values and histogram features were compared between benign and malignant lesions through unpaired two sample t or Mann Whitney test according to the results of normality and lognormality tests. The histogram features that exhibit statistically significant differences between benign and malignant lesions in the stepwise logistic regression analysis were selected. The ROC curves were generated by integrating the predictive probabilities of both quantitative and morphologic indices to evaluate the diagnostic efficiency of morphologic and quantitative images derived from MAGiC. Delong test was used to compare the ROC curves. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction, and adjusted p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 65 patients with 69 lesions (mean age 53.7 ± 16.2 years, 49 males, 16 females) were enrolled in this study, including 57 malignancies (mean age 55.9 ± 13.7 years, 42 males, 13 females) and 12 benign lesions (mean age was 48.8 ± 24.4 years, 9 males, 3 females). The included malignant types were squamous carcinoma (49 lesions) and adenoid cystic carcinoma (4 lesions), mucoepidermoid carcinoma (1 lesion), diffuse large B lymphoma (1 lesion), clear cell carcinoma (1 lesion), lymphoepithelial carcinoma (1 lesion), and the benign types were oral submucous fibrosis (7 lesions), leukoplakia (1 lesion), lipoma (1 lesion), fibroma (1 lesion), lymphatic malformation (1 lesion), ectopic sebaceous gland (1 patient). Two males presented with two malignant lesions, while two males exhibited coexistence of one malignant and benign lesion each. The patients with coexisting different type of lesions were regarded as two independent individuals in malignant and benign samples, respectively. Five healthy volunteers (3 women and 2 men) aged 23–27 years were included in this study. No significant differences were found in age, gender or anatomical sites between malignancies and benign lesion groups. Table 1 listed the characteristics of all included patients.

The stability of quantitative values derived from magic sequence

The excellent inter-observer agreements of all quantitative values between two experienced radiologists were achieved with ICC > 0.8. Subsequently, the mean values were utilized for further analysis. The quantitative measurements demonstrated the high reproducibility of mappings derived from the MAGiC sequence in the oral cavity and surrounding tissues, with coefficients of variation (CVs) below 15%. The results showed the parotid gland with the minimum CVs, while the mandible with maximum CVs (Table 2). Additionally, the Bland-Altman plot visually demonstrated that the mean of the differences was approximately zero. Subsequently, a paired t-test was conducted to compare the difference between the mean of the differences and zero. The results showed that all p-values were greater than 0.05 (Body of tongue, T1, p = 0.2155, T2, p = 0.5022, PD, p = 0.2317; Root of tongue, T1, p = 0.6469, T2, p = 0.2185, PD, p = 0.8710), suggesting that the mean of the measurement results exhibited minimal deviation and the systematic error was negligible. Furthermore, the majority of the data points fell within the 95% limits of agreement (LoA), which implies that the random errors between the measured values were within an acceptable range, thus confirming good consistency of the measurements. Discussion

This study assessed the diagnostic performance of a novel MAGiC technique in distinguishing between benign and malignant lesions in oral cavity and oropharynx. After confirmation of high repeatability of quantitative values, we demonstrated the diagnostic ability of MAGiC sequence, especially by incorporating synthetic quantitative images with morphologic images.

The reproducibility of quantitative values is essential for making accurate quantitative comparisons in clinical practice. The consistent and highly reproducible results obtained from MAGiC align well with previous findings in the field of brain research [15]. However, there is limited validation of the repeatability of synthetic quantitative values in oral cavity and oropharynx studies conducted thus far. Therefore, our results provide reliable support and a necessary foundation for applying synthetic quantitative values in future investigations.

The quantitative values depend on the tissue characteristics, thereby affording a microscopic perspective of the properties through imaging. We found that malignancies exhibited a significantly higher relative change in T1 and T2 values, while there were no significant variations observed in PD values. The findings with the higher T1 values in malignancies are consistent with previous studies in breast cancer patients [16, 17], while other reports concluded the opposite results [11, 18–20]. The patient population, fat modules and pathologic microenvironment in different anatomic sites may affect quantification of the T1 values. The T1 relaxation time represents the duration required for energy transfer from the proton group to external molecules. It is dependent on the precession frequency of surrounding molecules and is sensitive to the presence of macromolecules and water content [21–23]. Therefore, the elevated T1 values are typically associated with a higher abundance of extracellular matrix with the presence of complex stromal cells, and are theoretically considered to be positively correlated with proliferation [16, 24, 25]. Additionally, one characteristic of malignant lesions is that, due to the rapid growth of cancer cells, there is insufficient oxygen within the lesion. Since oxygen molecules are paramagnetic, the reduced concentration of dissolved O2 in the core of a malignant lesion leads to an increase in T1 relaxation time [26]. T2 relaxation is primarily attributed to spin-spin interactions among protons, which are closely related to the intrinsic structural and molecular characteristics of the tissue, and is predominantly independent of the surrounding lattice environment. The higher T2 values observed in our study were consistent with the findings reported in a previous study on fast three-dimensional breast imaging using MR fingerprinting [18], but inconsistent with some prior studies [11, 16, 17]. Higher T2 values were found to be correlated with increased free water content, as free water exhibits a longer transverse relaxation time due to its smaller molecular size. The discrepancies in the results may be attributed to the majority of oral submucous fibrosis cases in our study involved benign lesions, characterized by the deposition of collagen fibers, which led to a decrease in T2 values. Moreover, neovascularization in the malignant lesions may have a greater impact on T1 and T2 values, which could explain the higher T1 and T2 values observed in our study. Notably, the quantitative values deeply depend on the tissue characteristics. Due to the different pathological types of tumors that predominantly occur in different regions of the head and neck, the applicability of our results to other sites beyond the oral cavity and oropharynx requires further validation.

The histogram analysis has been successfully conducted across various organs to facilitate differential diagnosis, demonstrating robust practical performance with ultrasound, computerized tomography (CT) and MR [27–32]. Previous studies have demonstrated the promising performance of histogram features derived from SyMRI with only quantitative images focused on establishing correlations with prognostic factors, molecular subtypes, and the prediction of lymph node metastasis [13, 14, 33]. Hence, there appears to be a scarcity of studies employing histogram analysis for the evaluation of differential performance in SyMRI encompassing both morphological and quantitative images. In our study, the histogram features of the energy and total energy obtained from both morphologic and quantitative images exhibited statistically significant variations between different pathologies. Energy, referring to the magnitude of voxel values in the image, is dependent on both volume and signal density [34]. Moreover, the total energy denotes the magnitude of the energy attribute adjusted according to the voxel volume in mm3. Higher values of energy and total energy may indicate the complexity of the tissue components [35, 36]. Therefore, the features of pixel gray distribution may confirm the heterogeneity of morphology and uncontrolled proliferation of cancer cells which are characteristic hallmarks of malignancies.

SyMRI enables the acquisition of both morphologic and quantitative information in one single scan, thereby effectively enhancing examination efficacy and reducing motion artifacts in the oral cavity and oropharynx. Previous studies on head and neck imaging have primarily focused on the values derived from synthetic quantitative images, thus failing to fully exploit the other potential benefits offered by this particular sequence [12, 19]. Meanwhile, the efficient sequences are accompanied by a lower resolution compared to conventional sequences, motion sensitivity, and challenges in small lesion detection, necessitating attention to the impact of noise and potential artifacts, particularly when assessing small lesions. Overall, based on its intrinsic benefits of generating multi-mode imaging, shortening examination time, providing confirmed stability in quantitative measurement values, and offering potential diagnostic information, the synthetic MRI technique (MAGiC) shows promising clinical potential for enhancing diagnostic performance in the oral cavity and oropharynx regions by combining pixel gray distribution with micro-cellular information.

The study had the following limitation. The results need to be conducted a detailed analysis by classification due to the wide variation in lesion characteristics, thereby enhancing their clinical interpretability for each type of malignant and benign lesion. However, the small sample size in some categories limits the ability to obtain reasonable data analysis results. Thus, the larger, multi-center cohorts are required for further validation to confirm the findings and explore the utility of advanced post-processing methods for improving diagnostic accuracy.

In conclusion, this study validated the clinical significance of integrating synthetic morphological and quantitative images for distinguishing malignant and benign lesions in the oral cavity and oropharynx regions by employing a time-saving MAGiC sequence without the need for contrast injection.
