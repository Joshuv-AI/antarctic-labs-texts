Assessing the status and challenges of vulnerability to viability transitions: small-scale fisheries in the transboundary Sundarbans mangrove forest
Authors: Md Ruyel Miah, Prateep Kumar Nayak, Jeremy Pittman, Simron J Singh, Ratana Chuenpagdee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall-scale fisheries around the world are confronted with multidimensional vulnerabilities related to social, natural, economic, institutional and political aspects. The issues around vulnerability are even more intense when it comes to transboundary fisheries systems. This study assesses the vulnerabilities of small-scale fisheries and the challenges in facilitating their transitions to viability in the transboundary Sundarbans mangrove forest shared between India and Bangladesh in the Bay of Bengal. Data were collected using household surveys, key informant interviews and focus group discussions. The results reveal that small-scale fisheries face diverse vulnerabilities sourced from social (e.g., reduced income and increased social injustices), natural (e.g., decreased fish stock) and governance systems (e.g., increased restrictions and limited access) of the forest. Several factors, for example, a lack of common governing action and mismatches of governance goals and implementation, cause barriers to transitions toward viability. The study suggests that increased participation of relevant stakeholders and their voices is crucial to make comprehensive governing actions from both sides of the forest and to facilitate the viability transitions.Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Economics, Economics, Environmental social sciences, Environmental studies

Introduction

Fisheries resources that span across political, social, and ecological boundaries make governance complex, especially for small-scale fisheries. In transboundary fisheries systems, i.e., shared social-ecological systems (SESs) between two or more countries, small-scale fisheries face multifaceted vulnerabilities, driven by social, political, economic, and cultural challenges. As Scholtens and Bavinck1 define it, transboundary fishing involves fishers harvesting marine resources across borders, beyond national jurisdiction. This practice raises sustainability concerns, impacting both social justice and ecological health2. These challenges compound when the resources in question are common-pool resources (CPRs). According to Ostrom3, CPRs are characterized by the difficulty of excluding users and the subtractability of resource units, where the use of resources by one diminishes the availability for others. Mangrove ecosystems are examples of CPR due to the difficulty of excluding potential users and the subtractability of the resource flow4–7. The physical nature of mangrove forests (i.e., intertidal zones, multiple entry points) makes it difficult and costly to exclude users from accessing resources, such as fish and honey5,8,9. Besides, these resources are subtractable; for instance, harvesting fish by one user can diminish what is available for others8–10.

Furthermore, the governance challenges get intensified due to mangrove’s unique intertidal ecological niche, providing key services, such as carbon sequestration, coastal protection, and nursery habitats for commercially important species4,11. Mangroves are home to various resources, such as fisheries (e.g., finfish, shrimp, crab) and timber and non-timber forest products (e.g., honey, wax)4,10,12, thereby contributing to income and livelihoods for millions of small-scale fishers and other resource users. In the transboundary region, the cross-border movement of fish and fishers makes it difficult to control, monitor and implement regulations13,14. These challenges align with the core CPR theory, which suggests that effective governance requires institutional arrangements that fit the resource system’s scale and dynamics15. In managing mobile resources, the structure and function of formal and informal institutions are unlikely to match within and across political boundaries16,17. The differences in jurisdictions, interests, and values among various institutions regarding transboundary resources make governance complex18.

Small-scale fishers’ border-crossing, following fish movements (such as migratory or straddling stocks) or accessing nearby fishing grounds, sometimes leads to overfishing, increases conflicts and competition between fishers from neighboring areas13,19. Additionally, they cross borders for shelter during disasters or in emergencies when drifting20. For many small-scale fishers, mobility or transnational migration is a livelihood pattern21, which is criminalized by state-biased contrasts in the context of mobile resource exploitation22. Therefore, the vulnerabilities of small-scale fisheries in the transboundary context are not confined to a particular jurisdiction, domain, or boundary; they are often linked to broader problems (e.g., geopolitical disputes), making them challenging to address in isolation23,24. Such problems are unlikely to be addressed through a one-size-fits-all approach.

This is the case for the transboundary Sundarbans mangrove forest between India and Bangladesh in the Bay of Bengal. The total area of the forest is approximately 10,000 km2, with 60% of the forest located within Bangladesh’s jurisdiction and 40% in West Bengal, India25,26. The Sundarbans are recognized for providing essential goods and services to human societies and playing crucial ecological roles25,27. The CPRs of the mangrove forest provide, including fisheries and honey, on which millions of small-scale fishers from both sides of the forest rely28,29. While there is no universal definition of small-scale fisheries, which are context-specific30, the fishers in the Sundarbans use small boats (both motorized and non-motorized, between 10.6 and 11.6 m) for short trips within the forest, in groups of 2–6 people, with minimal investment. More than 12 million fishers are making a significant contribution to food and nutritional security, employment, and poverty reduction for communities living around the forest28,29,31,32. The fishers exploit the diversity of fish species, including commercially important Hilsa shad (Tenualosa Ilisha), Mud crab (Scylla sp.), Tiger shrimp (Penaeus monodon) and Prawn (Macrobrachium rosenbergii)33. Fishers use a variety of fishing gears, including gill nets, cast nets, set bag nets, sips, and specialized crab traps. A notable fishing practice by women is the collection of shrimp post-larvae (PL) for sale to aquaculture farms. Additionally, they are actively involved in crab harvesting alongside men and form the major workforce in the post-harvest processing industry, as well as the labor force for shrimp and crab farms34.

Both men and women small-scale fishers face a number of vulnerabilities in the Sundarbans, including climate change-induced sea level rise35, salinity intrusion, and pollution36, overfishing, lack of alternative livelihood sources, poor health and safety conditions, large-scale aquaculture practices and weak governance arrangements11,37, that affect fishers earning their livelihoods from the forest. Furthermore, the fishing activities are neither open year-round nor throughout the forest. The Bangladesh part of the forest comprises three wildlife sanctuaries, i.e., the Sundarbans East, South, and West, where fishing is prohibited. Additionally, fishing in the Bangladesh Sundarbans is restricted for four different durations each year: January-February, May 20 to July 23, June-August, and October 12 to November 238. Similarly, the Indian part of the Sundarbans is divided into a core zone (prohibited to fishing) and a buffer zone (with limited fishing access)39. Bangladeshi fishers need a forest entry pass, and in India, a Boat Licence Certificate (BLC) to access the forest.

The governance of Sundarbans forest resources (e.g., fish, honey, wax) is predominantly top-down, driven by state-centered policies and priorities37,40. Initiatives are taken to decentralize the governance through co-management committees (CMCs) in Bangladesh41 and Joint Forest Management Committees (JFMCs) in India42. A Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) was signed between Bangladesh and India in 2011, and one of its key focuses was to address the livelihood issues of communities living around the forest, but this has not yet progressed. Each of the forest’s CPRs presents, to some extent, distinct governance challenges. For example, forest timber products are formally regulated by the forest department, while fisheries and non-timber products are often utilized under a mix of formal rules and informal local community practices41,43. The involvement of the local communities in the decision-making and policy enforcement is often limited or superficial41,44. While the CPR literature suggests that meaningful participation of local communities can foster more sustainable and legitimate institutions3,45, it is important to recognize that such participation does not automatically equate to equitable control or better outcomes. To some extent, it can reinforce local power inequities or impose burdens without granting real agency46,47.

While studies are conducted on transboundary fisheries and water resource governance around the world2,13,16,19,20,48–52, the issue of transboundary fishing remains poorly studied in the Bay of Bengal1,2,53, and no such studies have been conducted on the Sundarbans mangrove forest through the lens of CPRs. This study aims to assess the vulnerabilities of small-scale fisheries in the transboundary Sundarbans and the factors that enable or hinder the viability of small-scale fisheries by analyzing the characteristics of the social, ecological, and governing systems. In doing so, the study will answer the following two questions: (i) What are the key vulnerabilities of small-scale fisheries in the Sundarbans transboundary fisheries system? (ii) What are the factors that hinder or facilitate the vulnerability to viability transition for small-scale fisheries in the transboundary context? In analyzing the complex, multi-layered challenges in transboundary CPR governance, this study draws on interactive governance theory54. This theory provides a robust framework for understanding the interplay between the small-scale fisheries SES of the Sundarbans and the governing system (comprised of multiple levels and actors). This theoretical lens is crucial for analyzing the interactions between diverse actors and institutions across borders, as well as the structural and functional elements that enable or hinder effective governance. It provides a comprehensive tool for diagnosing transboundary problems, including CPR dilemmas and transboundary conflicts, and for identifying potential pathways toward viable small-scale fisheries and the sustainability of the mangrove ecosystem.

The following section presents the main findings on the social, ecological, and governance systems of the transboundary Sundarbans mangrove fisheries. In the discussion, the study identifies key vulnerabilities and emerging themes that facilitate or hinder the transition from vulnerability to viability. The study then concludes with key research findings, recommendations for governing bodies and public policies, and future research directions. Finally, the study describes the conceptual framework, key methods, and data collection and analysis techniques.

Results

The results section first presents the characteristics of transboundary mangrove ecosystems, with particular focus on small-scale fisheries and the common vulnerabilities that fishers face. The governance approaches in practice for managing the transboundary mangrove CPRs and small-scale fisheries’ access are explained. The common channels of interaction between the governing systems and the system-to-be-governed are also presented.

Small-scale fisheries in the transboundary Sundarbans

The small-scale fishermen and women who responded to the survey are dominated by men in both India and Bangladesh. According to the survey respondents, fishing in the mangroves is predominantly practiced by older individuals aged 30-65 in both countries (Fig. 1a), indicating a gap in intergenerational renewal. Talking to some older fishers and youth (below 30 years of age), the findings suggest that younger generations are not encouraged much to continue in the fishing profession due to a number of reasons, including poor income, emerging restrictions (e.g., increased fishing bans), reduced availability of fish, and seasonality. Among the full-time fishers surveyed, all depend on small-scale fishing for their income, primarily on fish and mud crab (Fig. 1b), suggesting a high dependency on fisheries resources. This study found that the sources of alternative income among the research participants are very limited, mostly working as day laborers in fisheries farms or in the agricultural field.

The study reveals systemic vulnerabilities plaguing small-scale fishing communities, with alarming deficiencies in healthcare access, infrastructure, and food security. As presented in Table 1, about 83% of Indian and 77% of Bangladeshi fishers surveyed report inadequate medical services. The survey data expose particularly grim realities for maternal health, with pregnant women enduring dangerous journeys to distant clinics, sometimes requiring hours-long boat rides in India. Nutritional insecurity presents another crisis, with 73% of Indian and 51% of Bangladeshi parents surveyed expressing their struggle to feed children adequately. While fishers in Bangladesh show marginally better outcomes (45% can afford basic nutrition), the broader picture remains dire. According to survey respondents in the study, 75% in India 85% in Bangladesh expressed a lack of subsistence capacity, relying entirely on purchased food. They also reported that during financial shocks, 54% and 34% of surveyed fishing households in India and Bangladesh, respectively, reduced their daily meals from three to two. The educational enrollment rates for the next generation are increasing, as reported by survey respondents in India and Bangladesh (Table 1). Although women are actively involved in fishing, especially in crab and shrimp PL harvest, they still depend on their husbands or male counterparts to fulfill their needs, as expressed by around 80% survey respondents in India and Bangladesh.

According to key informant interviewees, small-scale fisheries in the Sundarbans mangroves usually comprise groups of 3–5 people, and crab harvesters in smaller groups of two or sometimes alone. Some fishers enter the forest for fish on a daily basis, while some spend weeks. Those who enter the forest for a week or two take the necessary food and other logistics with them for the whole trip. Given the lack of savings and fair credit support, they are often bound to middlemen (locally known as Dadondar) for financial assistance to purchase necessary logistics, such as boats, or to rent fishing gear. As shown in Fig. 1c, more than 35% of the fishers surveyed in India and around 25% in Bangladesh have a contractual, informal agreement with a middleman group. As part of this contract, they either need to sell their products to the respective middlemen or share a major portion of the profit from the harvested products. This is one of the reasons why 54% of Indian and 42% of Bangladeshi fishers survey respondents reported being unable to obtain equitable benefits from their fishing trips (Fig. 1d). Furthermore, several key informants reported that the Indian government has stopped renewing the Boat Licence Certificates (BLCs) for the last couple of decades, which are the entry requirements for fishers to enter the forest and harvest fish. As a result, fishers borrow the BLC from those who have it or inherited it from their previous generation. Currently, almost all the fishers surveyed in India do not have the BLC and borrow it for $800–1000 USD/year. On the Bangladesh side, fishers have an ongoing forest entry pass issuance system, and according to more than 55% of the fishers surveyed, they can get a fair share of the benefits from their harvest if they are allowed to fish inside the forest.

For fishing in the forest, small-scale fishers use a variety of nets, traps, and hooks/sips. In India, almost 90% survey respondents reported that they rely on nets (e.g., drag net/push net, seine net, push net, cast net, and lift net) and traps (trap for crab: dhon dhori, atol), for their regular fishing activities (Table 2). In Bangladesh, almost all survey respondents use a variety of nets, similar to those in India. They also use hooks/sips for crab harvest. A majority of the fishers responded that they do not have fishing boats and borrow them from middlemen; consequently, they sell their catch to middlemen. According to several key informants, this practice is more common in Bangladesh than in India. About 94% of respondents in India said the main buyers of fish in markets are local traders, who are also important market actors in Bangladesh. There is another group, locally known as Faria or commission agent, who collects fish and crabs from the villages and sells them in the market, and they are more active in Bangladesh than in India. As key informant fishers reported, local markets are mainly controlled by middlemen groups and local traders. A significant factor in the income vulnerability of small-scale fishers is the substantial decline in fish stocks in the forest, as perceived by 80% of the surveyed fishers.

Common vulnerabilities

Natural disasters are one of the key vulnerabilities that small-scale fishing communities face on both sides of the forest, with 90% of survey respondents from both sides reporting frequent cyclones, floods and salinity intrusion (Table 3). According to 47% of respondents, salinity intrusion is lower in Bangladesh, mainly because their households are not located within the forest. In India, many fishing communities live in and around the islands of the forest. As a result, 42% of respondents in India reported that fishing communities suffer from significant river erosion. Around 40% of respondents in India and 30% of respondents in Bangladesh agree that sea level rise and waterlogging are gradually increasing. In Bangladesh, more than 50% of respondents agree that rainfall has become more erratic.

Common natural hazards in the Sundarbans mangrove forest

Small-scale fishers operating in the Sundarbans face multiple intersecting threats during fishing operations. An overwhelming 90% of survey respondents identified storms and confrontations with law enforcement as pervasive challenges (Fig. 2). These encounters often involve conflicts over fish they harvest, permits, equipment, and territorial access, frequently exacerbated by corrupt practices, as added by several key informants interviewed. Additionally, fishers must contend with criminal elements (locally termed dakhat) and dangerous wildlife, including tigers, wild boars, snakes, and crocodiles. The human-tiger conflict emerges as particularly severe, with nearly all survey respondents in India reporting an increase in attacks. Many key informant fishers interviewed in India attribute this to government-led tiger reintroduction programs, while government officials interviewed added that the growing tiger population reflects successful conservation measures. Although survey respondents in Bangladesh report fewer attacks (68%) than in India, the threat remains significant. The survey findings suggest that the spatial conflicts among fishers are intensifying as conservation zones expand while fisher populations grow, creating competition for dwindling fishing grounds. Many fishers who participated in surveys, interviews, and focus group discussions also reported that border-related incidents affect fishers in both Bangladesh and India, with transboundary movement primarily driven by seasonal resource availability, honey collection for Bangladeshis and fishing during bans for Indians.

Transboundary governing system

A fundamental issue is the lack of joint transboundary governance mechanisms, as both Bangladesh and India maintain strictly national approaches to governance, despite the shared ecosystem. The governance of transboundary Sundarbans mangroves remains highly hierarchical, with India’s system being entirely top-down (as reported by all survey respondents) and Bangladesh’s predominantly so (71% of respondents). While Bangladesh has attempted to introduce more inclusive co-governance through its Co-Management Committees (CMCs), they have not functioned, as 78% of fishers’ respondents reported being unaware of these initiatives. Only 22% of respondents have recognized some hybrid governance elements (a mix of hierarchical and co-governance), suggesting that these efforts exist more on paper than in practice. This situation reveals a profound disconnect between policy intentions and ground realities, where traditional top-down approaches continue to exclude fishing communities from decision-making processes, despite their critical knowledge and stake in sustainable resource management.

This study finds that there have been some changes in formal governance approaches, such as co-management in Bangladesh and joint forest management in India. In Bangladesh, co-management is formalized under the Wildlife Act (2012) and Protected Area (PA) Management Rules (2017), establishing a governance structure from Village Conservation Forums (VCFs) to the Co-management Executive Committee (CMEC) and the Co-Management General Committee (CMGC). The purpose of the Act is to conserve and safeguard the biodiversity, forests and wildlife in Bangladesh by enacting new legislation. Under this Act, the PA Management Rules 2017 formalize and strengthen the co-management involving local communities. VCFs are formed by local communities at the village level, and each VCF elects one male and one female representative to serve on the People’s Forum (PF). The PF representatives, along with other relevant actors, including forest, agriculture, fisheries, civil society, law enforcement authorities, and NGOs, form a CMEC and a CMGC or council. This system mandates the inclusion of elected fisher representatives (via a PF) and other stakeholders in a deliberative body responsible for governance and implementation. In India, Joint Forest Management (JFM) are formed under the National Forest Policy 1988. The main purpose of this policy is to achieve ecological balance by maintaining one-third forest cover through conservation, community involvement, and sustainable use. The JFM creates Joint Forest Management Committees (JFMCs) at the village level, which are further divided into Forest Protection Committees (FPCs) and Eco-Development Committees (EDCs). These committees consult with elected community members, forest officials, and NGO representatives and grant rights to use and protect resources. In both countries, the formation of these committees is legally mandated, with specific provisions for community representation alongside state and non-state actors.

Despite the CMC in Bangladesh, more than 80% of survey respondents reported having no opportunity to participate in the decision-making process (Fig. 3a). As part of CMC activities, fishers sometimes participate in monthly meetings organized by the forest department to raise awareness among local communities. More than 90% survey respondents reported not being consulted on the formation or implementation of governing actions in both India and Bangladesh (Fig. 3b). However, fishing communities are connected to some formal and informal organizations. In Bangladesh, the majority of respondents reported their connection to NGOs for various forms of support, such as loans, relief, and livestock farming, whereas in India, this is comparatively less (Fig. 3c). The key informants interviewed and survey respondents reported that women in Indian fishing communities form Self-Help Groups (SHGs) to obtain loans at the lowest interest rates from government agencies, a finding also confirmed during the focus group discussions. According to the respondents, there are no significant governance initiatives to address the vulnerabilities of small-scale fisheries in the transboundary forest. In each jurisdiction, there are some initiatives, but fishers have yet to benefit from them (Fig. 3d). A couple of prominent initiatives in Bangladesh that respondents reported include eliminating piracy in the forests and initiating co-management involving local communities. On the other hand, key informants and survey respondents from India stated that many parts of the forest are fenced by the West Bengal Government to protect communities from tiger attacks and to prevent illegal access to the forest for resource harvesting.

Transboundary governing interaction

The main channels of interaction between the government and small-scale fishers are policy implementation, informing decisions or policies, and non-compliance with rules and regulations, as reported by the fishers surveyed (Fig. 4a). Non-compliance is a powerful form of feedback through which fishers send a strong message to governing bodies, signaling that the formal rules are perceived as illegitimate or unjust. Some key informants interviewed reported that fishers’ true participation in forest and fisheries-related decision-making, consultations on their choices and preferences, and mutual collaboration and cooperation with the government were significantly limited in both countries. One key outcome of the focus group discussion is that the current system responds with punitive measures, including fines and imprisonment, rather than addressing the root causes of conflict, such as inadequate consultation, unrealistic restrictions, or a lack of alternative livelihoods.

The data presented in Fig. 4b reveal significant dissatisfaction among respondents in both countries, with over 70% survey respondents expressing discontent with current governance approaches. The respondents further added that the key grievances include harassment by forest and law enforcement authorities and excessive restrictions on accessing forest resources. In Bangladesh, 33% of respondents directly accused forest guards of corruption, a claim acknowledged by some officials interviewed as partially valid. Furthermore, the exclusion of communities from decision-making processes is particularly alarming in both countries, as many survey and interview participants agreed.

Discussion

The study aimed to analyze the vulnerabilities of small-scale fisheries in the transboundary Sundarbans through the lens of interactive governance and vulnerability to viability approach. The findings reveal that the vulnerabilities do not occur in isolation, but are interconnected outcomes of profound governability challenges. Here, the study’s emerging themes are discussed, which are arguably directly or indirectly causing these failures.

The findings suggest that small-scale fishing communities in the transboundary Sundarbans are hardly able to benefit from fisheries resources, and one of the main reasons is the middleman-bound credit system. While more intense in Bangladesh, these credit-bound relationships can create cross-border tensions through indebted fishers, who are compelled to maximize catch to repay their debts and are more likely to risk illegal border crossings for better fishing grounds, exacerbating conflicts. As evident in the cases of Vietnam, Kenya, Zanzibar and Mexico, fishers are exploited by middlemen who capitalize on their social position and financial crisis, leading to the overexploitation of fisheries resources and hindering the economic viability of fishers55–57. This creates a classic patron-client relationship based on debt, resulting in unfair market prices that undermine their rights and ability to benefit from their access to the forest58. This is not only a market failure, but a governance failure in the interactive sphere46,59. The formal governing system fails to provide fair credit support to fishers or regulate the informal market, leaving room for the empowerment of non-fishing people whose interests are misaligned with fishers’ economic viability and forest sustainability56,57.

These patron-client relationships contravene CPR design principles, specifically those of “congruence between appropriation and provision rules and local conditions” and “collective-choice arrangements”3,57,60. In the Sundarbans, small-scale fishers, as users, are indirectly subjected to debt-imposed harvesting rules that are incongruent with sustainability and established without their meaningful participation. This undermines the fishers’ self-governance capacity and distorts their incentives, leading to participation in a ‘race to fish’ to repay the debts3,45,60,61. This creates a governability failure, in which the de facto rules of informal economic spheres override formal governance goals (e.g., forest sustainability), perpetuating a reinforcing cycle. In this study context, economic vulnerability (i.e., debt) directly drives ecological vulnerability (overexploitation), which in turn, deepens social vulnerability. This systemic failure also poses a barrier to progress towards Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 14 target ‘b’, which aims to provide access to marine resources and markets for small-scale fisheries. Especially on the Bangladesh side of the forest, resource access is hindered by debt, and the market is exploitative, undermining the target ‘b.’ Therefore, this dysfunction acts as a critical barrier to a transition towards viability for small-scale fishers, as it inherently severs the alignment between fishers’ well-being and sustainability of the resources they depend on62–64. A similar study along the Brazil-Bolivia border shows that a fragile governance structure and weak governing institutions within and between countries increase the vulnerability of the fishery system to pressing threats, for instance, climate change19.

Furthermore, the transboundary nature of the Sundarbans mangroves creates a governability challenge rooted in a scalar and jurisdictional mismatch. In a transboundary CPR setup, fishers cross borders for many reasons (e.g., fish availability)16,19; however, the findings show that governing systems remain non-integrated and comprise two separate regimes. Similar issues have been reported in the Palk Bay between Sri Lanka and India2, in the Brazil-Bolivia transboundary region19, and in Lake Kariba between Zambia and Zimbabwe16. This misfit criminalizes the traditional livelihood strategy of fishers, leading to conflicts between fishers and governing institutions and creating social vulnerability through legal instability16,19,20. The rules and responsibilities of governing institutions sometimes overlap, often in conflict with each other, or result in unexpected outcomes for other stakeholder groups65. For example, in the absence of BLC issuance for Indian fishers, they rely on informal rental of BLC to continue fishing, exemplifying the incongruence between formal rules and the system’s dynamics, forcing informal adaptations that deepen economic vulnerability and insecurity. Conversely, the forest pass issuance in Bangladesh is ongoing.

The findings suggest that, rather than developing common policies, regulations, and action plans to sustain forest and fishing communities, the two countries continue to create boundaries within the forest as core, buffer, protected, or restricted areas, also reported by refs. 66,67. A parallel dynamic is evident in the Kenya-Tanzania Transboundary Conservation Area, where mismatched governance creates a critical split: unsustainable, illegal extraction on one side undermines binational conservation goals, highlighting how the lack of joint governance threatens ecological integrity and entrenches user inequity8. Additionally, without sufficient consultation with fishers, government bodies simply impose their decisions (e.g., new restrictions), leading to confusion, non-compliance, and further deepening the viability gap13,63, also confirmed by this study. These institutional failures, including fragmentation, incongruence, and exclusion, intensify the interconnected social, economic, and governance-related vulnerabilities that trap the system in a downward trajectory63,68. This suggests that the pathway to viability is systematically hindered by the structure of transboundary governance, which prioritizes jurisdictional control over the integrated social-ecological functionality required for sustainable small-scale fisheries.

While the transboundary governance structure is fragmented, the countries have attempted to address the governability gaps within their jurisdictions through CMCs in Bangladesh and JFMCs in India. This participatory approach was designed to decentralize decision-making, incorporate local knowledge, and improve the congruence between rules and local dynamics39,41. However, their designs contrast with a more integrated transboundary governance approach in the case of the Mono Transboundary Biosphere Reserve in West Africa, where Togo and Benin established a single, joint governance framework that led to mangrove conservation success69. This suggests that the absence of a unified governance approach between Bangladesh and India limits their ability to address transboundary issues in the Sundarbans. Additionally, the effectiveness of CMCs and JFMCs in facilitating the transition toward viability is constrained by their limited functionality with persistent top-down practices in both countries39,70. Another limitation of these co-management approaches is that they remain within their respective jurisdictions, lacking formal mechanisms for the Bangladesh CMC to collaborate with the Indian JFMC to manage shared forest resources. Although the positive outcomes under co-management are not guaranteed, an asymmetry in mangrove risk levels between Togo and Benin69,71 suggests that continuous, science-based monitoring, integrated with community participation, is crucial to facilitate a transition toward viability.

The governing systems have not only failed to coordinate transboundary governance, but also, within the country’s jurisdiction, to provide basic protections or viable economic alternatives, trapping fishers in a cycle of danger and deprivation. To a large extent, the existing governing bodies failed to provide safety and security for fishers due to financial, manpower and political limitations31,72. Fishers face year-round natural disasters, causing loss of lives and property. Although pirates in Bangladesh are not a big issue lately, fishers often die or get hurt by tiger, crocodile, or snake attacks. On the Indian side, pirates and wild animal attacks are both common vulnerabilities for small-scale fishers. In addition to pirates and wild animals, women are sometimes not safe on both sides of the forest from their fellow fishermen34. Despite their significant contribution, women face barriers in accessing both resources and markets34,73. Women are still economically dependent on their male counterparts, suggesting a lack of financial autonomy73,74. The absence of women’s meaningful participation in the mangrove governance highlights the lack of quality of governing interactions and undermines the CPR principle of inclusive collective choice3,75. Addressing these interconnected gendered failures, including social safety, economic agency and governance participation, is crucial to support the transition toward viability34,63,74.

Even though fishers know the risks of being a Sundarbans fisher, they still desire to go fishing for income, food and nutritional security11,31. The failure of the governing system in providing basic social safety services highlights the systematic neglect of the social foundation for a viable transition. The findings of this study suggest that the ecological sustainability of mangroves is a priority for governing bodies, as evidenced by historical and ongoing initiatives, such as the declaration of protected areas. The view of the forest as a social-ecological system, where human well-being is integral to sustainability, is often overlooked or narrowly focused70,76. In contrast, the Brazil-Bolivia transboundary fishery case shows that the inherent sustainability of small-scale fisheries, along with the relationships among local stakeholders, has proven resilient in maintaining transboundary fishery stocks, despite fragile governance19. In the Sundarbans, fishers often circumvent regulatory measures through various means, such as using illegal fishing gear, poaching in neighboring countries during fishing bans, and crossing borders to collect honey and harvest fish76. This not only threatens the present sustainability of the forest and viability of fishing communities, but also drives the intergenerational crisis. Parents envision a different profession for their kids, given the uncertainties and vulnerabilities they face77, stripping the system of the youth and the innovation they need for a viable fishing sector.

Overall, the study confirms that vulnerabilities are deeply connected to the governability issues, where the formal governance system misaligns with the transboundary social-ecological system to be governed. For example, the lack of a transboundary governance plan and approach results in small-scale fishers being victimized, especially due to governance and policy mismatches (e.g., fishing bans and access mechanisms to the forest) between countries. This misalignment creates barriers to the viability of small-scale fishers by fragmenting collaborative processes across borders, suppressing social learning through non-participatory regulations, and fostering perverse institutional innovation that exploits fishers through a middleman-bound credit system. Also, the desired changes for viability transition are hindered by the absence of crucial processes, such as collaboration, learning, and innovation. To support this transition, the governance system needs to make context-specific efforts to enable these processes. This can be achieved by creating and prioritizing transboundary collaboration and cooperation, institutionalizing participatory feedback loops for co-learning, and encouraging and supporting community-led innovation and practices. Therefore, the vulnerability to viability transition for small-scale fisheries in the transboundary context hinges on a fundamental shift in governability. This requires moving beyond the current fragmented and narrow ecological focus to co-design institutions that address exploitative economic traps, integrate social well-being with ecological goals, and enable communities to exercise collective-choice rights to ensure viability and just sustainability.

This study suggests that the interconnected vulnerabilities faced by small-scale fisheries and the governability issues are rooted in the transboundary CPR dilemma. Building on the findings, the study suggests that countries make policy priorities in governing the transboundary Sundarbans mangroves. First, both countries should formalize a joint co-governance body to govern the Sundarbans as a single social-ecological system. One immediate action is to co-design and implement a common forest access system for resource users on both sides to maintain steady access to resources. In this process, it is important to consider the voices of small-scale fishers and what they consider ideal. Second, both the decision-making and enforcement systems should be decentralized, institutionalizing participatory feedback mechanisms that ensure true participation and forming a joint review council with greater representation of fishers and other relevant stakeholder groups. This can facilitate two-way interactions between the community and governing authorities. Critically, women’s participation in decision-making and their financial autonomy should not remain confined to official policy documents; practical implementation is crucial to the collective progress of fishing communities toward viability and forest sustainability. Third, the governance system should establish a process to assess governability, identify CPR dilemmas, and determine whether governing actions and policies are effective. This process can help address the overlapping rules of different governing institutions and help facilitate the transition toward viability. It can also help governing authorities better understand the diversity, complexity, and interconnectedness of social and ecological issues, enabling them to address them effectively. Finally, using the conceptual framework of this study, future research can provide a systematic, more granular diagnosis of governability. For instance, research could involve systematically assessing specific dimensions of governability, such as capacity (examining the structure, modes and orders) and quality (evaluating the goodness of fit, responsiveness, and performance) of the transboundary Sundarbans governing system. Such a detailed assessment can help identify the challenges and opportunities to facilitate transitions from vulnerability to viability.

In the medium to short term, a critical next step of this study is the effective dissemination of these findings to the stakeholders involved. The findings of this research will be communicated to the fishing communities, local co-management committees, and forest department officials in both Bangladesh and India through community workshops during the winter school organized by V2V Global Partnership. The findings will also be shared through conference presentations, such as the Annual Conference on Sustainable Development in Dhaka, Bangladesh and the Indian Fisheries and Aquaculture Forum in India.

Methods

Conceptual framework

This study was guided by the conceptual framework presented in Fig. 5 below. Through a review of relevant literature [e.g., 54, 75, 78–80, this study developed the framework by integrating the emerging vulnerability to viability (V2V) perspective81,82 with interactive governance theory54 and the governability lens78, to analyze the role of the governing system in facilitating the V2V transition for small-scale fisheries (SSF). Governability refers to the “overall quality of governance” of a societal system79. This framework is grounded on the understanding that small-scale fisheries are complex social-ecological systems. The V2V analysis logically starts by diagnosing the system’s current state of vulnerability, which helps identify the barriers and entry points for intervention. The understanding of vulnerability here is the susceptibility of small-scale fisheries to harm from socio-economic, institutional and environmental stresses (e.g., cyclones), reflecting the negative impacts of changes on fishers’ livelihoods and the ecosystem itself68,83,84. The desired endpoint of the transition is viability. Building on the V2V literature62,81,82,85 and similar to ref. 63, this study defines viability for the analysis as the state in which small-scale fisheries develop the capacity to navigate stresses and disturbances, adapt and transform, and build resilience to unexpected circumstances. This definition shifts the focus from static outcomes, such as sustainability and resilience, to the dynamic process of building the multidimensional capacities necessary for a robust transition, addressing the understudied interplay between vulnerability and viability81,82. This study contributes to the emerging V2V approach by presenting an integrated framework (Fig. 5) to assess the governability barriers to transitioning toward viability in the understudied transboundary Sundarbans mangroves.

A conceptual framework to assess the characteristics of transboundary mangrove ecosystems, the governing system and the governing interaction.

Transitioning from vulnerability to viability is not a linear or simple process, but rather a complex transition involving non-linear, thorough, and structural changes in systems86,87. Such sustainability transitions require fundamental shifts in social, economic, technological and institutional regimes88,89. The analysis of transitions focuses on processes, such as collaboration, learning, cooperation, and institutional innovation, that can either facilitate or hinder the shift90. This is where the analytical aspects of interactive governance theory and governability are helpful. The analysis can help determine where and how the transitions can be supported through the unpacking of the transboundary mangrove ecosystems into three interconnected elements: the system-to-be-governed, the governing system, and the interactions between the two.

As presented in Fig. 5, the light yellowish box is the system-to-be-governed (SG), representing the diversity, complexity, dynamics and scales characteristics of the transboundary mangrove ecosystems. These characteristics are both sources of vulnerability (e.g., scalar mismatch between fish mobility and political borders) and opportunities for viability (e.g., strong social networks for resilience). The higher the diversity, complexity, dynamics and scale issues of transboundary mangrove ecosystems, the higher the vulnerabilities for small-scale fisheries likely to be79,91. The light gray box represents the governing system (GS), which is a multi-level system of institutions, actors, and instruments. This study focuses on those that directly or indirectly influence the use and governance of the transboundary Sundarbans mangrove ecosystem. This includes fisheries and forest departments, as well as cross-sectoral policies (e.g., in health), whose actions or failures (e.g., fishing bans) directly impact the vulnerability or viability of small-scale fisheries. This study recognizes that the GS is not the sole driver of vulnerability to viability transition, but a critical catalyst. The three small bubbles inside the grayish box represent the common governing approaches, i.e., self-, co-, or hierarchical, which correspond to different institutional arrangements with varying capacities to foster learning, innovation, and adaptation – the key processes for a transition90. This study defines self-governance as a governing arrangement in which the state or central government intervenes minimally or not at all, as in a fishing community organization within a village. In co-governance, responsibilities are shared between the governed and the governors. Hierarchical governance is a system in which the community (or those governed) has minimal to no input into decision-making. The governing interactions (GI) between the SG and GS (the long greenish rectangle at the bottom) can take the form of compliance or non-compliance with policies, cooperation, collaboration, participation, learning, and beyond. The analysis of GI can help determine the flow of information, the legitimacy of rules and regulations, and the system’s capacity to adapt75. Thus, through these interactions, the GS can either exacerbate vulnerabilities or facilitate the social, economic, and institutional changes needed for viability.

While transitions can occur in SG and through GI, the conceptual framework used in this study primarily draws on GS concepts. Through the integration of vulnerability to viability approach with interactive governance, this conceptual framework does not presume a seamless or linear transition solely driven by GS. Rather, it provides a tool to systematically diagnose the vulnerabilities of a transboundary CPR (SG), identify the governance impediments in the GS and interactions failures in the GI that hinder the transition, and find out the potential pathways for processes, such as social, institutional and economic, necessary to shift the system toward more viable trajectory. The central concept of the analysis is governability. For example, lower governability of transboundary mangrove fisheries occurs when there is a poor fit between SG and GS, leaving the system in a vulnerable state78,79. Conversely, higher governability, achieved by improving GS’s responsiveness and the quality of GI, is a fundamental process for enabling a transition toward viability79,80.

Data collection and analysis

This study was carried out in several locations in India and Bangladesh from September 2023 to April 2024 (Fig. 6). Data were collected from eight villages in Kumirmari and Chhota Molla Khali (Gram Panchayat: third tier of rural local self-government) in Gosaba Block (an intermediate local administrative unit overseeing multiple Gram Panchayats) in the South 24 Parganas district of West Bengal, India. Gosaba block is one of the 19 administrative blocks that comprise the Sundarbans mangrove forest in India92 and directly shares a border with the forest. In Bangladesh, the research was conducted in 10 villages across the Gabura, Burigoalini, Munshiganj, and Kaikhali union parishads (UPs) (the lowest level of elected local government) in Shyamnagar upazila (third tier of local administrative unit) under the Satkhira district of Khulna division. These UPs cover most of the areas (72%) next to the Sundarbans and 31% of the population in Shyamnagar93. The study covered these areas by considering three factors: the adjacent transboundary mangrove forest shared with India, the same language, and the familiarity of the first author with the study areas. The small-scale fishers from both sides use Bengali as their first language. The study employed a comparative design, with in-depth fieldwork in Bangladesh over six months as the primary study site, supplemented by two months of fieldwork in the corresponding Indian side to develop a comparative view of vulnerabilities across the transboundary Sundarbans.

The map shows the geographical position of the Sundarbans mangrove forest in the Bay of Bengal coast of Bangladesh and India, and the study areas.

This study employed a mixed-methods approach to ensure a comprehensive and triangulated understanding of the transboundary fisheries system. This research used a purposive sampling approach to select the research participants. The first author has 4 years of prior research experience on small-scale fisheries in the Sundarbans of Bangladesh, during which they established contacts with fishing communities. For India, extensive informal consultation was conducted with a couple of scholars from the Vulnerability to Viability (V2V) Global Partnership and Too Big To Ignore (TBTI) research network to choose the study areas and participants. Data collection comprised household surveys, key informant interviews (KIIs), and focus group discussions (FGDs).

Household surveys were conducted across selected villages in India and Bangladesh to capture a diverse and informed perspective of the community. The study areas were purposively selected to ensure coverage of key transboundary dynamics, including proximity to the forest border, dependence on major forest resources, and inclusion within different formal governance zones. As presented in Table 4, a total of 52 household surveys (N = 52) were conducted across 8 villages in India, and 99 household surveys (N = 99) were conducted across 10 villages in Bangladesh. While not statistically representative of the total fisher population across the entire Sundarbans, this sample provides a robust basis for qualitative analysis. In India, the study areas cover one of the eight Gram Panchayats in the Gosaba block, where Kumirmari and Chhota Molla Khali represent 7% and 4% of the block’s total population, respectively92. According to93, the total population of Shayamnagar Upazilla in Bangladesh is 365,953, and the percentages of the population each study UP represents are 9% in Burigoalini, 8.5% in Munshiganj, 10% in Gabura, and 9% in Kaikhali. These UPs directly share a border with the Sundarbans mangroves. These population figures provide a contextual scale for the study sites but do not equate to the sampling fraction for the specific population of interest, i.e., small-scale fishing households. The participants for the household survey were selected based on their fishing profession and legal age (i.e., not less than 18 years old). The selection of households for the survey represented the key livelihoods (e.g., fishing, honey collection, fish trading and processing). The household survey questions mostly comprised closed-ended questions regarding social, ecological, and governance information of the transboundary fisheries system. There were some open-ended questions as well to support community responses, for example, whether people were happy with the governing system, and, if not, a follow-up question was asked to share reasons for not being happy. The surveys were mainly helpful in identifying the vulnerabilities of small-scale fisheries. The survey questions were designed in a way that did not take more than 60 minutes for the participants.

The study areas and the methods used to collect data

The study conducted KIIs and FGDs to complement the surveys and provide expert and contextual insights. A total of 14 KIIs in India and 18 KIIs in Bangladesh were performed. KIIs were conducted with fishing community leaders and other stakeholders, including traders, cooperative societies, government bodies, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and academia. The informants were selected purposively to represent the critical stakeholders with knowledge of transboundary fisheries and cross-border issues. The KII questions covered general information about transboundary fisheries, the major sources of vulnerability for small-scale fisheries, the governing systems, and suggestions for making small-scale fisheries viable. The KIIs were helpful in identifying the attributes crucial for vulnerability to viability transitions. Each KII last for around an hour.

Additionally, one FGD was conducted on each side of the Sundarbans mangrove forest, inviting and ensuring a mix of participants by age, primary occupation, and village affiliation. The FGDs were conducted with representatives from fishers, NGOs, cooperative societies, women’s groups and government bodies. A few specific discussion questions related to vulnerability to viability transitions in the transboundary fisheries system were listed and discussed during the focus group. These questions and the discussions were designed to gather data on community norms, collective perspectives, and nuanced debates that are difficult to capture through individual interviews. The FGD lasted 2 hours each.

The data collected using each method were compiled in a Microsoft Excel sheet. The household survey data were converted to numbers using ‘yes=1’ and ‘no=0’ against each response. For most questions, participants provided/chose multiple answers for each response. All the responses were analyzed in the Excel sheet and converted to percentages. The interview responses were analyzed using the content analysis method. The responses to each question were coded using a few words, and the codes were then sub-categorized into broader terms. The sub-categories were further grouped based on their similarities and dissimilarities. The categories are broadly related to three themes, i.e., transboundary social and natural systems, governance systems and governing interactions. The data were analyzed thematically based on the themes of the conceptual framework introduced above. The information gathered from FGDs helped validate and cross-check the survey and interview data.

The objectives, content and affiliations of the research were explained to the research participants, and consent was taken prior to participation.
