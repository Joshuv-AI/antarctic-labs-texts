Characteristics and risk factors for thrombocytopenia in malignancy patients with pulmonary embolism: a case control study
Authors: Hongyuan Kong, Kexin Dong, Yuan Wang, Yan Bian, Lin Wang, Limei Qian, Zhaoxing Cui, Jin Yang, Rui Fu, Zhenkun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThrombocytopenia represents a frequent yet severe complication among malignancy patients with pulmonary embolism (PE). Contrary to the assumption that low platelet counts might inhibit thrombosis, thrombocytopenia in malignancy patients poses significant challenges for anticoagulation therapy. This study aimed to investigate the clinical characteristics and identify risk factors associated with thrombocytopenia in malignancy patients complicated by PE. The findings may provide a foundation for developing targeted interventions to mitigate thrombocytopenia and guide biomarker and clinical characteristics-based management strategies.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study analyzing clinical data from malignancy patients diagnosed with PE who were admitted to the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University between January 2014 and June 2024. The cohort was stratified into two groups: 66 patients with thrombocytopenia and 429 patients with normal platelet counts. Clinical characteristics and potential risk factors were systematically collected and analyzed.ResultsMany clinical indicators were found to have statistically significant differences between the low platelet group and the normal platelet group (P < 0.05). Multivariable logistic regression analysis further identified coronary heart disease, right ventricular dysfunction, anticoagulant therapy, anti-tumor drug therapy, dyspnea, hemoptysis, fever(>37.3℃), neutrophil (NEUT), standard deviation in red cell distribution width (RDWSD), platelet distribution width (PDW), procalcitonin (PCT), international normalized ratio (INR), fibrinogen (FIB) were significantly associated with a 6.703, 3.335, 0.311, 4.176, 4.244, 4.533, 3.527, 0.288, 3.995, 2.616, 5.129, 2.641, 0.212 times higher risk of thrombocytopenia in malignant patients with PE respectively.ConclusionMultivariate analysis identified several independent risk factors for thrombocytopenia in malignant patients with PE, including coronary heart disease, right ventricular dysfunction, anti-tumor drug therapy, dyspnea, hemoptysis, fever (> 37.3 °C), RDW-SD, PDW, PCT and INR. Conversely, anticoagulant therapy, NEUT, and FIB emerged as independent protective factors against thrombocytopenia in this patient population.Keywords: Thrombocytopenia, Malignancy, Pulmonary embolism (PE), Risk factors

Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE) and deep vein thrombosis (DVT) are collectively recognized as venous thromboembolism (VTE), both of which are highly prevalent in patients with active malignancy [1, 2]. Conversely, malignancy increases the risk of VTE by ninefold [3]. Consequently, VTE has become a major global health burden among malignancy patients. Notably, PE can also occur when a thrombus from a DVT dislodges and migrates to the pulmonary artery. Furthermore, VTE is the second leading cause of death in malignancy patients [4]. More critically, diagnosing and treating PE in patients with concurrent malignancy is particularly challenging because PE symptoms are often nonspecific and may be masked by those of the underlying cancer. Despite its clinical significance, malignancy-associated PE has not received sufficient attention.

Thrombocytopenia is a common complication of PE in malignancy and is closely related to delayed PE treatment [5–7]. Heparin is one of the main treatments for PE but can induce thrombocytopenia, which may contribute to PE recurrence [8–11]. In fact, thrombocytopenia is highly prevalent in patients with malignant diseases and thrombosis, occurring in 22% of patients with solid tumors and 47% of those with hematologic malignancies [12]. One study reported that 43% of critically ill patients admitted to the ICU also had thrombocytopenia, and these patients exhibited poorer prognosis and higher mortality [13]. Thrombocytopenia lasting over 30 days is associated with a 4-fold increased risk of recurrent PE and DVT [8, 14]. Additionally, thrombocytopenia can be caused by various conventional treatments for malignant tumors, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and interventional therapy [6, 7]. However, hospitalized patients with malignant tumors are less likely to receive VTE prophylaxis than those without malignancy, as thrombocytopenia significantly increases bleeding risk [8, 9]. Among patients with hematologic malignancies who did not receive anticoagulant therapy, the 28-day mortality rate was 15% [10]. Consequently, treating malignancy patients with PE and concurrent thrombocytopenia is challenging, and optimal management remains controversial [11].

The optimal anticoagulant treatment for malignancy-associated PE with concurrent thrombocytopenia remains uncertain, and additional clinical evidence is urgently needed. This study aimed to characterize thrombocytopenia in malignancy patients with PE and identify its risk factors, thereby providing further clinical insights for the management of this high-risk population.

Methods

Study design and patient population

We conducted a retrospective analysis using data extracted from the Hospital Information System (HIS) electronic medical records at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University between January 2014 and June 2024. The initial search identified 12,165 inpatients with clinically or pathologically confirmed PE diagnoses. After applying exclusion criteria, we sequentially removed:

3,039 cases with duplicate same-day PE diagnostic codes,

3,039 cases with duplicate same-day PE diagnostic codes,

7,757 cases without malignancy diagnoses, and.

7,757 cases without malignancy diagnoses, and.

874 cases (780 with incomplete data parameters and 94 lacking CTPA reports).

874 cases (780 with incomplete data parameters and 94 lacking CTPA reports).

The final cohort comprised 495 malignancy patients with PE eligible for data analysis. (Fig. 1).

Screening step for the patients with both malignant tumor and PE

PE cases confirmed by 256-slice CTPA were finally divided into two groups: thrombocytopenia group (n = 66; aged 64.64 ± 13.39 years, platelet count < 100 × 103/µL) and platelet normal group (n = 429; aged 65.07 ± 12.04 years, platelet count > 100 × 103/µL) (Table 1). The diagnosis of malignant tumors is established through pathological or clinical confirmation. The analyzed patient parameters comprised age, sex, histopathological classification of malignancies, medical history, comorbidities, electrocardiogram (ECG) and echocardiography (UCG) results, PE-associated symptoms, vital signs, PE risk stratification, laboratory results, therapeutic interventions and in-hospital adverse events.

Clinical characteristics of malignancy patients with PE included in the study

Risk level of initial PE

High-risk pulmonary embolism (PE) was defined by hemodynamic instability requiring vasoactive agents or a systolic blood pressure (SBP) drop of > 40 mmHg from baseline or an absolute SBP < 90 mmHg. Intermediate-risk PE was classified into two subgroups: Intermediate-high-risk: Patients with both elevated cardiac troponin levels and right ventricular (RV) dysfunction but without hemodynamic instability. Intermediate-low-risk: Patients with either elevated troponin or RV dysfunction but not both.

Clinical symptoms and signs

The clinical manifestations of PE encompass dyspnea, cough, chest pain, and hemoptysis. Pertinent vital signs include body temperature (> 37.3 °C indicating fever), pulse rate (with tachycardia as a notable feature), respiratory rate, blood pressure, SpO₂ levels, and the presence of lower limb edema.

Peripheral blood tests

Peripheral blood samples were collected within 24 h of hospital admission for complete blood count, biochemical profiling, infection markers, and coagulation function analysis. Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis was performed prior to oxygen therapy using a GEM Premier 3000 analyzer (Instrumentation Laboratory, USA) for immediate testing.

Echocardiography and color doppler ultrasound for DVT

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using a GE Vivid E9 system (GE Healthcare, USA) to assess cardiac function in malignancy-associated pulmonary embolism (PE) patients. Participants were positioned in the left lateral decubitus position with the anterior chest fully exposed. Standard parasternal and apical views were obtained to evaluate atrioventricular structure, wall motion, valvular function, and chamber dimensions. The following parameters were recorded: Right ventricular end-diastolic diameter (RVDd), Right atrial diameter (RAD), Right ventricular outflow tract diameter (RVOT), Pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP).

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Review Board of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (Approval No.: PJ-2024-177).

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed or median (interquartile range, IQR) if non-normally distributed. Group comparisons were conducted using: Independent samples t-test (for normally distributed data) and Mann-Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data). Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test and reported as frequencies and percentages. To identify risk factors for thrombocytopenia, multivariate logistic regressionwas performed, yielding crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The predictive performance of key indices was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC) calculated. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while < 0.01 indicated high statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics of malignancy patients with PE

As shown in Table 1, no significant difference was observed between the two groups with respect to the gender, age, smoking history, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), hypertension, heart disease (not coronary heart disease), intracranial disease, surgery within 3 months, pericarditis or pericardial effusion, pleural effusion, as well as radiotherapy (P > 0.05). However, in comparison with the group with normal platelet level, the incidence of diabetes, coronary artery disease, bleeding within 3 months, shock, right ventricular dysfunction, anti-tumor drug therapy were higher in the thrombocytopenia group (22.7% vs. 12.1%,P = 0.019, 13.6% vs. 5.1%, P = 0.008; 30.3% vs. 16.1%, P = 0.005; 16.7% vs. 4.2%,P<0.001; 37.9% vs. 23.3%, P = 0.011; 31.8% vs. 13.5%, P<0.001, respectively), whereas the rate of anticoagulant therapy were lower (62.1% vs. 85.8%, P<0.001༉.

The clinical symptoms and signs were present in Table 2, No significant difference was observed between the two groups in terms of chest pain, body temperature, pulse, breaths, SPO2, tachycardia. However, patients in the thrombocytopenia group exhibited significantly higher rates of dyspnea (53.0% vs. 31.7%; P = 0.001), hemoptysis (28.8% vs. 10.7%; P < 0.001), lower extremity edema (27.3% vs. 14.7%; P = 0.010), and fever (31.8% vs. 12.1%; P < 0.001) compared to controls. Conversely, this group demonstrated lower prevalence of cough (25.8% vs. 49.7%; P < 0.001) and significantly reduced systolic blood pressure (119.3 ± 19.5 vs. 124.8 ± 19.2 mmHg; P = 0.032) and diastolic blood pressure (72.0 ± 12.6 vs. 76.5 ± 12.9 mmHg; P = 0.008).

The results demonstrated that there were no significant differences between the thrombocytopenia group and the platelet normal group in terms of extended hospital stay (exclusive of surgery), increased hospitalization costs (exclusive of surgery), automatic discharge during hospitalization, adverse bleeding events during hospitalization (surgery not ruled out), ICU admission rates, and rescue rate. Discussion

Malignancy-associated PE differs significantly in mechanism and treatment from PE in non-malignant diseases [15]. Abnormal tissue factors, proteins, and procoagulant factors produced by cancer cells interfere with the exogenous coagulation pathways, platelet activation, fibrin synthesis, coagulation cascade, and inhibition of fibrinolysis, collectively promoting a prothrombotic state and cancer-related thrombosis [16]. Thrombocytopenia often leads to the delay or even termination of pulmonary embolism treatment. Therefore, investigating the clinical characteristics and risk factors for thrombocytopenia in patients with malignancy and PE is of considerable clinical importance.

PE frequently induces dyspnea [17]. Respiratory impairment secondary to lung cancer, including reduced lung volume and pleural effusion, is also well-documented. However, dyspnea specifically associated with thrombocytopenia has not been extensively reported. This study observed a higher incidence of dyspnea in the thrombocytopenia group, multivariate analysis further identified thrombocytopenia as an independent risk factor for dyspnea (adjusted OR: 4.244, 95% CI [1.724–10.449], P = 0.002). The primary physiological role of platelets is hemostasis, and thrombocytopenia markedly elevates bleeding risk [18]. When bleeding involves the pulmonary parenchyma or tracheobronchial tree, hemoptysis may occur. In this cohort, hemoptysis was significantly associated with thrombocytopenia (adjusted OR: 4.533, 95% CI [1.496–13.736], P = 0.008). Frequently observed in thrombocytopenia, fever is typically attributed to viral/bacterial infections or immune-mediated mechanisms [19, 20]. In some diseases, thrombocytopenia is often acc-ompanied by fever [21]. However, data on fever incidence among malignancy-associated PE patients with concurrent thrombocytopenia remain scarce. We found fever (>37.3 °C) as an independent risk factor for thrombocytopenia (adjusted OR 3.527, 95% CI [1.380–9.015], P = 0.008). our findings provide novel clinical insights into this underreported association.

Malignant diseases are associated with hypercoagulability, placing cancer patients at high risk for both myocardial infarction and PE [22]. Our study revealed a significantly higher incidence of coronary heart disease (CHD) in the study group compared to the control group (13.6% vs. 5.1%, P = 0.008). Subsequent multivariate analysis demonstrated that CHD was most strongly associated with thrombocytopenia in malignant patients with PE (adjusted OR 6.703 [95% CI: 1.914–23.469], P = 0.003). To our knowledge, this is the first study to report that CHD is more prevalent in patients with malignancies complicated by thrombocytopenia, suggesting high predictive value. Clinical decision-making in cancer patients with concurrent thrombosis and thrombocytopenia remains challenging, as thrombocytopenia increases bleeding risk without conferring protection against thrombosis [12].

It is well established that the leading cause of death in PE patients is acute right ventricular (RV) dysfunction. Plasencia et al. reported that cancer-associated PE patients with RV enlargement (diameter >45 mm) experienced significantly more 15-day complications (58% vs. 40%, P = 0.001), establishing RV dysfunction as a poor prognostic indicator [23]. Multivariate analysis in our study identified RV dysfunction as a strong independent predictor of thrombocytopenia (adjusted OR 3.335, 95% [CI: 1.289–8.626], P = 0.013). The clinical consequences of acute RV dysfunction are particularly severe, including: marked reduction in right cardiac output, systemic hypoperfusion of vital organs, increased risk of sudden death, hemodynamic instability (hypotension), tissue hypoxia. Once RV dysfunction occurs, antithrombotic treatment is even more necessary. However, the reduction in antithrombotic treatment caused by thrombocytopenia further aggravates the poor prognosis.

Our study demonstrated a significantly lower rate of anticoagulant therapy administration in the thrombocytopenia group compared to controls (62.1% vs. 85.8%, P < 0.001). This clinical decision pattern likely reflects physician reluctance to initiate anticoagulation or invasive procedures in the setting of low platelet counts. After multi-factor calculation, our research found that thrombocytopenia has a huge impact on anticoagulant therapy (adjusted OR 0.311, [95% CI: 0.127–0.763], P = 0.011). These findings suggest that thrombocytopenic malignancy patients with PE often fail to receive optimal antithrombotic therapy [8, 10–13], potentially due to concerns about bleeding complications. This therapeutic challenge is well-documented in the literature. Chalayer et al. reported that only 64% of clinicians would adjust treatment strategies even when platelet counts fell below 50 × 10⁹/L [24]. The management of PE in thrombocytopenic cancer patients presents a particular clinical dilemma, as standard anticoagulant therapy requires careful platelet count monitoring and risk-benefit assessment [8, 10, 11, 13]. The clinical consequences of this therapeutic gap are significant. Thrombocytopenia in cancer-associated PE may contribute to: suboptimal long-term mortality outcomes [25], increased bleeding risk concurrent with thrombosis progression [10], frequent anticoagulation regimen modifications [10]. These findings underscore the critical need for individualized treatment strategies that carefully balance thrombotic and bleeding risks in this vulnerable patient population.

Targeted therapies for lung adenocarcinoma frequently induce thrombocytopenia as an adverse effect [26]. Similarly, immunotherapy has been associated with immune-mediated thrombocytopenia [27]. In our study, we found that anti-tumor treatment increased the risk of thrombocytopenia by 4.176-fold (adjusted OR 4.176, [95% CI: 1.726–10.102], P = 0.002). These findings further support the clinical evidence regarding the association between malignant tumor treatment and thrombocytopenia, particularly in patients with concurrent PE.

Our multifactorial analysis revealed that low neutrophil count (NEUT) and high red cell distribution width standard deviation (RDW-SD) and platelet distribution width (PDW) had predictive value for thrombocytopenia (adjusted OR 0.288, [95% CI: 0.128–0.648], P = 0.003, adjusted OR 3.995, [95% CI: 1.826–8.744], P = 0.001, adjusted OR 2.616, [95% CI: 1.199–5.708], P = 0.016, respectively) in malignancy patients with PE. Previous studies have reported associations between thrombocytopenia and poor clinical outcomes. Zhang et al. found that septic shock patients with thrombocytopenia had a higher 28-day mortality rate than those without thrombocytopenia, with white blood cell count identified as an independent risk factor for thrombocytopenia [28]. Yan X et al. found that decreased immune function was associated with thrombocytopenia [29]. Wang K et al. showed that immune cell counts are associated with prognosis [30]. Additionally, severe thrombocytopenia in cancer patients is frequently accompanied by neutropenia during treatment [9]. Notably, our study is among the first to demonstrate that low neutrophil count, and high RDW-SD and PDW are independent risk factors for thrombocytopenia in PE patients with malignant tumors. In the multifactorial analysis, PCT was a strong predictor of thrombocytopenia (adjusted OR 5.129, [95% CI: 2.214–11.879], P<0.001). Fatal fever is often accompanied by high PCT and thrombocytopenia syndrome [31]. Elevated serum PCT often indicates the progression of severe bacterial infections and sepsis, and its prognosis is closely related to thrombocytopenia [32]. Several infections are associated with thrombocytopenia [33]. Studies have proven that the higher the inflammatory indicators, the worse the prognosis of cancer patients [34, 35]. No similar reports were found in the PE of malignant tumor patients, our study provides a new basis for exploring thrombocytopenia in these patients.

Our results show high INR (adjusted OR 2.641, [95% CI: 1.174–5.944], P = 0.019) and low FIB (adjusted OR 0.212, 95% CI: 0.085–0.530, P = 0.001) were independent risk factors for thrombocytopenia. A study published by Kurisu et al. showed prolongation of INR could predict thrombocytopenia [36]. Deng et al. reported in the thrombocytopenia group, FIB levels were lower than the platelet normal group (3.4 vs. 4.5, P < 0.001) [37]. But evidences specific to cancer patients are limited. Our outcomes provide preliminary evidence of these relationships in malignant PE.

We found an increase of death rate in hospital and adverse bleeding events during hospitalization (surgery ruled out) rate but a decrease of surgery rate in the thrombocytopenia group (9.09% vs. 2.80%, P = 0.029; 18.18% vs. 4.20%, P = 0.000; 9.09% vs. 27.9%, P = 0.001, respectively). These findings may help explain the worse prognosis during hospitalization in the thrombocytopenia group. A study reported that low platelets lead to poorer outcomes in patients with liver transplantation [38]. Thrombocytopenia is common in critically ill patients admitted to ICU and has a poor prognosis [13]. The result also supports the study that inflammation/nutritional status is a key factor in the prognosis of patients with malignant tumors [39]. However, in patients with malignant tumors complicated with PE, to our knowledge, this is among the first report.

The study has several limitations. Its retrospective design introduces potential for unmeasured confounding. Although missing data were minimal, the sample size—especially the thrombocytopenia group (n = 66)—may limit the generalizability and statistical power of subgroup analyses. The study did not analyze specific cancer types or stages, which may influence thrombocytopenia risk and outcomes. Furthermore, the single-center design may affect external validity. Future large-scale, multi-center, prospective studies incorporating cancer subtype analyses and standardized laboratory and outcome assessments are needed to validate these findings and develop robust risk prediction models [40].

Conclusion

In malignancy patients with PE patients, when compared with the thrombocytopenia group and the platelet normal group, we found the coronary heart disease, right ventricular dysfunction, anticoagulant therapy, anti-tumor drug therapy, dyspnea, hemoptysis, fever(>37.3℃), NEUT, RDWSD, PDW, PCT, INR, FIB were significantly associated with a 6.703, 3.335, 0.311, 4.176, 4.244, 4.533, 3.527, 0.288, 3.995, 2.616, 5.129, 2.641, 0.212 times higher risk of thrombocytopenia respectively.
