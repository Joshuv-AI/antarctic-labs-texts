The role of super-enhancer-driven lncRNAs in cancer
Authors: Yuan-Ying Su, Fang-Yuan Ji, Ze-Jun Zheng, Jiang-Yun Peng, Jian-Jun Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, the global burden of cancer has grown substantially, yet available treatments and clinical outcomes remain inadequate. Accordingly, research into novel therapeutic targets for cancer has become a major focus. Super-enhancers (SEs), which are clusters of multiple enhancers, represent essential epigenetic oncogenic factors that are critical for maintaining cancer cell identity. Moreover, SE-driven long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) play a crucial regulatory role in tumor initiation and progression. Targeting cancer-specific SE-driven lncRNAs can slow tumor development and offer a novel strategy for cancer treatment. This review first outlines the characteristics of SEs, including their relevance to phase separation (PS) and the core transcriptional regulatory circuitry (CRC). It then describes the fundamental characteristics, intracellular localization, and functions of SE-driven lncRNAs, with emphasis on the analytical methods for these lncRNAs and their roles in tumors. Finally, the review highlights the clinical applications of existing SE inhibitors in oncology and their potential for targeting SE-driven lncRNAs, aiming to advance translational research in this field.Keywords: Super-enhancers, Long non-coding RNAs, Cancer therapy

Introduction

Cancer represents a major threat to human health and imposes a substantial burden on the global economy. Increasing evidence indicates that the cancer pathogenesis is closely associated with transcriptional dysregulation within oncogenic and tumor-suppressive pathways, which are primarily driven by genetic and epigenetic alterations. Among epigenetic regulators, dysregulation of super-enhancers (SEs) frequently results in the aberrant overexpression of oncogenes, thereby sustaining the growth and survival of cancer cells [1]. SEs were first documented in mouse embryonic stem cells (ESCs) as expansive clusters of transcriptionally active enhancers located in the upstream or downstream regions of gene promoters [2]. SEs function as cis-regulatory elements with a strong transcriptional activation potential [3]. It is now understood that SEs exhibit a hierarchical structure that contains both hub enhancers and non-hub enhancers [4]. Compared to their neighboring non-hub enhancers, hub enhancers play a predominant role in modulating local chromatin organization, recruiting transcriptional regulators, and governing the transcriptional activity of SE-associated target genes [4], [5]. Co-activators at SE loci undergo phase separation (PS) to form condensates [3], which regulate target gene transcription [6]. Recent research has demonstrated that facilitators within SEs, which lack intrinsic enhancer function, enhance the activity of the canonical enhancers to drive target gene expression [7].

Approximately 98 % of the human genome does not encode proteins, with confirmed non-coding RNA (ncRNA) genes accounting for about 4 % of this portion [8]. Among these, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), defined as ncRNAs longer than 200 nucleotides, function as either oncogenes or tumor suppressors in regulating tumor progression [8]. The expression of these lncRNAs can be driven by SEs, enabling them to perform various biological functions [9], [10].

This review offers a comprehensive overview of SEs, including their characteristics, their relationship with PS, and the SE-mediated core transcriptional regulatory circuitry (CRC). In addition, the historical development, characteristics, cellular localization, and functions of lncRNAs in tumors are summarized. The methodologies for identifying SE-driven lncRNAs and their implications in cancer are also discussed. Finally, the clinical applications of SE inhibitors in oncology are reviewed, and their potential to advance translational research in this field is examined.

SEs

Characteristics of SEs

Cis-acting regulatory elements, including promoters, enhancers, and silencers, represent specific DNA sequences located near structural genes. Cis-acting elements serve as binding sites for transcription factors (TFs), which regulate the precise initiation and efficiency of gene transcription. Typical enhancers (TEs) are a class of non-coding DNA cis-acting elements located near structural genes, activating or enhancing gene transcription by binding to TFs, cofactors, and chromatin complexes at promoters during eukaryotic development. Although both SEs and TEs function as cis-acting elements and are marked by histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) and histone H3 lysine 4 monomethylation (H3K4me1), the exceptionally high density of these marks at SEs constitutes as a key diagnostic criterion [2], [11]. SEs and TEs share similar regulatory mechanisms and are bound by the same factors, including TFs, histone modifying enzymes (HMEs), cofactors, and RNA polymerase Ⅱ (RNA pol Ⅱ). However, SEs exhibit a more complex structure, being significantly longer than TEs (which are typically 200–500 bp) and capable of forming clusters of neighboring enhancers within a ∼12.5 kb region [2]. The transcriptional activity of SEs is significantly higher than that of TEs, enabling them to drive highly specific gene expression (Fig. 1) [12].

TEs and SEs recruit the same factors, including TFs, HMEs, cofactors, and RNA pol II. HMEs include E1A binding protein P300 (EP300) and histone deacetylases (HDACs), while cofactors include bromodomain (BRD) and extraterminal domain, cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), and the mediator complex. SEs are characterized by a higher density of these regulatory factors and are significantly longer than TEs. Facilitator elements can enhance TE activity while serving dual roles as core executors of SE maintenance and "catalysts" for the conversion of TEs into SEs, explaining the higher pro-transcriptional activity of SEs compared with TEs.

The organization and activation of SEs involve two critical steps: the ordered recruitment of pioneer TFs, followed by the addition of co-activators, including HMEs, ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complexes, and the mediator complex [13]. This process transitions SEs from an inactive to an active state. By forming three-dimensional loop structures that bring enhancers close to promoters, relevant TFs are recruited to this region, thereby enhancing gene expression [14]. This unique spatial conformation and efficient transcriptional regulatory mechanism underscore the critical roles that SEs play in determining cell fate and identity. However, cancer cells can assemble their own SEs, leading to the overexpression of oncogenes and promoting tumor progression and malignancy. Therefore, studying the aberrant enrichment of SEs in cancer cells has emerged as a critical research focus [2].

SEs mediate transcriptional regulation through PS

In recent years, a PS model specific to SE organization and activation has been proposed (Fig. 2). The process of liquid-liquid PS in living organisms was first observed in Caenorhabditis elegans embryos, where P granules collided like droplets, forming dispersed small droplets that rapidly fused into larger ones [15]. Transcriptional co-activators form phase-separated condensates, which compartmentalize and concentrate the transcriptional machinery associated with SE-regulated genes, including promoters, RNA pol II, and co-activators [16]. These condensates also assist in the identification of SE components potentially involved in PS. It is widely thought that SEs recruit co-activators, such as the mediator complex and BRD-containing protein 4 (BRD4), at unusually high densities. The Mediator subunit MED1 and BRD4 undergo PS within cells to form condensates, thereby clustering the transcriptional machinery near SEs to create a compartmentalized transcriptional environment. Intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) play a crucial role in this PS process [17]. When phase-separated condensates of transcriptional co-activators are located within proximity (<1 μm) to the target gene regulated by the SE, transcriptional bursting is initiated [6]. In prostate cancer, lysine demethylase (LSD1) forms a transcriptional regulatory network with BRD4 and FOXA1, accumulating at SE sites and driving the formation of phase-separated condensates that promote cancer progression [18]. It has been reported that in hepatocellular carcinoma, the Twist1-YY1-p300 complex promotes malignancy by forming phase-separated condensates at the SE of MIR-9 [19]. Interestingly, in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, the SE-driven lncRNA CYTOR interacts with FOSL1, forming phase-separated condensates at the SE of SNAI2 to facilitate the metastasis of tumor budding cells, thereby promoting cancer progression [20].

The PS model of SE-mediated transcriptional activation. Transcriptional co-activators, including MED1, BRD4, and p300, along with TFs and RNA pol II, densely cluster at SE sites to form phase-separated condensates, thereby triggering episodic transcriptional activation.

SE-mediated CRC in tumors

The CRC refers to an interconnected network of key TFs and coactivators that collectively govern cell fate determination and identity maintenance. These TFs and coactivators bind to specific genomic loci, forming a highly orchestrated regulatory loop that coordinately regulates the expression of downstream target genes [21]. A hallmark of the SE-mediated CRC is that SEs form circuits with CRC TFs to synergistically control gene expression programs, and these TFs co-occupy the same SEs [22]. Bioinformatic methods such as “CRC Mapper” [23] and “Coltron” [24] have been employed to identify CRC TFs and their regulatory networks. Additionally, CRC components can be distinguished from non-CRC TFs and co-activators through an integrated approach combining transcriptomic, genomic, and proteomic analyses. First, comparative RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) across different cellular states or developmental stages can identify candidate factors whose expression patterns correlate with cell fate determination and identity maintenance. These candidates are then validated by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) to confirm their CRC-characteristic expression dynamics. Subsequently, chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) mapping reveals their genomic binding profiles, with true CRC components typically exhibiting enrichment at SE regions (marked by H3K27ac and MED1) and co-occupancy with other CRC members. Finally, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays are used to demonstrate the dense protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks among CRC components.

Targeting the CRC in tumor cells via pharmacological or genetic inhibition can significantly attenuate oncogenicity and malignancy [25]. For instance, in chronic lymphocytic leukemia, the SE-mediated CRC utilizes PAX5 as a key TF, and its expression is markedly reduced after treatment with SE inhibitors, thereby suppressing tumor cell growth [26]. Similarly, in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, the TFs TP63, SOX2, and KLF5 establish an SE-mediated CRC that governs the epigenetic and transcriptional landscape of these cancer cells [27]. In neuroblastoma cells, the SE-mediated CRC regulates susceptibility to retinoic acid [28]. Therefore, inhibiting the effects of SE-mediated CRCs may hinder cancer progression, offering a novel therapeutic approach for this patient population.

LncRNAs

Characteristics of lncRNAs

XIST and H19 were among the first lncRNAs identified, discovered in the 1980s and 1990s by complementary DNA library screening [29], [30]. Initially, lncRNAs have long been thought to be transcriptional "noise" or by-products of RNA pol II transcription with no biological function [31]. However, it was later discovered that lncRNAs may play crucial roles in disease pathogenesis, cellular development, and metabolism [32]. Since then, many lncRNA databases have been developed, including the NONCODE database [33] and LNCipedia [34].

LncRNAs are noncoding RNAs ranging from 200 nucleotides to over 100 kb in length [35]. Their subcellular localization, whether in the nucleus or cytoplasm, is closely linked to their function. Some lncRNAs show distinct localization patterns; for example, Gomafu is confined to nuclear speckles [36]. Beyond general nuclear or cytoplasmic localization, lncRNAs can also be precisely mapped in various organelles, including the nucleus, cytoplasm, mitochondria, and endoplasmic reticulum using the ascorbate peroxidase-RNA immunoprecipitation (APEX-RIP) method [37]. Subcellular localization is crucial for lncRNAs, as it determines their interactions their interactions with binding partners, their regulatory roles in post-transcriptional or co-transcriptional processes, and their overall contribution to cellular homeostasis. Most lncRNAs are transcribed by RNA pol II and undergo splicing, forming mRNA-like structures, with many featuring 7-methyl guanosine (m7G) 5′ cap [38] and poly(A) 3′ tail [39]. Based on their genomic location, lncRNAs can be classified as (a) antisense lncRNAs, (b) intronic lncRNAs, (c) divergent lncRNAs, (d) intergenic lncRNAs, and (e) enhancer lncRNAs. Although lncRNAs were initially considered unstable, studies in 2012 revealed that only a minority exhibit instability. Intriguingly, most lncRNAs achieve stability through polyadenylation, while non-polyadenylated lncRNAs can be stabilized by the formation of secondary structures [40].

Functions of lncRNAs

LncRNAs primarily exert their biological functions by participating in the regulation of gene expression. Generally, lncRNAs regulate gene expression at three levels: chromatin modification, transcriptional regulation, and post-transcriptional regulation [41], and can act as either oncogenes or tumor suppressors to modulate tumorigenesis and cancer progression. At the chromatin modification level, lncRNAs can form silencing complexes that remodel chromatin to precisely regulate transcription. Recruitment of chromatin remodeling complexes is a key mechanism in lncRNA-mediated gene regulation. LncRNAs such as HOTAIR [42], Kcnq1ot1 [43], and XIST [44] have been shown to be involved in the targeted recruitment of these complexes. For instance, mammals harbor four homeobox (HOX) cluster genes, HOXA, HOXB, HOXC, and HOXD, located on different chromosomes. HOTAIR, a 2.2 kb lncRNA located at the HOXC locus on chromosome 12q13.13, is upregulated in primary and metastatic breast cancer. By binding to polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) at its 5′ end, HOTAIR induces chromatin changes that silence the HOXD cluster gene to promote cancer invasion and metastasis [42], [45]. An example of transcriptional regulation is an upstream lncRNA that binds to the promoter region of dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), an important chemotherapeutic target in cancer, inhibiting its interaction with TF TFⅡB, and thereby suppressing DHFR gene expression [46]. Another example is Angelman syndrome, which is caused by the dysregulation of overlapping lncRNA transcription of the imprinted ubiquitin-protein ligase E3A (UBE3A) gene located on chromosome 15q11.2-q13 [47]. At the post-transcriptional level, lncRNAs can form double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) structures through intermolecular base pairing with the 3′-UTR sequences of mRNAs, a process mediated by Staufen double-stranded RNA-binding protein 1 (STAU1) that promotes mRNA decay. This mechanism contributes to the regulation of cellular functions and cancer progression [48].

SE-associated lncRNAs and SE-driven lncRNAs

SE-lncRNAs exist in two main forms. The first form, known as enhancer lncRNAs (eRNAs) or enhancer-derived RNAs, is transcribed directly from the SE regulatory elements [49]. The second form comprises SE-driven lncRNAs, whose expression is regulated by interaction between SEs and their promoters. Numerous high-throughput sequencing and next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have been applied in SE-lncRNA research, including RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, DNase I hypersensitive site sequencing (DNase-seq), SE-lncRNA microarrays, assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with high-throughput sequencing (ATAC-seq), and high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) [50]. Public databases such as TRlnc [51] and SELER [52] have been established to identify SE-lncRNAs with critical functions in various cancers. Although there is a research has explored the role of eRNAs in cancer [53]. This review primarily focuses on SE-driven lncRNAs, which are discussed in the following sections.

Identification of differentially expressed lncRNAs between tumor and normal tissues

The process begins with the analysis of both gene expression microarray data (including cDNA and oligonucleotide arrays) and RNA-seq data. For RNA-seq analysis, sequencing reads are aligned to the human reference genome using splice-aware aligners, such as HISAT2 [54] and STAR [55]. HISAT2 supports the prediction of complex splice sites while maintaining low memory usage and high computational efficiency, making it well-suited for large-scale analyses. STAR, by contrast, provides ultra-rapid alignment and is highly efficient in mapping long reads and highly expressed genes. Notably, the selection of the reference genome can significantly influence the results. Currently, the most widely used human reference genomes include GRCh37 [56], GRCh38 [56], and Telomere-to-Telomere CHM13 (T2T-CHM13) [57]. Compared with GRCh37, GRCh38 provides improved genome coverage and accuracy, and it remains widely used in most clinical studies [56]. In contrast, the T2T-CHM13 assembly represents a fully gapless genome, with higher accuracy in complex regions such as centromeres and telomeres, making it particularly suitable for studies of genetic variation [57].

Subsequently, assembly tools are employed to reconstruct complete transcripts from sequencing reads. Depending on the assembly strategy, these tools can be categorized as reference-based, such as StringTie [58] or de novo, such as Trinity [59]. StringTie is a fast and memory-efficient tool that effectively identifies novel transcripts and alternative splicing events, making it a widely used mainstream option. Trinity, a classic de novo assembler, reconstructs full-length transcripts and distinguishes alternative splicing from homologous genes, but it requires substantial computational resources and shows reduced sensitivity for lowly expressed genes.

Besides, lncRNA genomic coordinates and transcript structures are annotated using comprehensive databases like GENCODE [60], NONCODE [33], and Reference Sequence (RefSeq) [61]. GENCODE provides high quality genome annotations for humans and mice, integrating manual curation with high-throughput data, making it suitable for basic research [62]. NONCODE incorporates lncRNAs from a wide range of species, which support cross-species analysis [33]. In contrast, RefSeq emphasizes sequence integrity and experimental evidence in its lncRNA annotations, making it a reliable resource for functional validation [63]. For microarray data, raw intensities are typically normalized using platform-specific methods.

Finally, expression quantification and differential analysis of lncRNAs from RNA-seq data can be performed using lightweight alignment-free tools such as Salmon [62], Sailfish [63] and kallisto [64]. Among these, Salmon uses transcript-indexed abundance estimation, offering high computational efficiency with minimal memory requirements. Kallisto is suitable for large-scale data analysis and can efficiently process single-cell datasets, while Sailfish prioritizes speed but lacks some of the advanced analytical features present in Salmon and kallisto.

Differential expression analysis tools should be selected based on the data type, for RNA-seq data, R-based DESeq2 [65] or edgeR [66] are commonly used methods. DESeq2 has higher sensitivity in detecting lowly expressed genes, supports multi-factorial experimental designs, and produces stable results, while edgeR performs well with small sample sizes or technical replicates, making it suitable for studies on non-model organisms. For microarray data, limma [67] is widely applied, as it provides high accuracy in detecting differences in highly expressed genes while maintaining computational efficiency. Meanwhile, the choice between "transcript level" and "gene level" quantification should be determined by research objectives. Indeed, transcript-level analysis offers a more detailed view, enabling the detection of differentially expressed splice variants. In contrast, gene-level analysis provides a broader perspective by summarizing the overall expression of all transcripts for a given gene, which is simpler and more robust for large-scale screening of differentially expressed lncRNAs. Although lncRNA-associated databases are continuously updated with longer-read technologies, they still contain significant amounts of noise and inconsistencies in lncRNA annotations. These variations can lead to difficulties in replicating research results on lncRNAs, especially for SE-driven lncRNAs, and may even cause misinterpretation of their functions. Addressing this issue requires a multi-dimensional approach. To ensure the reproducibility of experiments, researchers should prioritize database versions that have undergone experimental validation or offer more comprehensive annotations for a given research objective. In addition, documenting the specific database versions is crucial for method traceability. Researchers can also cross-reference lncRNA IDs from different databases to clarify the corresponding relationships for the same lncRNA. To address inconsistent naming across various databases, unified naming principles can be consulted [68]. The authenticity and precise boundaries of lncRNAs can also be verified experimentally. For lncRNAs with controversial annotations in databases, rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE) can be used to determine their accurate 5′ and 3′ ends, thereby verifying transcript authenticity.

Identification of SEs using bioinformatics tools

SEs are characterized by open chromatin, high levels of histone modifications, and TF binding. The histone mark H3K27ac is particularly distinctive and widely used as a marker for SEs [69]. ChIP-seq [70], CUT&RUN [71], and CUT&Tag [72] are standard methods for identifying SEs. ChIP-seq is commonly used to map protein-DNA interactions, providing a robust tool for analyzing transcriptional activity and identifying active enhancer sites. Compared with ChIP-seq, CUT&Tag and CUT&RUN produce experimental results with lower background noise and higher reliability, without the need for cross-linking, chromatin fragmentation, or immunoprecipitation. In addition, these two methods have shorter experimental cycles, require fewer sample cells, and necessitate lower sequencing depths. However, they have a limited ability to distinguish between direct and indirect protein-DNA binding, and their sequencing depth is not as high as that of ChIP-seq [71], [72].

Based on the characteristics of SEs, computational methods have been developed for their identification, including Rank Ordering of Super Enhancers (ROSE) [2], imPROSE [73], DEEPSEN [74], and DeepSE [75]. ROSE identifies SEs by combining histone-specific binding regions (detected within 12.5 kb genomic regions in H3K27ac ChIP-seq experiments) and ranking them based on H3K27ac signal intensity. The threshold for distinguishing SEs from TEs is determined by the geometric inflection point of the signal [76]. ROSE yields highly accurate analytical results from experimental data, making it a classical method for SE identification. For example, SEs associated with TMEM44-AS [77] have been identified in gliomas, and those associated with LINC00880 [78] have been documented in lung adenocarcinoma. Nevertheless, the ROSE algorithm relies on specific ChIP-Seq data, resulting in high experimental costs and considerable time consumption. The imPROSE tool, in contrast, integrates multi-omics data based on machine learning algorithms for accurate identification of SEs and their constituent components from candidate enhancers. However, it requires complex data collection and integration, and its model performance can fluctuate significantly, making practical application difficult [73]. Both DEEPSEN and DeepSE rely solely on DNA sequence information without the need for experimental data, offering low cost and reduced time requirements. However, they share the drawback that relying only on sequence information may miss key features such as histone modifications or fail to capture SE characteristics [74], [75] fully. In summary, the choice of a tool for identifying SEs should be based on existing conditions and research objectives. Despite the availability of these alternative methods, tools other than the ROSE algorithm are rarely used in practical applications.

SEs can also be identified through techniques such as chromosome conformation capture (3 C), chromosome conformation capture-on-chip (4 C), chromosome conformation capture carbon copy (5 C), and Hi-C [79]. After identification, the genomic locations of SEs and lncRNAs are aligned to determine lncRNAs that are located within or adjacent to SE regions, thereby predicting SE-driven lncRNAs.

Validating the regulatory relationship between SEs and lncRNAs through biological wet-lab experiments

To assess whether an SE drives lncRNA expression, SE inhibitors such as JQ1 or THZ1 or CRISPR-Cas9-mediated editing [80], can be employed to knock out the lncRNA's SE. The effect of these interventions can then be measured by quantifying lncRNA transcription levels.

Assessing the function of SE-driven lncRNAs in tumors

To investigate the role of SE-driven lncRNAs in tumor development and progression, both in vivo and in vitro phenotypic assays can be conducted using RNA interference (RNAi) [81] to downregulate their expression, along with knockout strategies targeting the lncRNAs and their associated SEs. ‌Notably, unlike protein-coding genes, lncRNA knockout strategies require multiplexed sgRNAs (2–3 guides) to generate large genomic deletions that encompass promoter or enhancer regions, since small indels often fail to disrupt lncRNA function [82], [83]. Moreover, while protein-coding genes exhibit high conservation across different species and can therefore be studied in non-human model organisms, the sequences and structures of most lncRNAs are poorly conserved [84], with only a few notable exceptions, such as XIST [44] and H19 [29]. For these non-conserved lncRNAs, research has often focused on conserved functional "modules" rather than their complete sequences, or has constructed "functional analogy" models to explore the biological processes in which they are involved. By contrast, the use of human-derived cancer cell lines, patient-derived cancer organoids, their xenograft models, and patient-derived tumor xenografts in tumor research essentially circumvents the issue of interspecies conservation of lncRNAs.

SE-driven lncRNAs in cancer

Cancer cells are characterized by their virtually unlimited proliferative capacity and resistance to apoptosis, which collectively promote uncontrolled survival and growth. These features pose major challenges to effective tumor treatment. Furthermore, the migration and invasion of tumor cells enable their dissemination via the bloodstream or lymphatic system, leading to distal organ metastasis, which is a key cause of hematogenous and lymphatic metastasis in cancer. Besides, tumor cells possess other capabilities, such as sustained angiogenesis for continuous nutrient supply, immune evasion to escape immune surveillance, genomic instability with a high mutation rate, and the ability to induce inflammation [85]. Regulatory functions of SE-driven lncRNA in tumors.

Effects on proliferation and apoptosis

In hepatocellular carcinoma, SE-driven lncRNAs such as HCCL5 [86], FASRL [9], DAW [87], and LINC01004 [88] have been observed to accelerate cancer cell proliferation, while HCCL5 has also been shown to inhibit cancer cell apoptosis in vitro and in vivo. In lung adenocarcinoma, the SE-driven lncRNA LINC01977 [89] and LINC00880 [78] have been reported to increase cancer cell proliferation. Similarly, in non-small cell lung cancer, the SE-driven lncRNA LINC01503 [90] exerts a similar effect. In glioma, the SE-driven lncRNAs LINC00945 [91], LIMD1-AS1 [92], and TMEM44-AS1 [77] promote cancer cell proliferation. Furthermore, in squamous cell carcinoma, the SE-driven lncRNAs LINC01503 [93] and CCAT1 [94] contribute to enhanced cancer cell proliferation. Notably, the SE-driven lncRNA LINC00857 [95], promotes colorectal cancer cell growth by regulating glutamine metabolism.

Effects on migration and invasion

SE-driven lncRNAs have been shown to play a significant role in tumor cell invasion and migration. In nasopharyngeal carcinoma, the SE-driven lncRNA LOC100506178 [96] has been implicated, and in colorectal cancer, the SE-driven lncRNA RP11–569A11.1 [10] has been demonstrated to regulate cancer cell invasion and migration. Among these, lncRNA RP11–569A11.1 acts as a tumor suppressor by regulating IFIT2 expression in colorectal cancer, thereby inhibiting cell progression and metastasis and consequently slowing cancer progression. In contrast, the most SE-driven lncRNAs have been shown to promote cancer cell migration and invasion. In this context, the SE-driven lncRNA MANCR significantly enhances the migration and invasion of both triple-negative breast cancer [97] and prostate cancer [98] cells. Similarly, LINC01503 [93] in squamous cell carcinoma, CYTOR [20] in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and LINC00094 [99] in cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma have been shown to drive these metastatic behaviors.

Other functions

SE-driven lncRNAs have been linked to immune regulation and cancer progression. A correlation has been identified between the SE-driven lncRNA TM4SF1-AS1 [100], in stomach adenocarcinoma, and immune regulation. Besides, TM4SF1-AS1 has been shown to promote the production of angiogenic factors such as VEGF under hypoxic conditions, thereby facilitating tumor angiogenesis and progression. The SE-driven lncRNA CCAT1-L [101] in colorectal cancer regulates long-range chromatin interactions at the MYC locus. Furthermore, SE-driven lncRNAs, such as ATP1A1-AS1 [102] and DSCAM-AS1 [103], contribute to the development of tamoxifen resistance, while tamoxifen itself increases the risk of estrogen receptor (ER) positive breast cancer in high-risk women [104], [105]. Moreover, the SE-driven lncRNA LINC01116 [106] induces the transformation of human astrocytes into glioma-like cells, thus increasing cancer susceptibility. Most of the above-mentioned SE-driven lncRNAs, such as HCCL5, LINC01503, and DSCAM-AS1, are well-characterized and included in databases like GENCODE and RefSeq. However, some are annotated in only one database, for example, CCAT1 and LOC100508128 are found only by RefSeq, while RP11–509A11.1, HSAL3, and LINC00094 are listed exclusively in GENCODE. To demonstrate that a lncRNA is SE-driven, it is essential to prove that deletion of the SE abolishes its oncogenic effects. However, many current studies only focus solely on lncRNA knockdown without elucidating the mechanistic link between SE regulation and lncRNA function. Notably, an increasing number of studies are now meeting this standard by employing CRISPR/Cas9 to delete SE regions associated with lncRNAs [88], [89], [92] or by rescuing lncRNA expression after SE inhibition [78], [89], [93]. However, several critical caveats warrant careful consideration. For instance, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated SE deletion may lead to off-target effects that confound experimental outcomes. Similarly, lncRNA rescue experiments only provide limited evidence as it remains technically challenging to precisely restore pathological expression levels. Furthermore, the use of SE inhibitors is still considered an indirect approach to establishing the link between SEs and lncRNAs.

Clinical applications of SE inhibitors in tumors

Increasing evidence has demonstrated that SEs play a critical role in cancer development and progression, suggesting that targeting SEs may offer a promising approach to cancer therapy. SE inhibitors mainly include bromodomain and extraterminal (BET) inhibitors as well as CDK inhibitors. Compared with TEs, these inhibitors can preferentially bind to their targets on SEs due to the densely clustered architectural features of enhancer elements within SEs, thereby specifically modulating SE-mediated gene transcription [111]. Another important reason for this selectivity is that these inhibitors typically target SE-regulated cell identity and disease-related genes, without interfering with TE-regulated genes that are involved in essential for normal cellular functions [112].

The BET family is primarily comprised of four members, namely BRDT, BRD2, BRD3, and BRD4, with BRD4 serving as the main transcriptional regulator. BET inhibitors can disrupt SE activity, thus inhibiting the expression of SE-driven oncogenes and reducing cancer cell proliferation [113]. OTX015, a pan-BET inhibitor, specifically binds to BRD2, BRD3, and BRD4, blocking their interaction with acetylated histones and showing efficacy in leukemia, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma [114]. JQ1, the first BET BRD-targeting inhibitor, competitively binds to the BRD of BRD4, preventing BRD4 from binding to acetylated lysine residues, as well as preferentially targeting oncogenes with SEs. JQ1 has demonstrated inhibitory effects in various cancers, such as acute myeloid leukemia [115] and breast cancer [116], by suppressing MYC and its downstream transcriptional mechanisms. I-BET762 also targets BRD4, showing anti-cancer effects by downregulating SE-associated genes in acute myeloid leukemia [117]. Both OTX015 and I-BET762 inhibit the growth of gastric cancer cells [118]. Moreover, the BRD4 inhibitor DW-71177 displays anti-leukemic activity and therapeutic potential in acute myeloid leukemia [119].

The CDK family is an essential kinase family involved in the regulation of the cell cycle, including cell cycle-related CDKs such as CDK1, CDK2, CDK4, and CDK6, as well as transcription-related CDKs such as CDK7, CDK8, and CDK9. Similar to the BET family, CDKs represent critical targets for SE inhibition. THZ1 is a covalent inhibitor that targets CDK7, blocking phosphorylation of the C-terminal domain (CTD) of the RNA pol II large ribosomal subunit [120], thereby inhibiting transcription and reducing SE activity to achieve anti-cancer effects. THZ1 has shown preclinical efficacy in MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma a [121] and triple-negative breast cancer [122]. Targeted inhibition of CDK7 leads to decreased transcript levels of oncogenic TFs, particularly those associated with SEs. SY-1365, another CDK7 inhibitor, has been demonstrated to inhibit hematologic malignancies, breast cancer, ovarian cancer, and small-cell lung cancer [123]. Moreover, CT7001 targets CDK7, suppressing the proliferation of prostate cancer cells, thereby inhibiting the progression of prostate cancer [124]. CDK4/6 inhibitors function by blocking hyperphosphorylation of the retinoblastoma protein (Rb) a transcriptional co-repressor, which in turn suppresses the expression of E2F target genes and downregulates cell proliferation and DNA replication. This cell-cycle arrest subsequently disrupts SE-driven oncogenic transcription [125]. For instance, ribociclib inhibits CDK4/6 kinase activity, thereby blocking the G1 to S phase transition of the cell cycle and suppressing breast cancer cell proliferation [126].

SE inhibitors can also suppress tumor progression by targeting SE-driven lncRNAs. For instance, LINC00094, a SE-driven lncRNA in cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, was found to be significantly downregulated following treatment with JQ1 and THZ1, thereby inhibiting cancer cell invasion [98]. In prostate cancer, JQ1 has been reported to suppress the expression of the SE-driven lncRNA MANCR, thereby reducing cancer cell migration and invasion [98]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, both THZ1 and JQ1 have been found to broadly inhibit the transcription of SE-driven lncRNAs. Among these, HSAL3 displayed the most substantial transcriptional downregulation following JQ1 treatment, and its downregulation markedly inhibited cancer cell growth [109].

Summary, challenges, and prospects

Decades of research have significantly clarified the fundamental biological concepts of SEs and their role in tumorigenesis and cancer progression. Oncogenic SEs are present across multiple cancer types and are essential for maintaining cancer cell identity. SE-driven lncRNAs are closely linked to cancer pathogenesis, and research focused on SE-driven lncRNAs, as well as their targeted inhibition, offers new and promising avenues for cancer therapy. This review summarizes the latest advancements in the SEs, including their hierarchical structure and the role of facilitators, as well as their function in regulating gene expression, including lncRNAs, through mechanisms like PS and CRC. The review also consolidates the known functions of SE-driven lncRNAs in tumors and highlights contributions made by our team. In addition, it outlines the potential clinical applications of SE inhibitors for suppressing SE-driven lncRNAs in cancer.

However, SEs are not only crucial epigenetic factors that promote cancer, but they also play a role in tumor suppressive mechanisms. Resistance to small-molecule inhibitors targeting SEs has been observed in certain cancer cells. In some tumors, the limited anti-cancer effects of SE inhibitors may be related to the inhibition of tumor-suppressive genes driven by SEs, such as the breast cancer SE-driven RCAN1.4, which functions as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting cancer progression [127]. Besides, current SE inhibitors are broad-spectrum and lack the precision required to target oncogenic SE-driven lncRNAs. Moreover, it is challenging to design drugs that specifically target SE regions of lncRNAs due to their extensive binding with various regulatory elements and their considerable length [128].

Future research should focus on developing targeted drugs capable of specifically interacting with the structures of specific SE-driven lncRNAs to enable precision medicine. Small-molecule compounds targeting RNA structures have already been used in clinical settings. For instance, Risdiplam, an orally administered drug targeting the RNA structure of the survival motor neuron 2 (SMN2) gene, acts as an RNA splicing modifier and is approved for treating spinal muscular atrophy [129]. Additional RNA structure-targeting drugs are currently in clinical trials (NCT02719977 [130] and NCT04449029 [131]) and may soon become viable treatment options. Therefore, developing drugs that specifically target the structures of oncogenic SE-driven lncRNAs holds promise for future cancer therapies, given that they could potentially mitigate tumor growth by selectively inhibiting these lncRNAs.
