Solitary Fibrous Tumor of the Abdominal Wall With Long-Term Progressive Enlargement and Malignant Features
Authors: Keita Hosaka, Shunsuke Tamura, Shinichiro Irabu, Hirotaka Yamamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Solitary fibrous tumor (SFT) is a rare mesenchymal neoplasm that usually arises from the pleura, while extrapleural cases are uncommon. Abdominal wall SFTs are exceedingly rare, and their biological behavior remains unpredictable, with limited long-term follow-up data. An 83-year-old man was incidentally found to have an 18 mm mass on the liver surface in 2008 but declined further evaluation. Sixteen years later, he presented with abdominal pain, and imaging demonstrated enlargement of the lesion to 71 mm. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a well-circumscribed subperitoneal mass contiguous with the abdominal wall and supplied by the superior epigastric artery. Laboratory tests, including tumor markers, were within normal limits. Elective laparotomy revealed a smooth, well-demarcated mass continuous with the abdominal wall, and en bloc resection was performed. Histopathology showed spindle cell proliferation with necrosis and a mitotic count of two per 10 high-power fields. Immunohistochemistry was positive for cluster of differentiation 34 (CD34), CD99, B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2), and signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6), with a Ki-67 index of 6%, confirming malignant SFT. This case illustrates that indolent-appearing SFTs may exhibit progressive enlargement and demonstrate malignant potential over time, even after a prolonged period of clinical quiescence. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of an abdominal wall SFT with malignant transformation documented after extended follow-up. Given the potential for recurrence or metastasis, vigilant long-term surveillance is warranted.

Introduction

Solitary fibrous tumor (SFT) is a rare mesenchymal neoplasm originally described in the pleura, but it is now recognized to arise in various extrapleural sites [1]. Despite increasing recognition, the histogenesis and biological behavior of SFT remain incompletely understood, often making diagnosis challenging. Although most SFTs have an indolent clinical course, a subset exhibits aggressive clinical behavior, including local recurrence and distant metastasis. SFTs arising from the abdominal wall are particularly rare, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature [2]. Furthermore, long-term radiological follow-up of abdominal wall SFTs has rarely been reported, and their long-term biological behavior remains poorly characterized. We present a rare case of a primary abdominal wall SFT that demonstrated progressive enlargement over a 16-year clinical course and was ultimately treated with surgical excision, along with a brief review of the literature.

Case presentation

An 83-year-old man was incidentally found to have an 18 mm mass on the surface of the liver in 2008. He declined further evaluation and was subsequently lost to follow-up. Sixteen years later, he presented to a local clinic with right upper abdominal pain. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed a mass adjacent to the liver, and he was referred to the Department of Gastroenterology at our hospital for further assessment. Although the right upper abdominal pain had improved by the time of presentation, the patient continued to experience pain on inspiration. Physical examination revealed no palpable abdominal wall mass, no abdominal tenderness, and no signs of peritoneal irritation.

Serial imaging demonstrated progressive growth of the lesion, from 18 mm in 2008 to 71 mm in 2024. Given the patient’s abdominal pain and the potential risk of tumor rupture, a surgical consultation was requested. Laboratory tests revealed no elevated liver enzyme levels. Tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen, carbohydrate antigen 19-9, alpha-fetoprotein, neuron-specific enolase, and pro-gastrin-releasing peptide, were all within normal limits. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed a 71 mm subperitoneal mass located on the surface of segment four of the liver, immediately beneath the abdominal wall. The tumor appeared well-circumscribed with a smooth surface, showing mild blurring of the surrounding soft tissue and gradual, heterogeneous enhancement from the arterial to the equilibrium phase (Figure 1A, 1B). The lesion was supplied by the superior epigastric artery, which was considered the feeding vessel (Figure 1C).

Although the lesion was contiguous with the abdominal wall, it was clearly demarcated from adjacent structures, including the liver, duodenum, and pancreas. On the hepatobiliary phase of gadolinium ethoxybenzyl diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid-enhanced MRI, the lesion showed no uptake, and no evidence of hepatic metastasis was identified.

Based on the imaging findings and clinical course, a SFT was considered the most likely diagnosis, although desmoid-type fibromatosis and schwannoma were also included in the differential diagnosis. Although the patient’s symptoms had subsided, given the progressive enlargement and potential risk of rupture, an elective open surgical resection was performed [3].

Laparotomy was performed through an upper midline incision. A smooth, well-demarcated mass continuous with the abdominal wall was identified, without invasion of the intra-abdominal organs. En bloc resection, including the abdominal wall, which was considered the site of origin, was performed. The parietal peritoneum overlying the tumor was resected en bloc, and a portion of the posterior rectus sheath was additionally excised where necessary to achieve complete resection. Complete macroscopic resection was achieved, and abdominal wall reconstruction was not required. The resected specimen had a smooth surface with both elastic-soft and firm, whitish components. No intraoperative or postoperative complications occurred.

Histologically, the tumor showed irregular proliferation of spindle-shaped cells within a fibrous stroma, with areas of necrosis attributed to vascular compromise. Mitotic activity was observed at two per 10 high-power fields (Figure 3A). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed positivity for cluster of differentiation 34 (CD34) (Figure 3B), CD99, B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2), and signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6), with a Ki-67 labeling index of 6% (Figure 3C). These findings confirmed the diagnosis of malignant SFT.

Discussion

The diagnosis of SFT is often challenging because of its rarity, variable anatomical locations, and diverse histological features. Imaging may suggest a well-circumscribed soft-tissue tumor, but radiological findings are nonspecific; definitive diagnosis requires histopathological and immunohistochemical confirmation.

Histologically, SFTs display a wide range of patterns, the most characteristic being the so-called “patternless pattern,” in which spindle cells with oval to fusiform nuclei proliferate haphazardly within a collagen-rich stroma. Immunohistochemistry is essential for diagnosis. Markers such as CD34, CD99, and BCL2 are commonly expressed, with positivity for CD34 and BCL2 reported in 78%-100% and 96% of cases, respectively [4]. More recently, STAT6 has emerged as a highly specific marker for SFT and has become essential for diagnostic confirmation [5]. SFTs are generally regarded as benign neoplasms; however, a subset exhibits malignant potential. The histopathological criteria proposed for malignant SFT include a mitotic count greater than four mitoses per 10 high-power fields, increased cellularity, nuclear pleomorphism, and the presence of hemorrhage or necrosis [6]. Malignancy is determined by a comprehensive evaluation of these histopathological features rather than by any single criterion alone.

To the best of our knowledge, only two cases of abdominal wall SFTs were followed prior to resection and exhibited an increase in size; however, neither the exact duration of follow-up nor the specific changes in size were documented [2,7]. Although such progression has occasionally been observed, the malignant potential of SFTs has been reported mainly in the thoracic and retroperitoneal regions [8,9], and cases involving the abdominal wall, particularly those identified after extended follow-up, are exceedingly uncommon. In the present case, serial imaging over 16 years clearly demonstrated progressive enlargement of the tumor from 18 mm to 71 mm. Although the mitotic count was relatively low, subsequent histopathological examination revealed hemorrhage and necrosis, and the diagnosis of malignant SFT was established based on a comprehensive assessment of the pathological findings rather than the mitotic count alone. This clinical course highlights that even SFTs initially presenting with indolent features may exhibit malignant potential over time, underscoring the need for careful long-term follow-up to enable early detection of malignant progression.

The mainstay of treatment for SFTs, regardless of whether they are benign or malignant, is complete surgical excision with negative margins. Therefore, once an SFT is suspected, observation is generally not recommended, and surgical resection should be considered whenever feasible. Although this single case does not allow us to conclude that earlier surgical resection would have altered the biological behavior of the tumor, prolonged observation may delay definitive treatment. When complete resection is achieved, the prognosis is generally favorable. However, SFTs with malignant features or incomplete resection carry a risk of recurrence or metastasis. The recurrence rate of SFTs with malignant features has been reported to be approximately 22% [10]. Furthermore, local recurrence or metastasis has been described in 5%-10% of cases, even more than 10 years after the initial resection [11].

In the present case, the patient scored one point each for age, tumor size, and mitotic activity. Because the extent of tumor necrosis was not quantified, the metastatic risk was estimated to fall within the low- to intermediate-risk category. It should be noted that this model is intended to estimate metastatic risk rather than define histopathological malignancy. Although complete resection was achieved and the patient remains recurrence-free two years after surgery, the pathological findings suggestive of malignant potential indicate that recurrence or metastasis cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, long-term surveillance is warranted.

Conclusions

This case demonstrated progressive enlargement of an abdominal wall SFT over 16 years, with pathological findings suggestive of malignant potential. Although conclusions cannot be drawn from a single case, this report highlights the importance of careful long-term surveillance and timely surgical resection for abdominal wall SFTs, particularly when progressive enlargement is observed.
