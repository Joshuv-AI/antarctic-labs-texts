A case of marked improvement in pareidolia in untreated schizophrenia following antipsychotic treatment
Authors: Tomonobu Kato, Eiichi Seki, Tadanori Kumasaka, Takashi Tsujii, Daiki Mochizuki, Hisashi Kida, Shigeru Nikkuni, Masafumi Mizuno, Kei Sakuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPareidolia, the tendency to perceive meaningful images within ambiguous visual patterns, is commonly observed in neurodegenerative disorders and schizophrenia. Owing to the limited number of studies, the characteristics of pareidolia accompanying schizophrenia remain unclear.Case PresentationWe present the case of an 85‐year‐old woman with untreated schizophrenia for nearly 60 years, in whom pareidolia was observed. On evaluation using the Noise Pareidolia Test, the patient initially scored 16/40 correct responses, 24/40 pareidolic responses, and 0/8 misses. After antipsychotic treatment with 15 mg of olanzapine or 24 mg of aripiprazole, pareidolia completely disappeared. However, during the treatment course, pareidolia recurred when the antipsychotic dose was reduced owing to a medication switch.ConclusionEven in schizophrenia with an insufficient response to pharmacotherapy, pareidolia may still demonstrate treatment responsiveness.Keywords: antipsychotic drugs, pareidolia, schizophrenia, untreated schizophrenia, visual hallucination

BACKGROUND

Pareidolia is the tendency to perceive meaningful images, such as faces or animals, within ambiguous visual patterns and is commonly reported in Alzheimer's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB). 1 , 2 Previous studies have shown that individuals with schizophrenia exhibit pareidolic perceptions more frequently than do healthy controls 3 , 4 and individuals with bipolar disorder. 5 However, owing to the limited number of studies, the characteristics of pareidolia in patients with schizophrenia remain poorly understood.

Herein, we present a case of schizophrenia that remained untreated for approximately 60 years, during which pareidolia was observed. Pareidolia was assessed using the Noise Pareidolia Test, 6 which has demonstrated reliability and validity. Antipsychotic treatment resulted in the complete disappearance of pareidolia. However, symptoms, such as hallucinations, delusions, and thought disorders (including neologisms), persisted. These findings provide new insights into the responsiveness of long‐term untreated patients with schizophrenia.

Verbal informed consent was obtained from the patient; the method, content of the explanation, and details of the consent obtained were recorded.

CASE PRESENTATION

The patient was an 85‐year‐old woman born to a family of Shinto priests, with no history of psychiatric treatment. One day before admission, an internal medicine evaluation revealed no obvious physical illness. As far as we were able to confirm, she had no prior history of physical illness, inappropriate medication use, alcohol consumption, or smoking. According to her, she began hearing God's voice in her late teenage years. After marrying a Buddhist temple priest at the age of 34 years, her husband documented her behavior and speech in a book, 7 describing incidents suggestive of auditory hallucinations, thought insertion, and delusional ideation. However, as her husband believed what she said to be a divine message, she was never brought in for medical evaluation. The book also contained her speech on delusions of lineage, which were later confirmed during clinical interviews. Following her husband's death when she was aged 60 years, the patient became socially withdrawn, spending most of her time at home. During this period, she repeatedly encountered interpersonal disputes, such as sending letters to neighbors containing false information and illegally occupying property that she did not own. However, it was reported that she had never been subject to any legal sanctions. Although she was able to live independently, she relied on a helper for many daily activities, such as shopping and household chores. She was described as strong‐willed and unwilling to listen to others and was generally regarded by those around her as a difficult person to deal with. Finally, in the spring of Year X, the local authorities began to intervene. In November of Year X, she was found wrapped in bedding, in a severely weakened state, and was subsequently hospitalized in an internal medicine clinic.

At the initial assessment, she remained polite and was able to respond calmly to the interview. Orientation to time, place, and person was intact; however, her speech was dominated by delusional content, and she had no insight into her condition. Physical examination revealed no obvious motor deficits, and neurological examination did not detect remarkable findings. After admission to the ward, she was observed conversing with auditory hallucinations and making statements suggestive of delusional perception and thought insertion, which were considered Schneiderian first‐rank symptoms. Later, inquiries revealed that she had stopped eating because of auditory hallucinations and had been hoarding garbage throughout her room. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed on admission revealed age‐related cortical atrophy. The Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score was 20; however, immediate, recent, and episodic memory was preserved during the clinical evaluation. The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) total score was 75 (positive, 23; negative, 17; conceptual disorganization, 5; and general psychopathology, 35). Her statements encompassed various types of delusions, including religious and persecutory themes, and she did not deny that auditory hallucinations influenced her behavior. However, her thinking was idiosyncratic, but not disorganized. Symptoms suggestive of pareidolia were also noted, such as perceiving a stain on the ceiling as letters or the shadow of an air conditioner as the face of a bear. Furthermore, when she misidentified a stain on the ceiling as letters, she stated that they had been written by a ninja, indicating that her pareidolic experiences led to delusional perception. Therefore, we conducted the Noise Pareidolia Test, 6 a validated and reliable measure, which revealed 16/40 correct responses, 24/40 pareidolic responses, and 0/8 misses, indicating the presence of pareidolia.

Olanzapine was initiated at 2.5 mg and titrated to 7.5 mg, after which the reports suggestive of pareidolia disappeared. As hallucinations and delusional symptoms persisted, olanzapine was gradually titrated to 15 mg over 1 month. One month after reaching this dose, the Noise Pareidolia Test showed 39/40 correct responses, 0/40 pareidolic responses, and 1/8 misses, indicating the complete resolution of pareidolia. However, the PANSS scores remained unchanged at 79 (positive: 24; negative: 19; and general psychopathology: 36), and hallucinations and delusions persisted during the interviews. The MMSE score improved slightly to 23, likely because of the learning effect. Five weeks after the titration of olanzapine to 15 mg, the patient developed misty vision and lower limb edema, prompting a switch to aripiprazole at 12 mg. Notably, parkinsonism was not observed during treatment. One week after the switch, interactions with auditory hallucinations were observed during clinical interviews, while misty vision and edema improved. The Noise Pareidolia Test revealed a relapse of pareidolia, with 33/40 correct responses, 5/40 pareidolic responses, and 2/8 misses. The aripiprazole dose was gradually increased to 24 mg over 1 month. The PANSS scores worsened slightly to 81 (positive: 24; negative: 20; and general psychopathology: 37), but the Noise Pareidolia Test score improved to 40/40 correct responses, 0/40 pareidolic responses, and 0/8 misses. Despite efforts by the staff to encourage engagement, she tended to remain confined to her room, making non‐pharmacological interventions difficult. Aripiprazole (24 mg) was administered for 3 months without any therapeutic effect on her psychotic symptoms. The dose was temporarily increased to 30 mg; however, after 1 month, there was virtually no improvement, and she developed mild edema in her extremities. The dosage was therefore gradually reduced and ultimately set at 12 mg. Furthermore, approximately 11 months after admission, she developed a hand tremor, for which biperiden (2 mg) was added. In addition, magnesium oxide and Clostridium butyricum MIYAIRI were also used for constipation, and ultimately, 20 mg of C. butyricum MIYAIRI was administered. She was eventually discharged to a care facility 1 year after admission.

DISCUSSION

This report presents a case of schizophrenia with onset in adolescence or early adulthood, which remained untreated for several decades. In this case, pareidolia responded well to a certain level of antipsychotic medication; however, symptoms, such as hallucinations and delusions, which are included in the diagnostic criteria for schizophrenia, 8 showed treatment resistance.

First, regarding the score of the Noise Pareidolia Test, a previous study reported that it is significantly higher in patients with schizophrenia compared with healthy individuals. 5 They noted that the test score was 8.8 ± 6.7 (n = 50, mean ± standard deviation [SD]) compared with an average score of 2.4 ± 3.1 (n = 50, mean ± SD) for healthy individuals. This may indicate that pareidolia symptoms in schizophrenia show high variability. In the present study, the patient's pretreatment result on the Noise Pareidolia Test indicated the presence of pareidolia (24 points), and the result at the time of switching from olanzapine to aripiprazole, when the patient was maintained solely on aripiprazole at a dose of 12 mg, also indicated the presence of pareidolia (5 points). Therefore, fluctuations in the test score were observed at different medication doses, and the exact timing of the disappearance of pareidolia symptoms remains unclear. However, it can be stated that pareidolia was no longer observed at least by the time a chlorpromazine‐equivalent dose of 600 mg was administered. In patients with schizophrenia, abnormalities in top‐down information processing, that is, multiple levels of network dysfunction, have been proposed as reasons for the occurrence of hallucinations. 9 , 10 , 11 In contrast, it has been suggested that the reduced performance on pareidolial tasks reflects not only impairments in general cognitive abilities but also specific deficits in visual perceptual integration and social cognition. 3 In this case, the auditory hallucinations were treatment‐resistant, whereas the pareidolia showed treatment responsiveness, suggesting that the former may be based on neural mechanisms underlying so‐called treatment‐resistant schizophrenia that are resistant to ordinary D2 receptor antagonist‐based treatment, 12 whereas the latter may involve mechanisms that are responsive to ordinary D2 receptor antagonists.

Regarding the diagnosis, the possibility of comorbid schizophrenia and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) could not be completely ruled out. However, the increase in MMSE scores during the treatment course is considered to reflect a learning effect, which is unlikely to occur in MCI. 13 , 14 In addition, her delusional symptoms of schizophrenia likely contributed to a lower MMSE score. For example, she used her own self‐devised calendar and, based on persecutory delusions, refused to state the name of the hospital. Thus, the MMSE score may have underestimated her actual cognitive functioning. Moreover, among cognitive impairments, pareidolia is well‐documented in DLB. 1 In this case, no parkinsonism or other characteristic DLB features, including rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder and occipital signs, were observed; however, meta‐iodobenzylguanidine myocardial scintigraphy and fluorodeoxyglucose‐positron emission tomography were not available. 15 Although MCI cannot be entirely ruled out, it is unlikely that all the symptoms can be explained by MCI alone. Additionally, we also considered differential diagnoses that have been reported to induce pareidolia, including bipolar disorder, 5 obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), 16 Parkinson's disease, 2 and lysergic acid diethylamide abuse. 17 As a result, bipolar disorder was ruled out because the patient did not exhibit any clear manic or depressive episodes 18 ; OCD was excluded owing to the absence of obsessive thoughts or compulsive behaviors. 19 Parkinson's disease was excluded because no motor parkinsonism was observed at the time of admission, 20 and drug abuse was ruled out because, as far as available information allowed, there was no history of illicit substance use. Taken together, these considerations suggest that a diagnosis of schizophrenia most appropriately explains the present case.

A limitation of this report is that the age at which pareidolia first appears remains unclear. Specifically, it is uncertain whether pareidolia emerged during the course of schizophrenia or developed as a result of age‐related pathological changes. In addition, the possibility of auditory pareidolia may not have been fully assessed. Auditory pareidolia has been reported to occur in schizophrenia. 21 , 22 , 23 In the present case, she occasionally attributed bizarre meanings to non‐specific environmental sounds on the ward or perceived them as voices; however, based on these symptoms, it was difficult to clearly differentiate among auditory hallucinations, delusional perception, and auditory pareidolia. Finally, a limitation of this case is that a detailed assessment of visual function was not conducted. However, based on the clinical course, pareidolia had already improved by the time she complained of misty vision, suggesting that visual impairment was unlikely to have contributed to the occurrence of pareidolia.

CONCLUSION

This case represents schizophrenia with a prolonged untreated period, in which the overall response to antipsychotic treatment was insufficient. However, symptoms of pareidolia demonstrate a clear responsiveness to antipsychotic medications. These findings suggest that schizophrenia with insufficient response to medication may involve multiple underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, some of which may remain treatment‐responsive.
