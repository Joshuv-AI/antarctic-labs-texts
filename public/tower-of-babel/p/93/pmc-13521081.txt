Characterization of the splicing isoform TuSp1-sv provides novel insights into spidroin functional diversification
Authors: Xue Li, Haoxuan Xu, Jing Ge, Rongjun Zhan, Xiaoji Lin, Kangkang Wang, Rui Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpider silk exhibits extraordinary mechanical properties, with its functional diversity largely attributed to the sequence variation of spidroin genes. However, the contribution of alternative splicing to the functional diversity of spidroin, particularly in the case of tubuliform spidroin 1 (TuSp1), which is critical for tubuliform silk construction, remains poorly understood. In this study, we identified a novel splicing isoform of TuSp1 (TuSp1-sv) from the orb-weaving spider Araneus ventricosus and confirmed its expression in silk glands through RT-PCR and mass spectrometry. The TuSp1-sv retains only partial N-terminal and complete C-terminal domains, entirely lacking the repetitive core. Unlike the recombinant TuSp1-NC, which contains complete terminal domains, the recombinant TuSp1-sv protein forms stable dimers and self-assembles into amyloid-like fibrils with high β-sheet content, while also exhibiting unique shear-induced fiber formation. These results suggest that alternative splicing reprograms spidroin function by truncating the entire repetitive domain and a partial N-terminal domain, thereby shifting the role of TuSp1 variant from a primary structural component to a potential silk assembly regulator. Overall, our study provides new insights into the functional diversification of spider silk proteins mediated by alternative splicing, with significant implications for the design of bioinspired materials.Keywords: tubuliform silk, alternative splicing, recombinant protein, self-assembly, functional diversity

Spider silk is a natural protein-based fiber renowned for its excellent mechanical properties, high biocompatibility and inherent biodegradability (1, 2). An individual female orb-weaving spider can produce up to seven task-specific silk types using distinct silk-producing glands, with each silk exhibiting unique material properties (3). For example, tubuliform silk, synthesized in tubuliform glands, constitutes the tough outer layer of the egg sac to protect offspring and exhibits high tensile strength, while flagelliform silk exhibits high extensibility and is used in the capture spiral (4). Differences in the mechanical properties of these silk types are largely determined by the amino acid composition and the differential expression of structural proteins called spidroins (spider fibroins) (5). Therefore, uncovering the genetic structure of spidroins, their functional diversity, and the structure–function relationship contributes to harnessing their desirable properties.

Spidroins are encoded by members of the spider silk gene family and feature a central, lengthy repetitive region flanked by relatively short, conserved, non-repetitive amino (N)- and carboxyl (C)-terminal domains (NT and CT) (6). The amino acid motifs in spidroin repeats are highly variable, conferring specific physical properties to each silk type (7). Unlike the repetitive region, spidroin terminal domains serve as molecular switches to regulate silk formation and as phylogenetic markers due to their conservation between spidroin types (7, 8). To gain a deeper understanding of the diversity and evolutionary complexity of spidroin genes, recently, the genomes of several spider species have been sequenced and analyzed, revealing numbers of spidroin gene loci within individual species (7, 9). The generation of these diverse spidroin variants within a specific silk type is thought to result from gene duplication and concerted evolution, an important molecular mechanism for the emergence of new spidroins at the genomic level (10, 11). On the other hand, alternatively spliced spidroins have been found in nearly all silk types, suggesting that alternative splicing is a crucial mechanism for spidroin diversification by increasing transcript diversity (12). Due to the absence of terminal or repetitive domains, these splice forms from the same spidroin gene exhibit distinct structural, silk formation and mechanical properties (13, 14). Therefore, alternative splicing as a key molecular strategy for achieving functional diversity in spider silks, complementing genomic mechanisms such as gene family expansion and concerted evolution.

Despite the identification of several spidroin splice variants, current knowledge of spidroin isoforms remains limited. Here, we provide evidence of alternative splicing of TuSp1 transcripts in the orb-weaving spider Araneus ventricosus. The TuSp1 splice variant (TuSp1-sv) retains only a partial N-terminal sequence and a complete C-terminal domain. We aimed to examine the expression of the splice variant using mass spectrometry. To investigate the physicochemical properties of its protein products and the biological functions of the missing partial N-terminal region, we expressed the recombinant protein TuSp1-NC by fusing both TuSp1 N- and C-termini and the TuSp1-sv protein in Escherichia coli and compared their structural and assembly characteristics.

Results

Identification of TuSp1-sv

To determine whether alternative splicing of TuSp1 transcripts occurs, three pairs of primers were designed for RT-PCR analysis based on the mRNA extracted from the tubuliform silk gland. The RT-PCR results showed that a 765-bp cDNA fragment of TuSp1-v3 was successfully amplified using the F3 and R3 primers and designated as the TuSp1 splice variant (TuSp1-sv) (Fig. 1A). However, no PCR products were observed using the other two pairs of primers, indicating that the alternative splicing event was not detected for the TuSp1-v1 and TuSp1-v2 transcripts (Fig. 1A). The predicted TuSp1-sv protein is 254 amino acids in length, contains only terminal domains, and has a molecular weight of 24.5 kDa (Fig. 1B). Sequence alignment analysis further revealed that TuSp1-sv contains the first 105 amino acids of the N-terminal domain and the entire C-terminal domain, but lacks the complete repetitive region (Fig. 1C). After removal of the signal peptide sequence, TuSp1-sv was predicted to adopt a nine α-helical conformation (Fig. 2A) and to form a dimer (Fig. 2B).

To determine whether TuSp1-sv was spun on fibers, we collected tubuliform silk fiber from A. ventricosus spiders for biochemical studies. The fibers were solubilized using 8 M GdnHCl and subsequently digested with trypsin, followed by MS/MS analysis. Analysis of the tryptic peptides revealed a sequence, TASARAQALSTALASSLTDLLISNSAAASASAR, that displayed a 100% match to the junction region between the N- and C-terminal domains of the translated TuSp1-sv sequence following in silico trypsin digestion, providing evidence for the existence of the TuSp1-sv protein product in tubuliform silk (Fig. 3A). To examine the expression pattern of TuSp1-sv in egg case silks, we tested egg case fibers collected from the female A. ventricosus. Western blot analysis using an anti-TuSp1-sv antibody revealed immunoreactive species in the egg case silk. The experimental molecular mass of TuSp1-sv closely correlated to the predicted mass, which was ∼22.6 kDa (Fig. 3B).

Structural and assembly analysis of the recombinant TuSp1-sv protein

To investigate the structural and assembly properties of TuSp1-sv, we expressed and purified the recombinant TuSp1-sv protein. Furthermore, TuSp1-NC, which contains both complete N- and C-terminal domains, was designed, expressed, and purified. Compared with TuSp1-NC, TuSp1-sv lacks 51 amino acid residues from the N-terminal domain. Therefore, the functional significance of the missing N-terminal region was assessed by comparing the physicochemical properties of these two proteins. Both recombinant proteins were purified using their C-terminal His6-tag and subsequently analyzed by SDS-PAGE. The results showed that both proteins were purified to homogeneity and exhibited migration patterns consistent with their predicted molecular masses (Fig. 4). On SDS-PAGE under nonreducing conditions, TuSp1-sv migrates predominantly as a dimer, confirming disulfide-mediated dimerization via the single cysteine residue in the NT. In contrast, TuSp1-NC migrates as both a monomer and higher-order multimers, indicating that the two cysteine residues can form intermolecular disulfide bonds (Fig. 4).

12% SDS-PAGE analysis of purified TuSp1-sv and TuSp1-NC proteins, under reducing and nonreducing conditions ± DTT.

CD spectroscopy is frequently used to determine the secondary structure of proteins and polypeptides (15). The CD spectra of both TuSp1-sv and TuSp1-NC proteins in solution at pH 7.0 exhibited negative bands at 208 and 222 nm, characteristic of a predominantly α-helical conformation, indicating that the N- and C-terminal domains of TuSp1 adopt α-helical structures (Fig. 5A). When the pH was increased from 7.0 to 8.0, the CD spectra of both proteins did not show observable changes (Fig. 5A). As pH decreased from 7.0 to 5.0, the two negative bands for TuSp1-sv showed only slight weakening, indicating no significant changes in its secondary structure composition. In contrast, the negative band centered at 208 nm weakened noticeably for TuSp1-NC, suggesting a partial loss of α-helical structure and an increase in β-sheet conformation (Fig. 5A).

B, CD spectra of TuSp1-sv and TuSp1-NC as a function of temperature. C, CD signals of TuSp1-sv and TuSp1-NC at 222 nm over the temperature range from 25 °C to 95 °C under different pH conditions.

To further determine their thermal stability, the effect of temperature on the secondary structure was investigated. Both TuSp1-sv and TuSp1-NC display temperature-dependent structural transitions from α-to β-conformations (Fig. 5B). At pH 7.5, the apparent melting temperature (Tm) of TuSp1-sv was 56 °C, which was lower than that of TuSp1-NC (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, as pH decreased, the Tm of TuSp1-sv decreased from 56 °C to 33 °C, while that of TuSp1-NC decreased from 67 °C to 53 °C, indicating that TuSp1-NC exhibits higher thermal stability (Fig. 5C).

To investigate the ability of TuSp1-sv to self-assemble into fibrils, we analyzed its Thioflavin T (ThT) fluorescence under different conditions. The fluorescence intensity increases upon binding of ThT to β-sheet-rich structures in amyloid-like fibrils (16). Both TuSp1-sv and TuSp1-NC transitioned to a ThT-positive state at pH 7.5 and under more acidic conditions. Furthermore, acidic conditions significantly promote fibril formation in these two proteins (Fig. 6A). In contrast to pH, the effect of ionic strength on fibril formation differs significantly between the two proteins. For TuSp1-sv, a stronger ThT-positive signal was observed under low ionic strength conditions, whereas the ThT-positive fluorescence of TuSp1-NC remained similar across all tested NaCl concentrations, suggesting that low NaCl concentration promotes fibril formation in TuSp1-sv (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, the self-assembly behavior of TuSp1-sv was systematically investigated across a broad ionic strength range. Turbidity developed immediately upon addition of NaCl at concentrations exceeding 100 mm (Fig. S1A), indicating rapid aggregation. Consistently, the ThT fluorescence signal decreased under 150 mm NaCl relative to that at 100 mm NaCl (Fig. S1B), likely due to enhanced light scattering, rather than reduced fibril formation, as corroborated by TEM imaging, which confirmed the persistence of well-defined fibrillar structures at high salt concentrations (150 mm) (Fig. S1C). To further investigate the effect of disulfide bonds on self-assembly, dynamic ThT fluorescence was monitored under both DTT-treated and DTT-free conditions. Although the cleavage of disulfide bonds can partially inhibit the self-assembly of both proteins, this process exerts a more pronounced effect on TuSp1-NC compared to TuSp1-sv (Fig. 6C). In addition, TEM analysis of ThT-positive fibrils revealed typical amyloid-like structures with diameters ranging from 5 to 10 nm (Fig. 7).

Effect of shear forces on TuSp1-sv assembly

Shear stress has been identified as a key triggering factor for fiber assembly in various biological and synthetic systems, capable of inducing conformational changes in spidroin proteins by promoting their alignment and subsequent aggregation into well-ordered fibrillar structures (17, 18, 19). Despite the solubility of TuSp1-sv in aqueous buffers at pH 7.5, exposure of recombinantly produced TuSp1-sv to shear forces results in the formation of clearly visible fibrils within 24 h of rotational agitation (Fig. 8). In contrast to TuSp1-sv, however, TuSp1-NC containing complete N- and C-terminal domains underwent only a small amount of nonspecific aggregation (Fig. 8).

TuSp1-sv and TuSp1-NC were evaluated by light microscopy for their ability to undergo shear-induced fiber self-assembly.

Morphology and material properties of TuSp1-sv silk-like fibers

Silk-like fibers pulled from the purified TuSp1-sv protein solution were more than 2 cm long by manual drawing. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to examine the morphology of TuSp1-sv fibers. The TuSp1-sv fibers exhibited a smooth surface and a diameter of approximately 5 μm, which is smaller than that of native tubuliform silk fibers (∼8 μm) (Fig. 9A) (4). To investigate the material properties of TuSp1-sv fibers, we analyzed the mechanical characteristics of manually drawn TuSp1-sv fibers and compared them with those of other artificial silk fibers (13, 18, 20, 21). Typical stress-strain curves of TuSp1-sv fibers are shown in Figure 9B. The stress at break for TuSp1-sv fibers is 46.8 ± 8.5 MPa, which is lower than that of other engineered fibers and approximately one-10th of that of native tubuliform silk (Table 1). In terms of extensibility, the breaking strain of TuSp1-sv fibers was 7% ± 1%, which is comparable to that of MaSp1-1 fibers derived from dragline silk proteins, but only approximately one-fourth that of natural tubuliform silk (29% ± 2%) (Table 1). In addition, the Young's module of TuSp1-sv fibers was only 1.9 ± 0.3 GPa, which is much lower than that of native tubuliform silk (11.6 ± 2.1 GPa).

Previous studies have shown that spidroins exhibit an α-helical secondary structure in aqueous solution (20). However, during silk formation, spidroins undergo a structural transition to a β-sheet-rich conformation. To investigate the structural changes of TuSp1-sv before and after silk formation, the secondary structure contents of freeze-dried TuSp1-sv (fd TuSp1-sv) and TuSp1-sv fibers were therefore analyzed using FTIR spectroscopy. The FTIR spectrum of fd TuSp1-sv showed an absorbance maximum at 1660 cm−1, indicating the presence of α-helices (Fig. 9C). In contrast, the FTIR spectrum of TuSp1-sv fiber displayed a strong peak at 1625 cm−1, indicating a high β-sheet content (Fig. 9D). To further analyze the secondary structure, we performed deconvolution of the amide I band for fd TuSp1-sv and TuSp1-sv fibers. The secondary structure analysis showed an increase of the β-sheet content from 24% in fd TuSp1-sv to 51% in TuSp1-sv fibers. The random coil content decreased from 23% to 10%, and the β-turn content from 41% to 29% (Table 2).

Discussion

In the present study, we identified the TuSp1 splice variant, TuSp1-sv, from A. ventricosus spider by using RT-PCR and provided evidence for its protein expression in tubuliform silk. Although previous studies have shown that spider silk is a complex, multicomponent material, it is mainly composed of spidroins with unusually large protein sizes (22). Our previous studies identified three TuSp1 variants from the orb-web spider A. ventricosus, which exhibited sequence variation at the amino acid level (23, 24). Through sequence alignment, the TuSp1-sv characterized in this study was identified as an alternative splice product derived from TuSp1-v3, which contains 4382 amino acids and consists of 23 tandemly arrayed repeats. Compared with the full-length sequence of TuSp1-v3 (24), TuSp1-sv lacks the entire repetitive region and the last 48 amino acids of the NT. As the dominant domain in spidroins, the repetitive domain's amino acid sequence and composition largely determine the mechanical properties of spider silk (25). Despite the detection of the TuSp1-sv protein product in tubuliform silk, it is likely that TuSp1-sv is not the main component of tubuliform silk, given the absence of the repetitive region. Nevertheless, the identification of TuSp1-sv still supports the notion that spidroin diversity arises not only from the expression of silk variants at the gene level but also from transcript modification at the transcriptional level, both of which participate in tubuliform silk fiber biosynthesis.

Unlike the lengthy and variable repetitive domain, the terminal domains are short, evolutionarily conserved, and responsible for regulating silk formation (9). Due to the critical role of terminal domains in silk formation, the structural and self-assembly properties of TuSp1 terminal domains have been extensively reported (26, 27, 28). Given the absence of 48 amino acid residues in the TuSp1-sv NT, this variant may exhibit a distinct capacity for silk formation compared to other spidroins with complete terminal domains. The missing region contains several well-conserved hydrophobic residues (such as I64, I88, and V90) that contribute to TuSp1 self-assembly. Therefore, the recombinant TuSp1-sv and TuSp1-NC were produced to investigate the functional role of the missing region in the NT of TuSp1-sv. Previous studies have shown that both TuSp1 NT and CT are prone to form oligomers through hydrophobic interactions mediated by their surface-exposed hydrophobic residues, which is consistent with the heterogeneous oligomer formed by TuSp1-NC (27). Although no oligomer was observed in TuSp1-sv, TuSp1-sv primarily exists as a dimer in solution, mediated by the single remaining cysteine residue in its NT. In addition to differences in multimerization, the absence of a partial NT sequence significantly decreases the thermostability of the silk protein under neutral conditions and enhances the sensitivity of thermostability to pH. Taken together, alternative splicing of the A. ventricosus spider generates the TuSp1-sv protein with relatively low thermostability.

The observation shows that TuSp1-sv transitions more readily to a ThT-positive, β-sheet-rich fibril under acidic conditions and low ionic strength, and this process is less dependent on disulfide bonding compared to the full-terminal construct TuSp1-NC. This finding is consistent with the established role of terminal domains as critical molecular switches that respond to physicochemical cues in the spinning duct, thereby regulating the hierarchical transition from soluble proteins to solid fibers (29, 30). Furthermore, under low-salt conditions, diminished ionic screening may strengthen electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged residues on the TuSp1-sv surface, while simultaneously suppressing charge shielding and nonspecific aggregation, thereby favoring directional, ordered assembly into β-sheet-rich amyloid-like fibrils. The pronounced sensitivity of TuSp1-sv to acidic pH is particularly significant. In native spinning, the pH gradient serves as a primary trigger for spidroin assembly. The CT domain is known to undergo structural changes, such as partial unfolding or the formation of folding intermediates, at the acidic pH present at the distal end of the spinning duct (30, 31). Our data suggest that the truncated NT in TuSp1-sv may render its overall conformation more susceptible to this acidic trigger, thereby potentially facilitating earlier or less constrained nucleation of β-fibril formation compared to the more stable TuSp1-NC. Furthermore, the differential response to ionic strength and disulfide reduction further highlights the structural uniqueness conferred by alternative splicing. The enhanced assembly of TuSp1-sv at low NaCl concentrations suggests that electrostatic interactions play a more prominent role during its initial aggregation phase. More importantly, the relatively minor inhibitory effect of DTT on TuSp1-sv self-assembly, in contrast to its stronger effect on TuSp1-NC, provides direct experimental support for the conclusion inferred from SDS-PAGE result (Fig. 4). TuSp1-sv, lacking one of the key cysteine residues involved in intermolecular disulfide bonding within the full NTD, relies less on covalent stabilization for fibril formation. This "unlocked" state may confer greater conformational flexibility or expose alternative interaction interfaces, potentially explaining its propensity to form amyloid-like fibrils under a broader range of conditions. These mechanistic insights complement studies on other spidroins, such as pyriform silk proteins, in which pH-sensitive dimerization and liquid–liquid phase separation of terminal domains are critical for fiber formation (15).

Previous studies have demonstrated that physical shearing plays an important role in the silk formation of spidroins (32, 33, 34). Although nanofibril formation, which initiates and templates the assembly of amyloid-like fibrils critical for silk solidification, is observed in both TuSp1-sv and TuSp1-NC solutions, TuSp1-sv nanofibrils can be readily induced by shear forces to form macroscopic fibers, whereas TuSp1-NC exhibits only non-specific aggregation, suggesting that truncation of the NT domain in TuSp1-sv alters its response to mechanical stress. Given the critical role of shear-induced alignment in the transition from soluble protein to oriented and crystalline fibrils, it is implied that the enhanced shear sensitivity of TuSp1-sv may play a specialized role in rapid, environment-responsive matrix formation within tubuliform silk. Compared with fd TuSp1-sv without shear-force treatment, the significant increase in β-sheet content in TuSp1-sv fibers confirms a successful structural transition from soluble α-helical structures to solid β-sheet conformations during fiber pulling, highlighting the crucial role of shear forces in inducing conformational changes of spidroin molecules during silk formation. Despite having a β-sheet content similar to that of native silk fibers, the mechanical properties of TuSp1-sv are significantly lower than those of native tubuliform silk fibers due to the complete absence of the repetitive domain, which plays a major role in determining the tensile strength and extensibility of spider silk (35, 36). Therefore, TuSp1-sv can only form macroscopic fibers with aligned β-sheet nanocrystals derived from its limited terminal sequences, resulting in a less robust and less extensible polymeric fiber. In contrast to TuSp1-sv, TuSp1-NC with complete terminal domains cannot form any observable fiber under the shear force provided by manual drawing, suggesting that truncation of the TuSp1 N-terminus enhances the capacity for shear stress-induced silk formation. In conclusion, the terminal domains of TuSp1, even in a truncated form (TuSp1-sv), retain the fundamental capacity to drive amyloid-like fibrillogenesis and shear-induced fiber assembly, supporting their conserved function as assembly molecules. Furthermore, the absence of the lengthy and complex repetitive domain limits the mechanical performance of TuSp1-sv fibers, suggesting that in natural tubuliform silk, it likely does not function as a primary structural spidroin. Instead, its ability to serve as an efficient trigger for assembly and fibril formation may enable it to act as a regulatory or matrix component that modulates the assembly kinetics of silk formation within the tubuliform silk composite material, working in concert with the full-length TuSp1 protein.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified a novel splicing isoform of tubuliform spidroin 1 (TuSp1-sv) in the spider A. ventricosus and provided conclusive evidence for its expression in both the tubuliform silk glands through RT-PCR and mass spectrometry. The TuSp1-sv retains only a partial NT and the complete CT, while entirely lacking the long repetitive core region. By comparison with TuSp1-NC, which contains complete terminal domains, the recombinant TuSp1-sv protein exhibited a distinct self-assembly capacity, readily forming stable dimers and undergoing efficient, pH-sensitive fibrillization into amyloid-like structures. Despite exhibiting significantly inferior mechanical properties compared to native tubuliform silk, the TuSp1-sv protein still demonstrated unique shear-induced fiber formation. In light of our findings, these studies lies in elucidating how alternative splicing reprograms a spidroin's function. By removing the repetitive domain and truncating the N-terminal (NT) region, TuSp1-sv shifts from playing a primary structural role to potentially functioning as an assembly regulatory within the composite tubuliform silk. Overall, our findings provide new insights into spider silk protein diversification, revealing that alternative splicing is a crucial strategy for generating functional complexity in minimalist, stimuli-responsive silk biomaterials.

Experimental procedures

Sample preparation, RNA extraction, and RT-PCR analysis

Four adult female spiders of A. ventricosus were collected in Wenzhou, China. Prior to isolation of the tubuliform silk glands, spiders were anesthetized with CO2 gas, followed by severing of the cephalothorax at the midpoint to obtain the abdominal segment. Subsequently, tubuliform silk glands were isolated from abdominal tissue, and total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). For the RT-PCR experiment, first-strand cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using ExonScript RT SuperMix with dsDNase (EXONGEN, Chengdu, China). To determine whether the alternative splice event of the three TuSp1 genes (TuSp1-v1, TuSp1-v2, and TuSp1-v3) in A. ventricosus spider, three pairs of primers (F1 and R1 for TuSp1-v1, F2 and R2 for TuSp1-v2, and F3 and R3 for TuSp1-v3) were designed and used for PR-PCR experiment. The TuSp1-v1 transcript was amplified using primers F1 (5′-ATG GTT TGG CTG ACT AGC G-3′) and R1 (5′-TTA ACC AGA AAG AGC ACT CAG C-3′). RT-PCR amplification of the TuSp1-v2 transcript was carried out with primers F2 (5′-ATG ATT TGG ATA AGT AGC ATA GCG-3′) and R2 (5′-TTA ACC AGC AAG AGC ACT CAC-3′). The TuSp1-v3 transcript was amplified using primers F3 (5′-ATG GTT TGG CTT ACT AGC GTG-3′) and R3 (5′-ACC AGA AAG AGC ACT CAC TAA GGC-3′). Following amplification, PCR products were gel extracted and subjected to sequencing.

Bioinformatics analysis and UHPLC-MS/MS for the identification of protein products

Secondary structures were predicted using PSIPRED 4.0 (https://bioinf.cs.ucl.ac.uk/psipred/). SWISS-MODEL website (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/interactive) was used for tertiary structure prediction. Theoretical molecular weight was predicted using the expasy tools (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). Amino acid sequences were aligned by Geneious v. 8.1.5 (37).

10 mg of the tubuliform silk was collected and dissolved in 200 μl of 8 M GdnHCl, followed by heating at 95 °C for 10 min to facilitate fibroin solubilization, then adding an adequate amount of iodoacetamide (IAM) solution and incubating in the dark for 1 h. Next, samples were completely mixed with precooled acetone by vortexing and incubated at −20 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, samples were centrifuged at 12 r.p.m for 10 min at 4 °C, and then the precipitate was collected. Following washing with cold acetone, the pellet was dissolved in dissolution buffer (6 M urea, 100 mm TEAB, pH 8.5). After protein extraction, protein samples were incubated with lysis buffer (6 M GdnHCl, 100 mm TEAB, pH 8.5), trypsin, and 100 mm TEAB buffer at 37 °C for 4 h. Next, formic acid was added to the digested samples to adjust the pH to below 3, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 r.p.m. for 5 min at 25 °C. The supernatant was loaded onto a C18 desalting column, washed three times with washing buffer (0.1% formic acid, 3% acetonitrile), and eluted with elution buffer (0.1% formic acid, 70% acetonitrile). Finally, the eluates were collected, lyophilized, and subjected to analysis using an EASY-nLC 1200 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher) coupled with a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) operating in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode at Novogene Co, Ltd (Beijing, China). Mass spectra raw data were analyzed using MaxQuant software 1.3.0.1 (38). Peptide searches were performed via Andromeda search algorithms (39). To further confirm the presence of TuSp1-sv in egg case fibers, the peptide (SSLTDLLISNSAAASASAR), corresponding to the translated region of TuSp1-sv, was synthesized and used for co-immunization in rabbits.

Construction, expression and purification of TuSp1 splice variant

To obtain the protein product of the TuSp1 splice variant, the TuSp1 splice variant cDNA was amplified by PCR using the forward and reverse primers 5′- TCT CAG TCA GTC GCA GTT ACT GC-3′ and 5′- ACC AGA AAG AGC ACT CAC TAA GG-3′, respectively. NdeI and XhoI sites were engineered on the 5′-termini of both primers to facilitate cloning of the cDNA. The TuSp1 splice variant cDNA fragment was amplified by PCR using the RT-PCR product obtained above. The TuSp1-NC was amplified using overlap PCR with the two pairs of primers (N-F: 5′- TCT CAG TCA GTC GCA GTT ACT GC, N-R: 5′- GGC GGC TGC AGC TGC AGC ATT GCT ATT TTG AGA TAG AAC-3′, C-F: 5′- GCT GCA GCT GCA GCC GCC-3′, and C-R: 5′- TTA ACC AGA AAG AGC ACT CAC TAA G-3′). Following amplification, the PCR products were purified using the DNA gel extraction kit (KeyGEN), ligated into the pET-32a vector and transformed into BL21 (DE3) cells for protein expression.

For protein expression experiments, the cells were grown in LB medium supplemented with 0.1 mg/ml ampicillin at 37 °C until the OD600 reached ∼0.6. Protein expression was induced by adding 0.1 mm IPTG (isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside) (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co, Ltd), and the cells were cultured at 25 °C for 12 h. The cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mm Tris-HCl, pH 8.0), and lysed by sonication (5 s"ON, 8 s "OFF") for 30 min at 4 °C. Since the majority of TuSp1-sv and TuSp1-NC proteins were found in the insoluble fraction, the pellets were resuspended in 8 M urea and sonicated 25 times at 250 W under ice-cold conditions. The samples were then centrifuged (12,000 r.p.m.) to collect the supernatant. The supernatant was loaded onto a Ni-NTA column and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. The resin was washed twice with lysis buffer containing 20 mm imidazole. Subsequently, the recombinant proteins were eluted with lysis buffer containing 250 mm imidazole and dialyzed against the lysis buffer at 4 °C overnight. The purified samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE.

Circular dichroism spectroscopy analysis

CD spectra of recombinant proteins were recorded at 25 °C using a Chirascan spectrometer (Applied Photophysics Ltd). Samples were diluted in 10 mm sodium phosphate buffer at different pHs (pH 5.5, 6.5, and 7.5) to a final concentration of approximately 0.2 mg/ml, loaded into quartz cuvettes with a 0.1 cm path length, and scanned from 190 to 260 nm. For the thermal stability analysis, protein solutions at pH 7.0 were heated from 25 °C to 95 °C and measured after temperature stabilization for 3 min at 10 °C increments. To monitor protein unfolding, the CD signal at 222 nm was recorded, and the fraction of folded protein was calculated as described previously (40). Raw data were processed by subtracting the blank spectrum and applying smoothing.

Dynamic thioflavin T (ThT) assay

The dynamic ThT assay was conducted using a Tecan Spark reader (German). The recombinant protein solutions were diluted in 10 mm sodium phosphate buffer at varying pH values and NaCl concentrations to a final protein concentration of 4 mg/ml, then mixed with 400 μm ThT aqueous solution and added to a black Corning 96-well plate. The same buffer without protein was used as a negative control. The ThT fluorescence was recorded every 5 min at 25 °C using a 440 nm excitation filter and a 490 nm exmission filter.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For analysis of amyloid fibrils, samples (4 μl) were adsorbed onto 400-mesh copper grids, stained with 0.75% uranyl formate in 50% ethanol for approximately 20 s, and then examined and photographed using a Hitachi H7100 electron microscope at 75 kV.

Shear-induced fiber assembly

The TuSp1-sv and TuSp1-NC proteins were dissolved in 20 mm Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0), and the concentrations were adjusted to 2 mg/ml. The samples were incubated at 25 °C for 24 h in 10 ml reaction tubes with overhead rotation at 20 r.p.m. Upon observation of visible fibrous structures, the fibers were collected and analyzed on glass slides using optical microscopy.

Fiber pulling procedures

Silk fibers were directly drawn from purified protein solutions in 20 mm Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) at 25 °C. Fiber formation was carried out as previously described (20). Briefly, 50 μl of protein solution (∼1 mg/ml) was placed on a glass slide, and silk fibers were manually pulled from the solution using fine tweezers at a speed of approximately 3 mm/s.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Dry fibers were placed on stubs, coated with gold for 30 s, observed, and imaged using a Hitachi SU8010 instrument (Japan) at an accelerator voltage of 5 kV at 25 °C.

Tensile testing

Prior to tensile testing, 1 cm-long fibers were mounted on a 10 × 10 mm mechanical testing frame made of cardstock, and the fiber diameter was measured at three equidistant points along the longitudinal axis using light microscopy. Mechanical tests were conducted at 25 °C and 55% relative humidity using an Agilent T150 UTM instrument (Agilent Technologies) equipped with a 5 μN load cell, at a strain rate of 3% s−1. Engineering stress and strain at break, toughness, and Young’s modulus were calculated using Agilent NanoSuite software. Mechanical data are reported as mean values ± standard deviation.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy experiments were performed using a Nicoletln10MX FTIR instrument (Thermo Fisher). Each measurement consisted of a 64-scan interferogram with a resolution of 4 cm−1. The amide I band (1600–1700 cm−1) was used to estimate secondary structure contents through curve fitting, as described in previous studies (20). A seven-point Savitzky-Golay filter with a second-derivative function was applied to resolve 11 Lorentzian peaks corresponding to different secondary structure components in the amide I region. The secondary structural content was calculated as the percentage of the respective peak areas, as previously described (41). In brief, the deconvoluted peaks between 1605 to 1640 cm−1 were assigned to β-sheet structures. Random coil peaks were observed between 1641 and 1655 cm−1. The α-helix and β-turn peaks were located at 1656 to 1662 cm−1 and 1663 to 1695 cm−1, respectively.
