Novel UHMWPE sleeve for stabilizing loose suture anchors in rotator cuff repair
Authors: Che-Kuang Lin, Fu-Shan Jaw, Sing-Soon Sam, Tiing-Foong Siow, Shyi- Chun Yii, Yin-Jen Chang, Tai-Horng Young
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Suture anchors are frequently used for repairing torn rotator cuffs. However, post-operative anchor loosening is a common complication often caused by low bone density. The purpose of this study was to evaluate if a novel UHMWPE cortex locking sleeve could improve the anchor pullout strength after revision in synthetic bone. The initial failed anchor fixation was simulated by inserting a 4.5 mm anchor and pulling it out of the bone, which created an enlarged insertion hole for the revision anchor. After revision, two loosening conditions were simulated; (i) intra-operative anchor loosening where the revision anchor was directly pulled out from the block after insertion, and (ii) post-operative anchor loosening where the revision anchor was first subjected to cyclic loading and then pulled out. For both loosening conditions, the pullout strength and energy absorbed was recorded for the following configurations: (i) Group A, same-diameter anchor (SDA; 4.5 mm × 10 mm); (ii) Group B, larger-diameter anchor (LDA; 5.5 mm × 10 mm; ); and (iii) Group C, same-diameter anchor combined with the novel sleeve (SDA + NS). For intra-operative anchor loosening, the highest pullout strength was recorded in the SDA + NS group, with a mean value of 370.077 ± 32.699 N, which was significantly greater than both SDA and LDA groups (p < 0.05). Similarly with post-operative anchor loosening, the SDAP + NS group had a significantly higher pullout strength (311.114 ± 28.239 N) than the other groups. Using the UHMWPE sleeve with same diameter anchor as the initial insertion produced a significantly greater pullout strength than the revision with a larger diameter anchor but no sleeve.

Introduction

Rotator cuff tears are common shoulder injuries and are often associated with pain and restricted range of motion [1–3]. Metal suture anchors are commonly used for arthroscopic rotator cuff repair to provide a fixed support for the rotator cuff tendon to be stretched back to the original anatomical position on the humerus [4, 5]. Traditional anchors have a threaded body for insertion into the bone, so the density of the surrounding bone is an important factor influencing the anchor’s stability [6, 7]. Most rotator cuff tendon tears occur in the supraspinatus tendon and the reattachment point for tendon repairs is usually between the greater tuberosity and the articular cartilage of the humeral head [8]. However, the cortical bone in this region is less than 1.5 mm thick [9], and hence it can be difficult to achieve sufficient purchase for a secure anchor fixation.

Archor loosening is frequently reported following rotator cuff tendon repairs, commonly caused by poor bone quality, large pilot holes, anchor design, and over-tensioning of the repaired tendon [10, 11]. A loose anchor indicates that the original pilot hole has been compromised and techniques have been developed to salvage such cases, such as using a larger anchor to plug the hole, changing the anchor insertion location, cement augmentation, or using a threaded Steinmann pin [12]. However, the limited space presents challenges to changing the anchor insertion location or using a larger anchor. Cement augmentation also presents a risk of bone necrosis [13].

Our team developed a novel spinning top-shaped sleeve (Fig. 1) made of ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) for insertion into the hole during revision surgery for a failed anchor fixation. The sleeve increases the contact area with the cancellous bone and the wings on the anchor open during screw insertion to contact the inner cortex. Our hypothesis was that the novel UHMWPE sleeve would enhance the anchor fixation strength when used for revision surgery. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the effects of the novel sleeve on the anchor pullout strength after an initial anchor loosening event.

Materials and methods

Test block preparations

This study used composite synthetic bone (Sawbones, Pacific Research Laboratories, Vashon, WA) with a porous block and dense upper layer to replicate a large cancellous body with a dense cortical surface. To simulate a healthy human humerus, the porous foam body was 40 mm thick with a density of 0.24 g/cm3 (15 PCF, polyurethane block) and the upper surface was 2.0 mm thick with a density of 0.64 g/cm3 (40 PCF, polyurethane block) [14].

Two anchor loosening conditions were simulated in this study. First, to model intra-operative (acute) anchor loosening, a 4.5 mm × 10 mm metal suture anchor (TwinFix™ Ti 4.5, Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) was inserted vertically into a synthetic test block to a depth of 10 mm. The anchor was then completely pulled out from the test block using an MTS machine at a displacement rate of 1 mm/s. The resulting hole after the pullout test was defined as the intra-operative anchor loosening condition. This process was repeated five times using separate anchors and the average value was considered as the primary pullout strength for the intra-operative baseline (BL-IO) group.

Second, to model post-operative anchor loosening, a 4.5 mm × 10 mm TwinFix™ metal suture anchor was inserted vertically into the synthetic test block with an insertion depth of 10 mm. The anchor was then subjected to cyclic tensile loading along its longitudinal axis, applied uniaxially for 200 cycles between 0 N and 60 N [15, 16]. After cyclic loading, the anchor was pulled out from the test block at a displacement rate of 33 mm/s. The resulting hole after the pullout test was defined as the post-operative anchor loosening condition. This process was repeated five times using separate anchors and the average was considered as the primary pullout strength for the post-operative baseline (BL-PO) group.

The baseline pullout strengths (BL-IO and BL-PO) were used as reference values for comparison with the corresponding revision techniques.

Pullout test following revision for intra-operative anchor loosening

To evaluate the strength of the revision following intra-operative anchor loosening, three revision groups were investigated: (i) Group A, same-diameter anchor (SDA; 4.5 mm × 10 mm; TwinFix™ Ti 4.5, Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA); (ii) Group B, larger-diameter anchor (LDA; 5.5 mm × 10 mm; TwinFix™ Ti 5.5, Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA); and (iii) Group C, same-diameter anchor combined with the novel UHMWPE sleeve (SDA + NS; 4.5 mm × 10 mm; TwinFix™ Ti 4.5, Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA). Each setup was tested five times using separate anchors and the energy absorbed to failure was determined as the area under the load-displacement curve.

Pullout test following revision for post-operative anchor loosening

Similarly, to evaluate the strength of the revision following post-operative anchor loosening, three revision groups were investigated: (i) Group A, same-diameter anchor (SDAP; 4.5 mm × 10 mm; TwinFix™ Ti 4.5, Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA); (ii) Group B, larger-diameter anchor (LDAP; 5.5 mm × 10 mm; TwinFix™ Ti 5.5, Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA); and (iii) Group C, same-diameter anchor combined with the novel sleeve (SDAP + NS; 4.5 mm × 10 mm; TwinFix™ Ti 4.5, Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA). Each setup was tested five times using separate anchors and the energy to failure was calculated as the area under the load-displacement curve.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons among anchor configurations were performed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When a significant main effect was identified, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test to control for multiple comparisons. For groups in which catastrophic failure occurred during cyclic loading, the specimens were excluded from the corresponding statistical comparisons. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 for all analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using commercially available statistical software (SPSS, version 29; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Baseline pullout strength

The initial baseline pullout strength of the 4.5 mm × 10 mm metal suture anchor tested in intact synthetic bone was 258.216 ± 16.955 N for the intra-operative condition (BL-IO) and 181.695 ± 18.257 N for the post-operative condition (BL-PO). The pullout strength of the anchor after cyclic loading was approximately 70% of the intra-operative value, reflecting the detrimental effect of cyclic loading on anchor fixation.

Revision for intra-operative anchor loosening

Revision with the same-diameter anchor (SDA) resulted in a significantly lower pullout strength (18.769 ± 18.127 N) than the baseline condition (BL-IO). In contrast, revision using a larger-diameter anchor (LDA) achieved a pullout strength (305.994 ± 19.268 N) comparable to or exceeding the baseline.

The highest pullout strength was achieved when using the same-diameter anchor combined with the novel sleeve (SDA + NS), with a mean value of 370.077 ± 32.699 N, which was significantly greater than both SDA and LDA groups (p < 0.05). These results indicate that the novel sleeve markedly enhanced the intra-operative anchor fixation strength after revision.

Revision for post-operative anchor loosening

No pullout strength data was obtained for the SDAP group as the anchor pulled out of the bone block during cyclic loading due to lack of engagement between the screw thread and the enlarged bone tunnel. Using a larger diameter screw achieved a pullout strength (LDAP: 184.811 ± 21.157 N) similar to the baseline post-operative condition (181.695 ± 18.257 N). The SDAP + NS group demonstrated a significantly higher pullout strength (311.114 ± 28.239 N) than both BL-PO and LDAP.

Statistical analysis

One-way ANOVA revealed statistically significant differences among revision techniques within both intra-operative and post-operative loosening conditions (p < 0.05). Post hoc analysis confirmed that the sleeve-augmented groups (SDA + NS and SDAP + NS) exhibited significantly greater pullout strengths than anchor-only revision techniques. All statistical comparisons were conducted with n = 5 specimens per group, and results are reported as mean ± SD.

Discussion

In addition to bone quality being a major factor affecting the fixation strength of bone anchors, other mechanical considerations such as anchor design, anchor size, and suture tension can also affect the success rate and the risk of anchor loosening [6, 7]. In orthopedics, bone cement is often used to improve the bond between an implant and surrounding cancellous bone [13]. However, for rotator cuff repairs, studies have reported humeral head necrosis following the application of bone cement and the exothermic heat from the cement may cause thermal damage to surrounding tissues [17]. To overcome these drawbacks, we developed a novel plug sleeve for use in revision surgery to stabilize the anchor by providing a firm interface between the anchor and cancellous bone. The results showed that using the sleeve increased the pullout strength of the anchor in both intra-operative or post-operative conditions.

When simulating a revision case of intra-operative anchor loosening, the anchor with a sleeve (SDA + NS group) had the highest pullout strength among the three groups, and was approximately 20% greater than the LDA group. For the SDA + NS group, in all cases the anchor failed by pulling out from the sleeve due to the firm hold the wings on the sleeve maintained within the bone block (Fig. 2). The energy absorbed to failure in the SDA + NS group was higher than in other groups. In addition, the load-displacement curve for SDA + NS showed a greater displacement of the maximum pullout strength. This indicates that firm anchor fixation was maintained for longer periods during pullout. The SDA group had the lowest pullout strength and energy absorption to failure among all revision groups. Visual inspection of the anchor after pullout did not show any continuous residual block material around the thread. This is likely because the initial anchor created a void after being forcibly pulled out, so the revision anchor with the same diameter could not achieve a firm hold in the bone block. Notably, the SDA group demonstrated substantially lower pullout strength than the BL-IO group and showed a wide range of individual pullout values, from 2.37 N to 46.41 N. A similar trend was observed for energy absorbed to failure, with the SDA group showing markedly lower values than the BL-IO group. These findings can be explained by the variable degree of material loss, tunnel enlargement, and structural damage around the anchor tunnel after intra-operative anchor loosening. Once the surrounding material adjacent to the anchor threads was disrupted, insertion of a revision anchor with the same diameter could not reliably achieve sufficient thread engagement with intact test-block material. As a result, the mechanical fixation of the SDA group became highly dependent on the remaining local tunnel structure, leading to both reduced pullout strength, reduced energy absorbed to failure, and greater inter-specimen variability. In the SDAP group, cyclic loading after postoperative anchor loosening likely produced more extensive tunnel damage; consequently, the revision anchor could not achieve sufficient thread purchase, and pullout strength and energy absorbed to failure could not be reliably measured. This interpretation is consistent with previous biomechanical findings showing that suture anchor pullout strength is strongly influenced by bone quality, anchor design, and the amount of effective thread engagement within the surrounding substrate [18, 19]. Manon et al. [20] developed a medial-lateral expansion plastic sleeve concept for pedicle screw fixation. The pullout strength of all revision models following post-operative anchor loosening was less than the value for the corresponding revision model for intra-operative loosening. The pullout strength of the SDAP + NS group was significantly higher than the LDAP group, and the pullout strength could not be recorded for the SDAP group because the screw threads did not sufficiently engage with the bone tunnel. Postoperative bone tunnel enlargement is a known condition following rotator cuff repair [21]. In our study, the cyclic loading likely caused a greater loss in block material around the bone tunnel, so the revised implants failed at lower forces post-operatively. For the SDAP and LDAP groups, the difference in pullout strength between the intra-operative and post-operative simulation was over 40%. Large diameter anchors are commonly required for revision surgery, primarily because of bone tunnel enlargement or poor bone quality around the implant. Unfortunately, larger anchors may not always be feasible because of anatomical restrictions or a need for more than one anchor in the region. A biomechanical study by Kawakami et al. [22] showed that the minimum distance required between anchors without negatively affecting the pullout strength was 6 mm for rotator cuff repair. Notably, our novel sleeve design does not require a larger anchor and was found to improve the anchor pullout strength for revision procedures when using the same diameter anchor.

There are some limitations to this study. First, the study was conducted using synthetic bone blocks with homogenous and isotropic properties which cannot fully replicate the anisotropic and viscoelastic properties of natural bone. Synthetic bone is commonly used for pullout testing [14, 20, 22] because it offers a consistent and reproducible structure that cannot be achieved with natural bone. Second, a single commercially available anchor design was assessed in this study, which may not be fully representative of other anchor designs. Third, the pullout test was performed per ASTM standard and may not reflect physiological conditions. However, this approach ensures the test methods are standardized and can be reproduced by others for comparison. Fourth, this study evaluated 4.5-mm and 5.5-mm threaded anchors because these diameters are commonly used in clinical practice, based on the authors’ experience. This selection was also intended to reflect the clinical rationale of preserving bone stock while maintaining additional options for potential future revision procedures. Future studies should quantitatively characterize post-loosening anchor tunnel morphology and evaluate additional sleeve dimensions including 5.0-mm and 5.5-mm configurations.to determine the appropriate sleeve-anchor configuration for different defect sizes and revision conditions. In addition, the insertion angle and depth of the anchors were standardized in this study to ensure consistency, but in practice these factors may vary and are determined by the surgeon. Finally, although UHMWPE has been extensively used in joint replacement protheses, the long-term in-vivo behavior of UHMWPE in anchors is still unknown. These factors were beyond the scope of this biomechanical study but are being considered for a follow up animal study.

Conclusion

For revision after anchor loosening, our novel UHMWPE sleeve was shown to improve the pullout strength when larger diameter anchors were not feasible in both intra-operative and post-operative conditions. The sleeve may reduce the need for larger diameter anchors in revision surgery and can maintain a secure fixation for rotator cuff repair.
