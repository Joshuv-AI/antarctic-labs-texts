Acoustic Scene-Aware Processing and Auditory Model-Based Compensation Strategies
Authors: Torsten Dau, Tobias May
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite significant advances in our understanding of human hearing and assistive hearing technologies, the benefits of hearing loss compensation continue to vary widely across individuals. A central challenge is the complexity and heterogeneity of hearing loss, whose perceptual consequences often extend far beyond reduced sensitivity. Conventional strategies rely on signal processing algorithms—such as spatial filtering, noise reduction, and dynamic range compression—to improve audibility and enhance target signals. These components are usually optimized in isolation, yet their combined effects may interfere with one another and therefore do not necessarily yield an overall benefit. More recently, machine learning techniques have been used to further improve the performance of individual components. To tailor compensation strategies to specific acoustic environments or listeners, various steering mechanisms have also been proposed, guided by acoustic cues, audiovisual input, or listener attention. While these approaches show promise, a consistent and objective computational target for optimization has yet to be established. As an alternative, auditory model-based strategies, increasingly combined with machine learning, have emerged. These approaches aim to minimize the discrepancy between simulated auditory representations of normal and impaired hearing, thereby providing a physiologically motivated optimization goal. Although both categories of strategies offer considerable potential, achieving effective compensation under real-time, real-world conditions remains a major challenge. This paper reviews opportunities and limitations of these approaches for individualized hearing aid compensation.Keywords: Hearing aids, Hearing loss, Auditory models, Scene-aware processing, Model-based compensation, Speech-in-noise, Machine learning, Auditory profiling, Suprathreshold deficits, Individualized fitting

Introduction

People with normal hearing are remarkably skilled at navigating complex acoustic environments. Whether in a busy café or a crowded meeting, the auditory system can isolate a voice of interest while suppressing competing sounds [1, 2]. This ability relies on sophisticated processing along the peripheral and central auditory pathways, which support sound source segregation and selective attention—even under adverse conditions such as background noise or reverberation [3–6]. Hearing impairment disrupts these mechanisms, making it considerably harder to focus on a target sound in the presence of competing inputs [2, 7]. A central challenge in auditory research is therefore to understand the specific deficits caused by hearing loss and to develop strategies that restore—or at least approximate—the perceptual robustness of the normal hearing system [8, 9].

Despite major advances in hearing aid technology over the past two decades, many people with hearing loss continue to struggle in complex listening situations. Even when audibility is restored through amplification, the benefit varies widely across individuals [10–14]. This variability likely stems, at least in part, from an incomplete understanding of the diverse ways in which hearing loss affects auditory perception. The most common form—sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL)—is highly heterogeneous, reflecting differing underlying cochlear pathologies such as outer or inner hair cell damage, synaptopathy, or metabolic dysfunction [15–17]. These pathologies affect not only hearing thresholds, as reflected in the audiogram, but also suprathreshold auditory functions such as loudness growth, frequency resolution, temporal precision, and spatial hearing [18–26]. Consequently, effective compensation may need to be tailored to subpopulations with common perceptual distortions—or ideally, to an individual’s specific auditory profile [23, 24, 27].

Even the most advanced strategies often fall short of restoring normal auditory function, in part because we still lack a full understanding of how hearing aid signal processing interacts with impaired auditory systems [13, 28, 29]. Different deficits may require distinct processing solutions, and when several coexist, their combined perceptual consequences can be hard to predict [24, 30, 31]. Nonlinear compensation presents challenges: aided hearing involves a cascade of two nonlinear systems—the signal processing algorithm and the impaired auditory system—making it difficult to emulate the dynamic response properties of the normal hearing system across the range of everyday inputs [32–34].

Traditional hearing aid processing is typically built from modular components such as dynamic range compression, directional microphones, and noise reduction algorithms. These modules are generally developed and optimized in isolation, with a primary focus on addressing peripheral deficits by amplifying soft sounds and compressing loud ones to accommodate the reduced dynamic range of the impaired ear [35, 36]. Some strategies are biologically inspired—for example, fast-acting multiband compression to mimic cochlear compression loss, or spectral enhancement to compensate for reduced frequency selectivity [22, 37, 38]. However, the perceptual benefits of such approaches remain modest, particularly for speech-in-noise [39–41].

To overcome these limitations, recent developments are shifting toward more context-aware and individualized approaches. Rather than relying on fixed parameter settings, modern systems increasingly incorporate control signals that adapt hearing aid behavior in real time to both the acoustic environment and user-specific needs. Two promising directions are scene-aware processing and auditory model-based compensation. Scene-aware algorithms classify the listening environment and adjust processing accordingly—for example, applying stronger noise reduction and directional processing in a noisy restaurant, while prioritizing sound quality in quiet or musical settings. These adaptive strategies build on conventional modules by steering them according to inferred scene characteristics [42–45]. Scene classification is typically driven by supervised or unsupervised machine learning that extracts features such as signal-to-noise ratio, modulation depth, or spatial configuration [46–49].

In contrast, auditory model-based compensation employs computational models of hearing to guide signal processing. These models simulate both normal and impaired auditory function and can predict how an algorithm alters internal auditory representations [50, 51]. The central idea is to minimize the discrepancy between modeled responses of impaired and normal hearing systems, thereby providing a physiologically grounded optimization target—something not available in scene-aware processing. Another distinction lies in the optimization strategy: conventional components are often developed in isolation, and their combined effect may not reflect the sum of individual improvements. Model-based approaches seek to optimize enhancement and compensation jointly, considering the full processing chain in relation to auditory outcomes. They also offer a pathway to individualization: beyond audiograms, which capture little about supra-threshold deficits, auditory models can simulate individual impairments—such as reduced temporal precision or degraded spectral contrast—provided the model supports it. This makes it possible to design compensation strategies tailored to a listener’s auditory profile [52–57]. Such strategies can complement standard fitting methods, especially when amplification alone is insufficient [38, 58]. Finally, both scene-aware and model-based approaches have been substantially advanced by progress in machine learning, which provides powerful tools for classification, optimization, and adaptive real-time control [59–61].

This paper reviews the current state of scene-aware processing and auditory model-based compensation for hearing aids. Section "Scene-aware processing and steering strategies for hearing devices" examines scene-aware algorithms that dynamically adjust processing in response to changing environments. Section "Auditory model-based compensation strategies" explores model-based approaches, from biologically inspired compression schemes to closed-loop systems that integrate auditory modeling and machine learning. We emphasize data-driven methods across the processing chain, from target-speaker detection to individualized fitting, and conclude with limitations and directions for future work.

Scene-Aware Processing and Steering Strategies for Hearing Devices

Acoustic Scene-Aware Compression: Steering with Acoustic Features

The central idea of scene-aware processing is to adapt hearing aid behavior based on the acoustic characteristics of the environment, using a control or steering signal. This signal guides algorithms such as wide dynamic range compression (WDRC) and noise reduction by estimating the presence and properties of the target sound source. For example, WDRC parameters—such as release time constants—can be dynamically adjusted across frequency channels. Fast-acting compression may be applied in time-frequency regions dominated by the target, while slower release times may be used in noise-dominated regions to minimize distortion. In addition, nontarget components such as background noise or reverberation can be selectively attenuated to enhance signal quality and intelligibility.

Steering signals can be derived purely from acoustic features or from multimodal cues, including visual or attentional information. Figure 1 illustrates the general workflow of an acoustically driven steering system. The noisy mixture is transformed into the spectral domain using a short-time Fourier transform (STFT). The steering signal is then estimated from the STFT representation and used to dynamically adjust key WDRC parameters. A particular challenge for hearing aid users is maintaining speech intelligibility in reverberant environments, where temporal cues are smeared, and the contrast between speech onsets and pauses is reduced. [62] proposed a scene-aware WDRC strategy that uses the local direct-to-reverberant energy ratio (DRR) as a steering signal. Regions with high DRR (direct sound) were processed with fast-acting compression, while regions with low DRR (dominated by reverberation) were processed more conservatively. This approach preserved the spatial impression of the acoustic scene for normal hearing listeners (Fig. 1b), while simultaneously increasing the short-term audibility of the target source. By contrast, conventional fast-acting compression often produces broader, sometimes internalized sound images, as well as image-splitting artifacts.

In noisy environments, steering can be based on local SNR estimates. [63] proposed an SNR-aware WDRC framework in which release times are adapted according to the estimated SNR in each frequency band. Regions with high SNR (typically containing speech) are processed with fast-acting compression to improve audibility of soft phonemes, whereas low-SNR regions (dominated by noise) are processed with slower release times to avoid amplifying background noise. This hybrid approach retained the benefits of fast-acting compression while reducing its distortion. A perceptual evaluation compared scene-aware WDRC with conventional fast- and slow-acting compression in terms of speech intelligibility and subjective listener preference. As shown in Fig. 1c, hearing-impaired listeners demonstrated improved intelligibility with the SNR-aware strategy relative to slow-acting compression and reported higher preference ratings compared to both conventional systems [43].

Realistic acoustic environments often contain both noise and reverberation. To address this complexity, [42] proposed a generalized scene-aware framework that classifies the target speech—including early reflections—as foreground, while treating both background noise and late reverberation as background. This enabled a unified steering strategy across acoustically complex environments. [44] extended this framework and demonstrated that jointly accounting for noise and reverberation further improved perceptual outcomes and increased preference ratings among hearing-impaired listeners compared to conventional fast-acting compression. As shown in Fig. 1d, adding single-channel noise reduction (SCNR) as a preprocessing stage before WDRC further increased subjective preference across all strategies [45].

Audiovisual Scene Analysis: Steering from Sight and Sound

When a single talker is masked by noise or reverberation, steering based purely on acoustic cues is often sufficient [42, 62, 64]. In multitalker scenarios, however, acoustic features alone may be inadequate for reliably isolating the intended target. Acoustic scene analysis under such conditions is inherently challenging due to the ambiguity and overlap of competing sound sources, many of which are irrelevant to the hearing aid user’s communicative intent [1, 2].

To address these limitations, researchers have increasingly explored audiovisual (AV) scene analysis, leveraging the fact that humans naturally rely on visual information—such as lip movements and facial gestures—to support speech perception in complex environments [65–67]. Incorporating visual cues can significantly enhance the ability to identify the target speaker in crowded scenes where acoustic cues are insufficiently distinctive [68–70]. A striking demonstration of this potential is the “Looking to Listen” system developed by [71], which uses deep learning to align facial landmarks with the acoustic signal and thereby isolate a speaker’s voice from a mixture. By tracking a talker’s face and synchronizing lip movements with speech, this system achieves separation performance well beyond that of audio-only approaches. Although current commercial hearing aids do not include cameras, such work points toward future integrations with external devices—such as smartphones or smart glasses—that could enable vision-guided steering, automatically focusing on the person the user is looking at in group conversations.

A more hearing technology-focused example targeting hearing applications introduced an AV framework for speech source separation based on canonical correlation analysis (CCA) [72]. The goal was to learn robust, generalizable features that align auditory and visual modalities in a speaker-independent way—crucial for real-world scenarios where the system cannot be retrained for every user. Specifically, the authors combined acoustic modulation features, which capture the rhythmic structure of speech, with visual features derived from facial landmarks, such as lip and jaw movements. By applying CCA across a large set of talkers, they identified low-dimensional transformations of the input signals that consistently captured cross-modal correlations between what is heard and what is seen. Figure 2a illustrates this framework, showing the extraction of audio and visual features and their joint projection into canonical components, and Fig. 2b shows example canonical loadings that reliably link acoustic streams to the corresponding visual input. In experiments, this CCA-based AV representation was shown to robustly attribute separated audio streams to the correct talker in video recordings, supporting applications such as speaker identification, AV source attribution, and attention-guided selection in multitalker scenes.

For hearing technology, relevant applications include visually informed steering through integration with smartphone cameras or smart glasses [73, 74]. Such systems could help determine which of several separate audio streams correspond to the attended speaker. Since CCA is a linear method with low computational cost, it is potentially well suited for real-time implementation in hearing aids and wearable devices.

Brain-Guided Scene Analysis: Cognitive Steering Through EEG

While audiovisual scene analysis leverages external cues such as lip movements to guide signal processing, an even more direct approach is to monitor the listener’s cognitive state itself. This shift has motivated the development of brain-guided strategies that use electroencephalography (EEG) to decode auditory attention in real time. In controlled laboratory settings, EEG has been used to determine which talker a listener is focusing on within complex acoustic scenes. Neural responses—particularly in the low-frequency envelope-tracking range—tend to align more strongly with the attended speech stream than with competing inputs. This attentional modulation can be captured using advanced decoding techniques [70, 75], with several studies reporting decoding accuracies of 80–90%, demonstrating the robustness of neural speech tracking as a biomarker of auditory attention [68].

Building on these findings, [76] introduced a closed-loop system in which EEG signals directly informed a speech separation algorithm about the listener’s attentional focus. Their brain-informed speech separation (BISS) framework combines deep neural networks with EEG-derived attention markers to enhance the SNR of the attended speaker. Related work [77, 78] further demonstrated that EEG-informed source separation can improve intelligibility and reduce listening effort in simulated hearing aid environments.

A key advancement in this direction was presented by [79], who developed a real-time neurosteering system that enables hearing devices to enhance the talker a user is attending to, based directly on EEG signals. Their system integrates a real-time brain–computer interface with a stimulus–response model based on CCA for continuous EEG-based attention decoding (Fig. 3a). Combined with a multi-microphone hardware platform, the system performs speech separation via spatial beamforming techniques. [79] provided a detailed account of the system architecture, covering both the signal processing pipeline and hardware configuration, and demonstrated its feasibility through case studies in which listeners dynamically steered the acoustic focus between competing talkers using only their attentional intent (Fig. 3b). Importantly, the software implementation was made publicly available, supporting further research and replication. This work represents an important step toward practical, real-time cognitive control of hearing devices, where user intent—not just acoustic or audiovisual cues—guides the listening experience.

While real-time, wearable implementation of EEG-based attention decoding remains challenging, recent advances in miniaturized biosensing technologies—such as earbud-integrated EEG systems [80, 81]—make this vision increasingly realistic. Incorporating the user’s mental focus directly into the processing loop extends traditional scene-aware strategies and provides a promising, user-driven approach to the longstanding cocktail party problem: enabling hearing devices to selectively enhance not only the most prominent sound but specifically the sound the listener intends to follow.

Principal Limitations of Scene-Aware Processing

Noise reduction algorithms typically pursue a well-defined computational goal: estimating clean speech from noisy mixtures. In contrast, compression-based strategies such as wide dynamic range compression (WDRC) lack such a clear optimization target. WDRC does not operate relative to a normative reference framework but is instead tuned through behavioral listening experiments or optimized using physical or perceptually motivated metrics, such as the speech intelligibility index (SII), the hearing aid speech perception Index (HASPI), or measures of speech envelope fidelity [28, 82, 83]. Consequently, the effectiveness of compression must often be evaluated indirectly, either through extensive perceptual validation or by correlating performance with objective measures.

A central complication is that sensorineural hearing loss is associated with reduced or absent cochlear compression [20, 84]. Yet there is no universally accepted definition of what constitutes the “optimal” output of a compression algorithm. In principle, nonlinear preprocessing that mimics normal cochlear compression could help restore more natural auditory perception in individuals with hearing loss [85]. However, numerous studies have reported that fast-acting, multichannel compression provides only limited improvements in speech intelligibility and may even degrade critical cues [86–88]. In particular, fast compression can flatten temporal amplitude contrasts, reduce speech envelope fidelity, and introduce distortion products that interfere with perception, especially in noisy environments. Demonstrating consistent perceptual benefits of fast-acting compression therefore remains difficult and often requires time-consuming listening experiments or reliance on indirect correlations with objective metrics.

These limitations have motivated the development of auditory model-based compensation strategies, which are discussed in the following section. By simulating internal auditory representations for both normal hearing (NH) and hearing-impaired (HI) listeners, such models provide a physiologically grounded computational target. The goal is to preprocess acoustic signals so that the resulting internal representations in an impaired system resemble those of a healthy system. This framework opens the door to more principled, individualized, and potentially real-time optimization of hearing aid processing [51, 89, 90].

Auditory Model-Based Compensation Strategies

Auditory model-based compensation begins with a fundamental question: What exactly should hearing aid processing aim to restore or optimize for a listener with hearing loss? Traditional fitting approaches primarily focus on restoring audibility—making soft sounds audible and normalizing loudness growth—guided largely by the audiogram, which reflects hearing thresholds across frequencies. While this strategy improves access to sounds, it does not fully address suprathreshold deficits such as reduced frequency selectivity, impaired temporal resolution, or abnormal loudness perception, all of which can significantly affect speech understanding and listening effort [18–20, 23, 24, 27].

Auditory modeling offers a principled framework for characterizing these perceptual deficits and guiding the development of signal processing strategies that move beyond empirical gain prescriptions. Early “deficit-inspired” strategies—such as fast-acting compression to mimic lost cochlear compression or spectral sharpening to compensate for broadened auditory filters—showed only limited success in improving speech intelligibility or user satisfaction [91, 92]. These limitations have motivated a shift toward more integrated, model-based approaches aimed at restoring internal auditory representations to more closely resemble those of normal hearing listeners.

Matching Internal Representations Using Auditory Models

A key strategy involves running auditory models in parallel: one representing the normal hearing periphery (including cochlear filtering, compression, and neural encoding), and one representing the impaired system. Signal processing is then optimized so that the simulated response of the impaired-ear model (after processing) approximates the simulated response of the normal hearing model (without processing). In this framework, the hearing aid functions as an inverse processor, compensating for distortions in auditory encoding caused by hearing loss [51, 93, 94].

This approach can be implemented in iterative optimization frameworks, where the algorithm adapts processing parameters to minimize the difference between the two model outputs. For example, if the impaired-ear model simulates reduced excitation for a particular speech sound due to a high-frequency dead region, the algorithm may amplify or otherwise modify the relevant spectral content until the output aligns with that of the normal hearing model. Such strategies enable individualized compensation that reflects nonlinear loudness growth, masking effects, and temporal dynamics—factors typically absent from static, audiogram-based fittings.

Examples include model-informed frequency-lowering strategies that improve access to high-frequency speech cues in individuals with sloping high-frequency loss [95, 96], as well as joint optimization of gain and noise reduction parameters using auditory models to maximize simulated speech intelligibility in noise [32, 97].

Integrating Auditory Models into Machine Learning Frameworks

A recent and promising development is the integration of auditory models into deep learning (DL) systems. By embedding an auditory model within the loss function of a neural network, hearing aid processing algorithms can be trained to minimize differences between the outputs of normal hearing and impaired-hearing models. In this approach—referred to as model-based learning—the network adjusts parameters such as gain, compression, or filtering based on differences between the simulated internal representations of the two auditory systems [54, 56].

This setup enables supervised training even without access to clean reference signals—an advantage in real-world listening environments where clean speech is often unavailable. Differentiable auditory models, such as dCoNNear [55], simulate cochlear and auditory nerve responses using fully differentiable DNNs, making them compatible with gradient-based optimization. Embedding dCoNNear within an end-to-end training loop enables joint optimization across several objectives, including speech intelligibility, sound quality, and listening comfort. Multiobjective loss functions can balance factors such as auditory-nerve fidelity, spectral-contrast preservation, and artifact minimization. Perceptual metrics like HASQI [98] can also be incorporated directly into the training.

Early implementations of DL-based hearing loss compensation (HLC) primarily targeted clean listening conditions, optimizing the match between normal hearing and hearing-impaired model outputs (Fig. 4a). More recent extensions explicitly incorporate environmental degradations such as noise and reverberation, enabling joint optimization for both compensation and noise reduction by minimizing their combined impact on internal auditory representations. One limitation of many existing models is their lack of adaptability: most rely on a fixed mapping between input and output, restricting their usefulness across different user profiles, preferences, and listening situations. A notable exception is the multitask DNN framework proposed by [99], which outputs two parallel signal streams—one optimized for noise reduction and the other for hearing loss compensation. During inference, users can dynamically adjust the balance between the two streams, providing direct control over the trade-off between audibility restoration and noise suppression (Fig. 4b).

A major strength of the model-based approaches lies in their ability to transcend traditional, modular hearing aid architectures. Rather than treating noise reduction, dynamic range compression, and signal enhancement as separate building blocks, auditory model-based compensation enables holistic optimization by embedding a model of hearing impairment directly into the processing framework. This unified approach allows the system to address multiple perceptual goals simultaneously—including audibility restoration, speech intelligibility, noise suppression, and sound quality—without the need for independently tuned subcomponents. In principle, this opens the door to truly individualized, perception-driven hearing aid strategies optimized not only for a listener’s specific auditory profile but also for the acoustic environment.

Towards Integrated, Perception-Driven Hearing Aid Strategies

The model-based compensation strategies described above primarily rely on simulations of relatively peripheral auditory representations, such as those after cochlear filtering, compression, and auditory-nerve encoding. In principle, this approach is appealing: if peripheral representations could be perfectly compensated, one might expect perception to be restored as well. However, detailed peripheral models are highly complex and strongly nonlinear, making perfect compensation in a differentiable modeling framework unattainable. This raises the question of which deviations between normal and impaired-hearing model outputs at the peripheral level are truly critical for perception.

A natural extension is therefore to shift the focus towards auditory models that directly predict perceptual outcomes (Fig. 5a). One way to achieve this is to use internal representations from candidate models in a forward-prediction paradigm: before deploying a model for compensation, its utility is evaluated by asking how well its internal representations account for human performance in key perceptual tasks. These tasks may include speech intelligibility, sound quality ratings, or more basic perceptual measures involving temporal, spectral, spatial, or intensity resolution—tested across both normal hearing and hearing-impaired listeners. Such forward-prediction frameworks need not reproduce every physiological detail. Instead, they must capture the essential transformations in auditory processing that shape perceptual behavior. If a model’s internal representations are sufficiently detailed to predict perceptual outcomes—for example, the impact of a hearing deficit on speech intelligibility—then the model may be deemed suitable for use in model-based optimization.

Beyond peripheral and cortical representations, recent modeling work has begun to incorporate processing stages extending into the midbrain and brainstem, including mechanisms related to efferent feedback and adaptive gain control. For example, models incorporating inferior colliculus representations and feedback-driven modulation have been shown to account for level-dependent encoding and context-sensitive response properties that are difficult to capture with purely feedforward peripheral models [102]. Similarly, recent extensions of auditory modeling frameworks toward midbrain processing and adaptive population coding suggest new pathways for linking neural representations to perceptual robustness in complex scenes [103]. While such models are not yet widely integrated into hearing aid compensation schemes, they represent an important future direction for forward-prediction frameworks, particularly in the context of adaptive and closed-loop strategies that aim to account for central gain control and efferent influences.

Over the past decades, several model frameworks have been developed to explain behavioral performance across a wide range of psychoacoustic tasks, including signal detection in quiet and in noise, auditory masking, and perceptual resolution in different domains [93, 100, 104–108]. A prominent example is the “effective” auditory processing model [100], which successfully predicted amplitude-modulation detection and other psychoacoustic measures by simulating signal transformations along the auditory pathway followed by an ideal-observer decision stage. Speech intelligibility modeling has likewise evolved from classical metrics such as the speech transmission index (STI; [109]) and the speech intelligibility index (SII; [110]) to perceptually informed frameworks. Examples include the speech-based envelope power spectrum model (sEPSM; [83, 111–113]), the framework for auditory discrimination experiments (FADE; [114, 115]), and models incorporating explicit spectro-temporal modulation analysis [116]. More recently, physiology-inspired approaches have been introduced that link auditory-nerve encoding to speech intelligibility, including stochastic auditory-nerve models [117] and models of envelope and temporal fine-structure coding [50, 51, 118].

Together, these frameworks demonstrate that internal representations closer to perception—rather than purely peripheral simulations—provide a stronger link to observed behavior. Conceptually, such perceptually informed models could be embedded into closed-loop compensation strategies, in which processing is optimized to minimize the mismatch between simulated outcomes for impaired and normal hearing systems. Importantly, however, such forward-prediction frameworks have not yet been tested in closed-loop hearing loss compensation but may represent a promising complement to existing strategies. A key step in this direction is to use perceptual-outcome models in forward-prediction tasks with established auditory profiling batteries (Fig. 5b). For example, the BEAR test battery [23, 24, 27, 101] captures suprathreshold deficits across multiple perceptual domains. Demonstrating that a perceptual model can reproduce these outcomes in hearing-impaired listeners provides a stringent benchmark of its adequacy and complexity. Such validation is particularly relevant for machine learning-based frameworks, where the richness of learned representations must be balanced against interpretability and generalizability. Forward-prediction testing therefore offers a principled way to verify whether models used within compensation strategies capture perceptually relevant features before being embedded into closed-loop applications.

One limitation of many existing psychoacoustic models is that they were developed and validated primarily with simplified stimuli (e.g., pure tones or narrowband noises) and highly idealized tasks. More recently, efforts have shifted toward modeling perception under more realistic conditions. For example, [119] developed a generalizable auditory perception model by training a deep neural network (DNN) to recognize speech in diverse ecological conditions using simulated auditory-nerve representations as input (Fig. 6a). Although optimized solely for task performance, the model arrived at human-like solutions, capturing trends observed in real-world speech-in-noise listening and basic psychoacoustic phenomena. Follow-up work [120] demonstrated that the model strikingly captures perceptual behaviors, including modulation detection and masking (Fig. 6b), suggesting that the DNN may have learned internal transformations functionally similar to elements of traditional perceptual frameworks, such as modulation filterbank processing. Once validated in forward-prediction tasks, such models can provide richer, task-optimized predictors of auditory behavior. By freezing their parameters after training, they can be repurposed as generalized perceptual-outcome models forming the basis for model-in-the-loop compensation strategies. Crucially, these strategies rely on representations that are closer to perception than those based solely on peripheral stages, thereby opening the door to more robust, individualized, and ecologically valid compensation schemes.

Discussion

Acoustic scene-aware processing and auditory model-based strategies—often supported by machine learning—represent complementary avenues for advancing hearing aid signal processing. As outlined in section "Scene-aware processing and steering strategies for hearing devices", scene-aware systems steer established processing modules such as WDRC or noise reduction using acoustic, audiovisual, or neural control signals. Section "Auditory model-based compensation strategies" described model-based approaches that embed computational models of hearing into the optimization process, either by aligning impaired internal representations with normal hearing references or by predicting perceptual outcomes more directly.

While both approaches aim to improve real-world listening, they differ in their primary optimization targets and modes of adaptation. Scene-aware strategies adapt processing primarily to the listening context, exploiting moment-to-moment information about the acoustic or multimodal scene and the characteristics of the target signal. Importantly, these systems typically build on processing blocks that are already individualized to some extent—for example through audiogram-based gain prescription in WDRC—but their adaptive behavior is largely target- or scene-specific, with limited scope for deeper individualization beyond standard clinical measures. In contrast, auditory model-based strategies aim to adapt processing explicitly to the listener, using internal auditory representations or predicted perceptual outcomes as computational objectives, and thus offer a clearer pathway toward compensation that goes beyond the audiogram. In practice, these approaches need not be mutually exclusive and may ultimately be most effective when combined.

To make these conceptual differences more explicit, Table 1 provides a comparative overview of scene-aware processing and auditory model-based compensation across several dimensions relevant to research, implementation, and clinical translation. The comparison highlights differences in optimization targets, degree of individualization, computational demands, validation status, and clinical readiness. Importantly, it illustrates that the two approaches address complementary aspects of the hearing aid problem: scene-aware strategies excel at robust, context-dependent adaptation in real time, whereas model-based strategies offer a principled route toward listener-specific, perception-driven optimization. This complementarity also suggests that hybrid solutions may be advantageous, rather than treating the approaches as competing alternatives.

Scene-aware strategies already demonstrate tangible benefits. As shown in section "Scene-aware processing and steering strategies for hearing devices", scene-aware compression can improve subjective preference compared with conventional fast-acting compression, and combined noise-reduction and compression systems can approach the performance of idealized reference systems operating on clean targets. A further advantage lies in their practical feasibility: these strategies extend well-established processing blocks and can be integrated into real-time systems within the constraints of wearable devices.

Auditory model-based strategies offer a more unified framework by addressing audibility, intelligibility, noise suppression, and sound quality within a single optimization criterion. This is a promising direction, but important questions remain open. It is still unclear how much physiological detail is required for auditory models to yield perceptual benefit, which deviations between normal and impaired-hearing representations are perceptually most relevant, and how competing goals—such as noise reduction versus preservation of temporal and spatial cues—should be balanced without compromising transparency or controllability.

Importantly, not all biologically inspired or model-driven strategies have translated into clear perceptual benefits. Early deficit-specific approaches—such as spectral sharpening to compensate for reduced frequency selectivity or aggressive fast-acting compression to mimic cochlear nonlinearity—often yielded modest or inconsistent improvements and, in some cases, introduced new distortions that outweighed potential gains. These mixed outcomes highlight that restoring individual auditory features in isolation does not necessarily restore robust perception, particularly when multiple suprathreshold deficits coexist.

Generalization and robustness remain critical challenges for any learning-based approach [121]. Although large and diverse training datasets can improve average performance, everyday listening often involves ambiguous or rapidly changing conditions—such as overlapping talkers, nonstationary noise, or unexpected transients—that may strain trained systems. Evaluation should therefore extend beyond conventional metrics such as predicted intelligibility or SNR improvement and explicitly test behavior under deliberately challenging or out-of-distribution conditions. This issue is particularly pressing for neurosteered systems based on EEG attention decoding, where even rare misclassifications can be highly disruptive. Practical deployment will likely require mechanisms for uncertainty detection, graceful fallback strategies, and stabilization across time.

Relatedly, many optimization and evaluation frameworks rely heavily on predicted intelligibility metrics such as SII, HASPI, or sEPSM. While these measures provide valuable objective benchmarks and are indispensable for large-scale optimization, they capture only selected aspects of speech perception and may not fully reflect listening effort, comfort, spatial awareness, or sound quality. Consequently, improvements predicted by such metrics do not always translate into subjective benefit. Direct behavioral validation with hearing-impaired listeners therefore remains essential, particularly for individualized strategies where small mismatches between model predictions and perceptual experience may be amplified.

Multiobjective optimization adds further complexity. Hearing aid users value not only intelligibility but also naturalness, comfort, localization, and music enjoyment. Aggressive noise reduction may improve intelligibility while degrading listening comfort or spatial awareness. Multitask formulations offer a principled way to balance such trade-offs, but the relative weighting of objectives will likely need to be individualized since objective gains do not always align with subjective preferences. Supporting intuitive user control and enabling gradual personalization—through clinician input or user-driven adaptation—appear particularly important for real-world acceptance.

Beyond algorithmic performance, substantial barriers to clinical translation remain. Auditory model-based strategies often require individualized measurements beyond the standard audiogram—such as loudness growth, spectrotemporal sensitivity, or auditory profiling batteries—which may increase demands on clinical workflows. In addition, integrating algorithms developed in academic settings into proprietary hearing aid architecture poses challenges related to computational resources, power consumption, latency, and regulatory approval. Regulatory frameworks for AI- and ML-based medical devices increasingly emphasize transparency, robustness, and validation across diverse populations and real-world conditions, posing additional hurdles for highly individualized approaches trained on limited datasets. These considerations suggest that real-world adoption is likely to proceed incrementally, with hybrid solutions and decision-support tools preceding fully autonomous model-driven compensation.

Individual variability further complicates the picture. Similar audiograms may conceal very different suprathreshold deficits, underscoring the need for individualized models that incorporate listener-specific measures. Forward-prediction validation—requiring models to reproduce perceptual profiling outcomes before being used for closed-loop compensation—may serve as an important safeguard. In practice, human-in-the-loop fine-tuning through clinician guidance or user feedback is likely to remain essential, with machine learning supporting gradual personalization during everyday use.

In summary, scene-aware and auditory model-based strategies represent two complementary approaches that may enrich the toolbox of hearing aid signal processing. Scene-aware steering already shows practical benefit and is close to real-world application, whereas model-based strategies offer a more unified and physiologically grounded perspective but remain at an earlier stage of clinical readiness. Rather than prescribing a single future direction, this review highlights conceptual frameworks and emerging evidence that may help advance individualized and robust hearing aid compensation.

Summary and Conclusion

This paper reviewed two complementary approaches to advancing hearing loss compensation in hearing aids: acoustic scene-aware processing and auditory model-based strategies. Scene-aware approaches aim to improve listening by steering established signal processing modules, such as compression or noise reduction, according to features of the acoustic, audiovisual, or cognitive environment. These systems typically build on audiogram-based individualization but adapt primarily to the characteristics of the scene or target signal. Auditory model-based approaches, in contrast, embed computational models of hearing directly into the optimization process, seeking to align impaired auditory representations with normal hearing references or to predict perceptual outcomes more directly. Both strands have been strongly stimulated by recent advances in machine learning and share the broader goal of providing more individualized and context-sensitive forms of compensation.

Looking ahead, central challenges lie not only in refining algorithms but also in establishing clear and robust links between processing objectives and perceptual benefit. For scene-aware systems, this entails understanding when and how steering improves perception across diverse real-world listening conditions and ensuring reliable operation when acoustic or multimodal cues are uncertain or ambiguous. For model-based strategies, it requires careful calibration and validation of auditory models against behavioral data so that reductions in model-based discrepancies can be interpreted as meaningful perceptual gains. More broadly, validation under ecologically realistic conditions, the use of profiling batteries to capture suprathreshold variability, and frameworks that incorporate user preferences and clinician input will be essential to anchor these approaches in real-world benefit.

Taken together, these perspectives point toward promising directions for hearing aid signal processing that is both context-aware and perception-driven—capable of adapting flexibly to environmental demands while remaining aligned with the capacities and needs of the individual listener. The approaches discussed here are not intended as prescriptive solutions, but rather as conceptual and methodological avenues for further exploration. Ultimately, the success of any compensation strategy will be judged by the extent to which hearing aid users experience greater ease, comfort, and effectiveness in everyday communication.
