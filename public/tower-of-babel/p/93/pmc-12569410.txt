Critical Reappraisal and Validation of the Bern Score System for Diagnosing Spontaneous Intracranial Hypotension
Authors: Soyoun Choi, Michelle Sojung Youn, Young Hun Jeon, Mi Ji Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeDiagnosing spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) remains a clinical challenge. The Bern score offered a structured scoring system for estimating the probability of spinal extradural cerebrospinal fluid leaks. We aimed to validate the Bern score in an East Asian cohort from a headache clinic.MethodsPatients with SIH were recruited from 2022 to 2023, and age- and sex-matched controls with primary headache and normal brain MRI were included. All SIH patients underwent brain MRI and spine MR myelography before epidural blood patch. The Bern score was assessed and validated by two neurologists and one neuroradiologist. We evaluated its diagnostic performance with a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and visualized the temporal profile of Bern scores using a LOESS (locally weighted scatterplot smoothing) graph.ResultsA total of 45 patients with SIH and 45 age-sex-matched controls were included. The ROC curve showed moderate diagnostic accuracy (area under the curve 0.775, 95% confidence interval 0.675–0.874; sensitivity 42.2%, specificity 95.6%) using the original cutoff of 3. Using a revised cutoff was ≥2, the Youden index was highest (sensitivity 66.7%, specificity 84.4%, accuracy 75.6%). Still, its performance remains suboptimal. The Bern score declined after 50 days from symptom onset.ConclusionsThe Bern score showed high specificity but limited sensitivity in our cohort, indicating it should not be used alone as a screening tool. A lower cutoff may improve its diagnostic performance, and timing from symptom onset should be considered when interpreting the score.Keywords: spontaneous intracranial hypotension, diagnostic imaging, reliability and validity, sensitivity, specificity

INTRODUCTION

The diagnosis of spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) remains a significant clinical challenge. Factors contributing to its complexity include the mismatch between symptomatic (brain) and etiologic (spine) sites, a wide spectrum of clinical presentations, and the heterogeneity in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak mechanisms. Notably, three distinct types of CSF leaks have been described, each requiring a specific diagnostic modality.

The Bern score, introduced by Dobrocky et al.1 in 2019, offered a structured scoring system to assess probability of having spinal CSF leak based on contrast-enhanced brain MRI findings and thus prioritizing the need of invasive spinal study. While the Bern score offered a balanced information in assessing the brain MRI by including measures of intracranial structural displacement (e.g., suprasellar cistern distance and prepontine distance) in addition to SEEPS (subdural fluid collections, enhancement of the pachymeninges, engorgement of the venous sinuses, pituitary hyperemia, and sagging of the brain).2 However, relying solely on the Bern score to determine the likelihood of SIH may have limitations of low sensitivity3,4,5,6,7 and the limited temporal stability of the imaging findings.8,9,10 In addition, the original study by Dobrocky et al.1 was originated from a cross-sectional analysis of a European neurosurgical cohort. Therefore, we propose that the diagnostic value of the Bern score should be re-examined in different ethnicity (Asian), setting (headache clinic), and with the context of the disease’s temporal progression. In this study, we aimed to validate the Bern score in our cohort, assess its temporal profile, and discuss its limitation and propose a better interpretation of Bern score.

METHODS

Patient recruitment

We prospectively recruited 45 treatment-naïve patients with SIH at Seoul National University Hospital between April 2022 and June 2023. Additionally, we extracted age- and sex-matched controls from a prospective headache registry. Patients with primary headache disorders (migraine [n=22], trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia [n=7], new daily persistent headache [n=6]) and normal brain MRI were matched to patients and included as controls. Patients with SIH was diagnosed based on clinical history and the presence of at least one radiological abnormality, extradural fluid collection on noninvasive MR myelography or brain MRI findings strongly suggestive of SIH, pachymeningeal enhancement or subdural hygroma or hematoma of nontraumatic origin. All patients met the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd Edition (ICHD-3) criteria for SIH and had radiological evidence of CSF leakage.11 Only patients with brain MRI acquired in an EBP-naïve state were included. Patients with a recent history of dural puncture within the preceding month were considered to have post-dural puncture headache and excluded from this study. Patients suspected of SIH but with negative findings on both brain MRI and noninvasive MR myelography were also excluded. All patients provided written informed consent for participation in the registry study with prospective follow-up. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (approval number: 2205-026-1322).

Data collection

We collected clinical data, including demographic information (age, sex), date of symptom onset, dates from symptom onset to brain MRI, and the presence of spinal extradural fluid collections on noninvasive MR myelography. Headache onset time upon becoming upright and peak headache severity, assessed using the numerical rating scale (NRS), were recorded before epidural blood patch (EBP), 3 days after EBP, as well as during serial outpatient follow-up visits at 1 month and 3 months. Any orthostatic symptoms observed during the 3-month outpatient follow-up were also documented. Details of the EBP procedures (number, dates, and locations) were meticulously recorded.

All patients underwent brain MRI and spine MR myelography before EBP (SIGNA Premier, GE HealthCare; Magnetom Skyra, Siemens Healthineers; Magnetom Verio, Siemens Healthineers). Brain MRI was performed using 1.5T or 3T scanners with contrast enhancement, including pre- and postgadolinium T1-weighted sagittal images and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery axial images, as well as post-gadolinium T1-weighted axial and coronal images. Spine MR myelography was obtained using a 3.0T MR scanner with sagittal and axial T2-weighted images, both with and without fat suppression, along with 3D heavily T2-weighted images.

Application of Bern score

The Bern score is a three-tier predictive scoring system defined by six key brain MRI findings.1 The score was calculated using T1-weighted sagittal, coronal, and axial images.1 The findings include venous engorgement (2 points), pachymeningeal enhancement (2 points), a suprasellar cistern distance of ≤4 mm (2 points), subdural fluid collection (1 point), effacement of the prepontine cistern of ≤5 mm (1 point), and a mamillopontine distance of ≤6.5 mm (1 point). The total score classifies patients into low (2 points or fewer), intermediate (3–4 points), or high (5 points or more) probability categories for SIH.1

Pretreatment brain MRI was evaluated to calculate the Bern score. A single investigator (Sy.C.) assessed the Bern scores of all patients and controls. Another neurologist (M.S.Y.) and a neuroradiologist (Y.H.J.) independently rated the Bern scores of 10 randomly selected patients to validate the interobserver reliability of the scoring. The agreement among the raters demonstrated excellent reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient=0.914, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.848–0.944).

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations (SD) and ranges. For categorical variables, numbers and percentages are reported. Student’s t-tests were performed to assess statistical significance for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables.

A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated using the Bern score to evaluate its diagnostic performance for SIH. The sensitivity and specificity of the original cutoff point suggested by Dobrocky et al.1 were tested, and a new cutoff for diagnosing SIH was determined by identifying the Youden index12 for each Bern score value within our cohort.

A locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) graph was generated using R software to visualize the temporal progression of Bern scores. The span value was set to 0.9, and a second-degree polynomial regression was applied. The peak point was identified by determining the maximum Bern score and the corresponding disease duration.

The components of the Bern score were analyzed in the interquartile ranges from symptom onset to brain MRI to assess their temporal changes. The prevalence of each component was calculated across the quartiles.

A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp.) and R software version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

The mean age was 40.3 years (SD 10.6 years, range 23–68 years) for the SIH and 39.9 years (SD 10.4 years, range 23–68 years) for the control groups. Specifically, the control group consisted of 22 patients with migraine, 7 with trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia, 6 with new daily persistent headache, and 10 with other diagnoses (4 post-COVID headache, 2 dizziness, 1 visual phenomena, 1 tinnitus, 1 otalgia, and 1 epicranial headache), all of whom had normal brain MRI. The average duration from symptom onset to brain MRI in the SIH group was 88.3 days (SD 181.6 days, range 1–766 days). The mean peak headache severity was 8.9 (SD 1.0, range 4–10) on the NRS. Regarding the onset time of orthostatic headache, 26 patients (61.9%) experienced symptoms within 5 minutes of becoming upright, 12 (28.6%) within 1 hour, and 2 (4.8%) within 4 hours of becoming upright. Additionally, 2 patients (4.8%) reported experiencing headaches predominantly in the second half of the day. Extradural CSF was observed on MR myelography in 43 patients (95.6%) with SIH. The two patients with negative MR myelography findings had abrupt-onset orthostatic headaches and brain MRI finding of pachymeningeal enhancement. Bern score comparison between the groups

Significant differences were observed in the distribution of Bern score profiles between the SIH group and the control group (Table 2). The mean Bern score was 2.7 (SD 2.2, range 0–8) in the SIH group and 0.6 (SD 0.9, range 0–3) in the control group. In the SIH group, the Bern scores were classified as low in 26 patients (57.8%), intermediate in 9 patients (20.0%), and high in 10 patients (22.2%). In contrast, the control group had low Bern scores in 43 patients (95.6%) and intermediate scores in 2 patients (4.4%). In the SIH group, 7 patients experienced complications: 6 had subdural hemorrhage (SDH) and 1 had cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT). Among the patients with SDH, 4 were in the high Bern score group, while 2 were in the low Bern score group; the patient with CVT was in the low Bern score group.

Sensitivity and specificity of Bern score

The ROC curve demonstrated an area under the curve of 0.775, indicating moderate diagnostic accuracy (95% CI 0.675–0.874, p<0.001) in distinguishing patients from controls (Fig. 1). When applying the original cutoff points in our study, a Bern score of 3 exhibited a sensitivity of 42.2% and a specificity of 95.6%, while a Bern score of 5 showed a sensitivity of 22.2% and a specificity of 100%. Using the Youden index, we identified the optimal cutoff point at a value of 2 or higher, which showed a sensitivity of 66.7% and a specificity of 84.4%.

Fig. 3 shows the prevalence of abnormalities in Bern score components according to the time from onset to MRI acquisition. Overall, pachymeningeal enhancement, suprasellar cistern, prepontine distance, and mamillopontine distance findings exhibited the lowest prevalence during the first quartile (initial 6 days after onset), the highest prevalence in the second quartile (7–23 days after onset), followed by declining prevalence in the third and fourth quartiles. Subgroup analyses

There were no statistically significant differences in time-related and procedure-related variables or headache characteristics between the low and intermediate/high Bern score groups (Table 3). Non-significant trends were observed in time-related variables: the time from symptom onset to brain MRI (125 days vs. 38 days), time to first EBP (105 days vs. 29 days), and time to last EBP (165 days vs. 62 days) were numerically longer in patients in the low Bern score group compared to those in the intermediate and high Bern score groups.

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to validate the Bern score as a diagnostic tool for SIH in an independent cohort of Korean patients recruited from a tertiary headache clinic. While the original Bern score was developed and validated in a European cohort, subsequent studies have primarily focused on American populations. One study validated the score in patients with CSF leaks using decubitus CT myelography, and another used lateral decubitus digital subtraction myelography.13,14 Additionally, a recent study evaluated the specificity of the Bern score in normal patients.15 However, no study has assessed both sensitivity and specificity in an East Asian cohort. This highlights the novelty and clinical significance of our findings.

In our cohort, the Bern score demonstrated only moderate diagnostic accuracy. Traditional cutoff of 3 or 5 yielded a high specificity but low sensitivity in our cohort. Our findings indicate that a cutoff score of 2 showed a better performance, sensitivity 66.7% and specificity 84.4%, which is still suboptimal for use as a screening tool.1 Additionally, in our cohort, the majority of patients with SIH presented with a low Bern score, even though the diagnosis was confirmed with MR myelography. The Bern score may change over time, with each component showing temporal patterns, and the overall score increasing during the first 50 days then declining thereafter. Given this temporal pattern, brain MRI may be more informative if obtained within the first 120 days of symptom onset.

In our study, a high Bern score was associated with high specificity for SIH, but a low Bern score did not necessarily correspond to a low likelihood of SIH. Previous studies have reported low sensitivity of MRI in screening for SIH, with pachymeningeal enhancement observed in 56%–59%, subdural fluid collection in 31%–36%, and brain sagging in 44%–51% of patients with SIH.3,4,16 Our results suggest that a low Bern score cannot exclude the possibility of SIH. A cutoff score of 2 yielded the highest Youden index in our cohort; however, this threshold has limitations. A score of 2 can be reached by a single dominant finding, such as pachymeningeal enhancement, rather than a combination of subtle abnormalities. Moreover, the sensitivity of 66.7% remains insufficient for use as a screening tool. Considering the limitations of the revised cutoff, a low Bern score alone should not be used to rule out SIH. While the Bern score was originally developed to guide decisions on invasive imaging, MR myelography allows reliable detection of extradural CSF collections, particularly in type 1 leaks. Accordingly, MR myelography should be considered in patients with clinical suspicion of SIH, even when the Bern score is low. If MR myelography shows extradural fluid collection, invasive studies may be considered for precise localization. This stepwise approach may help reduce false negatives and improve diagnostic accuracy.

Another significant finding was that, in our cohort, the majority of patients with SIH were classified into the “low Bern score” group when applying the original Bern score system. This contrasts with the original study, where the majority of patients were classified as having a high probability of a spinal CSF leak.1 This discrepancy suggests that the scoring system may require further validation in different study cohorts. Several factors could explain the differences between cohorts. First, the timing of brain MRI may have influenced the results, as SIH-related findings change significantly over time.9,10,17,18 The interval from symptom onset to imaging should be considered when interpreting the Bern score. Second, the reported sensitivity for detecting CSF leaks ranges from 41% to 75% for CT myelography19,20,21 and from 86% to 94% for MR myelography.22,23,24,25,26 MR myelography has demonstrated superiority over CT myelography in detecting slow leaks and those along the nerve sleeves,27 and including these types of leaks may have unmeasured effects on the brain imaging outcomes. Third, our patients were primarily recruited from a headache clinic, encompassing a broader clinical spectrum and potentially capturing milder or atypical cases of SIH, whereas Dobrocky et al.’s study1 recruited from a neurosurgery department, potentially including patients with more severe radiological or neurological complications. Finally, anatomical differences associated with ethnicity may have contributed to the observed imaging discrepancies. Therefore, our study suggests that the Bern score may need to be redefined to represent patients with broader spectrum of disease severity.

Our study found no significant difference in headache severity between the low and intermediate/high Bern score groups. This finding suggests that a low Bern score does not necessarily indicate a “milder” form of SIH. In our cohort, 2 of the 6 patients with SDH were in the low Bern score group, and the only patient with CVT also had a low Bern score. This indicates that fatal complications can occur even with a low Bern score. Clinicians should not underestimate the severity of the disease in patients with a low Bern score and should consider further evaluation in suspected cases of SIH, regardless of the Bern score. Our LOESS graph illustrates that the Bern score changes over time, initially increasing and then declining. Each component of the Bern score follows its own time course, suggesting a limited window for applying the Bern score to screen for SIH. According to the Monro–Kellie doctrine, the sum of brain, CSF, and intracranial blood volumes remains constant. When CSF leaks, intracranial blood volume increases, leading to “reactive” changes in brain MRI, such as venous engorgement, pachymeningeal enhancement, and pituitary hyperemia.28 However, over time, physiological compensation may occur, possibly through CSF overproduction or by reducing the amount of CSF leakage,29 causing reactive changes 18 and intracranial structural displacement to diminish. Therefore, the role of MRI in diagnosing SIH may be limited after the acute phase.

Recent studies have proposed novel brain MRI-based approaches to complement the limited sensitivity of conventional imaging in SIH. These include diffusion tensor imaging, which assesses subtle white matter changes, and choroid plexus volumetry as a potential marker of CSF dynamics. While these approaches may provide useful adjunctive information, they remain indirect, and their clinical applicability has yet to be clearly established.30,31 In addition, ultrafast dynamic CT myelography has demonstrated comparable diagnostic accuracy to digital subtraction myelography in localizing ventral dural defects.32 Taken together, the diagnosis and evaluation of SIH require a multifaceted approach that integrates both brain and spinal imaging modalities, along with an understanding of their respective limitations.

Our study has strengths in validating the Bern score in patients with confirmed SIH within our own cohort, thereby underscoring the diversity of the SIH spectrum as observed in brain MRI. However, our study has limitations regarding generalizability, as it was conducted in a single university hospital with participants of a single ethnicity. Additionally, the accuracy of the LOESS approach may improve with a larger sample size in future studies. Although our control group comprised patients with primary headache disorders rather than healthy controls, their Bern score distribution showed a similar pattern to that of the original Bern study (low: 43 vs. 56, intermediate: 2 vs. 3, and high: 0 vs. 1). As the Bern score is based on brain MRI findings, it reflects the modality’s limited sensitivity in detecting SIH. Additionally, the use of both 1.5T and 3T scanners may have contributed to variability in image quality. Therefore, future studies should include a larger and more ethnically diverse group of participants to ensure a more robust analysis.

In conclusion, our study suggests that the Bern score may have limited sensitivity in diagnosing patients with SIH, highlighting the need for caution when using it alone as a screening tool. To address this issue, a lower cutoff than the original may improve diagnostic performance, and time factors should be considered when interpreting the Bern score before excluding the probability of SIH.
