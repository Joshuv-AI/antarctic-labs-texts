Introduction

Olfactory dysfunction (OD) is a common symptom [1], and the prevalence of OD is around 22.2 % [2] in humans. Although it rarely endangers life, OD can affect the quality of patients’ lives in multiple aspects, such as the inability to enjoy the pleasure brought by fragrance, the difficulty in identifying spoiled food, and the failure to detect potential hazardous chemical gases. Even the social activities, daily work, and mental health of patients are also affected by OD. OD can be caused by multiple factors, such as infections of the upper respiratory tract [3], traumatic brain injury [4], chronic rhinosinusitis [5], neurodegenerative diseases [6], senescence [7], and so on. It is a direct clinical symptom of nasal-related diseases and an early indication of many other diseases. Accurate assessment of OD is essential for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of these diseases. The insidious onset and slow progression contribute to the difficulty of early diagnosis of OD. The small death of olfactory receptor neurons (ORNs) and the occurrence of OD in the unilateral nasal cavity [8] are also factors of difficulties in OD early assessment. Psychophysical Smell Testing and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) are commonly used in the diagnosis of OD. However, the subjectivity and high cost are not beneficial for applying olfactory assessment. New assessment, such as molecular biomarker detection, may be a valuable and complementary method in the early diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring of OD.

As a cAMP-binding protein, olfactory marker protein (OMP) is a soluble acidic protein expressed abundantly in mature ORNs, which are the beginning of olfaction. Increasing evidence shows that OMP in essential in the early stages of olfactory signal transduction. cAMP is the second messenger of olfactory transduction. OMP can produce cAMP-signaling by activating the Gαs-protein coupled receptors [9], modulating the kinetics of cAMP [10] and acting as a cAMP reservoir by increasing the basal cAMP concentration and enhancing the actions of tonic cAMP [11]. With the help of cAMP, OMP is involved in sharpening the odorant response profile, accelerating the odorant response kinetics of ORNs, and targeting the olfactory bulb of ORN axons [11]. OMP can speed up the signal transduction of ORNs or terminate the transduction of olfactory signal swiftly and maintain the sensitivity of ORNs [12]. At the same time, the lack of OMP abolishes the differences between ORNs, which express different olfactory receptors, making different odors difficult to distinguish [13]. In addition, OMP can also provide energy for the ciliary movement during the olfactory signal transduction by interacting with other adenosine nucleotides, such as ATP, ADP, and AMP [14]. In conclusion, OMP plays a critical regulatory role in the physiology of ORNs. However, the conventional detection methods, such as immunohistochemistry and fluorescence quantitative PCR, cannot meet the large-scale, rapid and sensitive requirements of OMP clinical detection. Therefore, developing specific, sensitive and convenient methods for OMP quantification is urgently needed in OD clinical detection.

Multicomponent Nuclear Acid Enzyme (MNAzyme) is a self-assembled multi-nucleic acid molecular polymer that can cleave the signal nucleic acids without an enzyme catalytically. The cleavage procession of MNAzyme relies on target-activated and metal ion-assisted. A typical catalytic core structure of MNAzyme consists of partzyme A (PA) and partzyme B (PB). When the nucleic acid target, the assembling facilitator, is present, the catalytic core assembles to cleave the nucleic acid substrate into two fragments. In this procession, the amplification of the target signal is achieved. MNAzyme has been widely used for its excellent stability and easy preparation in nucleic acid detection and clinical therpy [15]. The lateral flow biosensor based on the MNAzyme amplification system [16] can sensitively detect mycotoxin in food samples. The asymmetric MNAzyme probes can be used in general Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) assay [17]. Furthermore, under laser irradiation, the nanomachine based on MNAzyme can regulate the expression of HSP and PTEN, leading to a significant inhibition in pancreatic tumor [18]. However, the application of MNAzyme is limited due to the limited signal amplification efficiency. MNAzyme is often combined with other nucleic acid amplification techniques to improve signal sensitivity.

Isothermal Self-Primer EXPonential Amplification Reaction (SPEXPAR) was a novelty invention in recent years. The target sequence specifically recognizes and binds to the hairpin molecule, causing a conformational change of the hairpin. As a template, the hairpin generates a new nucleic acid sequence at its 5′ end under the action of DNA polymerase without additional primers. The new nucleic acid product is cut off under endonuclease and released from the hairpin. The target sequence is also released from the hairpin in the amplification process. The target and the product can specifically recognize and recombine with the hairpin again, continuously promoting the reaction cycle, and finally a large number of nucleic acid products are produced. Due to its clever design, SPEXPAR has become a new star in isothermal amplification reactions. SPEXPAR can be used as a sensitive method in detecting of the typical swine fever virus and thrombin [19]. The SPEXPAR reaction was also applied to detect the demethylase of fat mass and obesity related protein [20]. However, the value of SPEXPAR in the combined application needs to be further explored.

Herein, a new fluorescent biosensor based on SPEXPAR and MNAzyme was constructed to detect OMP in this study, named the Dual targets driven SPEXPAR assisted MNAzyme (D-S-M) fluorescent biosensor. Two sequences in the OMP gene, OMP 1 and OMP 2, were selected as the targets. When they existed alone or simultaneously, the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor can both be activated. First, we prepared the dual targets driven metastable hairpin@magnetic beads (D-MH@MBs) by binding the dual targets driven metastable hairpin (D-MH) of SPEXPAR to the surface of streptavidin magnetic beads (SA-MBs) through the interaction between biotin and avidin. The subsequent reaction SPEXPAR occurred on the surface of this material. D-MH@MBs can be specifically recognized by OMP 1 and/or OMP 2. Next, the SPEXPAR reaction was activated and the nucleotide products were amplified. Then, unlike the typical MNAzyme reaction, the amplicons of SPEXPAR acted as the catalytic core structure of MNAzyme to assemble with the substrate ssDNA. The 5′ and 3′ end of ssDNA were modified with the fluorescence reporter group FAM and the quenching group BHQ1, respectively. Finally, ssDNA was cleaved into two fragments, and the fluorescence signal was produced. The qualitative and quantitative analysis of OMP can be measured by the analysis of the fluorescence intensity. This new fluorescent biosensor of the OMP detection was sensitive, specificity and stable with a low detection limit of 1.06 pM. It had successfully quantified the OMP expression level in human nasal swab samples, which may provide a possible and valuable auxiliary method in the clinical diagnosis of OD.

Experimental section

Materials and instruments

All the sequences in this work were purchased from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and were shown in Table S1. The ssDNA and D-MH were modified by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). DNA polymerase Klenow, endonuclease Nb.BbvCl, 10 × NEB buffer and DNA marker (25–500 bp) were bought from TaKaRa (Tokyo, Japan), 10 × TBE buffer, DEPC-treated water, dNTP, 6 × loading buffer, 30 % acrylamide, TEMED, Ammonium persulfate substitute (APS), NaCl, MgCl2 were obtained from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). YeaRed Nucleic Acid Stain was purchased from Yisheng Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). SA-MBs were purchased from Suzhou Beaver Biomedical Engineering Co., Ltd (Suzhou, China). The serum and nasal swab samples were provided by volunteers from Nantong University Affiliated Hospital (Nantong, China). The non-denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis was performed by Bio-Rad Mini-Protean and Bio-Rad PowerPac (Bio-Rad, USA) and imaged on a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP imaging analyzer (Bio-Rad, USA). The fluorescence signals were captured by a RF-6000 spectro fluorophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan).

The preparation of D-MH@MBs

The core of SA-MBs was Fe3O4, which was coated with polystyrene. 2 mg/mL SA-MBs and 2 μM D-MH-biotin were rotational mixed in 1 mL Buffer I (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 1 mM EDTA, 1 M NaCl, 0.01 %–0.1 % Tween-20) at room temperature 30 min. D-MH@MBs was rinsed with Buffer I 3 times and obtained by magnetic separation.

The SPEXPAR reaction

100 nM D-MH@MBs, 0.1 U/μL DNA polymerase Klenow, 0.1 U/μL endonuclease Nb.BbvCl, 0.25 mM dNTP, 1 × NEB buffer (1 mM Tris-HCl, 5 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM DDT, pH 7.9) and different amounts of OMP 1 and/or OMP 2 were mixed and incubated at 37 °C 1 h, and finally, the reactant was heated to 80 °C for 15 min to inactive the enzymes.

The MNAzyme reaction

100 nM FAM-ssDNA-BHQ1, 30 mM MgCl2 and different amounts of the SPEXPAR supernatant or OMP 1 + 2 were mixed and incubated at 70 °C 0.5 h.

Characterization of D-MH@MBs

The morphology and particle size of D-MH@MBs were analyzed by Particle Size Analyzer, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and Atomic force microscopic (AFM). Zeta potential analysis was used to measure potential changes on the surface of SA-MBs after D-MH modification. Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy analysis and Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrometer were used to analyze the composition differences between D-MH@MBs and SA-MBs.

The non-denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis

The images of the products of this strategy were evaluated by 20 % non-denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (NPAGE). The NPAGE was performed in 0.1 × TBE buffer at 100 V for 60 min, followed by 1 × YeaRed nucleic acid dye staining for 20 min and imaged on the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP imaging analyzer (Bio-Rad, USA).

Fluorescence measurement

The fluorescence signal of the MNAzyme reaction was measured by a RF-6000 spectro fluorophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). The results were recorded at an excitation wavelength of 490 nm and an emission spectrum of 500–700 nm.

Clinical sample analysis

The serum and nasal swab samples were provided by volunteers from Nantong University Affiliated Hospital (Nantong, China) with ethical approval. Blood samples (4–5 ml) were collected with serum separator tubes containing clot activation additive and a barrier gel (Vacuette, Kresmunster, Austria), then centrifuged at 1600g for 10 min, and stored at −80 °C until use. The cotton swabs of nasal swab were soaked in PBS buffer for 10 min, then PBS buffer in the cotton swab was squeezed out and stored at −80 °C until use. The fluorescence spectra was measured with the RF-6000 spectro fluorophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan) at the excitation wavelength of 490 nm and the emission spectrum of 500–700 nm.

Results and discussion

Principle of the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor

The principle of the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor is shown in Fig. 1. To expand the detection range of the OMP gene and improve the accuracy and sensitivity of the OMP detection, the hairpin D-MH was designed to recognize and bind two different sequences in OMP, OMP 1 and OMP 2 (Table S1). In our study, the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor can be activated by OMP 1 and/or OMP 2. The strategy of this multi target hairpin was also beneficial for the rapid detection of other diseases with multiple biomarkers. When the target was present, the hairpin D-MH was explicitly recognized and bound, then the conformation of D-MH changed. The zipper-like sliding of D-MH caused the specific complementary pairing between the 5’ end of D-MH, which was initially free, and the complementary sequence on D-MH. Next, with the D-MH own sequence as the template, the amplicon OMP 1 + 2 was extended out from the 5′ end of D-MH under the action of Klenow without the additional primers. Due to the sequence design of D-MH, there was a recognition site of endonuclease Nb.BbvCI at the 5′ end of OMP 1 + 2. Under the action of Nb.BbvCI, OMP 1 + 2 was cut off at this site and fell off from D-MH. The target was replaced in the extension process of OMP 1 + 2 and also fell off from D-MH. Based on the principle of base complementary pairing, the target and OMP 1 + 2 can recognize and recombine with D-MH again, leading to a continuous cycle of the SPEXPAR reaction and ultimately producing a large number of OMP 1 + 2. During this process, the signal of OMP 1 and/or OMP 2 was magnified through the amplification of OMP 1 + 2, while D-MH was not consumed. In our biosensor, SA-MBs was modified by D-MH through the interaction between biotin and avidin to form D-MH@MBs. D-MH@MBs was beneficial for the recognition and binding of target sequences with D-MH, and the improvement of the efficiency of SPEXPAR. Furthermore, D-MH@MBs can be recycled through magnetic separation, thus reducing the cost of the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor.

Schematic illustration of the principle of the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor for OMP detection. A. Workflow for detection of OMP in nasal swab; B. Isothermal Self-Primer EXPonential Amplification Reaction (SPEXPAR) amplification; C. The production of fluorescent signal by Multicomponent Nuclear Acid Enzyme (MNAzyme).

In our study, OMP 1 + 2 was designed as the catalytic core sequence of MNAzyme, and the ssDNA sequence was intended to serve as both the assembling facilitator and the substrate of MNAzyme. We intended to simplify the components and reduce the cost of this biosensor by this strategy. When the target was present, OMP 1 + 2 was amplified by the SPEXPAR reaction, and then according to the principle of base complementary pairing, they assembled with ssDNA to form an activated MNAzyme polymer, which cut the ssDNA into two segments. The two ends of ssDNA were modified with FAM and BHQ1, respectively. The detectable fluorescent signal was generated accompanied by the cleavage of MNAzyme on ssDNA. In briefly, the signal of OMP was amplified and changed into the detectable fluorescence signal based on the reactions of SPEXPAR and MNAzyme in the D-S-M fluorescence biosensor. The qualitative or quantitative analysis of OMP can be measured by the analysis of the fluorescence intensity.

Characterization of D-MH@MBs

To verify the successful preparation of D-MH@MBs, the zeta potential, particle size analyses, TEM, AFM, FT-IR spectrometer and UV–vis spectroscopy analysis were used. The TEM, AFM and particle size analyses showed that D-MH@MBs and SA-MBs were spheres with a relatively uniform size (Fig. 2A, B and C). The SA-MBs was approximately 304.9 nm in diameter. After modification with the hairpin D-MH, the D-MH@MBs was approximately 433.3 nm in diameter. The particle size of D-MH@MBs was larger than that of SA-MBs. It may be the result of the connecting of D-MH onto the SA-MBs. The results of zeta potential showed that the zeta potential was −32.6 mV in SA-MBs, and −35.0 mV in D-MH@MBs (Fig. 2D). The increase of negative charge indicated that the SA-MBs were successfully modified by D-MH. UV–vis spectroscopy analysis showed that the characteristic absorbance peak for SA-MBs was around 594 nm, and D-MH@MBs was around 568 nm (Fig. 2E), validating the difference of the composition between D-MH@MBs and SA-MBs. The FT-IR spectral features and major peaks for D-MH@MBs were similar to SA-MBs, however the peaks of D-MH@MBs were higher than SA-MBs at 2850, 2923 and 3312 cm−1 (Fig. 2F), which may due to the adding of –C–H, –OH and N–H through modification of D-MH. These results indicate that D-MH was bound to SA-MBs successfully.

Feasibility of the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor

The combinations between OMP 1/2 and D-MH were important to the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor. The ΔG of D-MH was-26.45 kcal/mol, while the ΔG of the heterodimer OMP 1:D-MH and OMP 2:D-MH were −57.31 kcal/mol and −53.86 kcal/mol, respectively. It suggested that the binding between OMP 1/2 and D-MH was feasible, which facilitated the subsequent isothermal amplification.

To verify the amplification feasibility of OMP by SPEXPAR, 20 % non-denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis was performed, and the results were shown in Fig. 3. In Fig. 3A, Lane 1 to Lane 3 represented the electrophoretic bands of OMP 1, OMP 2 and D-MH, respectively. Their migration rates varied due to the difference in the base pair size and the conformation of the nuclear acid. When no complementary binding happened between the target and D-MH, the SPEXPAR reaction was not activated, and no new band appeared, so only the bands of D-MH, OMP 1 and OMP 2 were found in Lane 5 and Lane 6. While in Lane 4, D-MH was complementary bind to the targets and incubated with other necessary components of SPEXPAR (Klenow, Nb.BbvCl, dNTP, and NEB buffer) at 37 °C 1 h, SPEXPAR were activated, and the band of OMP 1 + 2 (marked with a red box) was observed. The excess OMP 1, OMP 2 and D-MH bands were also found in Lane 4. We also investigated whether the SPEXPAR reaction can be triggered by OMP 1 or OMP 2 alone. Lanes 1–3 in Fig. 3B represented the SPEXPAR reactions triggered by OMP 1+OMP 2, OMP 1 and OMP 2, respectively (Table S1). It was found that the bands of OMP 1 + 2 appeared in all three lanes (marked with a red box), while the brightness of the OMP 1 + 2 band in lane 2 and lane 3 was significantly weaker than that in lane 1. The above results suggested that both OMP 1 and/or OMP 2 can initiate the SPEXPAR reaction, and double targets may significantly increase the amplification efficiency of SPEXPAR.

To verify the ability of self-assembling and catalytic cleavage of MNAzyme, we detected the fluorescence signal of the MNAzyme reaction mixture by fluorescence spectrofluorometer. In Fig. 3C, there was a strong fluorescence peak when all the components of MNAzyme were mixed (the curve marked in red), while no obvious fluorescence peak was observed in the MNAzyme mixture without OMP 1 + 2 or ssDNA (the curve marked in green and blue). It indicated that OMP 1 + 2 can combine with ssDNA to form a multi-nucleic acid molecular polymer with self-assembling and catalytic cleavage of the signal nucleic acids. The typical MNAzyme system consists of PA, PB, the assembling facilitator sequence, and the substrate sequence. In this study, due to the unique design of the sequences, PA and PB were both OMP 1 + 2 and ssDNA served as both an assembly facilitator and a substrate for cleavage. The components in the MNAzyme reaction were greatly simplified, resulting in a cost reduction. The cleavage ability of MNAzyme relied on Mg2+-assisted. In Fig. 3C, no obvious fluorescence peak was observed in the MNAzyme reaction mixture without MgCl2 (the curve marked in yellow), which was consistent with this character.

Furthermore, we combined SPEXPAR with MNAzyme to find out whether MNAzyme can be triggered by the products of SPEXPAR (Fig. 3D). In the MNAzyme mixtures without OMP 1 + 2 or SPEXPAR (the curve marked in green and yellow), we didn't observe an obviously fluorescence peak, while there was a strong fluorescence peak in the MNAzyme mixture with SPEXPAR (the curve marked in red). It suggested a successful activation of MNAzyme by SPEXPAR.

Conditions optimization of the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor

The SPEXPAR reaction was optimized to reduce the cost and increase the efficiency. Too high concentration of D-MH produced more non product bands, while too low concentration of D-MH led to a low amplification of OMP1+2, so 100 nM was chosen as the optimal concentration of D-MH (Fig. S1A). The optimal concentrations of dNTP and Klenow (Fig. S1B) were 0.1 U/μL and 0.25 mM, respectively. At these concentrations, they were enough to initiate the SPEXPAR reaction.

The system and condition of MNAzyme were also optimized. There was a significant fluorescence peak in the MNAzyme mixture with 100 nM ssDNA (the curve marked in red) (Fig. S1C), so 100 nM was chosen as the optimal concentration of ssDNA in this study. The concentration of Mg2+ is crucial for the efficiency of MNAzyme. We investigated the effects of the concentrations of Mg2+ from 2.5 mM to 40 mM on the fluorescence signal of MNAzyme. It was found that the fluorescence signal of the MNAzyme mixture reached its peak at the Mg2+ concentration of 30 mM (Fig. S1D). So the optimal concentration of Mg2+ was 30 mM. The temperature of MNAzyme is important for the binding and dissociation of OMP 1 + 2 with ssDNA. Therefore, we referred to the annealing temperature of OMP 1 + 2 and ssDNA and detected the fluorescence signals of the MNAzyme mixture at incubation temperatures ranging from 55 °C to 75 °C. It was found that as the incubation temperature raised, the fluorescence intensity increased, and the fluorescence intensity of the MNAzyme mixture reached its peak at 70 °C (Fig. S1E), so 70 °C was chosen as the optimal incubation temperature. The response time is of great significance for the clinical applications, so we investigated the fluorescence signals of the MNAzyme mixtures with different incubation time. It showed that the fluorescence signal of MNAzyme reached its peak after 0.5 h incubation (Fig. S1F), indicating that the cleavage of MNAzyme had been finished after 0.5 h incubation, so 0.5 h was chosen as the optimal time of incubation.

Performance evaluation of the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor

The performance of the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor based on SPEXPAR and MNAzyme was further investigated under optimal conditions. The fluorescence intensity was gradually enhanced as the concentrations of OMP 1 and OMP 2 increased from 1 pM to 10 μM (Fig. 4A). The line graph was drawn by the fluorescence intensity at 522 nm and the logarithm of the concentration of the target (lg C).There was a linear relationship between the fluorescence intensity and lg C when the target concentration was from 1 pM to 100 nM. This relationship was described by equation Y = 302.08X + 1467.6 (R2 = 0.9975) (Y was the fluorescence intensity at 522 nm, and X was the logarithm of the concentration of the target) (Fig. 4B). The slope (S) of the linear equation was 302.08. The standard deviation (N) of blank multiple determinations was 106.43. With the formula LOD = 3N/S, the limit of detection (LOD) was determined to be 1.06 pM. It was suggested that the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor showed a broad linear detection range and a low detection limit compared to the other biosensor based on the fluorescent generation by catalytic cleavage (Table 1). In our study, the LOD did not reach the fM level compared with ref. 23–26, 28. It may be caused by the components of the SPEXPAR reaction's buffer solution, such as Tris-HCl and NaCl. These components were necessary for the SPEXPAR reaction, while the background of MNAzyme may be increased by them (Fig. S2).

Moreover, the specificity of the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor was investigated with the OMP sequence and its mismatch sequences (Table S1), and the fluorescence intensity at 522 nm was shown in Fig. 4C. Compared with the fluorescence intensity of 10 μM mismatch sequences, the fluorescence intensity of 100 nM OMP was much higher. The fluorescence intensity was observed to be a significant further increase in the 5 μM OMP group. These results indicated that the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor had a higher specificity.

Feasibility of the clinical application of the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor

To verify the feasibility of the OMP detection in the clinical samples with the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor, we collected 12 serum samples and 11 nasal swab samples provided by volunteers from Nantong University Affiliated Hospital (Nantong, China) with ethical approval. In Fig. 4D, it was found that the median of the fluorescence intensity at 522 nm in the nasal swab samples (2307.5, interquartile range: 2054.6–2380.8) was significantly higher than that in the serum samples (1436.2, interquartile range: 1078.2–1734.9) (P < 0.0001). The concentrations of OMP in serum and nasal swab samples were calculated by equation Y = 302.08X + 1467.6 (R2 = 0.9975) (Y was the fluorescence intensity at 522 nm, and X was the logarithm of the concentration of the target), and it was shown that the concentrations of OMP in nasal swab and serum samples were 603.1 pM (interquartile range: 87.7–1054.5 pM) and 0.91 pM (interquartile range: 0.05–7.68 pM), respectively (Fig. 4E). There was a significantly difference in the concentrations of OMP between the nasal swab samples and the serum samples (P < 0.0001). OMP was expressed abundantly in mature ORNs in the nasal mucosa, so the concentration of OMP in the serum sample was much smaller than in our study's nasal swab sample.

Furthermore, the nasal swab samples of 12 OD patients and 13 healthy controls from Nantong University Affiliated Hospital (Nantong, China) with ethical approval were detected by the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor. In Fig. 4F, it was found that the median of the fluorescence intensity at 522 nm in OD patients (1767.0, interquartile range: 1691.3–1780.6) was significantly lower than that in the healthy controls (2316.6, interquartile range: 2118.5–2344.7) (P < 0.0001), and the concentrations of OMP in OD patients and healthy controls were 9.8 pM (interquartile range: 5.6–10.9 pM) and 646.4 pM (interquartile range: 142.8–800.8 pM) (P < 0.0001), respectively (Fig. 4G). In the future, we will increase the sample size to make the above data more accurate.

The above results suggested that the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor could identify and detect OMP in nasal swab samples. It may provide a valuable and complementary method in the early diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring of OD.

Conclusion

Detecting of OMP in nasal swabs may be a proper complementary method in the early diagnosis and treatment monitoring of OD. However, conventional techniques, such as immunohistochemistry and fluorescence quantitative PCR, are unsuitable for OMP detection in nasal swabs. In this study, we provided a specific and sensitive magnetic nanobead assisted fluorescent biosensor for detecting OMP in nasal swabs based on SPEXPAR and MNAzyme.

Compared with other SPEXPAR reactions, in our study, the hairpin of SPEXPAR, D-MH was designed to have two target recognized sites. Two different sequences in OMP, OMP 1 and OMP 2 can bind to the hairpin D-MH, and SPEXPAR can be activated by OMP 1 and/or OMP 2. It may expand the detection range of the OMP gene and improve the accuracy and sensitivity of the OMP detection.

The dual targets driven hairpin was connected on the streptavidin magnetic beads through the interaction between biotin and avidin to form D-MH@MBs. D-MH@MBs was beneficial for the recognition and binding of target sequences with D-MH, and the improvement of the efficiency of SPEXPAR. Furthermore, D-MH@MBs can be recycled through magnetic separation, thus reducing the cost of the D-S-M fluorescent biosensor.

Different from the traditional MNAzyme reaction, the products of SPEXPAR were not used as the recognition target of MNAzyme, but its catalytic core. ssDNA was designed to play a dual role, the assembling facilitator and the substrate of MNAzyme. The products of SPEXPAR were assembled with ssDNA to form an activated MNAzyme polymer, which cut ssDNA into two segments. This design can simplify the components, facilitate the operation and reduce the cost.

The above innovation increased the sensitivity, simplified the components, reduced the cost, and facilitated the operation. This biosensor had a lower LOD and a more comprehensive linear detection range, and OMP was successfully detected in nasal swab samples. It may be helpful in the early diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring of OD.
