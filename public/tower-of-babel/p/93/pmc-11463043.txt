The impact of perfectionism on suicidal ideation among medical residents in Iran: the mediating role of psychological distress
Authors: Ali Hosseinzadeh Oskouei, Mohammad Sadegh Zamani Zarchi, Fatemeh Safarpour, Amir Sam Kianimoghadam, Jamal Shams, Maryam Bakhtiari, Abbas Masjedi-Arani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmong medical residents, suicide is the second most prevalent cause of mortality. In Iran, the suicide of medical residents has become one of the most important challenges. This study aimed to investigate the mediating role of psychological distress in the relationship between perfectionism and suicidal ideation among medical residents in Iran.MethodsThis research was a descriptive-correlational study based on structural equation modeling. The statistical population of the present research included all the residents in Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (SBMU) in 2023, of whom 277 people were selected as a sample using the available sampling method. The Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (BSSI-1997), the Tehran Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale (TMPS-2007) and the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21-1995) were used to collect the data. Data was analyzed using structural equation modeling with SPSS and Amos software version 22.ResultsOut of 277 participants, 30% (N = 83) were male and 70% (N = 194) were female. The findings showed that the research model had goodness of fit. There was a significant relationship between perfectionism and psychological distress (β = 0.76, P < 0/001) and between psychological distress and suicidal thoughts (β = 0.66, P < 0/001). However, there was no direct relationship between perfectionism and suicidal ideation (P > 0.01), but perfectionism played a role in residents’ suicidal ideation through psychological distress (β = 0.50, P < 0/001). In other words, perfectionism led to suicidal ideation by increasing the psychological distress of the residents. In addition, perfectionism and psychological distress were able to explain 42% of the variances of suicidal ideation (R2 = 0/42).ConclusionThe results highlighted the crucial role of psychological distress in suicidal ideation of medical residents. Accordingly, substantial support from health-sector managers and policymakers is essential to meet the requirements of medical residents.Keywords: Perfectionism, Psychological distress, Residency program, Suicide

Introduction

Among medical residents, suicide is the second most prevalent cause of mortality [1], with a significant increase of 36% observed since 2000 [2]. This alarming trend is further underscored by recent Medical Council data reported 16 suicides last year among medical residents in Iran, signaling a deeper crisis. The suicide rate among Iran’s approximately 15,000 medical residents is about 100 per 100,000—nearly 15 times the general population’s rate, which reveals an urgent need for investigation and action [3]. Recent meta-analytic studies have estimated that one in ten medical students experience suicidal ideations [4], highlighting the growing recognition of medical student suicide as an important public health concern.

Given these alarming statistics and the high-pressure environment of medical training in Iran, examining perfectionism as a contributing factor to suicidal ideation is crucial. Perfectionism, characterized by setting excessively high standards and persistently striving for flawlessness, is often accompanied by a negative self-view and overly critical assessments of one’s actions [5]. Flett and Hewitt [6] emphasize the crucial role of perfectionism in suicide risk, noting that “it should be seen as a warning sign when someone known to be in psychological pain is also a perfectionist.” They further caution that even when perfectionists appear to function well on the surface, stressful experiences can signal underlying psychological distress. This theoretical perspective highlights the importance of focusing on perfectionism as a critical factor in understanding suicidal ideation among medical residents.

When intensified by the competitive demands of medical residency, perfectionism can lead to severe psychological distress and increase the risk of suicide. Over the past 35 years, scholars and empirical research have consistently demonstrated that perfectionism is a comprehensive and multifaceted personality vulnerability associated with various disorders and potentially life-threatening behaviors, such as suicide [7, 8]. This association is supported by both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies, which consistently identify perfectionism as a significant predictor of suicidal ideation e.g [9, 10]. , . Meta-analyses conducted in 2018 and 2021 further confirm the robustness of this relationship, showing that perfectionism significantly contributes to suicidal ideation across various study designs and populations [11, 12].

Although hopelessness is widely recognized as a strong predictor of suicidality, perfectionism has been shown to uniquely predict suicidal ideation even beyond the influence of hopelessness, underscoring its distinct role in the development of suicidal ideation [8, 13]. Specifically, medical students exhibiting maladaptive perfectionism tend to experience higher levels of depression, burnout, and a greater inclination towards suicidal ideation [14]. Indeed, there is a proposition that the medical course itself might attract individuals with specific dispositional traits, such as elevated levels of perfectionism [15].

However, contradictory findings suggest that the relationship between perfectionism and suicidal ideation may not be direct. Instead, this connection could be mediated by other factors [16], emphasizing the need to explore additional variables in understanding how perfectionism contributes to suicidal ideation. One such variable is psychological distress, which plays a central role in this relationship. Various studies have reported that maladaptive perfectionism is linked to higher levels of psychological distress [17–19]. Research consistently shows that psychological distress, including feelings of anxiety, depression, and stress, is highly prevalent among medical students and residents [20–22]. The findings of Collin further indicate that maladaptive perfectionists score significantly higher on measures of psychological distress among medical students [23]. Moreover, the personal effects of psychological distress can profoundly impact mental health, potentially leading to suicidal ideations among hospital workers [24]. In other words, psychological distress is a key factor in the development of suicidal ideations [25, 26], particularly among medical students who experience notably higher levels of distress and a greater tendency towards suicidal ideations compared to non-medical students [27]. Additionally, individuals with perfectionist tendencies may be more susceptible to maladaptive behaviors and all-or-nothing thinking, which can amplify their psychological distress and increase suicidal ideation [28, 29]. The recently introduced Three-Step Theory of suicidal behavior also posits that distress coupled with hopelessness can drive individuals towards contemplating suicide, particularly when perfectionism magnifies their sense of failure and inadequacy [30]. Recent statistics indicating 16 suicides among medical residents in Iran last year highlight a significant gap in our understanding of the factors contributing to these outcomes [3]. While perfectionism is widely recognized as a predictor of suicidal ideation, the mechanisms through which it exerts this influence—particularly through the mediating role of psychological distress—remain insufficiently explored. This gap is especially pronounced among Iranian medical residents, who face unique cultural and systemic pressures that may intensify these effects.

This study aims to fill this critical gap by employing structural equation modeling to investigate how perfectionism contributes to suicidal ideation through psychological distress in this population. By focusing on this vulnerable group, our research not only deepens the global understanding of the link between perfectionism and suicide but also offers a novel exploration of these dynamics within the Iranian context. This is among the first studies to apply such a model to Iranian medical residents, providing culturally relevant insights that could shape targeted interventions. The findings are expected to guide policymakers and healthcare providers in developing strategies to reduce psychological distress and prevent suicide in this high-risk group (Figures 1 and 2).

Sampling process

Methods

The current research was a descriptive-correlational study based on structural equation modeling. The statistical population of the present research included all the residents in Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (SBMU) in 2023. According to Kline [31] a typical sample size in SEM studies is about 200 to 300 subjects. In the present study, 300 subjects were selected as the sample using the available sampling method, and finally the data of 277 subjects were analyzed. The inclusion criteria included being medical residents SBMU, no history of mental disorder, no history of psychiatric medication, no history of suicide in family and relatives, and no acute and incurable physical illness. The exclusion criteria were incomplete questionnaires and univariate and multi-variate outlier data. To collect the data, after attending hospitals affiliated to Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences and getting phone numbers from the residents, the Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (BSSI), the Tehran Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale (TMPS) and the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21) were sent to them via links in WhatsApp and Telegram. Considering the heavy workload of medical residents and difficulties they have in answering questions during working hours, the questions were provided to these people through a link and they were asked to answer the questions at the right time. It should be noted that the participants were assured about the confidentiality of their data and identity. In the present study, the authors followed the ethical considerations of the research based on the Declaration of Helsinki (1975), as revised in 2008. After explaining the purpose of the research and ethical considerations to participants and obtaining informed consent, they were asked to answer the questionnaires. In addition, this study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of “Research Department of the School of Medicine Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences” (IR.SBMU.MSP.REC.1401.6).

Measures

Beck scale for suicidal ideation (BSSI)

This self-report measure was developed by Beck, Brown and Steer in 1997 and has 19 items and three subscales including desire to die, readiness to commit suicide, and actual desire to commit suicide. The first five items of this scale are designed for screening purposes. The scoring of this Likert type scale is in the range of zero to two [32]. This scale has been adapted in Iranian culture. In addition, the content validity of this scale has also been investigated in Iran. the convergent validity of this scale was 0.57 and Cronbach’s alpha was higher than 0.80 [33]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.92.

Tehran multidimensional perfectionism scale (TMPS)

This scale was developed by Besharat in 2007 and has 30 items that measures three dimensions of self-oriented perfectionism, other-oriented perfectionism, and socially prescribed perfectionism. Participants respond to the items using a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from one (strongly disagree) to five (strongly agree). The test-retest reliability of the scale was assessed twice using a two to four-week interval and the results showed a retest coefficient for self-oriented perfectionism, other-oriented perfectionism and socially prescribed perfectionism 0.85, 0.79, and 0.74 respectively [34]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha for self-oriented perfectionism, other-oriented perfectionism and socially prescribed perfectionism were 0.87, 0.84 and 0.89 respectively.

Depression, anxiety and stress scale (DASS-21)

This scale was designed by Lovibond and Lovibond in 1995 and consists of 21 statements that measure emotional states of depression, anxiety and stress. The scale is scored based on a 4-point Likert type from zero to four [35]. This scale has been adapted in Iranian culture. In addition, the content validity of this scale has also been investigated in Iran. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for stress, depression, and anxiety subscales were 0.87, 0.85, and 0.75, respectively, and its devergent validity with the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ) and the Mental Health Questionnaire (MHQ) were from 0.10 to 0.68 [36]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.88 for stress, 0.85 for anxiety, and 0.90 for depression.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using Pearson’s correlation with SPSS and Amos software version 22 to investigate the relationship between perfectionism, psychological distress and suicidal ideation. In addition, the mediating role of psychological distress in the relationship between perfectionism and suicidal ideation was investigated using structural equation modeling and the Maximum Likelihood method. To analyze the data, after examining the normality of data distribution, chi-square (X2), the normed chi-square (X2/ df), Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Adjusted Goodness of Fit Index (AGFI), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Root Mean Square Residual represents (RMR), Normed Fit Index (NIF) and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) were used to determine the model fit. Bootstrap 5000 with a significance level of 0.95 was used to determine the indirect effect of variables and confidence intervals for fit indexes.

Findings

Participants in the present study included 277 residents. The mean and standard deviation of the age of the participants were 32.10 ± 4.33, respectively. Of these 277 participants, 30% (N = 83) were male and 70% (N = 194) were female. In addition, 9.4% (N = 26) were in the first year, 22% (N = 61) were in the second year, 23.8% (N = 66) were in the third year, 43% (N = 119) were in the fourth year, and 1.8% (N = 5) were in the fifth year of residency. In terms of residency field, their specialty distribution was as follows: 13.7% (N = 38) psychiatry, 15.9% (N = 44) internal medicine, 5.1% (N = 14) ophthalmology, 10.1% (N = 28) radiology, 4% (N = 11) orthopedics, 8.3% (N = 23) pathology, 5.8% (N = 16) obstetrics and gynecology, 10.5% (N = 29) cardiology, 4.3% (N = 12) neurology, 2.2% (N = 6) general surgery, 2.2% (N = 6) otolaryngology, 4% (N = 11) dermatology, 1.8% (N = 5) urology, 2.5% (N = 7) neurosurgery, 1.8% (N = 5) infectious disease, 2.5% (N = 7) anesthesiology, 2.2% (N = 6) pediatrics, 1.8% (N = 5) physiatry, and 1.4% (4) other. In addition, 43.7% (121) of the participants were single, 53.1% (147) were married, 2.2% (6) were divorced, and 1.1% (3) were in white marriage.

Discussion

This research aimed to examine if psychological distress mediated the relationship between perfectionism and suicidal ideation among medical residents in Iran. Based on the results, the relationship between perfectionism and suicidal ideation is not as simple as a linear and direct relationship; it is mediated by psychological distress. To make it more explicit, contrary to previous studies e.g [11, 38–43]. , , no significant direct relationship was found between perfectionism and suicidal ideation when controlling the mediating role of psychological distress. In fact, the results showed that perfectionism indirectly contributes to suicidal ideation by increasing psychological distress. These findings provide significant insights into the relationship between perfectionism and suicidal ideation, and in line with the results of a number of studies, highlight the determining and crucial role of psychological distress in suicide ideation [25, 44–47].

Regarding the link between perfectionism and suicide ideation, although reviews have summarized the literature and concluded perfectionism was related to suicidality e.g [48]. , , notable inconsistencies remain between studies in terms of findings. While some studies found a direct relationship [11, 38, 39], others suggested that the connection might be mediated by factors [16, 49]. Thus, no concrete conclusion has been reached regarding perfectionism link with suicidality, and inconsistent findings between studies clouded our understanding of perfectionism’s relationship with suicidality.

Accordingly, unlike the previous studies that investigated simple correlations between variables, this research used the structural equation modeling method to reach more detailed and holistic data by examining multivariate relationships. The results showed the mediating role of psychological distress in the link between perfectionism and suicide ideation, further supporting the theory that it is the interplay between different factors with mediating mechanisms, rather than perfectionism alone, that plays a role in a person’s suicidal ideation. In fact, striving for success and trying to achieve perfection cannot lead to suicide on their own; rather, perfectionism becomes the basis of other factors like distress and leads the individual to develop suicidal ideation. Consistent with this, studies found perfectionism to be a risk factor for suicidal ideation, but it does not have a direct link with it e.g [16, 49]. , . With regard to the previously mentioned Three-Step Theory of suicidal behavior [30], contemplating suicide becomes a consideration for individuals when they undergo distress, coupled with a sense of hopelessness regarding the potential improvement of that distress. Given that perfectionism can contribute to the emergence of psychological distress, it makes individuals more susceptible to engaging in suicidal ideation and behaviors. In fact, perfectionism, on its own, is not always a sufficient precondition for emergence of suicidal ideation. Rather, when perfectionism induces distress, suicidal ideation intensifies and may advance to actual suicide attempts.

It is noteworthy that perfectionism might be linked to psychological distress due to individuals’ excess doubts over action, concerns over mistakes, and the perception that others have unrealistically high standards on them [28]. In addition, people with perfectionist tendencies, by being more self-critical, may be more susceptible to maladaptive behaviors and absolutist all-or-nothing thoughts, which lead them to interpret failures as catastrophic and to present suicidal ideations more frequently [29]. Hewitt et al. [50] also postulated that the escalation of suicide ideation associated with perfectionism occurs through several mechanisms. To clarify further, perfectionism is thought to induce stress, magnify the challenges posed by stressors, and divert individuals’ attention away from their strengths and successes, compelling a focus on their perceived flaws or failures. This dynamic interplay is theorized to contribute to heightened suicidal ideation among those experiencing perfectionistic tendencies.

Conclusion

Overall, many studies have been conducted on the relationship between perfectionism and suicidal ideation, but little research has investigated the mediating mechanisms in the relationship between these two variables. Based on theoretical considerations and research literature, we proposed a model using a factor which is believed to play a mediating role in suicide, i.e. psychological distress. Accordingly, the present study investigated multivariate relationships using structural equation modeling and the results showed that psychological distress played a mediating role in the relationship between perfectionism and suicidal ideation among medical residents. In other words, through increasing psychological distress, perfectionism led to suicidal thoughts in medical residents.

Despite the strengths of the study, there remain some limitations. The sampling method employed in this study was convenience sampling. To avoid bias and other limitations of convenience sampling, as well as enhancing the generalizability of the data, future research can utilize random sampling methods. The present study was cross-sectional and did not include longitudinal data; therefore, it is not possible to establish cause-and-effect relationships between variables. Since the demographic variables of the participants were not controlled in the present study, these variables can also be investigated in future studies. data collection process relied on self-reports. Subsequent studies might benefit from more reliable assessment instruments, such as interviews. In terms of practical implications, perfectionism and more importantly, psychological distress should be addressed when designing and implementing interventions to reduce the suicidal ideation of Iranian medical residents. Additionally, the psychological distress of medical residents and coping strategies should be considered by policy makers. Furthermore, other moderating and mediating mechanisms in the relationship between psychological distress and suicidal ideation can also be investigated in future studies.
