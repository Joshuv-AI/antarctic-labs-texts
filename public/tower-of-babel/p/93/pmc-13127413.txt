Differentiation of cord blood–derived Innate Lymphoid Cells 1 into KIR+NKG2A− natural killer cells in a human stem cell niche
Authors: Sabrina B Bennstein, Julian Reiß, Sandra Weinhold, Nadine Scherenschlich, Özer Degistirici, Katharina Raba, Lutz Walter, Johannes C Fischer, Gesine Kögler, Roland Meisel, Markus Uhrberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKIR+NKG2A- natural killer (NK) cells can detect and eliminate malignant and infected cells that have downregulated single HLA class I molecules to escape T cell recognition. So far, these KIRonly NK cells cannot be efficiently expanded in vitro without concomitant co-expression of NKG2A, which modulates their specificity. In this context, we recently demonstrated that circulating innate lymphoid cells 1 (cILC1s) have NK cell progenitor potential and can be differentiated into KIRonly NK cells using murine feeder cells. Here, we established an animal-free culture system enabling the generation and expansion of NK cells from cord blood (CB)-derived cILC1s using human mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) as feeder cells. Compared to the murine niche provided by the OP9-DL1 cell line, human MSCs generally enabled a much more efficient generation of NK cells, resulting in significantly higher yields of KIR+ NK cells. The frequency of KIRonly NK cells could be further increased by addition of the soluble NOTCH ligand DLL1. Furthermore, we utilized the cILC1/MSC platform to study education of KIRonly NK cells by HLA-C-encoded ligands in a human stem cell niche. This effect was strongest for homozygous (C1/C1) compared to heterozygous (C1/C2) donors, suggesting that cognate KIR/KIR ligand interaction mediates a gene-dosage-dependent education effect. Altogether, this optimized culture protocol overcomes previous limitations by enabling efficient generation of KIR-expressing NK cells in an animal-free, GMP-compatible system. The presented approach may facilitate the clinical translation of NK cell-based strategies for cellular immunotherapy and in addition provides a platform for mechanistic studies of NK cell education.Keywords: ILC1, NK cell development, NKP, cord blood, mesenchymal stem cells, licensing, KIR, NOTCH, GMP

Natural Killer (NK) cells of the KIR+NKG2A- (KIRonly) subtype can efficiently detect tumor cells that have downregulated single HLA-I allotypes. KIRonly NK cells are present in the circulation but cannot be efficiently expanded in vitro where they quickly upregulate NKG2A. Here, an animal-free culture protocol was established to generate KIRonly NK cells from cord blood-derived Innate Lymphoid Cells 1 (ILC1s) on layers of bone marrow-derived MSCs. We demonstrate that in vitro generated KIRonly NK cells are properly educated and functionally competent to eliminate tumor cells, suggesting suitability of the ILC1/MSC culture platform for clinical-grade generation of KIRonly NK cells.

Introduction

Human natural killer (NK) cells are an essential part of our innate immune system, partly due to their unique ability to recognize cells with downregulated HLA-class I molecules leading to the elimination of target cells, a process also referred to as “missing-self” recognition.1 Besides the conserved CD94/NKG2A lectin-like receptor that recognizes HLA-E, the main receptors mediating “missing-self” recognition are the killer immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs), which are highly polymorphic and specific for classical HLA class I molecules that express either the Bw4 (some HLA-A and many HLA-B alleles) or C1/C2 (all HLA-C allotypes) epitopes. In contrast to the inhibitory CD94/NKG2A receptor that surveys overall HLA class I expression, inhibitory KIRs are monitoring expression of individual HLA class I allotypes, enabling NK cells to detect downregulation of single HLA class I genes.2 The latter constitutes a critical “move” of tumor cells to escape T cell recognition since it prevents presentation of immunodominant tumor antigens to HLA class I-restricted T cells. NK cells expressing a cognate KIR for such a downregulated HLA class I allotype will still get activated unless they are inhibited by a co-expressed CD94: NKG2A receptor.3 Thus, KIR+NKG2A- cells referred to as “KIRonly” NK cells are filling a critical gap in the NK cell repertoires not covered by the HLA-E specific NKG2A+ subsets. KIRonly NK cells are mainly found in the circulation and the lung and are increasing in frequency from neonatal to adult blood, eventually constituting between 30% and 50% of the overall NK cell pool.4

Unfortunately, ex vivo expansion of KIRonly NK cells is challenging, since the culture conditions, whether using IL-15-mediated homeostatic proliferation conditions,5 stimulator cells,6 or feeder-free expansion strategies,7 lead to induced expression of NKG2A+ NK cells. Thus, NK cells expanded for immunotherapeutic purposes, such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) NK cells, are typically lacking KIRonly NK cells and are mostly KIR-NKG2A+ or KIR+NKG2A+.8 Furthermore, KIRonly NK cells cannot easily be generated from hematopoietic progenitors, since in the available protocols using CD34+ or CD117+ NK cell progenitors (NKPs) from bone marrow, tonsils or cord blood, stable expression of NKG2A systematically precedes expression of KIR during NK cell development.9 Similarly, also circulating CD56bright NK cells largely remain NKG2A+ during cultivation in vitro and do not proceed to the KIRonly stage.10 Correspondingly, iPSC-derived NK cells also generate mainly NKG2A+ NK cells without the ability to proceed to the KIRonly state.11 Of note, other strategies to avoid NKG2A engagement have been reported, such as selective deletion of NKG2A in CAR-NK cells12 or usage of an anti-NKG2A monoclonal antibody.13 So far, NKG2C+ “adaptive” NK cells constitute the only NK cell subset that enables stable in vitro expansion of KIR+NKG2A- NK cells. However, adaptive NK cells are quite different in terms of specificity (NKG2C-mediated activation by HLA-E) and functionality (improved CD16 signaling, but limited cytokine responsiveness), and in vitro expansion is restricted to selected HCMV+ donors with a priori high frequencies of NKG2C+ adaptive NK cells.14–16

Innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) are commonly defined as IL-7 receptor (CD127) expressing, nonT/nonB lymphocytes, which serve important functions in tissue homeostasis and mucosal barrier integrity.17 Among the three main ILC subsets (ILC1-3), the ILC1 subset is characterized by IFNγ-mediated effector functions and T-bet expression, reminiscent of Th1 cells. Their circulating counterparts (cILC1s), located in CB and PB, exhibited only moderate IFNγ expression and their physiological role as effector cells is poorly defined.18 We recently identified a CD34-CD117- natural killer precursor (NKP) with ILC1-like features (Lin-CD94-CD34-CD117-CRTH2-CD127+) in both human umbilical cord blood (CB) as well as in peripheral blood (PB).10 These cILC1s differentiated into mature NK cells with high diversity in KIR expression, and in contrast to established CD34+, CD117+, CD56bright, or iPSC-based NKPs, efficiently generated KIRonly NK cells.11,19,20 We further demonstrated that cILC1s are thymus-dependent and may be generated in the course of a rescue pathway for thymic T-cell progenitors that failed the β-selection step.20 Initial differentiation experiments using CB-derived cILC1s showed that the murine feeder cell line OP9 supported the development of cILC1s into NK cells, whereas feeder-less conditions were non-permissive. Moreover, the acquisition of KIR was strictly dependent on NOTCH signaling, since OP9 cells expressing the NOTCH ligand DL1 (OP9-DL1) supported KIR expression, whereas the paternal OP9 line did not.10 Mechanistically, reliance of cILC1s on NOTCH signaling might reflect their thymic origin and interaction with thymic epithelial cells (TECs), which strongly express NOTCH ligands such as DLL1 and DLL4.21–24

In the present study, we replaced the murine OP9-DL1 feeder cell line with human bone marrow (BM)-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSC) to differentiate cILC1s into NK cells in an animal-free and good manufacturing practice (GMP)-compatible system. We had previously shown that GMP-grade, BM-derived MSCs support the generation of ILC3s25,26 and NK cells19,25 from CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs), highlighting the overall value of MSCs to support lymphoid hematopoiesis in vitro. Human MSCs generally provide several advantages compared to murine feeder cells by constituting a more proficient human stem cell niche for NK cell development,19 and due to the lack of xenogeneic components, also facilitating the regulatory hurdles for the usage of in vitro-generated NK cells for clinical applications. Here, we show that MSCs support the efficient generation of NK cells of advanced maturation stage from CB-derived cILC1 and that we could specifically enhance the generation of KIRonly NK cells by addition of a soluble NOTCH ligand. Furthermore, we suggest that the cILC1/MSC coculture system provides a unique in vitro model to study the principles of HLA class I-dependent education during NK cell development. Together, the present study provides a versatile protocol for the generation of GMP-grade KIRonly NK cells that might be useful for future clinical application of NK cell products in tumor therapy.

Methods

Human samples and ethics statement

Umbilical CB used within this study was collected by the José Carreras Stem Cell Bank at the ITZ. The protocol was accepted by the institutional review board at the University of Düsseldorf (study number 2019-383) and is in accordance to the Declaration of Helsinki. CBs were processed directly without previous thawing.

Cell culture

GMP-grade MSC lines were isolated from bone marrow aspirates from healthy donors as previously described19,25,27 and cultivated in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium [+] 1 g/L D-Glucose, Pyruvate, [-] L-Glutamine, Phenol Red (Gibco) supplemented with 2 mM L-Glutamine (Lonza), 5% human fresh frozen plasma and 5% platelet lysate (both provided by the in-house blood bank).28 The murine stroma cell line OP9-DL1 was generously provided by Prof. Dr. Zúñiga-Pflücker, University of Toronto and was cultured as previously described.29 The HLA class I deficient cell line K562 was cultured in DMEM high glucose (4,5 g/L; Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat inactivated FCS and tested for HLA class I expression. All cell lines were negatively tested for mycoplasma. The OP9-DL1 were tested by flow cytometry for DL1 expression (cells were GFP positive).

RNA sequencing and analyses

For bulk RNA-sequencing, 5 × 106 MSCs from 3 different donors were stored in TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) for total RNA extraction. RNA extraction, library preparation, and Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) with the Illumina Truseq RNA preparation kit was performed in Göttingen (G. Salinas-Riester, NGS-Integrative Genomics Core Unit, University Medical Center, Göttingen) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. mRNA sequencing of the libraries was performed with an Illumina HiSeq4000 sequencer [paired-read 2 × 50 base pair (bp)]. Forward and reverse reads were mapped against the human genome (hg38, Homo. sapiens. GRCh38.dna.primary_assembly.fa) with the gtf file “Homo.sapiens.GRCh38.84.chr.gtf” using the nf-core/rnaseq pipeline30 via Nextflow (version 23.10.0).31 RNA-seq analyses was performed with R (version 4.2.1)32 and R studio (versions 2022.07.2).33 Normalization of read counts were performed with the R package DESeq2 (DESeq2_1.36.0).34

Isolation of MNCs from cord blood

CBs (1:1) were diluted with sterile 1xPBS (Gibco by Life Technologies, California, USA) and mononuclear cells (MNCs) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation (Biocoll, 1.077 g/cm3/Biochrom Merck Millipore). Erythrocytes were removed with ice-cold ammonium chloride solution (pH = 7.4, University Clinic Düsseldorf, 5 min at RT) with three additional washing steps. MNCs were counted and directly used for further analyses.

Flow cytometric analyses

Cells were sorted with the following lineage cocktail using FITC conjugated antibodies, as previously described35: anti-CD3 (UCHT1), anti-CD1a (HI149), anti-CD14 (HCD14), anti-CD19 (HIB19) anti-TCRαβ (IP26), anti-TCRγδ (B1), anti-CD123 (6H6), anti-CD303/BDCA-2 (201A), anti-FcεR1a [AER-37(CRA-1)], anti-CD235a (HI264), anti-CD66b (G10F5), anti-CD34 (581) all from BioLegend. The following antibodies were further used within this study (for sorting or phenotypic analyses): anti-CD94-PE/Cy7 or -APC (DX22); anti-CD3 Brilliant violet (BV) 510 (UCHT1); anti-CD56-APC/Cy7, BV650 or PE/Dazzle 594 (HCD56); anti-CD117-PE-Cy7 or BV421 (104D2); anti-CRTH2-PE/Dazzle 594 (BM16); anti-CD161-Alexa Flour 700 (HP-3G10); anti-CD158b1, b2, j (2DL2/L3/S2)- FITC or -PE (DX27); CD158e1 (KIR3DL1) –Alexa Fluor 700 or -FITC (DX9); CD158a, h, g (KIR2DL1/S1/S3/S5) –FITC or –PE (HP-MA4); anti-IFNγ-PE/Cy7 (B27); anti-TNFα-PE/Dazzle 594 (Mab11); and anti-CD107a-BV510 (H4A3) all from BioLegend (California, USA), anti-CD127-PE/Cy5 (R34.34), anti-NKG2A-APC (Z199), all from Beckman Coulter (California, USA), anti-CD158b2 (KIR2DL3)-FITC (180701). The KIR antibody mixture (KIR-MIX) consisted of antibodies with the following specificities and clones: KIR2DL2/L3/S2 (clone: DX27), KIR2DL1/S1/S3/S5 (clone: HP-MA4), and KIR3DL1 (clone: DX9). All flow cytometric analyses were performed on a Cytoflex (Beckman Coulter) with previously described settings.35 Analyses were performed on the Kaluza software 2.1.

Cell sorting

As previously described in detail,10 monocytes, T- and B-cells were depleted in CB MNCs via MojoSort Streptavidin Nanobeads (BioLegend) using the supplier’s negative selection protocol with the biotinylated antibodies anti-CD3 (OKT3), anti-CD14 (63D3), anti-CD19 (HIB19), and anti-CD66b (G10F5) from BioLegend using the MojoSort Streptavidin Nanobeads (BioLegend). For sorting, the cells were stained with a lineage panel and ILC inclusion antibodies. Cord blood-derived CD56bright NK cells (lin-CD94+CD56bright), and cILC1s (lin-CD94-CD127+CD117-CRTH2-) were sorted for differentiation experiments. Cell sorting was performed on a MoFlo XDP (Beckman Coulter).

Co-cultivation of cILC1 on MSCs and OP9-DL1

The feeder cell lines (MSCs or OP9-DL1) were seeded in the inner wells of 24-well plates (10.000 MSCs per Well) or 96-flat bottom plates (2.000-5.000 OP9-DL1 per Well) one day prior to sorting. Cell counts of sorted cILC1s varied between 400 and 2500 cells per well, depending on the CB. cILC1 were seeded on the feeder cell lines in NK2 medium (2/3 parts DMEM, 4,5 g/mL glucose), 1/3 parts Ham’s F12 (Biochrom), 10% human AB serum, 20 mg/L Ascorbic acid, 50 mmol/L ethanolamine, 50 mg/L sodium-selenite (all from Sigma Aldrich), 24 mmol/L 2-mercaptoethanol, 1% Penicillin/Streptavidin, and 1% L-glutamine (all from Gibco) containing IL-2 (500 U/mL, Novartis), IL-7 (10 ng/mL), and IL-15 (5 ng/mL, both from Miltenyi Biotec, Germany).10 For analysis of the impact of soluble human NOTCH Ligand DLL1, 50 ng/mL recombinant human soluble DLL1 (sDLL1) was supplemented (Gibco). Phenotypic analyses were done on day 8 and 14, functional analyses of generated NK cells were done at day 15/16. For single cell cloning, cILC1s were single-cell sorted into 96-well plates, precoated with MSC or OP9-DL1 feeder cell layers and cultured for 4 weeks. At week 2, clone growth was determined via microscopic inspection. Only wells with visible clones were further supplied with medium. Intracellular cytokines (IFNγ and TNFα) were stained using the Fixation buffer (Biolegend) with the corresponding protocol.

NK cell degranulation assay

After day 15/16 or 4 weeks (single cell culture), the in vitro generated (cILC1) or expanded (CD56bright) NK cells were filtered through a 30 mm strainer to remove the feeder cell lines. The cells were incubated with K562 at a 1:1 E/T ratio following addition of an anti-human CD107a-BV510 mAb (H4A3, BioLegend) for 4 h. After 1 h incubation, Brefeldin A (1000-fold dilution) and Monensin (20 nM, BioLegend) were added. After an additional 4 h, the cells were harvested, stained and analyzed via flow cytometry. As controls for the CD107a degranulation assay, both cell types were seeded into a well without target cells, the spontaneous CD107a degranulation and cytokine secretion was subtracted from the target-specific CD107a degranulation and cytokine secretion.

KIR and HLA genotyping

As previously described,36 KIR and HLA genotyping of CBs and MSCs was carried out by PCR using sequence-specific primers.

Statistical analyses

All tests were performed with a parametric or nonparametric assumption (depending on normal distribution) and a 0.05 significance level. All analyses were done using the GraphPad Prism 10.4.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California USA, www.graphpad.com). Throughout the manuscript, each n represents an individual donor in this study. Statistical details can be found within the Legend of each figure.

Results

Human MSCs support the differentiation of CB-derived cILC1s into NK cells

We previously described the successful differentiation of cILC1s into NK cells, displaying a broad repertoire of inhibitory HLA class I-specific receptors ranging from NKG2Aonly (KIR-NKG2A+) to KIRonly (KIR+NKG2A-) NK cells by using the murine feeder cell line OP9 transfected with the human NOTCH ligand delta-like 1 (DLL1).10 To find out if the differentiation potential of cILC1 into NK cells depends on the support by OP9-DL1 or can be similarly realized by using human feeder cells, we decided to explore the supporting capacity of human MSCs. We had previously shown that BM-derived MSCs support the generation of ILC3 and NK cells from CD34+ HSCPs, and thus, considered MSCs a promising source for supporting the NK cell differentiation from cILC1s, too.19,25,26 To this end, we flow-cytometrically sorted cILC1s from cord blood and co-cultured them in parallel on GMP-grade BM-derived MSCs and OP9-DL1 using the same media conditions as previously described (Figure 1A).10 After 14 days of culture, NK cells were generated on both platforms, as indicated by upregulation of NKG2A and CD56 (Figure 1B). However, higher frequencies of CD56+ NK cells were generated on MSC than on OP9-DL1 feeders (86.92% ± 4.08% vs 76.05% ± 3.54%, P = .0081), accompanied by a higher frequency of NKG2A+ expression (52.38% ± 5.09% vs 28.82% ± 5.88%, P = .0091; Figure 1C). More importantly, the number of newly generated total lymphocytes was on average more than 10-fold higher on the MSC (113.5 ± 23.73-fold, mean ± SEM) compared to the OP9-DL1 (9.115 ± 2.86-fold, mean ± SEM) platform after 14 days of culture (1.79 × 105 vs 1.48 × 104 total lymphocytes/well; Figure 1D, left panel). De novo generated lymphocytes were already present after 8 days of culture on MSC, whereas they were barely detectable on OP9-DL1 at that early timepoint. When the same CBs were directly compared on both platforms, MSCs (fold change of 79.53 ± 22.25-fold, mean ± SEM) were consistently superior to OP9-DL1 (fold change of 4.85 ± 1.48-fold, mean ± SEM; Figure 1D, right panel). Functionally, cytotoxic granule mobilization upon stimulation with the HLA-class I deficient cell line K562 led to slightly higher CD107 expression on OP9-DL1, but the difference to MSCs lacked statistical significance (59.95% ± 5.78% for OP9 vs 50.92% ± 5.61% for MSC; Figure 1E). Together, differentiation of cILC1s to NK cells was more efficient on MSC compared to OP9-DL1-supported cultures while functional activity was comparable.

Superior yield of KIR+ NK cells with the cILC1/MSC platform compared to OP9-DL1 support

Next, we performed a comparative analysis of KIR expression on NK cells generated on both differentiation platforms. As outlined in Figure 2A, de novo KIR expression with and without co-expression of NKG2A was induced on cILC1s on OP9-DL1 as well as MSC feeder cells. Whereas no significant differences were noted for the overall frequency of KIR+ NK cells after 8 days of culture, the frequency of KIRonly (KIR+NKG2A-) NK cells was higher on OP9-DL1 than MSC (17.19% ± 2.17% vs 10.61% ± 2.23%, P = .025; Figure 2B). However, due to the 10-fold higher expansion rate on MSCs (Figure 1C), the absolute cell yield was significantly higher in the MSC-based platform after 8 days for overall KIR+ as well as KIRonly NK cells (P = .0004 for KIR+ and P = .0154 for KIRonly). A similar picture emerged after 14 days of culture (Figure 2C): the proportion of KIR+ NK cells was slightly higher on OP9-DL1 (49.15% ± 7.49% vs 37.59% ± 5.5%) and the frequency of KIRonly NK cells showed significantly (P = .0025) higher values for OP9-DL1 compared to MSC (26.87% ± 5.08% vs 13.69% ± 3.82%). Nevertheless, the yield of generated NK cells, which were KIR+ (P = <.0001) and the subset of KIRonly NK cells (P = .0097) was consistently higher on the MSC platform (Figure 2C). Starting from a mean of 1.5 × 103 cILC1s/well, we generated a mean of 2 × 104 KIRonly cells/well with some samples reaching up to 8 × 104 KIRonly cells. Deeper analysis of the distribution of inhibitory KIR receptors revealed the generation of diverse and broad KIR repertoires comprising KIRonly as well as KIR+NKG2A+ NK cells on MSCs compared to less variability on OP9-DL1 (Figure 2D). Expression of KIR for the 3 major HLA class I ligands, C1 (KIR2DL2/3), C2 (KIR2DL1), and Bw4 (KIR3DL1) revealed higher frequencies for all single-KIR expressing NK cells on MSCs compared to OP9-DL1.

Notably, although the MSC platform was consistently more efficient than the OP9-DL1 platform, there was a large donor-specific variability in terms of KIR expression in the differentiation assays (Figure 1). Since we had previously shown that cILC1s are decreasing in frequency in cord blood with increasing gestational age at the time of birth,10 we next analyzed if the variability in KIR expression in the present differentiation cultures is related to the gestational age, too. Indeed, the frequency of newly generated NK cells with KIR expression, and even more so the frequency of KIRonly NK cells, decreased with the gestational age of the CB product (all were “term,” i.e., 37-40 weeks at birth; Figure 2E). The data are, thus, compatible with the notion, that cILC1s from CBs of earlier gestational age have a somewhat higher potency to differentiate into KIRonly NK cells than those from later time points.

The cILC1/MSCs platform enables highly efficient generation of clonal KIR+ NK cell populations

We next examined if MSCs provide a suitable niche to analyze the developmental potential of cILC1s on a clonal basis. To this end, single cILC1 cells were flow-cytometrically deposited in MSC-coated 96-well plates. Clone growth was validated on day 14 and selected clones were analyzed by flow cytometry on day 28 (Figure 3A). The MSC platform enabled a high cloning efficiency (39.55% ± 1.05%), which was superior to that previously achieved on OP9-DL1 (23.25% ± 3.02%)10 (Figure 3B). NK cell clones expressed a wide variety of KIR/NKG2A combinations with KIR+NKG2A+, KIR+NKG2A-, and KIR-NKG2A+ phenotypes each representing ±30% of clones, whereas KIR-NKG2A- clones remained rare (Figure 3C and D). We next tested the functional capacity of cILC1-derived NK cell clones by assessing cytotoxic granule mobilization following exposure to the HLA-class I-deficient cell line K562. NK cell clones showed minimal spontaneous CD107a mobilization in the absence of target cells (9.43% ± 2.97%) but displayed a significant increase when K562 target cells were present (42.01% ± 9.74%, P = .011; Figure 3E). Together, we demonstrate that the cILC1/MSC platform enables the efficient generation of a wide variety of functional NK cell clones including many with KIRonly phenotype. It, thus, constitutes a versatile platform to study how KIR expression is regulated and how KIRonly NK cells can be maintained and expanded in vitro.

Soluble DLL1 enhances the differentiation of KIRonly NK cells on the cILC1/MSC platform

Given that the development of KIR+ NK cells on murine OP9 cells requires ectopic expression of the human NOTCH ligand DLL1,10 we wondered if the human bone-marrow-derived MSC lines, that were utilized in the present study, also expressed DLL1 or any other NOTCH ligand. We, thus, performed RNAseq analysis of 3 different MSC lines that were previously shown to support NK cell differentiation.19 As shown in Figure 4A, MSCs did not express ligands of the DLL (DLL1, 3, 4) or DLK family (DLK1-2), but they instead exhibited expression of Jagged 1 (Jagged 2 was not expressed). To find out if the efficiency of human MSCs to support NK cell development would profit from DLL1 expression, similar to the OP9-DL1 platform, or if the endogenous Jagged expression provides sufficient NOTCH signaling, we added recombinant human soluble DLL1 (sDLL1) to the cILC1/MSC co-culture system.

As outlined in Figure 4B, the frequency and amount of newly generated NK cells (CD3-CD56+) did not significantly change upon addition of sDLL1 and also the frequency of NKG2A+ NK cells remained similar between both conditions (CD56+NKG2A+: 54.35% ± 5.97% without sDLL1 vs 51.72% ± 6.64% with sDLL1; Figure 4C). Furthermore, also the frequency of overall KIR expression did not change (Figure 4D, upper panel), although a somewhat higher yield was reached due to the slightly higher expansion of NK cells in presence of sDLL1 (Figure 4B, right panel). Strikingly, analysis of the KIR+NKG2A- subset revealed a significantly higher frequency (P = .0078) and also a significantly elevated cell yield (P = .0078) of this highly mature NK cell subset (Figure 4D, lower panel). Together, these results demonstrate that the soluble NOTCH ligand sDLL1 specifically promotes the generation of the KIRonly NK cell subset.

Human MSCs enables the study of licensing in cILC1-derived NK cells

In contrast to murine OP9 feeder cells, human MSCs express the full complement of HLA class I genes, which constitute the main ligands for inhibitory KIR receptors. Since the cILC1/MSC platform enabled efficient generation of KIR+ NK cells, it thus, provides a unique system to study how KIR expression modulates NK cell education in a human stem cell niche. For this purpose, we first determined the HLA class I genotype of the MSC lines as well as the HLA class I and KIR genotype of CB donors. MSC lines homozygous for HLA C1/C1 were selected to specifically assess licensing via the cognate inhibitory receptor KIR2DL3. CB-derived cILC1s from donors heterozygous or homozygous for the HLA class I molecule C1 were co-cultured on the MSC lines for 14 days. Subsequently, we compared CD107a mobilization of KIRonly NK cells expressing cognate (KIR2DL3only) and non-cognate (KIR2DL1ony) KIR in response to the HLA-deficient target cell line K562. Indeed, cognate cILC1-derived NK cells demonstrated significantly stronger CD107a mobilization than the non-cognate counterparts (P = .0001, Figure 5A). Furthermore, the functional difference between NK cells with cognate (KIR2DL3+) and non-cognate (KIR2DL1+) KIR was more pronounced in homozygous C1/C1 donors compared to heterozygous C1/C2 donors (P = .0020, Figure 5B and C). This points to a dose-dependent effect of HLA-C on NK cell education and furthermore suggests a model, in which NK cells educate each other during the expansion phase of culture, when MSCs have largely disappeared (Figure 5D). Taken together, we demonstrate that by assigning MSC feeder cell lines with a defined HLA class I background, the cILC1/MSC platform enables the study of KIR-mediated NK cell education.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that human MSCs support the differentiation of CB-derived cILC1s into mature NK cells. MSCs enhanced our previous OP9-DL1-based protocol by generating superior cell yields for all major NK cell subsets. The generation of the KIR+NKG2A- NK cell subset, which is inefficient with most established in vitro protocols, could be specifically increased by addition of the soluble NOTCH ligand DLL1, which is endogenously lacking in the utilized human MSC lines. Mechanistically, we suggest that human MSCs provide HLA class I molecules, which constitute the natural ligands of inhibitory KIR, and thereby enable education of KIR+ NK cells. However, our data also suggest that NK cells themselves can educate each other once MSCs are eliminated in the later culture phase. The cILC1/MSC culture system, thus, provides a versatile platform for studying the regulation of KIR expression and the molecular requirements of NK cell education. Moreover, by supporting the in vitro generation and expansion of KIRonly NK cells, the cILC1/MSC platform could pave the way to develop NK cell-based immunotherapies, enabling to selectively target tumor cells on the basis of their aberrant HLA class I expression or by exploiting KIR ligand mismatching between cell product and patient.

The CD34-CD117- phenotype of cILC1 is unique among established NKPs, which are either CD34+ (stages 1 and 2) or CD34-CD117+ (stage 3) NKPs and develop into CD94+ NK cells of progressing maturity (stages 4-6) according to the model established by the Caligiuri group.37 It is so far unclear how cILC1s fit into this scheme: they are more closely related to the T cell than the NK cell lineage in terms of their transcriptional landscape,38 they lack the typical signs of epigenetic imprinting of NK cell genes,10 and they do not express any of the established NK cell markers including EOMES, NKp46, CD94, KIR, or perforin. It is, thus, remarkable that advanced stages of NK cell maturation, including KIRonly NK cells, are readily detectable after 8 days in the ILC1/MSC setting. cILC1s might, thus, constitute a more advanced kind of NKP that has already received mandatory developmental signals in vivo that are not provided by current in vitro protocols. Considering that thyILC1 are progenitors of cILC1s, such early signals could be provided by the thymus.20 That would explain why cILC1s, but not CD34+ and/or CD117+ NKPs from BM or secondary lymph nodes, efficiently progress to advanced NK cell stages such as KIRonly NK cells.

One promising improvement we have identified in this work was the addition of a soluble NOTCH ligand (sDLL1) to the MSC-based co-culture system. Notably, it was already shown previously that overexpression of activated NOTCH in NK cells promotes KIR expression.24 Furthermore, we had previously shown that the in vitro development of KIR+ NK cells on OP9 stroma cells from CD34+ hematopoietic progenitors19 as well as from cILC1,10 is dependent on ectopic expression of the NOTCH ligand DLL1. Notably, we found that the only NOTCH ligand endogenously expressed on our human MSC lines was Jagged1 (Figure 4A). It is presently unclear, if there is a qualitative difference between DLL1 and Jagged1 in terms of how they support KIR expression or if the increase of KIRonly NK cells upon addition of the soluble DLL1 is due to an overall increased availability of NOTCH ligands. Further studies are needed to dissect the quantitative and qualitative contributions of the different NOTCH ligands on regulation of KIR expression. In any case, these findings suggest that combining MSC feeder support with targeted signaling cues may enable significant optimization of protocols for clinically grade generation of functionally mature NK cells, including the clinically useful but “hard-to-get” KIRonly subset.

The developmental cues that enable differentiation of cILC1 into NK cells, such as NOTCH signaling, are obviously provided by MSCs as well as OP9-DL1. However, a major advantage of the present protocol compared to the OP9-DL1 cells is the substantially increased NK cell yield. The MSC niche apparently provides additional signals that promote increased proliferation and survival of developing NK cells. These signals have to be cell-based, since culture conditions and cytokines were identical in the murine and human stroma niche. The NK cell output of the ILC1/MSC platform after a 2-week culture period was on average 10-fold higher than on OP9-DL1. Moreover, in single-cell cloning experiments, MSCs supported a significantly higher NK cell cloning efficiency, again suggesting that constant presence of HLA-class I molecules provided by MSCs may contribute to improved stability and survival signals during clonal outgrowth. Notably, on both platforms some CBs performed much better than others. We could correlate the difference with the gestational age of the CBs, with cILC1s from early-term CBs performing better than those from full-term CBs. If verified in a larger cohort, early-term CBs would provide a high-yield source for the generation of KIR+ NK cells since they also contain higher frequencies of cILC1s than CBs of more advanced gestational age.10 Notably, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) using CB as source led to significantly higher frequencies of mature NK cells, associated with improved 2-year overall survival, compared to BM and peripheral blood.39

Finally, the cILC1/MSC platform constitutes a versatile model to study NK cell education by providing a human stem cell niche with physiological levels of HLA class I expression and, due to the efficient generation of KIR-only NK cells, avoiding interference with NKG2A expression. During NK cell development in vivo, the HLA-E-specific CD94: NKG2A heterodimer is generally expressed before KIR and it also constitutes the predominant HLA class I inhibitory receptor following allogeneic HSCT.40 Similarly, NKG2A+ NK cells prevail in established in vitro models of NK cell differentiation,9 thereby impeding the analysis of KIR-specific education. Here, we focused our analysis on the cognate interaction between KIRonly NK cells, expressing KIR2DL3+ as their only KIR, and MSCs homozygous for the cognate ligand HLA-C1. The “missing self” model of education predicted a stronger education of NK cells expressing the cognate KIR2DL3 in HLA-C1/C1 individuals compared to the non-cognate, HLA-C2-specific, KIR2DL1. Indeed, we observed significantly higher mobilization of CD107+ cytotoxic granula in KIR2DL3only NK cells compared to KIR2DL1only NK cells. Unexpectedly, we observed significant higher CD107 mobilization in homozygous C1/C1 donors compared to heterozygous C1/C2 donors, suggesting a gene-dose effect. On the basis of these results, we have developed a 2-step model, in which developing NK cells are first exposed to MSC-encoded HLA class I ligands followed by a second phase, where MSCs have already largely vanished and the increasingly abundant NK cells could present the educating HLA class I ligands to each other (Figure 5D). Alternative explanations are possible such as education of KIR by HLA class I ligands on the same cell. This interaction in cis was suggested for cognate pairs of murine Ly-49 and MHC class I ligands, but there is so far no evidence for cis interaction between KIR and HLA class I.41 Further studies are required, including employment of MSC lines homozygous for C2, to further unravel the mechanisms of KIR-mediated NK cell education.

Altogether, the cILC1/MSC platform enables the efficient in vitro development of NK cells of advanced maturation stage, including KIR+NKG2A- NK cells, providing several advantages compared to previously available protocols. The animal-free culture conditions established in this study will facilitate translational applications including cell-based cancer therapies. Using our protocol and acknowledging that cord blood units (CBU) display considerable variability in cILC1 frequency,10 CBUs with higher cILC1 counts would be selected for production purposes. In these selected CBUs, cILC1 levels range between 15 and 25 cells/µL CB (depending on the gestational age).10 Given typical CB volumes of 90—150 mL per unit, this corresponds to approximately 1.35 × 106 to 3.75 × 106 cILC1s per selected CB. After ∼50-fold expansion and ∼14% of KIRonly NK cells, these yields to around 9.45 × 106 to 2.625 × 107 KIRonly NK cells per CBU. It has to be acknowledged that to achieve sufficiently large cell numbers for therapeutic applications, additional steps have to be implemented to establish large-scale expansion protocols, for example by employment of stimulator cells.42 Finally, the protocol provides a versatile in vitro tool to analyze the process of KIR-acquisition and education in vitro. Given the high need for cell-based therapies in cancer treatment and the emerging role for NK cells therein, the animal-free cILC1/MSC platform could facilitate the setup of allogeneic “off-the-shelf” therapies, exploiting the tumor-specific activity of KIRonly NK cells.
