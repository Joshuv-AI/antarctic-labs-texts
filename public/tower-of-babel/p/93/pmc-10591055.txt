Glutathione dynamics is a potential predictive and therapeutic trait for neoadjuvant chemotherapy response in bladder cancer
Authors: YongHwan Kim, Hyein Ju, Seung-Yeon Yoo, Jinahn Jeong, Jinbeom Heo, Seungun Lee, Ja-Min Park, Sun Young Yoon, Se Un Jeong, Jinyoung Lee, HongDuck Yun, Chae-Min Ryu, Jinah Lee, Yun Ji Nam, Hyungu Kwon, Jaekyoung Son, Gowun Jeong, Ji-Hye Oh, Chang Ohk Sung, Eui Man Jeong, Jaehoon An, Sungho Won, Bumsik Hong, Jae Lyun Lee, Yong Mee Cho, Dong-Myung Shin
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryRadical cystectomy with preoperative cisplatin-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) is the standard care for muscle-invasive bladder cancers (MIBCs). However, the complete response rate to this modality remains relatively low, and current clinicopathologic and molecular classifications are inadequate to predict NAC response in patients with MIBC. Here, we demonstrate that dysregulation of the glutathione (GSH) pathway is fundamental for MIBC NAC resistance. Comprehensive analysis of the multicohort transcriptomes reveals that GSH metabolism and immune-response genes are enriched in NAC-resistant and NAC-sensitive MIBCs, respectively. A machine-learning-based tumor/stroma classifier is applied for high-throughput digitalized immunohistochemistry analysis, finding that GSH dynamics proteins, including glutaminase-1, are associated with NAC resistance. GSH dynamics is activated in cisplatin-resistant MIBC cells, and combination treatment with a GSH dynamics modulator and cisplatin significantly suppresses tumor growth in an orthotopic xenograft animal model. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the predictive and therapeutic values of GSH dynamics in determining the NAC response in MIBCs.

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BC) has high incidence and mortality rates and is ranked as the sixth most common malignant tumor in men worldwide, with an estimated 573,278 new cases and 212,536 deaths in 2020.1 BC exhibits significant clinical and pathological heterogeneity.2,3,4,5 Based on the extent of bladder wall invasion, BC is classified as either non-muscularis propria invasive BC (NMIBC; Ta, carcinoma in situ, and T1) or muscularis propria invasive BC (MIBC; ≥T2). NMIBC accounts for most BC patients (70%–80%) at the time of initial diagnosis. NMIBC is generally an unaggressive tumor and is managed with transurethral resection of the bladder tumor (TURBT) followed by intravesical chemotherapy or BCG vaccine. However, the high rate of recurrence and progression to MIBC imposes costly lifelong surveillance.6 MIBC, which accounts for 25% of tumor incidences, can progress rapidly to become metastatic BC and is responsible for most patient mortalities, emphasizing that proper management of MIBC is critical to decrease BC mortality.7 After diagnosis through TURBT, radical cystectomy with preoperative cisplatin-based chemotherapy, so-called neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC), is the current standard of care for MIBC. NAC is effective in a subset of patients, with 30%–40% pathological complete response rates, but the nonresponsive patients could be subject to drug toxicity without oncologic benefit and a delay in definitive therapy.7 However, current clinicopathologic features of MIBC patients are inadequate to prospectively predict the NAC response.

Genome-wide gene expression profiling has generated precise molecular insights into the complexity of the heterogeneous behaviors of MIBCs, including assessment of prognosis and response to treatment.8,9,10,11,12,13,14 For example, patients with the p53-like molecular subtype exhibit poor response to NAC.9 In contrast, patients with basal-like tumors received the most oncologic benefit, with increased overall survival (OS) following NAC relative to an unmatched patient cohort that received surgery alone.11 However, prior studies reported that the NAC response rate was decreased in patients with the basal/squamous (Ba/Sq) expression subtype.14,15 In addition, a statistically significant association was not observed in prediction of NAC response with consensus classification in a study that integrated previously published major datasets.10 These disparate results highlight the complexity of determining the response to NAC in MIBC. Therefore, identifying the underlying mechanisms of chemotherapy resistance and predictive biomarkers to enhance the accuracy and efficacy of MIBC clinical management contributes to a significant unmet clinical need.

The sensitivity of tumors to the cytotoxic effects of anticancer agents, especially cisplatin, is affected by the level and activity of glutathione (GSH), a highly abundant nonprotein thiol tripeptide consisting of glutamate, cysteine, and glycine.16 GSH maintains the redox balance, is involved in the antioxidant defense against oxidative stress, and catalyzes xenobiotic conjugation and detoxification reactions.16 Cellular GSH homeostasis is regulated by the rate of GSH synthesis, utilization, and extracellular export.17 GSH synthesis requires two consecutive ATP-dependent steps. In the first step, glutamate-cysteine ligase (GCL) forms γ-glutamylcysteine (γ-GC) from glutamate and cysteine, and GSH synthetase (GSS) ligates glycine to γ-GC, forming GSH.18 The GSH biosynthetic capacity of various cells and tissues is controlled by multiple factors, including substrate availability, especially that of cysteine and glutamate, and by the activity of GCL, the rate-limiting enzyme of GSH synthesis. GCL enzymes are heterodimeric complexes consisting of catalytic (GCLC) and modifier (GCLM) subunits, which are encoded by different genes.18 GCLC is responsible for the catalytic activity, while GCLM, via direct physical interaction, enhances the catalytic efficiency of GCLC by lowering the Km of glutamate and increasing the Ki of GSH inhibition.

In particular, glutamate availability significantly affects GSH production, and two isozymes, glutaminase-1 (GLS) and glutaminase-2, catalyze the hydrolysis of glutamine to glutamate, which is then shunted into GSH biosynthesis and diverse metabolic pathways with carbon and/or nitrogen.19 Uptake of cystine (the oxidized form of cysteine) via the xCT transporter (gene name SLC7A11) is coupled to glutamate efflux.20 Moreover, glutamate contributes to the maintenance of GSH in its reduced form by supporting production of NADPH via regulation of malic acid,21 which is crucial for conversion of oxidized GSH (GSSG) to reduced GSH via GSH reductase (GSR). These metabolic reprogramming events are key features of many cancers, not only maintaining the high energetic demand for supporting proliferation, but also facilitating chemoresistance and survival of other medical interventions against cancer progression.22

In the present study, gene expression profiling of MIBC tumors from four independent cohorts revealed the biological basis of the molecular heterogeneity of the MIBC NAC response, defining the significance of coordinated upregulation of these GSH-related metabolic pathways. The digitalized analysis of immunostaining of MIBC tumor tissues using machine-learning-based tumor-stroma classifiers highlighted the clinical relevance of GLS protein as a potential predictive biomarker for the MIBC NAC response. Furthermore, by integrating live-cell real-time GSH monitoring, in vitro cell culture, and in vivo animal model analyses, we demonstrated that GLS-mediated GSH dynamics is a potential therapeutic target to resensitize chemoresistant MIBCs.

Results

Transcriptome profiling based on MIBC NAC response

Samples from 63 MIBC (cT2-4aN0M0) patients who underwent transurethral resection (TUR) and NAC with subsequent curative radical/partial cystectomy were retrospectively collected (Table S1). Among the cases, 20 patients were assigned to an Asan Medical Center (AMC) discovery cohort, as “incomplete TURBT” was documented on the TURBT surgical record, indicating that residual tumor remained after TUR, thereby excluding the possibility of complete tumor removal by TUR prior to NAC and enabling assessment of the therapeutic response to NAC. The discovery cohort was classified into two groups with marked differences in NAC response: 8 NAC response (R) patients with absence of residual invasive tumor (ypT0N0, ypTaN0, ypTisN0, or ypT1N0) and 12 NAC nonresponse (NR) patients with large residual MIBCs (4.9 ± 2.5 cm, the size of residual tumor) in the radical/partial cystectomy specimens. To investigate the stroma-free, tumor-specific molecular nature of the NAC response, we performed laser capture microdissection (LCMD) of tumors from formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) TURBT specimens of 16 patients (n = 8/group) from the discovery cohort and subsequently compared the genome-wide transcriptome profiles (Figures 1A and S1A). According to principal-component analysis (PCA), whole transcriptomes exhibited little difference between the NR and the R groups (Figure S1B). We performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to examine whether the transcriptomes of the NR and R groups correlated with the published molecular classifications that define MIBC molecular subtypes.8,9,10,11,23 The NAC-sensitive tumors were enriched with both basal and Ba/Sq classifiers (Figure S1D), as was the p63 pathway, which was elevated in the basal MIBC subtype.9 However, the NAC-resistant tumors were not consistent with the published MIBC molecular classifiers.

The transcriptomes of the tumors in the AMC discovery cohort were thus further characterized for gene networks, biofunctions, and canonical pathways using MetaCore and GSEA methods. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis indicated that tumors from the NAC NR and R groups exhibited differential expression of genes involved in several metabolic processes and the response to organic substances (Figure 1B), as well as Hippo and YAP/TAZ function, cell cycle, inflammation, and immune response pathways (Figure S1E). MetaCore gene network analysis revealed that genes related to metabolic processes were enriched in NR-group tumors, and immune processes were enriched in R-group tumors (Figure S1F).

Consistently, GSEA indicated that cell-metabolism-related gene sets, including valine_leucine_isoleucine (VLI) amino acid (AA) degradation (normalized enrichment score [NES] = 1.72; false discovery rate [FDR] = 0.143), cytochrome P450 (CYP) drug metabolism (NES = 1.53; FDR = 0.183), and GSH metabolism (NES = 1.54; FDR = 0.166), were significantly enriched in the transcriptomes of NR-group tumors (Figures 1C and S1G). In contrast, the majority of gene sets enriched in the R-group tumors included those involved in the immune response, including cytokine (NES = −2.19; FDR = 0.000) and chemokine (NES = −1.660; FDR = 0.051) signaling and natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity (NES = −1.720; FDR = 0.036) (Figures 1C and S1H, and Table S2).

GSH-related metabolic processes were upregulated in NAC-resistant MIBCs

To further confirm these findings, we used the 63 MIBC patients (cT2-4aN0M0) as a validation cohort. All patients had undergone radical or partial surgery, and the pathological response could thus be assessed. A pathological response to NAC (≤ypT1 and ypN0) was noted in 37 patients (58.7%) (Table S1). Among the 63 cases in the validation cohort, we collected the transcriptome datasets of 39 patients (NR, n = 18; R, n = 21) by performing LCMD of tumors from FFPE TURBT specimens (Figure S2A). Based on published MIBC molecular classifiers, NAC-sensitive tumors in the AMC validation cohort were enriched with the Ba/Sq or p63 pathway, and NAC-resistant tumors were enriched with luminal classifiers (Figure S2B).

These findings supported the use of the consensus MIBC classification method,10 which showed that the AMC cohort samples could be classified into three subtypes, LumP (46.15%), LumU (25.64%), and Ba/Sq (28.21%), in the absence of the LumNS, stroma-rich, and neuroendocrine-like (NE-like) subtypes, likely due to BC cell-targeted LCMD and low incidence of the NE-like subtype (Table S3). Tumors of the Ba/Sq subtype exhibited significantly decreased expression of luminal markers (GATA3, FOXA1, and CK20), but high expression of the basal marker CK5 (Figure S2C), validating the assigned subtypes. A similar result was obtained in measuring expression levels of luminal and basal marker proteins in the tumor composition (Figure S2D). The NAC response rate was not significantly associated (p = 0.320) (Table S3), and OS did not differ among molecular subtypes, which were based on the consensus MIBC classification method (Figure S2E and Table S3).

We subsequently performed GSEA using 30 gene sets to characterize the discovery cohort (Table S2). NR tumors from the validation cohort were strongly enriched with gene sets related to metabolic processes involved in CYP or GSH metabolism, as well as folate biosynthesis, which plays a critical role in regulation of the redox state by producing GSH (Figure 1D). Tumors in the R group from the validation cohort were characterized for cytokine and antigen-presenting gene sets (Table S2). Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) were counted in TURBT tumor specimens from the AMC validation cohort, showing higher TILs in the R group compared with the NR group (Figure 1E).

Importantly, MetaCore analysis indicated that in NR-group tumors, antioxidant pathways mediated by nuclear factor erythroid-2 related factor-2 (NRF2) were significantly upregulated (Figures 1F and S2F). NRF2 is a master regulator of genes involved in antioxidant responses, including the GSH metabolic pathway.24 Furthermore, the NAC-resistant tumors in the validation cohort were strongly represented by a subset of cellular metabolic processes and gene networks related to detoxification of inorganic compounds (Figures S2G and S2H), suggesting a critical role for NRF2-mediated antioxidant, metabolic, and detoxification pathways in determining the NAC response of MIBCs.

Subsequently, the 30 gene sets differentially associated with the NAC response in the discovery cohort were applied to three independent publicly available gene expression profiling datasets from pre-NAC TURBT specimens, which included records of the pathological response to NAC (Figure 1G). The external institute cohorts included (1) an “MDA MVAC cohort” of 23 patients treated uniformly with MVAC within the context of a phase III clinical trial,9 (2) an “MDA DDMVAC cohort” of 38 patients from a phase II trial of DDMVAC and bevacizumab,25 and (3) an “NAC metadata cohort” of 299 patients from seven institutions, who were treated with various NAC regimens.11 Consistent with the findings from the AMC validation cohort, the CYP drug/xenobiotic metabolism and GSH metabolism gene sets were significantly enriched in the NR-group tumors from both the MDA MVAC and MDA DDMVAC cohorts (Figures 1H and 1I and Table S4), further supporting the significance of GSH metabolism in MIBC presurgical chemotherapy sensitivity. Likewise, several immune response processes were significantly enriched in the R-group tumors from the NAC metadata cohort (Figure 1J and Table S4). Collectively, transcriptomic profiling of MIBC patient tumors from four independent cohorts revealed GSH-related metabolic processes as features of NAC-resistant MIBC subtype and immune response pathways as features of NAC-sensitive subtypes.

Predictive biomarkers characterizing MIBC NAC response

To identify potential biomarkers defining distinct molecular signatures of the NAC response, we further explored the gene network (MetaCore) and leading-edge (GSEA) analyses using transcriptome datasets from the AMC discovery cohort characterized by incomplete TURBT. MetaCore analysis revealed that several zinc-finger protein (ZNF) family genes were upregulated in NR tumors by networking with genes related to metabolic processes, peptide biosynthetic processes, translational elongation, and termination (Figure S2I). Likewise, the GSEA leading-edge analysis revealed that several GSH S-transferase (GST) and CYP genes were highly expressed in NR tumors, as demonstrated by clustering the GSH metabolism gene set (Figure 2A). We subsequently validated the expression of these putative biomarkers (Table S5) by quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis of the cDNA libraries that were established using LCMD tumor specimens. NR tumors, compared with R tumors, exhibited distinct expression of genes representing (1) GSH metabolism, (2) metabolic processes, (3) the WNT/MYC pathway, (4) neural markers, (5) the immune response, (6) cell adhesion, and (7) stemness features (Figure 2B and Table S5). Among these pathways, GSH-building AA metabolism (GLS and CBS), GSH synthesis (GCLM and GCLC), and GSH utilization (GSR, GGT7, PRDX1, GPX1, and GPX4) were significantly enriched, suggesting their potential use as NAC-resistant MIBC biomarkers (Figure 2C and Table S5). In contrast, genes related to the immune response (TRAF6 and IL15RA) were activated in the R-group tumors.

Upregulation of GSH-related proteins in NAC-resistant MIBCs

To investigate the potential clinical significance of these biomarkers validated at the transcript level, we examined protein levels of the biomarkers using immunohistochemistry (IHC) on tissue microarray (TMA) constructs generated from pre-NAC TURBT specimens. Initially, the AMC discovery cohort was examined semi-quantitatively with visual assessment by a uropathologist. Consistent with the transcript expression results, GSH-related and immune-response proteins exhibited differential expression between the NR and the R TURBT specimens (Figure 2D). Among the affected proteins, GLS, GSR, and GCLM were significantly increased, while CD11c protein was decreased, in NR tumors (Figure 2E).

Importantly, high expression of GLS protein was also observed in the NAC-resistant tumors from the AMC validation cohort, supporting the clinical significance of GLS in defining the MIBC NAC response (Figures 3B and 3C). Aligned with transcriptome profiling, NAC-sensitive tumors exhibited high expression of proteins related to the immune response (CD11c) and cell adhesion (EOGT and KIFC2) (Figures 3B, 3C, and S3G–S3I). The expression of CD11c and CD247/CD3Z proteins was increased in the stroma in the NAC-sensitive group (Figures 3D, S3J, and S3K). Especially, the stroma of the NAC-resistant group exhibited significant upregulation of SOX2 protein (Figure S3L), which contributes to the maintenance and progression of BCs.26

The spatial distribution of these proteins was not significantly associated with molecular subtype, as determined by the consensus model, except that EOGT protein was upregulated in the stromal compartment of Ba/Sq subtypes, and SOX2 protein was upregulated in the stromal compartment of LumU subtypes (Figure S4A). Regarding biomarkers related to GSH dynamics, GSR protein was significantly increased in LumU-subtype tumors, and PRDX1 protein was upregulated in the Ba/Sq subtype in both the tumor and the stromal compartments (Figure S4B).

Association between clinicopathologic and biomarker expression data for prediction of NAC response and clinical outcomes

Among the clinicopathological features and expression levels of multiple biomarkers, univariate analysis revealed that NAC resistance was associated with incomplete resection of the tumor during TURBT (p = 0.002), high levels of GLS protein (p = 0.002), and low expression of CD11c (p = 0.035) in tumor cells (Figure 3E and Table S6). High expression of SOX2 in the stroma compartment was also associated with NAC nonresponsiveness on univariate analysis (p = 0.036). On multivariate analysis, high expression of GLS (p = 0.004) and low expression of CD11c (p = 0.010) in the tumor cells remained independent prognostic factors for prediction of NAC resistance (Figure 3E). Notably, high GLS expression significantly correlated with tumor upregulation of GSR proteins (p = 0.038), supporting the coordinated activation of pathways related to GSH dynamics (Figure 3F).

During the median follow-up period of 57.0 months (range 7–122), 29 patients (46.0%) died, 16 (25.4%) of them from the disease. Patients with pathologic NAC responses exhibited longer cancer-specific survival (CSS; p < 0.001) and OS (p < 0.001) relative to NAC NR patients, but this correlation was not noted for the expression levels of GLS or CD11c proteins (Figures S4C and S4D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that expression of GSH metabolic proteins, especially GLS, is clinically significant in defining the molecular signature of MIBC subtypes by NAC response.

A prior study demonstrated that the pathway signatures of urothelial cancer cell lines are associated with the therapeutic response to cisplatin.28 Expression levels of proteins related to GSH metabolism were measured in vitro, revealing that GLS, GSR, and GCLM proteins were upregulated in the HT1376 and RT4 cell lines, which are less sensitive to cisplatin relative to the 1197 and 5637 cell lines, which are highly sensitive to cisplatin (Figures S5I and S5J), supporting the association between the GSH metabolic pathway and the cisplatin response.

To investigate the functional relevance of GSH-related metabolic pathways, we monitored the qualitative and quantitative aspects of GSH dynamics in living T24 MIBC cells in real time using FreSHtracer, a reversible chemical probe for GSH that allows nondestructive, integrated, and image-based high-throughput assays.29,30,31 The GSH recovery capacity (GRC) was evaluated for approximately 1 h after exposure to 100 μM diamide, a thiol-specific oxidant (Figure 4C). Consistent with the gene expression results (Figures 4A and 4B), Cis_NR T24 cells exhibited a higher GSH dynamics index (GI) than did Cis_R cells, based on the increase in both initial F510/F580 fluorescence ratio (FR) (for baseline total GSH level) and slope (for GRC) after short-term exposure to diamide (Figures 4D−4F). Furthermore, Cis_NR T24 cells exhibited increased NADPH/NADP ratio, which is crucial for the conversion of GSSG to GSH (Figure 4G).

GSH metabolism biomarkers modulate MIBC cell redox homeostasis

We employed the high-throughput GRC assay to investigate the roles of major target genes related to GSH biosynthesis (GLS, GCLM, and GCLC) or utilization (GSR and PRDX1), which were altered in tumors from NAC-resistant MIBC patients (Figures 2 and 3) or upregulated at the protein level in Cis_NR human MIBC cell lines (Figure 4B). Infection with lentivirus containing short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting each gene significantly decreased the basal level of GSH and impaired GRC, following diamide treatment in Cis_NR T24 cells (Figures 4H and 4I) and naive T24 MIBC cells (Figures S6A and S6B). Consistent with a previous study,21 we showed that silencing GLS decreased the intracellular NADPH/NADP ratio in Cis_NR T24 cells, supporting the crucial contribution of GLS in maintenance of redox homeostasis in MIBCs (Figure 4J). The GRC assay was used to evaluate the biological outcomes of specific small compounds that inhibit GLS (bis-2-(5-phenylacetamido-1,3,4-thiadiazol-2-yl) ethyl sulfide [BPTES]), GSR (carmustine [BCNU]), and GCL (buthionine sulfoximine [BSO]), indicating that these selective GSH inhibitors significantly impaired GSH dynamics in Cis_NR T24 cells (Figures 4K, 4L, S6C, and S6D). Moreover, ectopic expression of GLS, GSR, or GCLM increased the intracellular GSH level and GRC in naive T24 MIBC cells (Figures S6E−S6G). Similar roles for these GSH metabolism biomarkers were observed in KU-19-19 bladder carcinoma cell lines (Figures S6H–S6L) that were either sensitive (Gem_R) or resistant (Gem_NR) to gemcitabine, another chemotherapeutic used for NAC.32

To determine the operative mechanism of these findings, we examined the expression and activity of the redox homeostasis master regulator NRF2, which was strongly enriched in NR tumors of the AMC validation cohort (Figure 1F). Both transcript and protein NRF2 levels were increased in Cis_NR T24 cells (Figure 4M), in which the majority of NRF2 protein was localized to the nucleus, indicating activation of the NRF2 pathway (Figure S6M). A chromatin-immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay revealed that binding of NRF2 to the promoters of GSH metabolic genes was increased in Cis_NR T24 cells (Figure 4N). Accordingly, silencing NRF2 significantly decreased the GSH index profile (Figures S6N and S6O), concomitant with repression of genes related to GSH dynamics (Figures S6P and S6Q). Consistently, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were significantly decreased in Cis_NR T24 MIBC cells (Figure S6R). GSH could be conjugated to xenobiotics by GST for subsequent detoxification followed by facilitating cisplatin efflux mediated by multidrug resistance-associated protein-2.33 Notably, a majority of GST isoforms, including GST-P1, were upregulated and GST activity was higher in Cis_NR T24 cells relative to Cis_R cells (Figures S6S and S6T), indicating that enhanced GSH dynamics could regulate cisplatin resistance by multiple mechanisms.16,34

We next explored the significance of NRF2-mediated GSH-related biomarkers in the stemness features of human MIBC cells. When GLS, GSR, or GCLM were knocked down in Cis_NR T24 cells (Figure 5E), their tumor sphere formation (Figure 5F) and clonogenic capacities (Figure 5G) were significantly diminished, confirming the critical role of these mediators in the stemness features of MIBC cells. Invasive capacity was also significantly impaired by silencing of each gene (Figure 5H). Consistent with the gene-silencing results, treatment with GSH inhibitors (BPTES, BCNU, or BSO) strongly repressed the stemness features of Cis_NR T24 cells based on their tumor sphere formation and clonogenic and invasive capacity (Figures 5I−5K). Together, these findings demonstrate that increased GSH dynamics regulated by coordinated upregulation of GLS, GSR, and GCLM stimulate cell growth, stemness features, and invasiveness of MIBC cells.

GSH disruption resensitizes chemoresistant MIBC cells

Silencing of each gene significantly decreased the growth of Cis_NR T24 cells in the presence of cisplatin and slightly decreased cell growth in the absence of cisplatin treatment (Figure 6A). In addition, ectopic expression of GLS, GSR, or GCLM increased the viability of naive T24 MIBC cells exposed to cisplatin (Figure 6B). Consistent with these results, the combination of cisplatin with GSH inhibitors (BPTES, BCNU, or BSO) severely impaired cell growth at a suboptimal concentration of cisplatin (1 μg/mL), although treatment with GSH inhibitors alone did not robustly impair cell growth (Figure 6D). Combination treatment with GSH inhibitors and cisplatin synergically decreased the growth of Cis_NR T24 cells in a dose-dependent manner (Figure S7A). Moreover, chemical disruption of GLS, GSR, or GCLM led to apoptotic cell death upon treatment with cisplatin (Figures 6E and 6F), activating effector caspase-3 and induced cleavage of poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) in Cis_NR BC cells (Figure 6G). However, neither GSH modulators nor suboptimal cisplatin alone affected cleavage of caspase-3 and PARP or apoptosis. Furthermore, disrupting GSH dynamics with targeted small-molecule GSH inhibitors increased gemcitabine sensitivity and activated apoptotic cell death in Gem_NR KU-19-19 cells (Figures 6H and 6I). Taken together, these results suggest that disrupting GSH dynamics could be a potential therapeutic approach to enhance the chemosensitivity of MIBCs to cisplatin.

GSH disruption enhances the efficacy of cisplatin chemotherapy in MIBC

To examine the in vivo relevance of these findings, we examined the effect of each GSH modulator (BPTES, BCNU, or BSO) alone or in combination with cisplatin in an orthotopic xenograft BC model, in which 1 × 106 Cis_NR BC cells were transplanted into the outer layer of the bladder in NOD/ShiLtJ-Prkdcem1AMCIl2rgem1AMC (NSG) immunodeficient mice.3,4 After 2 weeks, the xenograft-implanted mice were randomly divided into four groups and intraperitoneally injected with (1) PBS vehicle, (2) cisplatin (1 mg/kg) alone, (3) GSH inhibitor (BPTES 5 mg/kg, BCNU 5 mg/kg, or BSO 2 mg/kg, or (4) cisplatin plus GSH inhibitor via 10 injection cycles at 3 day intervals, except for daily injection of BSO (Figure 7A). Tumor formation was measured 6 weeks after transplantation, revealing that tumor growth was suppressed by 17.1% ± 3.76% in the cisplatin monotherapy group (Figures 7B and 7C). Histological analysis revealed that cotreatment with cisplatin and GSH inhibitors significantly suppressed tumor growth in the bladders, and the inhibitory effect was not detected in animals treated with vehicle, cisplatin monotherapy, or GSH inhibitor monotherapy (Figures 7D and S7D). We subsequently investigated the effect of combination cisplatin and GSH inhibitor treatment on expression of the genes related to GSH dynamics. Immunofluorescent staining revealed that xenograft tumors with vehicle treatment exhibited higher levels of GLS, GSR, and GCLM proteins relative to bladder tissues in the sham group, and cisplatin alone further increased these proteins in xenograft tumors relative to the vehicle-treatment group (Figures 7E and S7E). However, this induction was significantly repressed by combination treatment with cisplatin and GSH inhibitors. Consistent with the findings of the in vitro cell model assays, cotreatment with GSH inhibitors and cisplatin efficiently repressed expression of BC stem cell markers, including CD44v6 and KRT14 (Figure 7E). Specifically, some cancer cells with high levels of CD44v6 protein coexpressed GLS protein in xenograft tumors from animals treated with cisplatin alone or vehicle, and the frequency of the CD44v6+/GLS+ coexpressing cells was significantly decreased by combination treatment with both cisplatin + BPTES and cisplatin + BSO (Figure 7F).

To maximize the clinical relevance of this preclinical study, we used an additional GLS inhibitor, CB-839, which is currently undergoing clinical trials for treatment of several cancers.36 Treatment of Cis_NR T24 cells with CB-839 decreased intracellular GSH level and GRC in a dose-dependent manner (Figure S7F). Combination treatment of Cis_NR T24 cells with CB-839 and cisplatin impaired cell growth (Figure S7G) and activated apoptotic cell death (Figures S7H and S7I). CB-839 significantly augmented the antitumor effect of cisplatin in an orthotopic xenograft BC model using Cis_NR BC cells (Figure S7J). Collectively, these results demonstrate that GSH dynamics play a crucial role in determining the response to available chemotherapeutic drugs for MIBC and that cotreatment with GSH inhibitor compounds could potentially increase the sensitivity to chemotherapeutics such as cisplatin in MIBC patients.

Discussion

Radical cystectomy with preoperative cisplatin-based NAC is the standard of care for MIBCs.6,37 However, the response rate remains low, and current clinicopathologic features of MIBC patients and molecular classifiers are not effective in prospectively assessing the NAC response. In the present study, by clustering gene expression profiling and digitalized IHC analysis of TURBT patient specimens, together with real-time live-cell imaging, in vitro MIBC cell models, and in vivo xenograft MIBC models, we demonstrate that the GSH dynamics signature distinguishes distinct subsets of MIBCs by differential responses to NAC and that cotreatment with disruptors of GSH could resensitize MIBC to cisplatin.

With advances in investigative tools such as gene expression analysis, several molecular classification schemes have been proposed to stratify MIBC patients in the assessment of prognosis and response to treatment.8,9,10,11,14,15,23 However, there have been conflicting findings in previous reports comparing NAC response rates across transcriptome-based molecular subtypes using different classification systems. Retrospective analysis of patients that received NAC in our institute revealed that the Ba/Sq classifier and p63 pathways were highly enriched in tumors from NAC responders, but this finding conflicts with recent studies reporting a low NAC response rate in patients with Ba/Sq subtype tumors.14,15 This discrepancy could be explained by differences in the study designs of each cohort.

Compared with the three public cohorts used in the present study, BC tissues in the AMC cohort were microdissected using LCMD to isolate tumor cells. The other studies used macrodissected samples (MDA MVAC and MDA DDMVAC cohorts) or tumor tissue harvested using a 1 mm biopsy punch (NAC metadata cohort). Therefore, samples from these cohorts could contain varying amounts of stromal components, influencing gene expression profiles. Furthermore, NAC regimens and cycles were predefined in the prospective clinical trials (MDA MVAC and MDA DDMVAC cohorts) but varied in the retrospective trials (AMC and NAC metadata cohorts). Finally, the MDA DDMVAC cohort added bevacizumab to DDMVAC. Beyond cohort variation, the use of different definitions of pathological response or different transcriptome analysis pipelines could affect seemingly disparate results.

Despite these variations, the transcriptome profiles of MIBCs from the AMC cohort and three public cohorts collectively demonstrated that, in MIBC, GSH metabolism-related genes were enriched in the NAC NR group, and immune response pathways were enriched in the NAC R group. Specifically, Taber et al. demonstrated increased tumor immune cell infiltration and PD-1 protein levels in patients that responded to NAC.14 In addition, it was reported that the cytotoxic:regulatory T lymphocyte ratio was positively associated with NAC treatment response.38 Therefore, further studies are necessary to investigate the in-depth operative mechanisms for immune response signatures that likely affect chemoresistance to cisplatin-based NAC in MIBCs.

To maintain the redox balance, the flux of glucose and glutamine-derived carbons and cofactors such as NAD(P)H/NAD(P) could be diverted to antioxidant production.22 For the first step in glutamine metabolism, GLS converts glutamine to glutamate, and this metabolic process, known as glutaminolysis, maintains the redox status of cancer cells through multiple mechanisms.39 Glutaminolysis plays a direct role in GSH biosynthesis, providing glutamate. Exchange of intracellular glutamate through the transporter protein xCT/SLC7A11 mediates uptake of cystine, which affects the substrate availability for GSH biosynthesis.17 Furthermore, glutamine metabolism contributes to the maintenance of GSH in its reduced form by supporting production of NADPH through malic acid regulation.21 In this context, glutamine depletion or GLS knockdown could increase ROS levels and promote cellular stress in a wide range of cancer cells. Accordingly, GLS inhibitors increase ROS levels, resulting in cell death and resensitization of chemotherapy-resistant cancer cells.34,40 In the present study, GLS protein upregulation was significantly associated with resistance to NAC in both of the AMC discovery and validation cohorts, suggesting that the GLS-mediated glutamine metabolism could significantly affect the NAC response of MIBCs. The functional significance was further supported by the findings that genetic or chemical disruption of GLS impaired GSH dynamics in Cis_NR BC cells, improving the efficacy of cisplatin chemotherapy in an in vivo xenograft MIBC model.

GLS is a potential therapeutic target, with the CB-839 inhibitor displaying promising effects in several cancers.36 Combination treatment with GLS inhibitors could enhance the efficacy of paclitaxel and cisplatin, which are the most widely used chemotherapeutic agents. Resistance to these chemotherapeutics is a major burden for treatment for multiple cancers, including MIBCs.36 CB-839 in combination with paclitaxel is currently in phase II clinical trials for advanced triple-negative breast cancers (NCT03057600). Notably, the present study provides evidence supporting the clinical utility of GLS, not only to predict the NAC response in MIBCs but also to reverse chemoresistance of cisplatin-based NAC. Furthermore, the present study provides an in vivo proof of concept that CB-839 significantly enhances the efficacy of cisplatin treatment, attenuating resistance to cisplatin-based NAC. To successfully translate this study into clinical settings, further studies to elucidate the precise roles of GLS inhibitors in the chemosensitization of MIBCs should be conducted.

In conclusion, the present study utilized a multiplatform analysis of clinical MIBC samples from four independent cohorts with transcriptome and digitalized IHC profiling, providing insights into the GSH dynamics profiles in NAC-responsive and -resistant MIBCs. The study identified predictive and therapeutic potentials for GSH dynamics in the context of MIBC and established a framework for distinguishing tumor subtypes with clinical options. Components of the GSH dynamics system, including GLS, are relevant parameters for defining the response to presurgical chemotherapy in MIBCs, and this regulatory mechanism could be targeted as a therapeutic strategy for enhancing the accuracy and efficacy of clinical management of MIBC patients.

Limitations of the study

The current retrospective study, with its limited patient count, inherently restricts accurate OS and CSS prediction. This could explain the lack of significant correlation between GLS/CD11c expression and OS/CSS, despite their independent predictive value for pathologic NAC response. Further discussion is available in Figure S4. In addition to the small case count, this study’s limitation lies in transcriptome analysis of LCMD tumors from FFPE TURBT specimens, excluding stroma and the diverse tumor microenvironment. This could explain disparities with other cohort findings, which is further discussed in Figure S1.

To overcome the limitations, transcriptome-based patient stratification methods could be integrated with other comprehensive analyses such as digital pathology or genomic analysis to strengthen their predictive ability, clinical relevance, and utility. Digital pathology analysis of IHC findings could support transcriptome-based subtyping, as IHC assays are a commonly used and clinically applicable ancillary test that provides remarkably consistent results. Furthermore, we developed a machine-learning classifier that could discriminate tumorous epithelial cells from stroma in IHC images. This classifier was adapted to QuPath, an open-source software platform for bioimage analysis, ensuring accurate high-throughput digitalized analysis for separately evaluating protein expression intensities in epithelial and stromal compartments of IHC images. In addition, it will be informative to integrate gene expression subtypes with genomic data, including the presence of genomic instability and hotspot mutations in ERCC241 or BRCA2.14 These integrative multiomics approaches need to be validated in separate studies with a prospective design and a larger number of MIBC cases.

Experimental model and study participant details

Patient sample collection and cohort organization

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of AMC (2020-0064). Patients gave informed consent to use of their samples and relevant clinical data for academic purposes. The study included 63 consecutive MIBC (cT2-4aN0M0) patients who underwent TUR and followed by NAC then curative surgery with available FFPE tumor tissues between August 2011 and August 2017 at AMC. Demographic data on patients including the distribution of median age, gender and NAC treatment regimens are provided in Table S1. All patients were available for the assessment of pathologic response using the surgical specimens, therefore they were designated as the validation cohort. Among the 63 patients, 20 patients who were recorded as “incomplete TUR” and exhibited marked contrast in pathologic response were selected for the discovery cohort. The patients’ clinical information, including NAC, surgical TUR record, tumor progression, and survival time, was obtained from electronic medical records. CSS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and the resultant curves were compared by log-rank test.

All pathologic materials were reviewed by a urological pathologist (Y.M.C.) and reassessed according to the 2016 World Health Organization Tumor Classification, and staged according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer Staging System, 8th edition. NAC response was defined as the absence of invasive tumor or downstaging to ≤ypT1 in the absence of lymph node metastases (ypN0) at pathologic examination of radical or partial cystectomy specimens. Lack of pathologic response was defined as residual tumor invading the muscularis propria and more, or as lymph node metastasis (≥ypT2 and/or ypN0).

Cell culture

Human T24 MIBC cells and their derivatives with differential cisplatin response (Cis_R) and nonresponse (Cis_NR) behaviors27 were maintained in McCoy’s 5a Medium Modified supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS (Hyclone, Pittsburgh, PA, USA) and penicillin/streptomycin (Cellgro, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). The gemcitabine nonresponse (Gem_NR) and response (Gem_R) KU19-19 cells were maintained in RPMI1640 Medium Modified (Hyclone) supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin. The human BC cell-lines 5637, HT1197, HT1376, and RT4 were maintained, as previously reported.3,4 Cells were treated with cisplatin (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA), gemcitabine (Sigma-Aldrich), or GSH-modulating molecules including BPTES (bis-2-(5-phenylacetamido-1,3,4-thiadiazol-2-yl)ethyl sulfide; Sigma-Aldrich), BCNU (Carmustine; Sigma-Aldrich), BSO (buthionine sulfoximine; Sigma-Aldrich), and CB-839 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h at the indicated concentrations.

Orthotopic xenograft MIBC animal model

All animal experiments in this study were approved and performed in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Ulsan College of Medicine (IACUC-2021-12-172). Eight-week-old male NOD/ShiLtJ-Prkdcem1AMCIl2rgem1AMC (NSGA) mice were purchased from JA BIO (Suwon-si, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea). After 1 week of adaptation in the AMC laboratory animal facility, mice were treated with cisplatin-resistant human T24 MIBC cells (Cis_NR T24) at (1.0 × 106 cells in 100 μL) by direct injection into the outer layer of the anterior wall and dome of the bladder, using a 500 μm syringe and a 26-gauge needle, as previously reported.3,4,43 Two weeks after orthotopic implantation of Cis_NR T24 cells, mice were intraperitoneally injected with cisplatin (1 mg/kg) or GSH inhibitor alone including BPTES (5 mg/kg), BCNU (5 mg/kg), BSO (2 mg/kg), or CB-839 (20 mg/kg) or in combination treatment with cisplatin and each GSH inhibitor via ten injection cycles with 3 day intervals, except BSO and CB-839 which were injected daily. The mice and injection sites were monitored every 3 days for 42 days after initial administration of BC cells. At the experimental endpoint, tumors were excised to measure tumor size and perform histological examinations or immunofluorescence staining. Mice were randomly allocated to treatment groups (n = 10/group), and the order of cell transplantation, treatment, and evaluation, as well as daily examinations, was randomized. Investigators involved in tumor size measurements and histological assessments were blinded to the treatment groups.

Method details

Laser capture microdissection (LCMD) and isolation of RNA

LCMD was performed on hematoxylin-eosin-stained tissue slides using a Leica LMD6500 laser capture dissection microscope (Leica Microsystems, Deerfield, IL, USA) as described by the manufacturer. Urothelial carcinoma areas were meticulously microdissected as close as possible to the perimeter of tumor cells, to avoid contamination of stroma and inflammatory cells while avoiding necrotic tumor areas. Total RNA from LCMD samples was isolated using an RNeasy FFPE kit (73504, QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA). RNA quality was assessed with an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer using the RNA 6000 Nano Chip (G2939BA, Agilent Technologies, Amstelveen, The Netherlands), and RNA quantification was performed using an ND-2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Library preparation and sequencing

The construction of library from the isolated RNA was performed using QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq Library Prep Kit (Lexogen, Inc., Austria) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, each total RNA was prepared, an oligo-dT primer containing an Illumina-compatible sequence at the 5’ end was hybridized to the RNA, and reverse transcription was performed. After degradation of the RNA template, second-strand synthesis was initiated by random primers containing an Illumina-compatible linker sequence at the 5’ end. The double-stranded library was purified using magnetic beads to remove all reaction components. The library was amplified to add the complete adapter sequences required for cluster generation. The finished library was purified from PCR components. High-throughput sequencing was performed as single-end 75 sequencing using a NextSeq 500 (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Transcriptome dataset analysis

QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq reads were aligned using Bowtie2.44 Bowtie2 indices were generated either from genome assembly sequences or the representative transcript sequences for aligning the genome and transcriptome. The alignment file was used for assembling transcripts, estimating their abundances, and detecting differential gene expression. Differentially expressed genes were determined based on counts from unique and multiple alignments using coverage in Bedtools.45 RC (Read Count) data were processed based on a Quantile normalization method using the R EdgeR package (R development Core Team, 2020) using Bioconductor.46 Gene classification was based on searches conducted by DAVID (http://david.abcc.ncifcrf.gov/) and Medline databases (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Data mining and graphic visualization were performed using ExDEGA (Ebiogen Inc., Seoul, Republic of Korea). A consensus MIBC classification model was applied to classify samples to the six consensus MIBC subtypes using the R-based Markov cluster algorithm for the Cohen’s Kappa-weighted network construction.10

MetaCore and GSEA transcriptome analysis

Functional transcriptome analysis was performed using MetaCore (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) or GSEA (Broad Institute, Cambridge, MA, USA) software with default settings, which presented core analyses of gene networks, biofunctions, and canonical pathways as well as enrichment of genesets, respectively. In the MetaCore analysis, 1.5-fold up- or downregulation with p < 0.05 was defined as the cutoff value for significant changes. Details of the statistical values and significant genes for biological processes and pathways in MetaCore analysis are described in Data S2 provided as a separate Excel file. The genesets used for GSEA were obtained from the classifiers based on published literature or a curated functional geneset (C2) database using several library sources. Significant differences were determined based on false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25. A detailed list of genesets with the corresponding genes is described in Data S2.

Tissue microarray (TMA) construction and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis

TMA blocks were constructed from FFPE tissue blocks of TUR specimens, which were harvested prior to NAC, using a tissue microarray (Beecher Instruments, Silver Spring, MD, USA). To overcome intratumoral heterogeneity, three representative cores of 0.6 mm diameter were included from different areas of each tumor. When the tumors contained areas of various histologic grades and stages, the TMA was generated in the tumor area of the highest grade and stage.

IHC staining was performed on the TMA constructs using an automated staining system (BenchMark XT; Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, AZ, USA) and an ultraView Universal DAB detection kit (Ventana Medical Systems). Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Detailed antibody information and staining conditions are summarized in the key resources table.

Computational analysis of IHC staining images

IHC slides were scanned with a Pannoramic 250 Flash slide scanner (3D HISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) at ×20 magnification with a resolution of 0.22 μm per pixel and subjected to computational analysis based on QuPath, an open-source software platform for bioimage analysis.42

To separately evaluate the expression intensities in the epithelial and stromal compartments, we developed a machine learning classifier that discriminates tumorous epithelial cells and stroma, in immunohistochemical images stained with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) and counterstained with hematoxylin. First, we selected representative IHC slide images to construct training, test, and validation sets (CBS and CD73 of the discovery cohort; CD11c and CDK1 of the advanced BC cohort not analyzed in this study). Using QuPath, the images were opened, and areas of tumorous epithelial cells and stroma were annotated (Figure S3A). The annotated areas were subsequently segmented by cell detection functionality of QuPath using default parameters (Figure S3B). The QuPath function is intended to identify cells in a given area. To classify the detected cells using a deep learning-based approach, we wrote a QuPath script to extract 224 × 224 pixel-sized patches centered at the centroid of cells on an IHC slide. As a result, 224,101 patches labeled as either “tumor” (96,149 patches) or “stroma” (127,772 patches) were obtained (Figure S3C). Using the StratifiedShuffleSplit method of Scikit-learn, a python library package for machine learning,47 the patches were randomly partitioned into the training set (40%), validation set (30%), and test set (30%) while preserving the percentages of “tumor” and “stroma.” We constructed a model by training a pretrained model provided by PyTorch, an open-source machine learning framework.48 Using ResNet34,49 a state-of-the-art convolutional neural network model for image classification with pretrained parameters as a starting point, a model with an accuracy of 0.96 was obtained (Figure S3D).

For inference, cell patches named by unique ID numbers were extracted from each IHC slide. A python script was written to apply the deep learning model to classify the patches, and to export a table consisting of cell IDs and the result of classification as either “tumor” or “stroma”. A QuPath script was written to load the table and visualize the inference result. While construction of the model, including annotation of tumorous epithelial cells and stroma, was performed by a pathologist (S-.Y.Y.), the classification result was verified by another pathologist (J.A.J.); misclassified areas were adequately corrected if necessary (Figure S3E).

To quantify the levels of protein expression, DAB staining intensity of each cell, represented as the measurement ‘Cell: DAB OD mean’ in QuPath, was exported. For a TMA core, the protein expression levels at the tumorous epithelia and stroma were separately computed by averaging the DAB intensities of cells classified as “tumor” and “stroma,” respectively. Because we had three TMA cores per tumor, the mean results of the three cores were subsequently obtained to represent the tumor expression level (Figure 3A). Most biomarkers validated by qPCR assays showed higher intensity for protein expression in tumor cells than in stromal compartments (Figure S3F).

Analysis of clinical MIBC patient cohorts

Clinicopathological analysis of MIBC patients in the AMC cohort was performed using SPSS version 21.0. All continuous data were compared using a Student’s t-test, and categorical data were compared with the Pearson’s Chi-square, Fisher’s exact, and Kruskal-Wallis tests. Categorical IHC expression data were classified as high versus low expression of the indicated biomarkers based on the cutoff value, which was determined by the heuristic method. Univariate analyses of NAC response were performed to determine the clinical significance of each protein expression and clinicopathological parameter. Statistically significant variables from univariate analyses were analyzed in multivariate analyses, in which binary logistic regression was applied. Independent variables were chosen by backward stepwise selection, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

GSH-recovering capacity (GRC) by real-time live cell imaging

Real-time monitoring of GSH changes in individual live cells were assessed as described previously.29,30,31 This GRC assay allows nondestructive, integrated, and image-based high-throughput assays for qualitative and quantitative aspects of GSH dynamics in human MIBC cells under different culture conditions. The GRC assay was based on the unique properties of FreSHtracer (Fluorescent real-time thiol tracer; Cell2in, Inc., Seoul, Republic of Korea), a reversible chemical probe for GSH.29 Upon reacting with GSH, FreSHtracer exhibits a spectral shift in the λmax of its ultraviolet-visible absorption from 520 nm to 430 nm, resulting in decreased fluorescence emission intensity at 580 nm (F580, λex 520 nm) and increased fluorescence intensity at 510 nm (F510, λex 430 nm).29 Thus, to determine the fluorescence ratios (FRs) of FreSHtracer, fluorescence emissions were measured at 510 and 580 nm after excitation at 430 and 520 nm, respectively. These fluorescence signals during the entire experimental period were recorded in real time in live cells using FreSHcell Q, an automated, high-content, live cell imaging system with an sCMOS camera (Cell2in, Seoul, Korea) at ×200 or ×400 magnification, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence images were analyzed using NIS-Elements AR software (Nikon, Minato-ku, Tokyo, Japan). Each fluorescence image was reconstituted for cell segmentation via the rolling ball process, which corrects image background intensity, followed by the shading correction, which corrects illumination inhomogeneities in the image. From these images, cells were distinguished from the background and segmented based on an artificial intelligence module from NIS-Elements AR software. The results were analyzed to calculate the GI, related initial FRs (for baseline total GSH), and slopes after diamide treatment (for GRC) of each plot, which are presented in Data S2.

NADPH/NADP quantification

The NADPH/NADP+ ratio was determined using an NADP/NADPH Quantitation Colorimetric Kit (K347-100, Biovision Incorporated, Milpitas, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 4 × 106 cells were trypsinized and lysed on ice in extraction buffer, and then incubated at 60°C to decompose the NADP particles. Total NADP/NADPH and NADPH were measured by incubation with NADP cycling enzyme mix. NADP+ and NADPH were detected at OD450nm and presented as the ratio of NADPH to total NADP (NADPH plus NADP+).

Intracellular ROS measurement

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using a DCFDA / H2DCFDA - Cellular ROS assay kit (ab113851, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Briefly, cells were seeded into a dark-wall 96-well plate at 1 × 104 cells/well and allowed to attach overnight. Cells were washed once with PBS and incubated with 10 μM 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFDA) for 30 minutes at 37°C in dark conditions, washed with the kit-provided buffer, and subsequently analyzed on a microplate reader (VICTOR X Multilabel Plate Reader, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) with excitation at 485 nm and emission at 535 nm according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Unlabeled cells were analyzed and used as negative controls.

Glutathione S-transferase (GST) activity assay

GST activity analysis was performed using a Glutathione-S-Transferase activity assay kit (E-BC-K278-S, Elabscience, TX, USA). Briefly, 1 × 105 cells/well were seeded into a 12-well plate overnight, and subsequently treated with 10 μM cisplatin for 24 hours. The cells were then washed with 300 μl pre-chilled PBS and detached by scraping. Cells were collected and lysed by sonication (200 W) for 2 sec with 3 sec intervals for a total time of 5 min, and then centrifuged at 4°C and 10,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was mixed with the assay buffer and absorbance (340 nm) was measured at 20 sec (A1) and 320 sec (A2) to calculate the ΔA, which was obtained from A2 subtracted A1.

Gene expression analysis

Transcript levels of the indicated target genes were quantitated as described previously.50 Total RNA (50 ng) isolated from human MIBC cell-lines was reverse transcribed using Taqman Reverse Transcription Reagents (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA), and the threshold cycle (Ct) was subsequently determined using quantitative PCR (qPCR) as previously described.51 To validate the expression of individual genes in AMC cohort patients, RNA (50 ng) extracted from LCMD samples was reverse transcribed and amplified using SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v2 (634411, Takara, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the amplified cDNA libraries were used for qPCR analysis. The relative expression level of the target genes was determined using the 2-ΔΔCt method, and β2-microglobulin (B2M) was used as the endogenous control gene. All primers used in the qPCR assay are listed in Table S7.

Western blot analysis

Cell extracts (30 μg) were prepared in RIPA lysis buffer (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA) supplemented with the protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche, Indianapolis, IN) and separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gels. The expression levels of the indicated proteins were measured by probing with specific antibodies, which are listed in the key resources table. The uncropped western blot results are presented in Data S1.

Chromatin-immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP analysis was performed using a Magna ChIP G kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA) using 3 μg ChIP grade anti-NRF2 antibody (ab62352, Abcam) or rabbit immunoglobulin (Ig)G control antibodies (2729S, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), as previously described.52 Enrichment of NRF2 protein was calculated as the ratio of bound to unbound amplicon fractions and represented as mean ± SEM of four independent experiments. All primers used in the ChIP assay are listed in Table S7.

RNA interference (RNAi) and ectopic expression

For RNAi-mediated knockdown (KD) or overexpression of genes of interest, shRNAs designed to each target gene or human GLS open reading frame (ORF) were cloned into the pLKO.1 (Sigma-Aldrich) or pCDH-CMV (Addgene, plasmid #72265, gift from Kazuhiro Oka) plasmids, respectively. pEZ-CMV lentiviral plasmids encoding human GSR (EX-Z1404-Lv105) or GCLM (EX-M0714-Lv105) ORF constructs were purchased from GeneCopoeia (Rockville, MD, USA). Lentivirus was produced using a four-plasmid transfection system (Invitrogen) and concentrated using the Lenti-X Concentrator kit (Clontech, Mountain View, CA, USA) as previously described.50,53 Gene expression and functional assays were performed 4 days after lentiviral infection. The information of each ORF and the target sequences for each shRNA are listed in the key resources table and Table S7.

Cell proliferation and apoptosis assays

Cell proliferation capacity was determined using an MTT assay (Sigma-Aldrich). Apoptotic cell death was analyzed using a FITC Annexin-V Apoptosis Detection Kit I (556547, BD Biosciences., San Diego, CA, USA). FITC- and/or propidium iodide (PI)-labeled cell populations were quantified and analyzed using the BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Tumor sphere formation, limiting-dilution, and in vitro cell invasion assays

The stemness features of human MIBC cells were examined by assessing tumor sphere formation and clonogenic capacities, and with an in vitro Matrigel invasion activity, as described previously.3,4

Immunocytochemistry analysis and histological examinations

For immunocytochemistry, human MIBC cells fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) were stained with antibodies against NRF2 (ab62352, Abcam) followed by visualization using Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit antibodies (A11008, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The stained cells were imaged using a Zeiss LSM710 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Munich, Germany).

For histological analysis of xenograft samples, bladders were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 day. After cryoprotection in 30% sucrose for 24 h, each bladder was cut into 20 μm sections using a cryostat (Leica, Lussloch, Germany) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). For immunofluorescence (IF) staining, bladder sections were stained with specific antibodies, which are listed in the key resources table. Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated (A11001 and A11008) anti-mouse and anti-rabbit antibodies or Alexa Fluor 546-conjugated anti-mouse antibody (A11060) were used as secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; D9542; Sigma-Aldrich). The stained samples were imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (EVOS® FL Color Imaging System, Life Technologies).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance for more than three groups was determined using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post-hoc tests. Statistical significance between two groups was analyzed using unpaired Student’s t-tests. Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) or SPSS version 21.0 software. Significance was assumed for ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
