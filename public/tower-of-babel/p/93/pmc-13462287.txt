Sustained efficacy and long-term outcomes of autologous oral mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation for pediatric esophageal anastomotic restenosis
Authors: Yasushi Fuchimoto, Yuki Yamamoto, Akihiro Fujino, Naoki Shimojima, Akihiro Yoneda, Tetsuya Ishimaru, Nobutaka Isogawa, Katsuhiro Arai, Takako Yoshioka, Makoto Abe, Nobuo Kanai, Ryo Takagi, Masanori Maeda, Akihiro Umezawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRefractory anastomotic restenosis following surgery for congenital esophageal atresia (CEA) or congenital esophageal stenosis (CES) remains a serious complication in pediatric surgery. Repeated endoscopic balloon dilatation (EBD) under general anesthesia often impairs growth, feeding function, and overall quality of life (QOL). We have developed a regenerative therapy using autologous oral mucosal epithelial cell sheets. This study aimed to evaluate the long-term safety and efficacy of epithelial cell sheet transplantation for refractory esophageal restenosis in pediatric patients. Between 2018 and 2024, epithelial cell sheet transplantation was performed immediately after EBD in six patients (aged 8–41 years) with refractory anastomotic restenosis following surgery for CEA and/or CES. The cell sheets were fabricated by culturing oral mucosal tissue harvested from each patient in temperature-responsive culture dishes with autologous serum, then non-enzymatically detached and endoscopically transplanted onto the stricture site. Postoperatively, patients were monitored for 48 weeks to assess EBD frequency, endoscopic patency, dietary status, and adverse events. In later cases, evaluation for eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) was also performed. No serious adverse events were observed in any of the patients, confirming the safety of the procedure. One patient showed only temporary improvement and required surgical reconstruction. In four patients, long-term patency was achieved without the need for additional EBD, with the longest recurrence-free period lasting up to five years. Swallowing function, dietary intake, and overall QOL markedly improved in all of these cases. Notably, two patients had concomitant EoE, suggesting that inflammation control may influence therapeutic outcomes. Transplantation of autologous oral mucosal epithelial cell sheets appeared to promote safe and sustained epithelial regeneration and functional recovery in pediatric patients with refractory esophageal strictures. Combining intervention before irreversible fibrotic remodeling is established with control of inflammation and optimization of the local environment to support engraftment may further enhance therapeutic efficacy. Background

Congenital esophageal atresia (CEA) and congenital esophageal stenosis (CES) are representative congenital disorders in the field of pediatric surgery. Advances in perioperative management and surgical techniques have markedly improved survival rates in recent decades. However, anastomotic stricture remains a major postoperative complication, occurring at a high frequency. It has been reported that 30–40% of patients develop anastomotic strictures after CEA or CES repair, and although most cases show temporary improvement with endoscopic balloon dilatation (EBD), 10–20% become refractory and require repeated EBD procedures under general anesthesia1–4. Possible causes of restenosis include anastomotic tension, anastomotic leakage, and gastroesophageal reflux; however, the mechanisms underlying refractoriness have yet to be elucidated. In such cases, feeding difficulties, malnutrition, and growth retardation may occur, leading to significant deterioration in the quality of life (QOL) of both patients and their families.

Various therapeutic approaches have been attempted, including local steroid injection, temporary stent placement, radial incision and cutting (RIC), administration of mitomycin C to suppress scarring, and surgical reconstruction. However, these methods are associated with adverse effects, procedural complications, and unsatisfactory long-term outcomes. Therefore, new treatment strategies are strongly needed for refractory esophageal strictures in children.

We have developed a regenerative therapy in which epithelial cell sheets, fabricated from the patient’s own oral mucosal epithelial cells, are transplanted onto the mucosal tear immediately after EBD5. Oral mucosa possesses the unique ability to heal rapidly with minimal scarring, making it a highly suitable source of transplant material. Moreover, by maintaining the cells in a “sheet” configuration, this approach is expected to achieve superior engraftment and exert localized paracrine effects that cannot be obtained with a dispersed single-cell suspension.

We have previously confirmed the safety of this therapy in our initial clinical application5, and in a subsequent study with additional cases, we reported that the treatment could potentially restore tissue flexibility at the stenotic site and achieve long-term relief from the need for EBD in some patients6. In the present study, we further expanded the number of cases and summarized the clinical outcomes of six pediatric patients, while also examining potential factors influencing therapeutic efficacy, such as the presence of eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) and the anatomical location of the stricture.

Methods

Participants

Eligible patients were those who developed anastomotic stricture after surgical repair of CEA or CES and experienced recurrent restenosis despite undergoing at least five sessions of balloon dilatation (Table 1). Patients were required to be between 5 and 50 years of age (specifically those who had undergone esophageal anastomosis during the neonatal or early infantile period and subsequently experienced recurrent anastomotic strictures) and to provide written informed consent (obtained from the patient, a legal representative, or both according to age; assent was also obtained from minors whenever possible). The lower age limit of 5 years was established because endoscopic procedures and oral mucosal tissue harvesting are technically challenging in younger children, and because most postoperative strictures improve within approximately 18 months after the initial surgery (generally by around 3 years of age). Therefore, patients who continued to require dilatation beyond this period were considered refractory. In addition, based on international treatment algorithms in which surgical re-anastomosis or stent placement is recommended after failure of three sessions of balloon dilatation and up to three additional sessions combined with adjunctive therapy, a threshold of five or more dilatations was adopted to define truly refractory cases1,7. Exclusion criteria included severe underlying diseases (such as significant cardiac, renal, or hepatic disorders), active infections (hepatitis B or C, HIV, HTLV, or syphilis), significant laboratory abnormalities, uncontrolled psychiatric disorders, oral mucosal disease precluding tissue harvesting, a history of drug hypersensitivity or medication use that could interfere with the study, allergy to porcine or bovine products, or any condition deemed inappropriate for participation by the treating physician. Of the six enrolled patients, two had undergone surgery for CEA and/or CES and subsequent treatment for refractory anastomotic restenosis at the National Center for Child Health and Development (NCCHD), while the remaining four were referred from other institutions to participate in this study.

Fabrication of oral mucosal epithelial cell sheets

In accordance with previous studies, cultured autologous oral mucosal epithelial cell sheets were fabricated by using buccal mucosal tissue and serum derived from the patient at CellSeed Inc., a cell processing facility (CPF) (Supplementary Table 1)5,6,8,9. Procedures for fabrication of the epithelial cell sheets are described in standard operating procedures (SOP), and the procedures were carried out according to the SOPs. In brief, buccal mucosal tissue was harvested from the oral cavity of the patient at NCCHD and put into a 50 mL centrifuge tube with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) supplemented with 57.1 µg/mL ampicillin and 28.6 µg/mL sulbactam (Unasin-S, Pfizer, NY, USA), 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Meiji Seika Pharma, Tokyo, Japan), and 1.0 µg/mL amphotericin B (Fungizone, Bristol-Myers Squibb, NY, USA). Blood derived from the patient was also collected into 50 mL centrifuge tubes. The centrifuge tubes with the mucosal tissue and the blood were transported to the CPF at 4 °C and room temperature, respectively. Autologous serum of the patient was prepared by the conventional method; centrifugation two times at 1830xg, and used as a supplement for culture medium. The oral mucosal cells were prepared by treatment with 1000 U/mL Dispase I (Godo Shusei, Chiba, Japan) for two hours at 37 °C and 2.5 mg/mL trypsin and 380 µg/mL ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid tetrasodium (EDTA) salt dihydrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) for 20 min at 37 °C. The disaggregated oral mucosal cells were suspended in a keratinocyte culture medium (KCM) composed of a basal medium consisting of three parts of DMEM and one part of nutrient mixture F-12 Ham (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 40 µg/mL gentamicin (Gentacin, Takata Pharmaceutical, Saitama, Japan), 0.25 µg/mL amphotericin B (Fungizone, Bristol-Myers Squibb, NY, USA), 5% autologous serum, 5 µg/mL insulin (Humulin; Eli Lilly, IN, USA), 10 ng/mL human recombinant epidermal growth factor (Higeta-Syoyu, Chiba, Japan), 1 ng/mL cholera toxin (List Biological Laboratories, CA, USA), 2 nmol/L triiodothyronine (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals), and 0.4 µg/mL hydrocortisone (Saxizon, Teva Takeda Pharma, Aichi, Japan). The epithelial cells were seeded onto temperature-responsive cell culture inserts (CellSeed, Tokyo, Japan) at a density of 7.5 × 104 cells/cm2 and cultured in KCM at 37 °C under 5% CO2 in the cell culture incubator. Medium replacement was implemented according to the SOP, and the supernatant fractions were used for quality control test. After passing quality control, the epithelial cells were transported to the National Center for Child Health and Development at 37 °C. Before transplantation of the epithelial cell sheets onto the esophageal wound, the epithelial cells were harvested as a cell sheet by reducing the temperature from 37 °C to room temperature for retrieval by surgical forceps.

Quality control tests

Quality control tests were performed in accordance with our previous study (Supplementary Table 2)5,6. Culture media collected after media replacement were subjected to sterility tests consisting of (1) cultivation for aerobic and anaerobic bacteria and fungi, (2) endotoxin detection by a limulus amoebocyte lysate assay, and (3) mycoplasma testing by cultivation and detection of DNA derived from mycoplasma pneumoniae by loop-mediated isothermal amplification. These quality control tests were outsourced to SRL, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan).

Quantification for cellular density, viability, and epithelial cell percentage of the cultured autologous oral mucosal epithelial cell sheets were implemented using similar protocols to a previous study5,6. In brief, the epithelial cell sheet was harvested from the temperature-responsive culture insert and treated with trypsin EDTA for 20 min at 37 °C. The disaggregated oral mucosal cells were suspended in KCM and counted with a hemocytometer to calculate cellular density of the epithelial cell sheet. Quantification of cellular viability was determined by the trypan blue dye exclusion assay, and flow cytometry was employed to determine the percentage of oral mucosal epithelial cell in the cell sheet. The BD Cytofix/Cytoperm kit (BD Biosciences, NJ, USA) and Anti-Cytokeratin/Keratin Type II, FITC (American Research Products, MA, USA) were used in accordance with manufacturer’s protocols. Flow cytometry analysis was performed with Gallios Software Ver.1.2 and Kaluza 1.3 ver1.0 (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA).

Endoscopic balloon dilatation (EBD)

As in our previous studies, esophageal dilation was performed under general anesthesia using an esophageal balloon dilation catheter (CRE Fixed Wire, Boston Scientific Corporation, Natick, Massachusetts, USA)5,6. An endoscope (XQ240, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was advanced to the site of the stricture, and a guidewire was passed through the biopsy channel of the endoscope. The outer diameter of the endoscope was 9.8 mm, and the working channel diameter was 2.8 mm. The characteristics of the stricture (location, contour, and estimated diameter) were assessed, and the balloon size was selected according to each patient. The endoscope was then withdrawn while leaving the guidewire in place, and under fluoroscopic guidance, the balloon catheter was positioned at the center of the stricture. The balloon was inflated with radiopaque contrast medium at a pressure appropriate for the selected balloon size and maintained for 180 s; this inflation was repeated three times.

Pediatric cell sheet transplantation device

A novel device for endoscopic cell sheet transplantation was designed5. The device was composed of two parts, a cell sheet carrier and an air tube, as shown in Fig. 3. The carrier was comprised of a body in the shape of a boot, and a medical grade latex balloon sheet which was glued to the body with a biocompatible cyanoacrylate instant adhesive. A female screw as a connector was installed at the proximal end of the body to connect with the air tube. The latex balloon sheet was originally fabricated using the material and production method for ultrasound endoscope balloons. The air tube consisted of a polyurethane tube, stainless male screw connector and proximal hub, attached to a syringe. Since steel wires were braided inside the tube wall, the tube could transfer linear rotation torque from the proximal end to the distal end. The carrier was connected to the air tube in an airtight manner, and was passed through a biopsy channel (> 2.0 mm) of an endoscope. Through the air tube, an operator could supply air into the carrier to expand the balloon and use suction to remove air from the carrier with a syringe. A cell sheet was loaded onto the balloon of the carrier by water surface tension. Then, the cell sheet was placed inside the carrier by air suction, which led the balloon with the cell sheet to adhere on the inner wall of the carrier. Then, the cell sheet could be reliably delivered to the transplantation site in the esophagus through an overtube. After delivery and alignment of the cell sheet within the site, the cell sheet could be attached to the site by balloon inflation to achieve transplantation.

Endoscopic transplantation of the cell sheet

As in our previous studies, epithelial cell sheet transplantation was performed immediately after EBD using a dedicated endoscopic delivery device5,6. The epithelial cell sheet was harvested from the culture insert after reducing the temperature to 20 °C. It was then trimmed with a scalpel to a size compatible with the balloon surface of the transplantation device (approximately 3–5 mm square) and mounted onto the device. The balloon was inflated with air, and the cell sheet was applied to cover the deepest portion of the mucosal tear created by EBD. Gentle compression was maintained for several minutes to secure the sheet. Because the cell sheet can be detached noninvasively by simply lowering the temperature without the use of proteolytic enzymes such as trypsin, extracellular matrix components and cell adhesion molecules are preserved, allowing sutureless engraftment10. Procedural success was evaluated intraoperatively by direct endoscopic visualization.

Follow-up examination

Follow-up examinations were conducted for about 48 weeks after transplantation under conditions consistent with previous reports (Supplementary Table 3)5,6. Eight evaluations were performed during follow-up: (1) measurement of vital signs, (2) determination of difficulty in swallowing and sensation of food impaction, (3) blood tests, (4) X-rays, (5) presence of stenosis by endoscopic examination, (6) sheet fixation and epithelialization by endoscopic examination, (7) presence of stenosis by esophagography, and (8) adverse events. The timing of each test is summarized in Supplementary Table 3. If symptoms such as difficulty in swallowing occurred, endoscopic examination or esophagography were performed as needed. In addition, from Patient 5 onward, given the potential contribution of EoE to the stricture, EoE-related evaluations (endoscopic findings and histopathological examination) were conducted.

Endpoints

The primary outcome of this study was the occurrence of serious adverse events. Secondary efficacy outcomes included the presence or absence of dysphagia-related symptoms, such as the sensation of food impaction during normal meals and the ease with which impacted food could be expelled; the number and interval of repeat EBDs; and the ability of the endoscope to pass through the stricture site during follow-up examinations. Secondary safety outcomes included clinical symptoms and vital signs, laboratory findings (including blood tests and radiographic examinations), abnormalities at the transplantation site confirmed by endoscopy, and the occurrence of any other adverse events.

Results

Between 2018 and 2024, autologous oral mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation was performed in six patients with refractory anastomotic restenosis after surgery for CEA and/or CES. No serious adverse events were observed in any case, confirming the safety of the procedure. The clinical courses of each patient are summarized below.

Patient 1

A 15-year-old boy with CEA required EBD every two weeks before transplantation, with a total of more than 100 procedures. After the first transplantation, the interval between EBD sessions was temporarily prolonged, but later shortened again, leading to a second transplantation at the request of the patient and his family. However, no long-term benefit was obtained, and surgical esophageal reconstruction was ultimately selected (Table 1). Histopathological examination of the resected stricture site revealed marked fibrosis and submucosal thickening6.

Patient 2

A 19-year-old boy with CEA had undergone 2–6 EBDs per year since infancy. Three months after each pre-transplant EBD, he could take only a paste-like diet, but after transplantation, he achieved good luminal patency and could eat a regular diet. Whereas EBD had been required every two months in the year before transplantation, no EBD was required during the 5 years since transplantation (Table 1, Figs. 1A–C, 5). Although comparison of the endoscopic images obtained before transplantation (Fig. 1A) and at 4 years after transplantation (Fig. 1B) shows no marked visual change in the diameter of the stricture or the surrounding tissue appearance, patient-reported outcomes improved following transplantation.

Patient 3

A 13-year-old boy with CES had undergone 2–4 EBDs per year since infancy. Following transplantation, he remained EBD-free for 1 year, with stable oral intake of a regular diet. The duration of each meal shortened from about 2 h to 30 min. Approximately one year after transplantation, the patient complained of dysphagia, and two EBDs were performed between 1 and 1.5 years post-transplantation. Endoscopy before transplantation had revealed extensive white exudate on the esophageal wall, and additional biopsy at the time of EBD identified EoE. Treatment with a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) was initiated for EoE, and no restenosis has occurred for 2 years and 11 months since then (Table 1, Figs. 1D–E, 5).

Patient 4

A 41-year-old woman with CEA had received multiple EBDs since adolescence, but restenosis persisted, and she had discontinued active treatment in her thirties. Before transplantation, her diet was limited to liquid or jelly foods, and QOL was severely impaired. After cell sheet transplantation, she has remained EBD-free for 3 years, maintaining good luminal patency. Before transplantation, a 10 mm endoscope could not pass one month after EBD; however, at 1 year and 3 years post-transplantation, it could easily pass. Both the variety and quantity of food markedly increased, and her QOL improved to the point where she could enjoy eating out (Table 1, Figs. 2, 5).

Patient 5

A 13-year-old boy with CEA had required EBD every 6 weeks before transplantation and had undergone 50 EBDs, 9 RIC procedures, and steroid pulse therapy without sufficient improvement. After transplantation, he has remained EBD-free for 2 years and 2 months, with a markedly improved ability to eat various foods. His body weight increased by 6 kg, and positive psychological and social changes were also observed (Table 1, Figs. 3, 5). Based on the findings in Patient 3, EoE screening was subsequently performed in all patients from Patient 5 onward.

Patient 6

An 8-year-old girl with combined CEA and CES had previously undergone RIC, steroid pulse, and local steroid injection therapies and required EBD every 3 months before transplantation. Because the stricture was located immediately above the gastroesophageal junction, the transplanted sheet was washed away by gastric reflux, preventing stable engraftment. A total of 7 sheets were transplanted, but no engraftment was observed. As a result, the transplantation was unsuccessful, and restenosis occurred approximately 3 months after the procedure, necessitating an additional EBD (Table 1, Figs. 4, 5). This case indicated that in lower esophageal strictures, particularly in patients with a gastrostomy, combined use of retrograde balloon techniques to prevent reflux should be actively considered. Histopathological evaluation confirmed the presence of EoE in this patient as well.

Summary of outcomes

Although the efficacy of cell sheet transplantation varied among cases, several patients achieved long-term EBD-free periods, improved food passage and endoscopic patency, and significant enhancement of nutritional status and QOL. Sustained therapeutic benefit was particularly evident in Patients 2–5. Conversely, some cases required EoE management or strategies to prevent sheet detachment due to gastric reflux.

Discussion

In this study, we performed autologous oral mucosal epithelial cell sheet transplantation for refractory anastomotic restenoses after surgery for CEA and/or CES. As a result, four of six patients (Patients 2–5) achieved a long-term course without the need for EBD, with stricture suppression lasting up to five years5,6. Marked improvement in dietary intake and endoscopic passage was also observed before and after transplantation. This finding indicates not only a reduction or cessation of dilatation procedures but also a sustained recovery of esophageal flexibility and resolution of dysphagia. Previous reports on the prevention of post- endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) strictures in adults have demonstrated that complete epithelialization suppresses fibrosis, and our findings are consistent with this mechanism8,10. Furthermore, in Patient 5, the efficacy of the transplantation was confirmed even though the patient had previously undergone multiple conventional treatments, including steroid pulse therapy (systemic administration of steroids by intravenous infusion) and the RIC procedure, both performed in combination with EBD, without sufficient improvement. This represents a significant therapeutic achievement.

The favorable outcomes observed in this study likely resulted from the combination of several factors. First, the immediate coverage of the mucosal tear after EBD by the transplanted sheet likely protected the wound surface from external stimuli, allowing rapid epithelial regeneration10. This early epithelial coverage may have suppressed inflammation and reduced the risk of excessive fibrosis. Second, the sheet-like structure of the cells itself is a key advantage. Unlike dispersed cell suspensions, the sheet remains in place and continuously releases cytokines and growth factors for a certain period, thereby creating a local “paracrine environment” with anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects11,12. Moreover, sheets harvested non-enzymatically from temperature-responsive culture dishes retain extracellular matrix components and adhesion molecules, which contribute to their high engraftment potential. In addition, oral mucosa, the source material for the sheets, possesses intrinsic properties of rapid wound healing and minimal scarring13,14, which are thought to be influenced by anti-inflammatory cytokines such as TGF-β3 and IL-10, as well as salivary factors15,16. Together, these combined mechanisms likely promoted flexible epithelial regeneration while suppressing inflammation.

Conversely, Patients 1 and 6 did not show favorable outcomes. Patient 1 represented a severe case of post-CEA refractory stricture, having undergone numerous EBD procedures over many years. Histopathological analysis confirmed scarring extending from the submucosa to the muscular layer6, suggesting that although epithelial regeneration may have occurred, the flexibility of the deeper esophageal wall was not restored, making it difficult to prevent restenosis. In Patient 6, the stricture was located near the esophagogastric (EG) junction, where reflux of gastric fluid led to detachment of the transplanted sheet. These findings suggest that performing cell sheet transplantation before advanced fibrotic changes occur in the stricture site—that is, while the number of prior EBD sessions remains limited—may improve therapeutic outcomes. In addition, for cases where the stricture is located near the gastroesophageal junction, implementing concurrent measures to prevent gastric reflux could further enhance the efficacy of the transplantation.

Patients 3 and 6 were found to have concurrent EoE. Recently, EoE has been increasingly recognized as an important gastrointestinal complication following CEA or CES4. EoE is known to promote fibrosis and stricture formation through chronic inflammation17,18. Conversely, pre-existing strictures may induce mechanical stress and local barrier dysfunction in the esophageal epithelium, thereby perpetuating inflammation and exacerbating luminal narrowing, creating a vicious cycle19. In Patient 3, EoE may have already been present before transplantation, and it is possible that PPI therapy alone contributed to improvement. However, significant improvements in dietary content, meal duration, and dysphagia symptoms, as well as freedom from EBD for one year post-transplantation, were all observed prior to PPI administration. It is reasonable to conclude that the cell sheet transplantation itself contributed to the therapeutic effect. After initiating PPI treatment, the patient has remained EBD-free for 2 years and 11 months. EoE is a major contributing factor in postoperative restenosis. Therefore, a combinatorial therapeutic strategy integrating EBD, inflammation control, and epithelial regeneration is considered essential for effective management.

The oral mucosa in children is thought to possess a greater regenerative potential than that in adults due to a higher abundance of epithelial stem cells20. Aging is associated with functional decline and phenotypic alteration of epithelial stem cells, whereas young individuals maintain a larger and more functional stem cell pool. In addition, children are not exposed to harmful factors such as smoking or alcohol, preserving the integrity of epithelial stem cells20. The oral mucosa itself also demonstrates rapid healing and minimal scarring, and salivary components further contribute to efficient wound repair13,14. These features may have enhanced the therapeutic effects of the transplanted sheets. Thus, it is possible that the superior regenerative capacity of epithelial stem cells associated with younger age, as well as the anti-inflammatory microenvironmental advantages provided by the use of oral mucosa, contributed to the therapeutic outcome.

At present, the molecular mechanisms underlying fibrosis suppression and inflammation control by oral mucosal epithelial cell sheets remain unclear. In the future, it will be important to clarify the antifibrotic and anti-inflammatory signatures characteristic of oral mucosal cell sheets through comprehensive transcriptomic and proteomic analyses. In particular, the balance of TGF-β isoforms, regulation of the IL-1/TNF axis, the MMP/TIMP ratio, and epithelial barrier–related molecules such as claudins and the trefoil factor family are noteworthy candidate factors12,14,15. Functional validation of these molecules may lead to the development of next-generation cell sheets with enhanced suppressive effects on fibrosis and inflammation. Furthermore, if this approach can be extended to cell-free therapy using factors or extracts secreted by the cells, it may lead to the development of a novel regenerative treatment that does not require any device, with potential for standardization and broader clinical application12.

Conclusion

Taken together, the long-term success of epithelial cell sheet transplantation is considered to depend on three key factors: (1) intervention before irreversible fibrotic remodeling becomes established, (2) effective control of the inflammatory environment, and (3) maintenance of a local milieu conducive to engraftment. In the future, it will be necessary to perform preoperative screening that includes inflammatory diseases such as EoE and to establish an integrated therapeutic protocol that combines inflammation control with epithelial regeneration. Furthermore, based on molecular insights obtained through comprehensive omics analyses, this approach is expected to evolve into a simpler and more reproducible therapeutic strategy. Specifically, we plan to further evaluate it from the perspective of the source of transplanted material (oral mucosa versus other tissues) and the form of transplantation (cell sheet versus alternative formats). In addition, preparations are underway for a physician-initiated clinical trial based on the findings of this study, with the ultimate goal of clinical implementation and social dissemination.
