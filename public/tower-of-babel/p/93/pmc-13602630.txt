Prognostic significance of nodal subclassification and adjuvant therapy in surgically treated T1-4N1-2M0 non-small cell lung cancer: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Bekir Elma, Ahmet Uluşan, Maruf Şanlı, Ahmet Ferudun Işık
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study evaluated the prognostic significance of nodal subclassification in surgically treated patients with non-small cell lung cancer who had pathological N1-N2 disease, based on the ninth edition of the Tumor-Node-Metastasis classification.MethodsAll patients with pathologically confirmed stage T1-4N1-2M0 non-small cell lung cancer who underwent anatomical lung resection with systematic mediastinal lymph node dissection were retrospectively reviewed. Patients who received preoperative therapy or had incomplete resections were excluded. Nodal involvement was classified as pathological N1, N2a, and N2b; N2 cases were divided into N2a1, N2a2, N2b1, and N2b2 according to skip and station status. In addition, single-zone and multi-zone metastases were evaluated to investigate the spatial distribution of mediastinal spread. Overall and recurrence-free survival were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier and multivariable Cox regression methods.ResultsThe median overall survival was 45, 30, and 19 months for the pathological N1, N2a, and N2b groups, respectively. Patients in the N2a2 subgroup demonstrated the most favorable survival, whereas those in the N2b2 subgroup had the poorest outcomes. Skip metastasis was not associated with any survival benefit, while multi-station and multi-zone involvement were related to worse long-term results. In multivariable analysis, multiple-station disease and postoperative complications were independent adverse factors, whereas younger age and administration of adjuvant chemotherapy were independently associated with improved survival.ConclusionThe ninth edition of the Tumor-Node-Metastasis classification provides a more accurate prognostic stratification for surgically treated patients with pathological N2 disease. Multi-station and multi-zone metastases represent more aggressive patterns, while carefully selected single-station N2a2 cases can achieve survival similar to N1 disease, supporting the clinical usefulness of refined nodal and zone-based subclassification.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, Lymph node metastasis, N2 classification, Skip metastasis, Single-station disease, Prognosis, Surgery

Introduction

Despite advances in multimodal therapies, the prognosis of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) largely depends on nodal involvement. Among patients with mediastinal metastasis, survival varies markedly even within the same stage, reflecting the biological heterogeneity of N2 disease. Several multicenter and real-world studies have shown that outcomes after surgical resection differ according to the number and distribution of metastatic lymph nodes [1–3]. Although surgery remains an essential component of treatment in selected cases, postoperative survival still varies substantially across nodal patterns, highlighting the need for refined prognostic stratification [4].

The Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) classification has evolved to reflect the thoracic disease’s anatomical extent better. The seventh edition first introduced station-based nodal evaluation [5], followed by the eighth edition, which maintained this structure but lacked assessment of nodal burden or spatial spread [6]. The ninth edition addressed these gaps by subdividing mediastinal disease into N2a (single-station) and N2b (multi-station) categories, and for the first time incorporated skip metastasis (N2a1-b2) and zone-based mapping to represent the spatial distribution of metastasis [7]. Early validations have shown that this classification improves prognostic discrimination in resected NSCLC [8–10], yet data from uniformly managed surgical cohorts remain limited.

Emerging evidence suggests that both biological and radiological factors influence nodal dissemination. Larger tumor size, high metabolic activity, and endobronchial invasion have been associated with advanced nodal disease and inferior outcomes [11, 12]. Furthermore, the station- and zone-based approaches appear to capture the spatial heterogeneity of mediastinal spread [13, 14]. However, few studies have integrated these factors into the ninth TNM nodal subclassification framework. Understanding how these features correlate with postoperative survival could improve preoperative staging and inform individualized surgical strategies.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of nodal subclassification according to the ninth TNM edition in surgically treated T1-4N1-2M0 NSCLC. By comparing survival among pN1, pN2a, and pN2b groups and further analyzing subcategories (N2a1-N2b2) and zone-based patterns, we sought to clarify the prognostic heterogeneity of N2 disease and identify clinicopathological factors associated with mediastinal metastasis and long-term survival after complete resection.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients who underwent curative-intent anatomical lung resection with systematic mediastinal lymph node dissection at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Gaziantep University Faculty of Medicine, between January 2006 and December 2024. Clinical and pathological data were reviewed from a prospectively maintained institutional database.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) pathologically confirmed NSCLC staged as T1-4N1M0 or T1-4N2M0 according to the 9th TNM edition; (2) complete (R0) resection with mediastinal lymph node dissection per ESTS guidelines; and (3) no history of neoadjuvant therapy.

Patients were excluded if they had incomplete resection (R1/R2), pathologic N0 or M1 disease, or insufficient data. All cases were restaged according to the 9th TNM classification. N categories were confirmed through histological examination of dissected nodes.

Preoperative evaluation and clinical staging

All patients underwent thoracic computed tomography (CT), and 18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET-CT), with brain MRI performed when clinically indicated. Patients with clinical N0 or N1 disease on CT and PET-CT were generally considered suitable for upfront surgical resection. Patients with radiologically suspected multi-station N2 disease or features suggesting extracapsular nodal extension were not considered candidates for primary surgery and were typically referred for induction therapy according to institutional practice. In cases with suspected single-station N2 involvement or equivocal mediastinal lymph node findings (short-axis > 10 mm or SUVmax > 2.5), invasive mediastinal staging was performed using endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) or cervical mediastinoscopy. Patients with pathologically confirmed multi-station N2 disease or extracapsular nodal extension were referred for induction therapy and were therefore excluded from the present upfront surgical cohort.

Endobronchial tumor presence was defined as a visible intraluminal tumor detected during preoperative bronchoscopy.

Surgical procedures

Surgery was performed by thoracic surgeons using posterolateral thoracotomy or VATS. Systematic mediastinal lymph node dissection included removal of at least three stations, including the subcarinal nodes. Lobectomy was the standard procedure; bilobectomy or pneumonectomy was performed when required for complete resection.

The extent of lymphadenectomy corresponded to the primary tumor location:

Right-sided tumors: Stations 2R, 4R, 7, 8, 9

Right-sided tumors: Stations 2R, 4R, 7, 8, 9

Left-sided tumors: Stations 5, 6, 7, 8, 9

Left-sided tumors: Stations 5, 6, 7, 8, 9

Hilar and interlobar nodes (stations 10–11) and lobar/subsegmental nodes (stations 12–14) were dissected en bloc with the specimen.

Pathologic evaluation and nodal classification

All specimens were examined by thoracic pathologists blinded to clinical data. Histologic typing followed the WHO classification. The number of examined and metastatic nodes, involved stations, and skip patterns were recorded.

Nodal status was categorized as:

pN1: peribronchial or hilar involvement

pN1: peribronchial or hilar involvement

pN2a: single-station mediastinal metastasis

pN2a: single-station mediastinal metastasis

pN2b: multi-station metastasis

pN2b: multi-station metastasis

Subgroups were defined as N2a1 (single-station skip), N2a2 (single-station non-skip), N2b1 (multi-station skip), and N2b2 (multi-station non-skip). Mediastinal nodal distribution was also evaluated as single- vs. multi-zone.

Postoperative treatment and follow-up

Adjuvant chemotherapy (cisplatin-based doublet) was recommended for all N-positive patients unless contraindicated. Postoperative radiotherapy was selectively applied in cases with extracapsular nodal invasion or other high-risk pathological features. Patients were followed with clinical evaluation and thoracic CT every 3–6 months for 2 years, and annually thereafter. Recurrence was confirmed radiologically or histologically.

Endpoints and statistical analysis

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from surgery to death from any cause or last follow-up, and recurrence-free survival (RFS) as the interval from surgery to the first recurrence or death. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. When comparisons involved more than two groups, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mantel-Cox method. Numbers at risk were presented below the survival curves. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariable analysis were entered into a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model to identify independent prognostic factors. Variables entered into the multivariable Cox regression model were selected based on clinical relevance and univariable screening. Overall pathological stage was not included in the multivariable model because it inherently incorporates nodal status and could introduce conceptual overlap with nodal classification variables. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Tables 1 and 2 summarize the clinicopathological characteristics of the 332 included patients. Of these, 221 (66.6%) had pN1 and 111 (33.4%) had pN2 disease. The median age was 63 without a significant difference among nodal subgroups (p = 0.058). Males predominated (91.3%) and were more frequent in pN2 cases (p = 0.001). Tumor size differed significantly, being largest in pN1 and smallest in pN2b patients (p = 0.021). Endobronchial tumors were more common in N2 disease (p = 0.001). Pneumonectomy was performed more often in advanced nodal stages (p = 0.037).

Histologically, squamous carcinoma was most frequent (56.9%), followed by adenocarcinoma (29.8%). Adenocarcinoma was more common in skip than non-skip metastasis, although the difference was insignificant (p = 0.362). Stage distribution differed across nodal groups, with pN2b patients predominantly in stage IIIB and pN2a/pN1 mostly in stage IIIA or earlier (p = 0.001). Adjuvant chemotherapy rates were similar, whereas radiotherapy was significantly more frequent in N2b patients (p = 0.001). No significant demographic or clinical differences were observed between skip and non-skip N2 groups.

Regarding N2 subclassification and zone-based distribution (Table 2), tumor diameter was comparable among subgroups (p = 0.573), but adenocarcinoma predominated in N2a2 (p = 0.015). N2b2 and multi-zone involvement were more frequently observed in patients with stage IIIB disease, which is consistent with the current TNM staging structure (p = 0.002 and p = 0.001).

Regarding preoperative mediastinal staging, 57 of the 111 patients with pathological N2 disease (51.4%) had PET-suspected mediastinal nodal involvement, whereas 54 (48.6%) had no PET evidence of N2 disease. All PET-positive patients underwent invasive mediastinal staging with EBUS-TBNA and/or mediastinoscopy. Among these 57 patients, 31 had no pathologically confirmed mediastinal involvement preoperatively and therefore proceeded to upfront surgery, whereas 26 had confirmed single-station N2 disease and were also treated surgically. Final pathology showed multi-station pN2 disease in 20 PET-positive and 15 PET-negative patients, indicating that occult or underestimated mediastinal disease remained frequent despite standard preoperative staging.

OS and RFS by nodal subclassification

According to nodal status, the median OS was 45 months for pN1, 30 months for pN2a, and 19 months for pN2b, with 5-year OS rates of 42.1%, 33.8%, and 12.1%, respectively. While OS did not differ significantly between pN1 and pN2a (p = 0.173), survival was significantly worse in pN2b than in pN1 (p = 0.001, Fig. 2A). The difference between pN2a and pN2b did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.080).

For RFS, the median values were 41, 24, and 19 months in pN1, pN2a, and pN2b, respectively, with corresponding 2-year RFS rates of 59.5%, 48.5%, and 39.2%. RFS was significantly worse in pN2b compared with pN1 (p = 0.010), whereas differences between pN1 and pN2a (p = 0.146) and between pN2a and pN2b (p = 0.306) were not significant (Fig. 2B).

Skip vs. non-skip N2

When stratified according to skip status, median OS was 26 months in non-skip N2 patients and 23 months in skip N2 patients, with 5-year OS rates of 31.0% and 20.6%, respectively. The two groups had no statistical difference in OS (p = 0.165, Fig. 3A). Median RFS was 25 months in the non-skip group and 19 months in the skip group, with 2-year RFS rates of 49.2% and 44.7%, respectively. Although the non-skip group had slightly better outcomes, the differences were insignificant (p = 0.365, Fig. 3B).

N2 subgroups (N2a1, N2a2, N2b1, N2b2)

Among the 111 patients with pathological N2 disease, subgroup distribution was N2a1 (n = 25), N2a2 (n = 51), N2b1 (n = 7), and N2b2 (n = 28). Across the four N2 subgroups, median OS was 19 months in N2a1, 47 months in N2a2, 39 months in N2b1, and 19 months in N2b2, with 5-year OS rates of 18.2%, 40.1%, 21.5%, and 4.5%, respectively. Survival was significantly better in N2a2 than in N2a1 (p = 0.039) and N2b2 (p = 0.018). For RFS, median values were 19, 30, 19, and 21 months in N2a1, N2a2, N2b1, and N2b2, respectively, with corresponding 2-year RFS rates of 34.3%, 54.1%, 30.0%, and 38.7% Collectively, these findings indicate that N2a2 patients had the most favorable outcomes, whereas N2b2 patients had the poorest prognosis. Zone classification (single-zone vs. multi-zone)

Evaluation by zone-based classification revealed a median OS of 30 months (95% CI: 15.9–44.0) in single-zone and 15 months (95% CI: 1.1–28.9) in multi-zone patients, with 5-year OS rates of 32.3% and 4.4%, respectively (p = 0.092; Fig. 5A). For RFS, median values were 24 months (95% CI: 15.0–33.0) and 25 months (95% CI: 5.1–44.9) for single- and multi-zone patients, with corresponding 2-year RFS rates of 46.7% and 42.0% (p = 0.676, Fig. 5B). Although statistical significance was not reached, these findings may suggest a possible trend toward poorer long-term survival in patients with multi-zone mediastinal involvement and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Cox regression analysis

Cox regression analyses for overall survival are presented in Table 3. In univariable analysis, older age, postoperative complications, higher pT category, pN status, and absence of adjuvant chemotherapy were associated with worse survival. In the multivariable Cox regression model, age < 65 years (HR 0.543, 95% CI 0.412–0.716, p < 0.001) and receipt of adjuvant chemotherapy (HR 1.980, 95% CI 1.457–02.688, p < 0.001) were independently associated with improved survival. Conversely, postoperative complications (HR 1.456, 95% CI 1.039–2.041, p = 0.029), pT4 tumors (HR 1.649, 95% CI 1.050–2.589, p = 0.030), and pN2b status (HR 2.251, 95% CI 1.477–3.433, p < 0.001) were identified as independent adverse prognostic factors.

Discussion

This study evaluated surgically treated T1-4N1-2M0 NSCLC patients and suggests that nodal subclassification according to the 9th TNM edition may provide more refined prognostic stratification compared with conventional N staging. OS and RFS declined stepwise from pN1 to pN2a and pN2b, suggesting that the extent of mediastinal involvement, rather than its mere presence, may have a greater influence on postoperative outcomes. The comparable survival between pN1 and pN2a, contrasted with the poorer outcomes of pN2b, is consistent with the findings of Park et al. and Xu et al., who proposed that multi-station nodal metastasis reflects biologically advanced disease with limited curative potential [15, 16]. These observations may support the concept that mediastinal involvement represents a biological continuum, in which single-station disease may still fall within a surgically treatable window.

The 5-year overall survival observed in the pN1 group in our cohort was somewhat lower than that reported in several contemporary surgical series. However, as shown in Table 1, the pN1 group had a higher proportion of adverse clinicopathological features compared with the N2 groups, including a greater prevalence of squamous cell carcinoma, a higher rate of endobronchial tumors, and a larger proportion of pneumonectomies. These factors are known to negatively influence survival and may partly explain the relatively modest survival observed in the pN1 cohort.

Analysis based on skip status revealed no significant difference in survival between skip and non-skip N2 metastasis. This finding accords with the reports of Katsumata et al. and Kojima et al., who found that skip involvement was not an independent prognostic factor, whereas the number and extent of involved stations had more substantial prognostic weight [14, 17]. Similarly, Wei et al. emphasized that quantitative nodal burden might better reflect prognosis than topographic patterns alone [18]. Nevertheless, Wang et al. (2023) observed that skip N2 metastasis was associated with superior survival in stage III adenocarcinoma, implying that skip spread could represent a distinct biological subtype with limited mediastinal dissemination [19]. In our cohort, multi-station rather than skip distribution was the principal determinant of outcome. Conversely, Li et al. and Zhao et al. found that skip metastasis might confer better survival, particularly in adenocarcinoma, highlighting potential histologic or population-based variability [20, 21]. The absence of a skip-related survival advantage in our data may partly reflect the predominance of squamous histology, which tends to exhibit more contiguous rather than discontinuous nodal progression.

Among N2 subgroups, single-station non-skip metastasis (N2a2) was associated with the most favorable prognosis, whereas multi-station non-skip disease (N2b2) showed the poorest outcomes. These patterns suggest the potential prognostic relevance of combining anatomical extent with skip status, as previously reported by Wang et al. and Kim et al. [9, 10]. Our findings further highlight the clinical heterogeneity within pathological N2 disease and support the relevance of nodal subclassification proposed in the 9th TNM classification.

Zone-based analysis revealed a tendency toward inferior OS and RFS in multi-zone metastasis compared with single-zone disease, although statistical significance was not achieved. This pattern resembles the findings of Yun et al. and Kim et al., who noted poorer outcomes in multi-zone N2 involvement [9, 13]. Although the zonal model is not yet widely implemented, it may complement the station-based system by capturing the spatial complexity of mediastinal dissemination. Thus, spatial nodal mapping could offer additional prognostic insight beyond the absolute number of stations involved.

The Cox regression results were consistent with these findings. Advanced nodal category (pN2b) and postoperative complications independently predicted worse survival, whereas younger age, earlier stage, and receipt of adjuvant chemotherapy were favorable prognostic indicators. The observed survival benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy aligns with the reports of Hayakawa et al. and Ağaoğlu Şanlı et al., who showed improved outcomes, particularly among patients with single-station or skip N2 disease, -following postoperative therapy [3, 4]. In our cohort, adjuvant chemotherapy was associated with improved survival, supporting its continued role within multimodal management after complete resection. However, this association should be interpreted cautiously given the retrospective design and the potential for treatment-selection bias. Because this was a retrospective study, patients with better postoperative recovery and functional status were more likely to receive adjuvant treatment, which may have introduced selection bias and led to an overestimation of its apparent survival benefit. In addition, a standardized performance status variable such as ECOG was not consistently available in the present dataset and therefore could not be adjusted for in the multivariable analysis.

Our findings are broadly consistent with the 9th TNM proposal, which refines the N descriptor into N2a (single-station) and N2b (multi-station) categories and highlights clinically relevant heterogeneity within them. Consistent with this, Wei et al. demonstrated that the number of metastatic nodes provides stronger prognostic separation than conventional N staging, reinforcing the predictive role of nodal burden [18]. These results suggest that future staging refinements could integrate both the quantitative burden and spatial extent of metastasis to achieve higher prognostic precision.

The recent EORTC consensus by Houda et al. also reported that only single-station, non-bulky N2 disease was widely considered resectable across disciplines, emphasizing that resectability depends not only on anatomy but also on tumor morphology and institutional experience [22]. Our results, derived exclusively from R0 resections, align with this perspective and further indicate that prognosis differs substantially between single- and multi-station N2 disease. A careful multidisciplinary assessment is essential for patient selection, surgical planning, and optimizing postoperative adjuvant strategy.

Study limitations and clinical implications

This retrospective, single-center study is subject to inherent selection bias. At our institution, patients with clinically evident multi-station N2 disease or extranodal extension were typically referred for induction therapy. As a result, the present cohort represents a surgically selected population with relatively lower mediastinal tumor burden. Therefore, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all patients with N2 disease. The limited sample size of specific subgroups, especially N2a1 and N2b1, may reduce statistical power. Our subgroup analyses should be interpreted carefully and primarily considered as exploratory observations from a single-center cohort. In addition, molecular data were unavailable for most cases. In addition, adjuvant treatment protocols evolved over the study period, which might have introduced heterogeneity in postoperative management. Nevertheless, uniform surgical principles and systematic mediastinal dissection provide a consistent framework for evaluating nodal subclassification.

Clinically, these results highlight the diversity of pathological N2 disease. Patients with limited, single-station involvement may experience survival comparable to N1 cases, suggesting that surgery may remain a reasonable component of multimodal treatment in selected individuals. Conversely, multi-station or multi-zone disease was consistently associated with inferior survival, underscoring the need for comprehensive multimodal therapy and vigilant follow-up. The findings also reinforce the value of multidisciplinary tumor boards, where individualized treatment planning can balance surgical benefit against systemic therapy needs.

Future perspectives

Further multicenter, prospective investigations are warranted to validate these findings and refine nodal classification by incorporating anatomical and quantitative dimensions, such as the number or ratio of metastatic nodes. Emerging imaging and radiomic technologies may enhance preoperative detection of complex nodal disease and support personalized staging algorithms. Integrating refined nodal descriptors with molecular, genomic, and immune markers may ultimately yield more biologically grounded staging and treatment paradigms for surgically resected NSCLC. Ultimately, such integrative approaches may help refine risk stratification and guide individualized treatment strategies in surgically treated NSCLC.
