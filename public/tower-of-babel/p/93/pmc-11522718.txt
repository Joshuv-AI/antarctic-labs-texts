Unveiling Rheumatoid Arthritis
Authors: Muhammad Majid, Ankit Agrawal, Aro Daniela Arockiam, Akiva Rosenzveig, Ashwin Kumar, Jaideep Singh Bhalla, Muhammad Sohaib Asghar, Thomas Shimshak, Chad Brands, Allan L Klein
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 69-year-old man presented with corticosteroid-dependent colchicine-resistant chronic recurrent pericarditis initially thought to be idiopathic in etiology. Transitioning from anakinra to rilonacept revealed rheumatoid arthritis as the underlying cause. Anakinra demonstrated superior efficacy in managing both pericarditis and rheumatoid arthritis symptoms.Key Words: anakinra, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, IL-1 blockers, rheumatoid arthritis, recurrent pericarditis, rilonacept, transthoracic echocardiography

History of Presentation

A 69-year-old man presented to the rheumatology clinic with new-onset generalized arthralgias, worse in the bilateral ankles and feet, accompanied by bilateral foot swelling, which was worse at night. The pain was functionally limiting, preventing him from ambulating. Notably, these symptoms began a few weeks after tapering off anakinra and initiating rilonacept. Importantly, the patient denied recurrent chest pain flares since rilonacept initiation.

On physical examination, the patient was normotensive, with normal oxygen saturation. There was no limitation of plantarflexion or dorsiflexion of either ankle, but there was limited range of motion and pain with eversion and inversion bilaterally. In addition, there was a small effusion on the left ankle with generalized tenderness to palpation as well as pain at the base of the fifth metatarsophalangeal joint on the right foot and the first metatarsophalangeal on the left foot. Otherwise, there was no evidence of synovitis of the hands, knees, or elbows.

With regard to the cardiac examination, there was no evidence of jugular venous distention or Kussmaul sign. Cardiac auscultation revealed regular rate and rhythm, with no pericardial friction rub or knock appreciated. Lung auscultation revealed clear breath sounds bilaterally, and there was no peripheral edema observed.

Past Medical History

The patient’s medical history included idiopathic recurrent pericarditis that was complicated by numerous pericardial effusions and tamponade requiring pericardiocentesis, nonischemic cardiomyopathy with reduced ejection fraction, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease stage 3a, and hypertension.

Initial workup for pericarditis revealed elevated C-reactive protein levels of 12.6 mg/dL and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 57 mm/h, indicating significant inflammation. Transthoracic echocardiogram upon initial presentation demonstrated findings consistent with pericardial involvement, including a small pericardial effusion and annulus reversus without evidence of significant respiratory flow variation across the mitral and tricuspid valves. Serial cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging revealed severe pericardial inflammation characterized by late gadolinium enhancement and pericardial edema.

The medical management of the patient’s pericarditis was intensified to a regimen including ibuprofen 800 mg 3 times daily, colchicine 0.6 mg twice daily, and prednisone 40 mg with a tapering schedule every 2 to 4 weeks for presumed idiopathic pericarditis. However, despite triple therapy, the patient continued to experience similar chest pain, prompting a switch to anakinra 100 mg. Prednisone was discontinued, and the patient experienced significant improvement in symptoms while on anakinra. However, the patient experienced a recurrence of symptoms repeatedly after attempting to taper off anakinra, including the current episode of chest pain (Figure 1). Pericardial inflammation on CMR had previously improved following the initiation of anakinra therapy, but recurrence of pericardial inflammation on CMR was present after sudden discontinuation of anakinra (Figure 2).

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) flare previously controlled with anakinra but exacerbated in the setting of transition to rilonacept.

Investigations

The newly emerging joint pain and swelling prompted a re-evaluation of autoimmune markers. Subsequent tests revealed positive rheumatoid factor (25 IU/mL), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (CCP) antibodies (81 units), and qualitative CCP antibody immunoglobulin G, resulting in the diagnosis of RA. Notably, these tests were previously performed at the time of diagnosis, and the results had been negative, although they were performed while the patient was on steroid therapy. Follow-up echocardiogram demonstrated resolution of pericardial effusion and annulus reversus, with no respirophasic variation across the mitral and tricuspid valves. Subsequent CMR showed improvement in pericardial inflammation and edema (Figure 3).

Management

The patient was started on azathioprine, but joint pain and swelling persisted. Subsequently, rilonacept was switched back to anakinra, and azathioprine was replaced with hydroxychloroquine, resulting in resolution of symptoms.

Discussion

This case presents a unique and complex clinical scenario involving a 69-year-old man with corticosteroid-dependent, colchicine-resistant chronic recurrent pericarditis, initially considered to be idiopathic but eventually determined to be secondary to RA. Initially thought to be autoinflammatory, it then morphed into an autoimmune condition in which interleukin (IL)-1 blockers are usually not given. However, recently there has been an interest in treating autoimmune pericarditis with IL-1 blockers. The patient’s presentation is notable for the unusual presentation of pericarditis as the initial symptom of undiagnosed RA.1 The atypical presentation underscores the importance of considering autoimmune etiologies, particularly in patients with refractory pericarditis and a history of autoimmune disease.

Diagnostic challenges were encountered in distinguishing between idiopathic pericarditis and autoimmune pericarditis.2 Despite initial negative autoimmune workup results, including rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibodies, the subsequent clinical course and serologic findings supported the diagnosis of RA.3 This highlights the limitations of relying solely on initial laboratory investigations and the importance of clinical vigilance and a high index of suspicion for underlying autoimmune conditions.

CMR played a pivotal role in the diagnostic evaluation of recurrent pericarditis in this case. Serial CMR imaging with late gadolinium enhancement demonstrated severe pericardial inflammation, which improved significantly upon the initiation of anakinra. However, recurrence of pericardial inflammation and chest pain occurred after sudden anakinra withdrawal, highlighting the dynamic nature of pericarditis and the importance of serial imaging in monitoring treatment response.4

The transition from anakinra to rilonacept unmasked RA symptoms, leading to a delayed diagnosis. This highlights the need for clinicians to consider medication effects and closely monitor patients for changes in clinical presentation, particularly in the context of autoimmune diseases with variable manifestations. Additionally, this case also suggests that the use of certain medications, such as anakinra, may have masked RA symptoms, and as those medications were tapered, the RA symptoms became more evident.

In the management of pericarditis, IL-1 blockade stands out as a promising therapeutic approach compared to traditional anti-inflammatory treatments.5,6 Notably, IL-1 blockade does not hinder the response of T and B lymphocytes to infectious antigens.7 Furthermore, IL-1 blockade is associated with mild, self-limiting side effects, if any, distinguishing it as a favorable treatment option.7 Anakinra, a synthetic IL-1 receptor antagonist, effectively impedes the actions of circulating IL-1α and IL-1β by specifically binding to IL-1 receptors.6 Initially approved for RA in 2001, anakinra has demonstrated efficacy in various severe inflammatory conditions.7 Conversely, rilonacept, an IL-1α and IL-1β trap, shares an indication for recurrent pericarditis but lacks approval for RA treatment.8 Thus, although both anakinra and rilonacept are used in clinical practice to treat chronic recurrent pericarditis, this case may suggest that anakinra is more efficacious in treating chronic recurrent pericarditis secondary to RA. Future investigations should focus on comparing the effectiveness of IL-1 inhibitors in addressing RA-related pericarditis.

Overall, this case underscores the complexity of managing autoimmune-mediated pericarditis and RA, highlighting the importance of clinical vigilance, individualized treatment approaches, and further research into optimal management strategies.

Follow-Up

Upon follow up, the patient was doing well, with no further chest pain or arthralgias while being maintained on anakinra and colchicine for his pericarditis symptoms and hydroxychloroquine for his RA.

Conclusions

Pericarditis can rarely manifest as an initial symptom of previously undiagnosed RA, emphasizing the importance of considering autoimmune etiologies. Anakinra may be preferred over rilonacept for recurrent pericarditis secondary to RA, but further studies are needed to establish optimal treatment strategies. The International Position Statement highlights advances in multimodality cardiac imaging and provides updated guidelines for managing pericarditis.9
