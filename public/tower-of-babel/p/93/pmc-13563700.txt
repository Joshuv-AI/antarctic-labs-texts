Cross-national analysis of learning approaches, achievement motivation, and academic success among nursing students
Authors: Lizy Sonia Benjamin, Sumathi Robert Shanmugam, Allen Joshua Dominguez, Aida S Alqarni, Iman Awad Siddig Mohammed, Kawther Eltayeb Ahmed, Maysa M Mohsen, Rania A Zaki, Thilagavathi Krishnasamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcademic success among nursing students is vital for developing the professional skills necessary to ensure the safety and quality of patient care. This study examines the relationship between learning approaches (Deep, Strategic, and Surface), achievement motivation, and academic performance among nursing students.MethodsThis study employed a sample of 770 participants, balanced by country (n = 110 per country) but not by year of study. With 110 nursing students recruited from each of the seven participating countries through a multi-phase process designed to ensure cross-national data integrity. The research was conducted across seven different countries: Egypt, India, Indonesia, Jordan, Oman, Saudi Arabia, and Sudan. The Approaches and Study Skills Inventory for Students, Achievement Motivation Scale and academic performance tools were adopted in this study to assess the study approaches, achievement motivation and academic performance of the nursing students respectively.ResultsDeep learning (DL) was found to be the strongest positive factor (p < .001) for predicting high achievement, each unit increase in DL is associated with an 8.7% increase in the odds of being a high achiever (OR = 1.087), not the probability. Conversely, surface learning (OR = 0.864) and marital status (OR = 0.124) were significant negative factors; each increased the likelihood of an individual achieving a lower level of academic achievement.ConclusionDeep learning and the active cognitive search for meaning have a significant association with academic success for nursing students. This shows that deep learning is significantly more important than achievement motivation or SL. However, a “pedagogical gap” exists as engagement in all three types of learning declines during the last year, demonstrating that maintaining deep engagement is the most important, yet most at-risk, factor for achieving excellence in both clinical and academic practice.Keywords: Learning approaches, Achievement motivation, Academic performance, Nursing, Students

Introduction

Success in academia of nursing students is critical to developing professional skills, and ultimately impacts the safety and quality of patient care [1, 2]. Due to the rigorous nature of nursing programs, understanding the psychological and situational factors that contribute to their success has become an urgent issue for nursing faculty [3]. Although there is existing research regarding many predictors of success, the interaction between students’ use of learning strategies and their levels of achievement-motivated behavior remains largely unexplored in virtually every geographic and educational setting [4].

This investigation is centered on the tripartite typology of deep, strategic, and surface learning. Deep learning (DL) involves meaningful synthesis of knowledge and SL refers to structured, purposeful goal-directed behaviors while surface learning is characterized by rote memorization [5, 6]. All three types represent aspects of achievement-motivation. As per Self-Determination Theory (SDT), when the fundamental psychological needs (i.e., autonomy, competence, and relatedness) are satisfied, they foster autonomous motivation and deep learning. This study, however, did not directly assess those need constructs. Rather, we investigated achievement motivation and learning approach as operational variables that may capture SDT-derived processes, recognizing that our data could never directly test SDT mechanisms [7].

Student academic activity is highly malleable over time; therefore, it is heavily influenced by how and in what contexts demographic and other relevant variables are operationalized. Moreover, motivation has been demonstrated to fluctuate significantly within cohorts of students transitioning from novice first year students to practitioner-competent final year students [7]. Furthermore, the cultural/societal context within which each respective country is situated influences the preparedness to engage in independent/ self-directed learning [8]. However, other contextual factors (e.g. age and marital status) create unique barriers. For example, although mature students have a higher probability of being successful due to prior experiences [9], marital status may present conflicting obligations that can impede students’ capacity to adhere to the demands of high-level academic expectations [10].

Very few studies have assessed these variables using a multi-country design simultaneously. This study aims to address this void by assessing the interrelationship of the three variables (learning approaches, achievement motivation and academic performance) across seven different countries. This study tests four hypotheses: First is, both deep and SL will be positive predictors of academic performance and surface learning will be a negative predictor; second is greater achievement motivation will positively relate to a more organized and strategically focused studying style; third both country and year of study will have a statistically significant effect on the extent to which learners are engaged; and fourth, both marital status and age will have a statistically significant effect on learners’ capacity for sustained academic excellence. The objective of this cross-national study was to investigate the relationships between the learning approaches of nursing students (deep, strategic, surface) and their academic performance.

Methods

Design, setting, population, and sample

This study employed a cross-sectional descriptive design to investigate the learning approaches, academic performance, and achievement motivation.

The sample size for this study was determined using power analysis (G*Power 3.1.9). To ensure sufficient sensitivity for the planned seven-country ANOVA (alpha = 0.05, power = 0.80, effect size f = 0.15), a minimum total sample of 612 was required. Furthermore, to detect the anticipated smaller effect sizes in achievement motivation (eta2 approximately 0.013), a sample of at least 734 was calculated as necessary. To enhance robustness and account for potential attrition, the target was set at 110 participants per country (N = 770). The final achieved sample of 770 provided a post-hoc power of 0.82 for motivation analysis and 0.90 for cross-country comparisons, ensuring the statistical integrity of the findings.

A convenience sampling method was used to recruit nursing students from Universities A, B, C, D, E, F, and G. The university in Saudi Arabia, served as the primary recruitment center. Inclusion criteria for the study were participants to be voluntarily and informed aged between 18 and 40 years of age. Exclusion criteria were: (1) students currently on academic leave or suspension; (2) students enrolled in non-nursing degree programs; (3) participants with incomplete survey responses (20% missing data); and (4) students who did not provide informed consent.

Instruments

Data were collected using a questionnaire that included several demographic characteristics such as age, gender, marital status, years of study, country. Moreover, three questionnaires were used.

First is the Approaches and Study Skills Inventory for Students (ASSIST): The ASSIST has 52 items, all of which relate to three different ways (deep, strategic and surface) of studying and learning. Respondents rated every item with a five-point Likert scale; 5 = agree, 4 = somewhat agree, 3 = unsure, 2 = somewhat disagree, 1 = disagree. The scores for the individual items that make up the learning style scales for each respondent were added together, and then the mean was calculated for each respondent’s score for each scale. The “deep” learning style consisted of 16 original questions from four categories: seeking meaning, relating ideas, using evidence, and being interested in ideas. The “surface” learning style consisted of 16 original questions from four categories: no sense of purpose, unrelated memorization, focused on the syllabus, and fear of failure. The “strategic” learning style consisted of 20 original items from five categories: organized study, time management, awareness of what is expected by the assessor, achieving effectiveness, and monitoring the effectiveness of one’s own learning [11, 12]. In the current study, the ASSIST subscales demonstrated acceptable internal consistency: Deep learning alpha = (0.79), SL alpha = (0.81), Surface Learning alpha = (0.81).

The second is the Achievement Motivation Scale. The achievement motivation was assessed using a 10-item, 5-point Likert scale adapted from McClelland’s Achievement Motivation Theory. The original Cassidy and Lynn [13] instrument is a 22-item true/false scale, however, for this cross-cultural study, we employed an abbreviated 10-item version to reduce participant burden and enhance response sensitivity across seven countries. This approach aligns with contemporary nursing education research, where abbreviated motivation scales with 5-point Likert formats have been successfully validated in nursing student populations [14]. The scale assesses three behavioral domains: goal setting, persistence, and mastery. Items are rated on a 5-point scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree), with scores ranging from 10 to 50. For interpretability, scores were categorized into three levels based on theoretical tertile divisions: high (40–50), moderate (25–39), and low (10–24). These cut-offs were established a priori to facilitate cross-country comparisons. We acknowledge they are author-defined and recommend future validation against objective criteria. Internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.82).

The third is the Academic performance by Terry and Peck [15]. Accordingly, academic and clinical performance can be effectively measured through a concise self-report scale that prompts nursing students to evaluate their own competence relative to their peers. Students are asked to respond to items such as, “How would you rate your academic performance compared to other students in your year level?” and “How would you rate your clinical performance compared to other students in your year level?” While these ratings are often collected on a 5-point scale ranging from “Very Poor” to “Very Good,” they are frequently analyzed as a 3-point categorical measure (Below Average, Average, or Above Average) to determine how subjective success correlates with psychological traits like grit. This single-item peer-comparison measure does not allow internal consistency assessment (Cronbach’s alpha cannot be computed for single items). Its validity is limited by subjective self-reporting, and findings should be interpreted as predictors of perceived rather than objective academic performance.

Data collection process

The data collection process was carried out systematically after having obtained ethical approval from the Ethics Review Committee (ERC) of King Khalid University. Data collection was carried out systematically from June to December 2024. The process was planned and executed in four phases to help eliminate culture-specific biases and ensure the consistency of data collection and cross-cultural data integrity among the seven countries. In the planning phase, formal permission was obtained from the Deans or department heads of the participating nursing colleges. To clarify and ensure consistency of the language and the constructs of the research questions, the instruments of the study were translated and validated, where applicable, before the implementation of the study.

In the implementation phase, potential participants were invited using a combination of digital invitations and actual visits to the classrooms of the targeted participants. For ease of data collection, the researchers trained student representatives to distribute the survey link and sent personalized reminder messages through WhatsApp. Each participant was provided with an information sheet that explained the purpose of the study and the academic costs of withdrawing from the study.

The process of obtaining consent was separate from the administration of the study; it was obtained through an “I Agree” checkbox on digital survey forms and through written consent on paper survey forms. The data collection process included a three-part survey administered through Google Forms. The first part of the survey included demographic data; the second part included the Learning Approaches Scale (which measures deep, strategic, and surface learning); and the third part included the achievement motivation scale.

In order to reduce as much response bias as possible, students were given approximately 15–20 min to complete the questionnaires in a quiet environment. Students were assured the utmost anonymity, and as such, identifying measures such as names and ID numbers were omitted. During the last stage of data management, digital data were exported to Microsoft Excel, and for the paper surveys, data were manually entered into the same database. The same database then underwent a stringent process of data cleansing and quota management to achieve a balanced design of n = 110 for each country. In accordance with data privacy policies, the final aggregate data were encrypted and stored on a password-protected data storage location, which only the principal investigators have access to for complete confidentiality.

The exact response rate could not be calculated due to the open recruitment strategy (digital invitations and classroom visits without tracking non-respondents). Quota management was achieved by continuing recruitment in each country until n = 110 complete responses were obtained, with incomplete responses excluded from the final dataset.

Ethical consideration

To protect the rights and welfare of the participants, this study strictly adhered to established ethical guidelines for human research, beginning with formal approval from the Institutional Review Board and the administrative heads of the nursing departments across the seven participating countries. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality and anonymity were assured throughout the research process. The students were briefed on the study’s objectives and their right to withdraw at any stage without academic penalty. To ensure strict anonymity and confidentiality, no personally identifiable information was collected; instead, each questionnaire was assigned a unique alphanumeric code, with all data stored on secure, password-protected servers or in locked cabinets accessible only to the principal investigator. The study posed minimal risk to participants, and while no financial incentives were provided, the benefits focused on the global improvement of nursing curricula and academic support strategies. To address potential language barriers and ensure ethical understanding, the informed consent forms and instruments were provided in the primary language of instruction for each program, utilizing a rigorous back-translation method to maintain cultural and technical consistency across all regions.

Data analysis

The overall demographic data was presented in terms of frequencies and percent. Students’ age and score on the three aspects of the learning style were quantified using means and SDs. The Chi-Square tests were conducted to assess if there were relationships between the students’ country and their level of achievement motivation and academic performance. To compare the mean scores for the learning styles among the seven countries and four levels of study, one-way ANOVA was applied. Using eta2, the effect sizes of the ANOVA analyses provided an indication of how large the differences were between the groups. The associations between the achievement motivation and learning styles were examined by assessing Pearson’s r.

The predictors of academic performance were identified using binary logistic regression. Since the inter-correlations between Deep and SL styles were very strong (r= .934), VIF diagnostics were utilized to evaluate the multicollinearity present within the predictor variables. The independent predictive power of the predictor variables in the final multivariate model was determined by applying Wald statistics and odds ratios (Exp[B]) along with a 95% confidence interval. The alpha level was set to p<.05.

For the binary logistic regression, academic performance was dichotomized as High Performers (ratings of 4 ‘Good’ and 5 ‘Very Good’, coded as 1) versus Non-High Performers (ratings of 1 ‘Very Poor’, 2 ‘Below Average’, and 3 ‘Average’, coded as 0). This dichotomization aligns with the theoretical distinction between successful and less successful students while ensuring adequate cell sizes for stable parameter estimation. All categorical demographic predictors were dummy coded with the following reference categories: marital status (Unmarried = 0, Married = 1), basic education (Higher Secondary = 0, Graduate = 1), area of residence (Urban = 0, Rural = 1), and country (Saudi Arabia as the reference category). Year of study was treated as a continuous variable. Prior to interpreting the final model, logistic regression assumptions were assessed: linearity in the logit was evaluated via Box-Tidwell tests, multicollinearity via VIF diagnostics, and model fit via the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Prior to conducting ANOVA, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed. Normality was evaluated using Shapiro-Wilk tests, and homogeneity of variance was verified using Levene’s test. Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated that Deep Learning (p = .142), SL (p = .215), and Surface Learning (p = .342) did not significantly deviate from normality. Levene’s test confirmed homogeneity of variance for all ANOVA models (p= .05).

More than half of the participants (57.5%) were aged 21 years or older, while 42.5% were aged 20 years or younger. Most are unmarried (94.42%) with Basic education of Higher Secondary (74.4) and from Urban background (71.6). There were 14.3% students from each country. The sample was unevenly distributed across year of study, with 7.3% in first year, 24.2% in second year, 39.5% in third year, and 29.1% in final year (Table 1).

Significant differences were found when comparing student engagement in the seven different countries, in terms of SL (F = 2.89, p = .009) and surface learning (F = 3.05, p = .006). The Indonesian cohort had the greatest amount of engagement in both SL (M = 73.80, SD = 15.78) and deep learning (M = 58.99, SD = 12.28).

While the Indonesian cohort recorded the highest numerical mean for Deep Learning (M = 58.99, SD = 12.28), the one-way ANOVA revealed no significant differences in Deep Learning engagement across the seven countries (F = 0.93, p = .468). This suggests that the inclination toward deep learning is a universal characteristic among these nursing students, remaining consistent regardless of geographic or cultural location.

Academic performance categories were operationalized from the 5-point Terry and Peck [15] self-report scale as follows: High Performers = ratings of 4 (‘Good’) and 5 (‘Very Good’); Average Performers = rating of 3 (‘Average’); Low Performers = ratings of 1 (‘Very Poor’) and 2 (‘Below Average’). These categorical groupings were used for all univariate ANOVA analyses (Table 4). The univariate analysis presented in Table 4 shows a strong difference between the learning approaches and achievement motivation of students who achieve different grades within their course. It has been identified that the method of learning with the largest correlation to achieving success initially (p < .001) is SL. Therefore, when examining the means of both the high performing group and the low performing group, we can identify that high performing student use the majority of their time organizing, managing time, and planning, and report much higher mean values for both of these items (M = 74.45; SD: 14.66) when compared to the low performing students (M = 48.67; SD: 3.85). Deep Learning was the second highest correlation to academic achievement (p < .001) and as such, the high performing students have significantly higher mean values for deep learning when compared to low performing students (M = 58.5 vs. M = 52.24). On the other hand, surface level learning is negatively correlated to academic achievement (p < .001). The high achieving students reported using the least amount of rote memorization (M = 50.50; SD: 12.64), whereas low performing students utilize the most amount of rote memorization (M = 56.90; SD: 7.85).

Although achievement motivation is positively correlated to academic achievement, the effect size was small (eta2 = 0.013) and therefore although motivation does play a role in student success, it likely carries less weight than the specific methods of cognition (Deep & Strategic) used by the students. A statistical transition from the univariate model to the multivariate model shows that although SL was the most significant correlate in the univariate model, when looking at the multivariate model, it shares predictive ability with Deep Learning due to the high positive inter-correlation (r = .934). This indicates that for this nursing cohort, the “strategic” behaviors of the high performers are the result of the “deep” nature of their searches for meaning.

While there is a statistically significant association between achievement motivation and surface learning (p = .048), the relationship is negligible (r = .071). Given this extremely low effect size, it suggests that achievement motivation is not a primary factor determining whether a student relies on rote memory. Instead, surface learning appears more linked to external environmental pressures rather than a student’s internal drive to succeed. Due to the high multicollinearity between deep and SL (r = .934), both variables were entered into the multivariate model using VIF diagnostics. The VIF for these variables was approximately 7.8, indicating moderate multicollinearity that exceeds the conventional threshold of 5 [16]. However, since this value remains below the more lenient threshold of 10, and given the theoretical distinction between these constructs, both variables were retained in the model with caution. The independent predictive power of deep learning was interpreted with acknowledgment of this shared variance (Table 5).

Unmarried students have approximately eight times higher odds of being high performers compared to married students. Conversely, surface learning was negatively predictive of academic achievement (p < .001). The negative coefficients (-0.146) and Exp(B) of 0.864 indicate that as a student’s reliance upon rote memorization (surface learning) increases, the odds of them being classified as a high achiever decreases significantly. Therefore, while high achieving students typically possess motivation and organizational skills, it appears these traits are secondary to the “deep” search for meaning in content. Therefore, this analysis has identified a critical pedagogical gap: while overall engagement in deep approaches tend to decline by the final year of study, maintaining high levels of deep engagement continues to be the essential requirement for academic excellence.

Discussion

Levels of achievement motivation and academic performance among nursing students

In this current study, ‘country’ was identified as a highly statistically significant predictor variable of both achievement motivation and academic performance, consistent with more general literature from the MENA region demonstrating that national context including curriculum design and education policies has more impact on educational outcomes than motivation [17–19]. The country of Egypt diverged from this pattern, with students reporting low achievement motivation, but maintaining a very high level of academic performance (62.7%). This may suggest that, under conditions of high-stakes assessment, external pressure to perform well, and strict curricula, students’ performance may be sustained at a relatively high level, regardless of their level of intrinsic motivation [17, 20]. On the other hand, students in Oman demonstrated a high degree of correlation between their achievement motivation and their academic performance, suggesting that national support systems for education can enhance students’ motivation.

These results are also consistent with previous research on the educational infrastructure and clinical competencies required for academic success throughout the MENA region, which have shown that, regardless of variation in students’ motivation, these factors can provide a foundation for maintaining academic standards [17–19]. Therefore, one of the major implications of this research is that policymakers should address system-wide factors, such as curriculum development and accreditation requirements, as opposed to relying primarily on student-level motivational interventions [19]. Secondarily, the research highlighted several psychometric issues related to measuring motivation across cultures and indicated that researchers will need to take into consideration issues of measurement invariance and cultural differences in self-reporting if they wish to make direct comparisons of motivation across different Arab countries [21].

Learning approaches among students of different levels and countries

The current results demonstrate that the year of study has a significant association on the learning approach, in which both positive association were found for strategic and surface learning. Interestingly, the use of engagement and rote-study habits appear to have a negative linear trend as students’ progress through their studies. Further, although deep learning appears to have a similar downward trend, it remains relatively stable and is close to being statistically significant. This indicates that deeper cognitive processes are more resistant to changes related to year level [22]. The cross-national comparison showed a significant difference for the learning approach of Strategic and Surface Learning, where the Indonesian cohort had the highest engagement. However, no significant cross-national difference was found for Deep Learning, which may suggest an inherent inclination toward making meaning in nursing education regardless of geographic/cultural location [22, 23].

The findings support previous cross-cultural research that indicated while structural factors such as curriculum development and cultural norms determine both cognitive and affective learning outcomes, there exists some commonality among certain fundamental cognitive processes across different educational locations [23, 24]. It is likely that the decrease in engagement that occurs over time is due to the transitional stressors associated with clinical placement and increased workload; therefore, instructors should utilize instructional design that maintains deep learning engagement in more advanced practice roles [25]. Ultimately, the results have several key implications for practice and policy. Therefore, educational programs should provide targeted supports, for example, metacognition training, retrieval practice, and structured reflection to help maintain deep learning and prevent the regression of SL habits throughout the progression of a student’s studies [26, 27]. Similarly, policymakers and program directors should leverage curricular information from the country level to develop culturally safe pedagogies and to continue to engage students [28, 29]. International collaboration and the utilization of shared evaluation frameworks is recommended to identify effective strategies to be used in international settings and to improve understanding of what is universal vs. culture-specific regarding student success [30].

Learning approaches and achievement motivation as predictors of academic performance

Educators must also ensure that assessments measure true deep processing (and not simply superficial measures of effort) so that nursing graduates can demonstrate the critical thinking skills needed for advanced practice [31, 32]. When interpreted through the lens of Self-Determination Theory, these findings suggest that autonomous motivation which emerges when students experience satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs promotes the deep, meaning-oriented processing that drives academic success [33–35]. Conversely, the negative association between surface learning and achievement may reflect environments where learners feel controlled and lack perceived competence, leading to extrinsic or amotivated regulation [33, 36]. It is noteworthy that while both SL and Achievement Motivation showed significant univariate associations with performance, neither remained significant in the multivariate model. This suggests that organized study behaviors and motivational drive contribute to achievement primarily when they manifest as deep engagement with content rather than as independent constructs [33, 37]. The observed decline in deep engagement by the final year of study is particularly concerning from an SDT perspective, as it may indicate that the educational environment becomes less supportive of students’ basic psychological needs as they progress through the program [38].

Correlation between learning approaches and achievement motivation of nursing students

While there is a statistically significant relationship between achievement motivation and surface learning, the size of this relationship is very small. The evidence suggests that a student’s drive to succeed internally does not determine how much they rely on rote memory; rather, surface learning is more likely caused by externally imposed conditions (such as test pressure, limited time, and strict course structures) [39, 40]. Additionally, this disconnection is documented in research which shows that even highly motivated students will fall back into memorization-based practices if the instructional environment places an emphasis on short-term recall over sustained understanding [41, 42]. Thus, it appears that educational programs which intend to decrease the amount of surface learning occurring should place greater emphasis on changing the environmental aspects of education specifically clarifying what is expected of students and decreasing unnecessary memorization—than focusing solely on increasing the internal motivation of the students [40].

In terms of methodological concerns, the high correlation between Deep and SL was addressed by conducting Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) diagnostic checks. The VIF for Deep and SL was approximately 7.8, indicating moderate multicollinearity that warrants careful interpretation. While this exceeds the conventional threshold of 5, it remains below the more conservative threshold of 10. Therefore, both variables were retained, but the interpretation of their independent effects should be made with caution, acknowledging the substantial shared variance between these constructs. Therefore, allowing both to remain within the multivariate model to evaluate the independent predictive value of Deep Learning [43].

This methodological rigor ensured that the specific effects of deep learning—specifically those related to developing critical thinking and integrating information—would be appropriately separated from strategic engagement [43]. Although the literature has shown that deep encoding and spaced repetition consistently produce better long-term retention than rote rehearsal, the continued use of rote rehearsal in many settings demonstrates the necessity to develop context-specific pedagogical interventions designed to encourage meaningful learning [44, 45]. Ultimately, the findings suggest that although deep learning can be supported through contextualized means, surface learning is more often employed as a reactive response to assessments and therefore requires a paradigmatic shift in assessment design to promote more complex and meaningful learning approaches [46, 47].

Predictors of academic performance among nursing students using regression analysis

Married students have 87.6% lower odds of achieving high performance (OR = 0.124), meaning unmarried students have approximately 8 times higher odds of high achievement. Although marital status is considered a demographic factor, SDT suggests that marital status is an indirect measure of the social context in which students exist. Married students may experience an increase in role strain, or perceive less autonomy in the academic environment, that can prevent need satisfaction [46, 47]. The authors also suggest that the lack of significance of other factors such as student’s age, living situation, and country are indicative of research supporting SDT that suggests that the psychological environment in which students learn is more predictive of self-regulated learning than demographic characteristics [48, 49]. Research conducted in different parts of the world, such as China [47] Japan [48] and Indonesia [49] reinforces the fact that meeting all three needs creates a fertile ground for intrinsic motivation and academic achievement. However, the relative importance of each need may vary based on the culture in which the student exists [50, 51]. Therefore, while autonomy and competence may be consistent predictors of engagement, relatedness is a critical component of persistence and well-being in both high-pressure situations [51] and distance education settings [50].

The major implications for practice include moving away from focusing on student demographic characteristics and instead creating a learning environment that supports the three basic psychological needs. Teachers can support the three basic psychological needs through providing autonomy-supportive teaching (i.e., allowing students to make meaningful choices and provide reasons), competence building (i.e., providing scaffolding and timely feedback), and creating a relatedness structure (i.e., creating a collaborative environment and fostering a sense of belonging) [44, 52]. Future studies should attempt to move away from using grade point averages and incorporate more direct measures of need satisfaction to determine if marital status moderates the effect of these interventions or is merely acting as a proxy for other unmeasured variables (such as time availability and stress levels) [45, 53]. Shaping a climate that satisfies these three core needs is the most impactful way to promote autonomous motivation and academic success among students from various backgrounds.

Learning approaches and achievement motivation as predictors of academic performance among nursing students

Deep Learning was identified in multivariate analysis as the most prevailing single independent factor predicting High Academic Achievement, with an estimated increase of 8.7% (Exp(B) approximately 1.087) in the probability of achieving at a high level for each unit of increase in Deep Learning scores. The SDT model of motivation clearly predicts that students will develop an autonomous motivation through self-determined behaviors when their three basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness are met. Autonomous motivation promotes the processing of information in ways that lead to better retention and higher levels of performance [51, 54, 55]. However, surface-level learning was found to be significantly negatively correlated with High Academic Achievement, indicating that relying primarily on rote memory and/or superficial processing is associated with lower rates of high achievement. The SDT theory also suggests that reliance on surface-level learning is likely due to thwarted needs in the environment i.e., environments where the learner feels controlled and lacks the perception of having competent control over their own learning. Such environments can lead to either amotivation or extrinsic motivation [51, 56]. Of interest were the results of the univariate tests for both SL and Achievement Motivation.

While both factors reached statistical significance, neither SL nor Achievement Motivation remained statistically significant in the multivariate model. Therefore, it appears that organized habits and high motivation only contribute to high levels of achievement when the habits and motivation manifest as deep, meaning-oriented engagement [51, 57]. Additionally, these findings demonstrate the importance of teaching methods. Teaching methods are important because the data indicates that deep engagement is the primary driver of academic success. Unfortunately, however, the data demonstrates that the degree of deep engagement decreases dramatically by the final year of study. The SDT model of motivation would suggest that the decrease in deep engagement could be minimized if the educational environment maintains a consistent support for both the autonomy and social connectedness of the learners [58].

Study implications

To resolve the recognized pedagogical gap and to assist students to acquire the learning skills necessary to achieve success, nursing education programs will have to shift from the traditional factual-based educational model to a more inquiry-based model, which includes Case-Based and Problem-Based Learning. Since the research supports deep learning as the one positive factor predicting high academic performance, the curricula of nursing programs need to be specifically developed with the intent to encourage student involvement until the conclusion of the program. This can be achieved through guided reflective practice and sophisticated clinical debriefing, where students are compelled to develop and make sense of new information rather than simply apply previously learned material.

Furthermore, since faculty play an intentional role in supporting students’ metacognitive processes by assisting them in translating their motivation into focused, strategic clinical practice, faculty also need to revise assessment methods to favor the construction and application of knowledge over the simple reproduction of knowledge. Finally, since the performance impact of marital status is significant, there needs to be flexible learning pathways and asynchronous aids created to accommodate the time constraints associated with non-traditional students and to avoid limiting access to opportunities for deep cognitive engagement.

Strengths and limitations

This study is a multi-national one that assessed the various learning approaches among nursing students. However, limitations of the multi-national study are related to its methodology. Due to the nature of the study (cross-sectional design), the study collected data at only one point in time, so it could not establish a relationship (causal or otherwise) between the approaches to learning and the achievements of the students. Without a longitudinal study, the study was unable to assess any longitudinal changes in the ways students shift their approaches to learning over time. In addition, using a convenience sample of university students—although the study’s sample was balanced—the convenience sample used limited the study to an underrepresentation of the total global nursing student population. The study relied upon the use of scales (Learning Approaches, Achievement Motivation) that rely upon self-report, and self-report measures are subject to the possibility of “hawk” responses where students respond in socially acceptable or academically acceptable terms. This response bias is compounded when comparing motivational responses across different cultures, as culture also influences what type of responses individuals reports in terms of self-reporting. The measurement of academic performance was self-reported and therefore is subjective. Students may feel they have the ability to accomplish something, or may believe they have the ability to accomplish something, which may be different than how they actually perform, creating a discrepancy in perceived versus actual competence.

The Achievement Motivation Scale used in this study was a 10-item, 5-point Likert adaptation of the original 22-item true/false instrument. While this adaptation was necessary for cross-national applicability and participant burden reduction, it has not undergone independent psychometric validation. The author-defined categorization (high: 40–50, moderate: 25–39, low: 10–24) was based on theoretical tertile divisions rather than empirically validated cut-offs, and measurement invariance testing across countries was not conducted. Future research should validate this abbreviated format against objective performance criteria and establish measurement equivalence across cultures.

Finally, the study reported a very high correlation (r = .934) between the Deep and SL approach, which indicates that there is a significant amount of methodological overlap. Even after using VIF diagnostics to check for methodological overlap, it appears that the methods used to measure these two variables were highly correlated, and as such, this type of methodological overlap may be difficult to completely separate out in practice. Lastly, the study did not include any external environmental factors, such as workloads associated with clinical placements, financial constraints, or e-learning demands, that may cause students to adopt a surface learning approach.

Conclusion

Deep learning has a significant association with academic success for nursing students. There is an estimated 8.7% increase in the odds of high achievement for each additional one-unit increase in deep engagement. While univariate analysis indicated that above-average students reported higher levels of both Achievement Motivation and SL, neither remained a significant independent predictor in the multivariate model. Therefore, the active cognitive search for meaning (Deep Learning) was demonstrated to be the only significant independent predictor among these variables. Moreover, surface Learning and Marital Status were identified as being strong predictors of failure. Also, a pedagogical gap exists since student engagement in Strategic and Surface learning showed significant declines by year of study while Deep Learning decline approached but did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, maintaining deep engagement is the most important, yet most at-risk, factor for achieving excellence. This demonstrates that maintaining a high level of deep engagement is the most important, yet the most at-risk, factor for achieving excellence in both clinical and academic practice.
