Drug prescription before and after dialysis initiation in older patients: a nationwide study
Authors: Léa Faure, Pernelle Noize, Cécile Couchoud, Emilie Hucteau, Jessica Berdougo, Florent Guerville, Jerome Harambat, Christian Combe, Hélène Levassort, Olivier Moranne, Aghilès Hamroun, Jean-Baptiste Beuscart, Karen Leffondré, Mathilde Prezelin-Reydit, REIN registry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder patients on dialysis have a complex regimen of drugs to manage their chronic kidney disease (CKD), associated complications, and co-morbidities. Our aim was to describe drug use in the year before and after dialysis initiation in the older population.MethodsWe included all patients aged ≥ 65 years initiating chronic dialysis in France between 2010 and 2019 identified from the national kidney replacement therapy registry and matched with the national health data system. Drug use prevalence was described by pharmacotherapeutic classes, based on anatomical therapeutic chemical codes, and estimated quarterly for patients with ≥ 1 drug dispensing in the year before and after the initiation of dialysis. Analysis was stratified according to dialysis modality, sex, and age at dialysis initiation.ResultsAmong 55,238 patients included, hyper-polypharmacy (≥ 10 drugs) rose to 76% at dialysis initiation, and slightly decreasing afterwards. Non-CKD-related drugs, including insulins, psychotropic, analgesic and non-antihypertensive cardiovascular drugs, showed stable prevalence, except for proton pump inhibitors (PPI) use which rose sharply before dialysis (40.8% to 54.8%) and then stabilized. Conversely, CKD-related drugs varied. At dialysis initiation, antihypertensives (except diuretics), potassium binders, and hypouricemic agents use declined, while calcium supplementation rose then stabilized. Vitamin D and phosphate binder use increased steadily. Trends in drug use patterns were similar across dialysis modality, sex and age.ConclusionPrescription changes occur at dialysis initiation, particularly for CKD-related drugs. Hyperpolypharmacy was highly prevalent, yet much of it is likely appropriate given the complex clinical profile of older dialysis patients. Further work is needed to explain the high prevalence of PPI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07113-6.Keywords: Dialysis, Drug use, Elderly, Pharmacoepidemiology, Polypharmacy

Introduction

Older people in the general population frequently present multiple comorbidities which result in a higher prevalence of polypharmacy [1, 2] and therefore an increased risk of potentially inappropriate prescriptions (PIPs), poor adherence, and hospitalizations related to drug adverse drug reactions [3–5]. Improving drug prescribing in older people has over the past decade a major public health challenge recognized by health authorities worldwide [6]. Indeed, numerous recommendations have been established to optimize, in the general older population, the use of certain drugs, such as psychotropics [7, 8], antihypertensives drugs [7] and proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) [7–10]. Patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), particularly those on maintenance dialysis, often require a complex drug regimen to manage CKD and its related complications [11]. This frequently results in polypharmacy [12, 13] which, although often justified by multiple comorbidities, carries a significantly higher risk of adverse drug reactions compared to patients without CKD [14]. The main challenge lies in identifying and minimizing its inappropriate components.

The early period after dialysis initiation is critical for older patients since it is associated with poor outcomes such as fast cognitive and functional decline, poor quality of life and high mortality rate [15]. Few data are available on drug management at this time while many changes in prescriptions are expected after dialysis initiation. Indeed, patients on dialysis are often hospitalized in the first months after the initiation of dialysis [16], which may be related to changes in drug prescriptions [17]. Additionally, a change in the main prescribers is often observed at dialysis initiation and may also be associated with a change in drug prescriptions. For example in France, general practitioners are much more regularly consulted than nephrologists in the year before dialysis initiation, but they are much less consulted after dialysis initiation [18]. As a result, the percentage of drugs prescribed by a nephrologist in a given patient was 21.5% during the two years before dialysis initiation, and rose up to 55.5% in the year after dialysis initiation [18]. In the US, a study showed a rapid decrease in antihypertensive drug after initiation of dialysis in patients aged 67 years and older [19]. However, no study has examined overall drug use at dialysis initiation, despite it is a crucial first step before targeting actions to improve prescribing practices. No studies have also investigated whether potential changes at the time of dialysis initiation might differ by dialysis modality, sex or age group in the older dialysis population, although drug use has been shown to differ between these groups [20–23].

Our main objective was therefore to describe drug use before and after dialysis initiation in the whole population of older patients on dialysis in France, as well as by subgroups defined by dialysis modality, sex, and age.

Materials and methods

Data source

Data were collected through two national databases: (i) the French national kidney replacement therapy (KRT) registry (Renal Epidemiology and Information Network – REIN) including all patients receiving chronic dialysis or kidney transplantation (KT) in metropolitan France since 2010, and in overseas territories since 2012 [24, 25]; (ii) the Système National des Données de Santé (SNDS), a comprehensive database integrating French health insurance records with national hospital discharge data, capturing information on over 99% of the French population. The SNDS provides anonymous data on all outpatient healthcare reimbursements, including pharmaceuticals, physician consultations, and laboratory tests [26]. As there is no unique identifier between REIN and SNDS, an iterative and deterministic approach was used to link patients [27]. Data collection has been approved by the French Data Protection Authority (CNIL, N° 903,188), and patients were informed about their inclusion in the REIN registry.

Population

We included all patients aged 65 years and over who initiated a first KRT by chronic dialysis in France between 2010 and 2019. Subjects receiving preemptive KT as a first KRT were not eligible for the study. We also excluded subjects without any drug dispensing over the two years surrounding dialysis initiation (e.g. patients residing in nursing homes with in-house pharmacies, patients without health insurance coverage at the start of dialysis).

Drug use assessment

Drug use assessment was based on all the dispensations of reimbursed outpatient drugs, retrieved from the SNDS from one year before to one year after dialysis initiation, regardless the administration route. The SNDS database indeed captures all drug packages dispensed in community pharmacies, including the exact name of the drug (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) code), the number of packages dispensed, and the dispensing date. However, the SNDS database does not contain information on drug indication, prescribed posology, or duration of treatment. Furthermore, the SNDS does not cover drugs administered during dialysis sessions such as treatments for anemia, but only covers few very expensive drugs used in hospitals. The drug classes and subclasses were defined according to the ATC codes or pharmacologically-relevant groups of ATC codes (Table S1) [28].

Patients’ and dialysis characteristics assessment

We retrieved from the REIN registry including: (i) all patients’ characteristics at dialysis initiation: sex, age, body mass index (BMI), type of nephropathies (glomerulopathy, hypertension, diabetic, polycystic, pyelonephritis, vascular, undetermined, other), serum albumin, hemoglobin, and the number of nephrology consultations in the previous year, smoking status (former, current (≥ 1 cigarette per day) or never smoker), diabetes, active cancer, heart failure, rhythm disorders, coronary insufficiency, stroke or transient ischemic attack, chronic respiratory failure, behavioral disorders, and walking independence (independent walking, need for assistance, total incapacity); (ii) some dialysis characteristics: date of dialysis initiation, administrative status of the dialysis center at initiation (university hospital center, non-university hospital public center, for profit private center, non for profit private center), dialysis modality (hemodialysis (HD), peritoneal dialysis (PD)), and urgent start (yes/no) defined as a first dialysis session performed immediately (< 24 h) after evaluation by a nephrologist due to a life-threatening condition; (iii) dialysis exit events if applicable, KT, death and discontinuation of dialysis for any reason. We retrieved from the SNDS the hospitalizations, number and duration of hospital admissions from one year before to one year after dialysis initiation.

Statistics analysis

Drug use was examined quarterly [29] from one year before dialysis initiation to one year after. Time 0 corresponded to dialysis initiation. Quarter 1 (Q1) thus represented the first three months after dialysis initiation, Q-1 the three months preceding dialysis initiation, and so on till Q4 and Q-4. Until Q1, all patients included in the study contributed to each quarter. Starting from Q2, the total number of patients on dialysis contributing to each quarter was reduced to the number of patients alive while on dialysis at the first day of that quarter, to account for deaths, KT and dialysis discontinuation.

The prevalence of polypharmacy (≥ 5 different drugs) and hyper-polypharmacy (≥ 10 different drugs) were described in each quarter [30, 31] and derived as the number of patients with at least 5 (or 10) different drugs dispensed during the quarter, divided by the total number of patients on dialysis contributing to that quarter. The median number of drugs was described by quarter and stratified by dialysis modality (HD, PD), sex, and age. A drug corresponded to an ATC code at the fifth level, i.e. the chemical substance.

The prevalence of drug use was described for each quarter and derived as the number of patients with at least one dispensing of a drug from the classes (or subclasses) during the quarter, divided by the total number of patients on dialysis contributing to that quarter. To compare prevalence of drug classes before and after dialysis initation, we performed McNemar’s tests with prevalence at Q-1 and Q1. The prevalence was further stratified by dialysis modality for all classes (or subclasses) and by sex and age for three specific classes: management of (i) hypertension and fluid overload; (ii) metabolic disorders (hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperuricemia and hypocalcemia), (iii) pain and psychological disorders. The prevalence for management of hypertension and fluid overload class was stratified by heart failure at dialysis initiation, and the prevalence for management of pain and psychological disorders class was stratified by behavioral disorders at dialysis initiation.

We also described the trend over the study period (2010–2019) in the use of four drug classes that received specific recommendations for the older population (PPIs, analgesics and psychotropic drugs) [7–9] or the CKD population (calcium supplementation) [7–9, 32]. For each calendar year, the prevalence of each drug use was estimated in Q1 of dialysis initiation among patients who initiated dialysis within that year only.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

Among the 67,736 patients aged 65 years or older who initiated dialysis in France in 2010–2019, 10,029 were excluded because of lack of matching with SNDS (matching rate: 85.2%) and 2,469 because of unavailable data on their drug dispensing in the two years surrounding dialysis initiation (Fig. 1).

Selection process of the population aged 65 years and older initiating chronic dialysis between 2010 and 2019 in France. 1Several possible reasons (i) patients residing in nursing homes with in-house pharmacies; (ii) patients without health insurance coverage at the start of dialysis; (iii) record linkage issue between databases. 2Discontinuation of dialysis for any reason (decision to discontinue for end-of-life care, recovery of kidney function, conservative treatment)

A total of 55,238 patients were included. The majority were men (64.8%), with a median age of 77 years (interquartile range (IQR): 71.4–82.9). More than half of patients had hypertensive (32.3%) or diabetic (23.5%) nephropathy. Half of patients were diabetic, 32.0% had heart failure, 13.4% cancer, and 2.9% behavioral disorders. The median BMI was 25.9 kg/m2 (IQR: 22.8–29.7), and 78.8% were able to walk independently. Most patients (90.0%) initiated dialysis with HD, and 26.8% received urgent-start dialysis (Table 1). The majority of patients were hospitalized at least once in the quarter prior to initiation of dialysis (80.2%) and in the following quarter (67.5%), with relatively short median hospital stays in all quarters surrounding dialysis initiation, except for Q1 (Table S2).

Patients excluded from our study had similar characteristics as the included ones, except for a higher proportion of patients treated in non-university hospital (38% versus 28%) (Table 1).

Polypharmacy and top 10 drugs

Most patients (90.8%) received over 5 drugs in Q1, and 76.0% had over 10 with a median of 14 drugs (IQR: 10–17). Hyper-polypharmacy rose before dialysis (65.8% in Q-4 to 79.7% in Q-1) and slightly declined afterwards (76.0% in Q1 to 72.7% in Q4). The median number of drugs varied only slightly according to age, sex, and dialysis modality (Table 2, Fig. 2). In Q1, a patient on HD had a median of 14 drugs (IQR: 10–17), compared with 15 drugs (IQR: 12–18) for a patient on PD. In Q1, both men and women had a median of 14 drugs (IQR: 10–17). Regarding age, at Q1, patients aged 65–74 had a median of 14 drugs (IQR: 11–18), those aged 75–84 also 14 (IQR: 11–17), and patients aged 85 and older had a median of 13 drugs (IQR: 10–16). The 10 most common drugs at dialysis initiation are shown in Table 3. Some drugs were no longer in the top 10 used after dialysis initiation, such as allopurinol, while others emerged, such as PPIs.

Evolution of the drug class before and after dialysis initiation

We presented below the evolution of drugs according to classes and the statistical differences between quarters Q-1 and Q1of all the classes and subclasses are presented in Table S3.

Management of hypertension and fluid overload

Dialysis initiation was associated with reduced antihypertensive use (Fig. 3, Table S4). Calcium channel blockers and central/alpha blockers increased before dialysis (54.2% to 60.2% and 29.3% to 35.4%) but dropped sharply at initiation and kept declining consistently. Renin-angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors fell over two years (58.6% to 31.2%), with angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) more used than angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors (22.8% vs. 17.7%). Beta-blockers remained stable (~ 48.5%). Diuretic use, mainly loop diuretics, rose before dialysis (62.0% to 74.5%) and stayed high (68.2%). After initiation, PD patients used more antihypertensives than HD patients, with smaller decreases in calcium channel blockers, RAS inhibitors, and central/alpha blockers. RAS inhibitors dropped from 49.1% to 36.7% in HD vs. 49.4% to 45.6% in PD. Diuretic use was higher in PD (Fig. 4), and increased with age, unlike RAS inhibitors (Fig S1). Patients with heart failure experienced a greater reduction in antihypertensive use at dialysis initiation. For example, for agents acting on the renin–angiotensin system, patients with heart failure decreased from 47 to 32%, compared with a decrease from 50 to 40% among patients without heart failure. Patients with heart failure used more diuretics and beta-blocking agents, whereas patients without heart failure used more calcium channel blockers (Fig S2).

Management of metabolic disorders

Phosphate binder use rose from 5.2% to 31.3% around dialysis initiation (Fig. 3). Hypouricemic agents dropped at initiation (36.6% to 26.3%) and declined thereafter. Vitamin D use steadily increased (39.2% to 55.7%), calcium rose pre-dialysis then stabilized (~ 35%) (Fig. 3, Table S5). PD patients used more vitamin D, had a smaller drop in hypouricemic agents (36.9% to 31.7% vs. 34.8% to 23.2% in HD), but greater potassium binder decrease (Fig. 5). Use of these drugs declined with age; hypouricemic agents were less used by women (Fig S3). Calcium use fell from 42.4% to 31.5% (2010–2019) (Table S9).

Management of diabetes and cardiovascular diseases

Diabetes drug use declined over two years around dialysis, except for insulins/analogs, which remained stable (~ 25%) (Fig. 6, Table S6). Anticoagulants and antiplatelets increased (17.7% to 23.5% and 46.2% to 49.6%), as did dual antithrombotic therapy (5.0% to 9.3%). Lipid-lowering agents dropped at initiation (49.4% to 44.6%) (Fig. 6). No major differences were seen between dialysis modalities (Fig. 7).

Management of pain and psychological disorders

Psychotropic use, mainly benzodiazepines and derivatives, increased before dialysis (32.1% to 41.1%) then stabilized (Fig. 6). Pain treatment rose slightly, notably step 2/3 analgesics and neuropathic pain drugs (14.2% to 17.2%, 1.6% to 4.0%, 4.6% to 9.4%). Step 1 analgesics rose sharply in Q-1 (47.5% to 52.3%), while NSAIDs declined (5.2% to 2.4%) (Fig. 6, Table S7). In Q-1, PD patients had greater increases in step 1/2 analgesics (43.9% to 57.8%, 12.7% to 18.5%) than HD patients (47.9% to 51.7%, 15.1% to 16.6%) (Fig. 8). Psychotropic and analgesic use was higher in women; psychotropic use also rose with age (Fig S4). From 2010–2019, psychotropic use declined (48.0% to 35.9%), while analgesics increased (50.6% to 54.6%) (Table S9). Patients with behavioral disorders used more benzodiazepines and antidepressants than patients without behavioral disorders, both before and after dialysis initiation. For example, after dialysis initiation, approximately 40% of patients with behavioral disorders used benzodiazepines, compared with 31% of patients without behavioral disorders (Fig S5).

Discussion

In this nationwide study of patients ≥ 65 years, we analyzed drug use trends over the two years surrounding dialysis initiation. Hyper-polypharmacy was highly prevalent and persisted post-initiation, despite shifts in CKD-related treatments: antihypertensives declined (except diuretics), calcium, vitamin D, and phosphate binder use increased, while potassium binders and hypouricemic agents decreased. PPI use was unexpectedly high, rising before dialysis and stabilizing around 55%. Drug use trends before and after dialysis initiation were similar across dialysis modalities, sex, and age, though PD patients used more drugs, especially antihypertensives, laxatives, and vitamin D; women used more analgesics and psychotropics, and men more uric acid–lowering agents. From 2010–2019, trends in psychotropic drugs, analgesics, and calcium supplementation aligned with health authority and KDIGO guidelines [7, 8, 32], unlike PPI use.

Most observed changes at dialysis initiation can be explained by the clinical needs of CKD patients around dialysis initiation. First, antihypertensive drugs use rose before dialysis, then dropped sharply post-initiation and over the following year, aligning with a US study on older patients on dialysis [19]. A similar trend was expected for diuretics, as many patients become anuric after the start of dialysis. However, continuing loop diuretics may reduce hospitalizations and improve hemodynamic stability [33], explaining the moderate decline in use, which stabilizes after six months, consistent with the literature. The greater reduction in antihypertensive use at dialysis initiation among patients with heart failure aligns with clinical recommendations to carefully manage blood pressure and avoid fluid overload in this population, suggesting effective volume control. However, this pattern also raises concerns regarding the discontinuation of renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, which are cornerstone therapies in heart failure management. Their reduced use may reflect poor tolerability at dialysis initiation, particularly due to hypotension or hyperkalemia, rather than a lack of therapeutic indication. In contrast, diuretic use increased more markedly among patients without heart failure, likely reflecting predominantly renal-related fluid overload, and remained relatively stable thereafter. Notably, beta-blockers were largely maintained among patients with heart failure, which is consistent with current guidelines and represents a reassuring finding. Second, despite some improvements from dialysis, metabolic disorders treatments remain necessary [34]. Use of uric acid–lowering agents declined but did not disappear. Potassium and phosphate binders initially decreased but rebounded after dialysis, as reduced diuresis leads to hydroelectrolytic disturbances. Management of these disorders—through dietary adjustments, electrolyte supplementation, binders, and dialysis prescription—appears generally consistent with the clinical course of kidney disease. In addition, the gradual decrease in calcium supplementation over calendar time reflects adherence to current treatment guidelines. Third, since dialysis does not immediately affect cardiovascular disease or diabetes, glucose-lowering and non-antihypertensive cardiovascular drugs use remained stable. A slight increase in anticoagulant use was observed, which is expected given the ongoing risk of cardiovascular events in patients with CKD. Insulin therapy did not increase, probably because patients switched long before dialysis, as recommended. As expected, biguanides and sulfonylureas were rarely used, which likely reflects good clinical practice given that these antidiabetic agents are contraindicated in dialysis patients. This also underscores that dialysis initiation does not inherently improve glycemic control, so there is no reason to expect discontinuation solely due to improved diabetes. Fourth, analgesics use spiked in the quarter before dialysis, likely due to dialysis preparation procedures and worsening uremic symptoms. A moderate increase before and after dialysis, especially in step 2/3 and neuropathic pain analgesics, was expected, as pain is common in this population. However, their use remains low, suggesting inadequate pain management. The steady rise in analgesics use from 2010 to 2019 may be considered as a positive trend. Fifth, the higher use of benzodiazepines and antidepressants among patients with behavioral disorders was expected and is consistent with the underlying clinical profile of these patients, reflecting the ongoing management of neuropsychiatric symptoms that may persist or require adjustment during the transition to dialysis. However, these patterns should not be interpreted as indicative of appropriate or inappropriate prescribing practices, but rather as being coherent with patients’ comorbid conditions. Further studies are needed to investigate how much of these benzodiazepines prescriptions are inappropriate and their effects on patients’ outcomes.

Our study also highlights some age- and sex-related differences in drug use. Regarding psychotropic drugs, our study is consistent with several European studies conducted in general population which also found that their use was higher in women than in men [35] and in older age groups [36]. In our study, patients aged 85 and older used fewer phosphate binders than those aged 65–74. This is consistent with the knowledge that serum phosphorus levels decrease significantly with age due to a spontaneous reduction in protein intake [37].

In contrast to these expected results, the rise in PPI use before dialysis initiation and the prevalence were unexpected. Current guidelines and deprescribing recommendations support the reduction of PPI use in patients with CKD, emphasizing regular reassessment of indications because of their potential adverse effects [38–41]. In our study, the high prevalence of PPI use and the marked increase observed in the months preceding dialysis initiation raise concerns. Although dialysis and advanced CKD are associated with an increased risk of peptic ulcer disease [42], and ulcer prevalence may approach 30% in ESKD patients [42], this alone is unlikely to fully explain the widespread and persistent use of PPIs observed in our cohort. Upper gastrointestinal symptoms related to uremia and chronic inflammation in advanced CKD may lead to symptomatic or preventive PPI prescriptions prior to dialysis; however, such indications do not necessarily justify long-term treatment. Importantly, PPI use did not decline after dialysis initiation, suggesting limited deprescribing at the population level despite changes in clinical status. Moreover, only 12% of PPI users were receiving dual antiplatelet therapy after dialysis initiation, indicating that a large proportion of prescriptions were not supported by clear gastroprotective indications. This pattern is consistent with previous reports of substantial PPI overuse, with inappropriate prescriptions reported in 50% to 80% of patients in geriatric and internal medicine wards [43] and in 41% of patients with CKD [44]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the high prevalence of PPI use in our study likely reflects prolonged and insufficiently re-evaluated prescriptions rather than acute, well-justified indications. Further studies are therefore warranted to better characterize PPI indications, prescribing trajectories, and deprescribing opportunities at the transition to dialysis in CKD and ESKD populations.

Finally, one of our main results was to find a very high prevalence of patients with hyper-polypharmacy which did not decrease after dialysis initiation. The high prevalence of polypharmacy is consistent with German [45] and American [4] studies which found a polypharmacy in 91.5% and a hyper-polypharmacy in 61% of patients with non-dialysis CKD, respectively. However, the stabilization after dialysis initiation challenges the assumption that dialysis reduces treatment burden. While it may decrease the use of certain drugs, such as antihypertensives, this is often compensated, or even exceeded, by increases in other drugs. This is particularly important to communicate to nephrologists and geriatricians, who are increasingly involved in the choice of KRT. Indeed, they need to change the message sometimes given to older patients, for whom quality of life is a key priority, that dialysis initiation implies a medication regimen simplification.

Furthermore, we found that polypharmacy is particularly high in dialysis patients, but it should be remembered that this is often clinically appropriate given the complexity of their condition. The goal is therefore not necessarily a systematic reduction, but rather a careful identification of prescriptions that are not medically necessary. Deprescribing strategies in an older patient rely on an individual systematic review of medications, the identification of potentially inappropriate prescriptions, and the gradual discontinuation or replacement of treatments when appropriate. These approaches are especially relevant at dialysis initiation, a period characterized by changes in prescription, and precisely a time when there is an opportunity to carefully review medications, optimize prescriptions, and engage in shared decision-making with the patient. A coordinated medication review involving the nephrologist, geriatrician, and pharmacist can help identify treatments to adjust, prioritize interventions, and ensure safe follow-up tailored to the patient’s preferences and priorities. Such a geronto-nephrology partnership has been emphasized by Guerville et al., highlighting its added value in optimizing prescriptions and shared decision-making in older patients starting dialysis [46]. Importantly, early evidence from hemodialysis populations shows that structured deprescribing interventions—such as electronic decision support tools or pharmacist-led, algorithm-based approaches—can successfully reduce potentially inappropriate medications, including PPIs and benzodiazepines/Z-drugs. These findings support the relevance of carefully reviewing and optimizing prescriptions at dialysis initiation [47–49].

Finally, unlike a Canadian study, we found that PD patients over 65 used more drugs for CKD complications than HD patients [20]. Potential explanations are as follows: (i) we do not capture some drugs in HD patients such as vitamin D which is often administered during HD sessions; (ii) some drugs are also more likely to be used in PD patients, like laxatives to avoid constipation and prevent catheter displacement; (iii) fluid overload may be more prevalent in PD than in HD patients [50] leading to a greater need for antihypertensive drugs. While PD is often favored in older adults for its gentler, home-based approach and fewer dietary constraints, its potentially higher drug burden remains an important consideration when choosing a KRT.

Our study has some strengths and limitations. The major strength of our study lies in the completeness of the SNDS database, which provides an exhaustive record of healthcare consumption data, and the REIN registry including all patients treated by dialysis in France. The linkage rate between the SNDS and the REIN registry being excellent (85.2%), only a few patients on dialysis were not included in this study and the characteristics of unmatched patients do not appear to be different from those of matched patients except for the type of dialysis center. This allowed us to consider all outpatient drug reimbursements for each patient between 2010 and 2019, enabling us to accurately estimate polypharmacy and drug use prevalence. Additionally, the SNDS coding system for drugs relies on an international classification for identifying drugs, the ATC classification, which facilitates international comparisons.

A first limitation of our study lies in the fact that we relied on drug dispensing which might not fully reflect actual drug consumption [28]. We might thus have overestimated actual use for drugs requiring prescription and underestimated actual use for drugs that do not require prescriptions, like analgesics or NSAIDs [51, 52]. The latter is all the more problematic because over the counter drug use is common among older patients [53] and among patients with CKD [54]. However, drug dispensing is a valuable proxy for prescriptions and thus provides a reliable picture of clinical practices [55]. A second limitation is that the SNDS doesn’t capture drugs administered during dialysis sessions in hospitals, mainly hemodialysis. Since vitamin D and erythropoiesis-stimulating agents are predominantly given during dialysis, their use may have been underestimated, and the difference between PD and HD for these drug classes possibly overestimated. Additionally, the SNDS doesn’t include drugs dispensed during hospital stays, which may have led to an underestimation of overall drug use, particularly in Q-1, where the interquartile range of hospital stay was 1.0–11.0 days—the quarter with the longest hospitalizations. A third limitation is that although the SNDS does not provide prescription duration, forcing us to estimate prevalence quarterly, this quarterly estimation across all drug classes is a rarely performed approach. For a study of this scale and macroscopic nature, this limitation does not undermine the validity of the results.

Conclusion

In French older patients on dialysis, polypharmacy was highly prevalent and decreased only slightly after dialysis initiation, contrary to what we might expect. Nevertheless, a substantial part of this polypharmacy is likely to be appropriate, given the complex clinical condition of older dialysis patients. This study highlights the many changes in drug prescribing around the time of dialysis initiation, particularly in CKD-related drugs. This can be a source of errors and, consequently, of adverse events, particularly in the older population. Nephrology teams should be vigilant and support these changes through drug reconciliation and deprescribing. Further studies are needed to explain the very high use of PPIs in this older dialysis population and to study if this polypharmacy is accompanied by potentially inappropriate prescriptions.
