Single-chamber retrievable leadless pacemaker in adult congenital heart disease: A single-center experience
Authors: Giovanni Domenico Ciriello, Diego Colonna, Anna Correra, Giovanni Papaccioli, Emanuele Romeo, Berardo Sarubbi
Published: 2026

Key Findings ▪Single-chamber retrievable leadless pacemaker implantation (Aveir VR and AR) is feasible and safe in patients with adult congenital heart disease (ACHD), with excellent short-term outcomes.▪The retrievable design, active fixation, and mapping-guided implantation may be particularly advantageous in complex congenital anatomies.▪Leadless pacing expands therapeutic options for patients with ACHD at high infective, thromboembolic, or surgical risk.▪Aveir AR may broaden leadless pacing options by enabling AAI atrial-only pacing in selected patients with ACHD and isolated sinus node dysfunction.

Introduction

Bradyarrhythmias are frequent in patients with adult congenital heart disease (ACHD), resulting from both congenital and acquired abnormalities of the conduction system. Conventional pacing (transvenous and epicardial) in ACHD is technically challenging and associated with a high burden of short- and long-term complications.1,2 Leadless pacemakers (LPMs) have emerged as an appealing alternative. By eliminating transvenous leads and subcutaneous pockets, their fully intracardiac design makes them particularly suitable for patients with ACHD. The Micra device (Medtronic) has been most widely used, whereas the newer Aveir system (Abbott) introduces active screw-in fixation, retrievability, intracardiac electrical mapping capabilities, and extended battery longevity. However, real-world data on LPM experience in ACHD remains limited.

Therefore, we aimed to examine our preliminary clinical experience, evaluating feasibility, safety, and early outcomes of the single-chamber retrievable LPMs (Aveir AR atrial LPM and Aveir VR ventricular LPM) in a cohort of patients with ACHD.

Methods

We included patients with ACHD with no expected high pacing burden and at least 1 of the following conditions: unfavorable anatomy or venous access (occluded veins, congenital anomalies), significant tricuspid valve disease, unrepaired or residual intracardiac shunt, and high infective and/or surgical risk (previous infective endocarditis, multiple surgical and/or percutaneous interventions, multiple comorbidities). This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments and was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of our local ethics committee. Each patient provided informed written consent.

All patients underwent a comprehensive pre-procedural evaluation, including electrocardiogram, transthoracic echocardiography, chest X-ray, and Holter monitoring. All procedures were carried out under general anesthesia. The right femoral vein was the standard venous access. For the Aveir VR device, mapping was performed to evaluate pacing thresholds, impedance, and sensing parameters before deployment and fixation in the mid-apical septum. For the Aveir AR device, potential implantation sites in the right atrium were mapped after angiographic evaluation of the right appendage anatomy in left and right anterior oblique projections. At the end of the procedure, fluoroscopy and transthoracic echocardiography were used to confirm stable positioning of the device, absence of pericardial effusion, and adequate distance from the tricuspid valve without mechanical interaction. Hemostasis was obtained with pressure bandage and by application of the ProGlide closure system at the puncture site. After discharge, patients underwent clinical evaluation, electrocardiogram, echocardiography, and device interrogation 1 week and 1 month after the implant, and every 3 months thereafter.

One patient underwent Aveir AR implantation (Figure 1D). An atrial AAI LPM was selected instead of a dual-chamber system due to isolated sinus node dysfunction with preserved atrioventricular conduction (pacing threshold 1.75 V at 1 ms, P-wave sensing 3.6 mV, impedance 460 Ω). No acute procedural complications occurred, and the post-operative course was uneventful in all cases. Median total procedure time was 90 minutes (Q1–Q3, 75–100), with a median fluoroscopy time of 14 minutes (Q1–Q3, 7.5–16.5).

After a median follow-up of 6 months (Q1–Q3, 4.75–8), no device- or procedure-related complications (infections, device dislodgement, vascular complications, valve injury, or thromboembolism) were observed. Electrical parameters remained satisfactory. For the Aveir VR devices, median impedance was 550 Ω (Q1–Q3, 490–655), with median R-wave sensing of 10.8 mV (Q1–Q3, 7.0–11.9) and stable pacing thresholds ranging from 0.5 V to 1.25 V at 0.2 ms and from 0.25 V to 0.75 V at 0.4 ms. Median ventricular pacing percentage was 1% (Q1–Q3, 1–9.5). Estimated median battery longevity was 21.4 years (Q1–Q3, 19.4–22.4). For the Aveir AR LMP, pacing threshold was 0.5 V at 0.2 ms, P-wave sensing 5.4 mV, and impedance 310 Ω. Estimated battery longevity was 9.5 years with atrial pacing percentage of 48%.

Discussion

This study provides novel clinical evidence supporting the use of the Aveir LPM system in ACHD. Our experience, representing the largest European cohort reported to date and including our first documented implantation of the Aveir AR device for AAI pacing in this population, adds to the limited but growing literature on leadless pacing in ACHD.

Traditional transvenous systems are often contraindicated or technically unfeasible in ACHD due to abnormal venous pathways or venous obstruction, elevated thromboembolic and infective risk, and the potential for valvular injury or endocarditis. Epicardial pacing, although avoiding intravascular hardware, requires surgical access and is associated with higher long-term failure rates and progressively increasing thresholds, often necessitating repeat surgical interventions.1,2 In patients in whom both transvenous and epicardial approaches are contraindicated, undesirable, or not feasible because of their overall risk profile, leadless pacing may represent the only viable therapeutic alternative.

In this context, the Aveir LPM system represents a potentially transformative alternative. The active fixation mechanism ensures stable device positioning, whereas the ability to perform electrical mapping and select the optimal implantation site is particularly advantageous in congenitally remodeled chambers with areas of scar and fibrosis or low-voltage myocardium secondary to chronic hemodynamic overload or prior surgical and/or percutaneous interventions. Moreover, the retrievability feature and the potential for future upgrades to dual-chamber leadless pacing represent a significant advancement over earlier LPM generations, addressing long-term management concerns in younger patients who may require multiple pacing system revisions during follow-up.

Our findings align with those of Rahmat and Cortez, who reported successful Aveir VR implantation in 3 patients with ACHD, as well as isolated case reports of Aveir VR or DR systems used for atrial pacing in sinus node dysfunction.3, 4, 5, 6 In our cohort, both ventricular and atrial leadless systems demonstrated excellent acute and short-term outcomes, supporting the extension of LPM to anatomically complex congenital substrates.

The Aveir AR system introduces a new pacing option in ACHD. The ability to deliver atrial-only (AAI) pacing in patients with preserved atrioventricular conduction may offer important advantages, including avoidance of unnecessary ventricular pacing, reduction of pacing-induced dyssynchrony, and prevention of adverse ventricular remodeling. Sinus node dysfunction is highly prevalent among patients with ACHD. However, patients with ACHD also frequently present with concomitant atrioventricular conduction disturbances, which may limit the use of single-chamber Aveir AR implantation to highly selected cases only. For these patients, Aveir VR or DR devices appear to be the safest option.

Patient selection remains crucial. Ideal patients for single-chamber ventricular leadless pacing should theoretically have low expected pacing burden. However, the same does not apply to single-chamber Aveir AR when appropriate, nor should it apply to dual-chamber Aveir DR, where atrioventricular synchrony can be preserved. Aveir LPM is designed to be a modular dual-chamber system. Dual-chamber leadless capability, versatility for both single-chamber atrial and/or ventricular pacing, as well as potential for future upgrade from single-chamber to dual-chamber systems, may represent additional advantages of the novel Aveir LPM system compared with previous LPM device generations, avoiding the risk of pacemaker syndrome, and also providing the possibility for a tailored case-to-case approach for arrhythmia management.

Ideal candidates for LMP include patients with ACHD at high risk for complications from conventional systems, such as those with previous infective endocarditis, high infective risk, residual or unrepaired intracardiac shunts, prosthetic materials, unfavorable venous anatomy, multiple prior surgeries, or significant comorbidities. For such individuals, the Aveir system may represent not only a safer alternative but also may extend pacing therapy to previously ineligible patients.

In patients with ACHD with intracardiac shunts, transvenous leads should be avoided due to the risk of paradoxical embolism. When epicardial pacing is not feasible due to a prohibitive surgical risk, or refused, LPM may represent a viable option. LPMs are considered less thrombogenic than traditional leads due to their limited intravascular surface and complete encapsulation within a few months after implantation.7 To date, only a single case of paradoxical embolism through a patent foramen ovale following Micra implantation has been reported.8 In our case series, 3 patients with residual or unrepaired shunt were on oral anticoagulation therapy before implantation and restarted the same regimen after LPM implant. Two patients with unrepaired ventricular septal defect were not receiving antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy at baseline. Considering the extremely low thrombogenic risk associated with LPM systems and the restrictive nature of the shunt, in the absence of additional prothrombotic risk factors, no specific post-implant anticoagulation or antiplatelet therapy was initiated.

Similarly, LPM-related infection is exceedingly rare, with 4 cases of Micra infections and only 1 Aveir infection described in the literature.9,10 Complete endothelialization of the device contributes to this low infective risk by minimizing blood-exposed surface and preventing pathogen adhesion.9,10 This is especially important in the ACHD population, which is particularly predisposed to infective complications.

Although our preliminary results are encouraging, they must be interpreted with caution. The sample size is limited, and follow-up duration remains short. Multicenter studies could further expand the applicability of this technology in ACHD.

Conclusion

Our preliminary experience supports the use of the Aveir LPM system as a promising and safe alternative to conventional pacing strategies in patients with ACHD. Its unique combination of retrievability, mapping capability, and versatility for both atrial and ventricular pacing may provide a tailored approach to rhythm management in this complex and growing population.
