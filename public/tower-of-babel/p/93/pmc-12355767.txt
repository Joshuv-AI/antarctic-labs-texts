Culturally responsive assessment of help-seeking behavior among university students: a mediation-moderation analysis of cultural norms, mental health stigma, and digital engagement across cross-cultural contexts
Authors: Simon Ntumi, Joshua-Luther Ndoye Upoalkpajor, Daniel Gyapong Nimo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHelp-seeking behavior for mental health issues among university students is shaped by cultural norms, stigma, and digital engagement. In many African contexts, cultural perceptions of mental illness remain highly stigmatized, posing barriers to seeking support. This study examined the mediating role of mental health stigma and the moderating effect of digital engagement in the relationship between cultural norms and help-seeking behavior among Ghanaian university students. A cross-sectional quantitative design using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was employed, with data from 1,067 students across five traditional public universities. Findings revealed that cultural norms significantly increased mental health stigma (β = 0.420, p < .001), which in turn reduced help-seeking behavior (β = -0.370, p < .001). A direct negative effect of cultural norms on help-seeking behavior was also observed (β = -0.210, p < .001). The indirect effect through stigma was significant (β = -0.155, p < .001), confirming the mediating role of stigma. Digital engagement moderated both the direct and indirect relationships. Specifically, it weakened the negative impact of stigma on help-seeking (β = 0.125, p = .002) and buffered the effect of cultural norms (β = 0.098, p = .005). Moderated mediation analysis further showed that digital engagement acted as a protective factor: stronger mediation was observed at lower levels of digital engagement (β = -0.198, p < .001) and weaker mediation at higher levels (β = -0.082, p < .001). These findings underscore the complex interplay of cultural and technological factors in mental health behaviors. The study highlights the need for culturally responsive mental health interventions and supports the integration of digital tools to reduce stigma and enhance help-seeking among university students.Keywords: Help-seeking behavior, Mental health stigma, Cultural norms, Digital engagement, Cross-cultural context

Background

In recent years, mental health has increasingly become a cornerstone of global public health discourse, as nations and institutions grapple with the social, economic, and psychological ramifications of unaddressed mental illness [1, 9, 40]. The global burden of mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety disorders, and substance abuse is particularly pronounced among young adults, especially those in higher education. University students occupy a uniquely vulnerable demographic, frequently exposed to academic stress, financial uncertainty, identity exploration, and changing social roles [9]. These transitional pressures render them susceptible to a wide array of psychological difficulties, ranging from burnout and emotional dysregulation to more severe manifestations such as suicidal ideation or substance dependence. The World Health Organization [45, 46] underscores this growing concern, estimating that up to 35% of university students worldwide exhibit symptoms indicative of common mental health disorders, including depression, anxiety, and stress-related conditions. Alarmingly, this psychological distress often goes unacknowledged or untreated, with students reluctant to seek formal mental health services. Instead, many rely on informal coping mechanisms or peer support, leaving underlying conditions unaddressed and escalating the risk of academic failure, social withdrawal, or chronic mental health issues in later life [1, 9, 34, 40].

Despite increasing awareness, help-seeking behaviors among students remain persistently low across diverse regions. This paradox high prevalence of mental health issues but low service utilization raises critical questions about the socio-cultural, institutional, and individual-level factors that inhibit or facilitate access to care [21]. While structural barriers such as cost, access, and service availability are influential, a growing body of research emphasizes the cultural context as a crucial determinant of help-seeking. In many societies, beliefs about mental health are deeply entwined with cultural scripts about normality, morality, and personal responsibility, all of which shape individuals'interpretations of psychological distress and the legitimacy of seeking help [12, 25]. Particularly in collectivist cultures characterized by a strong emphasis on social harmony, interdependence, and familial reputation mental illness is often perceived not merely as an individual affliction but as a threat to group cohesion and identity [41]. In such settings, seeking help from external professionals may be discouraged, especially if it risks exposing family vulnerabilities or undermining social status. Emotional restraint, stoicism, and silence are often culturally valued responses to distress, contributing to a normalization of psychological suffering and a stigmatization of those who deviate from these norms [1, 9, 40]. These patterns are especially evident in sub-Saharan African societies, where traditional spiritual, religious, and moral frameworks often dominate interpretations of mental health conditions.

In the African context, and particularly in Ghana, mental illness continues to be viewed through a spiritual or moralistic lens, often attributed to curses, witchcraft, or divine punishment. Such explanations not only pathologize individuals but also intensify public stigma and self-stigma, discouraging open discussions about mental health or formal help-seeking [2, 12, 34, 45]. Furthermore, mental health systems in most African countries, including Ghana, are grossly underfunded, with limited access to trained professionals, culturally appropriate interventions, or supportive policy frameworks [27, 46]. These structural deficiencies are compounded by mental health illiteracy, where students may lack the language, concepts, or awareness needed to recognize symptoms and seek appropriate care [6–8, 20, 53]. Ghana’s rapidly expanding higher education system presents a microcosm of these challenges. With more youth entering universities than ever before, Ghanaian institutions face increasing pressure to respond to students'mental health needs. However, the intersection of cultural stigma, limited counseling infrastructure, and emerging but underutilized digital health platforms creates a complex terrain for mental health intervention [4, 35]. On one hand, digital mental health tools such as online counseling platforms, mobile health applications, and teletherapy offer innovative solutions for reaching students discreetly and at scale. On the other hand, these tools may not gain traction if they are not perceived as trustworthy, accessible, or culturally aligned with students’ lived realities [32].

To address these challenges, scholars have called for a more integrated analytical approach to understanding student help-seeking. Specifically, there is a need to examine how cultural norms shape mental health attitudes, and how these relationships are mediated by internalized stigma and moderated by engagement with digital platforms. Mental health stigma, in particular, has been shown to mediate the link between cultural expectations and willingness to seek psychological help. When students internalize negative stereotypes about mental illness such as beliefs that therapy is a sign of weakness or moral failure they are less likely to acknowledge their needs or pursue professional support [13, 43, 49]. Simultaneously, digital engagement defined in this study as the extent to which individuals use digital mental health resources such as mobile apps, online counseling platforms, and mental health-related social media content may function as a moderating factor that either reduces or exacerbates the effects of stigma [2, 34, 45]. For some students, the anonymity and accessibility of digital platforms may circumvent traditional stigma and facilitate help-seeking. For others, concerns about data privacy, cultural mistrust, or limited digital literacy may negate these benefits [13, 31, 32]. These nuanced relationships are best captured through mediation-moderation models, which can disentangle the direct and indirect pathways linking culture, stigma, and behavior, while accounting for conditional effects across different levels of digital engagement. However, there is a significant gap in the literature, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, regarding the use of such models within culturally responsive assessment frameworks. Many existing psychometric tools for assessing help-seeking behavior are developed in Western contexts and lack relevance or validity in African educational environments. As such, there is an urgent need to develop or adapt instruments that are sensitive to local linguistic, cultural, and contextual realities, ensuring that the constructs measured reflect students’ authentic experiences, interpretations, and help-seeking pathways [13, 31, 32].

Over the past decade, the mental health of young adults has gained prominence in global public health discourse, with particular attention to university students navigating the academic, social, and emotional demands of higher education. In response, several interventions ranging from institutional counseling services to digital mental health platforms have emerged to mitigate the psychological burden among students. However, despite this progress, a persistent gap remains in understanding and addressing help-seeking behavior among university students in Africa, particularly in a way that is culturally relevant and methodologically comprehensive. Existing models of help-seeking behavior are predominantly informed by Western psychological theories and constructs, which often assume individualistic orientations toward autonomy, self-disclosure, and mental health literacy. These models inadequately reflect the lived realities of students in African contexts, where cultural norms are shaped by collectivism, social conformity, and moral-spiritual interpretations of psychological distress. As a result, prevailing frameworks tend to misrepresent or oversimplify the sociocultural dynamics that shape mental health attitudes and service utilization in non-Western societies [2, 44, 46].

In Ghana, this disconnect is particularly evident. While university-based counseling services and a growing number of digital mental health platforms are theoretically available to students, actual utilization rates remain conspicuously low [6, 8, 25, 35]. Several studies have attributed this underutilization to entrenched cultural stigma, whereby mental illness is interpreted as a moral failing, spiritual curse, or source of familial shame beliefs that deter individuals from acknowledging distress or seeking professional assistance [3, 20, 34, 37, 53]. Such stigma is not merely external but also internalized by students, contributing to feelings of weakness, guilt, or embarrassment, which in turn suppress help-seeking intentions. Compounding this challenge is the growing integration of digital mental health tools, which offer novel avenues for access, privacy, and scale. While digital platforms including mobile applications, online therapy, and virtual support communities have gained traction in many high-income settings, their uptake in Ghana remains modest. Barriers such as digital literacy, infrastructural limitations, concerns about privacy, and the cultural misalignment of content may hinder the perceived relevance or effectiveness of these technologies. Importantly, the influence of digital engagement on students’ mental health behaviors is neither uniform nor well understood, particularly in settings where cultural and technological factors interact in complex ways [4, 6, 31, 49].

Moreover, a major limitation in the current body of research is the absence of integrated analytical approaches capable of capturing the mediating and moderating variables that structure help-seeking behavior [1, 9, 40]. Few studies examine how stigma mediates the relationship between cultural norms and help-seeking intentions, and even fewer consider how digital engagement moderates this relationship either amplifying or mitigating stigma’s influence. Without this analytical nuance, interventions remain generalized, ineffective, or culturally tone-deaf. Another critical gap lies in the use of culturally responsive assessment tools. Instruments commonly used to measure mental health attitudes and behaviors are rarely adapted for linguistic, spiritual, and contextual congruence with African populations. This lack of contextual sensitivity leads to misinterpretation of results and, ultimately, policy decisions and intervention strategies that do not resonate with the target population [42, 43]. Given these gaps, there is a pressing need for research that is not only methodologically rigorous but also contextually grounded research that interrogates how cultural norms shape attitudes toward mental health, how stigma functions as a psychological barrier, and how digital platforms may alter these relationships across diverse student populations.

This study addresses these limitations by employing a mediation-moderation analytical model within a culturally responsive assessment framework to investigate the determinants of help-seeking behavior among university students in Ghana. By analyzing how cultural norms influence help-seeking through stigma, and how this relationship is conditioned by the level of digital mental health engagement, the study seeks to unpack the layered socio-psychological and technological factors shaping mental health behavior. The significance of this study extends beyond theoretical contribution. It offers timely and actionable insights for mental health practitioners, university administrators, and policy makers seeking to design and implement inclusive, effective, and culturally appropriate mental health interventions in Ghana and similar low- and middle-income contexts. Furthermore, it contributed to decolonizing psychological research by centering indigenous values, local realities, and culturally nuanced interpretations of mental health in the Global South.

Theoretical framework: hofstede’s cultural dimensions theory

This study is anchored in Hofstede’s Cultural Dimensions Theory, which provides a robust analytical framework for examining how deeply embedded cultural values influence individual attitudes, perceptions, and behaviors across different societies [27]. Hofstede’s model comprises six dimensions Individualism vs. Collectivism, Power Distance, Uncertainty Avoidance, Masculinity vs. Femininity, Long-Term vs. Short-Term Orientation, and Indulgence vs. Restraint. These dimensions offer insights into the cultural underpinnings of social and psychological behavior, particularly in cross-cultural contexts. Among these, three dimensions Collectivism vs. Individualism, Uncertainty Avoidance, and Indulgence vs. Restraint are particularly relevant for understanding the psychological and behavioral dynamics underlying help-seeking for mental health among university students in Ghana. Ghana is widely recognized as a collectivist society, where values such as group loyalty, social interdependence, and communal responsibility are emphasized over individual autonomy [27, 35]. Within such cultural settings, individuals often refrain from disclosing personal or psychological struggles, especially those related to mental illness, due to the fear of social stigma, familial shame, or damaging one's social reputation [34, 56, 58]. The concern for preserving family honor and community harmony often leads to emotional suppression and avoidance of formal mental health services (Addai et al., 2014). Therefore, collectivist norms may indirectly reinforce internalized stigma, thereby creating significant psychological and social barriers to help-seeking behavior.

In addition, the dimension of Uncertainty Avoidance, which reflects the extent to which societies feel threatened by ambiguous or unknown situations, is highly applicable in the Ghanaian context [27]. Ghanaian culture demonstrates a moderate-to-high uncertainty avoidance index, indicating a preference for predictability, structure, and traditional approaches to problem-solving [23]. Mental health services especially professional counseling and psychotherapy are often perceived as unfamiliar, foreign, or stigmatized interventions. As a result, individuals may resist seeking help due to anxiety surrounding the unknown nature of these services and the potential disruption of normative social roles [17, 23]. Furthermore, Indulgence vs. Restraint pertains to the degree to which societies permit or regulate the gratification of desires and emotional expression. Ghana leans toward the restraint end of the spectrum, characterized by cultural norms that promote self-discipline, emotional control, and suppression of internal struggles [27]. This dimension is particularly significant in shaping attitudes toward mental health expression, as individuals in restraint-oriented cultures are less likely to seek help for psychological issues that may be interpreted as emotional weakness or moral failing [24, 34]. By grounding the study in Hofstede’s Cultural Dimensions Theory, this research seeks to unpack the ways in which cultural norms particularly those related to collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, and restraint mediate and moderate the relationships between mental health stigma and help-seeking behavior. Additionally, the study explores how digital engagement, including the use of mental health apps and online counseling platforms, may buffer or amplify these cultural influences, potentially serving as a modern tool to challenge traditional barriers to psychological support among university students in Ghana.

Methods

Design and population

This study adopted a cross-sectional quantitative research design to investigate the mediating role of mental health stigma and the moderating effect of digital engagement in the relationship between cultural norms and help-seeking behavior among university students in Ghana. The use of a cross-sectional approach was appropriate for capturing attitudes and behaviors at a single point in time across a large, diverse sample [15]. The analytical framework employed mediation-moderation modeling, which allows for the simultaneous examination of both indirect effects (mediation) and conditional effects (moderation) among key variables [22]. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was chosen as the primary analytic technique because of its capacity to test complex theoretical relationships and latent constructs with precision [25, 26]. A culturally responsive assessment lens guided the study, ensuring that measurement instruments and interpretations were sensitive to the social, linguistic, and normative realities of Ghanaian university students [3, 17, 19]. The study population consisted of undergraduate students enrolled in Ghana’s five traditional public universities: the University of Ghana (UG), Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (KNUST), University of Cape Coast (UCC), University for Development Studies (UDS), and the University of Education, Winneba (UEW). These institutions were purposively selected based on three key criteria: their geographical dispersion across major regions of Ghana (Greater Accra, Ashanti, Central, Northern, and Eastern), their large and diverse student populations, and the institutional variation in academic focus and governance structures. Collectively, these universities provided a robust and contextually varied platform for investigating cultural and contextual factors influencing help-seeking behaviors among university students [18, 19, 36]. A multistage sampling procedure was employed to ensure representativeness and minimize sampling bias. In the first stage, faculties were randomly selected from three broad academic domains: social sciences, humanities, and health-related disciplines. This ensured disciplinary diversity and relevance to the mental health topic.

In the second stage, stratified random sampling was used to select students within each faculty. Stratification was based on year of study (first through final year) and gender, with strata proportions mirroring the official enrollment statistics from each institution’s registry. Specifically, efforts were made to maintain near-equal gender representation (e.g., 50% male, 50% female where feasible) and proportional year-level distribution to reflect the typical student progression structure at each university. This approach enhanced sampling transparency and supported the generalizability of findings across diverse student subgroups [11, 15, 16]. The target sample size for this study was set at 1,200 university students, consistent with best-practice guidelines for structural equation modeling (SEM), which recommend large samples (typically n > 1,000) to ensure accurate and stable parameter estimation, improve the reliability of fit indices, and enhance the generalizability of model-based findings [10]. Beyond SEM considerations, a detailed a priori power analysis was also conducted using G*Power 3.1, tailored specifically to detect small-to-moderate effect sizes (f2 = 0.02 to 0.05) in mediation and moderation models via multiple regression analysis. The power analysis assumed a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a desired statistical power of 0.95, which is more conservative than the conventional 0.80, and suitable for detecting nuanced psychological and behavioral effects. Based on these parameters and accounting for multiple predictors and interaction terms in the model, the minimum sample required was estimated to be approximately 1,050 participants. Therefore, to further ensure robustness and accommodate potential missing data or nonresponse, the initial target was set at 1,200 participants. A minimum of 240 students per institution was also targeted to enable institutional-level comparisons and ensure adequate representation across diverse contexts. After data cleaning and removal of incomplete or invalid responses, 1,067 valid responses were retained, resulting in a response rate of approximately 89%. This large, demographically diverse, and geographically distributed sample provided sufficient statistical power for testing the study’s complex moderated mediation models, as well as for conducting meaningful subgroup analyses by gender, year of study, and institutional affiliation.

Measures

The survey instrument used in this study consisted of five comprehensive sections, each targeting a key construct central to the research framework: demographic characteristics, cultural norms, mental health stigma, digital engagement, and help-seeking behavior. The instrument was developed and refined through a culturally responsive lens to ensure contextual fit within the Ghanaian university setting, while maintaining psychometric integrity. Demographic data collected included participants’ age, gender, university affiliation, program of study, level/year of study, and prior exposure to counseling services. This section provided the background necessary for subgroup analyses and control in multivariate modeling. Cultural norms were assessed using an adapted version of the Cultural Values Scale (CVSCALE) by [48]. The scale was chosen for its grounding in Hofstede’s cultural dimensions theory and its wide use in cross-cultural psychological research. The adapted version focused on three core dimensions relevant to the Ghanaian cultural context: collectivism (e.g., prioritizing group harmony), uncertainty avoidance (e.g., discomfort with ambiguity), and power distance (e.g., deference to authority figures). Items were reworded slightly for semantic clarity while preserving the scale’s structure. Mental health stigma was measured using the Self-Stigma of Seeking Help Scale (SSOSH) developed by [43]. The SSOSH is a validated 10-item Likert-type instrument designed to capture internalized negative beliefs about seeking psychological help. The scale was selected due to its strong predictive validity in diverse populations and its applicability to help-seeking contexts.

Digital engagement was assessed using a self-developed scale specifically designed for this study to capture students’ interactions with digital mental health resources. Given the absence of a standardized or mature instrument for measuring digital engagement in the context of mental health help-seeking particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa the scale was constructed based on theoretical insights from existing literature on e-health literacy, digital mental health adoption, and youth digital behaviors [8]. The items were designed to capture both behavioral frequency (e.g., “How often do you use mental health apps or visit mental health-related websites?”) and perceived usefulness (e.g., “How helpful have online mental health platforms or social media content been for your emotional wellbeing?”), allowing for a multidimensional understanding of digital engagement. To strengthen content validity, initial items were reviewed by a panel of three experts in psychology, educational measurement, and digital health. A pre-test study was then conducted with a sample of 78 university students from one institution not included in the final study. Feedback from this phase informed item refinement for clarity and relevance. Following the main data collection, the scale’s psychometric properties were examined using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) with principal axis factoring and Promax rotation. The EFA yielded a two-factor structure consistent with the conceptual dimensions of “frequency of use” and “perceived utility,” with factor loadings ranging from 0.62 to 0.81. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.84 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ2 = 1,228.45, p < 0.001), confirming sampling adequacy and factorability of the data. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86 for the overall scale, and subscale alphas of 0.83 and 0.79, respectively. Despite these steps, some limitations remain. First, while EFA supported the initial dimensional structure, the absence of a Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) limits the generalizability of the scale beyond the current sample. Second, the instrument is inherently context-specific, developed within the Ghanaian university context, which may restrict cross-cultural applicability. Third, self-reported digital behaviors may be subject to recall bias or social desirability effects, potentially inflating reported engagement levels. Nonetheless, given the novelty of the construct in this setting, the scale provides a valuable exploratory tool, and its findings should be interpreted as part of an evolving research agenda into digital mental health practices among young adults in low- and middle-income countries. Help-seeking behavior was measured using the General Help-Seeking Questionnaire (GHSQ) developed by [39], which asks participants to rate their likelihood of seeking help from various sources (e.g., friends, counselors, online platforms) for mental health concerns. This scale was selected due to its flexibility and established validity across global youth populations. All scales underwent expert review and were pre-tested with a sample of 50 undergraduate students at the University of Education, Winneba (UEW) to ensure clarity, face validity, and cultural relevance. Based on feedback, minor revisions were made to remove ambiguous phrasing and align item examples with Ghanaian student experiences. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, with coefficients ranging from 0.78 (digital engagement) to 0.89 (mental health stigma), indicating acceptable to excellent reliability [33].

Procedures and ethics

Data collection took place between October and December 2024 and was conducted entirely online due to its efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and accessibility for university students accustomed to digital platforms [47]. An online survey was developed using Google Forms and shared via multiple institutional and social media channels, including official university mailing lists, WhatsApp groups, and faculty e-learning platforms. Prior to data collection, ethical approval was secured from each participating university’s Institutional Review Board (IRB), and administrative permission was obtained from the Students’ Affairs and ICT directorates to assist in disseminating the survey link. Participation was strictly voluntary, and the survey began with a detailed informed consent section, assuring respondents of confidentiality, data protection, and the right to withdraw at any time without penalty. To enhance participation, weekly reminders were sent through student leaders and departmental heads, and participants were offered a chance to win mobile airtime vouchers through a random draw. These incentive-based strategies are known to improve online response rates among student populations [17]. The questionnaire was administered in English, the official language of instruction in Ghanaian universities, and was carefully designed to be mobile-friendly to allow students to complete it on their smartphones devices that are widely owned and frequently used among Ghanaian youth [38, 44]. The estimated time to complete the survey was 12–15 min. To ensure quality data, logic checks and mandatory response settings were applied to prevent missing values and random answering. The survey was pre-tested with a small group of 50 students at UEW to assess clarity, length, and usability. Feedback from the pre-test was used to revise ambiguous items and improve the flow of the questionnaire before full-scale distribution.

Data analysis procedures

Quantitative data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 27 and AMOS Version 26. The analytic approach was structured into three major phases: data screening and preparation, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and hypothesis testing using mediation-moderation modeling. Preliminary data screening involved assessing for missing values, univariate and multivariate outliers, and violations of normality. Cases with excessive missing data or nonsensical responses were excluded. Normality of the data distribution was examined through skewness and kurtosis statistics, histograms, and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and no significant concerns were detected (VIF < 5). To evaluate the construct validity of the measurement model, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was performed using AMOS. Key fit indices used to assess model adequacy included the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Thresholds of CFI and TLI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.08, and SRMR ≤ 0.08 were used to determine acceptable model fit [7, 18, 23]. Following validation of the measurement model, the hypothesized mediation-moderation model was tested using Hayes’ PROCESS macro for SPSS (Model 8), which enables the simultaneous estimation of an indirect (mediated) path and a conditional (moderated) effect [11, 22, 26]. Specifically, the model assessed whether mental health stigma mediated the relationship between cultural norms and help-seeking behavior, and whether digital engagement moderated both the direct and indirect pathways. Bootstrapping procedures with 5,000 resamples were employed to generate bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals for indirect effects. Mediation was considered statistically significant if the confidence interval for the indirect effect did not include zero. The presence and direction of moderation effects were interpreted through interaction terms, and interaction plots were generated to illustrate how digital engagement influenced the strength of relationships at different levels of stigma and cultural norm endorsement. Statistical significance was determined at an alpha level of p < 0.05.

Results

This section presents the statistical findings from the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), mediation analysis, and moderated mediation analysis used to test the study’s hypothesized relationships. The analyses aimed to evaluate the structural validity of the measurement model and to examine the direct, indirect, and conditional effects of cultural norms, mental health stigma, and digital engagement on help-seeking behavior. Descriptive and inferential statistics were reported, including model fit indices, path coefficients, standard errors, confidence intervals, and significance levels. All analyses were conducted using established procedures, including bootstrapping for mediation and moderated mediation effects. The results are organized into three main parts: (1) model fit indices from the CFA, (2) mediation analysis of stigma as a mediator between cultural norms and help-seeking behavior, and (3) moderated mediation analysis examining the moderating role of digital engagement.

The results of the Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) in Table 1 indicate that the hypothesized measurement model fits the data well. Although the chi-square value (χ2 = 846.31, df = 356, p < 0.001) is statistically significant, this is expected given the large sample size, and thus not necessarily indicative of poor model fit. More importantly, the normed chi-square (χ2/df = 2.38) falls within the acceptable range (≤ 3.00), supporting the model’s adequacy. Fit indices such as the Comparative Fit Index (CFI = 0.94), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI = 0.92), Incremental Fit Index (IFI = 0.93), and Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI = 0.91) all exceed the recommended threshold of 0.90, indicating good model fit. The Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA = 0.06) and its 90% confidence interval (0.055–0.072) suggest an acceptable approximation error, with values well within the conventional cut-off of 0.08. The Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR = 0.05) also indicates a good fit. While the Adjusted Goodness-of-Fit Index (AGFI = 0.89) is just below the 0.90 benchmark, it remains within acceptable limits (≥ 0.85). Overall, these indices collectively confirm that the model fits the observed data well and is suitable for subsequent structural analyses.

The mediation analysis in Table 2 reveals a significant pathway through which cultural norms influence help-seeking behavior, mediated by mental health stigma. Specifically, cultural norms significantly increase mental health stigma (β = 0.420, p < 0.001), and in turn, stigma significantly decreases help-seeking behavior (β = −0.370, p < 0.001). The direct effect of cultural norms on help-seeking remains significant and negative (β = −0.210, p < 0.001), even after accounting for the mediating role of stigma, indicating partial mediation. The indirect effect through stigma is also significant (β = −0.155, p < 0.001), reinforcing the mediating role of stigma. Importantly, digital engagement appears to moderate these relationships. The interaction between digital engagement and stigma is significant (β = 0.125, p = 0.002), suggesting that increased digital engagement weakens the negative impact of stigma on help-seeking behavior. Similarly, digital engagement also moderates the direct effect of cultural norms (β = 0.098, p = 0.005), buffering their negative influence. The conditional indirect effects show that at high levels of digital engagement, the indirect effect of cultural norms on help-seeking through stigma is reduced (β = −0.082), while it is stronger when digital engagement is low (β = −0.198), both effects being statistically significant (p < 0.001). These findings suggest that digital engagement serves as a protective factor, reducing the harmful effects of cultural norms and stigma on help-seeking behavior.

Summary of the findings

This study explored how cultural norms, mental health stigma, and digital engagement interact to influence help-seeking behavior among university students in Ghana. The findings revealed that cultural norms significantly predicted lower levels of help-seeking behavior. Students embedded in more traditional or collectivist cultural settings were less likely to pursue professional psychological support. This aligns with previous literature indicating that in many African societies, help-seeking is often discouraged due to communal expectations, reliance on spiritual or informal support, and the stigmatization of mental illness. Mental health stigma emerged as a significant mediator in the relationship between cultural norms and help-seeking. That is, cultural norms do not simply discourage help-seeking directly they do so by fostering stigma, which then becomes the key psychological barrier. This highlights the pervasive role stigma plays as a mechanism through which cultural norms suppress help-seeking behavior, a finding consistent with global and African-based research on the subject. Importantly, digital engagement was found to significantly moderate the effect of mental health stigma on help-seeking behavior. Students who frequently used digital mental health tools, such as apps or online counseling services, reported higher levels of help-seeking, even in the presence of stigma. This suggests that digital tools serve as a buffer or protective factor that weakens the negative effect of stigma, supporting Hypotheses. Furthermore, digital engagement also moderated the direct relationship between cultural norms and help-seeking behavior, and moderated the mediation of stigma. In other words, the more digitally engaged students were, the less power cultural norms and stigma had in deterring them from seeking help. These findings collectively underscore the critical role of digital platforms in disrupting traditional barriers to mental health care in stigmatizing and culturally restrictive environments.

Discussion of results

As noted earlier, the CFA results suggest an acceptable model fit, with significant values across several indices (CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR). This aligns with guidelines set by [17, 23], who recommended using a combination of indices, rather than relying solely on Chi-Square, to assess model fit. Similar findings have been reported in other studies investigating mental health and help-seeking behaviors. For instance, in a study by [30], the authors conducted CFA to assess the fit of a mental health stigma scale, reporting an RMSEA of 0.05 and CFI of 0.91, supporting the adequacy of the current model's fit in representing the relationships between variables in this study. In line with this, the findings of acceptable fit indices in the current study further validate the robustness of the measurement model for cultural norms, stigma, and help-seeking behavior, suggesting it is appropriate for investigating the hypothesized relationships.

Hypothesis 1 (H1)

The results of the direct and indirect paths suggest that cultural norms have both a direct and indirect effect on help-seeking behavior, with mental health stigma acting as a mediator. This is supported by extensive research showing that cultural norms significantly impact mental health attitudes and help-seeking behaviors. For instance, studies by [38] have shown that societal attitudes and cultural norms around mental health influence whether individuals will seek professional help, with those in more collectivist cultures often more hesitant due to stigma. Moreover, the significant path from stigma to help-seeking behavior (β = −0.370, p < 0.001) reinforces findings from a wide range of studies that emphasize stigma as a major barrier to seeking mental health care. For example, [13, 14] found that individuals who perceived higher levels of stigma were less likely to seek professional help, highlighting the critical role that stigma plays in influencing mental health behaviors. Additionally, the negative relationship between Cultural Norms and Help-Seeking Behavior (β = −0.210, p < 0.001) aligns with the work of [29], who demonstrated that cultural norms such as shame and social judgment influence help-seeking behaviors, particularly in non-Western societies. Furthermore, the indirect effect of cultural norms → stigma → help-seeking behavior (β = −0.155, p < 0.001) supports previous studies like those of [5], who highlighted that stigma mediates the relationship between cultural attitudes and help-seeking behavior. These results are consistent with the social constructionist theory of stigma, which posits that societal norms shape the way individuals experience and internalize stigma, ultimately influencing their willingness to seek help [28, 30, 32].

Hypothesis 2 (H2)

The moderated mediation analysis presented in Table 3 offers compelling evidence that digital engagement acts as a significant moderator in the relationship between cultural norms, stigma, and help-seeking behavior. This finding is consistent with research that demonstrates how digital platforms can serve as a tool to reduce stigma and increase help-seeking behavior. For example, a study by [31] found that online mental health interventions reduced the impact of stigma by providing an anonymous space for individuals to seek help, which is particularly relevant in cultures where mental health stigma is prevalent. The moderating effect of digital engagement on the relationship between cultural norms and help-seeking behavior supports the idea that technology can break down cultural barriers by providing access to information and support without the fear of judgment. As [18] noted, digital interventions have the potential to bypass traditional stigma by offering an accessible and private means of seeking help. Similarly, a study by Griffiths et al. (2015) emphasized the role of online forums in helping individuals in stigmatized groups feel more comfortable discussing mental health issues, providing empirical support for the current study’s findings. The significant interaction between digital engagement and stigma (β = −0.100, p = 0.027) supports the work of [40], who found that digital mental health interventions can reduce the negative impact of stigma, thereby encouraging help-seeking behaviors. [40] noted that anonymity in digital spaces can significantly lessen the perceived stigma, making individuals more likely to reach out for support. Moreover, the finding that digital engagement moderates the indirect effect of cultural norms on help-seeking behavior via stigma provides additional evidence for the importance of technology in reducing the barriers posed by cultural norms. In line with studies by [39], who highlighted the role of eHealth initiatives in improving access to mental health services, the current study indicates that digital tools can mitigate the effects of cultural norms on help-seeking behavior, facilitating the overcoming of stigma and promoting mental health help-seeking.

Hypothesis 3 (H3)

The findings from the moderation analysis (Table 5) provide statistically significant evidence for H3. Specifically, mental health stigma emerged as a significant negative predictor of help-seeking behavior among university students (β = −0.395, p < 0.001), reinforcing extensive literature that highlights stigma as a major deterrent to mental health service utilization in African contexts [34, 48]. In Ghana, where mental illness is often interpreted through spiritual or moral lenses, stigma fosters fear of social judgment and self-concealment (Ofori-Atta et al., 2010), leading to reduced help-seeking behaviors. Encouragingly, digital engagement significantly moderated this relationship. The interaction between stigma and digital engagement was significant (β = 0.135, p = 0.002), with a small-to-moderate effect size (f2 = 0.050) and an R2 change of 0.029. This means that the negative effect of stigma on help-seeking becomes weaker among students who are more digitally engaged. These findings resonate with recent evidence showing that digital mental health tools such as self-help apps, online counseling, and social media platforms can mitigate stigma by offering privacy, anonymity, and convenience [56, 60]. Through digital tools, students are exposed to mental health education, peer testimonials, and virtual support systems, which can normalize help-seeking and counter negative cultural narratives. Furthermore, digital engagement was an independent positive predictor of help-seeking (β = 0.118, p = 0.003), suggesting that even outside of its moderating role, higher engagement with digital mental health platforms is associated with greater likelihood of seeking support. This aligns with findings from Sub-Saharan Africa indicating that mHealth interventions improve mental health literacy and promote positive behavioral intentions [23, 56, 59]. These results also have important gendered implications. Female students were significantly more likely than males to seek help (β = 0.072, p = 0.009), a trend widely reported in global mental health literature [49]. This gender difference may reflect socialization patterns where emotional openness is more culturally acceptable for women than for men, who often face compounded stigma for emotional vulnerability. Additionally, previous use of mental health services emerged as a strong positive predictor of future help-seeking (β = 0.192, p < 0.001). This reinforces the "gateway hypothesis" the idea that initial exposure to mental health care reduces fear and builds trust in the system, encouraging repeat utilization [57]. Taken together, these findings substantiate the protective function of digital engagement in reducing the inhibitory effects of mental health stigma on help-seeking among Ghanaian university students. They suggest that institutions aiming to improve mental health outcomes should invest in digital mental health infrastructure and promote online counseling, peer-support apps, and culturally tailored e-resources as integral components of student wellbeing programs. In culturally conservative environments where in-person therapy may remain stigmatized, digital pathways offer a discreet and empowering alternative.

Hypothesis H4

The moderation analysis presented in Table 6 provides compelling evidence in support of Hypothesis H4. Cultural norms were found to have a strong, statistically significant negative impact on help-seeking behavior (β = −0.231, p < 0.001), with a moderate effect size (f2 = 0.092). This finding is consistent with prior research demonstrating that in collectivist societies like Ghana, traditional beliefs about mental illness often rooted in spirituality, shame, and family honor serve as powerful barriers to seeking formal psychological support [55, 56, 58]. Such norms promote stoicism, discourage emotional vulnerability, and stigmatize professional help, especially among males and older individuals. However, digital engagement significantly weakened this negative relationship, as indicated by the significant interaction term (β = 0.109, p = 0.005), with a corresponding f2 = 0.043 and R2 change = 0.025. This means that among students with higher digital engagement, the suppressive influence of cultural norms on help-seeking was reduced. In essence, digital tools act as cultural disruptors, enabling students to circumvent traditional gatekeeping structures and access mental health support in ways that are more individualized, anonymous, and culturally flexible. This finding builds on studies that identify digital mental health resources as a form of cultural adaptation [34, 50, 52, 54]. Digital platforms can expose students to alternative mental health narratives through peer stories, psychoeducational content, and culturally tailored messaging that gradually reshape negative cultural perceptions. For instance, findings from a study in Nigeria by [51] showed that digital literacy and access to mental health apps significantly predicted positive help-seeking attitudes, especially among youth in rural communities. Furthermore, digital engagement itself was a significant positive predictor of help-seeking behavior (β = 0.106, p = 0.006), reinforcing its standalone value in promoting mental health awareness and support-seeking actions. This supports the broader body of literature that emphasizes the transformative role of e-mental health in enhancing access and acceptability, especially in contexts with limited formal infrastructure or pervasive stigma [43, 48]. Notably, the model’s control variables revealed additional meaningful insights. Female students were again more likely to seek help than males (β = 0.074, p = 0.009), aligning with global gender patterns in mental health behavior [34, 45, 47]. Age had a small but significant negative effect (β = −0.051, p = 0.039), suggesting that younger students may be more open to digital resources and progressive mental health attitudes. Previous use of mental health services (β = 0.184, p < 0.001) also strongly predicted help-seeking, reinforcing the “exposure-effect” of early mental health service utilization. Overall, the findings provide empirical backing for H4 by confirming that digital engagement reduces the cultural burden on mental health help-seeking. In culturally conservative environments, digital tools offer a non-confrontational avenue for challenging harmful norms and expanding mental health outreach. Institutions of higher learning should therefore invest in culturally contextualized digital mental health initiatives to engage students who are discouraged by traditional beliefs.

Hypothesis H5

The results presented in Table 7 confirm the presence of a statistically significant moderated mediation effect, offering nuanced insights into how cultural norms affect help-seeking through stigma, depending on the level of digital engagement. At low levels of digital engagement (− 1 SD), the indirect effect of cultural norms on help-seeking behavior through stigma was strong and statistically significant (B = −0.198, β = −0.207, 95% CI [−0.256, −0.142]. This indicates that when students are not engaging with digital mental health tools, cultural norms exert a profound negative influence by reinforcing stigma, which in turn suppresses help-seeking behavior. The κ2 effect size (0.078) further supports this as a moderate-to-large mediation effect (Preacher & Kelley, 2011), confirming the powerful psychological and cultural barriers to mental health help-seeking. However, the strength of this mediation diminished significantly as digital engagement increased. At average (mean) levels of digital engagement, the indirect effect was reduced (B = −0.145, β = −0.152, 95% CI [−0.193, −0.096]), with a κ2 = 0.056 representing a 26.8% reduction in mediation strength. This trend continued at high levels of digital engagement (+ 1 SD), where the indirect effect dropped further (B = −0.082, β = −0.086, 95% CI [−0.125, −0.038]), and the κ2 = 0.031, indicating a 58.6% reduction compared to low engagement. These findings reveal that digital engagement significantly attenuates the influence of cultural norms by weakening stigma’s mediating power. This is further confirmed by the Index of Moderated Mediation (B = 0.116, Boot SE = 0.028, 95% CI [0.062, 0.172]), which was significant and confirms that the strength of the indirect (mediated) relationship between cultural norms and help-seeking behavior via stigma varies systematically depending on digital engagement. These findings support previous studies showing that digital platforms offer alternative discourses and safe spaces for mental health exploration, which challenge culturally embedded stigma [50]. For example, [14] argue that web-based interventions can reduce mental health stigma among African American youth by offering culturally resonant stories and coping models. Similarly, [45, 46] found that digital interventions increased mental health service uptake in university students by reducing internalized stigma. Taken together, these results provide strong support for Hypothesis H5 by showing that the stigmatizing power of cultural norms is not static but conditional upon students’ level of digital mental health engagement. This moderated mediation underscores the strategic potential of digital tools in stigma reduction, suggesting that targeted digital interventions can disrupt entrenched cultural narratives that discourage help-seeking.

Implications for practice, policy, and research

Implications for practice

The findings of this study strongly suggest that mental health stigma, rooted in cultural norms, remains a substantial barrier to help-seeking among university students in Ghana. To address this, mental health professionals and university counseling centers must rethink how they deliver services, with an emphasis on culturally sensitive, technology-enabled interventions. First, mental health practitioners should incorporate digital platforms into their routine practice, not as supplementary tools but as primary access points for students who may be reluctant to pursue face-to-face counseling. For instance, integrating mobile apps that offer psychoeducation, self-assessments, relaxation techniques, and referral systems can help normalize mental health support. These platforms offer a sense of anonymity, reducing fear of judgment, and cater to students who value privacy due to cultural or personal concerns. Second, online counseling services and teletherapy options should be scaled up, particularly for students who may be located off-campus or who prefer text-based or asynchronous communication due to stigma or time constraints. Mental health professionals must be trained to deliver digitally mediated care competently, ensuring empathy and cultural awareness translate through virtual environments. Third, university counseling services should implement targeted outreach initiatives that use social media, podcasts, webinars, and campus influencers to demystify mental health care. These should specifically address prevailing cultural misconceptions for example, beliefs that mental illness is a sign of spiritual weakness or social failure. In practice, these steps will bridge the gap between need and access, ensuring that students receive the support they need in formats they find acceptable and accessible.

Implications for policy

From a policy standpoint, the results call for an urgent reform of institutional and national mental health strategies to reflect the shifting landscape of student behavior and technology use. At the institutional level, universities should develop formal digital mental health strategies as part of their broader student welfare programs. This involves creating policies that support the safe, ethical, and effective use of digital mental health tools, while ensuring compliance with data protection laws and professional standards. National education and health ministries, in collaboration with ICT authorities, should facilitate public–private partnerships to fund the development or adaptation of culturally tailored digital tools. For example, local developers could work with mental health professionals to design apps in local languages, incorporating cultural idioms and metaphors that resonate with Ghanaian students. Further, mental health policy should incorporate comprehensive stigma-reduction campaigns that go beyond awareness to foster behavioral and attitudinal change. These campaigns should challenge traditional beliefs that equate mental distress with personal failure or weakness and promote narratives that frame help-seeking as a strength. Policies should also mandate training of university staff and faculty in recognizing signs of psychological distress and in promoting supportive environments. These capacity-building efforts will ensure that students are not only encouraged to seek help but are met with understanding and appropriate referral pathways when they do. Additionally, policy frameworks must ensure equity in digital access. Given that digital engagement was found to buffer the effects of stigma and cultural norms, bridging the digital divide particularly in rural and underserved regions is not merely a technology issue but a mental health imperative.

Implications for research

The findings open up numerous avenues for further investigation that can deepen our understanding of the cultural, psychological, and technological dimensions of help-seeking. First, future research should unpack the specific features of digital engagement that are most effective in weakening the influence of stigma. Are students more influenced by anonymous peer forums, AI-based chatbots, video counseling sessions, or curated self-help content? Understanding these preferences will guide the design of more effective interventions. Second, longitudinal studies are needed to track the effects of digital engagement over time. Do students who begin with anonymous online help eventually transition to formal therapy? Does sustained use of mental health apps correlate with improved well-being or academic performance? Third, comparative studies could explore how digital engagement affects stigma and help-seeking across different cultural or regional settings within Ghana or across Sub-Saharan Africa. Such research would illuminate how ethnicity, religion, gender norms, or urban–rural divides shape the efficacy of digital tools in mental health. Moreover, experimental and mixed-methods research designs could provide richer insights into user experience and engagement patterns. Qualitative interviews or digital ethnographies, for example, could reveal the nuanced ways students navigate stigma online and the symbolic meanings they attach to various forms of digital help. Finally, there is a strong need for intervention-based research that tests culturally adapted digital mental health programs in university settings. Such studies should assess not only psychological outcomes (e.g., reductions in distress or increases in help-seeking) but also process variables like engagement, cultural acceptability, and perceived usefulness.

Limitations

One limitation of this study is the reliance on self-reported data, which may be subject to social desirability bias. Participants may have been inclined to provide responses that they believed were socially acceptable, particularly regarding sensitive topics such as mental health and help-seeking behavior. This bias could have influenced the accuracy of the findings, as individuals may have underreported stigma or overreported their engagement with digital platforms. Although efforts were made to ensure anonymity and confidentiality, self-reported data still poses a risk in accurately reflecting participants'true behaviors and attitudes. Another limitation lies in the generalizability of the results, as the study focused on a specific cultural context and sample population. The findings may not be directly applicable to other cultural or geographical contexts where cultural norms and stigma surrounding mental health may differ. Additionally, the sample size may have been too small to capture the full diversity of experiences within the population, limiting the ability to draw broader conclusions about the role of digital engagement in reducing stigma. Furthermore, potential confounding variables such as socioeconomic status, level of education, access to digital infrastructure, and previous mental health treatment history were not controlled for in the current analysis. These factors could have influenced both digital engagement and help-seeking behavior, thus potentially biasing the observed relationships. For example, students from higher-income backgrounds may have greater access to digital resources and may also be more familiar with or open to seeking professional help. Similarly, those with prior exposure to mental health services might have more favorable attitudes toward help-seeking, independent of stigma or cultural norms. Future research should consider including these variables in the analytic model to better isolate the effects of stigma, culture, and digital engagement on help-seeking behavior. Future research with a larger and more diverse sample would help validate and expand the findings, ensuring that the results are more universally applicable.

Conclusion

This study provides strong evidence that cultural norms and mental health stigma significantly hinder help-seeking behavior. In settings where collectivist values and community expectations shape perceptions of mental health, stigma acts as a powerful barrier, discouraging individuals especially students from accessing psychological support. These cultural attitudes not only discourage openness but also serve as a mediating force, linking norms to reduced mental health service use. Importantly, the study also highlights the moderating role of digital engagement. Digital tools such as mental health apps, online counseling, and awareness platforms emerge as effective buffers, weakening the negative impact of stigma and facilitating more help-seeking behavior. In cultures where discussing mental health is taboo, these platforms offer anonymity, accessibility, and reduced judgment, enabling users to engage privately with support resources. These findings align with growing research emphasizing the role of technology in expanding access to mental health care, particularly in stigma-prone or underserved communities. By examining the interplay between culture, stigma, and digital engagement, the study contributes valuable insights into the complex social barriers to mental health care. For mental health professionals and policymakers, the implications are clear: integrating digital mental health tools into intervention strategies can help reduce stigma and broaden service reach. However, digital solutions must be paired with long-term efforts to shift cultural attitudes, such as education, community engagement, and public policy initiatives. Overall, this research reinforces the need for culturally sensitive, digitally enabled mental health strategies. By addressing stigma at both cultural and technological levels, it is possible to foster more inclusive and supportive environments for mental health help-seeking, especially among university students navigating sociocultural pressures.
