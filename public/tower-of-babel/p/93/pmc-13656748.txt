Integrated multi-PTM omics reveals key host pathways in human coronavirus 229E infection
Authors: Xiaolu Li, Adam Kabza, Matthew J Gaffrey, Madelyn R Berger, Song Feng, Austin Gluth, Marina A Gritsenko, Patricia S Miller, Rosalie K Chu, Doo Nam Kim, Abdullah D Shouaib, Wei-Jun Qian, Katrina M Waters, Amy C Sims, Tong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractViruses manipulate host cellular functions to promote replication and evade antiviral responses, and many processes in host–virus interactions are regulated by protein post-translational modifications (PTMs). While previous studies have examined individual PTMs in host–virus interactions, a comprehensive, multi-PTM perspective of host remodeling during coronavirus infection remains lacking. To address this gap, we applied our multi-PTM omics platform to simultaneously quantify protein abundance, cysteine oxidation, phosphorylation, and lysine acetylation in human lung fibroblasts (MRC5) and epithelial cells (A549) infected with human coronavirus strain 229E (HCoV-229E) at 8, 16, and 24 h post-infection. We observed modest changes in the global host proteome, with only a small fraction of proteins affected even 24 h post-infection. In contrast, host PTM landscapes were rapidly and extensively remodeled, exhibiting pronounced and cell type–specific alterations in redox and phosphorylation states as early as 8 h post-infection. Phosphorylation profiling revealed widespread remodeling of host signaling networks with distinct temporal and directional patterns between MRC5 and A549 cells, while redox profiling uncovered divergent oxidative regulation of proteins involved in infection-related pathways. Notably, a subset of host proteins showed coordinated regulation across multiple PTM types without corresponding changes in abundance, highlighting potential PTM crosstalk during HCoV-229E infection. Among these, heat shock protein 90 beta (HSP90B) displayed dynamic regulation across cysteine oxidation, phosphorylation, and acetylation, and pharmacological inhibition of HSP90B significantly suppressed HCoV-229E replication. Together, these results provide a comprehensive, multi-dimensional view of host PTM remodeling during HCoV-229E infection and demonstrate that integrated multi-PTM omics can reveal functionally relevant host factors and therapeutic vulnerabilities not apparent from protein abundance measurements.Keywords: Multi-PTM, Multi-PTM omics, Host–virus interactions, Post-translational modification, Redox, Phosphorylation, Acetylation, Coronavirus, Host-directed antiviral targets

Introduction

Productive viral infection requires extensive reprogramming of host cellular pathways, including stress responses, innate immune signaling, translation, and metabolism [1,2]. These processes depend on a tightly regulated network of host–virus interactions that is extensively modulated by post-translational modifications (PTMs) [3]. In host cells, PTMs can rapidly alter host protein activities and thereby orchestrate antiviral defense pathways. For instance, dynamic rewiring of phosphorylation upon SARS-CoV-2 infection results in the activation of MAPK signaling pathways and the deactivation of mitotic kinases, resulting in cell cycle arrest [4]. In addition, redox-sensitive cysteine modifications fine-tune inflammatory and stress responses during host–respiratory virus interactions [5,6]. Protein acetylation can act as a molecular switch that regulates cell cycle progression in response to cytomegalovirus infection [6,7].

Conversely, viruses can hijack cellular PTM mechanisms to suppress innate immune responses and thereby promote replication. Viral infection commonly perturbs host phosphorylation networks by targeting kinases and phosphatases that regulate key antiviral signaling molecules such as TBK1, IRF3, and STAT1, thereby attenuating interferon induction and signaling [8]. Many viruses also encode proteins with deubiquitinating activity (e.g., the PLpro domain of CoV nsp3) or use cellular ubiquitin-modifying enzymes to remove activating ubiquitin chains from pattern-recognition receptors and adaptor proteins such as RIG-I, MAVS, and TRAFs, effectively dampening antiviral signaling cascades and facilitating viral propagation [9,10]. Moreover, viruses reprogram host acetylation machinery by modulating histone acetyltransferases and deacetylases, leading to altered acetylation of transcription factors and chromatin, suppression of antiviral gene expression, and establishment of a cellular environment permissive for viral replication [11,12].

Given this bidirectional dependence on PTMs, mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomics is a powerful approach to define the molecular details of PTM regulation in host–virus interactions and to identify host factors and therapeutic opportunities [4]. Phosphoproteomic analysis of SARS-CoV-2–infected Vero E6 cells demonstrated activation of casein kinase II (CK2) and p38 MAPK and suppression of mitotic kinases, leading to altered cytoskeletal organization, cytokine production, and cell cycle arrest [13]. Accordingly, pharmacological inhibition of CK2 with silmitasertib showed antiviral potential by reducing SARS-CoV-2 replication. Viral proteins themselves can also be extensively modified by PTMs such as phosphorylation, glycosylation, and ubiquitination, which regulate their folding, stability, and function. Phosphorylation of SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) by host glycogen synthase kinase-3 (GSK3) is essential for viral replication, and inhibiting GSK3 blocks N phosphorylation and suppresses replication [3,14].

Currently, most PTM studies in host–virus interactions focus on a single type of PTM, most often phosphorylation due to the maturity of phosphoproteomics technology. However, multiple types of PTMs can coexist on the same proteins, and it has been increasingly recognized that integrative PTM analysis can further uncover the coordinated regulation and PTM crosstalk [15]. To enable multi-PTM studies, we recently developed an integrative multi-PTM omics workflow that allows the quantification of phosphorylation, cysteine oxidation, and acetylation simultaneously from the same sample set [16,17]. The integrated workflow enabled direct identification of proteins and pathways that undergo coordinated regulation across PTM layers in beta cell in response to cytokine treatment, thereby prioritizing regulatory nodes in cell signaling for functional follow-up [18].

Here, we applied the multi-PTM omics workflow to systematically investigate the host PTM landscape in response to human coronavirus (HCoV) infection and to identify key host factors for future therapeutic development. Coronaviruses are a large family of enveloped, positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses responsible for both seasonal respiratory infections and recent global pandemics [19]. We focused on HCoV-229E, an Alphacoronavirus that typically causes mild upper respiratory symptoms, but can occasionally lead to severe disease in vulnerable populations [20]. Because HCoV-229E is genetically stable, replicates to high titers in multiple human cell types, and shares similar replication strategies with highly pathogenic coronaviruses (e.g., severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus or SARS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2, and Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus or MERS-CoV) [19], it has been widely used as a tractable model to study fundamental host–coronavirus interactions [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. We profiled infection time courses in two human lung cell types, A549 epithelial cells and MRC5 fibroblasts, representing airway epithelial response as the primary site of infection for respiratory coronaviruses [25,26] and lung fibroblast stromal remodeling in the respiratory tract [27], respectively. Our datasets revealed early, cell type–specific PTM remodeling and identified HSP90B as a host-directed therapeutic candidate for follow-up verification in future studies.

Materials and methods

Cell culture, virus stocks, and virus titration

The human lung fibroblast MRC5 cells (CCL-171 from ATCC) were cultured in α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM; Gibco, 12-561-056) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (Anti-Anti). The human liver epithelial cells HuH7 (JCRB0403 from Japanese Cancer Resource Bank) were cultured in low glucose Dulbecco's DMEM (Gibco 11-885-092) containing 10% FBS and 1% Anti-Anti. Human lung carcinoma cells (A549) expressing human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (NR-53522 from BEI Resources, NIAID, NIH) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% Anti-Anti.

Wild-type HCoV-229E (NR-52726 from BEI Resources, NIAID, NIH) was used for all infections. MRC5 cells were used to generate passage 1 (p1) viral stocks, while HuH7 cells were used to produce passage 2 (p2) working stocks and to quantify viral titers. Viral titers were determined by standard plaque assays [28]. Briefly, cells (4.5 × 105 per well) were seeded in 6-well dishes (Corning, Cat# 08-757-214) and grown overnight. Viral samples were serially diluted, plated, and overlaid with 0.8% agarose in medium. After 72 h, 1× neutral red dye was added to visualize plaques for counting. To enhance infection, serum concentrations in the culture medium were reduced to 4% prior to viral exposure.

Sample collection for proteomics

A549 and MRC5 cells were plated and the following day either mock-infected (sterile 1X phosphate buffer saline PBS) or infected with HCoV-229E (multiplicity of infection MOI 3, or 3 infectious virus particles per cell). After 1 h the inoculum was removed and the cells were washed twice with 1X PBS and fresh infection medium (4% fetal bovine serum) was added. Cultures were incubated at 34 °C at 5% CO2 for 8, 16, or 24 h prior to harvest. At 4 h post infection and at each harvest timepoint, 100 μL of media from each infected well was collected to determine viral replication kinetics. To collect samples for PTM analysis, media was removed from each well and the cells washed once with 1X PBS. A second wash in 1X PBS containing 100 mM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) was also performed for 4 of the 5 replicates for each condition. One replicate per condition was washed twice with 1X PBS to capture the total thiol sample. Plates were then flash frozen on dry ice and stored at −80 °C prior to downstream processing.

Protein extraction, digestion, and isobaric labeling

Plates containing infected or mock MRC5 or A549 cells were placed on ice and 200 μL of lysis buffer containing 250 mM MES (pH 6.0), 5% SDS, and 1% Triton X-100 (with or without 100 mM NEM) was added to each well immediately. Cells were scraped off the plate and incubated in the dark for 30 min. DNA was sheered using a probe sonicator (Fisher Scientific Series 60 Sonic Dismembrator) for 15 s × 3 cycles with 20 s ice incubation between each cycle. Cell debris was removed after centrifugation at 13,000 g and 4 °C for 10 min, followed by incubation in a ThermoMixer (Eppendorf) at 55 °C, 850 rpm for 30 min.

Automated sample cleanup and protein digestion was performed as previously described [18,29]. Briefly, 600 μg of protein from each sample were loaded into deep-well plates, and the sample volume was adjusted to 500 μL with lysis buffer. SP3 beads (of a weight ratio of protein: bead = 1:5) were washed and transferred into a bead plate, and 500 μL of absolute ethanol was added to a sample/binding plate. Three wash plates containing 1 mL of 80% ethanol per well were also prepared. The comb, bead, sample/binding, and wash plates were loaded onto KingFisher Flex (Thermo Scientific). The R2-P1 program was started to transfer beads to the sample/binding plate, followed by incubation (for binding) and washing. The digestion plate was prepared by adding 500 μL of digestion cocktail per well (50 mM HEPES buffer containing 1:100 trypsin and 1:100 LysC, pH 7.7) and loaded onto KingFisher after washing was done. The protein-bound beads were transferred to the digestion plate, and digestion was conducted offline on a ThermoMixer at 37 °C, 850 rpm for 3 h. The digestion plate was placed on a MagnaBot FLEX 96 magnetic plate (Promega) for 2 min, and the supernatant (i.e., the 1st elution) was collected. The beads were washed by adding 500 μL of the 2nd elution buffer (250 mM HEPES with 5% ACN, pH 7.7) to the plate and shaking at 850 rpm for 5 min. The 2nd elution was collected in the same fashion and combined with the first elution. Cleaned peptide samples were labeled with TMTpro 18-plex (Thermo Scientific) at TMT:peptide ratio of 2.5:1 (w/w) by incubation at 850 rpm, room temperature for 1 h, and quenched by 5% hydroxylamine. For each cell type, two TMT18 plexes were used with the detailed labeling schemes in Supplementary Data 1 (A549) and Supplementary Data 2 (MRC5). TMT-labeled samples were desalted with C18 SPE columns (Waters Sep-Pak, 50 mg) and split into fractions for global and PTM (i.e., redox, phosphorylation and acetylation) enrichment.

Enrichment for multi-PTM omics

Peptide-level resin-assisted capture (RAC) of cysteine-containing peptides was performed as previously described [30,31]. Briefly, 200 μg of cleaned TMT-labeled peptide samples were reduced with 5 mM DTT then the sample volume was adjusted to 120 μL to dilute DTT to less than 1 mM. Each sample was incubated with 30 mg of pre-washed and pre-conditioned thiol-affinity resin for 2 h, followed by extensive washing to remove unbound peptides and elution by 20 mM DTT. Samples were desalted by C18 SPE Microspin columns (Nest Group Inc) and fractionated by high-pH RPLC before LC-MS/MS.

Enrichment of phosphopeptides was performed using Immobilized Metal Affinity Chromatography (IMAC) as described by Cuesta et al. [32]. Briefly, an aliquot of the TMT-labeled peptides (around 2 mg) was fractionated into 12 fractions by high-pH RPLC, and each fraction was subjected to automated IMAC enrichment on an Agilent Bravo system according to Abelin et al. [33]. No fractionation was conducted for MRC5 samples due to limited protein recovery (around 300 μg of TMT-labeled peptides after cleanup).

The flowthroughs from IMAC enrichment were collected (and concatenated to 6 fractions for A549 samples, no fractionation was done for MRC5 samples) for acetylation enrichment using PTMScan HS Acetyl-Lysine Motif [Ac–K] kit (Cell Signaling Technologies #46784) on KingFisher as previously described [18,33]. Briefly, peptides were reconstituted in 1 × immunoaffinity precipitation (IAP) bind buffer (Cell Signaling Technology, #25144) containing 0.01% CHAPS and transferred to pre-washed immunoaffinity magnetic beads, followed by incubation for 3 h at 4 °C. Then beads were washed four times with 1× HS IAP wash buffer (Cell Signaling Technology, #42424) containing 0.01% CHAPS and once with ultrapure H2O with 0.01% CHAPS to remove unbound peptides. Acetylpeptides were eluted by 0.15% TFA and desalted using C18 stage tips prior to LC-MS/MS analyses.

LC–MS/MS

Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was performed on a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) as described before [18]. Briefly, chromatographic separation was performed on a custom-packed reverse-phase analytical column (25 cm × 75 μm inner diameter) containing 1.7 μm BEH C18 particles (Waters) using an 120 min gradient. The mobile phase A was water with 0.1% formic acid (FA), and mobile phase B was acetonitrile (ACN) with 0.1% FA. For MS data acquisition, the data-dependent acquisition method was used with full MS scans of m/z range of 400–1800 at a resolution of 70,000. The automatic gain control (AGC) target was set to 3 × 106 and the maximum injection time was set to 20 ms. The top 20 most intense precursor ions from each full scan were selected with an isolation window of 1.5 m/z and fragmented using higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD; normalized collision energy of 30%). MS/MS scans were collected at a resolution of 17,500 with an AGC target of 1 × 105 and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. The first mass was fixed at 110 m/z to enable detection of TMT reporter ions, and a dynamic exclusion window of 30 s was applied to minimize repeated sequencing of precursor ions.

Data analysis and statistics

Raw MS data were analyzed using MS-GF + [34] against the human UniProt protein database (UniProt release 2023-03). Search parameters included a precursor ion tolerance of 20 ppm and partial tryptic digestion with up to two missed cleavages. N-terminal and lysine labeling (+304.2071 Da) were set as fixed modifications. For global proteomics, variable modifications considered were methionine oxidation (+15.9949 Da) and cysteine carbamidomethylation by N-ethylmaleimide (NEM, +125.0477 Da). For redox proteomics, variable modifications were methionine oxidation, cysteine carbamidomethylation, and cysteine alkylation. For phosphoproteomics, phosphorylation of serine, threonine, and tyrosine residues (+79.9663 Da) was set as a variable modification. For acetylation, a variable modification on lysine residues was set as −262.1966 Da, accounting for the calculated mass difference between a fixed TMT label and an acetyl group. The obtained peptide spectrum matches were filtered by precursor mass accuracy (within 10 ppm) and peptide-level q-value (<0.01) to a final peptide false discovery rate (FDR) below 1%. Reporter ion intensities from TMT18 were extracted using MASIC [35].

Linkage of the MS-GF + peptide identification and reporter ion quantification was performed using the R package PlexedPiper (https://github.com/PNNL-Comp-Mass-Spec/PlexedPiper). For global proteomics, reporter ion intensities were summed at the protein level, log2-transformed, batch-corrected between the two TMT18 plexes for each of the two cell types, and median-centered. For PTMomics, reporter intensities were aggregated at the PTM site level, normalized against global proteomics abundance to adjust for channel loading. The resulting data were further batch-corrected, and the site-level PTM abundances of each protein were finally normalized by the global abundance of the same protein. Statistical comparisons between infected and mock conditions at each time point were performed using a Student's t-test. A minimum of two valid quantification values per condition was required for inclusion. Missing values arose from proteins or peptides that were quantified in only one of the two TMT plexes. Statistical significance was defined by a p-value cutoff of 0.01 and an absolute log2 fold change (FC) greater than 0.5.

Bioinformatics

Kinase activities were predicted using the Kinase Library Fisher Enrichment Analysis tool based on the substrate specificities of the human serine/threonine kinome [36]. Statistically significant phosphorylation sites (p-value <0.01, log2FC > 0.5 or < – 0.5) between infected and mock samples at 8, 16 and 24 h post infection (hpi) were used as input for enrichment. The enrichment threshold was set to 15. Significantly enriched kinases (Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value <0.1) in at least one of the three time points were presented in a heatmap. Top ranked kinases (by adjusted p-value) were presented in Fig. 4. Gene ontology enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler [37]. For structural analyses, AlphaFold2-predicted structures were visualized in ChimeraX. The distance among residues of interest were calculated as stated previously [18].

Impact of Alvespimycin on cell viability after infected with HCoV-229E

Alvespimycin efficacy (20 mM stock and tested concentrations ranging from 0.0062 nM to 41 nM) for HCoV-229E was tested at 48- and 72 h post infection of immortalized human lung cells (MRC5 fibroblasts). Alvespimycin was added to the MRC5 culture at the time of infection. In parallel, uninfected cells were assayed for cytotoxicity following drug treatment alone (cell viability assay). As a surrogate for virus replication, virus induced cell death was measured in the presence or absence of serially diluted drug (MOI of 0.125). All wells were assayed using Promega's CellTiter-Glo luminescent cell viability assay according to manufacturer's instructions at the times post infection/treatment listed above. Numbers of relative light units were determined by using Molecular Device's M5 Microplate Spectrophotometer using SoftMax Pro GxP software. Results were graphed as percent infection induced cytopathic effect and cytotoxicity calculated by normalization of the data to 0% inhibition (wells treated with only DMSO (drug solvent) and infected, least cell viability) and 100% inhibition (wells treated with DMSO and UV inactivated virus, highest cell viability). 100 x (1-100% inhibition – experimental)/(100% inhibition – 0% inhibition)) as determined by GraphPad Prism.

Molecular docking of Alvespimycin to HSP90

The cryo-EM structure of HSP90 dimer (PDB ID: 8QMO) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank and prepared by removing non-standard residues and water molecules. Using AutoDockTools, hydrogen atoms were added and the structure was converted to PDBQT format. Alvespimycin (17-DMAG) was used as the ligand, with its three-dimensional structure generated from SMILES representation using OpenBabel. Conformational optimization was performed using the MMFF94 force field through a series of energy minimization steps: conjugate gradient minimization (100 steps, 1.0e-3 convergence), fast and weighted rotor searches (100 cycles, 25 operations each), and final optimization (250 steps, 1.0e-4 convergence). The ligand was prepared for docking through PDBQT conversion using AutoDockTools.

A blind docking approach was employed using AutoDock Vina with a high exhaustiveness of 32 to thoroughly sample the conformational space without prior knowledge of the binding location. The grid box was automatically determined by calculating the bounding box of the entire receptor with an additional 10.0 Å padding in each dimension, centered at the geometric center of the receptor. Nine binding poses were generated and ranked according to their predicted binding affinities, with the best pose showing a binding affinity of −6.438 kcal/mol.

Results

Multi-PTM omics analyses reveal regulations at protein and multi-PTM levels in two cell types following HCoV-229E infection

To better understand how HCoV-229E activates and/or disrupts host signaling pathways, we performed a multi-PTM omics analysis encompassing the proteome, redox proteome, phosphoproteome, and acetylome in two human lung-derived cellular models for respiratory virus infections: MRC5 (fibroblast) and A549 (epithelial) cell lines (Fig. 1A). For each cell line, HCoV-229E infected and mock-infected cells were collected at 8, 16, and 24 hpi for multi-PTM omics analysis and in parallel samples were collected to determine levels of HCoV-229E replication. Minimal changes in replication-competent virus levels were observed at 4 and 8 hpi in both A549 and MRC5 infected cells (Fig. S1), while the highest replication levels were observed at 16 and 24 hpi; however, HCoV-229E titers were approximately 2 logs higher in MRC5 cells than in A549 cells (Fig. S1), consistent with our previous study [28].Using our multi-PTM pipeline [18], we generated comprehensive time-course datasets in A549 (Supplementary Data 1) and MRC5 cells (Supplementary Data 2). In A549 cells, our dataset identified 7,661 proteins, 35,031 cysteine sites, 32,101 phosphorylation sites (S/T/Y), and 7,760 acetylation sites (Fig. 1B). MRC5 cells showed similar in-depth coverage of the proteome (5,819 proteins) and redox proteome (29,435 cysteine sites), along with 8,409 phosphorylation sites and 4,387 acetylation sites. Notably, more than 70% of the identified proteins and PTM sites were quantified across all samples (Supplementary Data 1 and 2), using two TMT plexes for each cell type.

PCA plots depicting sample grouping by treatment (mock vs. infection) and time point. Results from global protein abundance are shown; PCA results for PTMomics are in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to visualize global and PTM differences across samples. In A549 cells, the global proteome clearly separated early time points (8 hpi) from later stages (16 and 24 hpi) (Fig. 1C), with resolution between mock and virus-infected samples at each time point. The PTM datasets for A549 cells exhibited more complex grouping patterns, with only the phosphoproteome data showed clear separation between mock and HCoV-229E-infected samples (Fig. S2). In MRC5 cells, PCA of the global proteome revealed separation primarily by treatment (HCoV vs. mock) along PC1 and by time along PC2 (Fig. 1C), while clear separation between mock and virus-infected samples observed across all three PTM datasets (Fig. S2). The divergence observed in the global and PTM datasets between the two cell types likely reflects distinct cell-type-specific responses to infection, particularly with regard to PTM regulation.

HCoV-229E infection causes minimal change in protein expression

To investigate the impact of HCoV-229E infection on host cells, we first conducted differential analysis of global protein expression by comparing infected cells to time-matched mock controls. Because the integration of two TMT plexes resulted in the missing quantification of some proteins, we required at least two valid quantification values for the comparison. In A549 cells, this analysis identified a limited number of differentially expressed proteins (DEPs)—five, five, and six proteins at 8, 16, and 24 h post infection, respectively. (Fig. 2A, left three panels; Supplementary Data 3). In contrast, MRC5 cells exhibited a higher number of DEPs at the same time points: 28, 34, and 139, respectively (Fig. 2A, right three panels; Supplementary Data 3). These results suggested that HCoV-229E infection induces minimal perturbation in protein expression within the first 24 h, particularly in A549 cells. This was consistent with our previous structural proteomics study, where HCoV-229E caused more widespread changes in protein structure than in global protein abundance [28]. Notably, the larger number of abundance changes reported in the previous study likely reflected differences in workflow and statistics (label-free quantification and a less stringent significance cutoff), whereas here we used TMT-based quantification (with known ratio compression) and applied a more stringent threshold (p < 0.01 and FC > 1.5) to focus on the most robust abundance changes.

Nevertheless, a significant proportion of differentially regulated proteins were shared between the two cell types (Fig. 2B, upper panels). Moreover, the magnitude of change in overlapping proteins, expressed as log2(fold change), showed a strong positive correlation at 8 and 16 hpi, indicating common regulatory mechanisms in both cell types (Fig. 2B, lower panels). At 24 hpi, 121 proteins exhibited significantly decreased abundance in HCoV-229E–infected MRC5 cells, while the number of DEPs in A549 cells remained similar to earlier time points. KEGG enrichment analysis of DEPs revealed shared pathways in both cell types, including complement and coagulation cascades, platelet activation, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, and Coronavirus disease–COVID-19 (Fig. 2C; full list in Supplementary Data 3). Other pathways, such as ECM-receptor interaction, PI3K-Akt signaling, and TGF-beta signaling, were enriched only at 24 hpi in MRC5 cells.

HCoV-229E infection regulates cell-type-specific host kinase signaling

In contrast to the limited changes in protein abundance following HCoV-229E infection, phosphoproteomic profiling in both cell types revealed substantial alterations in hundreds of phosphorylation events at each time point (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Data 4). Notably, the number of sites with up-regulated phosphorylation increased over the infection time course (195, 332, and 446 in A549 cells at 8, 16, and 24 hpi, respectively; and 42, 213, and 660 in MRC5 cells at the same time points). However, only a small fraction of significant phosphorylation events was shared between the two cell types at each time point (1, 5, and 20 for 8, 16, and 24 hpi, respectively; Fig. 3B, upper panels), showing reduced correlations between the cell types (Fig. 3B, lower panels).

The number of statistically significant phosphorylation sites were indicated (blue for decrease; red for increase). B. Venn diagrams showing common and unique phosphorylation sites between the two cell types at each time point. Note the reduced overlap between significantly regulated sites. For time points with multiple overlapping events (16 and 24 hpi), correlation plots are provided below the Venn diagrams. C. KEGG enrichment of functional pathways for proteins with differentially regulated phosphorylation events following HCoV-229E infection.

This suggests that phosphorylation plays a major role in the host response to HCoV-229E infection and that the two cell types employ distinct phosphorylation dynamics during infection. Indeed, KEGG enrichment analysis of proteins with significantly altered phosphorylation revealed divergent patterns. In HCoV-229E infected A549 cells, numerous pathways were enriched across all three time points, including Rap1 signaling, tight junctions, regulation of the actin cytoskeleton, and endocytosis (Fig. 3C; full list in Supplementary Data 4). These pathways mediate functions related to epithelial barrier integrity, viral entry, and trafficking as early responses to infection, consistent with our observation that A549 cells are more resistant to HCoV-229E infection than MRC5 cells [28]. In HCoV-229E–infected MRC5 cells, the regulated phosphorylation sites showed distinct pathway enrichment. At 8 hpi, tight junction pathways were enriched, similar to A549 cells; however, at later time points, spliceosome components and nucleocytoplasmic transport pathways were prominently affected (Fig. 3C), suggesting that host splicing machinery and mRNA transport were disrupted by viral infection, as previously reported in both HCoV-229E [28] and SARS-CoV-2 infections [38].

To investigate host signaling responses during HCoV-229E infection, we predicted kinase activities from significantly regulated phosphorylation events (adjusted p < 0.01, log2FC > 0.5 or < −0.5) in infected A549 and MRC5 cells using known kinase–substrate relationships (Fig. S4 and Supplementary Data 5 for all 311 predicted kinases; Fig. 4A for top-ranked kinases by adjusted p values). In A549 cells, nearly all top-ranked kinases were predicted to be strongly inhibited throughout the HCoV-229E infection time course, highlighting broad suppression of kinase signaling (Fig. 4A). Members of the MAPK pathway were widely predicted to be inhibited, including MAP3Ks (e.g., MAP3K7, MAP3K1, MAP3K20) and MAP4Ks (e.g., MAP4K3, MAP4K4), indicating deactivated p38/JNK stress axis and metabolic stress signaling upon viral infection in A549 cells (Fig. 4A–B). Indeed, the downstream kinases involved in energy metabolism and autophagy, such as AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) catalytic subunits alpha 1 and 2 (PRKAA1/2) and unc-51-like autophagy activating kinases (e.g., ULK1), were predicted to be inhibited following HCoV-229E infection in A549 cells. The inhibition of AMPKs was strongly supported by the decreased phosphorylation levels at the active sites, AMPKα1 (PRKAA1)-T183 and AMPKα2 (PRKAA2)-T172, especially at later timepoints (Fig. 4B). Additionally, predicted down-regulated kinases were involved in cell cycle control (e.g., checkpoint kinase CHEK2, serine/threonine-protein kinase NEK11) and innate immunity signaling (e.g., interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase IRAK1, receptor-interacting serine/threonine kinase RIPK2), suggesting global suppression of stress and inflammatory signaling and dampened host defense, consistent with a recent report that HCoV-229E blocked interferon signaling to evade host antiviral response in A549 cells [39]. Only leucine-rich repeat serine/threonine-protein kinase 2 (LRRK2) was predicted to remain consistently activated across all time points following HCoV-229E infection of A549 cells (Fig. 4A).

In contrast, HCoV-229E-infected MRC5 cells displayed a more dynamic kinase response. Over half of the kinases with significant activity changes were predicted to be inhibited at early times post-infection (8 hpi), whereas a larger subset were predicted to be activated by 24 hpi (Fig. 4A and C). By 24 hpi, several kinases associated with MAPK and p38 signaling pathways were predicted to be activated, including protein kinase C isoforms (PRKCB, PRKCG), MAP3K14, and MAPK-activated protein kinases 2 and 3 (MAPKAPK2, MAPKAPK3). Serine/threonine-protein kinase D3 (PRKD3) was also predicted to be activated in HCoV-229E-infected MRC5 cells, consistent with previous reports that PRKD3 activity is essential for HCoV-229E replication in MRC5 cells [40]. Notably, interferon-induced, double-stranded RNA-activated protein kinase (EIF2AK2), a key regulator of the innate antiviral response [41], was predicted to be strongly inhibited across all timepoints, consistent with prior findings that CoV nonstructural protein 15 (nsp15) can suppress EIF2AK2 activation [42,43].

These observations indicate that HCoV-229E engages host kinase signaling in a highly cell type–specific manner, with broad kinase suppression in A549 cells and a temporally dynamic activation–inhibition pattern in MRC5 cells. Such patterns were further illustrated by examining phosphorylation levels of known kinase substrates, which showed decreased substrate phosphorylation for MAP3K7 and PRKAA1 in HCoV-229E infected A549 cells (Fig. 4B). In HCoV-229E infected MRC5 cells, phosphorylation levels of multiple substrates of MAP2K (previously reported in Ref. [4]), AKT1, and PIM1 increased at later time points during infection (Fig. 4C).

Distinct redox regulation patterns between A549 and MRC5 cells upon HCoV-229E infection

Comparable coverage of the redox proteome in HCoV-229E infected A549 and MRC5 cells was observed. However, differential analysis revealed distinct redox regulation patterns between the two cell types. At all three time points, HCoV-229E-infected A549 cells exhibited a greater number of cysteine sites with increased oxidation compared to those with decreased oxidation (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Data 6). In contrast, HCoV-229E infected MRC5 cells at 8 hpi had similar numbers of cysteine sites with increased and decreased oxidation, whereas at 16 and 24 hpi, more than twice as many sites displayed reduced oxidation compared to increased oxidation. These observations suggest that A549 and MRC5 cells utilize distinct redox regulatory mechanisms in response to HCoV-229E infection. Indeed, only a small fraction of significantly changed cysteine sites were shared between the two cell types at any post-infection time point (Fig. 5B, upper panels), showing limited to no correlation (Fig. 5B, lower panels). Enrichment analysis further confirmed these distinct regulation patterns, identifying a broader set of pathways in HCoV-229E-infected MRC5 cells. HCoV-229E–infected A549 cells had redox regulated proteins in functional categories including ribosome, coronavirus disease, and carbon metabolism, whereas HCoV-229E–infected MRC5 cells showed proteins with altered redox states functioning in pathways such as phosphoinositide 3-kinase-protein kinase B (PI3K–Akt) signaling, phagosome, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, focal adhesion, and proteoglycans in cancer (Fig. 5C; full list in Supplementary Data 6).

To further illustrate the dynamics of redox regulation, we selected several pathways that are known to be regulated by redox regulation as well as being altered in our dataset during HCoV-229E infection in MRC5 cells. Among these, the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway is a key regulator of cell survival and metabolism that is frequently hijacked by viruses to promote entry, replication, and persistence by suppressing apoptosis [44]. In this pathway, we observed dynamic oxidation changes on multiple cysteine residues of key proteins. For instance, vascular endothelial growth factor C (VEGFC), with C137 located at the dimer interface and near the disulfide bond between C156 and C165, showed increased oxidation at 8 hpi followed by a decrease at 16 and 24 hpi (Fig. 6A). Similarly, integrin β1 (ITB1) and Gβ1/Gβ2 subunits of heterotrimeric G proteins exhibited significant oxidation at all three time points compared to controls (Fig. 6B and C). Together, these data illustrate coordinated regulation of the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway during HCoV-229E infection, encompassing upstream receptor tyrosine kinases, intermediate signal modulators, and integrin- and adhesion-related components, with distinct cysteine sites across these pathway tiers exhibiting shared dynamic redox behaviors in MRC5 cells.

Acetylation dynamics in A549 and MRC5 cells following HCoV-229E infection

Differential analysis of acetylation was also performed by comparing HCoV-229E-infected to mock-infected A549 and MRC5 cells. In A549 cells, HCoV-229E infection induced moderate acetylation changes, with 99 (8 hpi), 128 (16 hpi), and 119 (24 hpi) statistically significant sites detected (Fig. S6A; Supplementary Data 7). In contrast, HCoV-229E infected MRC5 cells exhibited 136 (8 hpi), 780 (16 hpi), and 299 (24 hpi) significant acetylation sites. While A549 cells showed roughly balanced numbers of up- and down-regulated acetylation sites, MRC5 cells displayed a pronounced shift toward increased acetylation, suggesting either a global upregulation of lysine acetyltransferase activity or inhibition of deacetylases upon HCoV-229E infection (Fig. S6B).

Despite these cell-type-specific differences, a subset of acetylation sites was consistently regulated in both cell types, with 2 (8 hpi), 19 (16 hpi), and 6 (24 hpi) shared significant sites following HCoV-229E infection (Fig. S6B). These shared acetylation sites showed moderate to high concordance between cell types (r = 0.50 and 0.82 at 16 and 24 hpi, respectively). Notably, the most highly concordant acetylation changes at 24 hpi included mitochondrial metabolic enzymes (ODP2K362; log2FC = 1.5 in both cell types), a cytoskeletal regulator (TPM2K48; log2FC = 0.7 and 1.1 in A549 and MRC5 cells, respectively), and the transcriptional co-activator and acetyltransferase CBP (K1587 and K1592; log2FC = −0.8 and −0.6). These conserved acetylation changes suggested HCoV-229E-induced remodeling of mitochondrial metabolism and cytoskeletal and translational reprogramming. Consistent with this interpretation, enrichment analysis highlighted pathways related to mitochondrial metabolism, glycolysis, HIF-1 signaling, motor proteins, and carbon metabolism (Fig. S6C; full list in Supplementary Data 7).

Multi-PTM crosstalk analysis identifies HSP90B as a host factor critical for HCoV-229E replication

The concurrent measurement of multiple PTM types on the same samples enabled us to capture the full spectrum of regulatory mechanisms and investigate potential crosstalk among distinct PTM sites. Focusing on proteins exhibiting significant HCoV-229E-induced perturbations across all three PTM types, we identified four such proteins in A549 cells and 30 in MRC5 cells (Fig. S7A–B; Supplemental Data 8). To assess whether alterations in one modification were accompanied by changes in other PTM events on the same protein following HCoV-229E infection, we performed correlation analyses among all significantly regulated PTM sites in MRC5 cells. The resulting Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were strongly biased toward 1.0 or −1.0, indicating robust positive or negative associations among the perturbed PTM sites (Fig. S7C).

Concurrent HCoV-229E–induced changes across multiple PTM types within the same protein suggest these proteins are key host factors in host–virus interactions and potential targets for host-directed antiviral therapeutics. To test this, we focused on heat shock protein 90-beta (HSP90B, encoded by HSP90AB1), an important host factor for viral infection [45] and an attractive target for drug development with the availability of inhibitors [46]. HSP90B displayed dynamic changes in cysteine oxidation, phosphorylation, and acetylation, but not in protein abundance, upon HCoV-229E infection in MRC5 cells (Fig. 7A). Structural mapping revealed that several PTM sites were located in close proximity, including K53 and K64, as well as S226, S261, and K273 (Fig. 7B and C), suggesting a potential “PTM code,” wherein the combination, order, and spatial arrangement of multiple PTMs collectively may dictate protein function.

HSP90B is a molecular chaperone central to protein folding and is frequently hijacked by viruses to ensure proper folding and stability of viral proteins, promoting replication and assembly [47]. To assess its role in infection, we treated MRC5 cells with Alvespimycin, an HSP90B inhibitor, during HCoV-229E infection. Because virus-induced cytopathic effect (CPE; cell death) was used as the primary readout of productive infection, and HCoV-229E induced robust CPE in MRC5 cells but not in A549 cells (Fig. S1), we performed these experiments in MRC5 cells. At 48 hpi, limited cytotoxicity of Alvespimycin was observed at the highest dose (41 nM) in uninfected cells (Fig. 7D), while the highest three doses (4.6–41 nM) reduced HCoV-229E–induced cytopathic effects (Fig. 7E). Similar observations were found at 72 hpi (Fig. S8). The finding that inhibition of HSP90B resulted in a dose-dependent reduction of virus-induced CPE supports a role for HSP90B in HCoV-229E replication. To explore whether PTM regulation contributes to HSP90B inhibition by Alvespimycin, we performed molecular docking analysis. In the top-ranked pose, the HSP90B–Alvespimycin interface included K435, a residue showing acetylation upregulation upon HCoV-229E infection (Fig. 7C). In the second pose, C366, a residue with infection-induced oxidation changes, was located within the inhibitor-binding interface (Fig. S9). Glutathionylation of C366 of HSP90 was previously identified [48]. These results suggest that Alvespimycin binding may interfere with accessibility of PTM-modifying enzymes, such as acetyltransferases or thioredoxin/oxidants, to critical PTM sites, thereby disrupting HSP90B function and impairing HCoV-229E replication.

Viral proteome and dynamics of PTM

Searching the datasets against the HCoV-229E proteome allowed us to quantify both the viral proteome and PTMomes during the infection. In total, 11 viral proteins were quantified, with 99 unique PTM sites detected in HCoV-229E–infected MRC5 cells, and 4 viral proteins with 28 unique PTM sites detected in A549 cells (Supplementary Data 1 and 2). Most viral proteins increased in abundance between 8 and 24 hpi (9 out of 11 in MRC5, 3 out of 4 in A549; Fig. 8A). These included nucleocapsid (N), spike glycoprotein (S), membrane protein (M), open reading frame 4a (Orf4a), and several non-structural replicase proteins, consistent with the viral titration data over the infection time course (Fig. S1).

HCoV-229E N protein exhibited detectable phosphorylation during infection in both cell types at residues S145, S153, S214, and S218 (Fig. 8C–D) but with cell type specific modification kinetics. Coronavirus N protein is known to be actively phosphorylated by host kinases such as glycogen synthase kinase-3 (GSK-3) and serine/arginine-rich protein kinases 1 and 2 (SRPK1/2) in the SR-rich region, which is highly conserved among alphacoronaviruses including HCoV-229E [18,[40], [41], [42], [43]]. Previous studies have shown that inhibition or knockout of GSK-3 blocks N phosphorylation and impairs SARS-CoV-2 replication in lung epithelial cell lines [13]. In our dataset, N–S145 and N–S153, located in the SR-rich region, exhibited significantly altered phosphorylation levels in HCoV-229E–infected A549 cells at 16 and 24 hpi compared to 8 hpi, suggesting potential regulation via these sites [49]. Correspondingly, both GSK3A and GSK3B appeared more active in A549 across all time points post-infection, inferred from decreased phosphorylation at the key inhibitory sites GSK3A-S21 and GSK3B–S9 compared to mock conditions (Supplementary Data 4). In contrast, in HCoV-229E infected MRC5 cells, phosphorylation of N–S145 and N–S153 decreased over time, while GSK3A-S21 phosphorylation increased at 16 and 24 hpi (Supplementary Data 4, Fig. 4B). These observations suggest cell-type–specific differences in the infection dynamics or underlying mechanisms, with HCoV-229E N likely acting as a substrate of GSK-3, although further experimental validation is required.

Discussion

Protein abundance signatures of HCoV-229E infection

This study was designed to provide molecular insights into host–virus interactions by quantifying dynamic changes in the proteome and PTM-omes following HCoV-229E infection. At the protein abundance level, only a handful of proteins showed an increase in response to infection. The fact that most of these proteins showed similar trends in both A549 and MRC5 cells suggested that they are involved in common cellular response mechanisms to infection. One example is C–C motif chemokine 20 (CCL20) (Fig. S3). Chemokines increase in response to respiratory virus infections and often correlate with disease severity [50]; for example, CCL20 is elevated in respiratory syncytial virus and influenza infections, as well as in SARS-CoV-2 infected COVID-19 patients [[51], [52], [53]]. Molecularly, CCL20 expression attracts CC-chemokine receptor 6 (CCR6)-expressing immune cells to the site of infection to control viral replication and also contributes to hyperinflammation. Blocking CCL20 has been shown to significantly reduce lung pathology through attenuated recruitment of conventional dendritic cells and decreased inflammation [52]. Fibrinogen subunits alpha, beta, and gamma (FGA, FGB, and FGG) also showed significantly increased abundance upon HCoV-229E infection in both A549 and MRC5 cells (Fig. S3), consistent with observations in SARS-CoV-2 infection [54,55]. Furthermore, fibrinogen is known to stimulate chemokine secretion and immune cell recruitment [56,57], supporting the notion of common inflammation and immune responses upon HCoV-229E infection in different cell types. Interestingly, an elevated level of apolipoprotein A-II (APOA2) was observed only in HCoV-229E infected MRC5 cells, which are more susceptible to the infection. APOA2 may be involved in regulating immune responses, high-density lipoprotein remodeling, and viral entry [58], although the mechanism underlying APOA2 upregulation remains unclear.

Differences in phosphorylation signaling between HCoV-229E infected A549 and MRC5 cells

In contrast to the limited protein abundance changes, our data revealed extensive and site-specific PTM remodeling in HCoV-229E–infected A549 and MRC5 cells. The distinct PTM dynamics between the two cell types coincided with their markedly different viral growth kinetics (Fig. S1) and likely reflect differences in baseline cellular state, cell signaling, metabolic demands, and stress response between the cancer cell line and the immortalized human lung fibroblasts [59].

In HCoV-229E infected A549, a notable infection-induced phosphorylation change is the time-dependent increase of TP53BP1–S1317 (Fig. 4B), which is associated with the activation of DNA damage signaling and G1/S checkpoint control [[60], [61], [62]]. An increase in CHK1–S296 phosphorylation (Supplementary Data S1) further supports activation of the ATR–CHK1 checkpoint axis and cell-cycle arrest [63]. In parallel, AKT-associated signaling was attenuated in HCoV-229E infected A549, as indicated by decreased phosphorylation of the canonical AKT target FOXO3-T32 (Supplementary Data S1), leading to increased FOXO3 activity and induction of stress-response [64]. Thus, the observed stress signaling, DNA damage response and cell-cycle regulation indicate a “repair/arrest” response in A549 cells. Indeed, cell cycle arrest has been observed in SARS-CoV-2 infected A549 [4], and a similar stress/checkpoint regulation has been reported in A549 cells exposed to drugs or environmental stressors [65]. Such a “repair/arrest” response may contribute to reduced permissiveness to HCoV-229E.

In contrast, HCoV-229E infected MRC5 cells displayed increased phosphorylation of AKT1-S129 and activation of the AKT–mTOR axis (Fig. 4B). These changes are consistent with induction of AKT–mTORC1–driven translation, which may increase cellular protein-synthesis capacity and thereby support viral protein production. Notably, AKT activation has been linked to permissiveness in myxoma virus infection, where inhibition of AKT restricts infection in permissive lines and constitutively active AKT promotes infection in nonpermissive cells [66]. Furthermore, the direction of AKT-mTOR regulation appears context-dependent: activation was reported in SARS-CoV-2-infected Vero cells, human derived lung air-liquid interface cultures and COVID-19 patient lung tissues [67], while deactivation was inferred from the phosphoproteome of SARS-CoV-2-infected Vero cells [4], potentially due to difference in MOI, infection stages, or sampling time.

Correlated with AKT1 activation in MRC5, an increase of GSK3A-S21 phosphorylation was found in only HCoV-229E infected MRC5 (Fig. 4C). In the context of viral infection, GSK3 plays a pro-viral role in many systems [68]. GSK3 activation was required for SARS-CoV-2 N phosphorylation and viral replication [13] and GSK3 inhibitors were effective in reducing SARS-CoV-2 and HCoV-229E infection in Huh-7.5.1 cells [69]. Consistent with these, our data in MRC5 cells showed a significant increase in multiple phosphorylation sites in the SR-enriched region of the N protein, which could be potential GSK3 substrates (Fig. 8D).

Redox regulation diverges between HCoV-229E infected cell types

The opposite overall trends in redox response (i.e., cysteine oxidation) of the two cell types (Fig. 5A) suggests divergent redox homeostasis and regulation during HCoV-229E infection. A549, as a cancer-derived epithelial line, typically maintains higher ROS due to increased metabolic demands and can be more sensitive to oxidative stress [70,71]. Consistently, A549 has been reported to have higher basal mitochondrial ROS than MRC5 [72]. In addition, they differ in antioxidant capacity. MRC5 showed higher basal total GSH and GSH/GSSG and stronger induction of antioxidant systems upon perturbation compared to A549 [73]. Consistent with this, we observed extensive redox changes in HCoV-229E infected MRC5 on mitochondrial redox/antioxidant proteins, including peroxiredoxin-5 (PRDX5-C100 and C204) and glutathione reductase (GSR-C278, C377, K354), while only limited changes were detected in A549 (Supplementary Data S1, 2). Furthermore, antioxidant proteins (SOD1, CAT) and glutathione-dependent enzymes, including functional cysteines on peroxiredoxin-6 (PRDX6-C47) and alcohol dehydrogenase class-3 (ADH5-C45, C97, C100, C103, C174), showed distinct dynamics between the two cell types (Supplementary Data S1, 2). Extending this workflow to additional airway models, including primary systems, will be important for assessing the generalizability of these findings and for delineating conserved responses from those that are cell-type-specific.

Integrated multi-PTM regulation of PI3K–AKT in response to HCoV-229E infection in MRC5 cells

Our multi-PTM omics workflow quantifies coordinated multi-PTM changes within the same signaling pathways, enabling a PTM-centric view of how HCoV-229E rewires host signaling. Because PI3K–AKT was enriched in both the redox and phosphoproteomes datasets and plays a central role in cell survival, metabolism, and immune responses [74,75], we summarized its multi-PTM changes in an integrated model in the productively infected MRC5 cells (Fig. 9). Activation of this pathway is primarily driven by receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), integrins, and G-protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs), which exhibited site-specific oxidation in our dataset. Notably, the C137 of VEGFC (a secreted ligand for VEGFRs), located at the dimer interface, showed increased oxidation at 8 hpi followed by a decrease at 16 and 24 hpi (Fig. 6A). Because oligomerization and receptor activity of VEGFRs are redox-regulated [76], our data suggested that modulation of this key residue's oxidation state could regulate receptor kinase activity following HCoV-229E infection. Integrins, such as integrin β1 (ITGB1), are transmembrane receptors that transmit extracellular signals to intracellular pathways including PI3K–AKT. ITGB1-dependent PI3K–AKT activation was reported to be necessary for vaccinia virus to enter host cells [77]. For SARS-CoV-2, the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of spike protein was found to serve as a αv-integrin agonist which can trigger AKT activation [78]. Upon HCoV-229E-infection, we detected significant oxidation of ITGB1 at all three time points in MRC5 cells (Fig. 6B). Previous studies have described a role for cysteine residues in the β subunit in integrin activation [79], although the specific sites were not mapped. Our results identified putative redox-sensitive cysteines following HCoV-229E infection, providing candidates for further study of thiol switch–mediated redox regulation of integrins. Additionally, a group of extracellular matrix (ECM) components were significantly regulated at redox level including collagen, laminin, fibronectin, etc. (Fig. 9, Supplementary Data S2) which can bind integrins and substantially turn on PI3K-AKT and related signaling pathways. The ECM proteins were found to be heavily remodeled in COVID-19 models [80]. Heterotrimeric G proteins, composed of α, β, and γ subunits, convey signals from GPCRs to downstream effectors such as PI3K. Specifically, Gβ1 and Gβ2 (encoded by GNB1 and GNB2, respectively) directly interact with and activate class I PI3Ks, leading to phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-triphosphate (PIP3) production at the plasma membrane, recruitment of AKT via its Pleckstrin homology (PH) domain, and pathway activation. We observed oxidation of Gβ1 and Gβ2 at all time points post-infection compared to mock-infected controls (Fig. 6C), suggesting another way that HCoV-229E may modulate PI3K–AKT signaling via redox regulation of GPCR-associated proteins in MRC5 cells.

Downstream of these receptors, the PI3K regulatory subunit p85 (PIK3R1) was more reduced at C498 (Fig. 9) in HCoV-229E infected cells. Because GSTP1-mediated S-glutathionylation at C498/C670 inhibits PIK3R1 phosphorylation and dampens AKT–mTOR signaling [81], the reduction of PIK3R1-C498 during HCoV-229E infection is consistent with the relief of a redox-sensitive inhibitory constraint and enhanced AKT1 phosphorylation. Beyond the mTORC1 translation arm, cell-cycle regulators were modulated at multiple levels in HCoV-229E infected MRC5 cells. The CDK inhibitor p21 (an AKT1 substrate) decreased in abundance and showed increased oxidation at C41, a redox switch reported to destabilize p21 and weaken inhibition of CDK2 [82]. Consistent with increased CDK2 activity, we observed decreased inhibitory phosphorylation at CDK2-T14 and reduced oxidation at CDK2-C177 following HCoV-229E infection. T14 phosphorylation inhibits CDK2, whereas C177 redox status has been linked to access of the T-loop phosphatase KAP, which can bind CDK2 and dephosphorylate T160 to modulate full kinase activity [83].

On the other hand, despite AKT1 activation, decreased phosphorylation of FOXO3-S253 at 24 hpi was found, indicating a more active FOXO3, which can subsequently inhibit CCND1 expression and slow cell proliferation [64]. Indeed, our data showed that CCND1 protein abundance significantly decreased at 24 hpi (Fig. 9). Thus, simultaneously quantifying protein abundance and PTMs shows the multi-level regulation of cell cycle that is otherwise inaccessible through single-PTM or abundance-only analyses. Collectively, the multi-PTM tuning of PI3K–AKT represents a coordinated host rewiring in MRC5 cells that is productively infected with HCoV-229E by regulating on protein synthesis, transcriptional reprogramming, and cell cycle control.

Multi-PTM-based identification of HCoV-229E infection regulatory hubs

Beyond providing an atlas of infection-induced PTM changes, our integrated multi-PTM omics framework offers a strategy for identifying key host pathways and network hubs that may serve as host-directed antiviral targets. The widespread remodeling across multiple PTM layers with relatively limited changes in protein abundance highlights PTMs as early regulatory mechanisms during HCoV-229E infection. Capturing coordinated alterations across distinct PTM types enabled prioritization of regulatory hubs, as exemplified by HSP90B. Concordant changes across PTM types and their spatial clustering on the protein structure indicate crosstalk rather than independent regulation. PTM regulation of HSP90 upon SARS-CoV-2 or severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus infection had been reported previously [45,84], highlighting the role of PTM regulation on HSP90B in host–virus interactions. Supporting its therapeutic potential, pharmacological inhibition of HSP90B with Alvespimycin resulted in dose-dependent suppression of HCoV-229E replication, and docking analyses indicated that Alvespimycin binding interfaces overlap with multiple residues undergoing HCoV-299E infection-induced PTM changes. Together, these observations support a combinatorial PTM code [85,86] governing chaperone activity and inhibitor engagement, and suggest that antivirals targeting PTM-regulated host factors may offer key advantages over virus-directed approaches, including reduced susceptibility to viral resistance.

Limitations of this study

Despite the depth and integrative nature of this study, there were several limitations. First, our temporal resolution was restricted to three post-infection time points (8, 16, and 24 hpi). While it captured early-to-intermediate host responses, a denser time course would provide a more comprehensive view of signaling trajectories, including transient or oscillatory PTM events that may occur outside these windows. This sampling reflected practical constraints of large-scale multi-PTM proteomics (sample preparation, LC–MS throughput, and cost), and higher-throughput workflows will be needed for finer-grained temporal profiling. Second, our prioritization of candidate host factors was stringent, focusing on proteins exhibiting significant changes across three PTM types. While it increases confidence, this approach may miss targets primary regulated by a single PTM, such as CK2 by phosphorylation in SARS-CoV-2 infection [4], for which inhibitors had been developed [87]. More flexible computational frameworks (e.g., network-based models, machine learning, or Bayesian inference) are needed to integrate multi-PTM time-course data by weighting PTM types, incorporating prior pathway knowledge, and inferring temporal PTM events to enable systematic yet less restrictive target prioritization. Third, functional verification was limited to a single pharmacological inhibitor targeting one prioritized host protein. Although Alvespimycin provided proof-of-principle, additional verification studies examining multiple targets is necessary. Future studies would benefit from higher-throughput functional screening platforms, such as small-molecule libraries, CRISPR-based perturbations, or targeted PTM-editing strategies, to rapidly assess the relevance to HCoV-229E infection of multiple candidates. For the most promising targets and compounds, more detailed mechanistic studies will be required to define how specific PTM changes regulate protein function, viral replication, and host cell viability. Finally, this study was conducted in two in vitro cell models, which, while enabling controlled mechanistic analysis, may not fully capture the complexity of tissue-level or in vivo responses. Extending multi-PTM profiling to primary cells, organoid systems, or animal models will be important for validating the physiological relevance of these findings.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that integrated multi-PTM omics provided a comprehensive view of host–virus interactions, capturing dynamic changes in protein abundance, cysteine oxidation, phosphorylation, and acetylation that were not apparent from global proteomics alone. By profiling two human lung cell types during HCoV-229E infection, we revealed rapid, cell type–specific remodeling of host PTM landscapes, identified coordinated PTM regulation across key signaling proteins, and uncovered mechanistic insights into how viruses exploit host machinery. Importantly, this approach enabled the functional prioritization of PTM-regulated host factors, exemplified by HSP90B, whose inhibition significantly reduced viral replication. These findings highlight the potential of PTM-centric strategies for discovering host-directed antiviral interventions and provide a roadmap for systematically identifying vulnerabilities in viral infection pathways. Moving forward, applying this workflow to additional viral systems and expanding temporal and PTM coverage will further enhance our ability to link dynamic PTM regulation to functional outcomes, ultimately guiding the development of more effective and broad-spectrum host-targeted therapeutics.
