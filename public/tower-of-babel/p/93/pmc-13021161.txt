A systematic review of shear bond strength of sixth- and fourth-generation adhesives in primary teeth
Authors: Maryam Hajiahmadi, Najmeh Akhlaghi, Hamid Mosleh, Ehsan Samani, Sheida Bagheri, Zohreh Salehi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The use of tooth-colored restorations, particularly composite resins, in the treatment of primary teeth has become increasingly common. The durability and clinical success of these restorations largely depend on the adhesive system and its application protocol. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically review the literature comparing the shear bond strength of sixth-generation self-etch adhesives with that of fourth-generation etch-and-rinse adhesives in primary teeth.Materials and Methods:A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane databases using relevant keywords. Out of 162 initially identified studies, 72 in-vitro studies comparing the shear bond strength of sixth- and fourth-generation adhesives on human primary teeth were screened. After assessing eligibility based on study objectives, population, sample selection, participation rate, available variables, data collection methods, statistical analyses, and results, five studies underwent full-text analysis. Ultimately, four studies met the inclusion criteria for this systematic review.Results:No statistically significant differences were observed in the shear bond strength between the two adhesive systems. However, minor variations were noted. Moreover, none of the included studies evaluated the long-term performance of these adhesives.Conclusion:Despite the high heterogeneity and risk of bias among the included studies, the evidence suggests that fourth-generation adhesives may exhibit slightly better shear bond strength compared to sixth-generation self-etch systems in laboratory settings. Nonetheless, due to reduced application time and technique sensitivity, self-etch systems may be preferable for pediatric patients.

INTRODUCTION

Restorative treatment in primary teeth aims to preserve these teeth until the eruption of their permanent successors. This is justified from functional, psychological, biological, and aesthetic perspectives.[1] With advances in restorative materials and increasing parental demand, greater attention is now given to tooth-colored restorations in children. Among these, composite resins hold a prominent place due to their superior aesthetics and strength.[2]

Given the fundamental role of composite restorations and the emphasis in pediatric dentistry on conservative approaches that preserve healthy tooth structure, the use of adhesives has become particularly important.[3] This is further influenced by the common challenge of limited cooperation among pediatric patients, which may compromise isolation, reduce bond continuity, increase microleakage, and ultimately weaken the restoration.[34]

Composites are bonded to dental tissues through various adhesive systems.[5] Adhesives serve as a thin layer of resin placed between dentin and the composite resin matrix.[6] In general, adhesive systems are designed to create a reliable bond to both enamel and dentin, significantly reducing marginal microleakage, secondary caries, and postoperative sensitivity.[7]

A variety of adhesive systems have been developed across different generations, up to the eighth. In this study, two systems are examined: the sixth-generation self-etch adhesives and the fourth-generation etch-and-rinse adhesives. Etch-and-rinse systems completely remove the smear layer and open the dentinal tubules; however, resin infiltration may sometimes be inadequate in deeper demineralized areas created by acid etching, leading to microleakage over time and adversely affecting bond durability.[8] In contrast, self-etch systems simultaneously achieve demineralization and resin infiltration into enamel prisms and dentinal collagen fibers without the need to remove the smear layer. This makes the clinical procedure simpler and less sensitive to dentin moisture, reducing technique sensitivity.[9]

The etch-and-rinse approach is typically a three-step system that employs phosphoric acid to create microporosities in the hydroxyapatite (HAp) layer and expose a HAp-free collagen network several micrometers deep in the dentin.[10] As the oldest adhesive approach, etch-and-rinse systems remain the gold standard; however, current trends favor simpler, self-etching materials.[1011]

Self-etch adhesives generally involve two steps and comprise hydrophilic and hydrophobic monomers, polymerization initiators, solvents, and other components. They include special monomers with carboxylate or phosphate groups that act both as mild etching agents, enabling surface demineralization, and as primers that facilitate resin penetration into dental tissues. These properties make self-etch adhesives easier to use, with a faster application process and reduced technique sensitivity compared to multi-step etch-and-rinse systems.[10111213]

A major challenge for dental adhesives remains their ability to provide an effective bond across various tooth substrates under different conditions of structural loss (involving either enamel alone or both enamel and dentin).[1314] Numerous studies have demonstrated that bonding to enamel yields a stronger and more durable seal compared to dentin.[15] Despite manufacturers’ continuous efforts to develop new materials, the question persists whether these innovations truly offer improved bond strength.

In assessing the clinical success of adhesive systems, shear bond strength is a critical parameter.[16] It is defined as the resistance of a material to yielding or fracturing under an applied shear force.[17] One of the primary challenges in bonding resin composites to tooth structure is polymerization shrinkage due to free radical reactions, which generates stresses at the adhesive interface. Therefore, adhesives must achieve a high initial bond strength to withstand these contraction stresses as well as subsequent functional, thermal, and chemical forces exerted on the restoration–tooth interface during mastication and over time.[1819] While numerous studies have assessed adhesive systems in permanent teeth, comparable data specific to primary teeth remain scarce. Due to structural differences between primary and permanent teeth, results from adult dentition cannot be directly extrapolated. Therefore, this review addresses this gap by systematically comparing sixth-generation self-etch and fourth-generation etch-and-rinse adhesives in primary teeth.

Given previous research and the limited data available on the shear bond strength of these materials in primary teeth, the aim of this study was to evaluate and compare the shear bond strength of sixth-generation self-etch and fourth-generation etch-and-rinse adhesive systems to primary teeth.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This study was a systematic review conducted between October and December 2021. It focused on evaluating the shear bond strength of sixth-generation (self-etch) adhesives and fourth-generation (etch-and-rinse) adhesives in primary teeth.

Inclusion criteria

Laboratory (in vitro) studies comparing the shear bond strength of sixth-generation self-etch adhesives and fourth-generation etch-and-rinse adhesives in human primary teeth.

Exclusion criteria

Meta-analyses, studies without a control group, and studies conducted on nonhuman teeth such as bovine or canine specimens.

Data collection and search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted using reputable scientific databases, including ProQuest, PubMed, ClinicalKey, UpToDate, Springer, ScienceDirect, Cochrane, Scopus, Embase, and Web of Science (ISI), to identify studies published between 2000 and 2021. These databases were selected due to their recognized indexing of high-quality articles and broad coverage. To identify Persian-language studies, additional searches were performed in Magiran, IranMedex, SID, ISC, and the thesis repositories of medical science universities.

The electronic search combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms based on the PICO framework, using relevant Boolean operators (AND, OR). The search strategy was as follows:

Search terms

(Primary teeth [Mesh] OR Deciduous teeth [Mesh]) AND (Sixth generation of self-etch bonding agents [Mesh] OR one-step self-etching adhesive system [Mesh]) AND (Fourth generation of etch-and-rinse bonding agents [Mesh] OR three-stage adhesive system [Mesh]) AND (Shear bond strength [Mesh] OR Shear bond stability [Mesh])

The same keywords were adapted and combined as appropriate when searching other databases. Relevant gray literature was also manually searched, including the reference lists of identified articles, to ensure comprehensive coverage.

After initial identification, all articles were screened for eligibility according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant studies were retrieved for detailed assessment.

Quality assessment

Selected articles were first screened by title and abstract. Full-text versions of eligible articles were then reviewed independently by two researchers using the quality assessment checklist recommended by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) (NIH Study Quality Assessment Tools). The checklist evaluated each study based on its objectives, study population, sampling method, available variables, data collection procedures, statistical analysis methods, and reported results.

Each study was scored out of a maximum of 12 points. Studies scoring ≥10 were classified as “good,” those scoring between 7 and 9 as “fair,” and those scoring ≤6 as “poor.” Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer. Only studies rated as “good” were included in the final analysis. The quality assessment results are presented in Table 1.

Descriptive analysis

For the included studies, shear bond strength values and 95% confidence intervals were extracted and analyzed using a random-effects model, taking into account different substrate types (enamel or dentin). Data were categorized and compared by adhesive system.

When applicable, meta-analyses were performed to compare the two adhesive categories (etch-and-rinse versus self-etch) across different tooth substrates. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Limitations

This review faced several limitations. Some relevant articles were published in languages other than English or Farsi, which limited their inclusion. In addition, restricted access to certain databases and full-text articles posed challenges during data collection. This review did not have a preregistered protocol in PROSPERO or any other registry. However, the methodology was meticulously planned in advance to ensure transparency, uphold methodological rigor, and minimize the risk of reporting bias.

RESULTS

Several studies comparing the shear bond strength of sixth-generation (self-etch) and fourth-generation (etch-and-rinse) adhesives in primary teeth were identified through searches of reputable scientific databases. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, five eligible studies were initially selected. One study was excluded due to low quality based on the checklist recommended by the U. S. National Institutes of Health (NIH), resulting in four studies of medium to high quality being included in this review. The selection process for the included studies is summarized in Figure 1.

Descriptive findings

All cases where the shear bond strength of sixth-generation self-etching adhesives were compared with etch-and-rinse adhesives were investigated. In addition, the shear bond strength for dentin was compared between the two adhesive systems, etch-and-rinse and self-etching, while enamel was also separately analyzed, comparing etch-and-rinse adhesives with self-etching adhesives.[20222324]

The effect of adhesive bond strength across studies was analyzed using descriptive statistics. Each study employed its own method for storing samples, with most studies storing samples in distilled water at 37°C for 24 h. In three studies, the bond strength results before and after thermal cycling were similar. In addition, one study placed samples in artificial saliva for a period exceeding 1 year. The shear bond strength results after 1 year were lower than those observed after 24 h. However, while wear appeared to reduce the shear bond strength, the overall results did not significantly differ between the two adhesive systems (self-etch or etch-and-rinse).[20222324]

DISCUSSION

This systematic review aimed to compare the shear bond strength of sixth-generation self-etch and fourth-generation etch-and-rinse adhesives in primary teeth. A comprehensive search for relevant articles led to the identification of several studies on adhesive systems. After applying the exclusion criteria and evaluating the articles using a quality checklist, four articles were selected for inclusion in the review. These studies investigated various aspects of adhesive systems, with the most common focus being the bond strength of adhesives.

As noted, dental tissues (enamel and dentin) are hydrophilic, whereas resin composite structures are hydrophobic. To effectively bond these two materials, an intermediate layer is required to connect the dental tissue components to the composite resin. This intermediate layer includes a hydrophilic head for bonding to dental tissue and a hydrophobic head to bond with the composite resin.[25]

Numerous laboratory studies have explored different adhesive systems to achieve successful composite restorations.[26] The present systematic review analyzed four laboratory studies. While in vivo studies are considered more reliable, they often face ethical challenges, and in vitro studies, although useful, cannot fully replicate oral and dental conditions. Therefore, results from in vitro studies may be subject to distortion or bias, making it difficult to directly apply these findings to clinical practice.[12526]

In the studies included in this review, the shear bond strength of both adhesive systems was assessed in laboratory settings. Overall, the bond strength results of the two adhesive systems were very similar, with only minor differences observed.

The current review found that previous acid etching had minimal impact on the shear bond strength of self-etch adhesives. Although acidic monomers may influence bond strength, no significant difference was noted in the shear bond strength of dentin between the etch-and-rinse and self-etch methods. The increased interaction of self-etch adhesives with the dentin layer, without prior phosphoric acid etching, may reduce postrestorative sensitivity, contributing to the stability of the bond over time.[20222324]

In addition, clinical research indicated that pre-etching enamel led to slightly better bond strength with self-etch adhesives. However, in clinical situations, it is challenging to apply phosphoric acid solely to enamel edges, as etching the dentin can result in increased sensitivity.[20222324]

According to the studies reviewed here, no statistically significant difference was observed between the shear bond strength of sixth-generation (self-etch) and fourth-generation (etch-and-rinse) adhesives in primary teeth enamel and dentin. However, a slight difference was noted, with the etch-and-rinse system showing slightly higher shear strength in both substrates. This small difference is likely due to variables such as stress distribution, sample construction, adhesive area, and loading methods during testing. These findings suggest that both adhesive systems exhibit similar shear bond strength in primary teeth. Given the trend toward faster and simpler clinical applications, self-etch adhesives, which are less sensitive to technique and do not require dentin etching, seem to be an attractive option in pediatric dentistry.[20222324]

Some studies provided information on adhesives, but there was limited data specific to primary tooth adhesives. For instance, Kaczor et al. conducted a review on adhesives for permanent teeth but did not address primary teeth. The study evaluated seven generations of adhesives, finding that etch-and-rinse adhesives significantly reduced microleakage, while self-etch adhesives reduced microleakage in All-Bond Universal. In comparison, etching did not significantly affect microleakage in PrimeandBond Elect or Scotchbond Universal, which showed the lowest nanoleakage.[27]

Similarly, Assis et al. compared one-stage self-etch adhesives with two-stage systems, noting no significant difference in postoperative sensitivity, secondary caries, color matching, margin discoloration, or anatomical shape. However, there was a statistical difference in marginal compatibility.[28]

In another study, Lagarde et al. reviewed adhesives for permanent teeth but did not focus on primary teeth. Their results showed no significant difference in bond strength between self-etch and etch-and-rinse adhesives.[29]

Gindri et al. compared etching times and bond strengths of different systems on primary teeth. They found that reducing etching time did not affect the dentin bond strength for either adhesive system. However, after a longer period, reducing etching time improved the bond strength of etch-and-rinse adhesives, indicating that shorter etch times might enhance long-term bond strength for these systems.[30]

Although many studies have provided valuable insights into adhesive performance, research on adhesives specifically for primary teeth remains limited. Manufacturers typically do not offer separate instructions for primary and permanent teeth. Furthermore, due to differences in the dental substrate structure between primary and permanent teeth, data from studies on permanent teeth cannot be directly applied to primary teeth.

In conclusion, there is a clear need for further comprehensive studies focusing on primary teeth. While existing research provides useful information, no studies have specifically investigated the shear bond strength of self-etch and etch-and-rinse adhesives in primary teeth. This gap in knowledge highlights the need for further research to optimize adhesive use and improve clinical outcomes in pediatric dentistry.

CONCLUSION

No significant difference was found between the shear bond strength of sixth-generation self-etch adhesives and fourth-generation etch-and-rinse adhesives in primary teeth, both in enamel and dentin. Although the etch-and-rinse system showed slightly higher bond strength, this difference is minimal. Given the trend toward faster clinical procedures, self-etch adhesives, with comparable shear strength and reduced sensitivity, offer an attractive option in pediatric dentistry.
