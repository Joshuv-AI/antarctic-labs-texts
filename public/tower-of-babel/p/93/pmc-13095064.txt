The Mediating Role of Professional Affect Between Social Distance and Occupational Stigma Consciousness Among Preclinical Nursing Students: Empirical Research Quantitative
Authors: Die Hu, Jiabao Zhai, Yanan Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimAlthough occupational stigma among nursing students has significant implications for workforce development, limited research has focused on students at different career decision‐making stages. This study aimed to investigate the current status of occupational stigma among preclinical nursing students in China and examine its relationships with professional affect and social distance, in order to promote professional identity and support the stability of the nursing workforce.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey was conducted in November 2024 among 522 Chinese preclinical nursing students using the Occupational Stigma Consciousness Scale, the Professional Affect Subscale, and the Social Distance Scale.ResultsParticipants reported a moderately high level of occupational stigma consciousness. No statistically significant differences in occupational stigma consciousness were found across gender or academic year. Social distance was found to directly exert a negative predictive effect on occupational stigma consciousness, and it also indirectly influenced occupational stigma consciousness through professional affect.ConclusionProfessional affect partially mediates the relationship between social distance and occupational stigma consciousness among Chinese preclinical nursing students.Patient or Public ContributionThe study participants were recruited after being informed about the study and providing their consent.Keywords: occupational stigma, preclinical nursing students, professional affect, social distance

Introduction

With the emergence of the biopsychosocial model of medicine and the development of holistic nursing, nurses have gradually gained greater autonomy and a leading role in care delivery, no longer simply subordinates to physicians. However, public recognition of this professional advancement varies across countries. Surveys in Australia (Allnutt et al. 2010) and the United States (Donelan et al. 2008) indicate that nursing is highly respected, and widely recommended as a career choice, whereas in some countries, the public still views nurses as “doctor's assistants” or “the ones who give injections,” reflecting persistent negative biases toward the profession. According to the Report on the Development Status of the Nursing Workforce in China (Wang 2017), a national survey conducted across 30 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions (excluding Tibet) and initiated in December 2016 (published in May 2017), the public primarily perceives nurses' work as administering injections and medications (94.1%), conducting ward rounds (79.9%), and assisting doctors in diagnosis and treatment (77.8%). Meanwhile, 92% of nurses believe nursing work holds a low social status, and 80.7% consider respect the most important aspect of their work. Under the dual influence of public misperceptions and nurses' own negative perceptions of their occupational status, the profession has lost its appeal to prospective talent, as reflected in unstable career choices and increased turnover (Ben Natan et al. 2024; Ji et al. 2022).

However, most existing research on nursing‐related stigma has focused on mental health stigma (Shi et al. 2024; Özel Erçel et al. 2025) and its consequences (Liao et al. 2025), with relatively limited attention to occupational stigma in nursing, particularly in China. One reason is historical: an independent nursing profession only emerged in China following the introduction of Western medicine in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Before that, nursing‐related care was mainly provided by family members, medical apprentices, or other non‐professional caregivers under physicians, and therefore occupational stigma toward nursing has received relatively little attention. The few available studies emphasize the influence of individual psychological traits (Atta et al. 2025), with limited attention paid to the pre‐internship stage. Under China's nursing education system, students typically begin clinical internships in their final academic year, by which time most have already made career decisions. By comparison, the preclinical period is when these decisions are being formed. Excessive perceived stigma during the preclinical phase may hinder workforce stability, particularly given that many nursing students are assigned to this major rather than choosing it voluntarily (Zhang et al. 2014) and may have weak professional identification. Therefore, exploring the current status and influencing factors of occupational stigma consciousness among preclinical nursing students in China is of great significance for guiding their career choices and expanding the nursing workforce.

Social distance refers to an individual's perceived closeness or remoteness in relationships with others or groups (Ai and Zhou 2018; Wu and Sun 2021). It is a form of psychological distance that interacts with other dimensions such as temporal and spatial distance. During the pre‐internship stage, nursing students are primarily engaged in theoretical learning with limited exposure to clinical patients, and coupled with the physical separation from clinical settings, a certain degree of social distance may exist between them and patients. The Construal Level Theory provides a theoretical framework for the relationship between individuals' perceived distance and their level of construal of things (Jiang and He 2017). When nursing students perceive a greater distance from patients, they tend to focus on the overall characteristics of the nursing profession from a high construal level. And occupational stigma, as a negative overall social evaluation of the profession, is naturally more likely to be perceived. Additionally, some studies have provided support for the relationship between social distance and stigma, such as the association between social distance and the stigma of depression (Liu 2021). Based on this, Hypothesis 1 of this study is proposed: Social distance positively predicts occupational stigma consciousness among pre‐internship nursing students.

It should be noted that examining only the effect of social distance on occupational stigma provides limited guidance for intervention, as reducing social distance mainly relies on early exposure and clinical experience—conditions that preclinical students often lack. Therefore, it is necessary to clarify whether other factors influence this mechanism when students remain distant from clinical patients. The halo effect provides a theoretical framework, suggesting that an individual's emotional attitude can influence cognitive evaluations (Thorndike 1920). Thus, professional affection—nursing students' emotional attachment and liking toward the nursing profession—may influence their perception of occupational stigma. According to the Construal Level Theory, the closer the perceived distance is, the more specific an individual's attention to things will be (Jiang and He 2017). When nursing students get closer to nursing practice, they will have a deeper perception of its specific value, thus stimulating a stronger investment of professional affect. In addition, existing studies have confirmed that social distance is associated with emotional responses (Sikorski et al. 2015), and since emotion constitutes a key component of affect, this supports the connection between social distance and professional affect. Based on these, this study proposes Hypothesis 2: The professional affect of nursing students who have not had internships plays a mediating role between social distance and occupational stigma consciousness.

Hence, the present study conducted a cross‐sectional survey among preclinical nursing students at a medical university in China to examine the current status of occupational stigma and to explore its pathway relationships with social distance and professional affect. The findings revealed a moderately high level of occupational stigma among preclinical nursing students, with professional affect partially mediating the relationship between social distance and occupational stigma. These results provide useful insights for reducing occupational stigma and stabilizing the nursing workforce.

Methods

Research Participants

A cluster sampling method was used to conduct a survey in November 2024 among sophomore and junior nursing students at Gannan Medical University in China who had not yet entered clinical internships. Freshmen were excluded because their curriculum primarily focuses on basic medical sciences, and they have not yet begun nursing‐specific courses or clinical observation. Their understanding of clinical nursing was too limited to be relevant for this study. In contrast, second‐ and third‐year undergraduate students have acquired preliminary knowledge and experience through nursing coursework and early clinical exposure, making their perspectives on career choice more meaningful. Meanwhile, unlike most fourth‐year students who have typically finalized their career plans, these students are still in the process of shaping their professional identity. Therefore, this study focused on nursing students in these two academic years. As a member of the research team was a teacher at Gannan Medical University, questionnaires were distributed during class breaks by this team member. The purpose of the study was explained, informed consent was obtained, and any questions raised during completion were clarified on site. To ensure a high response rate, questionnaires were collected on the spot after completion. The same investigator was responsible for both distribution and collection to ensure consistency in explanations provided to participants. A total of 550 questionnaires were distributed, with 534 returned (response rate: 97.09%) and 522 valid, yielding a validity rate of 94.91%. Among the participants, 113 were male (21.65%) and 409 female (78.35%); 254 were sophomore (48.66%) and 268 were junior (51.34%). Inclusion criteria: (1) Informed consent obtained; (2) No use of psychiatric medications or presence of mental health disorders during the survey period. Exclusion criteria: (1) Incomplete questionnaires; (2) Logically inconsistent responses. The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Gannan Medical University (Protocol code: Ref number 2023630). Instruments

Social Distance Scale

The Social Distance Scale, developed by Shechory and Idisis (Shechory and Idisis 2006), is used to measure the social distance between individuals and specific groups across various contexts. In this study, the scale was adapted to align with the research objectives. Specifically, references to “XX” in the original items were replaced with “patients they may care for in the future.” For example, the original item “Would you marry someone from group XX?” was revised to “Would you be willing to marry a patient you may care for in future nursing work?” Similarly, “Would you like to be friends with XX?” was adapted to “Would you like to become friends with a patient you may care for in your future practice?” To ensure that the localized items captured the intended construct, the items clarified that the “patients” referred to hypothetical individuals after recovery, with no ongoing caregiving relationship or conflict of professional interests. Participants were asked to respond in terms of attitudes toward social distance, rather than actual clinical or ethical judgments. The scale consists of 7 items and adopts a 7‐point Likert scoring system ranging from 1 (“Strongly unwilling”) to 7 (“Strongly willing”), with total scores ranging from 7 to 49. Higher scores indicate a shorter perceived social distance between nursing students and patients. In this study, the Cronbach's α for the scale was 0.921.

Occupational Stigma Consciousness Scale

This scale was originally developed by Pinel (Pinel 1999) for studying perceived stigma among women, and later adapted to the occupational context by Pinel and Paulin (Pinel and Paulin 2005). It has since been widely used in China to assess occupational stigma among employees, and has demonstrated good reliability and validity (Zhou and Huang 2018). The scale consists of 6 items and uses a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly agree”), with total scores ranging from 6 to 30. The total score of the scale is the sum of the scores for each item, with a higher total indicating stronger perceived career stigma. In this study, the Cronbach's α for the scale was 0.872.

Professional Affect Subscale

This study employed the professional affect subscale from the Professional Identity Questionnaire for College Students, developed by Qin Panbo (Qin 2009), to assess nursing students' emotional preference for the profession. The subscale consists of 8 items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = “Strongly disagree” to 5 = “Strongly agree”). The total score is the sum of all item scores, with higher scores indicating stronger professional affect. The questionnaire has been widely applied in studies among Chinese college students across various majors and has shown good reliability and validity (Mu et al. 2020). In this study, the Cronbach's α for this subscale was 0.917.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS 25.0 and the PROCESS macro (version 3.5). Descriptive statistics were reported as means ± standard deviations (M ± SD). Independent‐samples t‐tests were conducted to compare group differences. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships between variables. Mediation analysis was conducted using the PROCESS macro (Model 4). A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Common Method Bias

All data in this study were self‐reported by nursing students. To assess common method bias, Harman's single‐factor test was conducted prior to analysis. The results indicated six factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the first factor accounted for 34.44% of the variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%, suggesting no serious common method bias in this study.

Descriptive Statistics and Group Differences in Professional Affect, Social Distance, and Occupational Stigma Consciousness

The total score for professional affect among preclinical nursing students was 28.95 ± 5.51, while the scores for social distance and occupational stigma consciousness were 30.59 ± 7.99 and 20.63 ± 3.71, respectively. There were no statistically significant differences in occupational stigma consciousness scores between male and female students (t = −1.302, p = 0.194) or between students of different academic years (t = −1.454, p = 0.147). Mediation Analysis of Professional Affect Between Social Distance and Occupational Stigma Consciousness

Correlation analysis indicated statistically significant relationships among professional affect, occupational stigma consciousness, and social distance in preclinical nursing students. To further explore the pathways among these variables, the PROCESS macro (Model 4) (Hayes 2017) was used to test the mediating effect, with occupational stigma consciousness as the dependent variable, social distance as the independent variable, and professional affect as the mediating variable. Mediation analysis was conducted using the bootstrapping method with 5000 resamples and a 95% confidence interval. The indirect effect of professional affect on the relationship between social distance and occupational stigma consciousness was significant, as the confidence interval did not include zero (LLCI = −0.049, ULCI = −0.011), indicating a significant mediating effect. In addition, the direct effect of social distance on occupational stigma consciousness remained significant after controlling for the mediating variable, with a confidence interval of (LLCI = −0.132, ULCI = −0.050), suggesting that professional affect played a partial mediating role in the relationship between social distance and occupational stigma consciousness, accounting for 23.53% of the total effect. Discussion

Occupational Stigma Consciousness Among Preclinical Nursing Students Was at a Moderately High Level

The occupational stigma consciousness score among preclinical nursing students was 20.63 ± 3.71, with a mean item score of 3.44 (5‐point Likert), indicating a moderately high level. This aligns with Chen et al.'s (2023) finding of a 4.24 mean score (7‐point Likert) in Chinese nursing students. Similar moderately high levels were reported by Atta et al. (2025) in Egyptian nursing students and Zhou and Huang (2018) in Chinese nurses and physicians, with mean scores of 4.59 and 4.73 respectively (7‐point Likert). Several factors may explain the elevated levels of occupational stigma consciousness among preclinical nursing students. First, despite the shift in the healthcare model—where nurses have moved from being subordinate to doctors to becoming independent collaborators—public perceptions have not evolved in parallel with the development of the nursing profession. In traditional societal views, nursing is often perceived as a low‐status job involving “servitude,” requiring low educational attainment, and lacking technical skills, thus contributing to stigma (Girvin et al. 2016). Second, most Chinese nursing students are assigned to the major through redistribution, which may lead to initial reluctance toward the profession (Zhang et al. 2014). In addition, their perceptions and attitudes toward the profession are often influenced by their parents and surrounding social circles; in other words, these perceptions tend to form prior to formal nursing education (Wu et al. 2018). Under the influence of existing negative stereotypes, they are more likely to interpret information from their environment as stigmatizing (Lee and Harris 2013), which likely contributes to the elevated levels of perceived occupational stigma observed in this study.

Additionally, this study found no statistically significant differences in occupational stigma scores across gender or academic year. This contrasts with the findings of Atta et al. (2025), who reported that male students scored significantly higher than females and that occupational stigma consciousness decreased with increasing academic year. Gender stereotypes are often embedded in public portrayals of nursing, especially in films and on social media, where nursing is frequently depicted in highly gendered ways (Atta et al. 2025; Subu et al. 2022). It is commonly assumed that nursing does not align with masculine traits (Girvin et al. 2016), suggesting potential gender differences in stigma perception. However, the current study found no such differences. One possible explanation is that entrenched societal stereotypes and pre‐existing negative perceptions of the profession may overshadow any gender‐based differences in stigma perception. Regarding the non‐significant differences between academic years, this may be attributed to the relatively similar educational structure in China for pre‐internship students in the second and third years, where clinical exposure is limited to observational internships rather than full clinical practice.

Recommend that nursing schools enhance professional identity education for pre‐internship nursing students and incorporate explicit discussions of occupational stigma into the curriculum, rather than avoiding, concealing, or idealizing the issue (Zhang et al. 2022). By fostering a realistic understanding of the origins of stigma among students, nursing schools will be better equipped to develop targeted interventions to address stigma based on the specific perceptions of individual students.

Professional Affect Mediates the Relationship Between Social Distance and Occupational Stigma Consciousness

This study found that professional affect partially mediates the relationship between social distance and occupational stigma consciousness among preclinical nursing students. This suggests that social distance not only directly and negatively predicts occupational stigma but also indirectly influences it through professional affect. On one hand, social distance is a form of psychological distance. According to the construal level theory, an individual's perceived distance from an object or event influences their attitudes, behaviours, and levels of construal (Jiang and He 2017). When the perceived distance is far, individuals tend to focus on abstract characteristics, adopting high‐level construals such as emphasizing overall goals, moral norms, and values. In contrast, when the perceived distance is close, individuals focus on concrete information, adopting low‐level construals, such as short‐term goals and feasibility of decisions. When pre‐intern nursing students perceive a closer social distance with patients, they are more likely to focus on specific information about the patient—such as their medical condition—rather than the stigma associated with providing care. Thus, their perceived professional stigma is lower. Conversely, when the social distance is greater, students are more inclined to focus on abstract aspects of the profession related to patients and clinical care, including the stigma associated with the nursing profession as a whole, resulting in higher levels of perceived professional stigma. Therefore, social distance negatively predicts professional stigma consciousness of preclinical nursing students.

On the other hand, when pre‐intern nursing students perceive a closer social distance with patients, they are more willing to approach and engage with them. According to construal level theory, they tend to focus more on concrete information, such as whether the patient's condition is improving or whether nursing interventions have significantly enhanced the patient's physical and psychological well‐being. This, in turn, fosters stronger professional identity and greater emotional attachment to the nursing profession. According to the halo effect, individuals' emotional preference toward an object can influence their cognitive evaluation of it. Therefore, the more positive emotions nursing students hold toward their profession, the more positively they tend to perceive and interpret the profession. Conversely, more negative emotions may lead to more negative perceptions and interpretations, thereby increasing perceived professional stigma. Thus, professional emotion plays a mediating role in the relationship between social distance and professional stigma consciousness among preclinical nursing students.

Although the social distance between preclinical nursing students and patients is influenced by spatial distance, it is essentially a subjective perception. Therefore, it is recommended that nursing education programs incorporate teaching strategies, such as classroom role‐playing and narrative writing, that allow students to adopt the patients' perspective, thereby reducing perceived social distance and mitigating occupational stigma. Moreover, professional affect is a dynamic construct that can be shaped by educational interventions, organizational environment, social support, and other factors. To enhance professional affect, nursing curricula should continuously promote students' understanding of the profession and emphasize its social value throughout the entire training period, rather than limiting exposure to an introductory lecture on the history, development, and significance of nursing to improve students' professional cognition, further enhance professional affect, and thereby mitigate professional stigma. In addition, promoting role modelling by faculty and providing social support can help students perceive that their profession is valued by those around them, further strengthening professional affect and reducing occupational stigma.

This study has several innovations. First, it specifically focused on pre‐internship nursing students, a population often overlooked in previous research. Second, given that these students experience both spatial and social distance from patients compared to those in the internship stage, the study highlighted social distance as a key variable. Third, the findings revealed no significant differences in occupational stigma across gender or grade among pre‐internship students, which contrasts with prior studies. Finally, professional affect was found to partially mediate the relationship between social distance and occupational stigma, providing new insight into the mechanisms underlying occupational stigma in this population.

The limitations of this study are as follows. First, the variable “admission adjustment” was not included as a control, nor were potential moderating variables such as social support and professional cognition considered, which may limit the ability to reveal the moderating effects on professional affect in the current mediation model. Future studies should incorporate these variables to refine the model. Second, the sample was drawn from a single ordinary medical college, excluding students from prestigious medical schools and first‐year nursing students, and included a slightly higher proportion of male participants. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Future research should expand the sample to cover students from different academic years and types of institutions and conduct large‐scale empirical validation nationwide. Third, the measurement of perceived occupational stigma is influenced not only by external objective factors but also by internal subjective factors. Future studies could adopt more objective approaches to assess occupational stigma. Forth, although the social distance item was localized to fit the nursing context, the revised wording may introduce additional situational interpretation compared with the original version. Referring to a “patient” may implicitly evoke professional or ethical considerations, despite being framed as a hypothetical, non‐clinical scenario. Future studies could further refine the item wording to enhance content validity.
