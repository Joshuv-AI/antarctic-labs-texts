A case of carotid blowout syndrome following endoscopic endonasal surgery and proton beam therapy for chondrosarcoma: Considering the role of nasoseptal mucosal flap for minimizing bleeding
Authors: Atsushi Tsukada, Hiroyoshi Kino, Hideaki Matsumura, Koji Hirata, Yoshiro Ito, Kentoku Sato, Shuho Tanaka, Masashi Mizumoto, Hiroyoshi Akutsu, Yuji Matsumaru, Eiichi Ishikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Following advancements in endoscopic endonasal surgery (EES), neurosurgeons have become increasingly proficient in exposing the internal carotid artery (ICA) through EES. Consequently, they are also more frequently encountering carotid blowout syndrome (CBS), a potentially fatal complication. Therefore, it is essential for neurosurgeons to be well-prepared to manage the emergency effectively.Case Description: A 64-year-old man underwent EES for a left clival chondrosarcoma. Following tumor resection, the left ICA was covered with a nasoseptal mucosal flap to mitigate the risk of CBS associated with postoperative radiation therapy. Approximately 8 years after undergoing proton beam therapy, the patient presented with sudden epistaxis and was admitted to the hospital. After achieving temporary hemostasis in the outpatient department, a cerebral angiogram revealed a pseudoaneurysm at the end of the petrous portion of the ICA, which was suspected to be the bleeding point. Revascularization of the left ICA with high-flow bypass (HFB) and parent artery occlusion was planned. Due to recurrent preoperative epistaxis, emergent hemostasis through EES was performed. The bleeding was effectively controlled because the pseudoaneurysm was covered by the nasoseptal mucosal flap. The patient subsequently underwent the planned HFB procedure without complication.Conclusion: In the present case, the nasoseptal mucosal flap likely facilitated rapid, temporary hemostasis at the onset of CBS, playing a critical role in the patient’s survival. Neurosurgeons should consider the use of nasoseptal mucosal flaps not only for the prevention of cerebrospinal fluid leakage but also as a protective barrier and a means of facilitating hemostatic control in the event of CBS.

INTRODUCTION

Carotid blowout syndrome (CBS) is a well-known, potentially fatal complication that occurs in patients with a history of head and neck cancer.[6,20,22,30,31] In this context, CBS has been reported to occur in 3–4.5% of cases following surgery combined with adjuvant radiotherapy.[31] CBS can lead to massive bleeding and is often fatal.[30] Although CBS is a widely acknowledged entity in head and neck oncology, it is less familiar within the neurosurgical community. With the continued advancement of endoscopic endonasal surgery (EES), neurosurgeons now have the ability to expose the internal carotid artery (ICA) transnasally. Consequently, awareness of CBS is increasingly important in neurosurgical practice. To prevent CBS following treatment for head and neck cancer, the use of vascularized flaps to protect the ICA has been increasingly documented.[17,22] On the other hand, in EES performed in neurosurgery, nasoseptal mucosal flaps are commonly used to prevent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage,[29] although they are not routinely employed for the prevention of CBS. EES for petroclival tumors often results in residual tumor burden, requiring postoperative radiotherapy – a known risk factor for CBS. Therefore, neurosurgeons should also consider using nasoseptal mucosal flaps to protect the ICA and reduce the risk of CBS in patients receiving EES followed by radiotherapy. However, a nasoseptal mucosal flap does not entirely preclude the development of CBS. To date, no reports have specifically addressed the clinical advantages of these flaps in the management of established CBS.

In the current case, a patient with a skull base chondrosarcoma receiving proton beam therapy after EES developed CBS 8 years after irradiation. In this patient, a nasoseptal mucosal flap had been placed on the exposed ICA at the initial surgery to protect it from the proton beam. The nasoseptal mucosal flap reduced the amount of bleeding and facilitated temporary hemostasis when CBS developed. There are few reports of CBS after EES and proton beam therapy for skull base chondrosarcoma, and we report here a case in which a nasoseptal mucosal flap played a crucial role in reducing CBS severity.

CASE DESCRIPTION

The patient presented with the left abducens nerve palsy, and preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a tumor in the left petroclival region, appearing as a hyperintense signal on T2-weighted images [Figure 1a and b]. Based on these imaging findings, chondrosarcoma was initially suspected. Surgery was performed through EES using a technique previously described.[14,19] To minimize the risk of postoperative neurological deficits, a small portion of the tumor was deliberately left within the left Meckel’s cave and lateral to the left hypoglossal canal, after which the remainder of the resection was completed. Wide-ranging bone removal resulted in exposure of the left ICA to the nasal cavity [Figure 1c]. As proton beam therapy was planned for the residual tumor, the left ICA was covered with a vascularized nasoseptal mucosal flap [Figure 1d]. Postoperative MRI confirmed minimal residual tumor in the left Meckel’s cave and lateral to the left hypoglossal canal, consistent with intraoperative findings [Figure 1e and f].

Postoperative proton beam therapy was administered at a total dose of 70.2 Gy in 26 fractions to prevent regrowth of the residual tumor [Figure 1g and h]. Follow-up MRI was conducted annually, with no evidence of residual tumor regrowth observed.

About 8 years after receiving proton beam therapy, the patient was emergently transferred to our hospital due to sudden epistaxis. An otolaryngologist observed the nasal cavity through endoscopy in an outpatient clinic and detected an arterial hemorrhage from the left ICA. After achieving temporary hemostasis using oxidized cellulose and gauze packing, neurosurgeons followed with cerebral angiography. Left internal carotid angiography revealed a pseudoaneurysm at the end of the petrous portion of the ICA, which was suspected to be a bleeding point [Figure 2a and b]. A balloon test occlusion (BTO) of the left ICA revealed insufficient collateral flow to the left cerebral hemisphere through the anterior communicating artery, posterior communicating artery, and external carotid artery. Furthermore, the patient exhibited marked aphasia during arterial occlusion. Given the patient’s intolerance to ischemia, reconstruction of the left ICA was planned using high-flow extracranial-to-intracranial (EC-IC) bypass. On the fourth day of hospitalization, the patient developed epistaxis again. Emergency hemostasis was performed via EES. Intraoperatively, the bleeding point was located in the midline of the cavernous portion of the left ICA, on the distal side of the pseudoaneurysm shown in the cerebral angiogram [Video 1, Figure 2c]. The pseudoaneurysm was covered by a nasoseptal mucosal flap, and the bleeding was observed to drain into the nasal cavity through a gap under the nasoseptal mucosal flap. Hemostasis was easily completed by covering this gap with a gelatin sponge and placing the gauze on the nasoseptal mucosal flap to compress the “true” bleeding point of the pseudoaneurysm under the nasoseptal mucosal flap.

The patient received EC-IC bypass as planned. First, a single superficial temporal artery-to-middle cerebral artery bypass was performed as an intraoperative assistive revascularization procedure. Next, the left radial artery was harvested as a graft for EC-IC bypass, bypassing the proximal left middle cerebral artery and the left external carotid artery. Angiography was performed to confirm blood flow through the anastomosed vessels [Figure 2d]. A 6 French sheath was inserted into the left ICA, and parent artery occlusion was achieved using coil embolization across an extended segment spanning from the proximal to the distal portion of the pseudoaneurysm [Figure 2e].

After the patient received the EC-IC bypass, MRI revealed only a few small spotty areas of diffusion restriction, with no evidence of major ischemic complications. The patient developed transient and mild cognitive dysfunction, potentially attributable to prolonged intubation, two episodes of carotid artery hemorrhage within a short interval, and significant alterations in cerebral circulation following the EC-IC bypass. However, following transfer to a rehabilitation facility, the patient exhibited improvement in cognitive function. His Revised Hasegawa’s Dementia Scale, Mini-Mental State Examination, and Frontal Assessment Battery scores have now recovered to 27, 29, and 17, respectively, and he is able to live independently.

DISCUSSION

Incidence of CBS in chondrosarcoma

The treatment strategy for chondrosarcoma is primarily surgical resection.[27] However, total resection is difficult to achieve because the tumor invades the clivus and petrous apex.[15] Radiotherapy is the only remaining option for residual tumors. However, due to the slow growth rate of chondrosarcoma, conventional radiotherapy with a standard dose of 60 Gy is often insufficient to achieve optimal therapeutic efficacy.[13] Proton beam therapy has been proposed as an effective treatment modality for chondrosarcoma, as the Bragg peak allows for the delivery of higher radiation doses confined to the tumor target, surpassing the limitations of conventional radiotherapy.[28,32] The risk of developing CBS is reported to increase 14-fold when the radiation dose exceeds 70 Gy.[6] For chondrosarcoma, a dose of 70 Gy or more is usually required to achieve a therapeutic effect.[25] Therefore, the risk of developing CBS is likely to be high following proton beam therapy for chondrosarcoma. However, there have been few reports of CBS after proton beam therapy for chondrosarcoma. When the scope is extended to include chordoma, a histologically similar but more prevalent tumor, a few cases of CBS have been reported [Table 1].[1,2,8,9,16,35] All previous reports have focused exclusively on the treatment outcomes of proton beam therapy, with no mention of how the ICA was covered during surgery. Previous reports have indicated that CBS in patients with head and neck carcinoma typically develops from several months to approximately 3 years after postoperative radiation therapy.[6,20,22,30,31] Slijepcevic et al. suggested that this interval may be shortened when arterial exposure, infection, or fistula formation occurs during surgery.[30] Furthermore, Liang et al. reported that although most cases develop within 1 year after radiation therapy, a substantial proportion occur more than 5 years later.[22] In contrast, reports of CBS following proton beam therapy for chordoma and chondrosarcoma generally describe it as a late complication without specifying the timing of onset.[1,2,8,9,16,35]

In the current case, CBS developed 8 years after EES and proton beam therapy, representing a markedly delayed onset. This observation suggests that compared with head and neck cancers, chordomas and chondrosarcomas may follow a more chronic course leading to CBS. One possible explanation is that, in contrast to head and neck cancer surgery, direct dissection of the tumor from ICA is usually limited in surgeries for chordoma and chondrosarcoma. Consequently, in these tumors, late radiation-related vascular injury may play a more prominent role in the development of CBS than surgical factors. High-dose radiotherapy for chondrosarcoma and chordoma may carry a lifelong risk of CBS; therefore, long-term monitoring for potential vascular complications is essential. To the best of our knowledge, this represents a novel clinical observation.

Role of nasoseptal mucosal flap for prevention of CBS

Due to the rarity of CBS, there are currently no established studies evaluating the efficacy of nasoseptal mucosal flaps in preventing this complication in the context of neurosurgical EES. In head and neck surgery, muscle flaps harvested from the sternocleidomastoid muscle are commonly employed as a prophylactic measure to prevent CBS.[7,26] It is well established that intraoperative coverage of the carotid artery with a well-vascularized muscle flap reduces the postoperative incidence of CBS, and that vascularized tissue coverage significantly contributes to its prevention.[7]

In contrast, during EES, especially in cases of nasopharyngeal carcinoma, the nasoseptal mucosal flap is typically used to cover the exposed ICA.[34] When the nasoseptal mucosal flap is unavailable, the use of an inferior turbinate mucosal flap has been recommended as an alternative.[3]

CBS is known to be associated with an increased risk not only following postoperative radiotherapy but also in the presence of postoperative infection.[38] Even with the use of free flaps, studies have reported a reduction in the incidence of postoperative wound infections.[10] Therefore, it is plausible that a well-vascularized, adherent pedicled flap, particularly a nasoseptal mucosal flap, may effectively lower the risk of postoperative infection, thereby contributing to the prevention of CBS.

This report does not seek to prove that nasoseptal mucosal flaps reduce the incidence of CBS. Instead, it highlights how a prepositioned flap markedly facilitated hemostasis once CBS occurred. To the best of our knowledge, this is a novel perspective that offers clinically meaningful, hypothesis-generating insights.

Role of the nasoseptal mucosal flap in minimizing bleeding during CBS events

The initial priorities in managing a patient with CBS to improve the survival rate are to achieve temporary hemostasis and secure airway control.[20] CBS is classified into three types according to the severity of bleeding.[4] Acute CBS is characterized by active arterial rupture and massive hemorrhage that cannot be controlled by surgical packing. Impending CBS is characterized by transient bleeding that can be temporarily controlled by compression, whereas threatened CBS presents without active bleeding but with structural abnormalities of the vessel wall due to tumor invasion, infection, or other pathological processes. In this specific case, the prepositioned nasoseptal mucosal flap served as a critical barrier; without it, achieving hemostasis through simple gauze packing would have been insufficient, inevitably leading to uncontrollable acute CBS. However, due to the presence of the flap, temporary hemostasis was achieved with gauze packing alone. Consequently, although the clinical status was effectively downgraded to impending CBS, the patient remained at risk for rebleeding. In this context, the nasoseptal mucosal flap likely functioned as a means of modification of the bleeding pattern, converting a potentially catastrophic arterial rupture into a more manageable hemorrhage. Although CBS is a life-threatening event with an overall survival rate of only 28%, the prognosis improves markedly when temporary hemostasis and prompt initial management are achieved, facilitating a transition from acute to impending CBS, which is associated with reported survival rates of up to 96.5%. In the current case, the patient experienced two episodes of epistaxis, one during emergency transport and another after hospital admission, both of which were promptly controlled with gauze packing. One of the reasons for this may be that the nasoseptal mucosal flap covered the ruptured point of the pseudoaneurysm. In fact, the placement of the flap with good blood supply covering the carotid artery is considered to be an effective procedure for preventing rebleeding.[20] In cases of impending CBS such as the present one, coverage with vascularized soft-tissue enables effective management and allows for the application of endovascular treatment, whose indications have been expanding in recent years.[7] Neurosurgeons should consider utilizing a nasoseptal mucosal flap during EES to provide both structural protection for the ICA and to facilitate easier hemostatic control should CBS occur, especially for those undergoing high-dose postoperative radiotherapy.

Treatment for CBS

For the management of CBS, various hemostatic strategies have been reported.[5,11,12,18,21,23,24,30,31,33,36,37] Suárez et al. proposed a decision-making flowchart for CBS management, emphasizing that the most critical determinant in selecting an appropriate treatment is whether the patient has sufficient ischemic tolerance.[31] The use of a BTO is recommended to assess this ischemic tolerance preoperatively.[23,31]

In patients with adequate ischemic tolerance, the occlusion of the ruptured carotid artery can be performed safely

In contrast, patients lacking ischemic tolerance require hemostatic intervention that concurrently preserves cerebral blood flow. In such cases, two main approaches are considered: (1) carotid artery occlusion following ECIC bypass, and (2) flow-preserving endovascular treatment using a covered stent [Table 2]. Although technically complex, EC-IC bypass has been reported to yield favorable outcomes.[18,37] In comparative studies between patients who underwent carotid occlusion after passing BTO and those who underwent EC-IC bypass due to BTO failure, the incidence of postoperative neurological deficits was significantly lower in the EC-IC bypass group.[37] On the other hand, endovascular treatment using covered stents has been associated with a higher risk of rebleeding compared to carotid artery occlusion, as reported in several reviews including large cohort studies.[23,36] It should be noted that in Japan, intracranial placement of covered stents is not approved for reimbursement under the national health insurance system, and therefore, it was not a treatment option in the present case. In this patient, BTO indicated a lack of ischemic tolerance. Therefore, two potential treatment options were considered: EC-IC bypass and the placement of a flow diverter stent. However, in the present case, radiation-induced CBS was suspected, suggesting that the arterial wall of the left ICA surrounding the pseudoaneurysm was extensively compromised and fragile. Moreover, due to prior irradiation, neointimal proliferation on the flow diverter stent might not occur adequately. Given the high long-term risk of rebleeding associated with a flow diverter stent placement in such a context, EC-IC bypass was selected as a more reliable strategy for preventing rebleeding. As a result, a favorable clinical outcome was achieved.

CONCLUSION

In the present case, CBS developed 8 years after EES and proton beam therapy for clival chondrosarcoma. While nasoseptal mucosal flaps are conventionally utilized in neurosurgery to prevent CSF leakage and in head and neck surgery to mitigate the risk of CBS, our experience suggests an additional potential benefit of this reconstructive technique. Even after CBS had manifested, the prepositioned flap appears to have facilitated more effective hemostasis. This observation implies that nasoseptal mucosal flaps may offer clinical advantages not only in the prevention of CBS but also in the modification of bleeding patterns to facilitate emergency management.
