Silencing of BCAT1 induces ferroptosis and inhibits brain metastasis of non-small cell lung cancer via activating the cGAS-STING pathway
Authors: Fan Yang, Yuying Li, Huijiao Zhou, Xin Bi, Dongjie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrain metastasis (BM) in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is associated with the reprogramming of branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism. This investigation sought to comprehend the function and molecular process of BCAT1 during the BM associated with NSCLC.MethodsBCAT1 expression was assessed in NSCLC brain metastases (NSCLC-BM) tissues and NSCLC cell lines using quantitative real-time PCR and western blotting. In vitro, malignant cell processes, BCAA metabolism, and ferroptosis were evaluated. A mouse model of NSCLC-BM was created to evaluate the function of BCAT1 and the cGAS-STRING pathway in vivo. Western blotting was performed to determine whether BCAT1 mediated the cGAS-STING pathway.ResultsBCAT1 expression was elevated in NSCLC-BM tissues and cells. Knocking down BCAT1 inhibited cell viability, migration, invasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and stemness, disrupted BCAA metabolic reprogramming, and promoted ferroptosis in vitro. Moreover, BCAT1 silencing suppressed tumor BM in vivo. The cGAS-STING pathway was activated after BCAT1 knockdown via triggering mitochondrial DNA leakage, and inhibition of this pathway reversed the cellular behaviors in vitro and BM in vivo induced by BCAT1 silencing.ConclusionsIn conclusion, BCAT1 promotes NSCLC-BM by modulating BCAA metabolic reprogramming through suppressing the cGAS-STING pathway, highlighting the potential of BCAT1 to be targeted in therapy.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Knockdown of BCAT1 inhibits non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cell branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism and ferroptosis by activating the cGAS-STING pathway, thereby inhibiting NSCLC brain metastasis (NSCLC-BM).

What is known and what is new?

• It is known that BCAA metabolism is involved in NSCLC-BM. BCAT1 is an important factor in initiating BCAA metabolism and is upregulated in lung cancer metastases. However, it remains unclear whether BCAT1 can regulate the NSCLC-BM. This study clarified the role of BCAT1 in promoting NSCLC-BM through the BCAA metabolic pathway, and identified a novel molecular mechanism, i.e., BCAT1 inhibits the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings imply that BCAT1 is a potential target for anti-NSCLC-BM. This study expands our theoretical understanding of the pathogenesis of NSCLC-BM and suggests studying the comprehensive role and mechanism network of BCAT1 in NSCLC-BM, providing theoretical support for the development of targeted therapeutic approaches.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) continues to be the factor contributing to cancer deaths globally, largely owing to late-stage diagnosis and restricted therapeutic choices (1). Brain metastasis (BM) represents a devastating and frequent complication of NSCLC. Brain metastases occur in up to 10% of patients with NSCLC at initial diagnosis, and 20–40% will develop BM (2). Research shows that the median survival duration for those with untreated NSCLC brain metastases (NSCLC-BM) is merely 1 to 2 months (3). Even after receiving whole-brain radiotherapy, the overall survival period of patients is only 3 to 6 months (4). Therefore, it is necessary to clarify the biological mechanisms by which NSCLC metastasizes to the brain and colonizes, thereby introducing novel approaches for managing this disease.

A significant feature of cancer is the alteration of metabolic processes. Among the various metabolic pathways, branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism has gained increasing attention for its role in cancer progression. BCAAs, including valine, leucine, and isoleucine, contribute nitrogen to form macromolecules such as nucleotides, crucial for the growth of cancer cells (5). BCAT1 initiates BCAA breakdown by transferring the α-amino group to α-ketoglutaric acid (α-KG), forming branched-chain α-ketoacid and glutamic acid (6). BCAT1 is known to be upregulated in several malignancies, including breast, thyroid, and liver cancers, and correlates with poor patient outcomes (7). Notably, BCAT1 shows elevated expression in metastatic tissues of lung cancer and aids in the distant spread of tumors, implying the essential contribution of BCAT1 to lung cancer metastasis (8). Moreover, the enhanced BCAT1-mediated BCAA catabolism promotes NSCLC cell proliferation in the brain (9). However, there are still many unknowns regarding the mechanism of the regulation of NSCLC-BM.

The cGAS-STING pathway is a key element in mediating innate immune responses. It initiates an effective type I interferon response via recognizing cytoplasmic DNA, thereby influencing nearly every stage of tumor development, from the initial malignant transformation to the spread of cancer cells (10,11). Generally, this pathway inhibits tumorigenesis via enhancing the immune defense against cancer cells; however, chronic activation of STING may accelerate tumor growth in certain situations (12). In NSCLC, as a crucial therapeutic effector molecule, the cGAS-STING pathway is crucial for improving the prognosis of patients (13). However, if NSCLC-BM is linked to the cGAS-STING pathway, especially whether it pertains to BCAA metabolism, remains unknown.

This research focused on examining how BCAT1 influences malignant cell behaviors and the reprogramming of BCAA metabolism during NSCLC-BM. Moreover, we clarified whether BCAT1 plays functional roles by regulating the cGAS-STING pathway. This research offers fresh insights into the mechanism of NSCLC-BM by elaborating the relationship between the core metabolic enzymes of BCAAs and the innate immune pathway. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR and ARRIVE reporting checklists (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0269/rc).

Methods

Bioinformatic analysis

RNA profile data in the GSE18842 dataset were used from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE18842). The analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted using the R language, with criteria set as adjusted P value below 0.05 and |log2 (fold change)| >1.

The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to analyze genes associated with BCAT1. The signaling pathways involved in these correlated genes were subjected to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis.

Ethics statement

Study approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. Each patient gave their written informed consent. The procedures pertaining to humans were executed following the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Animal experiments and procedures followed the relevant guidelines and regulations. Experiments were performed under a project license (No. 20260413) granted by the ethics committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University, in compliance with relevant guidelines for the care and use of animals.

Tissue samples

A group of 23 patients who received a diagnosis of NSCLC-BM in The First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University was included. NSCLC diagnosis was determined through pathological results, and brain metastases were diagnosed using enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans. Patients with other primary intracranial tumors, severe internal medicine or neurological diseases, and those who had received targeted brain treatment before surgery were excluded. Brain metastases and surrounding normal tissues were collected from each patient. The tissues were kept at −80 ℃ after being frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Cell culture

NSCLC cells (H1299, RRID: CVCL_0060; A549, RRID: CVCL_0023; H1975, RRID: CVCL_1511; and SK-MES-1, RRID: CVCL_0630) were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). Another NSCLC cell line PC-9 was acquired from Procell (Wuhan, China). H1299, A549, H1975, and PC-9 cells were maintained in RoseWell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S; Gibco), while SK-MES-1 cells were maintained in Eagle’s minimum essential medium (EMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. Lung bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B; ATCC) were maintained in airway epithelial cell basal medium (ATCC) and bronchial epithelial cell growth kit (ATCC). The culture conditions were 37 ℃ with 5% CO2.

Transient transfection

Short hairpin RNA targeting BCAT1 (shBCAT1) and its negative control (shNC) were synthesized by Fenghui Biotechnology (Changsha, China). Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used for transfection in six-well plates. The cells were collected 48 hours afterward.

Cell treatment

G150 (a cGAS inhibitor; MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). A549 cells underwent treatment with a 5 µM G150 solution for 2 hours, while DMSO was used as the control.

After transfection, the BCAA mixture (leucine:isoleucine:valine =2:1:1.2 by weight) was added to the serum-free RPMI-1640 medium to incubate with A549 cells for 24 hours.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8)

A549 cells were placed in 96-well plates (2×103 cells/well). CCK-8 solution (10 µL; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was incubated with the cells for 2 hours. The measurement of absorbance was done with a microplate reader at 450 nm.

Transwell assay

Migration was evaluated using the 24-well Transwell chambers, and invasion was evaluated using the Matrigel-precoated Transwell chambers. A549 cells (1×105 cells/well) were arranged in the top chambers, and the lower chambers received complete medium. After 24 hours, the cells through the filter were stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime) for 20 min following fixation of 4% paraformaldehyde. The cells were viewed with a light microscope.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated using the Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) and then underwent reverse transcription to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA) using the M-MuLV first-strand cDNA synthesis kit (Sangon, Shanghai, China). mRNA expression was detected on the Light Cycler 480 system using the SGExcel FastSYBR mixture (Sangon). The comparative Ct method was used to calculate the results.

To detect mitochondrial DNA in cytosolic extracts, A549 cells were suspended using cytosolic buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), and 20 µg/mL digitonin. After 10 min, cells were collected by centrifugation three times at 1,000 ×g for 3 min. Then, mitochondria were precipitated using centrifugation at 17,000 ×g for 10 min, and the cytosolic supernatant was collected. DNA was isolated from cytosolic supernatant using the DNA clean & concentrator-5 kit (ZYMO Research, Irvine, CA, USA). qPCR was conducted to detect Dloop1, Dloop2, Dloop3, and Cox1 expression.

The specific primers were: BCAT1 forward 5'-AGCCCTGCTCTTTGTACTCTT-3' and reverse 5'-CCAGGCTCTTACATACTTGGGA-3'; β-actin (internal control) forward 5'-CATGTACGTTGCTATCCAGGC-3' and reverse 5'-CTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGAT-3'; Dloop1 forward 5'-AATCTACCATCCTCCGTGAAACC-3' and reverse 5'-TCAGTTTAGCTACCCCCAAGTTTAA-3'; Dloop2 forward 5'-CCCTTCCCCATTTGGTCT-3' and reverse 5'-TGGTTTCACGGAGGATGG-3'; Dloop3 forward 5'-TCCTCCGTGAAACCAACAA-3' and reverse 5'-AGCGAGAAGAGGGGCATT-3'; and Cox1 forward 5'-GCCCCAGATATAGCATTCCC-3' and reverse 5'-GTTCATCCTGTTCCTGCTCC-3'.

Western blotting

The extraction of proteins from A549 cells was performed using the radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer. After detecting protein concentration, 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was used to separate the proteins (50 µg per lane), followed by transfer onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were treated with 5% skim milk to block them, then exposed to primary antibodies overnight at 4 ℃, followed by a 2-hour incubation with secondary antibodies. The bands were made visible using the BeyoECL plus (Beyotime).

The antibodies (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) utilized were: anti-N-cadherin (ab76011, RRID: AB_1310479), anti-E-cadherin (ab40772, RRID: AB_731493), anti-Vimentin (ab92547, RRID: AB_10562134), anti-OCT4 (ab109183, RRID: AB_10864777), anti-SOX2 (ab92494, RRID: AB_10585428), anti-NANOG (ab109250, RRID: AB_10863442), anti-TFRC (ab214039, RRID: AB_2904534), anti-GPX4 (ab125066, RRID: AB_10973901), anti-SLC7A11 (ab216876, RRID: AB_3678921), anti-cGAS (ab302617, RRID: AB_3076687), anti-STING (ab181125, RRID: AB_2916053), anti-phospho (p)-TBK1 (ab109272, RRID: AB_10862438), anti-p-IRF3 (ab76493, RRID: AB_1523836), anti-β-actin (ab8227, RRID: AB_2305186), and goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab6721, RRID: AB_955447).

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS)/MS analysis

After transfection, A549 cells were suspended in methanol-ddH2O mixed solvent (methanol:ddH2O=4:1, v/v), vortexed, and then repeatedly frozen and thawed twice. The supernatant containing metabolites was gathered after being centrifuged at 13,000 ×g for 15 min. LC-MS/MS analysis was carried out using the SCIEXAB API4000 Q-Trap LC-MS/MS system (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). Standard leucine, iso-leucine, valine, and α-KG were acquired from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Determination of ferroptosis-related indicators

Reduced glutathione (GSH) levels were detected using the GSH and glutathione disulfide (GSSG) assay kit (Beyotime). Briefly, A549 cells underwent two rounds of being frozen in liquid nitrogen and warming in a 37 ℃ water bath, then were centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 10 min. The supernatant (10 µL) was placed into 96-well plates and incubated with 150 µL of the total GSH detection solution for 5 min. After that, 50 µL of 0.5 mg/mL NADPH was incorporated. Absorbance at 412 nm was examined with a microplate reader following a 25-min interval.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were measured using the lipid peroxidation MDA assay kit (Beyotime). A549 cells underwent lysis with RIPA lysis buffer, and the supernatant was collected following centrifuging at 12,000 ×g for 10 min. MDA detection working solution (0.2 mL) was added to incubate with 0.1 mL of supernatant at 100 ℃ for 15 min. After cooling and centrifuging, the absorbance was detected at 532 nm using a microplate reader.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) concentrations were determined with the ROS assay kit (Beyotime). A549 cells were treated for 20 min with a 10 µM DCFH-DA probe at 37 ℃. A laser scanning confocal microscope was used to detect fluorescence.

Animal study

BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks old; female, Vital River, Beijing, China) were kept in a specific pathogen-free animal house with a 12 h light/dark cycle, humidity of 40–60%, and room temperature of 21–25 ℃. The mice were assigned to four groups using a computerized randomization procedure: shNC, shBCAT1, shBCAT1 + DMSO, and shBCAT1 + RU.521 (n=6). A549 cells stably transfected with shNC and shBCAT1 lentivirus were labeled with luciferase and suspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a density of 2.5×106 cells/mL. The NSCLC-BM model was generated as previously described using the intracarotid injection method (14). Briefly, mice were administered 100 µL of cell suspension via injection into their right carotid artery. To assess the role of the cGAS/STING pathway, the cGAS inhibitor RU.521 (MCE) was dissolved in 10% DMSO and diluted with corn oil (the final concentration of DMSO was 1%). RU.521 or 1% DMSO was intraperitoneally injected into mice after cell injection and continued daily until the experimental endpoint. After six weeks, mice were anesthetized by inhaling isoflurane and injected with 150 mg/kg D-luciferin intraperitoneally. Bioluminescence imaging was executed with the IVIS Lumina III system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

Statistical analysis

The results from three independent repeated in vitro experiments or six mice were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data were analyzed using the GraphPad 8 software. The analysis of differences between the two groups was conducted using Student’s t-test, and comparisons among several groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was assigned to P values under 0.05.

Results

BCAT1 expression is elevated in NSCLC-BM

DEGs were analyzed using the GSE18842 dataset. The heatmap and volcano plot revealed 942 decreased genes and 768 upregulated genes in tumor samples (Figure 1A,1B). BCAT1, a BCAA catabolism-related enzyme, was selected for the following study. We collected 23 paired NSCLC-BM tissues and paracancerous normal tissues, and found high levels of BCAT1 in the tumor group (Figure 1C,1D). Additionally, we quantified BCAT1 expression in NSCLC cells. In comparison to BEAS-2B cells, BCAT1 expression was elevated in these NSCLC cells, especially in A549 cells (Figure 1E). Collectively, BCAT1 may be involved in NSCLC-BM progression.

Silencing of BCAT1 inhibits cell migration, invasion, and stemness, reprograms BCAA metabolism, and promotes ferroptosis

To examine how BCAT1 affects NSCLC cell phenotypes, A549 cells underwent transfection with shNC and shBCAT1. In shBCAT1-transfected cells, BCAT1 expression was markedly downregulated, compared to shNC (Figure 2A). Subsequently, we found that knockdown of BCAT1 inhibited cell viability (Figure 2B), migratory capability (Figure 2C), and invasiveness (Figure 2D). Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers, including N-cadherin and Vimentin, showed decreased levels, while E-cadherin was elevated after BCAT1 knockdown (Figure 2E). Additionally, BCAT1 knockdown induced the downregulation of OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG levels (Figure 2F), suggesting tumor stemness was inhibited. Furthermore, we assessed BCAA metabolism using LC-MS/MS analysis. The levels of leucine, iso-leucine, valine, and α-KG were all increased by BCAT1 knockdown (Figure 2G). Several ferroptosis-related indicators were measured. As shown in Figure 2H-2J, knockdown of BCAT1 reduced GSH levels, while increasing MDA and ROS levels. The protein levels of TFRC were upregulated, while GPX4 and SLC7A11 levels were downregulated after silencing of BCAT1 (Figure 2K). Taken together, knockdown of BCAT1 suppresses NSCLC cell viability, migration, invasion, EMT, and stemness, reprograms BCAA metabolism, as well as induces ferroptosis.

Exogenous addition of BCAA promotes ferroptosis

To further explore whether BCAT1 can affect ferroptosis by regulating BCAA metabolism, we use BCAA to culture cells after knocking down BCAT1. The levels of GSH, MDA, and ROS were decreased after BCAT1 silencing, and BCAA further reduced their levels (Figure S1A-S1C). In addition, silencing of BCAT1 upregulated TFRC levels and downregulated GPX4 and SLC7A11 levels, while BCAA further enhanced the role of BCAT1 knockdown (Figure S1D). In short, silencing of BCAT1 promotes BCAA accumulation and then facilitates ferroptosis in A549 cells.

Silencing of BCAT1 inhibits brain metastasis

We developed an NSCLC-BM mouse model to examine the function of BCAT1. Bioluminescence imaging displayed that knockdown of BCAT1 restricted A549 cells from metastasizing to the brain in mice, versus the shNC group (Figure 3), suggesting brain metastasis was suppressed by BCAT1 silence.

Silencing of BCAT1 triggers activation of the cGAS-STING pathway

To assess the underlying molecular mechanism, we analyzed the genes related to BCAT1 from the GSE18842 dataset. We discovered a total of 1,983 positively correlated and 1,109 negatively correlated genes (Figure 4A). These associated genes were employed in the KEGG enrichment analysis. These genes were found to be enriched in several pathways, including the cGAS-STING signaling pathway (Figure 4B), which was selected for the following study. Furthermore, we conducted western blotting to confirm BCAT1 could indeed regulate this pathway. Knocking down BCAT1 increased cGAS, STING, p-TBK1, and p-IRF3 protein levels (Figure 4C). Additionally, we measured the levels of mitochondrial DNA markers in the cytoplasm using qPCR. The expression of Dloop1, Dloop2, Dloop3, and Cox1 was elevated after BCAT1 knockdown (Figure 4D). The results demonstrate that silencing of BCAT1 activates the cGAS-STING pathway via triggering mitochondrial DNA leakage.

BCAT1 regulates migration, invasion, stemness, BCAA metabolism, and ferroptosis via the cGAS-STING pathway

We conducted rescue experiments with G150 to block the cGAS-STING pathway, confirming that BCAT1 influences cell phenotypes via this signaling route. We found that knocking down BCAT1 led to reduced cell viability, migration, and invasive capability, whereas G150 treatment counteracted this suppression (Figure 5A-5C). In addition, G150 prevented the decrease in N-cadherin, Vimentin, OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG levels, as well as the elevation in E-cadherin levels caused by BCAT1 knockdown (Figure 5D,5E). Furthermore, the elevation of leucine, iso-leucine, valine, and α-KG caused by BCAT1 knockdown was reversed by G150 treatment (Figure 5F). In terms of ferroptosis, G150 treatment counteracted the reduction of GSH and the increase of MDA and ROS levels caused by BCAT1 knockdown (Figure 5G-5I). An upsurge in TFRC levels and a decline in GPX4 and SLC7A11 levels induced by BCAT1 knockdown were reversed after G150 treatment (Figure 5J). Collectively, disabling the cGAS-STING pathway counteracts the suppression of cancerous activities and BCAA metabolism, as well as the promotion of ferroptosis induced by BCAT1 silence.

BCAT1 affects brain metastasis via the cGAS-STING pathway

In vivo rescue experiments were performed to assess the role of the cGAS-STING pathway. RU.521 was injected to inhibit cGAS. We found that knockdown of BCAT1 suppressed the metastasis of tumor cells to the brain, which was counteracted by RU.521 (Figure 6). These results suggest that blocking the cGAS-STING pathway reverses the inhibitory effect of BCAT1 silencing on NSCLC-BM.

Discussion

Our study elucidates a novel mechanistic pathway through which the metabolic enzyme BCAT1 drives NSCLC-BM by reprogramming BCAA metabolism and suppressing the cGAS-STING signaling pathway.

Increased BCAT1 expression was observed in NSCLC cells and NSCLC-BM tissues, suggesting that BCAT1 is involved in NSCLC, particularly concerning NSCLC-BM. This is consistent with the conclusion of previous studies that BCAT1 is a key factor in tumor cell metastasis (8). Tumor metastasis is a complex process that involves migrating and invading the stroma, and EMT provides conditions for metastasis (15). This research discovered that reducing BCAT1 levels decreased the viability, migration, invasion, and EMT of NSCLC cells, consistent with previous studies (16,17), suggesting that BCAT1 functions as an oncogene in NSCLC and may be related to metastasis. Moreover, BCAT1 knockdown inhibited NSCLC cell stemness, further demonstrating the cancer-promoting effect. Although this has not been reported in NSCLC, it is aligned with its role in promoting stemness in other tumors, such as stomach and colorectal cancers (18).

BCAA metabolism is crucial for the malignant progression of NSCLC. Incorporation of free BCAAs into tissue proteins provides a nitrogen source for NSCLC expansion (19). BCAT1 is the core metabolic enzyme of BCAA metabolism. Abnormally activated BCAT1 and BCAA metabolism are necessary for tumor progression (20). It is reported that BCAT1-mediated BCAA metabolism facilitates tumor cell behaviors, such as proliferation, invasion, and stem cell self-renewal, thereby accelerating cancer progression (21-23). Our investigation provides crucial mechanistic depth by demonstrating that BCAT1 silencing induces substantial metabolic reprogramming, characterized by the accumulation of BCAAs and α-KG, consistent with the research of Mao et al. (9). The findings highlight the importance of the BCAT1-mediated transamination process in upholding the metabolic stability required for the progression of malignancy.

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent and lipid peroxide-related cell death process. Inducing tumor cell ferroptosis may be a promising strategy for NSCLC metastasis (24). Earlier research has recognized BCAT1 as a regulator of ferroptosis in myocardial ischemia (25), subarachnoid hemorrhage (26), and glioma (27), its specific connection to ferroptosis in the NSCLC-BM process has remained entirely unexplored. This investigation delivers the first experimental confirmation that BCAT1 knockdown sensitizes NSCLC cells to ferroptosis. The findings suggest that BCAT1 may inhibit NSCLC-BM by promoting ferroptosis.

The most innovative aspect of our work lies in connecting BCAT1-mediated BCAA metabolic reprogramming to innate immune signaling. We discovered that BCAT1 knockdown activated the cGAS-STING pathway, and most importantly, that inhibition of this pathway reversed the tumor-suppressive effects of BCAT1 silence. Most studies focus on how this pathway contributes to immune responses against NSCLC (28,29), and also reveal that it also has good anti-cancer value in radiotherapy (30). In addition, we also found that knockdown of BCAT1 reduced the expression of mitochondrial DNA markers in the cytoplasm. Studies have demonstrated that mitochondrial damage releases mitochondrial DNA, thereby activating the cGAS-STING pathway (31,32). Although this is merely indirect evidence in this study, it indicates to some extent that BCAT1 knockdown activates the cGAS-STING pathway by promoting mitochondrial DNA leakage into the cytoplasm.

Importantly, inhibiting NSCLC cell metastasis pertains to the cGAS-STING pathway activation (33). Nevertheless, its relationship with BCAA metabolism remains unclear. Our research reveals the new association between BCAT1 and DNA-sensing mechanisms. We speculate that the BCAA metabolites regulated by BCAT1 may activate this signaling pathway through some mechanism, which will be the focus of further exploration in our subsequent work. Furthermore, reports indicate that regulation of ferroptosis-related factors involves the cGAS-STING pathway, thereby affecting the progression of diseases (34). In NSCLC, Bao et al. (29) have revealed that BIBR1532 triggers the cGAS-STING pathway to initiate ferroptosis. Zhu et al. (35) have revealed that disruption of RRM2 amplifies the ferroptosis induced by the cGAS-STING pathway. Our findings are in agreement with past studies, suggesting that the role of this pathway in NSCLC-BM involves ferroptosis.

In our in vivo studies, the inhibition of BCAT1 was found to decrease NSCLC-BM, which may be related to BCAA metabolism reprogramming and the induction of ferroptosis. These findings underscore the clinical significance of our results, confirming BCAT1 as a viable target for therapy. Moreover, inactivation of the cGAS-STING pathway reversed the inhibition of NSCLC-BM caused by BCAT1 silencing, demonstrating that the adverse effect of BCAT1 on NSCLC-BM partly depends on this pathway inactivation.

However, several limitations need to be acknowledged. In vitro experiments, we only used NSCLC cell lines rather than NSCLC-BM cells. A549 cells cannot fully recapitulate the brain metastatic process due to their relatively low brain tropism. We will prepare the brain-tropic A549-BrM cell subpopulations by establishing a mouse model of brain metastasis, thereby demonstrating the findings of this study. In addition, it remains unknown whether BCAT1 regulates BCAA metabolism in different tissues in the body and thereby affects brain metastasis. Therapeutic strategies targeting BCAT1 still need to be further verified through animal experiments and preclinical models.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our work demonstrates that silencing of BCAT1 inhibits NSCLC-BM by suppressing cell migration, invasion, EMT, and stemness, reprogramming BCAA metabolism, and facilitating ferroptosis. Mechanically, knockdown of BCAT1 activates the cGAS-STING pathway. Our research offers a fresh understanding of the molecular foundation of NSCLC-BM and unveils potential therapeutic avenues for this lethal complication.
