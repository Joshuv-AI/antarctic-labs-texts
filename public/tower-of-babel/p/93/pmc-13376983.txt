Technical and anatomical considerations in aortic stent graft infolding following thoracic endovascular aortic repair
Authors: Mouhammad Halabi, Michael Lee, Hassan Chamseddine, Alexander Shepard, Loay Kabbani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) is the preferred intervention for thoracic aortic pathology, offering favorable outcomes compared with open repair. Aortic graft infolding, although rare with an incidence of 0.4% to 3%, can pose significant clinical challenges. We present three patients who developed infolding post-TEVAR using the Terumo Relay Pro endograft, detailing their presentation, management, and outcomes. This case series, along with a review of the literature, provides insight into aortic endograft infolding after TEVAR and helps refine the risk stratification and management protocols.

Thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) has become the preferred treatment for thoracic aortic aneurysms and dissections because of its favorable outcomes when compared with open surgery1; however, it is not without complications. Complications following TEVAR may include endoleaks, need for reintervention, and stent migration.2 One underrepresented complication following TEVAR is graft infolding.

Graft infolding refers to the inward collapse of the stent graft into the aortic lumen. This has been associated with thrombus formation, luminal stenosis, and impaired blood flow.3,4 It is most commonly attributed to oversizing of the TEVAR graft to the lumen of the aorta or in the inner graft in the context of graft-in-graft deployment. The clinical presentation of graft infolding is variable, ranging from asymptomatic cases identified incidentally on follow-up imaging to other symptomatic presentations such as claudication.3

Despite its significance, infolding remains underrecognized. Literature consists primarily of single case reports or series mostly involving GORE or Cook devices.4, 5, 6 To date, there is limited literature describing infolding involving the Terumo Relay Pro endograft (Terumo Aortic). In this report, we present three cases of aortic graft infolding following TEVAR using the Terumo Relay Pro. We aim to identify possible risk factors and contribute to guiding clinical practice in the detection and management of graft infolding.

Case 1

A 61-year-old man with no significant past medical, surgical, or family history initially presented in 2018 with a type B aortic dissection extending from zone 3 to zone 10, which was managed medically. By 2021, the dissection had progressed, and the aortic diameter grew to 4.5 cm, with extension into zone 2, prompting a staged aortic repair. The first stage of the repair involved a frozen elephant trunk procedure using a 32 × 32 × 95 mm Terumo Relay stent graft, landing distally in zone 4. Eight months later, the patient underwent completion TEVAR. During the TEVAR procedure, a septostomy was performed in zones 4-5, and a 38 × 204 mm Terumo Relay Pro graft was deployed proximally from zone 4, followed by placement of a 46 × 164 mm endograft distally, with the distal seal zone positioned just above the celiac artery in zone 5. A type 1a endoleak was identified intraoperatively but managed conservatively.

Postoperatively, the patient remained hemodynamically stable, requiring transient need for vasopressor support, which was discontinued by postoperative day 2. He was transferred to the surgical intensive care unit and subsequently discharged home. A surveillance computed tomography (CT) angiogram at 1 month revealed proximal graft infolding (Fig 1) associated with a small, nonocclusive thrombus. The patient was offered options of anticoagulation and angioplasty, deciding against surgical intervention. The patient was initiated on rivaroxaban, which was discontinued after 3 months following confirmation of thrombus resolution. At that time, dual antiplatelet therapy was initiated with aspirin and clopidogrel. The patient relocated and was followed by an outside hospital system for several years before being referred back to our vascular surgery group given his complex repair. His repeat CT angiogram, 3 years postoperatively, demonstrated persistent graft infolding. He remained asymptomatic, and continued serial imaging surveillance was recommended.Fig 1One-month surveillance computed tomography angiography showing proximal graft infolding with nonocclusive thrombus in case 1.

Case 2

A 76-year-old woman with a medical history of cerebrovascular accident, myocardial infarction, and peripheral arterial disease and no significant previous surgical history presented for management of a descending thoracic aneurysm. She was an active smoker with no known family history of cardiovascular disease.

She underwent a TEVAR for a descending thoracic aortic aneurysm (covering zones 2-5) using a Terumo Relay Pro 36 × 36 × 150 mm endograft overlapped with 40 × 40 × 150 mm endograft and Aptus screws (Medtronic) at the distal aspect of the graft. Her postoperative course was complicated by a transient ischemic attack (developed transient dysarthria and transient limb weakness), and stroke workup was negative.

Four years after her initial TEVAR, surveillance imaging revealed progression of the paravisceral aortic aneurysm to 6.0 cm and a physician-modified endograft repair was offered. The repair extended the prior Terumo Relay Pro with a 44 × 26 × 190 mm tapered Terumo Relay Pro endograft followed by a modified 32 × 24 × 158 mm Cook TX2 (Cook Medical) endograft with four-vessel fenestrations for the celiac artery, superior mesenteric artery, and both renal arteries. The procedure was complicated by external iliac artery injury requiring open repair. Postoperatively, the patient developed transient slurred speech and bilateral lower extremity weakness. A spinal cord ischemia protocol was initiated, including urgent placement of a spinal drain with mean arterial pressure titration between 85 and 90 mmHg, leading to gradual neurological improvement. The spinal drain was removed on postoperative day 5. She was subsequently transferred to the general surgical ward and discharged in stable condition, with persistent leg weakness.

One-month surveillance CT demonstrated proximal stent graft infolding, in which the Terumo graft had collapsed within the TEVAR graft. This was attributed to graft oversizing and the angulated aortic anatomy (Fig 2). As the patient remained asymptomatic, the infolding was managed conservatively with low-dose aspirin (81 mg daily). Postoperative imaging was negative for any further endoleaks, and the patient was doing well at the last follow-up, 3 months after reintervention.

Case 3

A 47-year-old man with chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, and a history of a type A aortic dissection repair presented with degeneration of his thoracic aorta. His initial dissection was treated with ascending and total arch replacement using a frozen elephant trunk, incorporating fenestration and stenting of the left carotid and subclavian arteries. The frozen elephant trunk was constructed using a Terumo Relay Pro 34 × 100 mm stent graft. He was a former smoker and had no significant family history of cardiovascular disease.

Surveillance imaging 2 years after the frozen elephant trunk repair revealed a descending thoracic aortic aneurysm measuring 6.8 cm in diameter, and TEVAR was planned (Fig 3). To optimize true lumen capacity, a supraceliac septostomy was performed under intravascular ultrasound study and fluoroscopic guidance before stent deployment. A 36 × 199 mm Terumo Relay Pro stent graft was deployed in zone 4 and extended distally into zone 5 with a 42 × 204 mm Terumo Relay Pro device. Discussion

During the study period, a total of 60 TEVAR procedures were performed at our institution, of which three cases of graft infolding were identified, corresponding to an incidence of approximately 5%. Aortic stent graft infolding following TEVAR is a rare yet clinically significant complication. In this case series, all three patients underwent TEVAR using the Terumo Relay Pro device and developed infolding; oversizing ranged from 17% to 20%.

Although infolding is most commonly described in association with GORE or Cook endografts, our findings demonstrate that this complication may also occur with Terumo devices, especially with oversizing of the device. This observation expands the device-specific literature and underscores that infolding is likely a multifactorial phenomenon involving both graft design and patient-specific anatomy.

Despite the emphasis on proper sizing, literature describing infolding remains sparse (Table III), mostly describing infolding with GORE and Cook endografts. We describe our experience with infolding using Terumo endografts. All cases in our series were asymptomatic and identified incidentally on routine imaging, a pattern similarly observed by Appoo et al7 This under-recognition highlights the need for heightened clinical awareness. Oversizing remains a critical factor in endograft performance. Previous experimental work with Cook Zenith TX2 device demonstrated that oversizing beyond 50% resulted in infolding in approximately 20% of cases.8 Similarly, Chiang et al9 evaluated anchoring forces in GORE, Cook, and Medtronic grafts, noting fixation improved with oversizing up to 20%, but did not continue beyond that point. Infolding was not observed at tested oversizing levels but was not assessed beyond 25%. The mechanism of graft infolding can be understood through principles of structural mechanics. When a significantly oversized stent graft is deployed into a vessel or a graft with a smaller diameter, it may fail to fully expand, leading to mechanical infolding.6 Minor cases typically manifest as subtle fabric folds (V-folds), but in more severe mismatches, the surplus material lacks sufficient space to expand, resulting in pronounced inward buckling. This phenomenon is comparable to Euler buckling, a crucial concept in structural engineering, where structures under compressive force lose their cylindrical stability, causing localized infolding.3 In addition, the system naturally seeks to minimize elastic strain energy, and in this context, folding inward becomes the energetically favorable configuration compared with maintaining uniform compression. Finally, the radial force exerted by the oversized graft is unevenly distributed within the smaller lumen, and regions experiencing lower resistance allow the graft fabric to collapse inward, ultimately compromising luminal patency.19 Anatomical tapering and tortuosity further amplify this risk.20

Device-specific limitations may also contribute. The Terumo Relay Pro endograft employs a high-compliance fabric that allows for accommodation of tortuous anatomy; however, these features may come at the expense of radial force, particularly in the mid and distal segments where active fixation is absent. In the setting of excessive oversizing, this reduced radial support and delayed full expansion during the dual sheath deployment process may predispose the graft to infold. Many grafts lack gradient tapering or adaptability across various aortic diameters. In cases where the proximal seal zone is significantly smaller than the distal seal zone, as was observed in our series, the mismatch can predispose to mechanical failure of the graft structure. Although oversizing is typically described in terms of diameter, the relationship between diameter and cross-sectional area is nonlinear. Stepwise diameter increases of approximately 20% correspond to ∼40% to 45% increases in cross-sectional area. Importantly, in larger vessels, this translates to substantially greater absolute area expansion (eg, ∼527 mm2 for a 38→46 mm transition vs ∼138 mm2 for a 20→24 mm transition). This highlights why similar relative diameter changes may have disproportionately greater mechanical and hemodynamic impact in larger aortic segments and may contribute to graft instability or infolding. Accordingly, future graft selection strategies may benefit from incorporating cross-sectional area rather than diameter alone.

The clinical presentation of graft infolding is variable. Although some patients present with acute symptoms such as limb weakness, claudication, or renal dysfunction, others remain asymptomatic, with the infolding detected only incidentally on follow-up imaging.12 Management strategies for aortic infolding depend on symptom status and anatomic severity. Asymptomatic cases may be monitored with serial imaging,14 whereas symptomatic or hemodynamically significant infolding warrants intervention.19

Treatment of graft infolding can be challenging. In our cohort, medical treatment included anticoagulation for intrastent thrombus and close imaging surveillance. Balloon angioplasty may be sufficient in mild cases to apply radial force and re-expand the graft, but this approach is often ineffective when the graft is significantly oversized, as recoil or recurrent infolding may occur. In such cases, placement of a stiffer (high radial force stent) such as Palmaz balloon-expandable stent within the infolded segment can prop open the graft, preventing collapse into the lumen. The stent should match the true luminal diameter and exert sufficient radial force without risking rupture of the underlying vessel or graft.6 Another approach is to reline the infolded stent with appropriately sized stent graft inside the infolded segment to create a new lumen and seal the infolded material against the wall.3 However, when infolding is extensive, this may be insufficient. Finally, open conversion with graft explantation and replacement may be indicated for cases with persistent ischemia, thrombosis, or graft failure not amenable to endovascular correction.

Preventing graft infolding requires accurate vessel sizing and appropriate graft selection. Using cross-sectional imaging and/or intravascular ultrasound study ensures the graft diameter matches the vessel, minimizing excess material. Choosing conformable graft designs and predilating narrowed segments may further reduce the risk of infolding and maintain luminal patency.

Recognizing graft infolding as a potential complication after TEVAR is important for patient outcomes. Given the diverse clinical presentation, routine imaging and close monitoring are crucial for detection and treatment. Future studies should focus on refining risk assessments and management protocols for the safety of endovascular repair of the thoracic aorta.

Conclusions

Aortic graft infolding is a rare but clinically relevant complication following TEVAR, often identified incidentally because of its variable presentation. In this series, graft oversizing emerged as a consistent contributing factor, highlighting the importance of careful device selection and sizing. These findings underscore the need for heightened awareness and routine surveillance, particularly in complex repairs. A more conservative oversizing strategy may help mitigate the risk of this complication, while the use of new custom tapered grafts should alleviate this problem.
