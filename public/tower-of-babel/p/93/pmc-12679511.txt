Successful Revascularization Using Intravascular Ultrasound‐Guided Wiring in Severe Digital Ischemia With Occluded Radial and Ulnar Arteries: A Case Report
Authors: Yasuyuki Tsuchida, Naoki Hayakawa, Toshiki Tsurumaki, Hiromi Miwa, Masaomi Inoue, Shinya Ichihara, Shunichi Kushida
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWith recent advances in endovascular therapy (EVT), treatment of complex peripheral artery disease, especially below‐the‐knee lesions, has become increasingly feasible using intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)‐guided techniques. However, reports on EVT for critical digital ischemia remain scarce because of its rarity. We report a case of successful distal digital revascularization using IVUS‐guided wiring. The patient was a 62‐year‐old man with untreated diabetes who presented with progressive non‐healing ulcers on the right second to fourth fingers. Angiography revealed chronic total occlusions of the right radial and ulnar arteries. IVUS‐guided parallel wiring enabled guidewire passage from the ulnar artery to the common palmar digital arteries of the second and fourth digits. Balloon angioplasty resulted in adequate distal flow and clinical improvement. This case demonstrates the potential of IVUS‐guided EVT in managing complex upper extremity ischemia.

Introduction

Critical digital ischemia (CDI) is a rare but severe condition that often results in marked functional impairment or tissue loss. Although there have been several reports of acute upper limb arterial occlusion due to thromboembolism and vascular injury of the upper extremities caused by trauma, as well as some reports on surgical treatments for these conditions, reports on upper limb ischemia caused by atherosclerosis are limited [1, 2]. Although several reports of endovascular therapy for chronic upper limb ischemia have been published, the number remains limited [3, 4, 5]. Clinically, CDI is characterized by extremely difficult EVT due to an unclear vessel course and small blood vessel sizes (Figure 1). EVT has been widely adopted for lower extremity arterial occlusive disease, and many complex lesions, including long chronic total occlusion (CTO), can be treated using various retrograde approaches and reentry devices [6, 7, 8]. In particular, the development of intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)‐guidance [9, 10, 11, 12, 13] has led to improvements in the success rate of antegrade guidewire (GW) passage and the initial procedural success rate in the below‐the‐knee artery. However, reports on EVT for upper extremity digital ischemia remain limited. This case highlights the feasibility and clinical benefit of IVUS‐guided wiring to achieve successful revascularization in a patient with complex occlusion of both the radial and ulnar arteries.

Case Report

A 62‐year‐old man with a history of untreated diabetes mellitus presented with a 6‐month history of non‐healing ulcers on the distal interphalangeal joint of the right middle finger. The ulcers had expanded progressively to involve the second to fourth digits. Laboratory investigations ruled out collagen vascular diseases. Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography did not reveal marked stenosis of the right brachial artery, but clinical examination showed a poor right radial pulse on palpation. The patient was referred for further evaluation and possible revascularization.

Angiography was performed via right brachial artery puncture ipsilaterally. Imaging revealed hypoplasia of the right radial artery, with distal perfusion supplied via the interosseous artery (Figure 2A). The right ulnar artery (UA) was also occluded, although partial contrast inflow was observed. Therefore, we planned endovascular revascularization through the UA to restore digital perfusion.

A 6‐Fr 10‐cm sheath was inserted via the right brachial artery ipsilaterally. The UA was selected using a 0.014‐inch GW (CROSSLEAD Tracker; Asahi Intec, Aichi, Japan) and a microcatheter (Prominent Advance NEO2; Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan). CTO was encountered and initial wiring with a 0.014‐inch GW (Gladius®; Asahi Intec) was challenging because of poor visualization of the vessel course. Although we tried to perform hydrodynamic contrast recanalization (HDR) [13], the GW could not be advanced.

IVUS with an AnteOwl WR (Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) revealed that the guidewire had deviated into the subintimal space. Therefore, IVUS‐guided parallel wiring was performed (Figure 2B). The IVUS catheter was advanced into the palmar arterial arch, allowing clear visualization of the true lumen despite a vessel diameter of < 2.0 mm. Repeated IVUS‐guided parallel wiring enabled successful passage of a 0.014‐inch GW (Jupiter S6®; Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) from the UA to the common palmar digital artery of the fourth digit. After balloon angioplasty with a 2.0‐mm balloon (Figure 2C), sufficient distal blood flow was achieved (Figure 2D). The patient demonstrated marked clinical improvement, with resolution of fingertip discoloration and alleviation of ischemic pain.

Subsequently, the right third finger was amputated; however, because of poor wound healing of the second and fourth fingers, additional treatment was performed 3 months later. A 6‐Fr 10‐cm sheath was inserted via the right brachial artery, as before. Angiography revealed occlusion from the right UA to the common palmar digital artery of the fourth digit (Figure 3A). The UA was selected using a 0.014‐inch CROSSLEAD Tracker and a Carvel microcatheter (Asahi Intec). The CTO segment was crossed using a Gladius GW. IVUS with an AnteOwl revealed that the guidewire had deviated into a branch of the ulnar artery midway (Figure 3B). The AnteOwl was advanced, and under IVUS guidance, a second Gladius successfully crossed the main trunk of the ulnar artery. Because the wire deviated into the subintimal space at the superficial palmar arch, a Crusade (Kaneka Medix Corporation, Osaka, Japan) double‐lumen catheter was used to deliver a 0.014‐inch GW (Gladius MGES®; Asahi Intec), which successfully advanced into the fourth dorsal digital artery. The artery in the fingertip was dilated using a 1.5‐mm balloon, and the ulnar artery was dilated with a 2.0‐mm balloon. Furthermore, the Gladius successfully crossed the superficial palmar arch and was advanced into the second dorsal digital artery. Following dilation of the entire superficial palmar arch with a 2.0‐mm balloon (Figure 3C), satisfactory angiographic findings were achieved, and the procedure was completed (Figure 3D).

Discussion

Upper extremity digital ischemia is uncommon and presents unique challenges in revascularization because of the small vessel diameter, tortuosity, and complex lesion morphology [14]. In this case, conventional wiring failed to navigate through the CTO segment because of the unclear vessel course. In such cases, a retrograde approach is generally recommended. However, there are cases, such as the present case, in which a retrograde approach is not feasible. HDR is a safe and promising new contrast‐based GW crossing technique in select patients, and its usefulness in cases with an unclear vascular course has been suggested [15, 16]. However, in this case, even with this technique, GW passage was difficult, and HDR also had limitations.

IVUS provided real‐time visualization of the guidewire position relative to the arterial wall and enabled effective parallel wiring to achieve true lumen passage, in our patient. We have previously reported that IVUS‐guided parallel wiring performed on below‐the‐knee CTOs enabled antegrade GW passage even in cases with retrograde difficulty [9, 12, 13]. In this case, we used this technique in a patient with CDI for the first time, and although the vessel diameter was approximately 2.0 mm, the procedure was feasible. This case illustrates that IVUS‐guided techniques, which are used increasingly in lower extremity EVT, can be safely and effectively used for complex upper extremity lesions. There are several technical considerations for IVUS‐guided parallel wiring in distal digital ischemia compared with infrapopliteal intervention. First, since the target vessels are even smaller than those below the knee, establishing a parallel system can be more challenging. During the procedure, interference between the IVUS and the microcatheter system may cause the IVUS catheter to be pulled back when manipulating the microcatheter. Therefore, it is important to accurately identify the re‐entry points using both angiography and IVUS. Another consideration is that, due to the small vessel caliber, the true lumen may appear significantly collapsed. Thus, it is necessary to understand the continuity of the plaque proximally and distally, and to accurately determine the position of the guidewire route. Similar to IVUS‐guided wiring in percutaneous coronary intervention, IVUS‐guided techniques in EVT can involve tip‐detection antegrade dissection reentry (true lumen reentry after subintimal tracking) or tip‐detection intraplaque approaches for reentry [17]. However, it is essential to understand the distribution and characteristics of the plaque that facilitate easier reentry with a GW. Additionally, considering the limited lumen space within a 6‐Fr catheter, the combined use of a microcatheter and the AnteOwl system almost reaches the full capacity of the vessel; therefore, careful manipulation of devices is required. The limitation of this technique is that it cannot be used for lesions that cannot be visualized with IVUS, such as heavily calcified plaque lesions. Further investigation is needed to determine whether this approach is feasible for CDI. However, to our knowledge, no reports have described IVUS‐guided EVT for digital ischemia. Our experience suggests that this approach could expand the treatment options for critical hand ischemia and help avoid major amputation or prolonged disability.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of successful distal digital revascularization using IVUS‐guided wiring in a patient with severe digital ischemia and complete occlusion of the radial and ulnar arteries. IVUS‐guided parallel wiring enabled precise guidewire manipulation through challenging arterial anatomy, resulting in restored perfusion and clinical improvement. This case supports the potential role of IVUS‐guided EVT in the management of complex upper extremity ischemia.
