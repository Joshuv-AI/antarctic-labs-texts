Empowering African professionals to assess palliative care development: regional application of the WHO framework
Authors: Fernanda Bastos, Vilma A Tripodoro, Alvaro Montero, Ana Cristina Béjar, Eduardo Garralda, Virginia Gathoni Gichuru, Anthelme Kouessi Agbodande, Eve Namisango, Emmanuel Luyirika, Carlos Centeno
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe WHO has proposed a structured framework to assess palliative care development through 14 actionable indicators across 6 dimensions. While prior applications have been limited to individual countries, this study explores the feasibility of empowering professionals across an entire region to independently evaluate palliative care development using the WHO framework.MethodsAn expert-informed, participatory implementation study was conducted across the African continent. A structured survey based on the WHO indicators was developed and translated into multiple languages. A regional network of national consultants was established, and participants were trained through an asynchronous, tutored online course. Data collection involved self-assessment of national palliative care development, accompanied by narrative justifications and documentary evidence. Validation processes included peer review and, where feasible, endorsement by national associations or authorities. Empowerment was assessed through course completion, perceived impact via post-course surveys and participation in data validation.ResultsNational-level data from 50 African countries were included, based on 121 contributors. Most countries had at least two contributors, ensuring data triangulation. Validation processes were completed in 48 countries, with 21 reports endorsed by national associations or authorities. Post-course surveys indicated that 91% of respondents rated the course highly for professional value, and 95% expressed interest in ongoing collaboration. The data collected are already informing global comparative analyses, including the African Palliative Care Atlas (2025 edition).ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that with appropriate training and support, professionals across an entire region can independently assess palliative care development using the WHO framework. The approach fosters professional empowerment and provides high-quality, context-rich data, laying the groundwork for similar initiatives in other regions and contributing to a coordinated global assessment of palliative care development.Keywords: Health policies and all other topics, Health education and promotion, Public Health, Global Health, Africa South of the Sahara

Introduction

The development of palliative care (PC) has been defined by the WHO as a structured and progressive process aimed at integrating PC into national health systems in an accessible, sustainable and quality-assured manner.1 In its 2021 technical report Assessing the Development of Palliative Care Worldwide: A Set of Actionable Indicators, the WHO presents a conceptual framework symbolised by the image of a house. This model identifies six essential building blocks for PC development: empowered people and communities, strong health policies, dedicated research, availability of essential medicines, education and training, and the provision of integrated services.

Before the publication of the WHO’s ‘house’ model, the development of PC was primarily assessed through descriptive and comparative approaches.2 Key global initiatives included the Palliative Care Atlases developed by the ATLANTES Global Observatory, the multiple editions of the World Map of Palliative Care Development led by David Clark and collaborators, and The Economist’s Quality of Death Index, which offered a benchmarking tool based on expert perceptions and national indicators.3,7 These efforts provided valuable insights and helped raise awareness, but often relied on limited data sources and lacked a unified conceptual framework.

With the introduction of the WHO’s model, a new, structured approach to evaluating PC development was proposed. This model is accompanied by a set of 14 actionable indicators, grouped across 6 key dimensions.1 These indicators provide countries with a practical framework to identify gaps and monitor progress in a consistent and comparable way. The feasibility of this model has been demonstrated through pilot implementations in Benin,8 Morocco9 and Uruguay,10 where national workshops facilitated data collection, local validation and capacity-building processes. The experience and lessons learnt from these country assessments have been published and support the value of this methodology.11 12

Since 2022, the ATLANTES Global Observatory of Palliative Care at the University of Navarra has been designated as a WHO Collaborating Centre for the Global Monitoring of Palliative Care Development. As part of its official responsibilities, the Centre supports the WHO in advancing global strategies for the integration of PC within health systems. A core aspect of this mandate is to promote the dissemination and adoption of the WHO technical framework for evaluating PC development, including training, capacity building and methodological guidance to adapt the model across diverse regional and national contexts.

Until now, the WHO’s indicator-based framework has demonstrated its feasibility when implemented in individual countries through coordinated assessment projects.8,12 An important issue is whether this model can be applied independently by professionals within their own countries, without direct external oversight. Emerging evidence suggests that this is indeed possible. Several countries have already undertaken self-led or locally guided evaluations based on the WHO indicator framework or closely related methodologies. Notable examples include India (applying the WHO core indicators),13 Colombia (with a locally adapted version)14 and Mexico (with a focus on paediatric PC).15 Such experiences illustrate the growing capacity of professionals and national teams to carry out comprehensive assessments of PC development using structured frameworks.

As a WHO Collaborating Centre, we also considered whether it would be feasible to empower an entire region—its PC professionals and local leaders—to independently assess the development of PC in their own countries, simultaneously and using a common methodology. Such an approach would allow for meaningful comparisons between countries within the region and serve as a catalyst for collective development. Furthermore, if regional empowerment proved feasible, could we envision a coordinated global assessment—conducted either simultaneously or in close succession across different regions of the world? This vision aligns with recent calls for structural and regionally grounded responses to global inequities in PC, including investment in local leadership and decentralised monitoring mechanisms.16 It is this possibility that frames the present study. If achievable, it would represent a significant milestone: it would be possible to generate a global snapshot of PC development based for the first time on a validated methodology, locally implemented by professionals, at a specific moment in history.

To explore the feasibility of empowering an entire region to carry out a coordinated self-assessment of PC development, we have designed a structured study to test this approach in practice. The African continent was selected as the pilot region for this initiative, given its diversity of health systems, growing momentum in PC advocacy and the strategic support of regional and international partners. The aim of this study is to assess whether professionals across multiple African countries can be trained, supported and equipped to evaluate the development of PC in their respective national contexts using the WHO indicator framework. This article presents the conceptual basis, methodological design, initial steps and empowerment analysis of this regional pilot, intended as a proof-of-concept for a potential global strategy of regionally led assessments.

This study forms part of the Global Survey led by the ATLANTES Observatory to assess PC development worldwide.17 The African pilot offers an in-depth analysis of its training and data collection components. Descriptive findings will appear in a series of regional Atlases, and a full comparative analysis will be included in the 2025 World Map of Palliative Care Development.

Methods

This expert-informed, participatory implementation study combined capacity-building, data collection, collaborative analysis and the assessment of professional empowerment. National PC professionals were engaged as country-level informants to evaluate the development of PC using the WHO’s indicator-based framework. The project included five main components: (1) development of a structured survey based on WHO indicators; (2) creation of a regional informant network; (3) delivery of an online course for training and data collection; (4) a participatory data analysis and validation process involving peer review and (5) a course evaluation and empowerment criteria. The African region was selected as a pilot setting.

Indicator framework and survey instrument

The WHO framework’s 14 indicators, grouped across 6 dimensions, were adapted into a structured survey. Some indicators (eg, national policy) included subquestions. Each item required participants to select one of four levels of development of PC (from 1=Emerging to 4=Advanced) and justify their responses with narrative comments and documentary evidence.

The tool was initially developed as a paper-based survey and later digitised. It was offered in English, French and Spanish, and professionally translated into Portuguese and Arabic. All versions were reviewed by native-speaking PC professionals to ensure conceptual accuracy. The finalised instrument, previously piloted in Benin,8 Morocco9 and Uruguay,10 formed the backbone of both the course and the data collection process (see online supplemental material 1).

Creation of a regional informant network

Between December 2023 and January 2024, a regional network of national informants was established to support country-level assessments. Recruitment combined formal invitations to professionals from the ATLANTES network—particularly those involved in the 2017 African PC Atlas—with nominations from partners including the Association pour le développement des soins palliatifs en Afrique, the Fédération Francophone Internationale de Soins Palliatifs, the African Palliative Care Association (APCA), WHO, the World Hospice and Palliative Care Alliance (WHPCA) and the International Association of Hospice and PC (IAHPC). Additional outreach was conducted via social media and international association websites.

Eligible informants were ideally expected to meet at least two of six predefined criteria: 5 or more years of experience as a PC professional; recognition as a national leader of PC by an international association; prior participation in Atlas projects; active membership on the board of PC organisations; highly interested in PC development and authorship of publications specifically on PC. However, in recognition of the uneven distribution of expertise and the importance of capacity building, some contributors with fewer formal qualifications were also included, particularly in countries where PC development remains at the lowest levels globally. The study aimed to include a minimum of two qualified informants per country to ensure data triangulation and internal validation. Incentives included an accredited certificate from the University of Navarra and a 1-year membership to IAHPC and WHPCA.

Online course: development, structure and data collection

An asynchronous, tutored online course was developed both as a training tool and as the primary mechanism for collecting national-level data on PC development. Hosted on the NeoLMS platform and accredited by the University of Navarra (25 European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System [ECTS] hours), the course was available in English, French and Spanish. Tutors were accessible via the platform and email, with personalised support offered in Portuguese, Spanish, English and French.

The course consisted of seven units. Unit 1 provided a didactic guide outlining objectives, methodology, structure and key dates. Unit 2 formalised enrolment and collected personal data for certification. Units 3–6 introduced the WHO conceptual framework and its six dimensions—empowered people and communities, health policies, research, education and training, essential medicines and service provision—through short videos and structured texts. In unit 3, participants also completed an expertise profile. Units 4–6 involved the application of the framework: the WHO survey was transformed into an interactive, web-based questionnaire embedded in the course. Participants used it to assess each indicator based on their country’s situation, selecting a development level (1–4) and providing documentary justification. Unit 7 presented the national report from Benin as a real-world example of the framework’s implementation.

Rather than serving only as a technical support tool, the course was designed as a self-contained and replicable instrument to test the feasibility of this assessment method at regional level, beginning with the African continent.

Participatory data analysis and validation

All contributions were subject to the same validation process, irrespective of contributors’ profiles. Data from each country were compiled and reconciled into a single consolidated national draft report, integrating all inputs and cross-referencing them with relevant literature.18 As part of the validation step, these reports were then returned to the original contributors for blinded peer review. Each expert independently assessed the consolidated report, and a consensus was reached through iterative feedback.

Validated reports were included in the regional analysis. When feasible, they were submitted for review by national PC associations or, in two specific cases of countries without such associations, by external experts appointed by APCA. In the specific instances of Benin and Morocco, the reports were also endorsed by national health authorities. While these endorsements added legitimacy, they were not required for inclusion.

Course evaluation and empowerment criteria

Empowerment was considered present when a participant met at least one of the following criteria:

Completion of data submission: Full completion of all survey modules within the course, including justifications and supporting evidence, on which participants were awarded a certificate as national consultants.

Perceived empowerment through course experience: On completing the course, participants were invited to respond to an anonymous online survey aimed at evaluating both the course and its impact on their professional perspective. This 15-item questionnaire was developed by the research team, informed by previous training evaluation tools and empowerment dimensions relevant to this project. Reviewed by course tutors for clarity, it was designed as a descriptive rather than a formally validated psychometric instrument, to capture participants’ feedback and perceived empowerment. This questionnaire included closed questions on participants’ motivations, learning outcomes, perceived difficulties and interest in future collaboration, as well as rating scales to assess their personal experience and perceived gains. An open-ended item at the end invited comments and suggestions for improvement. Participation in the survey was voluntary and had no bearing on course certification. Patient and public involvement

This ecological study did not involve patients or the general public. However, the intended end users of the findings—PC professionals—were actively involved throughout the research process. African professionals contributed to the development of the multilingual training platform, completed the national assessments, participated in peer validation, and in several cases facilitated institutional endorsement. A few also contributed as co-authors to this manuscript.

Results

This regional pilot was designed not only to collect national data, but to explore whether professionals—when appropriately trained, supported and equipped—could take an active role in assessing PC development in their own countries. The following results reflect the extent to which this process contributed to strengthening professional engagement and fostering a sense of empowerment across the African region. Empowerment through full completion of the evaluation process (criterion A)

A total of 121 consultants from 50 countries provided national-level data for this study, covering all countries in the region except Algeria, Eritrea, Liberia and Mauritius. Of these, 102 completed the full online course and received certification as national consultants. Eleven submitted the assessment via a Word-based instrument, and 8 provided data via the endorsement process or through prior pilot assessments in Benin and Morocco. All data contributions were accepted as valid, enabling broad regional coverage. In fact, the information compiled in each country report is already serving as the foundation for more in-depth global comparative analyses, including a new iteration of the World Map of Palliative Care Development, regional Atlases, such as the African Palliative Care Atlas.

Most countries had at least two contributors, as intended to ensure internal triangulation. The group of 113 professionals forms a diverse and qualified regional network. Demographic data were possible to be obtained through the course registration, reflecting 102 contributors. The majority were female (56%), with a mean age of 46 years (range 21–80). Regarding institutional roles, most were clinicians (43%), followed by institutional leaders (33%), educators (16%) and researchers (12%), with many fulfilling more than one function. Other profiles included nurses, social workers, chaplains and health administrators.

Each contributor was assigned an expertise score based on six predefined criteria. The most frequently met were: a strong interest in PC development (85%), more than 5 years of experience (61%) and recognition by an international association as a national PC leader (46%). The median qualification score across all contributors was 3. 30 contributors did not meet at least two criteria, but their inclusion was retained given the project’s emphasis on capacity building. In all countries except Chad, Côte d’Ivoire, Comoros, Djibouti, Madagascar, Somalia and South Sudan, there were also other contributors with higher qualification scores. In these exceptional countries, participants held roles within the Ministry of Health, except in South Sudan where the contributor represented WHO. With the exception of Côte d’Ivoire and Madagascar, these countries are consistently classified among the lowest levels of PC development in successive global mappings, and their perspectives were therefore considered critical despite limited prior qualifications. Empowerment perceived by participants (criterion B)

To explore participants’ perceived empowerment, a final online evaluation survey was shared with certified consultants. A total of 57/102 responses (55%) were received. The most common way participants heard about the course was through direct invitations from ATLANTES (42%), followed by referrals from colleagues (30%). Their primary motivations included contributing to the national evaluation of PC (88%), advancing education (58%) and relevance to their professional role (47%). Accreditation by the University of Navarra was also an important factor, cited by 39% of respondents.

The majority of participants reported a significant increase in knowledge, skills and strategic awareness related to PC development. Many highlighted the course as transformative for their professional outlook:

The Course is essential to identify specific strategies to implement palliative care. All coordinators/managers of palliative care programs should take this course. (P3)

After this course I feel well equipped to propose reforms in my day-to-day work on palliative care. (P21)

The course itself has also helped a lot as to gaining experiences and as to be able to run our community-based palliative care project. (P33)

Survey responses revealed that 58% strongly agreed the course increased their awareness of national PC data; 65% felt it helped them identify system-level gaps and 72% acknowledged gaining a strategic understanding of how data supports public health planning.

Although 74% found the course accessible, 40% reported it required more time than expected. While 81% felt the content was sufficient, participants suggested future improvements such as adding forums (67%) and live webinars (72%). One participant noted:

The course could be interactive, i.e., live to allow participants to ask questions on the themes. (P20)

Notably, 91% of participants rated the course 8–10 out of 10 for its professional value, and 95% expressed interest in joining a global network of PC consultants. Open comments consistently emphasised the course’s empowering effect and the participants’ desire for continued collaboration, while also suggesting improvements such as greater interactivity, clearer instructions and timelines, additional support materials, and opportunities for networking and ongoing training.

Empowerment demonstrated through participation in validation and analysis (criterion C)

Following data submission, all country contributions were consolidated into a reconciled national report, combining consultant inputs and relevant documentary sources. These drafts were then returned to the original contributors for blinded peer validation within their country. Between October 2024 and February 2025, 48 country reports were submitted for review, and 84 validation responses were received (74% of possible respondents), providing feedback, corrections and confirmations.

A total of 46 countries completed the validation process, meaning that at least one contributor in each of these countries reviewed and confirmed the consolidated national report. In two countries, no contributor returned the validation, and these were recorded as non-validated.

Among the 22 national PC associations currently active in the region, 19 have formally endorsed their country’s final report to date. In addition, two countries without such associations (Lesotho and Eswatini), external validators appointed by the APCA reviewed and endorsed the reports.

Benin and Morocco were assessed prior to the course through WHO-supported pilots, with participation from national contributors and official endorsement by both PC associations and health authorities. Their inclusion completes the final count of 50 evaluated countries, of which 48 were validated.

Discussion

This regional pilot provides the first demonstration that professionals across an entire continent can be equipped and supported to assess the development of PC in their own countries using a common framework. This operationalises recent recommendations that call for decentralised, regionally led strategies to address structural disparities in PC development globally.16 It confirms the feasibility of regional empowerment in evaluation, with the potential to inform global monitoring strategies that are participatory, scalable and inclusive.

The findings confirm that many African PC specialists—despite being relatively few in number, heavily burdened with clinical responsibilities and often limited by unreliable internet access—responded positively to the flexible, self-paced format of the online training course. This strategy, which aimed to accommodate local constraints while expanding access, proved feasible and widely appreciated. Similar to previous findings on the importance of context-sensitive, short-format training for African professionals,19 this experience underscores the value of tailored educational approaches. Nonetheless, several participants expressed a preference for more interactive learning, such as direct engagement with tutors or synchronous sessions. These suggestions indicate that future iterations of the course may benefit from hybrid or blended designs that combine flexibility with opportunities for real-time exchange.

Interestingly, not all data contributions occurred through the formal course platform. Eleven contributors chose to complete the survey outside the course, using a Word-based version. This reveals the importance of offering alternative channels for engagement, especially in diverse technological and professional environments.

The initiative also sparked a demand for continued collaboration. Many participants expressed interest in remaining involved in future activities and in forming a regional network of PC consultants. This suggests that the evaluation process itself became a catalyst for professional connection and regional identity—a subtle but meaningful dimension of empowerment.

While peer validation between contributors was achieved in most countries, formal endorsement by national association or authorities was not always possible. More than half of African countries do not yet have a national PC association, and many professionals lack direct access to government authorities. In this context, endorsement—although desirable—should not be viewed as a strict requirement for legitimacy, but rather as an aspirational outcome dependent on local structures.

The study raises important questions about sustainability. A key issue is whether this sense of empowerment will translate into future national or regional initiatives led from within the region. It is also important to consider whether African PC professionals could organise a second cycle of assessment without the support of a WHO Collaborating Centre. These are critical reflections as we consider whether future global monitoring efforts should be led by international teams or entrusted to empowered regional actors.

The accessibility and zero-cost nature of the course also contributed to its reach. Offering the training freely, in multiple languages and in a flexible format made it widely available. In doing so, it also served a broader purpose: to democratise knowledge on how to evaluate PC development and spread capacity-building across the region.

This study gathered data from 121 contributors across 50 African countries, achieving broader regional coverage than previous efforts such as the APCA Atlas of PC in Africa, which collected data from 48 countries and relied on 60 informants overall.20 21 In contrast, the present initiative not only increased the number of countries with validated information but also significantly expanded the pool of contributors, with more than double the number of national experts. Moreover, the quality of the data was enhanced through systematic justifications and narrative explanations for each indicator, allowing for more precise and contextualised national assessments. This methodological improvement strengthens the reliability of the findings and positions the data as a foundation for future regional or global comparative efforts, including the next iteration of the World Map of Palliative Care Development and the African Palliative Care Atlas (2025 edition),launched at the APCA Congress in Botswana, with Health Ministers from 11 African countries and experts from across the continent.

This study relied on the voluntary participation of national experts, selected through predefined criteria rather than probabilistic sampling. While this approach ensured informed contributions and wide regional coverage, it may have limited the diversity of perspectives in countries with low participation or weak PC structures. The online format may have excluded some professionals due to connectivity or technological barriers. Not all countries achieved ideal levels of informant triangulation or validation, and endorsement was not universally feasible. These factors should be considered when interpreting the results or applying the model to other settings.

Globally, the experience provides strong evidence that region-wide empowerment is achievable. The African pilot lays the groundwork for similar processes in other regions, and eventually for a coordinated global snapshot of PC development—grounded in local leadership, standardised methodology and shared ownership.
