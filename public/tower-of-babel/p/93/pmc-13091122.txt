Investigating fine-tuning versus zero-shot learning for general large language models when predicting cancer survival from initial oncology consultation documents
Authors: T Phaterpekar, Z Zeng, Y Mali, B Leung, C Ho, RT Ng, AT Bates, J-J Nunez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnstructured oncology consultation notes contain rich clinical information that may support survival prediction. Open-weight large language models (LLMs) can utilize these notes with zero-shot inference or fine-tuning, but their relative value for this setting remains unclear. The objective of this study is to evaluate open-weight LLMs for predicting 60-month survival from initial oncology consultation notes, comparing (i) zero-shot performance, (ii) performance after fine-tuning, and (iii) smaller natural language processing models trained on the same dataset in prior work.Materials and methodsWe used Meta’s Llama models to predict patients’ 60-month survival using oncology consultation notes from a dataset of 59 800 patients. We tested both zero-shot and fine-tuning approaches. Metrics included balanced accuracy (BA) and weighted F1.ResultsZero-shot performance was limited. Llama-2-13B performed best among the zero-shot configurations (average performance across prompts: BA 0.596, weighted F1 0.644; performance on Prompt 4: BA 0.766, weighted F1 0.802). Fine-tuning improved performance across models: Llama-2-13B achieved BA 0.842, weighted F1 0.846, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) 0.905; Llama-2-7B achieved BA 0.840, weighted F1 0.843, AUC 0.911; Llama-3.1-8B achieved BA 0.829, weighted F1 0.829, AUC 0.881. Performance was numerically similar to smaller models trained on the same task and data.ConclusionsFor predicting 60-month survival from initial oncology consultation documents, fine-tuning open-weight LLMs meaningfully improves performance compared with zero-shot use, but does not consistently outperform smaller language models. This may suggest that both fine-tuned LLMs and smaller models merit continued investigation, with the most appropriate approach likely to depend on the outcome of interest, clinical context, and practical considerations such as hardware, privacy, and deployment feasibility.Key words: machine learning, LLM, cancer survival, oncology support, prognosis

Introduction

Cancer remains the leading cause of death in Canada and represents a large burden on both patients and the health care system.1,2 While the age-standardized incidence rate of some common cancers is decreasing, projections for 2028-2032 suggest an increase in the annual number of new cancer cases, predominantly influenced by an aging population,3,4 necessitating the importance of work in this area.

Prognostic factors are a critical aspect of oncology, significantly influencing treatment planning, patient counseling, and overall management strategies.5 Predicting prognosis is difficult for clinicians,6 paving the way for exploration of machine learning approaches to improve accuracy in predicting cancer susceptibility, recurrence, and mortality.7 Initial work on prognostic models utilized structured clinical data, tumor characteristics, gene expression, and imaging, to allow for more personalized predictions.7,8 Despite their promise, implementing these models in clinical practice can be technically demanding as there is often a mismatch9 between the structured data captured at different institutions and the specific variables required by published prognostic models, requiring substantial data preprocessing before the models can be deployed.9

Recent advancements in natural language processing (NLP) have opened new avenues for leveraging unstructured text data in clinical practice. In a recent review, prognostic machine learning models based on unstructured text were demonstrated to perform equivalently to those using structured data in various medical settings.10 The performance of text-based models was posited to depend on how different fields utilize text to embed information.

Initial oncologist consultations provide a rich source of unstructured text data and typically include detailed descriptions of the patient’s medical history, physical examination findings, psychosocial aspects, and patient-reported symptoms. Cancer-specific prognosis prediction using unstructured text has been explored in the form of radiological reports,11 clinical texts,12,13 and oncology-specific consultation notes.14

Some of this work has started to investigate the use of large language models (LLMs)—NLP models pretrained on billions of words—to leverage deep learning to learn not just complex representations of language but also to acquire broad general world knowledge hidden in the data. LLMs have demonstrated capabilities in tackling tasks that require non-trivial logical reasoning, such as being able to pass the United States Medical Licensing Examinations.15

LLMs have demonstrated zero-shot (the ability to carry out new tasks without task-specific examples) performance on a range of general tasks that previously required specialized NLP models. Several studies have explored this potential in classification settings.16, 17, 18, 19 However, zero-shot performance is usually reported with proprietary LLMs (e.g. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini).20,21 Their use in clinical and scientific contexts remains limited due to data privacy concerns, reproducibility challenges, and lack of access to model weights.22 In contrast, open-weight LLMs (models whose trained parameters are publicly released) are emerging as competitive alternatives,23 offering greater transparency and the ability to be hosted securely in on-premise environments without exposing sensitive health data.

Despite this promise, few studies have investigated the predictive capacity of open-weight LLMs in oncology, including the comparison of zero-shot configurations with fine-tuning. To date, one study has demonstrated the feasibility of zero-shot information extraction from radiology reports,24 while another has shown that fine-tuned Llama-2-7B could model cancer progression across five cancer types, outperforming ChatGPT.25 To our knowledge, no work has applied these models to oncology consultation notes. These documents are longer and more complex than radiology reports, but they contain detailed clinical, psychosocial, and patient-reported information that may support important tasks like survival prediction14 or the psychosocial needs of patients with cancer.26

In this study, we evaluate the ability of open-weight LLMs to predict 60-month survival outcomes from patients’ initial oncology consultation notes. We pursued three objectives: firstly, to test these models in a zero-shot setting to assess their baseline capacity; secondly, to fine-tune them for task-specific adaptation; and thirdly, to compare their performance with previously published NLP models trained on the same dataset, including smaller architectures such as bag of words (BoW) and convolutional neural networks (CNNs).14 By addressing these objectives, we aim to investigate whether the additional computational and resource costs of LLMs translate into gains in oncology survival prediction when using oncology consultation documents, and to inform future research considering how best to leverage open-weight LLMs for predictive modeling in clinical oncology applications.

Materials and methods

Data

We used a dataset of 59 800 patients who began cancer care between 1 April 2011 and 30 December 2016, as used in previous work.14,26 Data were provided after receiving ethics approval from the University of British Columbia—BC Cancer Research Ethics Board (H17-03309). The dataset was filtered before being used for experiments, yielding a final cohort of 47 625 patients. Figure 1 presents the characteristics of the filtered dataset, including cancer stage, gender, and mean age at diagnosis, and shows the estimated distribution of cancer types in the original cohort before filtering due to data availability constraints. For this cohort, BC Cancer provided all radiotherapy for malignant disease and for non-malignant or precancerous findings that required specialist cancer care. BC Cancer comprises 6 cancer centers and 53 community oncology network sites across the province, affiliated with ∼85% of working medical oncologists in BC and almost all radiation oncologists.

Data selection and preprocessing

The study included patients who had at least one medical or radiation oncology consultation within 180 days of diagnosis, using the document closest to the diagnosis date. Patients who were recorded as obtaining care for more than one cancer were excluded. Each sample in this dataset contains two components: consultation notes and patient outcome label. Consultation notes are free-text clinical narratives written by health care staff, with an average length of 868 words. The notes may include various sections such as allergies, past medical history, social history, examination, assessment, current medications, and other relevant information. Because the notes come from different clinicians and institutions, the structure, wording, and included keywords vary across samples. Patient outcome label (survival) was computed with the patient’s death date obtained from Provincial Vital Statistics as of 6 April 2022. The 60-month survival status was calculated from the date of the initial consultation note and represented as a binary label: 1 = survived at least 60 months, 0 = did not survive 60 months.

For consultation notes, we removed automatically added text at the beginning and end of documents, which included identifying information, dates and names of dictating providers, and otherwise conducted minimal preprocessing, to retain the original nature of the unstructured document and to simulate a clinical model in practice. The dataset was randomly split into training (70%), development (10%), and testing (20%) sets. Data preparation involved using Hugging Face’s AutoTokenizer to load the tokenizer associated with the selected LLM, with padding set to the left and truncation to a maximum token length specified in the configuration. The consultation notes text was appended with specified prompts and then sent to model for prediction.

Selection of LLMs

We selected Meta’s open-weight LLMs from the Llama family, initially investigating Llama2 and then also investigating Llama3 models when available, which have demonstrated competitiveness with other existing open-source and proprietary models.23,27 We accessed these models through the HuggingFace Transformers library.28 Given our hardware resources (1 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU) and due to privacy concerns around using clinical documents with cloud providers that we expect other health care providers and researchers may face, we ran the experiments locally. On the task of zero-shot and fine-tuning, we used the Llama-2-7B, Llama-2-13B, Llama-3-8B, and Llama-3.1-8B models. We also ran the zero-shot experiment on subversion of these models, including Llama-2-7B-Chat, Llama-2-13B-Chat, Llama-3-8B-Instruct, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. We then compared our results against those from our recent study which used trained BoW and CNN models.

Experiments and evaluation

Predicting survival using zero-shot learning

For the zero-shot tasks, the Llama models were used without any additional changes, with 8-bit quantization through the BitsAndBytes library for more efficient model loading and inference.29 Studies have shown that 8-bit quantization can preserve model accuracy for long-context tasks (with a reduction of ∼0.8%).30

The models were prompted to output ‘yes’ or ‘no’ to carry out the classification tasks on the hold-out test set. Inputs were limited to 4096 tokens—the model’s context length limitations—with end-based truncation for the few consultation notes that exceeded that limit. The outlines package was used to guide structured text generated responses from the models, using greedy sampling. In cases where the outlines package failed, binary answers were manually extracted. In both scenarios, greedy sampling and decoding were utilized. In greedy decoding, at each step, the model selects the token with the highest probability, resulting in deterministic and consistent outputs. We planned to drop examples if both techniques did not produce a viable result, but all experiments in the actual study produced viable results.

Given that the outputs of generative LLMs are often highly sensitive to the prompts provided, we investigated the impact of different prompts on the model’s zero-shot performance. Five prompts were created (Table 1), using inspiration from a previous study.16 The prompts have differing levels of complexity and provide explicit binary classification instructions. The prompts vary with regard to length, specifying a medical role, defining prognostic factors, as well as how the model is asked to approach the prompt (i.e. ‘review’ versus ‘comprehensive review’). Finding that these a priori prompts resulted in only one class prediction for Llama-3 models, we experimented with other prompts until achieving one that yielded actual predictions.

Predicting survival using fine-tuning

Since the original Llama models were designed and trained for text generation, we configured the LLMs using the AutoModelForSequenceClassification function. This removed its Language Modeling head for text generation and added a linear layer for classification. Based on this setup, we conducted supervised fine-tuning on the Llama models. Input tokens were limited to the model’s 4096-token context window. Due to GPU memory constraints, we trained the models with a batch size of 1 for Llama-2-13B, and batch size of 2 for Llama-2-7B and Llama-3.1-8B. Model weights were updated per batch using the Adam8bit Optimizer.31 Because of the small batch size during training, we experimented with learning rates of 0.00001, 0.00002, and 0.00005 to reduce the effect of gradient noise.

The models were fine-tuned on the training dataset, and the development set was used to monitor model performance and prevent overfitting during the training process. Our fine-tuning adopted an EarlyStopping strategy, terminating training early if no improvement in model’s performance was observed on the development set. The fine-tuned model achieving the highest validation performance was selected for final evaluation on the hold-out test set. More details about training can be found in Supplementary Material S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100703.

Calibration

We employed Temperature Scaling32 to calibrate the model, ensuring that the model’s prediction confidence more accurately reflects the true classification accuracy. We conducted calibration analysis on the calibrated model. The calibration curve can be found in Figure 2. The fine-tuned Llama-2-13B model achieves the best calibration performance, with a Brier score of 0.118 and an expected calibration error (ECE) (10-bin) of 0.063, demonstrating superior reliability compared with the Llama 3.1 8B (Brier score: 0.134, ECE: 0.098) and Llama 7B (Brier score: 0.122, ECE: 0.079) variants.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were carried out using the scikit-learn library in Python. For the evaluation of our fine-tuned models, several statistical metrics were calculated to assess performance on binary classification tasks.

To evaluate model performance, we recognized the critical importance of correctly identifying both survival and non-survival outcomes. Consequently, we employed metrics robust to class imbalance: the weighted F1-score and balanced accuracy. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was also used to assess the model’s overall discriminative power. For all three metrics, a score approaching 1.0 indicates superior classification performance, while an AUC of 0.5 and a balanced accuracy of 0.5 represent a performance equivalent to random chance. For the zero-shot experiments, AUC was not calculated as generated responses were in pure binary with no threshold of uncertainty.

Results

Performance of models using zero-shot learning

As shown in Figure 3, the overall model zero-shot performance on this task was limited. Llama2-13B demonstrated better performance compared with other variants, with a weighted F1 score of 0.644 and a balanced accuracy of 0.596 averaged across five prompts. The 7B models show strong classification bias, with sensitivity scores approaching 1.0 and considerably lower precision (0.662-0.677), indicating a strong tendency to predict positive class across all five prompts. Figure 4 shows notable variance in the 13B model’s performance across different prompts tested. Prompt 4 yielded the highest scores (weighted F1 0.802, balanced accuracy 0.766). Prompt 5, the longest in length, produced the second-best results. Prompt 3, which included the explicit phrases ‘clearly indicates’ and ‘no clear evidence’, resulted in one of the lower performance levels.

The Llama3-8B and Llama3.1-8B models show severe classification bias, with sensitivity scores equal to 1.0. Across five different prompt variations, these models almost always defaulted to a single-class prediction (see Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100703), classifying almost all patient examples into the positive class (‘Yes’). Llama3 families barely generate any negative class predictions. All tested models achieved a weighted F1-score of 0.527, with balanced accuracy recorded at precisely 0.50. A further zero-shot experiment using an additional prompt was conducted on Llama3-8B and Llama3-8B-Instruct. With this prompt, the models achieved a weighted F1-score of 0.784 and a balanced accuracy of 0.748, indicating a significant enhancement in their predictive capabilities.

Comparing LLMs with fine-tuned and zero-shot learning with smaller models

Fine-tuning the Llama-2-7B, Llama-2-13B and Llama-3.1-8B resulted in performance with numerically higher balanced accuracy, F1-score, and AUC than the zero-shot configuration (Figure 5). Numerical results were similar, when comparing the fine-tuned LLMs to the best performing models in our prior study, BoW and CNN,14 for predicting the same outcomes with the same dataset (Figure 5).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the potential of open-weight general-purpose LLMs, specifically Meta’s Llama models, to predict 60-month survival outcomes for patients with cancer from their initial oncologist consultation notes. Firstly, we assessed their zero-shot performance, finding numerically similar performance between the generation and parameter count of these general-purpose models. Secondly, we tested whether fine-tuning could improve performance and observed consistent gains across all models. Thirdly, we compared these results to a recent study that used smaller NLP models on the same data and task, noting numerically similar performance to fine-tuned LLMs. Taken together, these findings suggest several implications for future efforts to apply LLMs to predictive tasks in oncology.

For future efforts relying on zero-shot learning, our findings suggest that newer or larger general-purpose models may not necessarily yield better performance. Despite the generally improved capabilities of the Llama 3 architecture on broad benchmarks,33 it failed to produce meaningful predictions in this setting, consistently assigning all cases to a single class, even though Llama 2-13B was able to work with a few designed prompts. We do not interpret this as a generalized conclusion about model size and performance, since results are highly dependent on the prompts used and are likely to vary with different model versions, outcomes, and datasets. Rather, our results highlight the importance of testing a range of models, including different generations within a family and multiple parameter sizes, rather than assuming that larger or more recent models will perform better. Future work could extend this line of inquiry by systematically exploring the effect of prompts on model’s performance.

For those considering fine-tuning, our results showed performance gains against using the models in zero-shot. The fine-tuned models outperformed their corresponding original model with the zero-shot configuration by 0.294 in balanced accuracy on average, in part due to the relatively weak performance of most zero-shot models on the task. The fine-tuned Llama2-13B outperformed the best zero-shot configuration (Llama2-13B with prompt 4) by 0.076. We did not observe consistent trends in fine-tuned performance when comparing Llama 2 and Llama 3 or across model sizes, suggesting that gains from fine-tuning may be relatively stable across architectures within this range. Future work could extend this investigation to larger, smaller, or domain-specific models. In addition, we plan to examine how much data is required for effective fine-tuning, and whether this threshold varies between models, as this may have impact in specific clinical contexts when the amount of data available for training is limited.

When comparing our fine-tuned LLMs to smaller NLP models, we observed no clear difference in numerical performance. This finding may be helpful for researchers considering predictive NLP projects in oncology. Although recent work has emphasized LLMs,34 our results suggest that smaller models should not be overlooked. These models have orders of magnitude fewer parameters; for example, our BoW model has 5001 parameters compared with the billions in LLMs, and they carry several implementation advantages relevant to the health care context. They can be trained on widely available hardware and require substantially less electricity, an important consideration for planetary health given the growing energy demands of LLM-based applications.35 They may also pose fewer privacy risks, since models like BoW cannot leak patient data unlike models using neural networks.36 At the same time, recent work using the same dataset to predict psychosocial needs found that CNNs outperformed BoW models, suggesting that the added representational capacity of neural models can be beneficial when tasks depend on more subtle language features (e.g. repetition or nuanced phrasing) rather than word occurrence alone.26 Future work can continue to investigate when larger models are beneficial and should also include comparing domain-specific models such as Clinical BigBird37 and CancerLLM.38

Our work should be interpreted in light of several limitations, many of which reflect the challenges of conducting NLP research in clinical contexts. Firstly, our analysis relied on documents from a single cancer care organization. Although these represent hundreds of providers and nearly all patients with cancer in a large geographic area, results may differ in other settings. While death data from British Columbia Vital Statistics are highly reliable, there is a chance that there are some inaccuracies over time. Secondly, due to privacy restrictions, we could not use cloud providers or closed-source commercial models, and therefore did not evaluate systems such as ChatGPT; however, this limitation is partly mitigated by evidence that Llama models often achieve comparable performance,23 while also addressing privacy and reproducibility concerns associated with commercial models. Finally, we focused on a single outcome, one family of open-weight models, a limited number of parameter sizes feasible with our hardware, and a limited number of prompts. These restrictions limit the generalizability of our findings, and we believe truly generalizable analysis would likely require a synthesis of multiple studies.

Despite these constraints, we believe our study provides a foundation for future work by highlighting the need for further investigation of predictive NLP techniques across outcomes, models, and settings. Future work may explore several directions to further improve clinical relevance and model generalization. Firstly, incorporating disease-specific analyses may reveal cancer type-specific patterns that enhance predictive performance. Secondly, as our dataset includes only initial consultation notes, expanding the temporal scope to longitudinal clinical documents would likely improve robustness and generalizability. Recent work, such as SIGBERT, has demonstrated that NLP models can be combined with mathematical frameworks to capture the temporal dynamics of patient trajectories, enabling sophisticated time-aware modeling of patient survival outcomes. Thirdly, exploring alternative endpoints such as disease relapse represents a promising direction for future work. Finally, LLMs used in a zero-shot setting may also perform other tasks well. Prior work has shown that LLMs can be used to extract information from oncology documents,39 showing how predictive frameworks can be complemented by the use of zero-shot LLMs. Rather than pointing toward one definitive approach, our results suggest that a range of strategies should be pursued to advance the performance and feasibility of using NLP for predictive tasks in cancer care.
