Reinforcement K-wires reduce the risk of tibial plateau and lateral cortex fractures during open wedge high tibial osteotomy: a prospective comparative cohort study
Authors: Ebeed Yasin, Ahmed Darweash, Mohamed Salman, Alaa Abououf, Mohamed Abdel-Wahed, Hosam El-Din Amin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOpen wedge high tibial osteotomy (OWHTO) may be complicated by iatrogenic fractures of the tibial plateau or lateral cortex, potentially compromising osteotomy stability. Reinforcement with intra-operative Kirschner wire (K-wire) has been proposed to mitigate this risk, but robust clinical evidence is limited. This study aimed to evaluate whether intra-operative reinforcement wires decrease the incidence of iatrogenic fractures during OWHTO and to assess their impact on clinical and radiological outcomes.MethodsA prospective multi-centre comparative cohort study was conducted between March 2023 and March 2024, including 68 patients (72 initially enrolled) with medial knee compartment osteoarthritis and genu varum undergoing OWHTO who met predefined radiological and clinical criteria. Patients were allocated to a reinforcement group (Group A, n = 34; OWHTO with subchondral and lateral hinge K-wire reinforcement) and a control group (Group B, n = 34; OWHTO without reinforcement). Outcomes included intra-operative iatrogenic tibial plateau and lateral cortex fractures, weight-bearing milestones, radiographic parameters (medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA), mechanical axis deviation (MAD), posterior tibial slope), Lysholm knee scores at serial follow-up, and complications.ResultsBaseline demographics, comorbidities, and most intraoperative variables (excluding iatrogenic fractures) did not differ significantly between the reinforcement (Group A) and non-reinforcement (Group B) groups. No iatrogenic fractures of the tibial plateau or lateral cortex occurred in Group A, whereas 9 of 34 patients (26.5%) in Group B sustained such fractures (three each of Takeuchi types 1–3), with 6 of these 9 requiring additional lateral plating (P = 0.002). Patients in Group A achieved partial weight-bearing significantly earlier than those in Group B (P = 0.014), whereas Lysholm scores showed no significant differences between groups across all follow-up periods. Both groups exhibited comparable improvements MPTA, MAD, posterior tibial slope, and final Lysholm scores. Rates of wound complications and iliac crest bone graft harvest were similar between groups.ConclusionIntraoperative reinforcement of the tibial plateau and lateral cortex with subchondral and lateral hinge K-wires effectively reduced the risk of iatrogenic fractures in OWHTO and is associated with improved early functional recovery without adversely affecting alignment, union, or complication rates. These findings support the incorporation of K-wire reinforcement as a simple, reproducible adjunct to enhance the safety of OWHTO in appropriately selected patients.Keywords: Reinforcement K-wires, Iatrogenic fractures, Tibial plateau, Lateral cortex, Open wedge high tibial osteotomy, Medial compartment osteoarthritis, Lateral hinge, Lysholm score, Osteotomy stability

Introduction

Medial compartment knee osteoarthritis with varus malalignment is often associated with pain, functional limitation, and progressive joint degeneration. Among available surgical options, open wedge high tibial osteotomy (OWHTO) has gained widespread acceptance due to its capacity to effectively realign the mechanical axis of the lower limb, offloading the diseased medial compartment and thereby alleviating symptoms in appropriately selected patients [1, 2].

Despite its clinical advantages, OWHTO carries a substantial risk of intra-operative complications, most notably fractures of the tibial plateau and lateral cortex. These iatrogenic injuries not only undermine the immediate stability of the osteotomy site [3] but also may result in under-correction of the deformity [4]. The overall incidence of complications has been reported to be as high as 25% − 30.4% [5, 6]. Recognizing the significance of these complications, numerous preventive strategies have been proposed to fortify the osteotomy site and minimize the probability of adverse events [7, 8]. Recently, intraoperative reinforcement of the lateral cortex with Kirschner wire (K-wire) has emerged as a simple, readily implementable technique intended to mechanically support the lateral hinge during gap opening [9]. However, robust clinical data substantiating the routine adoption of reinforcement K-wires are still scarce, and the balance between potential benefits and risks has yet to be clearly defined.

In light of these considerations, our study was designed to address this critical gap in the literature. We conducted a prospective, multi-centre, comparative cohort study to evaluate whether intra-operative K-wire reinforcement reduces the incidence of tibial plateau and lateral cortex fractures during OWHTO, and to examine the subsequent impacts on clinical and radiological outcomes.

Patients and methods

Patients

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and the IRB approval as well as a written informed consent from the patients were obtained. Between March 2023 and March 2024, patients with medial knee compartment osteoarthritis and genu varum undergoing OWHTO were consecutively enrolled in the study if they were between 35 and 55 years old and if the medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA) was less than 85°. Patients were excluded if they had concomitant lateral knee compartment osteoarthritis, rheumatoid/septic arthritis, previous knee surgery, or did not complete the required follow-up protocol.

Seventy-two patients were initially enrolled prospectively in the study. Four patients did not complete the required follow-up protocol and were excluded from the final analysis; thus, the study cohort included 68 patients. All surgical procedures were performed by the first, second, third, and last authors across three centres, with each surgeon affiliated with all participating centres (Table 1). Patients’ allocation was centre-based; patients admitted to centres 1 and 2 were managed via reinforcement wires (group A, n = 34), whereas those in group B (n = 34) were operated on in the third centre. There were no differences between the centres regarding implant brand, operative equipment, and rehabilitation resources.

Surgeons’ experience in both groups

Patients in both groups were managed using standardized preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative protocols, with the sole exception of the utilization of reinforcement K-wires during the intraoperative phase for Group A. The surgical procedure was performed under spinal anaesthesia with the patient positioned in the supine position on a translucent orthopaedic operating table, and a pneumatic tourniquet was employed. A 6–8 cm vertical skin incision was made at the medial aspect of the proximal tibia, approximately 3 cm below the joint line. The pes anserinus and the superficial medial collateral ligament were subsequently carefully dissected subperiosteally without cutting.

Reinforcing wires (group A)

Before proceeding with the osteotomy, four 1.8 mm K-wires were inserted for structural reinforcement: two designated to protect the tibial plateau (subchondral wires) and two to safeguard the lateral cortex (lateral hinge wires). Initially, two parallel subchondral wires were introduced laterally to medially within the subchondral bone to shield the tibial plateau (Fig. 1a). Then two lateral hinge wires were subsequently advanced subcortically and positioned 2 to 3 mm beneath the lateral cortex of the proximal tibia, which was directed from the distal (lateral tibial flare entrance) to the proximal region (Fig. 1b). The trajectories of these wires were adjusted with fluoroscopic guidance to ensure that their intersection did not compromise the osteotomy pathway.

Osteotomy technique

To ensure accurate guidance of the osteotomy, two additional 2.5 mm K-wires (osteotomy guidance wires) were introduced from the medial tibial cortex 4 cm below the joint line and directed laterally towards the lateral hinge point beneath the anterior and posterior cortices. We aimed to guide the lateral cortical osteotomy line to pass above the head of the fibula. The osteotomy guidance wires aimed to intersect with the previously inserted K-wires (Fig. 1c, d). OWHTO was subsequently performed via the standard ascending biplanar technique [10]. A sharp, straight osteotome was carefully advanced beneath the osteotomy guidance wires until it reached the hinge point, where the reinforcement wires intersected (Fig. 2a). Next, the osteotomy was gradually and carefully opened until the mechanical axis of the lower limb was aligned normally or up to 7 degrees valgus (Fig. 2b). Throughout this procedure, it was ensured that the posterior and anterior cortices were precisely transected along the predetermined osteotomy line while maintaining a laminar spreader on the posteromedial side of the osteotomy to preserve the osteotomy gap and prevent increased posterior slope, then osteotomy guidance wires were removed and fixation was achieved via a T-locked plate (4.5 mm Titanium T Plate-TU-NC-L.C.P. manufactured by Orthomed E) (Fig. 2c).

Intraoperative fluoroscopy images showing the steps of the open wedge high tibial osteotomy: (a) Advancing the osteotome beneath the osteotomy guidance wires to reach the hinge point. b Gradual opening of the osteotomy. c Remove the osteotomy wires and perform plate fixation while keeping the reinforcement wire in place. d Osteotomy fixation by a T-locked plate with intact lateral cortex and plateau

The same T-locked plate brand/model was used at all centres. Following fixation, the reinforcing K-wires were removed (Fig. 2d). Autogenous iliac crest bone graft was used when the osteotomy gap is larger than 10 mm [11]. Finally, we closed the wound in layers, deflated the tourniquet, and checked the distal pulse. In group B, the same technique was performed without the reinforcement of K-wires. In patients complicated by iatrogenic fractures, we added a lateral plate for lateral cortex fracture in Takeuchi type II and III .

In both groups, a hinged knee brace was applied for 4 weeks, and gradual passive/active ROM was obtained as pain tolerated. Partial weight-bearing was initiated at 4–6 weeks postoperatively following osteotomy union, characterized by the absence of pain on weight-bearing and no radiolucency at the osteotomy site. For patients with iatrogenic fractures of the tibial plateau or lateral cortex, partial weight bearing was postponed for 8–12 weeks. Patients were followed up for 18–30 months.

The primary outcome of the study was recording the intra-operative iatrogenic fractures of the tibial plateau or lateral cortex. The secondary outcomes include the starting time of weight bearing, and the Lysholm knee score that was recorded for each patient at 3, 6 months and at the final follow-up [12]. Both groups were compared in terms of patient demographics, intra-operative iatrogenic fracture, osteotomy depth/gap, blood loss, surgery/imaging time, hospital stay, MPTA, mechanical axis deviation (MAD), slope, hinge position and union time. MAD was measured as the perpendicular distance (in mm) from the mechanical axis of the lower limb to the centre of the knee joint on full-length standing anteroposterior radiographs. Posterior tibial slope was measured on true lateral radiographs using the medial tibial plateau method: tangent to the medial plateau vs. line along posterior tibial cortex (5–15 mm distal to the tibial plateau). Patient data were recorded and reviewed by the first and last authors. Conflicts were resolved by the 3rd author.

Statistical analysis

The Sample size was calculated via two-proportion χ² test anticipating 26.4% fracture rate without K-wires (Ogawa et al.) [13]. vs. 3% with reinforcement (88% risk reduction).

Formula: n=[(1.96 + 0.84)²×0.2234]/(0.234)²=32/group (80% power, α = 0.05). With 10% dropout: 36/group targeted (72 total). Actual 34 completers/group achieved > 99.9% post-hoc power for observed 0% vs. 26.5% (p = 0.002). The study power was set at 80%, with an alpha level of 0.05. Data analysis was performed via IBM-SPSS 27.0 (IBM-SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics, means, standard deviations (SDs), medians, ranges, and percentages were calculated. The normality of the distribution of continuous variables was tested via the Shapiro‒Wilk test. Paired sample t tests and Mann‒Whitney tests were used to compare mean differences pre-/post-operatively, and bivariable analysis with an asymmetric distribution was performed. Categorical variables were analysed by the chi-square test (Fisher’s exact test was used for variables less than 5). Multivariable logistic regression: Primary outcome (fractures) adjusted for centre, surgeon volume, osteotomy gap, and BMI. P-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Discussion

The main outcome of the current study was that intra-operative use of reinforcement K-wires was associated with a significant reduction in the incidence of iatrogenic fractures (P = 0.002). We detected no cases of tibial plateau or lateral cortex fracture when protecting K-wires were used in group A. However, in group B, there were 9 cases (26.47%) of iatrogenic fractures, 3 cases (8.82%) of each type according to the Takeuchi classification [5]. Stability was affected in 6 patients (Takeuchi type II/III) and an additional lateral plate was added [15]. Partial weight bearing time was significantly earlier in group A, while the Lysholm knee scores at 3,6 months, and the final follow-up showed non-significant differences. There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in terms of patient demographics, surgery time, blood loss, and radiation exposure time.

The importance of preserving an intact, mechanically competent lateral tibial hinge in OWHTO is well established, as it is critical for achieving reliable osteotomy consolidation and maintaining angular correction [16]. Reinforcement of the tibial plateau and lateral cortex with K-wires has recently been highlighted in several studies as a protective strategy against iatrogenic lateral hinge fractures [16–19].

To rule out disparities in the hinge position, a radiographic comparison of the hinge position in both groups using Nakamura classification was performed. Hinge positions were equivalent between groups, isolating K-wire mechanical protection as the primary fracture prevention mechanism. These analyses definitively demonstrate that Group A’s zero fracture rate reflects direct mechanical reinforcement by K-wires, not improved hinge positioning or missed fractures.

The observed slope reduction (5°) reflects surgical technique variation (posteromedial spreader positioning). This prevented the typical OWHTO slope increase but exceeded the aimed neutral correction, likely due to posterior overshoot or anterior hinge compression. Consistency across both groups confirms equal application of the technique.

Dessyn et al. demonstrated, in an ex vivo mechanical study using five pairs of fresh-frozen tibiae, that the use of K-wires provides a mechanical protection/stabilization of the lateral hinge during OWHTO [16]. Erdem et al. performed a finite element analysis to evaluate the effectiveness of K wires in prevention of lateral hinge fractures and demonstrated a beneficial role in reducing iatrogenic fracture risk [17]. Gulagaci et al. used a single K-wire to protect the lateral cortex during OWHTO and reported that it could reduce the incidence of iatrogenic hinge fracture [9]. However, that wire could not protect the tibial plateau iatrogenic fractures. Gawish et al. described a technique involving an additional subchondral K-wire, similar to our technique, to protect the tibial plateau [18], however, they had no clinical/radiological comparative results. Mahmoud et al. reported results comparable to ours, although their study was retrospective and used a smaller sample size [19].

In the present cohort, the reinforcement K‑wires group decreased the risk of intra-operative tibial plateau/lateral cortex fractures, and the risk of delayed start of weight bearing. Theoretically, the reinforcement K‑wires increase the resistance of the lateral tibial segment to tensile/shear forces generated during gradual opening of the osteotomy gap. By acting as temporary internal splints, the wires distribute stresses away from the critical hinge zone and the subchondral plate, thereby maintaining continuity of the lateral cortex and the tibial plateau articular surface during the corrective osteotomy. This may help preserve the planned correction angle, reduce the need for additional lateral fixation, and allow standardized postoperative rehabilitation without substantial prolongation of restricted weight bearing.

From a practical standpoint, the reinforcement technique is simple, uses readily available K-wires, and does not require additional implants or specialized cutting guides. Reinforcement K-wires improved hinge safety during OWHTO without increasing surgical morbidity. These findings support the routine consideration of reinforcement wires, especially in cases with large planned corrections, osteopenic bone.

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective, multi-centre, comparative study that aims to evaluate whether intraoperative K-wire reinforcement reduces the incidence of tibial plateau and lateral cortex fractures during OWHTO, and to examine the subsequent impacts on clinical and radiological outcomes.

The primary limitation of the study remains the non-randomised, centre-based allocation, completely confounding the K-wire effect with centre/surgeon effects. So, these findings should be interpreted accordingly, with external validity enhanced by multi-centre design and historical benchmarking. Another limitation of the study is that comprehensive pre-study iatrogenic fracture rates were not systematically recorded at any participating centre. This precludes direct pre-post intervention comparison at individual centres. Furthermore, the restricted age range (35–55 years) limits generalizability to the broader OWHTO population. In addition, we did not include a validated patient-reported outcome measure to assess pain, function, and quality of life from the patient perspective; reliance on Lysholm knee scores alone may not capture all dimensions of patient-centred outcomes. Finally, Non-blinded radiographic assessment by treating surgeons represents a moderate performance bias risk, mitigated by excellent interobserver reliability and standardized methods.

Conclusion

Intra-operative reinforcement with subchondral and lateral hinge K-wires effectively decreased the risk of iatrogenic fractures of the tibial plateau and lateral cortex during OWHTO. This conclusion is derived from a centre-based comparative cohort, and prospective randomised validation is needed before universal adoption can be recommended.
