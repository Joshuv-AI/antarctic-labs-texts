Effect of informational videos on vaccine perceptions among unvaccinated individuals: a randomised educational intervention
Authors: Seung-Keun Martinez, Silvia Sonderegger, Andy Brownback, Guillermo Cruces, Monika Pompeo, Joanne Morling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractABSTRACTIntroductionBy 2022, COVID-19 vaccine uptake in the USA had plateaued. We tested whether unvaccinated individuals in 2022 were wilfully avoiding vaccine information, or if they would voluntarily accept vaccine education.MethodsIn this double-blind, parallel, randomised educational study, we recruited US residents to participate in an online survey-experiment between 27 January 2022 and 25 February 2022. Subjects were first screened for vaccination status. Unvaccinated individuals were then separately invited into the survey. We developed video-based informational interventions that provide detailed, yet easy-to-grasp, explanations of mRNA vaccine technology, development, testing and safety protocols, and potential side effects. Participants were randomised into three groups: (1) a control group who watched an unrelated placebo video, (2) a ‘video-optional’ group for whom every informational video was optional and (3) a ‘video-required’ group for whom the vaccine-technology video was mandatory, but all other videos were optional. Primary endpoints were future COVID-19 vaccine uptake intentions, COVID-19 vaccine efficacy beliefs and COVID-19 vaccine side-effect concerns (all measured on a 100-point scale). Secondary endpoints measured information-seeking behaviour and trust in healthcare professionals.ResultsRelative to control (3014 participants), the video-optional group (1320 participants) exhibited a 0.09 standard deviation (95% CI 0.02 to 0.15) improvement in COVID-19 vaccine intentions, a 0.10 SD (95% CI 0.03 to 0.17) improvement in COVID-19 vaccine-efficacy beliefs, and a 0.14 SD (95% CI 0.07 to 0.21) improvement in COVID-19 side-effect concerns. The video-required group (2756 participants) yielded improvements, relative to control, of 0.07, 0.18 and 0.08 SDs in the respective categories (95% CIs 0.02 to 0.12, 0.13 to 0.23, 0.03 to 0.13). 55%–60% of the participants in both treatment groups watched optional videos.ConclusionsWilful avoidance of vaccine information does not appear to explain entrenched vaccine hesitancy. Impartial and detailed information may be an effective communication and policy tool.Trial registration numbersISRCTN98065390 and American Economic Association Trial Registry (AEARCTR0008909).Keywords: Vaccination, Public Health, Communication, COVID-19

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Prior research has examined various interventions aimed at increasing vaccine uptake among hesitant individuals, including monetary incentives and informational messages delivered via text or videos, with mixed results. While financial incentives proved to be effective for some, even substantial payments failed to persuade those most distrustful of the healthcare system. Video-based interventions showed promise in enhancing vaccine intentions in the early months following the release of COVID-19 vaccines, but their impact on late adopters remains questionable. Moreover, research suggests that individuals with high levels of scepticism tend to have low institutional trust, making appeals to authorities largely ineffective in increasing their willingness to get vaccinated.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study, involving 4076 out of 7090 unvaccinated individuals, shows that providing information on (1) how mRNA vaccines work, (2) how the vaccines were able to be developed and tested so quickly and (3) the potential side effects of vaccines significantly increased COVID-19 vaccine intentions, increased confidence in COVID-19 vaccine efficacy and safety, and decreased scepticism concerning Food and Drug Administration regulation and testing standards. Our study documents that not only are detailed and disinterested explanations (not biased by personal interests or specific agendas) of vaccine technology, development and testing an effective means of softening anti-vaccination sentiment and improving COVID-19 vaccination intentions, but they are also highly demanded by even the most vaccine-sceptical individuals.

The most effective approach to outreach for vaccine-resistant individuals remains uncertain; however, this study indicates that even the most sceptical remain curious about COVID-19 vaccines, with detailed and accessible explanations on how vaccines interact with the immune system and their development and testing being actively sought after, suggesting that providing information designed to educate rather than persuade is a promising and potentially cost-effective means of intervention for the adoption of mRNA therapies and can be easily adapted for other vaccines.

Introduction

By 2022, Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved COVID-19 vaccines were widely available, yet vaccine compliance in the USA had plateaued.1 The sizeable population of vaccine-resistant individuals proved challenging to engage using existing messaging interventions—a challenge that remains to this day.1 Numerous initiatives have been undertaken to soften the vaccine hesitancy of this population.2 3 Some strategies, for example, involved monetary rewards for vaccination, which proved effective for certain subgroups. However, among the most vaccine-hesitant individuals and those showing distrust towards the healthcare system, even large monetary inducements up to US$1000 have been insufficient to increase vaccination uptake when offered alone.4 5

Our study examines the effects of information provision among highly vaccine-hesitant individuals. While simple text messages on vaccine eligibility and informational brochures and videos on the benefits of vaccination were shown to increase vaccine appointments in the initial months following COVID-19 vaccine availability,6,8 the effectiveness of these techniques among late adopters is questionable.9 10

Previous research has shown that a leading predictor of a country’s COVID-19 policy compliance is its population’s trust in institutions, people and government.11,13 Along similar lines, monthly Kaiser Family Foundation reports have found that the uninsured are among the least vaccinated groups in the USA despite free and widely available COVID-19 vaccines.14 15 Following public health advice towards vaccination thus appears least likely among those who lack familiarity with the healthcare system and exhibit low trust in institutions.

To address the challenge of reaching highly vaccine-hesitant individuals, we produced four short videos that provide information about mRNA vaccines. We described how these vaccines work without relying on existing trust in public health authorities. A recent study found that compulsory video messages improve vaccination intentions more than written brochures among more sceptical individuals.6 However, other research questions the effectiveness of video interventions, finding that only 21% of participants in their study chose to watch a video linked to their vaccination text reminder.7 In the context of COVID-19, this highlights the need to design interventions with high voluntary participation and also carefully avoid messaging that could inadvertently reinforce vaccine hesitancy or mistrust.16 For this reason, we let participants in our study choose how many videos to watch. In one of our treatments, all videos were optional. In the other treatment, one video was compulsory, but the others were left to participants’ discretion. This experimental design allows us to test whether positive treatment effects remain when intervention participation is fully voluntary.

Methods

Participants

This between-subjects randomised controlled educational study recruited US residents to participate in an online survey-experiment. Recruitment was conducted by Lucid, a survey company widely used in social sciences.17 Lucid contracts with independent survey suppliers who recruit survey-takers through nominal financial incentives. Respondents on the Lucid Marketplace are gathered through different channels, such as social networks, membership referrals, word of mouth, online and mobile games, banner ads, TV and radio ads or offline recruitment with mail campaigns. Using the phrasing of questions of the US Census, it gathers the information of the survey respondents, which will be used to ensure that the sample is representative. The use of Lucid as a representative sample has been validated by previous work.18 The maximum number of unvaccinated US residents that could be reliably recruited through Lucid at the time of the study was estimated to be 7000. In the period from 27 January 2022 to 25 February 2022 we recruited 7090 unvaccinated adults from across the USA. The data collection process was divided into two waves. In the initial phase, we aimed to recruit 4000 participants and successfully enlisted 3999 individuals. This phase was preregistered before any participant recruitment or treatment assignment began. Subsequently, in the second phase, after the completion of recruitment for the first phase, we aimed to recruit 3000 additional participants and successfully enrolled 3091 additional individuals. The registration of the second phase occurred after the completion of recruitment of the first phase but before beginning the recruitment for the second phase.

An initial screener asked multiple demographic and health-related questions to obscure its intended purpose. Those who stated they had received no doses of any COVID-19 vaccine were subsequently invited to participate in our survey. No other exclusion restrictions were made. To avoid selection issues, the invitation only informed participants that University of Nottingham researchers were conducting an ‘academic’ survey. They were informed about the survey duration (about 15 min) and the anonymity of answers.

Participants were required to affirm consent on the first survey screen. We also collected demographic and social-group information from them. The demographics were gender, age, race, zip code, education, political affiliation and employment status. Race and gender were self-reported using US census groupings. Social-group measures asked individuals what share of their closest peers were vaccinated, the general attitude of their peers towards the vaccine and those who received it, and if they believed their peers’ attitudes towards them would change if they got vaccinated.

The intervention included four videos designed to address information gaps in COVID-19 vaccine communication. Procedures

Rather than making vaccine-safety statements that rely on the recipient trusting the information given by the researchers, videos focused on explaining the technology at work in mRNA vaccines in a detailed yet easy-to-grasp manner.20 Their design was informed by social science work on correcting misperceptions. Studies show that while individuals may become more entrenched in their convictions when confronted with information that challenges or debunks their beliefs,21 22 misperceptions may be corrected with narratives that explain why an alternative point of view is viable.23 24 Additionally, studies show that familiarity with the scientific process is correlated with higher public acceptance of scientific conclusions.25 We thus avoided explicitly advocating for vaccination, instead presenting information about mRNA vaccine safety and efficacy in a neutral fashion.

Additionally, we did not rely on simplifying abstractions or metaphors to explain mRNA vaccine technology since the efficacy of such simplifications may depend on viewers’ trust in the messenger. Instead, we created videos with sufficient detail for an individual to develop their own understanding of what an mRNA vaccine is, how it provides protection against viral infections, and how they were developed and tested.

The videos were about 2.5 min long, with topics described as follows. We commissioned a professional animator to design videos (available as open-access resources) to complement a spoken script. Online supplementals 1–3 contain scripts, screenshots and video links.

To construct these indices, we coded all responses so that 0 is the least positive about COVID-19 vaccines and 100 is the most positive. We then pooled responses to each question in an index and standardised these aggregate measures within each recruitment wave to have a mean of zero and SD one. Perception1, Perception2 and Perception3 were pre-registered as primary endpoints, and Perception4 as secondary. These perception indices were created to thematically combine our survey outcomes and limit multiple hypothesis testing.

We were also interested in the information-seeking behaviours of our subjects. To capture this, we measured ‘information-seeking’ as (1) the number of subsequent videos watched for the video-required and video-optional groups and (2) the amount of time a participant spent watching those videos (pre-registered as secondary outcomes).

The study, conducted through an online questionnaire, does not reveal any adverse events or safety-related concerns.

Statistical analysis

We recruited 7090 unvaccinated adults from across the USA. Pilot results suggested that this sample size was sufficient for any conventional degree of statistical power. Our primary pre-registered analysis consists of OLS regressions with heteroskedasticity-robust SEs. For this analysis, we regress postintervention vaccine perception indices (Perception1, Perception2 and Perception3) on treatment assignment to produce intent-to-treat coefficient estimates describing the impact of the video-required (VREQUIRED) and video-optional (VOPTIONAL) treatments relative to the control group. For this analysis, we only control for the recruitment wave. As secondary pre-registered analysis, we also regress outcome Perception4 on treatment assignment (again controlling only for recruitment wave). In all cases, our null hypothesis is that there is no effect from being assigned to the (VREQUIRED) or (VOPTIONAL) treatments.

In post-hoc sensitivity and robustness analysis, we re-estimate the aforementioned regressions with additional controls for demographic and social group variables, where we recalculate the p values for our regression coefficients using Romano-Wolf multiple hypothesis testing corrections,27 and we evaluate whether the regression results could suffer from differential attrition across treatments or subject characteristics. For multiple hypothesis correction, adjusted p values were calculated conservatively by assuming 16 hypotheses—four outcome variables, two interventions and two regression specifications per outcome. To evaluate attrition, online supplemental 4 shows the results of regressing survey completion on treatment assignment and subject characteristics (online supplemental table S1).28 We also use inverse-probability weighting to correct for potential differential attrition in treatment effect estimates (online supplemental table S2).

To further delve into the mechanism at play, we look at information-seeking behaviour in the two treatment arms. Information-seeking behaviour is measured as the number of optional videos watched and the time spent watching them. In secondary post-hoc analysis, we study the effect of being compelled to watch the vaccine technology video on subsequent information consumption by comparing the number of optional videos watched and the time spent watching them between (VOPTIONAL) and (VREQUIRED) treatments. For this analysis, we conduct two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. Null hypotheses for both tests are that there are no distributional differences in observed behaviour. Finally, as an exploratory post-hoc analysis, we regress vaccine perceptions on the number of vaccine-related videos watched (OLS with heteroskedasticity-robust SEs). This analysis tests the null hypothesis that, on the intensive margin, there is no effect of additional videos watched on vaccine perceptions.

Online supplementals 5 and 6 mathematically detail all statistical models. All analysis was conducted using Stata (V.17). No data monitoring committee was included in the analysis. The educational study was registered retrospectively on ISRCTN (ISRCTN98065390). The study protocol and planned analyses were pre-registered on the American Economic Association Trial Registry (AEARCTR0008909). The pre-analysis plan and IRB-approved protocol are available in online supplemental 5.

Online supplemental table S2, which corrects coefficients for responses potentially lost to attrition, shows that the signs and significance levels of our primary results are unchanged.

Discussion

The information-seeking behaviour among our unvaccinated subjects provides guidance on how to reach vaccine-hesitant people in the USA. Informational interventions should impart a functional understanding of vaccines that individuals can use to make more informed decisions. We report the development of an evidence-based intervention that addresses the disconnect between professionals and the public and engages with the concerns of vaccine-hesitant individuals.29,31 Crucially, we do not find evidence of pervasive information avoidance among unvaccinated persons. Even the most sceptical individuals voluntarily engaged with disinterested information on COVID-19 vaccines.

While our intervention led to statistically significant improvements in attitudes toward COVID-19 vaccines, evidenced by the similar treatment effects, relative to control, in individuals’ COVID-19 vaccine intentions across both (VOPTIONAL) and (VREQUIRED), the effect sizes are modest. Additionally, changes in attitudes need not directly affect vaccine uptake. Our results highlight the potential of high-quality, scalable interventions to influence vaccine-related attitudes, while also identifying limitations to addressing entrenched vaccine hesitancy.

The high voluntary engagement with our informational videos differs from previous findings, where only 21% of participants chose to watch a COVID-19-related video.13 A possible reason for this discrepancy is that subjects in this study were encouraged to watch an ‘important video’ without details on its content, while our study informed subjects that the videos were designed to provide a better understanding of how mRNA vaccines work. A policymaker may think that making high-quality information compulsory would increase interest in additional information and increase vaccination intentions. We find that compulsory videos increase overall video watching but have no added effect beyond the video-optional group on measured intentions. This suggests that those who are most responsive to information are also those who self-select into engaging with it when made available.

Why were our videos effective? Our interpretation is that they provided enough detail to build a functional understanding of mRNA vaccine technology while also letting viewers form their own opinions. This may have shifted the emphasis from prescriptive guidelines to an impartial description, allowing viewers to make their own (better-informed) choices. That said, the modest effect sizes suggest that while these interventions are a promising part of the broader effort to combat vaccine hesitancy, they are unlikely to fully overcome resistance on their own. Future work should look more closely at the mechanisms at work.

Previous interventions focused on building trust between the public and healthcare institutions. This is certainly a key factor to consider, although it is not amenable to rapid interventions in emergency settings, such as a pandemic.32,34

A notable strength of this work is the number of participants (>7000)—considerably exceeding most comparable studies.35 We closely followed the Medical Research Council’s guidance and involved experts in writing video scripts to ensure accuracy. A wide variety of demographics is represented in our sample and treatment arms is well-balanced.

Our method of engaging with vaccine-hesitant people in the USA is promising. Misinformation (and particularly ‘scientific sounding’ misinformation) has constrained COVID-19 vaccine uptake.36 We present a potential remedy to the spread of such misinformation. Scientific-sounding misinformation can take hold in the gaps of people’s understanding of vaccines. Filling these gaps with a basic functional understanding of the science behind vaccines may curtail harmful misinformation and improve vaccine uptake.

We note that our sample is limited to those who are comfortable answering somewhat scientifically technical questions in English. Further work is needed to understand how to adapt our interventions for harder to reach populations within the USA. Another potential limitation of our work is that it only considers stated COVID-19 vaccination intentions, without directly measuring vaccine uptake. While there is suggestive evidence that stated vaccination intentions do translate into future uptake,6 future research is needed to establish whether this applies to our interventions. Finally, our study focuses on a sample of US residents. The causes of vaccine hesitancy may differ in other countries, making it difficult to infer how effective the interventions would be elsewhere.

Online supplemental table S1 shows that our study does not suffer from selective differential attrition—ensuring the internal validity of our primary estimates.28 However, the external validity of our results is limited to respondents who agreed to watch videos. The population who would refuse our videos may not be relevant for our policy evaluation, as it is unlikely that any video intervention would reach them.

Finally, this intervention is simple, scalable, inexpensive to deliver and it has the potential to be leveraged for other vaccines—especially future mRNA vaccines. While its absolute effects are limited, it has the potential to complement other strategies to address vaccine hesitancy, particularly by engaging with vaccine-hesitant individuals and countering misinformation. An important limitation of this educational study is the lack of follow-up data to assess whether the attitudinal changes observed—namely, improvements in vaccine intentions, beliefs about vaccine efficacy and trust in healthcare institutions—led to actual changes in health behaviours, such as vaccine uptake. While prior research suggests a link between COVID-19 vaccination intentions and actual behaviour,37 our design does not allow us to establish this connection directly. Even more, our cross-sectional design precludes us from evaluating the durability of the treatment effects. The attitudes we measured may have been temporary or subject to change in response to evolving pandemic dynamics, social influences or subsequent exposure to contradictory information. Future research would benefit from integrating longitudinal tracking of participants to determine whether the informational effects observed in this study persist beyond the immediate survey context and whether they ultimately influence medical decision-making or public health outcomes.
