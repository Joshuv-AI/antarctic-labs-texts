Interaction of Nanoparticles with Multidrug-Resistant Bacteria
Authors: Raman Preet Singh, Priya Hiteshi, Ankita Sharma, Deepak Kumar, Taranpreet Kaur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Infections due to drug-resistant bacteria have emerged as a global health risk and are expected to grow at an alarming rate in the coming decades. The number of new antibacterial compounds under preclinical and clinical development is dismayingly low; thus, efforts are underway to either increase the activity of existing compounds or identify other alternatives. Nanoparticles (NPs) have been shown to possess antimicrobial properties along with the ability to improve the activity of existing antimicrobial drugs, including against bacteria. The present study evaluated the effects of carbon nanotubes, graphene, and silver NPs against drug-resistant Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus. NPs increased nucleic acid and protein leakage, along with increased crystal violet dye uptake, suggesting damage to the cell envelope. Scanning electron microscopy revealed morphological changes in both bacteria, which are reminiscent of stress and cell envelope damage. An increase in the leakage of intracellular components and a decrease in the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) were observed in the drug–NP combinations. Coarse-grained molecular dynamics studies revealed interactions between NPs and bacterial membranes as well as peptidoglycan. These findings suggest that NPs enhance the activity of drugs, particularly those inhibiting cell wall biosynthesis, and that drug–NP combinations could aid in combating drug-resistant bacteria.

Introduction

Bacterial infections have remained a scourge since eternity and claim millions of lives annually. Estimates suggest that in 2019 alone, nearly 13.7 million deaths occurred due to infections, of which 7.7 million deaths were due to 33 pathogens. Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli were the top pathogens contributing to these deaths and accounted for 1.1 million and 0.95 million deaths, respectively. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR)-associated deaths in the same period were estimated at 4.95 million, while AMR-attributed deaths were estimated at 1.27 million. E. coli was found to have the highest mortality, followed by S. aureus (219,000 and 178,000, respectively). A later study estimated 4.71 million AMR-associated deaths in the year 2021 and a projected figure of 8.22 million AMR-associated deaths by the year 2050. The study also found that in the period 1990–2021, Staphylococcus aureus had the highest increase in AMR-related deaths, followed by Acinetobacter baumannii and Escherichia coli.

The increase in AMR requires urgent steps to mitigate the health crisis, including the development of new antimicrobials. However, due to the small number of antimicrobials under preclinical and clinical development, , alternative approaches need to be developed and evaluated. Nanoparticles (NPs), due to their versatility in physicochemical and biological properties, have emerged as a promising choice in treating infections, including those caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria. NPs can either show antimicrobial activity or enhance the activity of antimicrobial agents through a variety of mechanisms, such as acting as delivery vehicles, causing increased cellular entry by enhancing the permeability of the cell wall or cell membrane, and activating/inhibiting biochemical processes detrimental to cell survival, among others. − For example, carbon nanotubes and graphene can cause damage to bacterial cell walls and cell membranes and induce reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. − Metallic NPs, like silver NPs (AgNPs), release silver ions and induce ROS production, resulting in antimicrobial activity. , The ability of NPs to simultaneously act on multiple bacterial targets reduces the ability of bacteria to develop resistance against NPs. Although NPs appear to be a viable choice, their successful application is plagued with challenges. Physical mixtures of antimicrobial agents and NPs have been frequently employed to determine the synergistic activity of the mixtures. However, in several cases, NPs have also been shown to reduce antimicrobial activity, thereby suggesting that not all antimicrobial-NP combinations could be employed. ,,,,− NPs have also been shown to have toxic effects and can modulate the toxicity of coadministered chemicals; thus, concerns remain about whether the increased antimicrobial activity in selected antimicrobial-NP mixtures also results in higher toxicity to host cells.

The mechanism of antimicrobial activity of NPs is often studied by using experimental methods. However, several key details of drug–target interactions may not be accessible through routinely used experimental methods because such events occur on extremely small spatial and temporal scales. Computer simulations, most notably molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, have been a key technique for studying biological processes at extremely small spatial and temporal scales. All-atom MD employs models in which each atom is represented as a sphere with a Lennard-Jones potential and usually a partial charge. These atomistic models, although elaborate, increase the complexity of the system and are slow to run. Contrastingly, coarse-grained (CG) MD model groups of atoms are treated as “beads,” thus reducing the complexity of the system and significantly improving simulation speed. The Martini force field is the most commonly used CG model and has been employed to model proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, polysaccharides, organic molecules, and NPs. Although atomistic models of several bacterial biomolecules, such as membrane lipids and peptidoglycan have been reported over a decade ago, CG models of these key biomolecules were reported relatively later. , Despite these efforts, there is still a lack of several key biomolecules, particularly those specific to Gram-positive bacteria. Interestingly, MD simulations have been routinely employed to study NP–cell interactions representing mammalian systems, while similar studies have started appearing in bacteria only recently. ,

The present study was conducted to determine the effect of pristine multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), graphene, and AgNPs on antibacterial activity in a Gram-negative (E. coli) and a Gram-positive (S. aureus) MDR bacterium. These bacteria were selected because they are listed in the WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List, are responsible for AMR-related deaths , and are common pathogens in nonlethal infections. , Beta-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins, and carbapenems) are the most commonly used drugs in the treatment and prevention of bacterial infections in humans , and poultry. The inappropriate use of antimicrobials in humans and poultry has resulted in higher levels of AMR against beta-lactams. − Hence, E. coli and S. aureus resistant to two or more beta-lactam subclasses were selected. Numerous studies have highlighted the lack of reproducibility of results, which may be attributed to different experimental protocols. , Therefore, antibacterial activity (minimal inhibitory concentration; MIC) was determined using guidelines promulgated by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI), which are considered the gold standard in clinical antimicrobial testing. Physical mixtures of NPs and antimicrobials were tested to determine the increase in antimicrobial activity, and the effects on the cell envelope (cell wall and cell membrane) were determined experimentally and complemented by CG MD simulations.

Experimental Section

Materials

Cation-adjusted Mueller-Hinton broth (CAMHB), nutrient broth, nutrient agar, Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT), Tween-80, and sterile 96-well plates were purchased from HiMedia, Mumbai, India. Electron microscopy-grade glutaraldehyde was obtained from Sigma, India. All other chemicals used were of analytical grade. MWCNT and graphene were purchased from United Nanotech Innovation Pvt. Ltd., Bangalore, India. Ampicillin was a gift sample from Sun Pharma Pvt. Ltd., Paonta Sahib, India. Other antibacterial drugs were obtained as gift samples from SBS Biotech Pvt. Ltd., Kala Amb, India.

E. coli MTCC 739 (ATCC 10536) and S. aureus ATCC 43300 were provided by Prof. Kamal Dev, Shoolini University, India, and maintained by routine subculture on nutrient agar.

E. coli ATCC 35218 and Klebsiella quasipneumoniae ATCC 700603 were gifts from Venus Remedies Ltd., Panchkula, India. Both strains produce β-lactamase and are employed as quality control strains for β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations in CLSI guidelines. ,

Preparation of NP Suspensions

MWCNT (20–30 nm diameter, 20 μm length) and graphene (5–10 nm thickness, diameter <5 μm) were >98% pure, and their characterization has been reported earlier. , A 1 mg/mL stock suspension of MWCNT and graphene was prepared by dispersing 5 mg of the respective NPs in 5 mL of suspension medium (0.5% v/v Tween-80 in water). The suspension was prepared by probe sonication, autoclaved, and then suitably diluted in CAMHB to obtain the desired concentration. The highest working concentration (50 μg/mL in CAMHB) was stable for at least 3 days, as observed by no visible agglomeration in a 96-well plate. ,

AgNPs were prepared by the reduction of silver nitrate with citrate, following our previously reported protocol. The obtained AgNPs had a diameter of <50 nm and formed a suspension that was stable for at least 3 days.

The MIC of drugs, NPs, and their combinations was determined by broth microdilution following the CLSI M7 guideline. Briefly, bacteria were grown on nutrient agar for 18–24 h, and 2–3 colonies were suspended in sterile saline. The turbidity was adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard. The suspension was further diluted 1:20 with CAMHB, and the inoculum thus obtained was used within 15 min. Aliquots of 50 μL of drug solution (2X; prepared in CAMHB) were added to each well of a 96-well plate, followed by 40 μL of CAMHB and 10 μL of inoculum. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 16–20 h, and the growth in wells was visually examined after the completion of incubation. MIC was defined as the concentration of drug/drug–NP combinations that inhibited the visual growth of bacteria.

The highest working concentration of drugs and NPs was 50 μg/mL, and 2-fold serial dilutions were employed (50, 25, 12.5, 6.25, 3.13, 1.56, 0.78, 0.39, 0.19, 0.09, 0.05, 0.02 μg/mL). The drug and NP were combined as a 1:1 mixture; thus, the highest concentration in the mixture contained 50 μg/mL of the drug and 50 μg/mL of the NP. In preliminary screening, the drug and NP were combined in 5:1, 1:1, and 1:5 drug:NP ratios (with the highest NP concentration fixed at 50 μg/mL in all combinations, while the drug concentration was 250, 50, and 10 μg/mL, respectively). The 1:1 combination gave the most promising results in MIC reduction (data not shown); hence, the 1:1 drug–NP combination (50 μg/mL each of the drug and NP at the highest concentration) was selected for further studies. This is in agreement with earlier observations in mammalian cells, where a 1:1 drug:MWCNT mixture was found to increase the cytotoxic effect of drugs.

Effect on Cell Envelope

The loss of cell envelope integrity can be determined by measuring the leakage of intracellular nucleic acids and proteins into the culture medium through spectrophotometric measurements at 260 and 280 nm, respectively. Cell envelope integrity can also be assessed by measuring the permeability or uptake of dyes (e.g., crystal violet), which are otherwise impermeable but can penetrate and accumulate in cells following the loss of cell envelope integrity. Therefore, an increase in the release of nucleic acids and proteins into the culture medium and an increase in the uptake of crystal violet can be monitored spectrophotometrically to quantify the loss of cell envelope integrity.

Bacteria were grown on nutrient agar for 18–24 h; 4–5 colonies were then transferred to nutrient broth and further incubated for 4–5 h. The liquid culture was then centrifuged (8000g, 15 min), and the pellet was washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4). The pellet was suspended in PBS and adequately diluted with PBS to adjust the absorbance between 0.6 and 0.8 at 600 nm. The suspension (2 mL) was incubated with drugs, NPs, or 1:1 drug–NP combinations (0.1 mL) for 2 h. The suspension was centrifuged (8000g, 15 min) to remove bacteria, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 260 and 280 nm using a spectrophotometer. The pellet was suspended in 1 mL of crystal violet dye (2% w/v in PBS) and incubated for 15 min. The suspension was centrifuged (8000g, 15 min), and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader. NPs are known to interfere with several assays; hence, adequate controls were included to circumvent interference in spectrophotometric assays, as described in our previous studies. ,

The changes in bacterial morphology were determined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) following incubation with 50 μg/mL of NPs. Cells were incubated with 50 μg/mL NPs (final volume 2.5 mL) or without NPs (control) and centrifuged after 2 h of incubation. The pellet was washed three times with saline and then fixed with a glutaraldehyde solution (4% v/v in PBS). After 15 min, the culture was again centrifuged at 8000g. The supernatant liquid was discarded, and the pellet was suspended in 100 μL of PBS. The cells were sequentially dehydrated in 30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100% ethanol for 5 min at each concentration. The samples were air-dried, sputter-coated with gold, and observed by using SEM.

The changes in the microenvironment of membrane proteins were determined by observing the fluorescence of aromatic amino acid residues. Cells were incubated with 2-fold serial dilutions of NPs, as described for SEM. The highest concentration of NPs used was 10 μg/mL to avoid artifacts arising from fluorescence quenching at high NP concentrations. , The cells were incubated for 1 h, and then aliquots of 100 μL were transferred to each well of a 96 well black plate. The plate was further incubated for 1 h, and fluorescence was measured at 258 nm excitation and 280–400 nm emission.

Effect on Mammalian Cells

A549 (human lung epithelial cell line) and RAW 264.7 cells (mouse macrophage cell line), obtained from the National Center for Cell Sciences, Pune, India, were maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS at 37 °C in 5% carbon dioxide/95% air. Cells (10,000 cells/well in a 96-well plate) were incubated with drugs, NPs, or their 1:1 combination at the highest concentration (50 μg/mL) for 24 h, and cell viability was then determined by the MTT assay as described earlier. NPs can interfere with the MTT assay; therefore, adequate controls were included as described in our previous reports. , The cell viability in both cell types after 24 h of incubation with NPs and drug–NP combinations was >95% (except ∼90% in AgNP alone), suggesting that neither NPs nor drug–NP combinations showed appreciable cytotoxicity.

Statistical Analysis

Data were converted to mean and standard deviation (SD), and multiple group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA and post hoc Tukey’s test. Intergroup differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Membrane and Peptidoglycan Systems

CG Martini models of the bacterial cell wall and cell membrane were adopted either from the literature, where available, or parametrized in the present study, as described in the Supporting Information. The names of the CG phospholipids used in this study are in accordance with the Martini lipid nomenclature described previously. ,,, Detailed methods of MD simulations are provided in the Supporting Information and the system compositions used in the present study are summarized in Table S1.

The E. coli inner membrane (IM) was modeled as a symmetrical membrane composed of POPE:POPG:CDL2 in a 75:20:5 ratio. , E. coli outer membrane (OM) was modeled as an asymmetric membrane composed of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in the outer leaflet and phospholipids (POPE:POPG:CDL2 in a 90:5:5 ratio) in the inner leaflet. ,, LPS is associated with divalent cations like Ca2+ and Mg2+ hence, the charge on LPS was neutralized with Ca2+ ions, while the charge on phospholipids was neutralized by adding an equivalent number of Na+ ions. The IM and OM were generated with the CHARMM-GUI Martini Maker , and equilibrated for 5 μs. NP was introduced close to the outer leaflet of the IM/OM, solvated, charge-neutralized and osmolarity adjusted with Na+ and Cl– ions using the insane.py script. The production run lasted 500 ns in all cases. Antifreeze water beads (WF) were included to prevent the freezing of water on the NP surface. , A previously reported CG E. coli peptidoglycan (PG), composed of 7 glycan strands linked by polypeptide chains, was used in the present study which was based on an earlier all-atom (AA) model.

S. aureus membrane contains a significant proportion of lysyl-phosphatidylglycerol (Lys-PG). A CG model of Lys-PG was recently reported; however, the bead types and bonded parameters were not described in the report. Hence, we parametrized the bonded parameters of Lys-PG as described in the Supporting Information. S. aureus membrane was modeled as a symmetrical membrane composed of DPPG:Lys-PG:CDL2 in a 57:38:5 ratio. The symmetrical membrane was generated using insane.py and equilibrated for 5 μs.

A CG model of S. aureus PG has not been reported; hence, the PG network was parameterized using an all-atom model reported recently as a template. The PG model was equilibrated for 5 μs, as described in the Supporting Information.

The detailed methodology of MD simulations is provided in the Supporting Information.

NP Models

A small, end-capped CG CNT model reported by Baoukina et al. was used. , A 2.8 nm × 4.5 nm single-layered graphene sheet was constructed using a Python script developed by our group (https://github.com/ramanpsingh/CGGrapheneBuilder). Martini CG models of CNT and fullerene have been reported using a special bead type not available in the standard Martini model and termed CNP beads. ,, Several mapping schemes for graphene have been reported (ranging from 2, 3, or 4 carbon atoms mapped to 1 CG bead; referred to as 2-to-1, 3-to-1, and 4-to-1 mapping schemes, respectively); however, none of these have been extensively tested and validated. The CNP bead was originally described for fullerenes and later adopted for CNTs. , The nonbonded interactions of CNT beads with Martini beads have also been parameterized. The CNP bead type appears to be suitable for carbonaceous NPs and was thus adopted for graphene in the present study.

An implicit ligand model was used for CG AgNPs, as described earlier for citrate-coated gold NPs. The AgNP was modeled as a hollow sphere of C1 beads, as reported for gold NPs. The densities of gold and silver are 19.3 and 10.5 g/cm3, respectively; hence, the weight of the CG bead in AgNP was set to 302 compared to 556 in CG gold NP. The number of citrate molecules bound to the AgNP surface in atomistic simulations was used to calculate the surface charge, and the corresponding charge was uniformly distributed on the CG AgNP surface, as described in the Supporting Information.

AgNPs reduced the MIC of all drugs in the drug–NP combination for both bacteria (Tables and ). MWCNT and graphene generally tended to reduce MIC of drugs acting on the cell wall in the case of E. coli except for the ampicillin-graphene combination, where no change in MIC was observed (Table ). Contrastingly, MWCNT and graphene reduced the MIC of all drugs except erythromycin (Table ). The dimensions of MWCNT and graphene are large enough that these NPs could not be internalized; thus, these NPs can only physically interact with the cell envelope (cell wall and cell membrane). This interaction could damage the cell envelope and result in leakage of cellular contents and increased entry of drugs into the cytoplasm, culminating in increased activity of the drugs in the presence of NPs. Additionally, damage to the cell envelope could synergistically enhance the activity of drugs acting on the cell wall. , A particularly interesting observation here is that while MWCNT and graphene increase the activity of all drugs except azithromycin in S. aureus, the activity of only cell wall biosynthesis inhibitor drugs was increased in E. coli. This difference in activity may be related to the presence of a protective OM in Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli) which is absent in Gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus) suggesting potentiation of drug activity by cell envelope-damaging NPs. AgNPs could cross the cell envelope and interact with intracellular components primarily by releasing reactive silver ions. This interaction could interfere with the functioning of enzymes and other biomolecules, resulting in enhanced activity of antimicrobial drugs. , It is of paramount importance to understand if and how NPs interact with the cell envelope in order to decipher and delineate the mechanisms that could contribute to the enhanced antimicrobial activity of drugs; therefore, biochemical assays and MD simulations were performed to complement MIC studies.

Effect on Cell Envelope

Loss of cell envelope integrity results in the leakage of intracellular contents into the culture medium. Since nucleotides and nucleic acids absorb at 260 nm, while aromatic amino acids and proteins absorb at 280 nm, their release into the culture medium can be determined spectrophotometrically at 260 and 280 nm to quantify the damage. Additionally, the intact cell envelope is semipermeable and does not allow the permeation of most compounds, including dyes. The loss of cell envelope integrity can thus be determined by monitoring the uptake of crystal violet dye. E. coli and S. aureus incubated with NPs exhibited a concentration-dependent increase in the leakage of intracellular components absorbing at 260 and 280 nm, along with an increase in the uptake of crystal violet dye (Figure ), suggesting that all NPs caused cell envelope damage. The release of compounds absorbing at 260 and 280 nm, as well as dye uptake, was close to the maximal effect at the lowest concentration of NPs (0.78 μg/mL), suggesting that even low concentrations of NPs can induce loss of cell envelope integrity. The results were also verified in two other bacteria, E. coli ATCC 35218 and K. quasipneumoniae ATCC 700603, and cell envelope damage appeared to be maximal at <2 μg/mL NPs in both these strains as well (data not shown).

The effect of NPs on the cell envelope and cell morphology was further determined by SEM. SEM analysis of E. coli revealed that the control cells were rod-shaped, whereas cells treated with NPs appeared spherical and formed multicellular chains. These changes in morphology are indicators of osmotic stress and overall stress (Figure ). , Control S. aureus cells were spherical with uniform size, while NP-treated cells appeared to have a subpopulation with larger cell size along with a rough /ruptured cell envelope (Figure ). The results indicated that NPs induced stress responses in S. aureus, possibly by damaging the cell envelope. , The damage to the cell wall is expected to allow the underlying lipid membrane to protrude outward, resulting in leakage of cellular contents. Mathematical modeling has revealed that the critical pore size for bulging in Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria is ∼20 nm. , Although not determined in the present study, the dimensions of NPs used do not preclude the possibility that pores exceeding the critical pore size could be created due to direct interactions between NPs and the cell envelope. The damage to the cell envelope results in changes in the microenvironment of membrane proteins, which could be determined by observing changes in fluorescence characteristics. Fluorescence measurements revealed that fluorescence intensity was reduced and fluorescence maxima shifted slightly toward lower wavelengths following incubation of E. coli with all NPs, suggesting changes in the microenvironment of membrane proteins (Figure A). In contrast, S. aureus incubated with CNTs exhibited a decrease in fluorescence emission, while the fluorescence intensity of AgNPs and graphene was comparable to that of control cells (Figure B).

As expected, drug–NP combinations resulted in increased leakage of nucleic acids (260 nm) and proteins (280 nm) in E. coli and S. aureus (Figure ). A closer look at the results revealed that cell envelope damage in NP-drug combinations exhibited an additive response; i.e., the response of the combination was approximately the sum of the individual responses of the drug and the NP alone and was within the range of experimental error (±10%).

MD Simulations

The interactions between NPs and the cell envelope of E. coli and S. aureus were determined using CG MD simulations. The cell envelope of E. coli is composed of the OM, PG, and IM, whereas that of S. aureus is composed of PG and a bilayer membrane. E. coli OM appeared to interact stably with AgNPs since the distance between the OM and AgNPs was ∼0.47 nm throughout the 500-ns simulation run (Figure A). This is the minimum distance that can be achieved for regular Martini beads, thus suggesting close contact. The number of contacts between the OM and AgNPs increased progressively until ∼200 ns and then stabilized, further indicating a stable interaction (Figure B). On the other hand, CNTs and graphene interacted poorly with the OM, as evidenced by an increase in the distance from the OM and a decrease in the number of contacts during the initial 100 ns of simulation (Figure A,B). Trajectory snapshots of the initial configuration (0 ns) and after 500 ns of simulation also revealed that AgNPs was unable to cross the OM but remained in contact with it (Figure C,D) while CNTs and graphene (Figure E-H) effectively moved away from OM.

In contrast to the OM, NPs appeared to stably interact with E. coli IM. The minimum distance between the IM and NPs was ∼0.47 (Figure A) along with a high number of IM-NP contacts (Figure B). The interactions were rapid, and stable interactions were observed throughout the entire duration (500 ns) of the production run. Trajectory snapshots of the initial (0 ns) and final configurations (500 ns) show that AgNPs were unable to penetrate the IM (Figure C,D) whereas CNTs penetrated the membrane and aligned along the membrane normal (Figure E,F). Graphene was unable to penetrate the IM but aligned parallel to the IM surface early in the simulation (<100 ns) and remained parallel to the IM during the course of the simulation (Figure G,H).

AgNP remained close to the PG network (within 1 nm) and occasionally made a small number of contacts with PG. This was also supported by trajectory snapshots where AgNP (Figure C,D), CNT (Figure E,F) and graphene (Figure G,H) were observed to be farther from PG after 500 ns compared to the initial configuration. The observations from MD simulations suggest that the outer layers of the cell envelope (OM and PG of E. coli and PG of S. aureus) do not significantly interact with NPs; hence, their integrity appears to remain intact. This contrasts with the experimental results shown above, where the integrity of the cell envelope was compromised, as is evident from the leakage of intracellular components (nucleic acids and proteins) and an increase in crystal violet dye uptake. We believe that this discrepancy can be attributed to several factors, including the following. First, due to the computational cost of performing MD studies, simplified models are used, which may result in an incomplete understanding of the underlying processes. For example, the OM, periplasmic space (containing PG), and IM of E. coli contain different types of proteins which may either adsorb on NPs or misfold in the presence of NPs. Building such complex structures tremendously increases the size of the system, and simulating such systems requires prohibitively high computational costs. , Second, MD simulations are typically performed for a few hundred nanoseconds to several microseconds, while the time scales of interactions and resultant changes may span seconds to minutes. MD simulations may also fail to detect events that are either rare or have a high energy barrier, such as protein unfolding. Although the Martini CG methodology overcomes some of these issues by flattening the energy landscape, the possibility of missing some of these events cannot be ruled out. Third, the majority of force fields used in MD simulations, including Martini, are “non-reactive” meaning that they cannot model chemical reactions. This shortcoming is most evident in the case of AgNP, where antimicrobial activity is attributed to the release of silver ions from AgNP. , Fourth, NPs can stimulate various stress pathways, such as ROS formation, which cannot be modeled by MD. Fifth, NPs could also interfere with biological processes, alter bacterial physiology, and evoke homeostasis mechanisms, which are not modeled by MD. Sixth, NPs can sorb culture medium components and have a surface or corona that may not be easy to characterize. Since most bacterial culture media contain components with ill-defined/unknown/variable composition (peptone, yeast extract, etc.), predicting NP corona and resultant interactions reliably via MD presents a humongous challenge. Nevertheless, despite all these limitations, MD still remains a reliable tool in a large number of studies, including modeling whole cells. ,

Conclusions

This study evaluated the antibacterial activity of NPs against drug-resistant Gram-negative (E. coli) and Gram-positive (S. aureus) bacteria. Both bacterial strains were found to be resistant to drugs recommended for the treatment of their respective infections in humans, in accordance with CLSI guidelines. NPs increased cell envelope permeability in both bacteria and increased the activity of drugs that inhibit cell wall biosynthesis, suggesting a possible synergistic/potentiation effect of the NPs on the activity of these drugs. In vitro cytotoxicity results suggested that NPs did not enhance the toxicity of drugs toward mammalian cells; therefore, the enhancement of cytotoxicity was selective for bacteria. MD simulations were performed to investigate the effects of NPs on the bacterial cell envelope. Martini CG models of AgNP, Gram-positive membrane lipid (Lys-PG), and Gram-positive PG were developed. All NPs interacted with E. coli IM and S. aureus membrane while AgNP interacted with E. coli OM and PG of both bacteria. These results suggest that NPs induce cell envelope damage and could be combined with antimicrobial drugs to enhance their activity, including in drug-resistant strains. A pertinent observation here is that not all drug–NP combinations may be effective, and an initial screening is warranted to select the most appropriate combinations. This study was limited to the use of a single representative compound from each chemical class, and compound-specific effects may impact the outcomes of the study. Additionally, simplistic representations of biomolecules and cellular structures were employed to build MD models due to the high computational cost associated with MD simulations. Such simplifications may not allow complete replication of spatiotemporal effects observed in experiments; thus, MD simulations provide only a partial picture and complement experimental observations. Several types of NPs are under investigation for a variety of therapeutic applications − and drug–NP combinations could be used for dermal infections. Additionally, NPs may be combined with antimicrobial agents and used as surface coatings in medical devices and other abiotic surfaces to prevent microbial growth and biofouling.
