Nigella sativa oil attenuates inflammation and oxidative stress in experimental myocardial infarction
Authors: Raluca Maria Pop, Emilia Vassilopoulou, Mihaela-Elena Jianu, Ștefan Horia Roșian, Marian Taulescu, Mihai Negru, Crina Bercian, Paul-Mihai Boarescu, Ioana Corina Bocsan, Gavriela Feketea, Veronica Sanda Chedea, Francisc Dulf, Jeanine Cruceru, Alina Elena Pârvu, Anca Dana Buzoianu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA growing interest in using Nigella sativa oil (NSO) in the prevention or treatment of several cardiovascular diseases has prompted this study. The research aims to investigate the effect of NSO on cardiac damage prevention after long-term administration in induced myocardial infarction (MI) in rats.MethodsNSO was analyzed for its fatty acids composition using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis and administered in rats before and after isoproterenol (45 mg/kg body weight) induced myocardial infarction. The following parameters were assessed: electrocardiograms, histopathological examination, serum biochemical aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), creatine kinase-myocardial band (CK-MB), serum and heart inflammation (tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF), interleukin 1 beta (IL-1b), and interleukin 6 (IL-6)), and tissue oxidative stress (total antioxidant capacity (TAC), total oxidative stress (TOS), nitric oxide (NO), malondialdehyde (MDA), and the total thiols (THIOL)).ResultsLinoleic acid (C18:2n-6) and oleic acid (C18:1n-9) were approximately 89% of total fatty acids while palmitic acid (C16:0) was 6.10%. Administration of NSO for 28 days helped in preventing QT and QTc interval prolongation and reduced heart rate (HR), after MI induction. The histological assessment showed improvement in myofibrillary degeneration and necrosis and also better reduced inflammatory process in the groups treated with NSO. In serum, pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1b and IL-6 were downregulated in chronic conditions (for IL-1b, NSO vs. control was 86.09vs 150.39 pg/mL, and for IL-6 NSO vs. control was 78.00 vs. 184.98 pg/ml). In the heart tissue, the downregulation was observed only for TNF in both acute and chronic conditions (acute NSO vs. control was 132.37 vs. 207.63 pg/mL, and chronic NSO vs. control was 135.83 vs. 183.29 pg/ml). The pro-oxidant parameters TOS, NO, MDA, and OSI, were reduced in the groups treated with NSO only after 14 days of treatment, suggesting that the NSO antioxidant effect is time-dependent.ConclusionsNSO administration might have a favourable impact on the regulation of oxidative stress and inflammation processes after MI induction in rats, and it is worth considering its administration as an adjuvant treatment.Keywords: Nigella sativa oil, Myocardial infarction, Inflammation, Oxidative stress

Introduction

Affecting millions of people worldwide every year and claiming more lives than any of the other major illnesses [1], cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains one of the most significant health concerns of our time. While there has been a decrease in the incidence of CVD in high-income countries over the past few decades, statistical reports from European countries indicate that almost half of registered deaths have CVDs as an underlying cause, with nearly 250,000 people succumbing to sudden cardiac death annually within the European Union [2, 3]. Moreover, data reviews show that while we have had greater success in decreasing the mortality rate of CVDs through refining the treatment methods and pharmaceutical options available to patients, the decline in CVD incidence hasn’t been as sizeable [3]. Concerning this, myocardial infarction (MI) rates in young adults have not decreased as much as compared to older individuals [4]. Among CVD, MI continues to be the leading cause of chronic heart failure [5].

Due to MI, multiple negative alterations at the myocardium cellular levels were observed. As a result of ischemia and reperfusion innate immune pathways are activated, leading to cardiomyocyte acute necrosis, apoptosis, hypertrophy, ventricular remodeling [6], and myocardial repair process [4]. At this stage the pro-inflammatory response is complex, a marked cytokine upregulation being observed as a multifactorial response to various activated mechanisms like production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), complement activation, nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer (NF-kB) activation. In turn, these stimulate cytokine synthesis in both resident and blood-derived cells [7]. Nevertheless, at the cellular level cytokines are known to possess similar and overlapping actions manifesting different biological effects [8], making it difficult to decipher the contradictory effects or their exact functional role in the process of cardiac injury and repair processes [9]. For example, interleukin-1 (IL-1) generated by necrotic cardiomyocytes has been identified as an important danger signal that initiates the inflammatory response after MI [10]. Interleukin-1 inhibits myofibroblast transdifferentiation and encourages the activation of a pro-inflammatory status in leukocytes and fibroblasts [10]. Through the modification of fibroblast and macrophage phenotype, inhibitory lymphocytes are essential in the negative regulation of the inflammatory response following an MI. After MI, cardiac macrophages can coordinate the healing process due to their considerable phenotypic variability [10]. The stimulation of the inflammatory response after MI is significantly influenced by IL-1 signaling: apart from the discharge of IL-1α by necrotic cells, the upregulated type 1 IL-1 receptor (IL-1R1) in respondent leukocytes and fibroblasts is driven by de novo synthesis and activation of IL-1β [11]. Besides the inflammatory immune response, oxidative stress also occurs in the infarcted myocardium and neighbouring tissues [12]. During the reperfusion phase reoxygenation of the ischemic area results in a sudden and massive increase of oxygen supply, resulting in ROS excessive production and oxidative stress [13]. This promotes further myocardial tissue damage and inflammation, closing the vicious circle between inflammation and oxidative stress and thus causing heart failure progression [14].

For all these reasons remodeling and balance between inflammation and oxidative stress may represent an important target in obtaining new therapies needed to prevent MI, limit MI size, the left ventricular remodeling, and prevent MI-induced heart failure. In this regard, plants represent an important potential source of compounds with cardioprotective erects.

Such an example is Nigella sativa (NS), popularly known as black cumin. Writings from traditional medicine, as early as 1025 describe NS as an angiosperm belonging to the Ranunculaceae family, an annual plant endemic in Eastern Europe and Western Asia, with a long history as a spice and as medicine [15–17]. Other than traditional medicine, research supports the use of NS against several cardiovascular risk factors like high blood pressure [18, 19], high cholesterol [20–23], diabetes [24, 25], obesity [26], inflammation [27, 28], oxidative stress [29, 30], and having cardioprotective effects [31–33]. The effect of NS on high blood pressure is likely mediated by its antioxidant action [18, 19] and diuretic effect [34–36]. The decrease of total cholesterol, and triglycerides but increased high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol was mediated by increasing ghrelin and reducing orexin, peptides involved in appetite regulation [23], by decreasing cholesterol synthesis [22], or by increasing the antioxidant capacity of plasma and liver [21, 22]. Following NS administration, blood glucose, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein, and very low-density lipoprotein significantly decreased in rats, while HDL was significantly increased [24]. Also, the administration of NS in high-fat diet-induced obesity in rats showed a significant decrease in whole-body weight [26]. These effects can be explained by the chemical composition of the NSO, which is rich in thymoquinone [37, 38] and fatty acids [34, 39], with a high level of linoleic and oleic acids, known for improving heart health through its anti-inflammatory role [40, 41].

This study aimed to evaluate the possible therapeutic value of NS oil in controlling oxidative stress and inflammation in MI, and its potential to prevent MI consequences.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Isoproterenol (ISO) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Nigella sativa oil (NSO) and saline solution were purchased from the local pharmacy. Ethanol and carboxymethylcellulose sodium (CMC) were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). All chemicals were of analytical grade.

NSO fatty acids GC-MS analysis

Fatty acids extraction

For the extraction of total lipids, Folch’s method was used [42, 43]. The first step consisted of mixing 3 g of NSO with 5 mL of methanol, using a MICCRA D-9 homogenizer (ART Prozess-und Labortechnik, Mullheim, Germany). Afterwards, chloroform (10 mL) was added, and the mixture was further homogenized for 2 min. To ensure a better extraction yield the solid residue was re-extracted using a mixture of chloroform/methanol solution (2:1, v/v, 15 mL). Lipids purification was done in a separation funnel, where the combined filtrates were cleaned with aqueous potassium chloride (0.88%). Finally, the filtrates were dried over anhydrous sodium sulphate and the solvent was evaporated (Rotavapor R-124, Buchi, Flawil, Switzerland).

Fatty acids detection

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis was used for total lipids fatty acids methyl esters (FAMEs) quantification and identification. The NSO was analyzed using a PerkinElmer Clarus 600 T GC-MS (PerkinElmer, Inc., Shelton, CT, USA). Compound separation was performed on a Supelcowax 10 capillary column (60 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness; Supelco Inc., Bellefonte, PA, USA) as reported by Fărcaș et al. [44]. The starting temperature of the column was 140 °C, further increased by 7 °C/min until 220 °C and kept for 23 min. The carrier gas, helium, had a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. The mass spectra were acquired in positive ion-electron impact (EI) mode, with the mass scans ranging between 22 and 395 m/z. Compound identification was performed by comparison between the retention times of known FAMEs standards (FAME Mix, Supelco No. 47885-U), combined with the compound’s mass spectra, and by comparison with compounds from NIST MS Search 2.0 software database (Gaithersburg, MD, USA). The concentration of each fatty acid was considered to be the peak area percentage of total fatty acids.

Animals

The experiment was realized using 50 male rats (Wistar albino) obtained from the animal facility at the University of Medicine and Pharmacy “Iuliu Hațieganu” Cluj-Napoca, Romania. The animals were between 10 and 12 weeks of age and their body mass ranged from 150 to 200 g according to the initial weighing. The rodents were kept in environmental conditions of 22–24 °C, 50–65% humidity, 12 h/12 h light-dark cycle, with a standard diet and water ad libitum. They were allowed a week to acclimatize before the experimental procedures. The protocol was designed considering the principles of 3Rs (replacement, reduction, refinement) and was approved by the University of Medicine and Pharmacy “Iuliu Hațieganu” Ethics Committee and by the Sanitary-Veterinary and Food Safety Directorate from Cluj-Napoca (No.126/26.06.2018).

Experimental design

The rats were randomized into five groups (n-10) as shown in Fig. 1. Groups CMC_14 and CMC_28 were considered negative control groups and received CMC solution (0.4 mL/100 g) for 14 days and 28 days, respectively. Groups NSO_14 and NSO_28 received NSO (0.4 mL/100 g) for 14 days and 28 days, respectively. Group RAM_28, considered the positive control group received ramipril (0.1 mg/100 g) for 28 days. Experimental substances were administered daily by oral gavage. All animals were intraperitoneally injected with a single dose of ISO in a concentration of 45 mg/kg on day 13 of the experiment. The NSO dosage was established according to previously published studies that proved its safe administration and anti-inflammatory effect [31, 45, 46]. At the end of the experiment, on day 14th (for groups CMC_14 and NSO_14), and day 28th (for groups CMC_28, NSO_28, RAM_28), the rats were sacrificed utilizing an anesthetic overdose (intraperitoneal injection of ketamine 40 mg/kg and xylazine 10 mg/kg), decapitated, and their hearts were immediately excised. The experimental design is detailed in Fig. 1.

Electrocardiography

The ECG was recorded before ISO administration (day 0 and day 13) and after ISO administration (day 14 and day 28). Electrocardiography was performed on anaesthetized rats with an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (26 mg/kg) and xylazine (2.6 mg/kg). After 15 min from anesthesia rats were put in a fixed, supine position and electrodes were attached to their paw pads. The ECG was recorded with the help of a Biopac MP36 system, at lead II (right forelimb to left hind limb). The calibration of the ECG apparatus was done at 1mV/1 cm and 50 mm/s speed. Biopac Student Lab 3.7.7 software was used to measure the RR and QT intervals (ms), PR segment (ms), the duration of the QRS complex (ms), and the amplitude or the R wave (mV) [47], based on the registered recordings. To obtain the values for the corrected QT intervals (QTc in ms), the Bazett formula was applied [47].

Histopathological assessment

Following ethical guidelines and approved protocols, animals were humanely euthanized. The hearts were removed, and excess tissues, fat, and surrounding blood vessels were eliminated. Subsequently, the specimens underwent a 24-hour immersion in 10% phosphate-buffered formalin (pH 7.0). Following this fixation, standard paraffin-embedding procedures were applied, and the specimens were cut into 3-micrometer (µm) sections using a microtome. These sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Photomicrographs were taken using an Olympus SP 350 digital camera in conjunction with Stream Basic imaging software, both products of Olympus Corpo-ration based in Tokyo, Japan.

Assessment of heart sections focused on identifying toxic and degenerative lesions within the myocardium and classifying them following the Metias and Jokinen semi-quantitative scoring systems [48, 49].

Briefly, according to the Metias scoring system, the findings in the myocardial tissues were categorized as follows: no changes, +mild (indicating focal myocyte damage or small multifocal degeneration with a slight inflammatory process), ++moderate (denoting extensive myofibrillary degeneration and/or diffuse inflammatory process), and +++marked (reflecting necrosis with diffuse inflammatory process). Based on these histopathological findings, the rats were classified into three groups: group A (no histopathological change), group B (mild histopathological change), and group C (moderate and/or severe histopathological changes). By using the Jokinen system, microscopic lesions were graded for severity using the following numerical scale: 0 (no changes), 1 (minimal), 2 (mild), 3 (moderate), and 4 (severe).

Blood samples and heart tissue homogenates

At the beginning (day 0) and at the end of the experiment (day 14 for groups I and III or day 28 for groups II, IV, and V), blood was collected from the retro-orbital sinus plexus under general anesthesia (intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (26 mg/kg) and xylazine (2.6 mg/kg)). Serum was collected after blood centrifugation (4000 rpm; 15 min) and stored at -80 ºC until analyses were performed.

Heart tissues were weighed (100 mg), washed, and then homogenized in 2mL of phosphate-buffered saline solution (27,000 rpm) using an automated Witeg Homogenizer (HG-15D, Wertheim, Germany). Afterward, the homogenate was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min. The clear supernatant was stored at -80 ºC until analyses were performed.

Biochemical myocardial injury markers analysis

The serum levels of ALT, AST, and CK-MB were spectrophotometrically determined using the Applied Biosystem automatic analyser (Costa Brava, Barcelona (Spain)).

Inflammatory markers analysis

Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF), interleukin 1 beta (IL-1b), and interleukin 6 (IL-6) proinflammatory cytokines were measured using the ELISA method (Stat Fax 303 Plus Microstrip Reader, Minneapolis, USA) according to the commercial ABTS ELISA development kits (PeproTech EC, Ltd., London, UK).

Oxidative stress markers analysis

To establish the status of the oxidative stress, the total antioxidant capacity (TAC) [50], total oxidative stress (TOS) [51], nitric oxide (NO), malondialdehyde (MDA), and the total thiols (THIOL) were investigated from heart tissue samples. Using the values obtained for TOS and TAC, the redox status was appraised using the oxidative stress index (OSI) following the formula OSI = TOS/TAC [52]. For assessing the NO synthesis, the method used was the one described by Miranda et al. [50], thus, after the nitrate reduction by vanadium (III), the NO levels were detected using the acidic Griess reaction [50, 53]. The marker for lipid peroxidation, MDA, was detected using thiobarbituric acid [50, 54]. The absorbance values at 532 nm, obtained after the 30-minute incubation, were correlated with MDA concentrations expressed in nmol/mL. The THIOL analysis was performed following the indications of [52], through the medium of the reaction with Ellman’s reagent (2,2-dithiobisnitrobenzoic acid) [52, 55]. A Jasco V-350 UV-VIS spectrophotometer (Jasco International Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was used to measure the absorbances.

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, IBM SPSS Statistics software version 29.0.0.0 (IBM Corp. in Armonk, NY.) was used. Shapiro–Wilk test was used to check data distribution. Since the distribution was non-normal, the continuous data was presented using the medians and percentiles (25th–75th). The non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons between groups. Significance was statistically accepted at a level of p < 0.05.

Histological findings

Compared to normal heart tissue, the rats of negative control group CMC_14 consistently exhibited moderate (++) to marked (+++) scores, as per Metias’s scoring system, attributed to the presence of extensive myofibrillary degeneration and necrosis with a diffuse inflammatory infiltrate. However, based on Jokinen’s criteria, these same samples received a score of 2 (mild), aligning with myofibrillary degeneration and necrosis, accounting for 11–40% of the total examined surface. The rats included in the negative control group CMC_28 showed a replacement of degenerated and necrotic myocardium with fibroblast and collagen fibers (extensive fibrosis). In the group treated with NSO 0.4 mL/100 g before ISO administration (NSO_14), Metias’s scoring indicated mild (+) to moderate (++) multifocal to coalescing myocyte degeneration, and necrosis with a slight inflammatory reaction. At the same time, Jokinen’s criteria scored it between 1 (minimal) to 2 (mild). No statistical significance was detected between rats included in the CMC_14 and NSO_14 groups. Samples from NSO_28 which received NSO (0.4 mL/100 g) throughout the study, demonstrated significant improvements compared to previous groups (CMC_28), with minimal or no changes by Metias’s scoring (p = 0.001) and scores of 0 to 1 according to Jokinen’s (p = 0.002). In addition, mild fibroblast proliferation was observed in the necrotic area. The rats treated with ramipril RAM_28 group (ramipril, 0.1 mg/100 g/day) had significant changes as well when compared to the CMC_28 group (p = 0.005). Similarly, the RAM_28 group exhibited results comparable to the NSO_28 group, with minimal or no changes according to Metias and Jokinen scores of 0 to 1 (Fig. 4).

The effect of NSO on myocardial injury markers

The effect of NSO on the myocardial injury markers, enzymes AST, ALT, and CK-MB, was investigated in the rats’ serum. As shown in Fig. 4, the median levels of AST (334U/L, p = 0.001, ALT (121 U/L, p = 0.024), and CK-MB (993 U/L, p = 0.018) were significantly increased one day after ISO administration as compared to day 0 (116 U/L, 57 U/L, and 528 U/L, respectively). NSO administration before MI helped by reducing ALT and AST being non-statistically significant as compared to CTRL and did not prevent CK-MB serum levels elevation. As expected, the serum levels of CK-MB began to rise 1 day after MI induction and then declined and normalized by day 28 (491U/L). The follow-up period also showed that all parameters were normalized by day 28 with no statistical difference as compared to the CTRL group (Fig. 5).

The effect of NSO on pro-inflammatory cytokines

Pro-inflammatory cytokine levels were evaluated in serum and heart tissue homogenates (Fig. 6). ISO administration significantly increased the proinflammatory cytokines in both serum and heart tissue homogenates during follow-up (day 14 and day 28) when compared to CTRL (day 0) (Fig. 6). One day after ISO administration (day 14), NSO reduced the production of proinflammatory cytokines in serum as compared with the negative control group (CMC_14), suggesting its tendency towards an anti-inflammatory effect in the acute phase of MI. After 14 days from MI induction, the administration of NSO was able to reduce the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1b significantly (86.09 pg/mL, p = 0.011) and IL-6 (78.00 pg/mL, p = 0.001) as compared to negative control group CMC_14 that had 150.39 pg/mL and 184.98 pg/mL, respectively. Serum TNF levels were reduced compared to the negative control group CMC_14 and CMC_28 but with no significance.

In tissue heart homogenates, when compared to negative controls CMC_14 and CMC_28 that had 207.63 pg/mL and 183.29 pg/mL, respectively, NSO administration significantly reduced TNF production at both times points measurements (132.37 pg/mL, p = 0.008 - day 14 and 135.83 pg/mL, p = 0.07- day 28, respectively). The administration of NSO did not prevent significantly the production of IL-1b and IL-6, but it reduced their synthesis, showing a possible improvement of inflammatory conditions associated with MI. These suggest that the administration of NSO can attenuate inflammation associated with MI in both serum and heart tissues but in a time-dependent manner.

The effect of NSO on oxidative stress markers

The effect of NSO administration on oxidative stress parameters from heart tissue homogenates (Table 3) showed significantly higher TOS, OSI, NO, and MDA values as compared to positive control (CMC_14) one day after MI induction, indicating that NSO had rather a pro-oxidant effect rather than an antioxidant effect in the acute phase of MI. On the contrary, the antioxidant effect was validated on the 28th day after MI induction, when TOS, OSI, NO, and MDA values decreased and were significantly lower as compared to positive control (CMC_28) and even negative control (RAM_28) group in the case if TOS, OSI, NO, and tiols, indicating its antioxidant effect when administrated as treatment. TAC was not significantly influenced by the treatments.

Discussions

The present study showed that long-term administration of NSO prevented cardiac damage secondary to MI. Specifically, it helped in controlling oxidative stress and inflammation associated with MI, and emphasized NSO’s phytochemical composition, particularly its fatty acids, as potential contributors to its cardioprotective effects. Most studies in the literature are focused on NS seeds different extracts (ethanolic [56]), water [57, 58], oil [59, 60] or volatile fraction [61]) and their major constituents (e.g. thymoquinone [62–64]) to explain the cardioprotective effect. To state, thymoquinone was reported to induce protection against myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury and the suppression of reperfusion-induced arrhythmias [65] or attenuate the changes in the isoproterenol-induced myocardial infarction cytochrome C and matrix metalloproteinase-9 inhibition [62]. Our previous experiments were in line with the literature data and offered insights into the NSO pretreatment cardioprotective effect [31]. The obtained pharmacological effects were attributed to NSO-rich phytochemical composition in volatile compounds like α-thujene, p-cymene, α-pinene, and β-pinene as major compounds. The presence of phenolics and other bioactive compounds was considered [31]. Therefore, in line with the concept that plant extracts, pharmacological effects are due to the synergistic effect of contained biomolecules, NSO fatty acids composition was analyzed. As other studies reported, the major fatty acids identified were linoleic (PUFA ω-6 fatty acid) and oleic acid (MUFA ω-9 fatty acid) [66–68]. NSO also contained α-linolenic (PUFA ω-3 fatty acid) but in a lower percentage.

PUFAs and MUFAs through their pleiotropic mechanisms can induce cardiovascular protection via blood pressure decrease, antithrombotic effects, modulation of blood lipids, reduction of the endothelial dysfunction and arterial stiffness, metabolic conversion into anti-inflammatory mediators’ precursors, as well as antioxidant activity [69–72].

To have a functional perspective on the cardioprotective effect of NSO, the ECG parameters were measured. In ISO-induced MI RR interval was reduced and HR increased as ISO is a β1 and β2 adrenoreceptor agonist and the activation of these receptors has a positive inotropic effect [73]. Increased duration of the QRS complex, prolongation of the QT and QTc intervals, deviation of ST segment associated with T wave inversion, and reduction of R wave amplitude are electrocardiographic markers of ISO-induced myocardial injury [74]. All these changes were observed in rats treated with CMC.

Increased duration of the QRS complex and prolongation of the QT and QTc intervals after ISO administration could be also explained by the slowing of ventricular conduction of the action potentials due to the ISO cardiotoxic effect [75]. Deviation of the ST segment might be secondary to ischemia and necrosis [75]. ISO can also lead to impairment in perfusion of the myocardium as a result of acceleration of the heart rate [76]. Myocardial edema following ISO administration was observed to be responsible for the decreased R amplitude on the ECG [77]. No data was found about CMC influencing ECG parameters. CMC, a solvent used for drug delivery, is a physiologically inert substance [78], so it does not influence ISO-induced ECG changes.

The results of this study showed that after MI induction NSO administered for 28 days better prevented QT prolongation and reduced HR than RAM, after AMI induction. An experimental study that evaluated the chronic treatment with captopril, another angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI), stated that this drug has remarkably prevented the increase in the QT interval in aging rats [79]. It is already known that ACEI decreases systemic vascular resistance but causes little change in heart rate [80], so this is the reason why no differences were found in rats treated with RAM and those treated with CMC after 28 days. Also, treatment with NSO after ISO administration prevented prolongation of the QT and QTc intervals, showing that NSO might have beneficial effects on reducing the risk of malignant arrhythmias after acute MI. Preservation of cardiomyocyte function and structure in rats pretreated with NSO could explain the reduction in the QRS duration after ISO administration.

In another study, NS ethanolic extract was shown to protect against ISO-induced MI by alleviating oxidative stress, biochemical alterations, and histological damage [56]. The histological results of this study indicated a reduction in the myofibrillar degeneration and necrosis processes specific to the infarcted myocardium and lower inflammatory processes in the groups that received NSO. These findings are supported by the levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines in both serum and heart tissue. Thus, in serum, IL-1b and IL-6 were downregulated in chronic stages while in the heart tissue, the downregulation was observed only for TNF in both acute and chronic conditions. Thus, the production of proinflammatory mediators was significantly downregulated in heart tissue more than their plasmatic levels. This shows that NSO influenced the inflammatory status in the target organs, but longer treatment is required to notice a significant systemic anti-inflammatory result. These findings followed another research that implied that one of the mechanisms of cardiovascular protection was attributed to the anti-inflammatory effect [81, 82].

The downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines further observed as an anti-inflammatory effect can be explained by the presence of PUFAs and MUFAs. The molecular mechanisms by which these fatty acids act in reducing inflammation are various and complex since they can modulate multiple molecular pathways that have physiological roles [83]. This happens because fatty acids are cell membrane phospholipids integral components with functional, signaling, and metabolic roles [84]. Accordingly, it was determined that PUFAs can reduce several inflammatory biomarkers important in controlling cardiovascular risk factors [84]. It was observed that PUFAs are involved in the reduction of prostaglandins, thromboxane, and leukotrienes, of human adhesion molecules expression like E-selectin, P-selectin, intercellular adhesion molecule 1, vascular cell adhesion molecule 1, in T-helper 1 lymphocyte reactivity regulation, in the regulation of endothelial dysfunction, in TNF, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 pro-inflammatory cytokines as well as pro-inflammatory metalloproteinases 2 and metalloproteinases 9 decreases [83, 85, 86].

It is known that prolonged inflammation and oxidative stress processes have major roles in the prevention, progression, and pathogenesis of myocardial infarction [5]. Cardiac ischemia as a result of ISO administration in rats causes both inflammation and oxidative stress, leading to the generation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and ROS, which in turn causes modifications to cellular structures [31]. The anti-inflammatory effect of NSO observed in this study was associated with the reduction of the oxidative markers TOS, NO, MDA, and OSI, supporting the interplay between these physio-pathological processes. Other studies also demonstrated that myocardial fibrosis caused by inflammation in rats with induced myocardial fibrosis was decreased, presumably by enhancing the balance between oxidative and antioxidative processes [27]. Nigella Sativa extract prevented cardiac injury in rats with induced doxorubicin cardiotoxicity by decreasing troponin levels, TOS, and MDA and by increasing serum TAC [87]. Since most of the studies evaluated serum oxidative stress and inflammation, this study aimed to evaluate these processes in heart tissue for a more precise evaluation of MI.

Thus, TAC analysis offers limited information regarding the antioxidant status since this analysis is limited only to some of the antioxidant components [88]. In the treated group TOS, which is a pro-oxidant marker, and gives a perspective on the imbalance between antioxidants and pro-oxidation status [89] indicated the overall increased oxidation state [90] after one day from ISO administration and decreased until the 28th day suggesting that the NSO antioxidant effect is time dependent.

The overall lipid peroxidation status of heart tissue homogenates was evaluated by MDA which is considered an important marker [91]. NSO administration reduced tissue MDA levels. The same results were obtained when the seeds of NS were administrated after myocardial ischemic reperfusion injury induced in rats [92]. Also, the levels of NO were significantly reduced after MI induction. It is known that the overproduction of NO in the presence of superoxide synthesis influences the pathogenesis of inflammation by forming peroxynitrite with oxidative effects in MI, stroke, chronic heart failure, diabetes, and other chronic inflammatory diseases [93]. Therefore, inhibition of NO could represent an important step in reducing inflammatory response and oxidative stress specific to MI or other inflammatory diseases. The reduction of cardiac oxidative stress by NSO intake was reported in hypertensive rats as well [94].

Potential limitations and future research

No echocardiographic evaluation of heart functions such as fractional shortening, ejection fraction, left ventricular end-diastolic, and systolic diameter/dimension were performed during the experiment. Molecular evaluation of proteins involved in inflammation, fibrosis, and necrosis pathways would also have been of great interest. Future research will also include evaluation of hemodynamic parameters such as systolic arterial pressure (SAP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), diastolic arterial pressure (DAP), left ventricular pressure development over time [(±) LV dP/dt], and left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP). After MI the fibrosis development and extent could benefit from Masson’s Trichrome stain to be emphasized.

Conclusions

The long-term administration of NSO, rich in PUFAs and MUFAs, improved myocardial function, expressed as ECG parameters respectively prevented QT and QTc interval prolongation, and reduced HR. At the same time, structural myocardial histological improvements were found. These effects of NSO long-term administration were associated with systemic and myocardial inflammation and oxidative stress reduction. In conclusion, the study results suggest that the long-term administration of NSO might have a beneficial effect after MI and it is worthwhile considering it as adjuvant treatment. Further studies will have to demonstrate if these observations are valid in human patients.
