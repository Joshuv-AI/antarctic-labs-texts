Genetic predictors of lithium response in an ethiopian cohort of patients with bipolar disorder
Authors: Abebe Ejigu Hailu, Solomon Teferra, Ephrem Engidawork
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeveral lines of evidence suggest that genetic factors underlie variability in response to lithium, although pharmacogenetic studies, particularly in African populations, are limited. This study aimed to examine the genetic factors associated with lithium response among Ethiopian patients diagnosed with bipolar disorder (BD).MethodologyThis study was conducted at Amanuel Mental Specialized Hospital (AMSH) in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, involving 101 patients diagnosed with BD and on lithium therapy for at least six months. Participants were selected from a larger cohort recruited for the Neuropsychiatric Genetics of African Populations – Psychosis, Ethiopia (NeuroGAP-P-E) project. The study investigated the association between lithium response and genetic polymorphisms of 22 genes with 53 SNPs implicated in lithium’s mechanisms of action. Clinical response to lithium was assessed using the Alda scale, where those with total Alda > 7 were categorized as good responders (GR) and those with Alda < 7 as insufficient responders (IR). Genotyping was performed using PCR-free whole-genome sequencing.ResultsAmong the participants, 32.5% were classified as GR, while 67.5% were IR. Significant associations were identified between lithium's response and specific SNPs. Notably, the BDNF rs6265 variant (Val166Met) showed stronger correlation, with the CC genotype being more frequent (p = 0.0001) in IR, while the rs2030324 A allele and AA genotype were more frequent in GR (p < 0.05). Variants in GSK-3β (rs334558) and dopamine receptor genes, such as DRD1 (rs4532) and DRD2 (rs1800497) also demonstrated significant associations with treatment outcomes (p < 0.05). However, after adjustment for multiple testing using false discovery rate (FDR), only polymorphisms within BDNF and DRD1 remain statistically significant. Multivariable analysis revealed that whilst AKT1_rs10138227 TT (p < 0.05) genotypes were positive predictors, BDNF_rs962339 GG, DRD2_rs1800497 AG/GG and GSK-3β_rs334558 AG were negative predictors of good response.ConclusionsThe data collectively show that variants in BDNF, dopamine receptor genes, and the AKT1/GSK3B pathway were linked to lithium's response in BD. AKT1 rs10138227 TT genotypes predicted better response, while BDNF rs962339 GG, DRD2 rs1800497 AG/GG, and GSK-3β rs334558 AG were associated with poor outcomes. These findings highlight the role of genetic variations in predicting lithium's response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12991-026-00651-8.Keywords: Bipolar disorder, Lithium, Genetics, Response, Ethiopia

Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a serious and chronic mental health condition characterized by alternating phases of mania and depression [1]. According to the WHO, around 40 million individuals across the globe were living with BD in 2019, representing 0.53% of the total population [2]. BD is associated with increased rates of premature death, significant disabilities, and considerable socioeconomic impact, which exert a considerable burden on mental health-care systems. The risk of suicide remains a critical concern, with estimates indicating that up to 15% of people with BD may end their lives [3]. Given its substantial impact and extensive implications, BD is considered a significant public health challenge worldwide [4]. In Ethiopia, numerous studies have underscored the high rates of mental health disorders [5–8], with Type I BD recognized as one of the most debilitating mental illness [9].

Lithium has long been established as a fundamental pharmacological agent in the management of BD, demonstrating efficacy in mood stabilization and reduction of both manic and depressive episodes [10]. However, treatment responses vary significantly among individuals, with 30–40% of patients showing partial or no response to lithium [3, 11]. Indeed, despite its widespread application, variability in patient response remains a challenge, leading to some individuals experiencing substantial benefits while others do not respond adequately [12]. Genetic factors are believed to play a critical role in both the etiology of BD and the differential responses to lithium treatment. Evidence suggests that individuals who respond well to lithium may form a clinically and genetically distinct group, often characterized by a family history of BD [4, 13]. Furthermore, patients achieving stabilization on lithium frequently cluster within families, with twin studies indicating improved lithium prophylaxis among twins when both have BD [11, 14].

Currently, there is no reliable method to identify this subset of lithium responders, resulting in underutilization of lithium therapy, despite its potential efficacy for many patients. Consequently, the development of predictive methods for individual responses to lithium prior to treatment initiation could significantly enhance clinical decision-making, expedite recovery, and reduce healthcare costs [11]. Recent investigations into specific genetic variants have shown promise in predicting lithium response in patients with BD. Notably, polymorphisms in genes linked to neurotransmitter systems, neural signaling, and cellular stress responses have emerged as potential predictors of lithium efficacy [15]. Identifying genetic markers associated with lithium efficacy may thus provide insights into the biological mechanisms underlying lithium’s therapeutic action and could inform treatment strategies [16]. However, pharmacogenetic studies on lithium remain sparse, often limited by small sample size and inconsistent definitions of treatment response. Although three genome-wide association studies (GWAS) on lithium's response have been published, subsequent attempts to replicate these findings in Asian and European populations have largely been unsuccessful [3, 4]. Moreover, so far, no genetic studies have been conducted in Ethiopian population on candidate genes associated with patients’ response to lithium treatment. This study aimed to investigate the association between lithium response and candidate genes involved in the mechanisms of action of lithium, focusing on a cohort of Ethiopian patients at Amanuel Mental Specialized Hospital (AMSH).

Methodology

Study area

This study was conducted at AMSH, the oldest mental health hospital established in 1938 and located in Addis Ketema Sub-city, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. AMSH employs 344 support staff and 250 medical specialists, with varying levels of mental health training. The hospital has a bed capacity of 300 and an average of 400 ambulatory patients treated daily.

Patient population

This research is part of the Neuropsychiatric Genetics of African Populations – Psychosis, Ethiopia (NeuroGAP-P-E) project, which collects data across four African countries, including Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda, and South Africa. In Ethiopia, data are gathered specifically at AMSH. Patients with BD who were on lithium therapy for at least six months, 18 years and older, and without a history of psychosis were included. Patients with BD with psychiatric symptoms at consent, substance intoxication or withdrawal, current psychiatric hospitalization, involuntary detention, lack of fluency in the local language (Amharic), and insufficient capacity to consent as determined by the Understanding, Beliefs, and Attitudes about Clinical Research (UBACC) were excluded. We reviewed the medical records of 614 patients with BD recruited for the NeuroGAP project from April 2018 to January 2021 [17], and included 101 patients who met the study criteria of having complete phenotypic and genetic data. A trained research assistant approached potential participants and administered the UBACC, a 10-item questionnaire assessing comprehension, with a cut-off score of 14.5 for decisional capacity and those who scored above the cutoff point were recruited.

Data collection

Clinical and socio-demographic characteristics

Clinical and sociodemographic data were gathered through chart review and interview conducted by NeuroGAP project research staff. Demographic data included age, gender, education, and marital status, while clinical data encompassed onset and frequency of mood episodes, presence of psychotic symptoms, hospitalization history, substance use (alcohol, tobacco, and other drugs), and family history of mental illness. Evaluation employed the MINI Version 7.0.2 for DSM-5, Life Events Checklist for DSM-5, ASSIST V3.0, and CIDI Screener to assess mental health, trauma exposure, substance use, and chronic physical conditions.

Lithium response

The lithium response phenotype was assessed using the Alda scale, which evaluates long-term treatment response in BD patients based on two criteria. Criterion A measures clinical improvement by comparing illness severity during lithium treatment versus non-treatment periods, scored from 0 (no change or worsening) to 10 (complete remission). Criterion B assesses causal factors, including episode frequency, treatment adherence, and concurrent medication use, with higher B scores indicating confounding factors [18, 19]. The total Alda score was calculated as A minus B, with a cutoff of ≥ 7 for good responders (GR), ≤ 3 for non-responders (NR), and 4–6 for partial responders (PaR), who, along with NRs, formed the insufficient responders (IR) group; when the B score exceeded the A score, the total Alda score was set to zero [20–22]. A research psychiatrist trained in the Alda scale reviewed patient records to assess treatment response, ensuring data quality through standardized collection forms, training, source verification, and periodic audits.

DNA extraction and processing

To extract and quantify DNA from saliva, we utilized the Genotek kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Participants were instructed to refrain from eating, drinking, smoking, or chewing gum for 30 min prior to sampling. Each participant provided a 2.0 mL saliva sample by spitting into a funnel connected to the kit. Saliva samples collected in DNA Genotek kits were mixed thoroughly by inversion and gentle shaking, and 500 µL of the sample was transferred to a labeled 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. The sample was incubated at 50 °C for 1 h followed by addition of 20 µL of PT-L2P and vortexing for a few seconds. After incubation on ice for 10 min, the sample was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 5 min at room temperature, extending up to 15 min if turbidity persisted. The clear supernatant was carefully transferred to a fresh tube containing 600 µL of 95–100% ethanol, avoiding the pellet with impurities, and allowed to precipitate at room temperature for 10 min. The precipitate was centrifuged again, and the DNA pellet was washed with 70% ethanol, ensuring the pellet was undisturbed during supernatant removal.

The pellet was dissolved in 100 µL of Tris-EDTA (TE) buffer, vortexed briefly, and incubated overnight at room temperature or at 50 °C for 1 h with occasional vortexing for complete rehydration. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer, and samples with concentrations below 50 ng/µL were re-extracted and reconstituted with 50 µL of TE buffer. DNA was aliquoted into matrix tubes and cryotubes, with metadata, including storage location, recorded in an Excel sheet. Samples were stored at -20 °C for long-term use or at 4 °C for up to 2 months. The extraction process took place in the Department of Microbiology, College of Health Sciences, Addis Ababa University, with one aliquot sent to the Broad Institute in Cambridge, MA, USA, for sequencing and genotyping, while the remaining DNA was frozen and stored in designated biobanks at the Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia.

Sequencing and genotyping

PCR free whole genome sequencing was performed at the Broad Institute of Harvard and MIT, Cambridge MA, USA as described elsewhere [23]. Genomic DNA (350 ng in 50 µL) was fragmented to an average size of 385 bp using a Covaris ultrasonicator, followed by cleanup with SPRI beads. Library preparation was conducted using KAPA Hyper Prep kits without amplification, along with Roche forked adapters that included unique 8-base index sequences. Quantification of the libraries was performed via qPCR on Agilent’s Bravo platform, normalizing the libraries to 2.2 nM before pooling them into 24-plexes. These pooled samples were then loaded onto NovaSeq 6000 S4 flow cells, generating 151 bp paired-end reads. The resulting CRAM/BAM output files were processed using Illumina software and tracked through a Laboratory Information Management System (LIMS). Variant calling was conducted following GATK best practices, executed via Cromwell on the Google Cloud Platform, with depth of coverage calculated by excluding low-quality, unmapped, unpaired, and duplicate reads. Reads were down sampled using GATK Down sample Sam to account for non-human DNA contributions from saliva. All samples were assayed on the same genotyping array at a single facility. To enhance data utility and enable comparisons across studies conducted on different platforms, we performed genotype imputation for SNPs not available on specific arrays. This was accomplished using default parameters in IMPUTE2, employing suitable reference panels. Quality control has been undertaken to check for low genotype call rates, sex mismatches, related individuals, and accounting for ancestral population structure. Additionally, we established thresholds for including genotypes based on SNP genotyping rates, selecting higher quality SNPs with an INFO score greater than 0.8 and a genotype posterior (GP) greater than 0.9 as initial cutoffs. We analyzed specific SNPs in candidate genes, including brain derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK-3β), AKT serine/threonine kinase 1 (AKT1), glutamate receptor AMPA type subunit 2 (GIA2), glutamate ionotropic receptor kainate type 4 (GRIK 4), circadian locomotor output cycles kaput (CLOCK), Period circadian regulator 3 (PER3), arrestin beta 2 (ARRB2), tryptophan hydroxylase 2 (TPH2), glucocorticoid nuclear receptor 3C1 (NR3C1), nuclear receptor 1D1 (NR1D1), neurotrophic receptor tyrosine kinase 2 (NTRK2), calcium voltage-gated channel auxiliary subunit gamma 2 (CACNG2), 5-hydroxytryptamine receptor 2 A (5HT2A), cAMP response element-binding protein 1 (CREB1), inositol monophosphatase 2 (IMPA2), inositol polyphosphate-1-phosphatase (INPP1), ankyrin 3 (ANK3), and dopamine D1 (DRD1), D2 (DRD2), and D3 (DRD3) receptors, as well as peroxisome proliferator activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PPARCGC1A), using PLINKl. A total of 53 genetic variants across 22 candidate genes were selected for testing associations with lithium response. Genes were selected based on several criteria: (i) genes that had previously been reported to be associated with lithium's response; (ii) genes involved in lithium’s mechanism of action; and (iii) genes associated with risk for BD [24] (Supplementary Table 1).

Ethical considerations

The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the College of Health Sciences, Addis Ababa University (Approval No. 085/18/Pharmacy), as well as from the National Ethics Review Board (MOSHE/RD/4.1/2319/19) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Permission to conduct the study was also obtained from ASMH. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. The researcher thoroughly explained the study’s objectives, procedures, expected outcomes, potential risks, and any associated discomforts. To ensure comprehension, the UBACC score was utilized during the consent process, allowing participants to understand the study’s requirements and their right to withdraw. Participants who met the scoring criteria provided written consent. Additionally, confidentiality was maintained through an ID system that prevented direct linkage between participants and their data.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS software package, version 26. The data were sorted and coded for SPSS, and descriptive statistics were used to present socio-demographic, clinical characteristics, medication use and response. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, and categorical variables as percent proportions. SNPs were identified from the sequenced data using PLINK. Associations between SNPs and lithium response were assessed using Chi-square tests and logistic regression models. Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) was evaluated for each SNP. Chi-square tests were conducted to determine significant differences in genotype distributions between responders and non-responders and Fishers exact test was used for all contingency table cells with expected count less than five. Each SNP and other possible confounders, including, among others, age, sex, Body mass index (BMI), and other psychiatric diagnosis were subjected to a univariable logistic regression analysis to examine their relationships with response. Backward-stepwise logistic regression was then used in the multivariable analysis to identify independent predictors of the primary outcome. We applied false discovery rate (FDR) correction to account for multiple comparisons in both the chi-square tests and logistic regression. Post hoc power analysis to check the goodness of fit test was calculated using G* power 3.1. In addition, we checked for multicollinearity prior to conducting the analysis using standard diagnostic methods (such as variance inflation factor, VIF) to ensure that multicollinearity did not affect interpretability and predictive capacity of the model. A statistically significant correlation between the independent and dependent variables was declared at p < 0.05.

Results

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics

Table 1 depicts the socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants. Among these patients, 53.5% were female, with a mean age of 37.14 years (± 8.884 ). The majority (58.4%) were aged 30 to 44.9 years, and over half (53.5%) were never married. Living arrangements showed that 30.7% lived with a partner, while 21.8% lived alone. Educationally, 20.8% had completed college, and 19.8% had only primary education. Ethnically, the largest groups were Amhara (30.7%) and Oromo (28.7%). Lifestyle factors indicated that 25.7% used tobacco, 45.5% consumed alcohol, and 32.7% used khat, with only 1% reporting cannabis use. Most patients (51.5%) had a normal BMI (18.5–24.9), and 49.5% had comorbid conditions, primarily chronic back pain (15.8%) and severe headache (11.9%). Additionally, 79.2% had not recently been hospitalized, with common comorbidities including arthritis, hypertension, and diabetes. Among the total 101 patients receiving lithium treatment, 33 (32.5%) were classified as GRs, while the remaining 68 (67.5%) were categorized as IRs.

Allelic and genotype frequencies

Chi square analyses

The genotype and allele frequencies of various SNPs in GR and IR to lithium therapy is depicted in Table 2. The genotype frequencies and SNPs, except for one SNP (rs7103411) (p = 0.04), met the HWE assumptions, ensuring reliability of genotype distributions. As shown in Table 2, significant associations were observed between various gene polymorphisms and lithium's response. Notably, among the BDNF gene polymorphisms, the rs6265 variant (Val66Met) was linked to treatment outcomes, with the CC genotype being present in 95.6% of IR compared to 66.7% of GR (p = 0.0001). The rs2030324 polymorphism also revealed that the A allele (p = 0.005) and AA genotype (p = 0.001) were more prevalent among GR. Similarly, significant differences in genotype distribution were observed for BDNF rs988748 (p = 0.0001) and rs2203877 (p = 0.027), indicating that specific genotypes may influence treatment outcomes. Additional findings included the rs11030101 and rs7103411 polymorphisms, where the TT genotype and T allele of rs11030101 were linked to GR (p = 0.017), while the TT genotype of rs7103411 was more prevalent in IR (p = 0.0001). However, after adjustment with FDR, rs2203877 and rs11030101 failed to reach statistically significant levels.

In glutamate receptor genes (GRIK4), the TT genotype of the rs1954787 variant was more common in GR than in IR (p < 0.05). Among the dopamine receptor polymorphisms, the GG genotype and G allele of the rs4532 variant (DRD1) were more frequently observed in IR (p = 0.0001). Similarly, in the rs1800497 variant (DRD2), higher occurrences of the GG genotype and the G allele were noted in IR (p = 0.018). In the AKT1/ GSK-3β signaling pathway, the rs334558 polymorphism (GSK-3β) was found to be significantly associated with lithium's response, as the GG genotype was more common in GR, while the AG genotype was predominant among IR (p = 0.027). Likewise, the C allele of AKT1 (rs10138227) was very common in IR (84.3%) (p < 0.05). In the circadian rhythm pathway, the CLOCK gene polymorphism (rs534654) exhibited significant association, with the G allele being found more frequently in GR (95.5%) compared to IR (86.8%) (p = 0.05). Similarly, PER3 (rs228642), another circadian gene, was more commonly observed in GR (69.7%) than IR (44.1%) (p = 0.03). However, after correction for multiple testing using FDR, those significant polymorphisms in DRD2, AKT1, GSK-3β, CLOCK and PER3 were not statistically significant. By contrast, no significant associations were identified for the SNPs of ARRB2, TRH2, DRD3, NR3C1, INPP1, CREB1, ANK3, NTRK2, 5HT2A, CACNG2, IMPA2, GRIA2, NR1D1, PPARCGC1A, several other SNPs of CLOCK and PER3 polymorphisms between the response groups (Supplementary Table 2). Post hoc power analysis to check the goodness of fit test was calculated using G* power 3.1 and it was found to be 0.99, which is greater than the recommended 0.8 indicating the good statistical power of the samples.

Univariable and multivariable analysis

Similar to the chi-square results, the univariable analysis revealed significant associations of other psychiatric diagnosis from clinical factors and several SNPs, including BDNF (rs6265, rs988748, rs962339, rs2203877, rs10835210, rs11030101, rs2030324), CLOCK (rs534654 and rs4340844), PER3_rs228642, GRIA2_rs4302506, AKT1_rs10138327, GSK-3β_rs334558) and DRD2_rs1800497 with lithium's response. However, following multicollinearity diagnostics, rs10835210, rs11030101, and rs2203877 of the BDNF gene were excluded due to high VIF values (> 10) and/or low tolerance (< 0.1). Moreover, BDNF_rs988748 and CLOCK_rs534654 were excluded as there were rows with zero value and BDNF_rs6265 due to absence of the TT genotype. The remaining SNPs were subsequently included in the final multivariable logistic regression model as indicated in Table 3.

The multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed significant associations between specific genetic variants and lithium's treatment response. After adjusting for some potential confounders like age, sex, BMI and other clinical variables, and considering FDR, some SNPs showed a significant association with lithium's response. Accordingly, patients with the GG genotype at the BDNF_rs962339 demonstrated a markedly lower likelihood of being GR (AOR = 0.074, 95% CI: 0.012–0.457, p < 0.05) than those having the AA genotype. Similarly, the heterozygous AG (AOR = 0.014, 95% CI: 0.00-0.581, p < 0.05) and homozygous GG (AOR = 0.217, 95% CI: 0.011–4.329) of the dopamine D2R_rs1800497 locus had a lower chance of being GR than the AA genotype. The multivariable analysis also highlighted that the AG genotype of GSK-3β_rs334558 (AOR = 0.693, 95% CI: 0.086–5.565, p < 0.05) displayed a significant negative association with treatment response. In contrast, the TT genotype of AKT1_rs10138227 exhibited a strong positive association (AOR = 11.98, 95% CI: 0.939–15.99, p < 0.05). SNPs like BDNF_rs2030324, CLOCK_ rs4340844 and AKT1_rs10138227 TC genotype and the presence of other psychiatric diagnosis, however, failed to show significant association after FDR correction. In addition, a post hoc power analysis calculated using G* power 3.1, with a medium effect size, 10 predictors, and an alpha level of 0.05, yielded a power of 0.85. This value exceeds the recommended 0.8, indicating that the analysis is robust and likely not underpowered.

Discussion

Several studies have explored the relationship between lithium's response and various candidate genes. While lithium impacts multiple signaling pathways, its precise mechanisms of clinical efficacy remain unclear [25]. Notably, lithium may enhance serotonin signaling, regulate intracellular calcium, and interact with various second messenger systems, ultimately influencing the transcription of genes related to neuroprotection, synaptic plasticity, and circadian rhythm [26]. This study identified significant associations between specific genetic variants and lithium response. Variants in eight genes; BDNF, glutamate receptors (GRIK4 and GRIA2), DRD1, DRD2, AKT1, GSK-3β, CLOCK, and PER3 differed between GR and IR. However, variants in BDNF and DRD1 genes remained significant after FDR adjustment.

The significant association of the BDNF polymorphism rs6265 (Val66Met) with lithium's response corroborates earlier studies that have highlighted the role of BDNF in mood regulation and treatment efficacy. The rs6265 (Val66Met) variant was associated with treatment outcomes, with the CC genotype being significantly more common in IR than GR. This finding aligns with earlier studies linking the Met allele to better outcomes [27, 28] but contrasts with other studies [29, 30], which found no significant association. Notably, Wang et al. (2012) [28], reported subtype-specific effects, where Met carriers better respond in BD I, while Val/Val carriers outdid in BD II, a detail not examined in the current study. A literature review by Tsai (2018) [31] found that the Val66Met variant significantly influences the response to mood stabilizers, suggesting that genetic variability in BDNF may be crucial for predicting treatment outcomes.

The findings regarding the rs2030324 polymorphism lends further support to the idea that differences in the BDNF gene may affect how individuals respond to lithium treatment. In this study, it was noted that the A allele and AA genotype appeared more commonly among GR. This observation is consistent with prior studies that have indicated the A allele is linked to better treatment outcomes, reinforcing the notion that BDNF is involved in the neurobiological mechanisms underlying lithium’s therapeutic effects. Additionally, the noteworthy associations identified for the BDNF polymorphisms rs988748 and rs2203877 are in line with the findings of Liu et al. (2008) [32], which also recognized these variants as significant contributors to the treatment of mood disorders. The rs988748 variant demonstrated substantial differences in genotype distribution (p = 0.0001), confirming earlier research that associated the G allele with a poorer response [27]. The relationships observed with rs7103411 (with the TT genotype associated with IR, p < 0.001) may offer new perspectives on the genetic factors that affect responses to lithium. NTRK2 gene polymorphisms, which encode the TrkB receptor for BDNF, are associated with lithium's response, particularly in patients with euphoric mania. Specific SNPs in NTRK, including rs1387923 and rs2769605, have been linked to lithium's response [27]. However, these associations were not confirmed in our study, suggesting that the relationship between genetic variants and treatment response may vary across different populations and/or clinical contexts.

The interaction between lithium and glutamate receptors is believed to enhance synaptic plasticity and provide neuroprotection, both of which are essential for stabilizing mood in BD. In addition to NMDA receptors, the significance of other glutamate receptor subtypes, like AMPA receptors, has been increasingly explored concerning lithium’s mechanisms of action [33, 34]. In this study, the rs1954787 variant in the GRIK4 gene showed a link with lithium's response before FDR adjustment, with the TT genotype appearing more frequently among those who respond well. Rybakowski (2014) [34] also noted a link between glutamate receptor polymorphisms and the effectiveness of lithium. Taken together, these findings indicate the role of glutamatergic signaling in sub-serving lithium’s therapeutic benefits. On the other hand, a meta-analysis conducted by Kawaguchi and Glatt (2014) [35] revealed that individuals with the C allele or CC genotype of rs1954787 tended to benefit more from antidepressant treatment instead of lithium. This difference might be attributed to variations in treatment types, as the meta-analysis didn’t specify which antidepressants were analyzed, and the significantly larger sample size (over 2000 participants from various studies) could have contributed to the results by enhancing statistical power.

The relationship between the rs334558 polymorphism in the GSK-3β, gene is particularly noteworthy, as it supports earlier findings that identify GSK-3β, as a key element in the way lithium works. Studies have demonstrated that inhibiting GSK-3β is essential for the effectiveness of lithium's treatment [36], marking the importance of this genetic variant in forecasting treatment response. GSK-3β, a versatile kinase, plays a role in a host of cellular processes, including synaptic plasticity, programmed cell death, and the regulation of circadian rhythms, which are inhibited by lithium through both direct and indirect mechanisms [37–40]. The rs334558 variant is found in the promoter region of the GSK-3β gene [41]. Our investigation revealed that the AG genotype of rs334558 was linked to decreased response to lithium's treatment, although it failed to reach significant level after adjustment. This observation aligns with previous studies [42], which indicated that individuals homozygous for the common T allele (equivalent to AA in our findings) showed a poorer response to lithium compared to those carrying the rare C allele (G in our data). Additionally, Adli et al. (2007) [43] found variations in treatment responses associated with this polymorphism. Together, these results imply that polymorphisms in GSK-3β, may affect how well patients respond to lithium, highlighting the necessity for more studies to confirm these links.

The present study also found a significant link between the TT genotype of the AKT1 rs10138227 variant and the response to lithium before adjustment. This indicates that AKT1 might be crucial in determining how patients react to lithium therapy. Being a component of the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) signaling pathway, AKT1 plays a key role in phenomena like neuroplasticity and mood control. The PI3K pathway also interacts with GSK-3β, a well-known target of lithium, which might shed light on the genetic connection seen in the current study [37, 44].

Regulating circadian rhythms is promising targets for treatment of mood disorders, using mood stabilizer treatments [45, 46]. Although the unadjusted chi-square test and univariable analysis showed significant association with treatment response, with the G allele of CLOCK rs534654 being more frequent in GR than IR, the association failed to reach statistical significance in the adjusted chi-square test and multivariable analysis. Moreover, no significant association was observed with other SNPs of CLOCK and PER3. These results are in line with that of Rybakowski et al. (2014) [47], who found no significant association between circadian rhythm gene polymorphisms and lithium's response. The lack of association, given the role of these genes in mood disorder, could possibly be related to limited sample size. Thus, other studies with larger sample size might be needed to settle this issue. Moreover, no associations were observed for NR1D1 variants, despite prior reports linking the rs2314339 T allele to poor lithium response [48] and the rs2071427 A allele to a nominally good response [49], suggesting the need for other studies to clearly define the role of these SNPs in lithium's response.

The significant associations observed in dopamine receptor polymorphisms, particularly the rs4532 (DRD1) and rs1800497 (DRD2) variants suggests the importance of dopaminergic pathways in mediating the effects of lithium, aligning with the broader understanding of dopamine’s role in mood regulation. The G allele and G/G genotype of the rs4532 variant in the DRD1 gene have been linked to an increased predisposition to BD and poor response to lithium treatment [50], which is similar to our study, where the GG genotype and G allele were observed to be more common in IR. Similarly, the rs1800497 variant in DRD2 showed significant differences before adjustment, with higher occurrences of the GG genotype and G allele in IR. However, DRD3 variants were not associated with lithium's outcomes, consistent with other studies [51].

The role of lithium in modulating phosphatidylinositol intracellular signaling is widely considered as a key mechanism underlying its therapeutic effects in BD [52]. Variants in the INPP1 gene have been linked to lithium's response. Specifically, the rs2067421 variant was associated with improved outcomes in patients with BD [29]. Moreover, a significant association was found between the INPP1 haplotype rs3791809-rs4853694-rs909270 and lithium's response [53], though this finding was not replicated in a Brazilian [54] and our study. Conversely, research on other genes, such as inositol mono-phosphatase 2 (IMPA2), showed no associations with lithium's response [55, 56].

Lithium also impacts the cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) signaling pathway. Mamdani et al. (2008) [57] conducted an association study identifying links between BD, lithium's response, and two polymorphisms in the CREB1 gene. Accordingly, CREB1-1 H SNP (G/A change) and the CREB1-7 H SNP (T/C change) were found to be associated with BD and/or lithium's response. Furthermore, alterations in phosphorylated CREB (pCREB) signaling have been proposed as an endophenotype for lithium-responsive BD [58]. Other studies have linked polymorphisms in genes such as IMPA2, INPP1, and GRIK2 to lithium's efficacy, with significant haplotype associations noted for INPP1 and GSK-3β [53, 59], . However, the current study did not find any evidence of association for the INPP1 variants rs2067421, rs3791879, rs4853694, or for variants in IMPA2, GRIK2, and CREB. This finding is concordant with Miranda et al. (2019) [60], which reported an association only for CACNG2 with lithium's response among 45 candidate genes tested in the study.

The lack of significant associations for SNPs listed above and other genes such as ARRB2, TRH2, HT2A, NR3C1, NR1D1, ANK3, CACNG2, PPARCGC1A and others suggests that not all candidate genes contribute equally to lithium's response. These findings align with the ongoing challenges found in candidate gene research, where results have frequently been inconsistent. As Serretti and Mandelli (2008) [61] pointed out, differences in study designs, sample size, and methods could lead to the discrepancies seen in genetic studies related to mood disorders. This suggests that there is a complicated interaction of genetic elements, with only specific variants potentially having a significant effect. Although various studies have linked certain SNPs to lithium's response, conflicting outcomes have also emerged. Such inconsistencies in candidate gene investigations might stem from several causes. One significant issue is the limited comprehension of lithium’s action at the molecular level, complicating the task of identifying pertinent genes. Furthermore, a number of studies used small sample sizes, which diminishes their capability to uncover genetic effects that are less prominent. Negative results have also been noted for several genes that were previously considered to affect lithium's effectiveness, highlighting the difficulties in pinpointing genetic components involved in the response to mood stabilizers.

This study faces limitations that could possibly affect the variability in genetic findings concerning lithium's response. First, the sample size may be insufficient to identify subtle effects or generalize the findings. Second, the variations in how lithium's response is assessed in different studies and use of specific inclusion criteria’s limit comparability and may introduce selection bias. Third, concentrating solely on candidate genes may overlook significant variants in other areas, resulting in an incomplete view of the genetic framework and thus conducting GWAS is recommended with larger sample size. Lastly, the ongoing lack of understanding regarding how lithium works continues to hinder the identification of relevant genetic markers. Tackling these challenges in future studies is vital for enhancing our knowledge of the genetic factors influencing lithium's treatment outcomes.

Conclusions

The findings from this study underscore the important influence of genetic differences on how individuals with BD respond to lithium therapy. We identified significant associations between the effectiveness of lithium and genetic variants related to critical brain functions, such as those linked to neuroplasticity (BDNF), neurotransmission (dopamine receptors DRD1 and DRD2), and the AKT1/GSK3B signaling pathway. In particular, the AKT1 rs10138227 TT genotype was correlated with good response, while the BDNF rs962339 GG genotype, the DRD2 rs1800497 AG/GG genotypes, and the GSK-3β, rs334558 AG genotype were associated with poor response to lithium. These findings provide valuable insights into the genetic basis of lithium's response and have potential clinical implications for the development of precision medicine approaches in BD treatment. Future studies with larger cohorts and functional analyses of identified variants are warranted to validate these findings and inform personalized therapeutic strategies.
