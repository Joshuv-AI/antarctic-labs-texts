Case-Control and Longitudinal Study of Sex Steroids in Male Patients with CKD Before and After Kidney Transplantation
Authors: Karel David, Nick Narinx, Bjorn Meijers, Leen Antonio, Henriette de Loor, Frank Claessens, Dirk Vanderschueren, Dirk Kuypers, Maarten Naesens, Brigitte Decallonne, Pieter Evenepoel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSex steroids in male patients decrease along with the progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and are associated with worse outcomes. This study aimed to increase the knowledge of the pathophysiology of biochemical hypogonadism in male CKD and its reversibility after kidney transplantation (KT).MethodsWe comprehensively mapped the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis in 120 male patients (age 65 yrs, body mass index [BMI]: 26.8 kg/m2) with CKD stage 1 to 5, and 120 kidney-healthy controls, matched (1:1) for age and BMI. Additionally, we monitored the HPG axis in 50 male kidney transplant recipients (age 60 yrs) from the time of transplantation up to 12 months. Testosterone (T) was measured by liquid chromatography tandem mass-spectrometry. Luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle stimulating hormone (FSH), and inhibin B levels were assessed by immunoassay. T/LH ratio and inhibin B/FSH ratio served as a proxy for the function of Leydig and Sertoli cells, respectively.ResultsHypogonadism is prevalent in CKD and is characterized by depressed T/LH ratio (1.75 [0.71–2.96] vs. 3.99 [2.39–5.20], P < 0.0001) and inhibin B/FSH ratio (14.40 [1.24–33.64] vs. 32.24 [16.27–56.72], P < 0.0001). T levels showed a steady increase shortly after transplantation, whereas LH levels decreased, resulting in a higher T/LH ratio (P < 0.0001). Inhibin B levels decreased after 3 months, resulting in a lower inhibin B/FSH ratio after 3 months (P < 0.0001), with partial recovery after 12 months.ConclusionMale patients with CKD showed low T/LH and inhibin B/FSH ratios pointing towards testicular failure as underlying pathophysiological mechanism. Male CKD can be considered a state of premature testicular ageing. Leydig cell function rapidly recovered after KT suggesting a functional cause of CKD-induced hypogonadism. In contrast, Sertoli cell function showed a deterioration, especially in the early post-transplant period.

Hypogonadism is a common complication in men with CKD.1,2 T levels decrease parallel to the progression of CKD, with low levels being reported in up to 77% of males with kidney failure.3,4 Male hypogonadism may translate in erectile dysfunction and infertility, both prevalent in CKD.1 In addition, low T levels have been associated with worse cardiovascular outcome and mortality and may contribute to other comorbidities in CKD, such as bone loss and anemia.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 The pathophysiology of male hypogonadism remains poorly understood.10,11 KT may at least in part revert gonadal disturbances in male patients with CKD. Indeed, an increase in both T levels and improvement of erectile function and sperm parameters have been reported in incident kidney transplant recipients.12,13 However, information on the HPG axis and its function is fragmentary, as is the knowledge of risk factors for hypogonadism in CKD.

T production by the testes occurs in the Leydig cells upon stimulation by LH.14 Within circulation, the majority of T is bound to proteins such as albumin and sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG), but only the free, unbound fraction is believed to be biologically active.15 Inhibin B production by the Sertoli cells is stimulated by FSH and can be considered as a proxy for spermatogenesis.16,17 Adequate T production is essential for normal spermatogenesis, and both T and inhibin B act by means of a negative feedback mechanism for the production of the gonadotrophins LH and FSH, respectively.14 As a result, the T/LH ratio and inhibin B/FSH ratio can be regarded as a marker for Leydig and Sertoli cell function, respectively. These ratios have not been explored yet in the setting of male CKD before and after KT.

The present study aimed for the following: (i) to assess the prevalence and elucidate the pathophysiology of male biochemical hypogonadism across different stages of CKD by comprehensively mapping the HPG axis in patients with CKD not yet on dialysis, as well as in age- and BMI-matched kidney-healthy controls; and (ii) to evaluate the reversibility of male hypogonadism in patients with kidney failure by prospectively monitoring the HPG axis in male patients with CKD at the time of KT, and 3 months and 12 months thereafter.

Methods

Design and Patients

This study encompassed a cross-sectional case-control and a longitudinal substudy.

The cross-sectional case-control study comprised of an ancillary study on samples of male patients with CKD and kidney-healthy controls enrolled in 2 cohort studies—the Leuven mild to moderate CKD cohort (LMM, S63818, NCT00441623) and the second phase of the Leuven cohort of the European Male Ageing study (EMAS, S19410/ML1903).18,19 Patients with CKD (LMM cohort) were recruited between November 2005 and September 2006. Stage of CKD was defined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes classification. Patients with CKD taking drugs known to interfere with the HPG axis or androgen synthesis, or with a history of pituitary disease or any other disease or treatment known to interfere with the HPG axis were excluded (Figure 1). Controls were recruited for participation in EMAS between 2003 and 2005. Patients with CKD and controls were matched 1:1 according to age and BMI (Figure 1). LMM samples were collected random, whereas EMAS were obtained in fasting state. Samples of both cohorts were stored at −80 °C until batch analysis. The longitudinal study comprises an ancillary study on samples of kidney transplant recipients enrolled in a prospective, observational study on the natural history of mineral and bone disorder after KT (S52091, NCT01886950). Male patients free of drugs or history of diseases and/or surgeries known to interfere with the HPG axis (antihormonal therapy) similar to the cross-sectional study and with a functioning graft up to at least 12 months after transplantation were eligible for inclusion. All patients underwent KT between April 2011 and September 2018 at the University Hospitals Leuven, Belgium. Patients were treated with a steroid-sparing immunosuppressive regimen, including a bolus methylprednisolone (500 mg i.v.) administration at engraftment, and oral methylprednisolone being gradually tapered from 16 mg to 4 mg once daily over the first 3 months post-transplantation. Methylprednisolone was stopped in patients with reassuring protocol biopsy findings at Month 3. Demographic variables, medical history, and maintenance drug therapy were extracted from electronic patient files. Serum samples were collected immediately before transplantation (random) and at Month 3 and 12 after transplantation (fasting). Samples were stored at -80 °C till batch analysis for the measurement of sex steroid levels and associated markers, except for serum creatinine and albumin which were assessed immediately after sampling.

Informed consent was obtained from all study participants. The cross sectional and longitudinal study was conducted according to 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments and was approved by the local ethics committee of the University Hospitals Leuven (S60307). The clinical and research activities being reported are consistent with the principles of the Declaration of Istanbul as outlined in the “Declaration of Istanbul on Organ Trafficking and Transplant Tourism.”

Biochemical Analysis

Total T and total estradiol (E2) were measured by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry, as described previously.20,21 SHBG, LH, FSH, and prolactin levels were measured using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (cobas 8000 modular analyzer series, Hitachi/Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The T/LH ratio was expressed as total T in nmol/l divided by LH in IU/l. Hypogonadism categories were defined according to historical thresholds of biochemical parameters, as defined previously in the EMAS study; normogonadotropic hypogonadism: T < 300 ng/dl + LH ≤ 9.4 IU/l; hypergonadotropic hypogonadism: T < 300 ng/dl + LH > 9.4IU/l; compensated hypogonadism: T ≥ 300 ng/dl + LH > 9.4 IU/l; and eugonadism: T ≥ 300 ng/dl + LH ≤ 9.4 IU/l.22 Free T was calculated using the Vermeulen formula.23 Inhibin B was measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Beckman Coulter, CA). Creatinine, using the creatinine plus enzymatic assay, and albumin were determined on cobas 8000 modular analyzer series. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the CKD-Epidemiology Collaboration formula.24

Statistical Analysis

In the cross-sectional study, sample size was determined by the cohort size of parent cohort studies and were matched 1:1 based on age and BMI. In the transplant cohort, sample size was determined by findings from a previous study, in which a mean difference of 2.2 nmol/l in total T levels was observed 12 months after transplantation.25 To achieve a power of 80% with a level of significance of 5%, at least 45 pairs needed to be included, therefore, n = 50 was chosen. Data are shown as mean ± SD and median (interquartile range) for parametric and nonparametric data, respectively. Normality was evaluated by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Differences between cases and controls were evaluated using unpaired t test or Mann-Whitney U test as appropriate. Comparison of the different CKD stages was performed by one-way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey multiple comparison or Kruskal-Wallis test and Dunn’s multiple comparison test as applicable. Correlations were explored using the Spearman correlation. Multiple linear regression was applied with T/LH ratio as dependent variable. Longitudinal analysis was performed using paired one-way analysis of variance or mixed-effects analysis followed by Tukey multiple comparison test where appropriate. Two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0, GraphPad Software LLC).

Results

Gonadal Status in Patients With CKD Versus Controls and Across Different Stages of CKD

Patients with CKD had lower total and free T levels than controls, whereas SHBG and E2 levels were similar (Table 1). Inhibin B levels were markedly low in patients with CKD. Prolactin levels did not differ between the 2 groups, but men with CKD had higher gonadotropin levels (both LH and FSH) than controls. As a result, patients with CKD had lower T/LH and inhibin B/FSH ratios compared with age- and BMI-matched kidney-healthy controls (Table 1). Hypogonadism (all categories) was present in 56.7% (n = 68) of patients with CKD and in 13.3% (n = 16) of controls. In patients with CKD stages 1 and 2, 12.5% (n = 3) had normogonadotropic, 4.2% (n = 1) hypergonadotropic, and 16.6% (n = 4) had compensated hypogonadism, whereas in the matched controls, 1 patient suffered from normogonadotropic and 1 from compensated hypogonadism (Figure 2). In patients with CKD stage 3, 7.1% (n = 3), 14.3% (n = 6), and 31.0% (n = 12) had normogonadotropic, hypergonadotropic, and compensated hypogonadism, respectively. In patients in CKD stages 4 and 5, 27.8% (n = 15) were eugonadal; in the matching controls this was 79.6% (n = 43). Furthermore, 11.1% (n = 6) patients in CKD stages 4 and 5 suffered from normogonadotropic, 22.2% (n = 12) from hypergonadotropic, and 38.9% (n = 21) from compensated hypogonadism (Figure 2). In the CKD cohort, patients in stages 4 and 5 were older than patients in stages 1 and 2 (Table 2). LH and prolactin increased with increasing CKD stage. T/LH ratio was lowest in CKD stages 4 and 5 patients, and E2 levels were highest in CKD stage 3 patients. Correlates of Markers of the HPG Axis in Male Patients With CKD and Controls

Total T, free T, and T/LH ratio correlated positively with eGFR in patients with CKD, as opposed to gonadotropins which correlated negatively with eGFR (Supplementary Table S2). Overall, age had more influence on sex steroid levels and associated markers in patients with CKD than in controls (Supplementary Table S2). T/LH ratio declined with age both in patients with CKD and controls, but at every age T/LH ratio was lower in patients with CKD compared with controls (CKD: Y = −0.06X + 5.96 vs. control: Y = −0.04X + 6.49; difference between slopes: P = 0.2497; difference between intercept: P < 0.0001) (Figure 3a). Inhibin B/FSH ratio also diminished with age in both patients with CKD and controls (CKD: Y = −0.90X + 82.15 vs. control: Y = −0.52X + 73.21; difference between slopes: P = 0.3369, difference between intercept: P = 0.0002) (Figure 3b). Parameters of the HPG Axis in Incident Male Kidney Transplant Recipients

Relevant demographics and details on the immunosuppressive therapy are summarized in Supplementary Tables S4 and S5. Total T levels increased after KT (P = 0.0514) (Table 3). The increase of the free T levels was more pronounced and already significant at Month 3. In contrast to T levels, inhibin B levels significantly decreased 3 months after KT with only partial recuperation at Month 12. SHBG and prolactin levels were lowest, and E2 levels were highest at 12 months after transplantation. Although LH levels significantly decreased after KT, FSH levels tended to increase. As a result, the T/LH ratio increased after KT, but the inhibin B/FSH ratio decreased (Table 3). At time of transplantation, 42% (n = 21) of patients were eugonadal (Figure 4). The percentage of eugonadal patients increased to 62% (n = 31) and 72% (n = 36), 3 and 12 months after KT, respectively. Twelve months after transplantation the number of patients with normogonadotropic hypogonadism decreased from 5 to 2, with hypergonadotropic hypogonadism from 7 to 1, and with compensated hypogonadism from 17 to 11 (Figure 4). Discussion

Men with CKD had lower total and free T levels compared with age- and BMI-matched controls. Both the T/LH ratio and inhibin B/FSH ratio, markers for Leydig and Sertoli cell function, respectively, were lower in men with CKD than in kidney-healthy controls across age categories. After KT the T/LH ratio and the inhibin B/FSH ratio followed distinct patterns. T levels increased and LH levels decreased 3 months after transplantation, resulting in an increased T/LH ratio. In contrast, inhibin B levels decreased and FSH levels tended to increase 3 months after engraftment, leading to a decreased inhibin B/FSH ratio with only partial recuperation at 12 months after transplantation.

Our data confirm findings from previous studies demonstrating low total and free T levels in men with CKD across stages of disease.3,4,26, 27, 28, 29, 30 However, only few studies so far applied the gold standard technique for measurement of sex steroids,31, 32, 33, 34 that is, liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. In patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus, T levels were reported to be lower in patients with CKD than in kidney-healthy subjects; hypergonadotropic hypogonadism occurred more in the patients with CKD.31 Here, we confirm that hypogonadism in patients with CKD is accompanied by increased LH levels. Although not fully understood, the high levels of LH in male CKD have been proposed to be caused by both reduction in metabolic or renal clearance and increased production.10,35, 36, 37 Indeed, it has been suggested that the Leydig cell responsiveness to LH is diminished in uremic conditions,35,38,39 which is also the case with ageing.40 As such, gonadotrophins add to the growing list of hormones to which resistance or hypo-responsiveness has been reported in the setting of CKD (Figure 5). Here, we propose the T/LH ratio as a marker of interest in men with CKD. This marker has previously been explored in the setting of male infertility and cryptorchidism, and in community-dwelling men without CKD, in whom it shows a decrease with increasing age.43, 44, 45 In this study, T/LH ratio was markedly decreased in patients with CKD compared with age- and BMI-matched controls, and both age and eGFR were independent determinants of T/LH ratio in the patients with CKD. As such, Leydig cell dysfunction (and thus reduced gonadal efficiency) is the probable underlying pathophysiological mechanism of the observed hypogonadism; CKD can be seen as a state of premature testicular ageing. Indeed, according to the T/LH ratio in this cohort, a 45-year-old male patient with CKD has a testicular age of an 81-year-old healthy-kidney control.

To assess the reversibility of a dysfunctional HPG axis following recovery of kidney function, we monitored markers of HPG axis up to 1 year after KT. In agreement with literature, we show that T levels already recover by Month 3 post-transplantation.46, 47, 48 We furthermore demonstrate that the increase of T after transplantation is paralleled by a decrease in LH,46,47 indicating rapid recovery of Leydig cell function and/or improved gonadotropin responsiveness. At the time of transplantation only 42% of patients were biochemically eugonadal, whereas this rose to 72% after 12 months. The swift recovery suggests that hypogonadism in CKD is a functional, rather than structural issue. Importantly, erectile dysfunction, although multifactorial but often partially because of low T levels, is also reported to wane after KT.49,50

It is well-established that drugs can interfere with gonadal function and steroidogenesis. Chronic corticosteroid use is associated with lower T levels.51 However, in our cohort with the majority of patients still taking corticosteroids at 12 months, we could not establish an association between cumulative corticosteroid dose and sex steroid levels. Similarly, mammalian target of rapamycin-inhibitors (e.g., sirolimus or everolimus) have been shown to negatively impact T levels.52 In our cohort, only 2 patients were treated with mammalian target of rapamycin-inhibition. The large majority of patients were treated with the calcineurin inhibitors tacrolimus and cyclosporin A. The latter drugs do not seem to negatively impact gonadal function, especially when compared with immunosuppressive therapy with sirolimus.53, 54, 55, 56

Similarly, with decreased T/LH levels, inhibin B/FSH levels were also lower in patients with CKD than in their controls. In ageing men, both inhibin B levels and the inhibin B/FSH ratio decline.45 Here, we show a decrease in inhibin B and inhibin B/FSH ratio in men with CKD compared with age-matched kidney-healthy controls, which is in contrast with earlier findings of similar inhibin B levels in male dialysis patients compared with healthy subjects.57, 58, 59 Our findings of decreased inhibin B/FSH ratio and therefore Sertoli cell failure further points towards primary testicular failure as the underlying pathophysiological mechanism of hypogonadism in male CKD.

In contrast to recovery of the Leydig cell function, Sertoli cell function seems to further decline after transplantation, at least in the early post-transplant period (Figure 5). This finding aligns with data from a study comprising 12 living donor kidney transplant recipients, treated with a similar immunosuppressive regimen.25 Hamdi et al.58 found the decrease in inhibin B levels after KT to be more pronounced in patients treated with tacrolimus compared with cyclosporin A. The acute stress and inflammatory response of the surgery itself might negatively impact spermatogenesis.60,61 In addition, adequate T production is needed for normal spermatogenesis, and the different kinetics in restoration of the T/LH ratio and inhibin B/FSH ratio might reflect the fact that Leydig cell function needs to improve before Sertoli cells can regain functionality.14 Alternatively, immunosuppressive therapy after transplantation could negatively impact Sertoli cell function and/or spermatogenesis, and therefore inhibin B levels, as has been shown for drugs such as ibuprofen.17 Negative impact of immunosuppressive drugs on spermatogenesis has been demonstrated for therapy with sirolimus in particular.62 Nevertheless, despite the inhibin B/FSH ratio not being fully restored 12 months after transplantation, several studies show improvement of sperm parameters after 6 months.63,64 However, restoration of sperm viability, motility, morphology, and concentration comparable to kidney-healthy controls can take up to 2 years.65 Further studies will need to determine the impact of these changes in inhibin B levels and association with spermatogenesis in this specific patient population, and also the impact on fertility outcomes.

As discussed previously, low testosterone levels in men with CKD have been linked to worse cardiovascular outcome and mortality, and also other comorbidities such as anemia, worse bone health, and sexual dysfunction.32,66, 67, 68 In earlier studies, potential benefit of testosterone replacement therapy in reducing erythropoietin requirement has been demonstrated, but concerns about safety have not been resolved yet.69,70 Sexual dysfunction in male patients with CKD is multifactorial, and not strongly correlated with T levels.71 Therefore, indication and benefit of testosterone replacement therapy in male patients with CKD remains unclear and controversial. In addition, by observing a spontaneous improvement of Leydig cell function after KT, it is reasonable to consider a cautious approach towards testosterone replacement therapy in male kidney transplant recipients.

Our study is not free from limitations. Not all serum samples were taken in the morning and in fasting-state. We provide information on biochemical diagnosis of hypogonadism, but data on erectile dysfunction, other hypogonadal symptoms, or sperm parameters are lacking. We show hormonal changes up to 12 months after transplantation, but do not have long-term information. Our study has several strengths. We used cutting-edge liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry, which is considered the gold standard technique for the measurement of sex steroid levels. We provide a comprehensive assessment of the gonadal status including gonadotrophins, prolactin, E2, inhibin B, SHBG, and calculated free hormone concentrations. We explore the T/LH ratio and inhibin B/ratio for the evaluation of testicular function.

In conclusion, hypogonadism in male patients with CKD is accompanied by a decreased T/LH ratio and inhibin B/FSH ratio compared with matched controls suggestive for primary testicular dysfunction or hypo-responsiveness. As such, male CKD can be considered as a state of premature testicular ageing. We show that Leydig cell function improves after KT. The reversibility of gonadal function paralleling kidney function recovery points towards functional Leydig cell dysfunction. Sertoli cell function is not restored 12 months after KT. Additional studies are required to further elucidate the pathophysiology behind this difference, and to unravel causative factors of testicular dysfunction in CKD.
