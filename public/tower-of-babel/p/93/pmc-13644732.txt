Cognitive reappraisal modulates neural responses to aversive sensory stimulation in autistic youth
Authors: Urvi T. Shah, Karrin Evans, Megan Banchik, Apurva Chaturvedi, Sapna Ramappa, Katie Wong, Jennifer A. Silvers, Shulamite A. Green
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Sensory over-responsivity (SOR) is highly prevalent and impairing in autism spectrum disorder (ASD). SOR has been associated with sensory-limbic hyperresponsivity and reduced prefrontal top-down regulation during sensory stimulation. There are few evidence-based treatments for SOR, but cognitive reappraisal, an emotion regulation strategy, has been shown to reduce negative feelings through activation of frontal brain regions. The current study examined whether reappraisal changes neural reactivity to aversive sensory stimulation in autistic compared to typically developing (TD) children.Methods:Forty-seven ASD and 45 age/IQ-matched TD youth aged 8–15 years participated in fMRI while experiencing aversive tactile stimulation on their arm and alternately focused either on how the stimulus made them feel (‘React’), or how they would describe the stimuli objectively (‘Reappraise’). Aversiveness ratings were provided after each stimulus. Parents reported on participants’ SOR severity.Results:Autistic youth showed reduced activation in sensory cortical regions during Reappraisal compared to React. Reappraisal also resulted in increased dorsomedial prefrontal cortex activation, with a trend toward more increases in the TD group but no significant group differences. Despite these reappraisal-related neural changes across both groups, only TD youth showed a reduction in aversiveness ratings for Reappraisal compared to React. Among ASD youth, higher SOR was associated with fewer decreases in sensory cortices but greater increases in frontal regions during Reappraisal compared to React. Older ASD youth also showed increased activity in the ventral prefrontal cortex during Reappraisal.Conclusions:Results suggest that cognitive reappraisal modulates neural responses during sensory processing and, more specifically, may reduce hyperactivity in sensory cortical regions previously shown to be associated with SOR. However, individual differences such as age and SOR severity may affect the extent to which top-down cognitive processes are uniquely engaged during reappraisal.

Introduction

Atypical sensory processing is a characteristic of many psychiatric and neurodevelopmental diagnoses. Specifically, sensory over-responsivity (SOR) is characterized by extreme aversive responses to sensory stimulation (e.g., rough textures) and is found across many clinical populations including anxiety, fetal alcohol spectrum disorder, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD; Ben-Sasson et al., 2009; Carr, Agnihotri, & Keightley, 2010; Parush, Sohmer, Steinberg, & Kaitz, 2007). SOR is particularly prevalent in autism spectrum disorder (ASD; Baranek, David, Poe, Stone, & Watson, 2006) and is a major source of impairment, as SOR symptoms are associated with greater deficits in social skills and adaptive functioning and greater mental health challenges (Green & Ben-Sasson, 2010; Istvan, Nevill, & Mazurek, 2020).

Prior literature investigating the neurobiological mechanisms of SOR has mainly focused on autism and has shown that, compared to typically developing (TD) children, autistic children exhibit overactivity and reduced habituation in sensory and limbic brain regions during aversive sensory stimulation (Green et al., 2015, 2019). Emerging literature suggests that similar neural overactivity may also be found across other populations with SOR, such as youth with anxiety (Cummings et al., 2024).

Notably, reduced prefrontal cortical regulation of sensory reactivity may play a key role in SOR. Autistic youth with higher SOR show reduced prefrontal downregulation of the amygdala in response to aversive sensory stimulation compared to those with low SOR who actually show enhanced prefrontal–amygdala functional connectivity (Green et al., 2015, 2019). Such prefrontal downregulation is thought to reduce sensory reactivity by helping maintain habituation to sensory stimulation in sensory-limbic regions (Green et al., 2019; Tsukano, Garcia, Dandu, & Kato, 2024).

Relatedly, autistic individuals may have a delayed developmental trajectory of these neural mechanisms underlying sensory regulation. Cross-sectional research indicates that TD youth increase prefrontal responses to aversive sensory stimulation with age until mid-adolescence, when these responses peak and then decline. In contrast, ASD youth show a later increase in mid-adolescence, suggesting a delayed maturation of sensory regulation (Cakar, Cummings, Bookheimer, Dapretto, & Green, 2023). Taken together, these studies suggest a key role of atypical top-down regulation in SOR. Thus, interventions that target regulation ability may be helpful for sensory-related difficulties, but empirically based interventions for SOR are currently limited.

Reappraisal is a cognitive regulatory strategy that can change an individual’s emotional response to a stimulus by reinterpreting its meaning. Reappraisal has been shown to promote emotion regulation through activation of frontal brain regions, in both TD youth and in populations with emotion regulation challenges. Specifically, cognitive reappraisal has been shown to engage ventral and dorsal prefrontal cortex and increase functional connectivity between multiple frontal regions with amygdala across ages and clinical populations (Buhle et al., 2014; Materna et al., 2019; Ochsner, Silvers, & Buhle, 2012; Pitskel, Bolling, Kaiser, Pelphrey, & Crowley, 2014; Pizzie, McDermott, Salem, & Kraemer, 2020; Silvers et al., 2017). The neural mechanisms underlying emotion regulation also show age-related changes. For example, while processing unpleasant images, younger children activate more ventromedial prefrontal cortex, while older children and adolescents activate more dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC), suggesting a ventral-to-dorsal shift in the frontal regions supporting emotion processing as children age into adolescence and adulthood (Silvers et al., 2017). To the best of our knowledge, reappraisal has not been tested for its effect on sensory reactivity or related underlying neural mechanisms. However, given the demonstrated importance of top-down prefrontal involvement to sensory regulation, reappraisal could be an effective strategy for mitigating overactive neural responses to sensory stimulation through engagement of prefrontal cortex.

Notably, reappraisal is a common component of interventions targeting emotion regulation, such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for anxiety (Beck, 2021). Research testing whether reappraisal specifically can engage mechanisms underlying sensory regulation and reduce neural hyperresponsivity to sensory stimulation can inform the creation of more successful, targeted interventions. If reappraisal is demonstrated to engage mechanisms underlying sensory regulation and reduce neural hyperresponsivity, it could potentially be incorporated into existing CBT interventions, given that CBT has already been adapted to target co-occurring challenges such as anxiety (Wood et al., 2009) and emotion regulation (Weiss et al., 2018) in youth with ASD.

The current study investigates the effectiveness of reappraisal as a strategy for engaging neural mechanisms associated with sensory regulation to reduce sensory reactivity for ASD as compared to TD youth. We hypothesized that reappraisal would engage regulatory brain regions in ASD and TD youth when compared to reacting to sensory stimulation as usual, but would lead to greater reappraisal-related reductions in sensory cortical responses in ASD as compared to TD youth given the neural sensory hyperreactivity previously seen in this group. Since SOR symptom severity impacts neural responses to sensory stimuli in autism (Green et al., 2015, 2019), we also explored how individual differences in SOR relate to the effectiveness of reappraisal for ASD youth. Furthermore, we investigated how age impacts the effectiveness of reappraisal in autism, given that prior studies show a delayed maturation of prefrontal engagement during sensory processing for ASD youth (Cakar et al., 2023).

Methods

Participants

Participants in this study included 47 children and adolescents with autism and 45 TD youth, aged 8–15 years (mean = 13.0, SD = 2.08). ASD diagnoses were confirmed using the Brief Observation Symptoms of Autism (BOSA; Lord et al., 2020), the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised (ADI-R; Lord, Rutter, & Le Couteur, 1994), and clinical judgment. Participants were recruited through the UCLA health system, public school outreach, summer camps and community-based autism organizations, the UCLA Center for Autism Research and Treatment (CART), and the SPARK ResearchMatch database. Fourteen ASD participants were taking psychoactive medications (see Appendix S1 for medication details). Exclusion criteria for both groups included genetic, neurological, or co-occurring, severe psychiatric diagnoses (i.e., bipolar disorder or schizophrenia) besides autism, and for the TD group, youth were further excluded if they had any psychiatric diagnoses or history of developmental delays. Participants were only included in the study if they passed a practice scan indicating they were able to stay still while following task instructions inside the MRI environment, and were verbally able to respond to instructions between scans. Participants were age/IQ-matched and did not differ significantly in sex, ethnicity, race, or scan motion (Table 1).

The original sample included 80 ASD and 54 TD participants. Thirty-one ASD and nine TD participants were excluded due to motion during fMRI. Details on motion exclusions and comparison of included versus excluded participants are in Appendix S2. Further information on sample size and power analyses is included in Appendix S3.

All study procedures were approved by the University of California, Los Angeles, Institutional Review Board. Parents provided written consent; children provided written assent.

fMRI sensory paradigm

This task, adapted from a commonly used fMRI reappraisal emotion regulation task (Silvers et al., 2017), involves training participants to use a cognitive strategy to regulate responses to aversive stimuli prior to the MRI (Appendix S4). During this task, participants experienced aversive tactile stimuli rubbed on their inner left forearm and were instructed to either experience the stimuli as they normally would (‘React’ condition) or think about describing the stimulus objectively as if they were a news reporter, to distance themselves from it emotionally (‘Reappraise’ condition). Prior to each condition, participants saw a 2-s cue: ‘feel’ or ‘reporter’ (seen and heard) along with a picture (see Figure 1). After each trial, participants saw a 4-s affect rating scale with numbers corresponding to emotion faces and used a button box to answer ‘How badly do you feel?’, with 1 indicating the least bad and 4 indicating the most bad. One TD participant did not respond to over half of the prompts and was therefore excluded from behavioral analyses, for a total of 47 ASD and 44 TD participants. The task included six blocks each of 20-s React and Reappraise trials, with two kinds of tactile stimuli (artificial grass and scratchy wool fabric) counterbalanced between trial types. Between each block, and at the beginning and end of the scan, participants saw a 13.5-s fixation cross for a total task time of 7 min, 55 s (Figure 1).

Behavioral measures

SOR severity was calculated using the hyperresponsivity scale on the Sensory Experiences Questionnaire Version 3.0 (Baranek, 2009), on which parents indicated patterns of sensory responding (hypo-responsivity, seeking sensations, and hyperresponsivity) in their children. The current study focused on hyperresponsivity (e.g., avoids loud noises) for which higher scores indicate increased SOR severity. Higher scores of hypo-responsivity indicated sensory under-responsivity (SUR) severity. One ASD participant did not complete this measure and was excluded from sensory-related analyses. Anxiety symptoms were measured using the Screen for Child Anxiety-Related Emotional Disorders (SCARED), Parent Version (Birmaher et al., 1999) to control for the co-occurrence of anxiety and SOR (Green & Ben-Sasson, 2010). The Wechsler Abbreviated Scales of Intelligence, Second Edition Full-Scale IQ (WASI-II; Wechsler, 2011) was used to measure cognitive functioning.

fMRI data acquisition

MRI data were acquired from a Siemens Prisma 3-Tesla scanner. Participants wore magnet-compatible noise-canceling headphones (Optoacoustics OptoActive II ANC) and earplugs to reduce scanner noise. Visual cues were projected onto a BOLDscreen monitor. The fMRI paradigm included 609 volumes (gradient-echo, TR = 800 ms, TE = 37 ms, flip angle = 52°, 208 mm FOV, 72 slices, voxel size = 2 × 2 × 2 mm). A structural MPRAGE T1-weighted scan was acquired for registration purposes (see Appendix S5 for details).

fMRI data analysis

Details on preprocessing and motion correction are in Appendix S6. Group-level analyses were conducted with FSL’s Local Analysis of Mixed Effects State (FLAME 1 + 2; Beckmann, Jenkinson, & Smith, 2003), with within-group results thresholded at z > 2.7 and corrected for multiple comparisons at p < .05. Between-group results were thresholded at z > 2.3, p < .05 (to be conservative, only clusters with peaks of z > 2.7 are reported). To identify which group was driving between-group differences (e.g., ASD activation vs. TD deactivation in ASD > TD group differences), we masked between-group and between-condition contrasts post hoc by within-group/within-condition contrasts at z > 1.7 and p > .05. Group × condition interactions were masked first by within-group analysis and then between-condition analysis to ensure results were driven by positive activation (e.g., ASD > TD React > Reappraise was masked by ASD React and ASD React > Reappraise to ensure results were driven by regions where the ASD group showed greater activation in React > Reappraise and not by regions where the TD group showed greater activation in Reappraise > React, which was instead presented in the TD > ASD Reappraise > React figure).

To examine the unique effects of SOR and age on neural responses in ASD youth, SOR and age scores were entered together as regressors in a whole-brain group analysis. Anxiety scores were included as covariates to identify activation that was correlated with SOR independent of anxiety. Results were thresholded at z > 2.7, p < .05. SOR analyses were only conducted within the ASD group as SOR scores for the TD group had very low variability.

ROI analyses

To examine group × condition interactions in key areas of interest, we extracted parameter estimates from somatosensory cortex, auditory cortex, dmPFC, and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), and conducted repeated-measures ANOVAs with condition (React vs. Reappraise) as a within-subjects factor and group as a between-subjects factor (ASD vs. TD) for each region. Somatosensory cortex, dmPFC, and OFC were chosen as a priori regions of interest based on prior literature that highlights the role of these regions in regulation and sensory processing (Buhle et al., 2014; Green et al., 2015, 2019; Ochsner et al., 2012; Silvers et al., 2017). We additionally decided to examine auditory cortex interactions based on results that showed effects in auditory regions despite a lack of auditory stimuli. For the auditory cortex and OFC analyses, we also included laterality (right vs. left) as an additional within-subjects factor. Structural masks were used to define functional regions of activation across all conditions and groups, so that each functional mask included all regions active in any condition or group within the relevant structural area (see Appendix S7 for details on how masks were created).

Results

Behavioral results

To test for differences in behavioral ratings of tactile stimuli, we conducted a 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA with condition (React vs. Reappraise) and stimulus (Grass vs. Sweater) as within-group variables and group (ASD vs. TD) as a between-group variable. Results revealed a main effect of stimulus (F(1,89) = 83.1, p < .001), such that, across groups, grass (mean = 2.34, SD = 0.91) was rated as more aversive than sweater (mean = 1.44, SD = 0.62). There was also a three-way interaction between diagnosis, stimuli, and prompt (F(1,89) = 6.08, p = .02). Post hoc tests splitting the ratings by group showed the three-way interaction was accounted for by the TD group only rating the grass stimulus lower for Reappraise (mean = 2.23, SD = 0.72) than React (mean = 2.38, SD = 0.77; t (43) = 2.36, p = .02; see Figure S1). There were no significant differences for ratings or prompt within the ASD group.

Whole-brain fMRI results

React condition.

In the React condition, both ASD and TD participants displayed activation in primary and associative sensory processing regions including Heschl’s gyrus, insula, operculum, postcentral gyrus, and superior parietal lobule, as well as lobule VIIIa and VIIb of the cerebellum. They also activated frontal regions (i.e., frontal pole, OFC, inferior frontal gyrus, superior frontal gyrus, cingulate gyrus), and subcortical regions including putamen, caudate, and thalamus. The direct group comparison showed that the ASD group had significantly greater activation than TD in the cerebellum. See Figure 2A; Table 2.

Reappraise condition.

In the Reappraise condition, both groups exhibited activation in primary and associative sensory processing regions including temporal pole, middle temporal gyrus, insular cortex, postcentral gyrus, and superior parietal lobule, and frontal regions including OFC and dmPFC/frontal pole. Both groups also activated the cerebellum as well as putamen and caudate. There were no significant group differences in activation. See Figure 2B; Table 3.

React > reappraise within-group comparisons.

ASD participants displayed greater activation in sensory processing regions including Heschl’s gyrus, planum temporale, insula, operculum, superior parietal lobule, and postcentral gyrus during React compared to Reappraise. There were no regions that TD participants activated more in React compared to Reappraise. See Figure 3A; Table 4.

React > reappraise between-group comparisons.

ASD participants exhibited significantly more differences in activation compared to TD in sensory processing regions including insula, Heschl’s gyrus, operculum, supramarginal gyrus, superior parietal lobule, and postcentral gyrus. See Figure 3A; Table 4.

Reappraise > react within-group comparisons.

TD participants exhibited greater activation in frontal regions including dmPFC/superior frontal gyrus as well as middle temporal gyrus during Reappraise compared to React. There were no regions where ASD participants showed greater activation for Reappraise compared to React. See Figure 3B; Table 5.

Reappraise > react between-group comparisons.

TD participants exhibited greater increases in activation in sensory processing association regions (i.e., insula, supramarginal gyrus, operculum, lateral occipital cortex) compared to ASD. See Figure 3B; Table 5.

ROI analyses

Auditory cortex.

Results from the repeated-measures ANOVA predicting responses in primary auditory cortex indicated a main effect of condition (F(1,90) = 4.57, p = .035) such that activation in the auditory cortex was higher for React than Reappraise, as well as a significant condition × group interaction (F(1,90) = 5.55, p = .021; see Figure 4A). A post hoc analysis splitting by diagnostic group showed that for ASD, but not TD participants, activation was higher during React than Reappraise (F(1,46) = 10.85, p = .002). There was also a main effect of laterality (F(1,90) = 35.87, p < .001) such that right auditory cortex activation was higher than the left. There were no other significant main effects or interactions.

Somatosensory cortex.

There was a significant condition × group interaction (F(1,90) = 4.09, p = .046) predicting somatosensory cortex activation (see Figure 4B). These results were driven by each diagnostic group showing an opposite trend; the ASD group decreased somatosensory cortex activation from the React to the Reappraisal condition while the TD group increased activation. There were no other significant main effects or interactions.

Orbitofrontal cortex.

There was a significant three-way interaction between condition, group, and laterality predicting OFC activity (F(1, 90) = 8.16, p = .005). Specifically, in the right OFC only, the ASD group showed greater decreases in the OFC from React to Reappraisal as compared to the TD group (see Figure 4C,D). There was also a main effect of laterality (F(1,90) = 29.25, p < .001) indicating that activation in the left OFC was higher than the right OFC across diagnostic groups and conditions, and a two-way interaction between condition and laterality (F(1,90) = 8.12, p = .005) explained by a larger overall effect of condition in the right compared to the left OFC. There were no other significant main effects or interactions.

Dorsomedial prefrontal cortex.

There was a trending effect of condition across groups (F(1,90) = 3,54, p = .06) with higher dmPFC activation for all participants during Reappraise (mean = 0.21, SD = 0.32) compared to React (mean = 0.15, SD = 0.28; see Figure 4E). There were no other significant main effects or interactions.

SOR correlation analyses

For ASD participants, youth with higher SOR showed more increases in neural activation in Reappraise compared to React. In particular, SOR was positively correlated with activation in sensory regions (i.e., Heschl’s gyrus, insular cortex, planum temporale, operculum, postcentral gyrus, and superior parietal lobule) and in cingulate gyrus. See Figure 5A; Table S1.

Age correlation analyses

In the React condition, younger ASD youth showed more activation, particularly in visual cortical regions including occipital pole and lateral occipital cortex (see Figure 5B). In the Reappraise condition, older ASD youth showed more activation in frontal pole and OFC. See Figure 5B; Table S2. Analyses examining the effect of age on neural responses for TD youth are shown in Figure S2 and Table S3.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the impacts of reappraisal on neural activity in age/IQ-matched ASD compared to TD youth during aversive sensory stimulation. Overall, results suggest that both groups successfully engaged in cognitive reappraisal, which modulated their neural responses to aversive sensory information. Notably, reappraisal significantly reduced sensory cortical hyperactivity in ASD youth. Reappraisal also modulated frontal activation across both groups, though this effect was more strongly driven by the TD group, and individual differences in frontal engagement were related to both SOR severity and age in the ASD group.

Reappraisal as a strategy for sensory regulation

When instructed to feel aversive sensory stimulation as they normally would, ASD youth exhibited neural overactivity to tactile sensory stimuli compared to TD youth, consistent with prior studies (Green et al., 2015, 2019). However, when instructed to use cognitive reappraisal to think about stimuli objectively, the diagnostic groups no longer showed significant differences in activation, suggesting reduced hyperactivity during this condition in ASD relative to TD youth.

Results directly comparing Reappraisal and React showed that reappraisal modulated behavioral responses to aversive tactile stimulation only in TD youth but modulated neural responses in both groups. Importantly, ASD youth decreased neural reactivity in primary sensory cortices during Reappraisal compared to React, suggesting that they processed sensory stimuli differently during reappraisal, potentially due to top-down regulation. While reappraisal was not associated with a reduction in behavioral ratings for ASD youth, a longer term intervention might be needed for brain changes to translate into conscious awareness of changes in sensory experience. Additionally, difficulty modulating ratings in the moment could be associated with task-switching challenges seen in ASD (Schmitz et al., 2006; Yerys et al., 2015) as well as interoceptive differences that affect how they perceive changes in internal experiences (Garfinkel et al., 2016).

Reappraisal-driven decreases in primary sensory cortical responses were not seen in TD youth, likely because, on average, this group experiences less over-responsivity to sensory stimuli and thus has lower baseline sensory cortical responses than the ASD group. While reappraisal seems to engage top-down cognitive processing for TD youth, as discussed below, this cognitive processing may not be needed to downregulate sensory cortical responses. Region-of-interest (ROI) results further support these findings. In both primary auditory and somatosensory cortices, there was an interaction effect such that ASD youth decreased activation in sensory cortices from React to Reappraisal more so than TD youth. Interestingly, the task itself did not include any sound, so reappraisal may have led ASD youth to decrease auditory cortical responses to aversive noise from the MRI.

TD youth increased dmPFC activation during Reappraisal compared to React. This frontal activation is consistent with prior literature on reappraisal for emotion regulation in TD youth (Buhle et al., 2014; Ochsner et al., 2012; Silvers et al., 2017). It is also consistent with our findings that TD more than ASD youth upregulated multiple sensory association and language processing regions during Reappraisal compared to React, likely indicating increased cognitive effort while describing stimuli objectively. In contrast to TD youth, ASD youth did not show significant increases in dmPFC during Reappraisal compared to React. However, there were no significant between-group differences in these dmPFC changes, suggesting that the ASD group may have demonstrated a similar pattern of Reappraisal-driven dmPFC increases which did not survive the within-group statistical thresholding. This was confirmed by further ROI analyses which showed that while dmPFC increases appeared stronger in the TD group, the dmPFC also increased somewhat in the ASD group, such that there was no significant group by condition interaction. Thus, while the TD group engaged dmPFC more on average, there may be individual differences within the ASD group that could predict which autistic participants are more likely to engage dmPFC. Indeed, as discussed in the next section, frontal and sensory association cortex activation during reappraisal varied with SOR severity and age in the ASD but not the TD group, supporting a pattern of increased heterogeneity within ASD youth.

The differences in frontal activation may also be due to differences in cognitive flexibility between groups. ASD youth showed sustained frontal activation throughout both React and Reappraise, which is consistent with prior research that shows that, in simple task-switching activities, ASD youth require greater recruitment of frontal regions (i.e., superior frontal, middle frontal, orbitofrontal, and inferior frontal gyri) as compared to controls (Schmitz et al., 2006; Yerys et al., 2015). Thus, ASD youth may have been engaging sustained cognitive effort throughout both conditions simply to hold the instructions in working memory and to shift between conditions. Another explanation may be that ASD youth are engaging similar cognitive effort to verbalize their experience in both conditions, whether feeling words in the React condition or descriptive words in the Reappraise condition, given the challenges with language, interoception, and emotion description that are common to ASD (DuBois, Ameis, Lai, Casanova, & Desarkar, 2016; Huggins, Donnan, Cameron, & Williams, 2021; Mody & Belliveau, 2013). However, regardless of greater effort required for task-switching or putting words to their experience, ASD youth still exhibited a decrease in neural sensory reactivity during reappraisal, so a difference in how higher order cognitive regions are engaged (e.g., which words are used to describe the sensory experience) may be more important to downregulating sensory responses than how much these regions are engaged.

In addition to dmPFC, both groups showed strong responses in ventral prefrontal cortex, particularly left OFC. The left compared to right OFC may be involved more in sensory regulation, at least during left-arm stimulation (Green et al., 2015, 2019). It is unclear if this lateralization is related to stimulation of the left side of the body specifically, or to differential functions of the left vs. right OFC. The left OFC may play a specific role in linguistic aspects of reappraisal (Cheng et al., 2022), which is consistent with the current study that involved participants describing stimuli. Interestingly, our results showed that, in right OFC only, ASD youth showed greater decreases in activation from React to Reappraisal than TD youth. For autistic youth, reappraisal may have led to a more efficient response in which they inhibited right OFC to prioritize recruitment of left OFC for sensory regulation.

Individual differences in SOR during sensory regulation

Lower SOR severity was associated with greater decreases in sensory cortical responses during Reappraisal compared to React, suggesting reappraisal as used in this study may have been less effective at reducing hyperactive sensory responses for youth with higher SOR. Interestingly, autistic youth with higher SOR also increased dmPFC more during Reappraisal compared to React, which is surprising given that these youth showed fewer reductions in sensory cortical hyperreactivity. It is possible that these results indicate a less efficient response for youth with higher SOR for whom increased effort is needed to engage reappraisal strategies, but without a resulting improvement in sensory cortical hyperreactivity. This finding is consistent with prior research suggesting that autistic youth with higher SOR show a less efficient response to a cognitive attentional redirection strategy during simultaneous sensory and social information processing (Green, Hernandez, Bowman, Bookheimer, & Dapretto, 2018). While it is possible that reappraisal may be a more effective strategy for children with lower SOR, these results could also indicate that reappraisal of aversive sensory information is more effortful for those who are most bothered by the stimuli and thus may require more practice to improve efficient and effective use of this strategy.

Effect of reappraisal across ages

Within ASD youth, age was positively correlated with ventral frontal activation during Reappraisal, which may suggest that older children were engaging in greater sensory regulation when doing the task and is consistent with Cakar et al. (2023) who found that this same region of ventrolateral PFC/OFC increased with age during aversive sensory stimulation. However, in TD youth, an age-related shift from ventral to dorsal prefrontal activation during emotion regulation has been observed (Silvers et al., 2017). Thus, our results could suggest an immature or delayed development of frontal engagement in youth with ASD with maturation of ventral frontal cortex during a time in adolescence when more dorsal regions are maturing in TD youth. These findings could help explain the trend toward fewer reappraisal-related dmPFC increases in ASD compared to TD in this study, and are consistent with prior research suggesting that frontal regions underlying sensory regulation appear to mature earlier in adolescence for TD compared to autistic youth, particularly those with higher levels of SOR (Cakar et al., 2023). However, age was not correlated with decreases in sensory cortical responses between the React and Reappraise conditions, suggesting that regardless of strategy or frontal regions recruited, reappraisal may be effective in decreasing hyperactive sensory responses across ages.

Age was also negatively correlated with visual cortical activation during React, suggesting that younger children have higher visual activation when feeling stimuli as normal. Prior research has shown that both autism and SOR are associated with increased activation in visual cortex during unrelated sensory stimulation (Green et al., 2019; Jao Keehn et al., 2017), which is interpreted as an overactive neural response to aversive sensory stimuli. Our results suggest that this effect may diminish with age, suggesting a more efficient sensory response as children get older. This effect was only seen in the React condition, which suggests that reappraisal may improve the ability of younger kids to efficiently respond to sensory stimuli and inhibit unrelated sensory cortices.

Limitations and future directions

The current study suggests that reappraisal is a promising strategy for sensory regulation. While ASD youth with cognitive abilities from borderline to well above average participated in the task, younger ASD youth in particular struggled to stay still. The combination of cognitive effort needed to stay still and perform the task may have proved overly taxing for young participants. Therefore, future studies should investigate this strategy outside of the scanner. Additionally, participants with co-occurring intellectual disability may have more difficulty implementing reappraisal, so future studies should investigate strategies for SOR specifically for ASD participants with lower cognitive abilities.

Furthermore, this study looked at immediate neural effects of a brief reappraisal training. Prior studies have shown that reappraisal can lead to immediate brain responses (Silvers et al., 2017), but successful CBT interventions require consistent practice of cognitive strategies over multiple weeks for lasting behavioral change (Weiss et al., 2018; Wood et al., 2009). Therefore, future studies should examine the long-term effectiveness of reappraisal training on real-world SOR symptoms and related daily functioning.

This study looked at regulation primarily for SOR, given its impact on quality of life and daily functioning, but SOR is only one of many sensory processing differences. It is possible that reappraisal may be a beneficial strategy for managing other sensory challenges, such as SUR, but future studies would need to evaluate this.

Conclusions

Overall, results suggest that both groups were able to follow reappraisal cues and think about the stimuli differently during cognitive reappraisal. Importantly, in autistic adolescents, reappraisal led to decreases in sensory cortical responses to aversive stimuli, regions that have previously been shown to be hyperactive in this group. Furthermore, reappraisal appeared effective across ages in autistic youth, although it may be more effortful or less efficient in those with higher levels of SOR. The current study shows potential for reappraisal to be used as a sensory regulation strategy in daily life for autistic individuals with relatively high verbal and cognitive skills and provides support for further research investigating the use of reappraisal with autistic adolescents with varying levels of SOR in clinical settings.
