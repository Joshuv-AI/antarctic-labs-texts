Development and cross-site validation of machine-learning models for diagnosis and prognosis of stable angina with and without obstructive coronary artery disease: a study protocol
Authors: Jiawen Deng, Shubh K Patel, Marinda Fung, Kiyan Heybati, Briana Layard, Bo Wang, Barry Rubin, Trevor Simard, Benjamin Hibbert, Todd Anderson, Vallijah Subasri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAngina with no obstructive coronary artery disease (ANOCA) affects millions and is frequently under-recognised because diagnostic pathways and risk tools predominantly target obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD). This protocol describes shared methods for two machine-learning (ML) studies: (1) differentiating ANOCA from stable angina with obstructive CAD and (2) predicting long-term mortality among patients with ANOCA and obstructive CAD.Methods and analysisWe will develop and cross-site validate ML classification models using a multicentre retrospective cohort drawn from the Alberta Provincial Project for Outcome Assessment in Coronary Heart Disease registry and institutional datasets from the University of Ottawa Heart Institute and the University Health Network. Eligible participants are adults (≥18 years) undergoing initial cardiac catheterisation for chest pain/anginal equivalents since 1995, excluding prior revascularisation, major structural heart disease and predefined non-anginal indications. Outcomes are (1) ANOCA (0% to <50% stenosis) versus obstructive CAD (≥50% stenosis) and (2) 1, 3 and 5-year mortality, modelled separately for ANOCA and obstructive CAD.Model development will use nested cross-validation with stratified k-fold inner-loop tuning and leave-one-site-out cross-validation for repeated external validation. Candidate predictors will be harmonised across sites, filtered for missingness and refined using expert/directed acyclic graph-guided selection plus Boruta and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator. Preprocessing includes appropriate encoding, missing-data imputation (multivariate imputation by chained equations) and feature scaling. Algorithms will include elastic-net logistic regression, random forest, LightGBM and multilayer perceptron models; hyperparameters will be optimised via Bayesian optimisation. Performance and threshold tuning will be reported. Explainability and subgroup fairness will be assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations. Final models will be deployed as a web-based clinical risk calculator.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval has been obtained from the University of Calgary and the University Health Network (#24-5916). Analyses will use deidentified data in secure environments; only aggregate results will be reported. Findings will be disseminated via peer-reviewed publications, conferences and a web-based calculator.

Introduction

Chest pain frequently leads to emergency department (ED) visits and hospitalisations.1 Although most chest pain cases in the ED do not have a cardiac origin, about 20% are diagnosed with coronary artery disease (CAD).2 Among patients with ischaemic chest pain, many have angina with no obstructive coronary artery disease (ANOCA), which is characterised by stable angina without significant coronary artery stenosis (no stenosis >50%).3 ANOCA affects an estimated 3 to 4 million people annually36 and presents substantial diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, which results in considerable healthcare resource utilisation such as hospitalisations and repeat cardiac catheterisations.79 Patients with ANOCA are typically younger, have fewer cardiac risk factors compared with those with obstructive CAD, and are more likely to be women.1012

The diagnosis and management of ANOCA is faced with several challenges. First, current guidelines typically focus on the pretest probability of obstructive CAD for risk stratification in angina patients.13 14 This approach inadvertently categorises patients with ANOCA as low-risk and limits further diagnostic testing or extensive therapy.3 While established pretest probabilities and prognostic models—such as the Diamond-Forrester Classification,15 CAD Consortium Clinical Score16 and the Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) Risk Score17—exist to guide clinical decisions for obstructive CAD patients, validated risk stratification tools for patients with ANOCA remain scarce. Although a recent study has proposed and externally validated a risk prediction model for adverse outcomes in ANOCA using a smaller cohort from a tertiary care centre in China, broader validation across larger, diverse populations is lacking.18 In addition, traditional stress testing often fails to detect occult coronary abnormalities in patients with ANOCA.19 20 As such, patients with ANOCA often experience high symptom burdens and may need to visit multiple healthcare providers before receiving an accurate diagnosis. Moreover, patients with ANOCA can experience high risks of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and mortality that is comparable to patients with stable angina and obstructive CAD, as shown in a study by Kissel et al.21 A previous study by Jespersen et al also found that patients with ANOCA had a 52% higher risk of MACE and a 29% higher risk of all-cause mortality compared with a reference population without ischaemic heart disease.22

This proposal outlines the methodology for the development of machine-learning (ML) models for improving the diagnosis and prognosis of patients with ANOCA. ML is a data modelling technique that can identify complex and non-linear patterns in large datasets.23 24 Our first objective is to develop ML models to predict the pretest probability of an angina patient having non-obstructive versus obstructive CAD. This assessment can help clinicians decide whether to recommend further non-invasive testing or to proceed directly to coronary angiography to assess for obstructive CAD. Our second objective is to develop ML models that can predict the long-term mortality risk of patients with ANOCA and patients with stable angina with obstructive CAD to provide patients with angina individualised mortality risk predictions.

Methods and analysis

The proposed studies will be conducted and reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis+Artificial Intelligence (AI) Checklist for Prediction Model Development and Evaluation.25

Ethics and dissemination

This study has received ethical approval from the University of Calgary’s Institutional Ethics Review Board (REB20-1935), the Ottawa Health Science Network Research Ethics Board (OHSN-REB 20240469-01H and 20190224-01H), and the University Health Network Research Ethics Board (#24-5916). Informed consent was obtained at catheterization, with a waiver for use of anonymized retrospective data where applicable. Analyses will be conducted on deidentified data within secure institutional environments, and only aggregate results will be reported.

Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations, and via a web-based clinical risk calculator with accompanying documentation. In line with data-sharing agreements and privacy requirements, patient-level data will not be publicly released; where feasible, we will deposit curated, non-identifiable study artefacts (eg, code, model documentation) in an appropriate public repository and provide pathways for qualified researchers to request under ethics approval and data use agreements.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this study. As this is a secondary analysis of deidentified registry and institutional data, there are no plans to disseminate results directly to individual participants.

Data sources

Data for model training and validation will be extracted from the Alberta Provincial Project for Outcome Assessment in Coronary Heart Disease (APPROACH) database, as well as institutional datasets from the University of Ottawa Heart Institute and the University Health Network in Canada. The APPROACH database includes data from: (1) University of Alberta Hospital, (2) Royal Alexandra Hospital and (3) Foothills Medical Center. In total, data from five institutions are included in this model development effort. This reflects a large-scale multicentre cohort to ensure generalisability and robustness of our clinical AI tool and findings.

Inclusion criteria for the dataset include adults (≥18 years of age) who underwent initial cardiac catheterisation due to chest pain or anginal equivalent symptoms since 1995. Patients with a history of coronary artery bypass grafting, percutaneous coronary intervention, left main disease, left ventricular ejection fraction ≤50%, and prespecified indications (ie, congenital heart disease, congestive heart failure, pretransplant workup, post-transplant workup, serious arrhythmia and valvular heart disease) for catheterisation will be excluded. These criteria remove patients whose prior revascularisation, major structural heart disease or alternative clinical indications would independently influence coronary anatomy, symptom origin and outcomes; excluding them preserves a homogeneous, de-novo chest-pain cohort and prevents confounding in model development and validation. Data collected include patient comorbidities, laboratory results, medications at the time of catheterisation and sociodemographic characteristics. These data will be collated according to common definitions across sites (table 1).

All analyses will be conducted in secure institutional environments on deidentified/anonymised datasets with access controls; only aggregate results will be reported.

Sample size considerations

The study sample size is determined by the available retrospective registry and institutional data across the five participating sites and is expected to include approximately N≈60 000 eligible adults. This sample size is anticipated to provide a sufficient effective sample size for model development and repeated external validation under the leave-one-site-out (LOSO) framework. For the mortality models, feasibility will depend on the number of observed deaths at each time horizon (1, 3 and 5 years), which will be reported overall and by site.

Outcomes of interest

This protocol describes the methodology for two ML modelling studies that will share the same model development and validation workflows.

The first study aims to develop ML classification models to differentiate ANOCA from stable angina with obstructive CAD in adult patients presenting with angina. ANOCA is defined as either normal coronary arteries (0% stenosis in all epicardial coronary arteries) or minimal disease (>0% but <50% stenosis in any epicardial coronary artery). Stable angina with obstructive CAD is defined as ≥50% stenosis in any epicardial coronary artery. The study will focus on predicting the probability of the patient having ANOCA, and the predicted probability will be transformed into a binary classification using an optimised decision threshold.

The second study aims to develop multiple ML classification models to predict the risk of 1-year, 3-year and 5-year mortality in patients with ANOCA and patients with stable angina with obstructive CAD. Modelling will be conducted separately for ANOCA and stable angina with obstructive CAD patients. Similar to the first study, the modelling effort will focus on predicting the probability of mortality, and the predicted probability will be transformed into a binary classification to categorise patients into high and low-mortality risk groups based on a decision threshold.

Nested cross-validation

As described in detail previously,26 27 a nested cross-validation (CV) methodology will be used to evaluate the performance and consistency of the overall ML modelling process (figure 1). Nested CV involves two CV loops. The inner CV loop serves a similar purpose as normal non-nested CV and is used to perform feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, threshold tuning and model selection. In the outer CV loop, one fold is held out as the test set while the remaining folds are used for training involving the inner CV loop. The main function of the outer loop is to estimate the performance of the best-performing model as selected by the inner loop.26 27

For our proposed study, we will use stratified k-fold CV as our inner CV loop for model tuning and selection. For the outer CV loop, we will use LOSO-CV. In our study, LOSO-CV involves training models using data from four of the five included hospital sites and externally validating the best-performing model on data from the remaining site. This is repeated five times so that each hospital site serves as the external validation set at least once. By repeatedly externally validating our modelling process, we can better assess how models produced by our modelling process will perform on new, unseen data. Following the nested CV process, we will run the inner CV loop on the entire dataset to generate the final production model for deployment into a clinical calculator.

Causal risk assessment

Risk and potential failure modes will be assessed using a directed acyclic graph (DAG) to make explicit the data-generating process, key causal pathways and plausible sources of bias and dataset shift, including changes in case mix, measurement or coding practices and downstream treatment decisions. The DAG, informed by clinical expertise and literature-based priors, will be used to distinguish associative from potentially causal relationships between predictors and outcomes, guiding focused feature selection by prioritising variables that are causally upstream of the outcome while excluding or penalising mediators, colliders and non-informative confounders. Identified risks will then be summarised and prioritised using a Risk Assessment Matrix (likelihood×severity), which will guide the selection of outcomes, subgroup evaluations and sensitivity analyses.28

Feature selection

Candidate features (table 1) will be first filtered based on availability (≤50% of missing data) and expert domain. For each training set in the outer CV loop, the dimension of the feature set will be further reduced using random-forest-based Boruta29 and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) penalised logistic regression30 to reduce noise in the dataset, increase speed of point-of-care applications and improve model explainability. The α of the LASSO penalised logistic regression will be determined using a grid search of 1000 α values to minimise the LASSO objective function across stratified k-fold CV. A union of features selected by the two methods will be chosen as the final feature set used for predictive modelling.

Data preprocessing

Within each iteration of the outer CV loop, the training set will be split into 5 folds of different training and testing subsets for the inner CV loop. A data preprocessing pipeline will be constructed to identify and impute missing data, correct for class imbalance, and improve compatibility with ML algorithms before the data are inputted into the ML models (figure 2).

Categorical data encoding

Ordinal categorical features will be encoded as ordinal integer values. Categorical features with no clear ordinality will be transformed via one-hot encoding.31

Missing data imputation

We will assume data to be missing at random and perform missing data imputation using Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE).32 The number of MICE iterations will be determined based on the highest percentage of data missing (eg, if 30% of data needs to be imputed for one feature, then MICE will be run for 30 iterations).33 34 The training and testing subsets will be imputed separately to avoid data leakage.35 When assessing and tuning ML algorithms with native missing data handling methods, we will treat the use of MICE as a hyperparameter to trial both MICE and the algorithms’ native missing data handling approaches.

Data imbalance and resampling

We will not use resampling techniques as we aim to develop models that can produce real-world probability predictions based on probability distributions in the training dataset. Resampling will distort the outcome probability in the training dataset and render the probability estimates meaningless.26

Feature scaling

Before the dataset is entered into dimensionality reduction preprocessing steps and ML algorithms, the normality of each continuous feature within the dataset will be assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test36 and by visual inspection of the features’ histograms.37 If the distributions of the continuous features are close to normal, then we will perform Z-score normalisation to centre the feature data around the mean and scale the data according to its SD.38 If the distributions of the continuous features are determined to be skewed and not close to normal, the continuous features will be transformed using robust normalisation, which centres the data around the median and scales the data around its IQR.38 Compared with Z-score normalisation, robust normalisation is more robust towards the presence of outlier values in the dataset.39

Each algorithm will be tuned to minimise cross-entropy loss across stratified k-fold CV. The full list of hyperparameters that will be tuned for each model is tabulated in online supplemental table S1–S4. The optimal hyperparameters will be selected using Bayesian optimisation.47 Bayesian optimisation is empirically considered to outperform traditional methods such as grid search and random search.48 Because random search can reliably identify hyperparameter combinations from the top 5% of the most performant combinations with 60 iterations,47 we aim to perform Bayesian optimisation at 60 iterations as a minimum. Otherwise, the number of iterations will be set empirically to 20 times the number of hyperparameters in the search space.

Calibration

Because the objective of our hyperparameter tuning will be minimising average cross-entropy loss, and no resampling will be done, our model will likely be well-calibrated. Nevertheless, we will assess the calibration of our models using calibration metrics such as Brier score, and apply post hoc calibration methods such as Platt scaling or spline-based calibration, if required.49 50

Threshold selection

Following hyperparameter tuning, each model will undergo threshold tuning to maximise their binary classification performance. Youden’s Index51 will be calculated for every classification threshold between 0.01 and 0.99 at an interval of 0.01, and the threshold with the highest average Youden’s Index across stratified k-fold CV will be selected as the most optimal threshold. For clinical applications, we will additionally create high-sensitivity thresholds that aim to keep the sensitivity higher than and closest to 80%.

Model selection and evaluation

We will follow a previously published framework proposed for the evaluation of clinical prediction models52 to perform model selection and external validation. The classification performance of each model will be assessed using the area under the curve of the receiver operating characteristic curve, area under the precision-recall curve, F1-score, balanced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and Youden’s Index.53 Calibration performance will be assessed using log loss, Brier score, calibration curve slope and calibration curve intercept.

During model selection within the training set of each outer CV loop iteration, all metrics will be produced and averaged across k-fold CV, and 95% CIs will be used to assess performance variance. The optimal model will be identified via the lowest log loss.

For external validation within each outer CV loop iteration, the same set of classification and calibration metrics will be generated using the most optimal model configuration and data from the held-out site not involved in model tuning and selection. The external validation metrics will be averaged across all five iterations of the outer CV loop to assess the generalisation performance of our modelling process, and 95% CI will be used to assess the variance of the generalisation performance.

Model explainability and fairness

To understand how the final production model makes individual predictions and to assess potential model bias arising from over-reliance on patient demographics, we will use SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).54 We will also aggregate SHAP results over all patients in the training dataset to assess for global feature importance. Subgroup analysis will be performed to evaluate model performance across key demographic and clinical groups. The DAG will be leveraged to test causal robustness to feature changes by conducting adversarial testing to simulate input perturbations based on domain knowledge and identify sources of error in worst-case scenarios.55 56 Subgroup performance and fairness will be audited across sex, age bins and hospital type.

Model deployment

The final production models will be integrated into web-based clinical risk calculators based on Shiny. SHAP waterfall plots will be provided to allow users to provide local model explainability for each individual prediction.

Implementation of SHAP in the clinical risk calculator requires access to the full training dataset at runtime to allow SHAP to generate perturbations around a prediction to understand the local behaviours of the model. To facilitate SHAP without compromising the safety and privacy of our training data, we will generate a synthetic dataset for SHAP deployment using a Differentially Private Conditional Tabular Generative Adversarial Network (DP-CTGAN).57 Hyperparameters of the DP-CTGAN will be tuned manually to minimise generator and discriminator loss. The privacy budget ε will be set to between 0 and 10. We aim to generate a synthetic dataset that yields comparable SHAP value predictions and SHAP base values for use with the clinical risk calculator.

Clinical utility

In addition to discrimination and calibration, we will assess clinical utility using Decision Curve Analysis. Net benefit will be calculated across a range of clinically plausible threshold probabilities reflecting the minimum predicted risk at which a clinician would change management (eg, pursue invasive angiography, initiate escalated follow-up, or intensify preventive therapy). We will compare the net benefit of model-guided strategies to default strategies (intervene on all vs intervene on none) and to existing approaches used in current practice, where applicable, including guideline-based pretest probability/risk tools for obstructive CAD (eg, Diamond-Forrester and CAD Consortium models/clinical score) and commonly used clinical risk scores where relevant to the modelled endpoint (eg, TIMI). We will report net benefit curves and threshold ranges where the model provides incremental benefit, alongside the implied intervention rate at each threshold.

Statistical software

The modelling and evaluation processes will be completed using Python. Boruta-based feature selection will be conducted using the BorutaPy package, and LASSO-based feature selection will be conducted using scikit-learn. ML models will be fitted, tuned, calibrated and evaluated using scikit-learn, lightgbm, pytorch and scikit-optimize. SHAP will be implemented using shap.

Discussions

The proposed studies aim to develop ML models to enhance the diagnostic approach and mortality risk stratification for patients with angina. Traditional diagnostic and prognostic tools for angina, which are primarily designed for obstructive CAD patients, often fail to identify or provide proper risk prediction for patients with ANOCA. Leveraging a large, multicentre dataset and modern ML techniques, we seek to close this gap and deliver personalised, evidence-based guidance for all angina phenotypes.

Our models are designed to support clinical decisions that directly influence patient management and downstream resource utilisation within established standards of care and routine patient care pathways. For the diagnostic model, the intended use is to inform testing and triage decisions within the referral pathway by estimating the probability of ANOCA versus obstructive CAD, helping clinicians consider whether to proceed directly to invasive coronary angiography versus prioritise non-invasive evaluation and, when the likelihood of ANOCA is high, plan targeted coronary function testing to evaluate vasomotor and microvascular mechanisms. For the prognostic models, the intended use is to enable risk-stratified follow-up and preventive care by providing individualised predictions of 1, 3 and 5-year mortality risk for patients with ANOCA and obstructive CAD, thereby identifying higher-risk individuals who may benefit from closer surveillance, earlier specialty referral and intensification of evidence-based risk-factor modification and therapies.

Our proposed ML workflow has several notable strengths.58 Using a nested CV framework with LOSO-CV will allow us to perform repeated external validation of our modelling pipeline across diverse hospital settings. Advanced feature selection techniques, such as Boruta, will substantially reduce the dimensionality of our dataset and make our model more accessible for clinicians to use with fewer included features. Bayesian optimisation will allow us to systematically explore a wide range of hyperparameter combinations and identify the optimal settings for our ML models. By assessing permutation importance and incorporating Shapley values, we will be able to provide clear insights into how predictions are generated, which helps clinicians to understand and trust our models’ recommendations.

Despite the strengths of our approach, we acknowledge several limitations. The reliance on retrospective data may introduce biases due to inconsistent data collection procedures across different sites. Model performance may not directly translate to unselected primary-care chest pain populations with different disease prevalence and referral thresholds (referral/work-up bias); further validation and potential recalibration are required before use in primary care. Additionally, because our diagnostic labels are based on clinically used stenosis thresholds (<50% vs ≥50%) captured in routine angiography reporting, continuous vessel-level quantitative percent stenosis is not uniformly available across sites and years, and visual estimation may introduce inter-observer variability. We assume data are missing at random for imputation; if missingness is informative, performance estimates may be biased. The prognostic analysis treats 1, 3 and 5-year mortality as binary endpoints and therefore does not fully exploit time-to-event data or censoring; future work will assess dedicated survival modelling to enhance prognostic relevance. Practice patterns and treatments may also change over time and across institutions, which can lead to dataset shift and calibration drift after deployment, requiring monitoring and potential model updating.59 Mechanistic data relevant to ANOCA endotypes (eg, coronary function testing/physiologic indices) are not consistently available across the full cohort, which may limit granularity in characterising ANOCA subtypes. Where feasible, we will conduct targeted analyses to audit subgroup performance; however, fairness assessments may be restricted to specific datasets and remain subject to unmeasured confounding and unequal subgroup representation. Additionally, the computational demands of the nested CV approach may limit the performance budget available for Bayesian optimisation, as well as making it more difficult to replicate our study in resource-constrained settings. Lastly, while including data from multiple hospital sites can improve the generalisability of our model, these hospital sites may share similar triage or diagnostic protocols, which may constrain external validity beyond comparable health systems.60

Overall, our protocol outlines a comprehensive ML modelling workflow which can be used to create models to improve the diagnosis and management of ANOCA and angina with obstructive CAD.
