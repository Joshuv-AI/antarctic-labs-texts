Integrated multi-omics implicates the gut microbiota and bilirubin in abdominal fat deposition in Huainan yellow chickens
Authors: Yaxin Li, Zichun Dai, Jie Liu, Rong Chen, Huanxi Zhu, Mingming Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Huainan yellow chicken is prized for its meat quality, but excessive abdominal fat deposition hampers its production efficiency. To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we compared high-fat deposition (HFD) and low-fat deposition (LFD) chickens using lipid metabolomics and 16S rRNA sequencing. The HFD group exhibited significantly higher carcass weight and abdominal fat index. Concordantly, serum levels of total bilirubin, triglycerides, cholesterol, glucose, and very-low-density lipoprotein were elevated, alongside upregulated expression of hepatic lipogenic genes (FASN, PPARγ, and PPARα). Liver lipidomics identified 144 significantly altered metabolites. Ileal microbiota analysis revealed a substantial enrichment of Lactobacillus and reductions in Escherichia-Shigella and Candidatus_Arthromitus in HFD chickens. The integrated analysis revealed strong correlations between microbial abundance and lipid metabolites, suggesting that gut microbes, especially Lactobacillus, may influence bilirubin metabolism and enterohepatic circulation. Collectively, our findings implicate a synergistic interaction where the gut microbiota (especially Lactobacillus) may modulate bilirubin enterohepatic circulation and hepatic lipogenesis, regulating abdominal fat deposition in Huainan yellow chickens, providing a novel theoretical basis for modulating fat accumulation in poultry through microbial strategies.Keywords: Huainan yellow chicken, Abdominal fat deposition, Gut microbiota, Bilirubin

Introduction

The Huainan yellow chicken, an indigenous breed in China, is recognized as a source of high-quality dietary protein. Although its meat is highly palatable, a notable drawback is its tendency for excessive fat deposition, particularly in the abdominal region. Excessive fat accumulation reduces production efficiency by increasing rearing costs, worsening feed conversion, lowering meat quality, and potentially affecting consumer health (Chao et al., 2020; Moreira et al., 2015). Abdominal fat deposition is a highly heritable trait influenced by both genetic and nutritional factors (Fan et al., 2023; Matusheski et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2017). Individual variations in nutrient absorption capacity are closely linked to the genetic background. The small intestine, particularly its microbiota and metabolite profile, is critical in affecting nutrient assimilation (Wahlström et al., 2016). It is essential to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying divergent fat deposition, especially of abdominal fat, and identifying key differential proteins or metabolites are of great importance for reducing production costs and improving meat quality in Huainan yellow chickens (Shelton et al., 2023).

Abdominal fat, encompassing the adipose tissue surrounding the gizzard, not only serves as a storage depot but also as a metabolically active endocrine organ. It secretes various adipokines, including adiponectin and leptin, which play critical roles in the systemic regulation of lipid metabolism and energy homeostasis (Ali et al., 2013). Abdominal fat development involves adipocyte proliferation (genetically regulated) and triglyceride accumulation from hepatic lipid transport (Cartwright, 1991; Sun et al., 2024). A deeper understanding of these mechanisms is essential to develop strategies to modulate fat deposition in poultry.

The liver functions as the primary site of lipogenesis, providing lipids to both peripheral tissues and itself. Hepatocytes in chickens can synthesize saturated fatty acids from non-lipid substrates, such as dietary carbohydrates, through key enzymes, including fatty acid synthase (FASN) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor beta (PPARβ) (Cai et al., 2009; Rodríguez-Cruz et al., 2012). Subsequently, these saturated fatty acids are desaturated by enzymes, such as glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase (GPAM), stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD), and fatty acid desaturase 2 (FADS2), to form monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids (Lan et al., 2023). These products are further esterified into triglycerides, which are packaged and exported from the liver into the bloodstream through apolipoprotein-associated very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL). This process delivers lipids to the peripheral tissues to provide energy and contributes to fat deposition. Thus, hepatic de novo lipogenesis and subsequent lipid transport are the major sources of abdominal fat in chickens and play critical roles in abdominal fat deposition. Notably, the liver also modulates the metabolism of bilirubin, a molecule increasingly implicated in lipid regulation. While preliminary observations in poultry suggest bilirubin may influence fat partitioning (Hu et al., 2025), its specific relationship with abdominal fat deposition remains unclear.

The intestine serves as the major site for dietary digestion and absorption and has been increasingly implicated in regulating fat deposition in chickens (Lyu et al., 2024). In microbial communities, the gut microbiota modulates host lipid metabolism (De Vadder et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2024), energy homeostasis, and central appetite signaling (Torres-Fuentes et al., 2017). For instance, the transplantation of cecal or fecal microbiota from obese mice into germ-free recipients resulted in increased adiposity (Ridaura et al., 2013). Additionally, high-fat diets have been shown to reduce microbial diversity and alter the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio, namely, a pattern often associated with obesity in humans (Wan et al., 2019). These findings underscore the importance of gut microbiota in shaping nutrient absorption and lipid metabolism, thereby significantly influencing fat deposition.

Within the small intestine, the ileum is particularly critical for lipid absorption and host-microbiota crosstalk. As the distal segment, the ileum is responsible for the uptake of bile acid-conjugated lipids and fat-soluble vitamins, and it harbors a distinct microbial community that actively modulates host energy metabolism and immune homeostasis (Lyu et al., 2024). While the cecum has been extensively studied in poultry due to its dense microbial population and role in fiber fermentation (Rybicka et al., 2025), the ileum—despite being the primary site for nutrient assimilation—remains relatively undercharacterized. Therefore, understanding the microbial composition and metabolic activity of the ileum is essential for how intestinal function contributes to systemic fat deposition.

The molecular mechanisms of abdominal fat deposition in chickens, particularly the role of intestinal factors, have garnered considerable interest (Bao et al., 2025; Chao et al., 2020). However, the crosstalk between lipid metabolites and the gut microbiota across indigenous breeds is poorly understood. To address this issue, in our study, we employ an integrated approach of liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) lipid omics and 16S rRNA sequencing to examine the gut-liver axis, aiming to elucidate the physiological mechanisms governing fat deposition in Huainan yellow-feather chicken.

Matenrials and methods

Ethics approval

The experimental protocol was approved by the Research Committee of the Jiangsu Academy of Agricultural Sciences and was conducted in compliance with the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals (Decree No. 63, is-sued by the Jiangsu Academy of Agricultural Sciences on 8 July 2014). Euthanasia by exsanguination following electrical stunning conforms to the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020).

Animal and experimental design

The experiment was conducted at the Liuhe Experimental Poultry Farm of the Jiangsu Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Nanjing, China). One hundred 50-day-old Huainan yellow hens (a local breed from Anhui Province, China) were individually housed in battery cages under controlled conditions (temperature: 24°C; humidity: 85–90%). All birds were fed ad libitum with a growth pellet diet containing 20% crude protein and 12.54 MJ/kg metabolizable energy, with free access to water throughout the trial.

At 110 days of age, blood samples were collected from the wing vein using heparinized syringes (containing 100 IU heparin). Subsequently, all hens were subjected to electrical stunning (100 V, 50 Hz, 4 s) and euthanized via exsanguination following severance of the jugular vein and carotid artery. After slaughter, feathers, head, internal organs, and claws were removed, and the remaining carcass, including abdominal fat, was weighed to obtain the carcass weight. Abdominal adipose tissue and other extragastric fat deposits were carefully separated and weighed. The liver tissue was excised and weighed accordingly. An 8 cm segment from the mid-ileum was excised, and the luminal contents were collected, immediately snap-frozen on ice, and stored at –80°C for subsequent DNA extraction.

The abdominal fat and liver indices were calculated using Microsoft Excel with the following formula:

Based on the normal distribution of abdominal fat indices, 10 individuals with the highest values were assigned to the high-fat deposition (HFD) group, and 10 individuals with the lowest values were assigned to the low-fat deposition (LFD) group.

Blood samples were centrifuged at 4,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C within 4 h of collection, and the obtained serum (supernatant) was stored at –20°C until analysis. The concentrations of serum total protein, albumin, glutamic-pyruvic transaminase, glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, total bilirubin, VLDL, low-density lipoproteins (LDL), and high-density lipoproteins (HDL) were measured using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Measurements were performed using a Full-wavelength microplate reader (model XM-08).

RNA isolation, primer synthesis, and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the liver tissue using TRIzol reagent and a commercial kit (Catalog No: #1559602, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene-specific primers for β-actin,FASN, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα), and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) were designed with Primer-BLAST (NCBI) and listed in the Table S1. PCR reactions were conducted in a 20 μL reaction volume with SYBR Green I Master Mix reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and 2.5 pmol of specific primer pairs. qRT-PCR was performed using an ABI PRISM 7500 system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method, with β-actin as the internal reference gene. The geNorm algorithm was used to evaluate the stability of reference gene (β-actin) expression between HFD group and LFD group. The M value indicates was 0.385, indicating that β-actin could be used as an internal reference gene.

Lipid metabolomics analysis

Untargeted lipidomic profiling was conducted by GeneDenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Lipids were extracted using a methyl tert‑butyl ether (MTBE)-based protocol. Briefly, approximately 25 mg of liver tissue from 18 selected samples (9 per group) was homogenized. To the homogenate, 800 μL of a methanol/MTBE mixture (1:3, v/v) was added. The mixture was then sonicated in an ice‑water bath for 30 min, incubated at −20 °C for 1 h, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The upper organic layer was collected and dried under a gentle stream of nitrogen. The dried extracts were reconstituted in 100 μL of isopropanol/acetonitrile (9:1, v/v) and subjected to LC‑MS/MS analysis using a Vanquish UHPLC system coupled with an Orbitrap mass spectrometer. Separation was performed on a UPLC HSS T3 column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Separation was performed on a UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) maintained at 45°C. The mobile phase consisted of (A) acetonitrile/water (6:4, v/v) with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid, and (B) isopropanol/acetonitrile (9:1, v/v) with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid. The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min. Electrospray ionization (ESI) was operated in both positive and negative modes.

Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots from all tissue extracts and were injected at regular intervals throughout the analytical sequence to monitor system stability. Class-specific internal standards (Avanti Polar Lipids, Alabaster, AL, USA) were added prior to extraction for quantification. The relative standard deviation (RSD) of peak areas for major lipids in QC samples was < 15%, indicating good data reproducibility.

The raw data were processed using MassHunter B.08 (Agilent Technologies) for feature extraction. Orthogonal projections and Principal component analysis (PCA) to latent structure discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed using SIMCA-P (v13.0; Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden). Variables were Pareto-scaled, and metabolites with variable importance in P < 0.05 and projection (VIP) > 1.0 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

16S rRNA sequencing of ileal mucosa

Microbial genomic DNA was extracted from ileal mucosal samples using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). High-quality DNA samples with OD260/280 ratios between 1.9 and 2.1 were used as templates for 16S rRNA gene amplification. The V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primers (341F: 5’-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3’ and 806R: 5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). Raw paired-end sequencing reads were quality-filtered and trimmed using Trimmomatic. Forward and reverse reads were then merged using FLASH. Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) were clustered from the resulting high-quality sequences at a 97% similarity threshold using the UPARSE pipeline within the QIIME 1.9.1 ecosystem. Chimeric sequences were identified and removed against the Gold reference database using UCHIME. The representative sequence of each OTU was assigned taxonomy using the USEARCH software against the Silva 16S rRNA database (version 138). Finally, an OTU table was generated for subsequent ecological and statistical analyses. After quality filtering, an average of 80,599 high-quality reads per sample were obtained (Supplementary Table S2). Rarefaction curves plateaued, indicating sufficient sequencing depth to capture the microbial diversity (Supplementary Figure S1).

α-diversity indices (including Observed Species, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices) and β-diversity (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity) were calculated based on the normalized OTU table using QIIME2 (version 2023.5). Statistical significance of α-diversity between groups was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) based on Bray-Curtis distance was employed to evaluate β diversity differences. To infer the functional potential of the microbial communities, Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2 (PICRUSt2) was used to predict MetaCyc metabolic pathways from the 16S rRNA gene data. Functional predictions were generated using PICRUSt2 based on 16S rRNA gene data. While informative, these are inferred metabolic potentials and not direct measures of functional gene expression. Pathway enrichment P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) method.

Correlation analysis of gut microbiome and liver tissue metabolome

Pairwise Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between microbial genera and liver tissue metabolites. For the microbiota-metabolite Pearson correlation analysis, P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) method. Only correlations with |r| > 0.8 and an FDR-adjusted P-value (q-value) ≤ 0.05 were considered significant. Network visualization was performed using Cytoscape (http://www.cytoscape.org).

Hematoxylin-eosin staining evaluation

Liver tissue samples were subjected to H&E and Oil Red O staining following standard protocols. For H&E staining, tissues were rinsed with cold saline, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm. The stained sections were scanned with a Pannoramic DESK scanner (3DHISTECH, Hungary) for morphometric analysis of adipocytes.

For Oil Red O staining, samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. After staining, the sections were examined under a Leica DMi8 optical microscope, and images were captured with the accompanying LAS X software to visualize lipid droplets.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. The normality of the abdominal fat index was assessed using the frequency analysis module of SPSS 29.0 (IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Group comparisons of serum parameters, carcass weights, and tissue indices were performed using paired sample t-tests using SPSS. All figures were generated using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Changes in carcass weight, liver index, and abdominal fat percent

At 110 days of age, the HFD group of Huainan yellow-feathered hens exhibited significantly higher carcass weight than the LFD group. Postslaughter analyses confirmed significant differences in carcass weight and abdominal fat index (P < 0.01) between the two groups. No significant differences were observed in the liver index (Fig. 1).

Differences in carcass weight, abdominal fat, and liver indices between the low-fat deposition (LFD) and high-fat deposition (HFD) groups. ** P < 0.01, and ns P > 0.05.

Changes in serum metabolites

Through serum metabolite profiling, we identified several significant alterations in the HFD group (Fig. 2). In particular, concentrations of triglycerides and total cholesterol were significantly elevated (P < 0.05), as were the glucose and VLDL levels (P < 0.01). Total bilirubin was significantly increased (P < 0.001). Conversely, the levels of total protein, albumin, HDL, and LDL remained comparable between the two groups.

Changes in serum metabolites between low-fat deposition (LFD) and high-fat deposi-tion (HFD) groups. Data are shown as mean values ± standard error of the mean (SEM). * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, and *** P < 0.001.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Oil Red O staining of liver tissues

H&E staining of the liver tissues revealed a normal hepatic ultrastructure in the LFD group, characterized by hepatocytes with abundant cytoplasm and centrally located nuclei arranged radially around the central vein (Fig. 3A). In contrast, The HFD group showed disrupted hepatic cords, compressed hepatocytes, and abundant cytoplasmic lipid droplets (Fig. 3B). Oil Red O staining indicated a sparse distribution of lipid droplets in the LFD group, whereas the HFD group showed abundant and clustered lipid droplets (Fig. 3C and 3D).

Histopathological examination and lipid accumulation in the liver tissues of Huainan yellow chicken (H&E staining and Oil Red O staining, x 40 magnification).

Expression of FASN, PPARγ, and PPARα genes in the liver tissues

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying differential fat deposition, we analyzed the expression of key lipid metabolism-related genes (FASN, PPARγ, and PPARα) in liver tissue. The HFD group showed significant upregulation of FASN, PPARγ, and PPARα mRNA in the liver tissues, with PPARγ exhibiting the most pronounced increase (P < 0.001; Fig. 4).

mRNA expressions of FASN, PPARγ, and PPARα genes of the liver tissues between low-fat deposition (LFD) and high-fat deposition (HFD) groups. Data are shown as mean ± SEM. * P < 0.05, and *** P < 0.001.

Lipid metabolomic profiles of the liver tissue

Ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS) analysis identified 2,064 lipid species across 40 lipid classes in liver tissues. Among these, 144 lipid species were significantly differentially expressed between the control and HFD groups (variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1, P < 0.05; Fig. 5). They belonged to 17 classes: cardiolipins (CLs), ceramides (Cer), diglycerides (DGs), hexosylceramides (HexCer), lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), lysophosphatidyleth-anolamine (LPE), phosphatidic acid (PA), phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidyl-ethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylglycerol (PG), phosphatidylinositol (PI), phospha-tidylinositol phosphate (PIP), sphingomyelin (SM), sphingosine (SPH), stigmasterol ester (StE), and triglycerides (TG). Differential abundance was visualized using volcano plots (Figs. 6A and 6B), and heat maps of the 99 differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) in negative and positive ion modes are shown in Figs. 6C and 6D, respectively.

Discussion

Through integrated multi-omics analysis, we have revealed a significant association between abdominal fat deposition, liver metabolism and the ileal microbiome. VLDL particles are triglyceride-rich lipoproteins that are primarily synthesized in the liver (Shelness and Sellers, 2001). They play an essential role in lipid metabolism by transporting endogenously synthesized triglycerides from the liver to peripheral tissues, where they are either utilized for energy or stored as fat (Gibbons et al., 2004). In our study, HFD broilers had elevated lipid accumulation within the liver accompanied by significantly elevated serum VLDL concentrations and a greater abdominal fat index compared with LFD broilers. These observations imply an enhanced rate of hepatic lipid export and delivery to storage depots, such as abdominal adipose tissue, in HFD birds, thereby promoting overall fat deposition (Feingold, 2022). Further supporting this proposition, differential expressions of key lipogenic genes, such as FASN and PPARγ, were detected in liver tissues, indicating enhanced adipogenesis and lipid storage capacity in HFD chickens. The elevated serum bilirubin levels in the HFD group may represent a compensatory response to lipid accumulation, as bilirubin has been reported to protect against obesity and hepatic steatosis.

Bilirubin, namely, a yellow bile pigment derived from the catabolism of heme following the breakdown of red blood cells (Creeden et al., 2021), has been consistently associated with anti-obesity effects in both human and rodent models. Epidemiological and experimental studies have indicated an inverse correlation between serum bilirubin levels and the prevalence of obesity (Kipp et al., 2023) and improved metabolic profiles in rodents (Hinds and Stec, 2018). Bilirubin is known to activate PPARα, a key regulator that enhances fatty acid β-oxidation and suppresses lipogenesis, thereby mitigating hepatic lipid accumulation. Extensive research supports the conserved role of bilirubin as a modulator of metabolic homeostasis across species (Creeden et al., 2021; Tatikolov et al., 2025). In diet-induced obese mice, bilirubin significantly reduced hepatic fat, triglyceride accumulation, de novo lipogenesis, and VLDL levels, further supporting its role in regulating lipid metabolism and reducing fat accumulation via PPARα (Hinds et al., 2020). While bilirubin is negatively correlated with obesity in mammals, we observed the opposite trend in chickens, where bilirubin concentration was positively correlated with fat deposition. This finding aligns with recent observations in poultry; for instance, Hu et al. (2025) reported a similar positive association in a crossbred duck population, suggesting that this may be a common avian response to lipid overload. As proposed by Tatikolov et al. (2025), elevated bilirubin in birds may reflect an enhanced antioxidant and metabolic adaptation to hepatic stress induced by increased lipogenesis, rather than mirroring the purely protective role observed in mammals. Thus, elevated bilirubin levels in HFD birds may represent a physiological adaptive response to hyperlipidemia or a compensatory mechanism to alleviate metabolic stress.

Following its secretion from the liver, bilirubin is transported through bile into the intestinal lumen, where it undergoes microbial transformation into urobilinoids through the action of specific gut bacteria. Several microbial taxa, including Clostridium, Bifidobacterium, Bacteroides, Lactobacillus, and Saccharomyces, have been identified as key contributors to bilirubin reduction and metabolism of bilirubin (Hamoud et al., 2018; Lin et al., 2025). In our study, microbial MetaCyc pathways related to lipid and bilirubin metabolism, such as "secondary bile acid biosynthesis" and "glycerolipid metabolism," were enriched. Additionally, we observed significant differences in the relative abundances of Bacteroides and Lactobacillus between HFD and LFD chickens. Notably, the enrichment of the glycerolipid metabolism pathway, along with alterations in serum cholesterol and triglycerides, suggests a potential mechanistic link between gut microbial composition and lipid metabolism. Critically, Lactobacillus is also a key modifier of bile acid metabolism through its bile salt hydrolase activity (Devlin et al., 2015; Staley et al., 2017). Therefore, we hypothesize that the observed enrichment of Lactobacillus may not only influence bilirubin but also bile acid metabolism, and that this dual impact could synergistically disrupt enterohepatic signaling. Consequently, our findings suggest that the gut microbiota may profoundly impact the combined metabolism of key biliary components (bilirubin and bile acids), which collectively influence host lipid homeostasis and contribute to a high-fat deposition phenotype (Vítek et al., 2005).

The paradoxical coexistence of elevated serum bilirubin and increased fat deposition in broilers likely reflects a compensatory host response to metabolic stress. Bilirubin serves dual roles as an antioxidant mitigating lipid peroxidation and a signaling molecule that activates PPARα to promote fatty acid oxidation. This response is modulated by the gut microbiota, particularly enriched Lactobacillus, which may influence bilirubin enterohepatic cycling. Furthermore, microbial functional analysis suggested significant enrichment in pathways including secondary and primary bile acid biosynthesis, phosphotransferase system, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and glycerolipid metabolism. These microbial functions were strongly correlated with hepatic lipid metabolites, suggesting the collaborative role of bilirubin, bile acids, and gut microbes in regulating intestinal-liver crosstalk and systemic lipid homeostasis.

In liver metabolomics, several triglycerides (TGs) and phosphatidylethanolamines (PEs) were significantly elevated in HFD livers among the DEMs. This aligns with the upregulated hepatic expression of FASN and SCD and GPAM (a key enzyme in TG synthesis), indicating a concerted shift towards increased lipid synthesis and storage (Lan et al., 2023). Furthermore, the strong positive correlation between Lactobacillus and specific CLs like CL (20:5_16:1_16:1_18:2)-H is noteworthy, as CLs are mitochondrial membrane lipids crucial for β-oxidation; their alteration may reflect mitochondrial adaptation to lipid flux (Pessayre et al., 2004).

Although our integrated multi-omics approach reveals a significant association between the gut microbiota–bilirubin axis and abdominal fat deposition, our study has several limitations. First, the correlative nature of our findings precludes the establishment of causal relationships. Future studies employing fecal microbiota transplantation or germ-free animal models are warranted to validate the causal role of specific microbes. Furthermore, the lack of functional validation for the specific microbial taxa (e.g., Lactobacillus) and key lipid metabolites identified in this study represents another limitation. Their precise mechanistic roles in influencing host bilirubin metabolism and fat deposition remain to be experimentally confirmed through targeted interventions. Finally, the precise mechanistic interplay between bilirubin metabolism and gut microbiota, particularly the role of Lactobacillus, remains to be fully elucidated. Nonetheless, our study highlights the gut microbiota–bilirubin interaction as a promising target for further research aimed at modulating fat deposition in poultry. Moreover, comparative studies involving slow-growing indigenous breeds such as Huainan yellow chicken and fast-growing commercial broilers will be essential to determine whether this axis represents a universal regulatory mechanism or a breed-specific adaptation.

Conclusion

In this multi-omics study, we demonstrate that excessive abdominal fat deposition in Huainan yellow chickens is associated with distinct alterations in gut microbiota (enriched Lactobacillus), elevated systemic bilirubin levels, and profound reprogramming of hepatic lipid metabolism. Our findings highlight the gut microbiota-bilirubin-liver axis as a key component in regulating fat deposition, offering novel insights and potential microbial targets for strategies aimed at reducing excessive obesity in poultry breeding.
