Identifying patients with mild-to-moderate COPD who require intensified initial treatment to control annualized clinically important deterioration
Authors: Zihui Wang, Yumin Zhou, Wei-jie Guan, Junfeng Lin, Guannan Cai, Jinping Zheng, Fan Wu, Zhishan Deng, Nan-shan Zhong, Pixin Ran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) guidelines recommend selecting initial treatment based on the severity of respiratory symptoms or exacerbation history. However, some patients still experience clinically important deterioration (CID) despite this strategy. Therefore, we aimed to identify the risk factors to guide initial treatment, focusing on mild-to-moderate COPD patients whose annualized CID cannot be controlled by a single-bronchodilator.MethodsPatients in the tiotropium group from a randomized controlled trial were included. Over 2 years, annualized CID was defined as forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) decline at least 100 ml, COPD assessment test (CAT) increasing at least 2 points, or having experienced at least two moderate or one severe acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) in this study. Random coefficients model within a bayesian framework was adapted to estimate the change in FEV1 and CAT scores. The logistic regression analysis was adapted to detect the risk factors. The risk of annualized CID was evaluated using the incidence risk ratio (IRR) for patients with risk factors.ResultsOf 312 patients with mild-to-moderate COPD receiving tiotropium, 29.2% still experienced annualized CID. The proportions of patients who developed annualized CID regarding FEV1, CAT, and AECOPD was 20.5, 8.0, and 6.1%, respectively. Post-bronchodilator FEV1:FVC ≤ 60% (OR = 1.73, 95%CI: 1.06–2.84) and smoking pack-years ≥50 (OR = 1.95, 95%CI: 1.11–3.41) were associated with higher risk of experiencing annualized CID. The IRR of annualized CID was increased with more risk factors at baseline.ConclusionPatients with more prominent airflow limitation and higher smoking pack-years warrant intensified initial treatment to control their annualized CID.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a heterogeneous chronic airway disease characterized by chronic cough, sputum production, and usually progressive airflow limitation1. Globally, the prevalence of COPD among people aged above 40 years has reached 12.6%, with patients with mild-to-moderate COPD accounting for 85.7% of this population2. Although patients with severe to very severe COPD constituted a small portion, they accounted for a disproportionately large portion of healthcare burden, particularly hospitalizations3,4. If treatment is neglected or management is inadequate, patients with mild-to-moderate COPD may progress to more severe stages, further exacerbating the disease burden. Therefore, the management of mild-to-moderate COPD which focuses on delaying disease progression via early-stage interventions is necessary.

In the Tiotropium In The Early-Stage COPD (Tie-COPD) randomized trial5, tiotropium maintenance therapy could delay the progression of COPD by slowing the decline of forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) and reducing the frequency of COPD exacerbations over 2 years. These findings have significant implications for COPD treatment and support a shift from symptomatic relief of severe COPD to delaying the progression of mild-to-moderate COPD.

The Tie-COPD trial suggested that long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA) maintenance therapy could delay the overall progression in patients with mild-to-moderate COPD. In clinical practice, LAMA has been regarded as the first-line initial therapy for mild-to-moderate COPD. However, there remains a subset of patients who continue to experience annualized clinically important deterioration (CID) in lung function, symptoms, and exacerbations despite the LAMA maintenance therapy. Additionally, the deterioration in FEV1, COPD assessment test (CAT), and acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD) could be driven by specific risk factors. A history of frequent exacerbations is a predictor of further AECOPD6, while the risk factors for the decline in FEV1 include smoking7, a positive response to bronchodilators8, and a significantly decreased FEV1:FVC ratio9. However, it remains unclear which risk factors contribute more to the annualized CID among patients with mild-to-moderate COPD who have already been receiving LAMA therapy.

We prespecified the hypothesis that specific risk factors could identify patients with mild-to-moderate COPD who require intensified initial therapy, because their annualized CID cannot be controlled by single-bronchodilator treatment. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a post-hoc analysis on the Tie-COPD randomized controlled trial to investigate the risk factors that could identify patients with mild-to-moderate COPD who required intensified initial treatment to control annualized CID. This study could provide the evidence for individualized strategies of intervention in patients with mild-to-moderate COPD.

Methods

Study design and patients

The design of the Tie-COPD trial has been published previously5,10. Study participants were patients with mild-to-moderate COPD who were primarily recruited from the community COPD screening projects. The Tie-COPD trial conducted pulmonary function tests after bronchodilator (BD) inhalation at 0, 1, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months. The CAT score, modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) score, and AECOPD episodes were collected at the scheduled visits.

The key inclusion criteria were as follows: age between 40 and 85 years; diagnosed with COPD, defined as the ratio of FEV1 to forced vital capacity (FEV1:FVC) less than 0.70 after inhaling 400 μg salbutamol for 20 min, which was compatible with chronic respiratory symptoms and/or a history of exposure to COPD risk factors (smoking, air pollution, and biomass fuel use); and post-BD FEV1 ≥ 50% predicted. The main exclusion criteria included AECOPD within 4 weeks before screening, other coexisting pulmonary diseases (such as asthma), critical illnesses (such as cancer), and severe systemic diseases.

Patients from the intervention group of the high-dose N-acetylcysteine (NAC) trial were included to validate the risk factors for annualized CID. All study protocols were approved by the local institutional review boards or independent ethics committees of the participating sites. Each participant provided written informed consent.

Assessment

We included patients who adhered to tiotropium (compliance≥70%) to determine the disease progression and influencing factors on background maintenance monotherapy. Patients were divided into CID and non-CID groups based on whether they had experienced annualized CID (Fig. 1).

Enrollment of patients with mild-to-moderate COPD on tiotropium maintenance therapy.

Three hundred eighty-eight patients in the Tie-COPD trial underwent pulmonary function tests after receiving tiotropium treatment. Of these, 312 patients with an adherence≥70% were considered to have achieved maintenance therapy. Patients were grouped according to whether they reached any annualized CID in FEV1, CAT, or AECOPD over 2 years. Abbreviations: COPD, Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease; FEV1, Forced Expiratory Volume in One Second; CAT, COPD Assessment Test; AECOPD, Acute Exacerbation of COPD; CID, Clinically Important Deterioration.

We defined annualized CID as a sustained decline in FEV1 at least 100 mL11, a continuous increase in CAT at least 2 points12, or having at least 2 moderate or 1 severe AECOPD over 2 years13,14. Short-term CID cannot reflect the rate of lung function decline (e.g. FEV1 decline of 100 ml over 10 years indicates a favorable prognosis but would still meet the CID criterion) and may also be affected by the variability of repeated assessments (e.g., a CAT score may occasionally increase by 2 points and than return to baseline at subsequent follow-ups). Thus, all indicators were confined to the changes over 2 years to assess the annualized disease progression rather than short-term fluctuations.

Pulmonary function tests were performed by trained technicians based on international standards. Pre-BD pulmonary function tests were conducted on the day of the visit before using any medication. Post-BD pulmonary function tests were performed at 20 min after inhaling 400 μg salbutamol (GlaxoSmithKline, UK). The predicted value of FEV1 and FEV1:FVC ratio were calculated using the recognized reference equation for Chinese [i.e., the modified European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC93) reference]15. In COPD patients, post-BD FEV1 ranging from 50 to 80% predicted (not including 80%) was classified as GOLD stage 2, while the post-BD FEV1 greater than 80% was classified as GOLD stage 1.

In the Tie-COPD trial, symptom scores and the history of AECOPD were also assessed in the scheduled visits. The CAT score is a questionnaire used to evaluate the health status and severity of symptoms of COPD patients, and includes several aspects such as cough, sputum, chest tightness, and shortness of breath. The mMRC score is a questionnaire used to evaluate the severity of shortness of breath. A higher score (e.g., ≥ 10 in CAT or 2 in mMRC) indicates more severe symptoms. AECOPD is defined as the sustained worsening of at least two major symptoms (cough, sputum volume, purulence, wheezing, or shortness of breath) for more than 48 h, after excluding confounding conditions (i.e., heart failure, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, or arrhythmias). The duration and severity of AECOPD were ascertained by reviewing daily records and during on-site medical consultations. Moderate AECOPD was defined as an outpatient or emergency visit requiring antibiotics and oral corticosteroids, and severe AECOPD as an exacerbation requiring hospitalization.

External validation

External validation was conducted to evaluate whether the risk factors identified in the Tie-COPD study maintain their predictive capability in other contexts. We included COPD patients from the intervention group of the high-dose NAC trial, whose study design was published recently16. Lung function tests, CAT scores, mMRC score, and AECOPD events were collected at scheduled visits. We included patients who received maintenance NAC (adherence ≥70%) to assess disease progression and risk factors. Patients were categorized into the CID and non-CID groups based on whether they experienced annualized CID (Figure S1).

Statistical analysis

We utilized a random coefficients model within a Bayesian framework to estimate the annual change in FEV1 and symptom scores for each patient17,18. The random coefficients model allows each patient to have specific intercepts (i.e., the baseline FEV1) and slopes (i.e., the annual change in FEV1). The Bayesian framework employs the Markov Chain Monte Carlo simulations to draw representative samples from the posterior distribution, with 10,000 iterations starting with four chains. We assessed the quality of sampling and the fitness of the model based on the lower Rhat values (close to 1), a larger effective sample size, and smaller Monte Carlo standard errors. In light that patients receiving tiotropium treatment typically show a significant improvement in FEV1 during the first month, followed by a gradual decline, we calculated the annual decline in lung function for this group starting from the first month rather than the baseline visit5.

The annual incidence rate of moderate-to-severe AECOPD was calculated by dividing the total number of AECOPD by the duration of follow-up for each participant. We presented the distribution of FEV1, CAT, and AECOPD by using histograms. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as frequency (%). Between-group comparisons of the clinical characteristics were performed using t-tests, analysis-of-variance, Kruskal-Wallis tests, chi-square tests, or Fisher’s exact tests (depending on the data distribution and number of groups).

The logistic regression model was adapted to assess the associations of candidate indicators, including CAT score ≥10, mMRC score ≥2, positive bronchodilator responsiveness in FEV1, post-BD FEV1:FVC ≤ 60%, smoking pack-years ≥50 pack-years, body-mass index ≤18, and blood eosinophil counts (BECs) ≥ 300/μL, with the risk of annualized CID in the future. The candidate indicators that were statistically significant in the univariate model were then included in the multivariate model. We also assessed the incidence risk and incidence risk ratio (IRR) in the population with different numbers of risk factors at baseline.

The sample size for the Tie-COPD main study was calculated based on the statistical power required to detect a clinically meaningful difference in FEV1 between the intervention and placebo groups. The present study conducted post-hoc analyses using data from this trial. The P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The analysis used R software version 4.2 (rstanarm, lme4, tableone, and survival packages).

Results

In the Tie-COPD trial, 388 individuals underwent at least one pulmonary function test after receiving tiotropium. Of these, 312 had a compliance rate greater than 70% and were considered to have received tiotropium maintenance treatment. Based on the annualized progression in FEV1, CAT, or AECOPD during 2-year follow-up, 91 patients (29.2%) were divided into the CID group, and 221 into the non-CID group (Fig. 1).

Over 2 years, the most common subtype of annualized CID was a significant decline in FEV1 (20.5%), followed by the significant increase in CAT score (8.0%) and having experienced 2 moderate or 1 severe AECOPD (6.1%, Fig. 2). There were 24.4% (76 of 312) of patients who experienced one type of annualized CID, while 4.2% (13 of 312) experienced two types of annualized CID. The baseline characteristics of the CID group and the non-CID group are shown in Table 1. The baseline FEV1 levels did not differ between the two groups, despite the markedly lower FEV1:FVC ratios in the CID group.

Results from the univariate logistic regression analysis (Fig. 3) suggested that post-BD FEV1:FVC ≤ 60% (OR = 1.73, 95%CI 1.06 to 2.84) and smoking history ≥50 pack-years (OR = 1.95, 95%CI 1.11 to 3.41) were associated with a significantly increased risk of annualized CID. BECs ≥ 300 cells/μL (OR = 2.58, 95%CI 1.10 to 6.03) was associated with annualized CID in terms of CAT score. In contrast, CAT score ≥10 and mMRC score ≥2 were not associated with annualized CID risk under tiotropium maintenance therapy. Multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed that post-BD FEV1:FVC ≤ 60% (OR = 1.63, 95%CI 0.99 to 2.69) and smoking pack-years ≥50 pack-years (OR = 1.82, 95%CI 1.03 to 3.21) were independently associated with increased risk of annualized CID, and BECs ≥300 cells/μL (OR = 2.53, 95%CI 1.07 to 5.96) was independently associated with annualized CID in terms of CAT scores.

The baseline characteristics of the included and excluded patients were generally comparable (Table S1), but patients with a poor compliance (e.g., adherence <70%) had lower FEV1 and higher mMRC scores. During follow-up, the use of respiratory concomitant medications when clinically stable was consistent between CID and non-CID groups (Table S2), but the use of inhaled corticosteroids and long-acting beta-agonists was more frequent in the subgroup having experienced annualized CID.

To assess whether the risk factors identified in the Tie-COPD study are predictive in other contexts, we included 361 patients with moderate to severe COPD, who were treated with maintenance NAC in the external validation cohort (Figure S1). In both univariate and multivariate regression analyses, the post-bronchodilator FEV1:FVC ≤ 60% was consistently associated with an increased risk of annualized CID in FEV1, CAT, or AECOPD (Figure S2).

Discussion

Based on the post-hoc analysis of the Tie-COPD trial, 29.2% of patients with mild-to-moderate COPD still suffered from annualized CIDs over two years despite LAMA maintenance therapy, indicating that they did require intensified initial therapy. The symptom scores (e.g., CAT and mMRC score) were not associated with uncontrolled annualized CID, whereas a post-BD FEV1:FVC ratio ≤60% and a smoking history of ≥50 pack-years were associated with increased annualized CID risk. More intensive interventions should be applied to the patient population with risk factors to control the high annualized CID risk (IRR = 2.83).

We performed a post-hoc analysis based on two multicenter randomized controlled trials focusing on early-stage intervention in patients with mild-to-moderate COPD. The Tie-COPD and NAC trial enrolled patients with mild-to-moderate COPD who were identified through community screening. The patients had mild symptoms, rarely used medications, and mostly were diagnosed for the first time, reflecting the real-world scenario in community settings. Our study conducted repeated pulmonary function tests, CAT symptom assessments, AECOPD records, and medication adherence evaluations over two years. Each assessment followed strict operational protocols, and each visit was conducted when clinically stable. The rigorous standard operations and regular follow-up have ensured an accurate assessment of disease progression (i.e., annualized CID).

We found that among patients receiving tiotropium maintenance treatment, 20.5, 8.0, and 6.1% experienced annualized CIDs in terms of FEV1, CAT, and AECOPD, respectively. The potential mechanisms by which patients with mild-to-moderate COPD on LAMA maintenance therapy still experienced deterioration might be multifaceted. These discrepancies may be explained by several factors. First, the heterogeneity in the pathogenesis of COPD. Although all COPD patients are characterized by persistent airflow limitation (FEV1:FVC ratio <0.7), patients with the same GOLD grading exhibited a substantial heterogeneity in the degree of emphysema, BECs, and the expression of inflammatory mediators19–21. For some patients, disease progression is mainly driven by the heightened inflammation. They relied solely on bronchodilators for maintenance therapy without controlling either the local or systemic inflammation which was insufficient to control disease progression. Second, exposure to air pollution, occupational hazards, and other unobserved environmental risk factors. Long-term exposure to indoor and outdoor air pollutants, such as cooking smoke and traffic exhaust, can negatively impact lung function and increase the risk of AECOPD in COPD patients22–24. Third, the prevailing pathogens such as bacteria and viruses that elicited AECOPD. Viral and bacterial infections are major triggers of AECOPD25. Maintenance therapy with bronchodilators did not thoroughly prevent the transmission of pathogens in the population or alter the susceptibility to these pathogens. Furthermore, some underlying genetic diseases. Although less prevalent, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency can also contribute to the development of COPD26, which could not be effectively abrogated despite bronchodilator treatment.

A CAT score ≥10 and a mMRC score ≥2 were not associated with an increased risk of annualized CID, suggesting that a high baseline CAT score and mMRC score did not imply a higher risk for annualized CID. Therefore, we advocate for adopting the risk factors that could reflect the annualized CID risk to determine the initial therapy. Specifically, a post-BD FEV1:FVC ratio less than 60% and a smoking pack-years exceeding 50 pack-years independently associated with an increased risk of annualized CID, these findings have been supported by the previous studies. For example, patients with lower FEV1:FVC ratios had higher all-cause mortality, more frequent AECOPD, and faster FEV1 decline9. Cigarette smoking led to a more rapid decline in FEV17. Therefore, the risk factors might help guide escalated initial therapy to control for disease progression.

We also utilized the NAC trial to validate whether the risk factors identified in the post-hoc analysis of Tie-COPD trial could consistently demonstrate the predictive capability for annualized CID risk in other settings. The results indicated that a post-bronchodilator FEV1:FVC ratio of ≤60% was consistently associated with an increased risk of annualized CID (Figure S2). This suggests that our conclusions may remain applicable in other contexts.

Another important question is which therapeutic regimen could better control annualized CID in patients with mild-to-moderate COPD. Although there remains a lack of high-level evidence supporting the superiority of other inhaled agents in slowing the decline of FEV1 in mild-to-moderate COPD, the addition of LABA to the existing LAMA therapy could be more effective for improving lung function and slowering the decline in light of the documented effects of LAMA/LABA and ICS/LABA/LAMA in improving peak FEV127–29. Additionally, recent studies have shown that new dual phosphodiesterase inhibitors (e.g. Ensifentrine) can further improve FEV1 on top of a single bronchodilator (LAMA or LABA)30, which might be an emerging approach to delay the decline in lung function. Notably, some of the factors contributing to increased annualized CID risk can be readily addressed through lifestyle modifications, such as nutritional supplementation and proactive smoking cessation31–34. Improvement of the low FEV1:FVC ratio may be achieved through the use of more potent bronchodilators or combinations of bronchodilators35. The risk of AECOPD could be ameliorated via multiple measures, including the use of more potent bronchodilators, influenza and pneumonia vaccination, phosphodiesterase inhibitors, and biologics36–39.

This study has several limitations. First, the conclusions were derived from randomized controlled trials (RCTs), which typically employ stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria. This introduces potential selection bias, as the study population may not fully represent the broader COPD patient demographic encountered in real-world clinical practice. Consequently, the generalizability of these findings to real-world clinical practice remains uncertain, particularly regarding their applicability to patients with COPD and comorbid conditions, such as asthma. Second, the frequency of acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) prior to trial enrollment was not recorded, introducing information bias that hinders a comprehensive evaluation of exacerbation history as a prognostic factor. Third, the analyses were restricted to predetermined sample size and selected indicators available in the trial database. This constraint prevented the identification of additional risk factors for COPD progression, even in patients with acceptable adherence to inhalation therapy, including ongoing smoking during follow-up, continued exposure to harmful environmental factors (e.g., occupational hazards, indoor air pollution from biomass fuels, or ambient air pollution), adherence to recommended vaccinations (e.g., influenza and pneumococcal), nutritional status, and physical activity levels. As a result, although the study provides valuable insights into specific predictors—such as post-bronchodilator FEV1:FVC ratio ≤60% and smoking pack-years ≥50—the overall utility in informing individualized clinical decision-making is limited, as it does not comprehensively address the multifaceted modifiable factors that influence clinically important deterioration in broader real-world settings.

In summary, a considerable proportion of patients with mild-to-moderate COPD who were receiving LAMA maintenance therapy still experience disease progression. Important predictors of annualized CID risk during longitudinal follow-up consist of post-BD FEV1:FVC lower than 60% and smoking pack-years higher than 50 pack-years. However, the CAT score or mMRC score is not a significant predictor. Patients with these risk factors might benefit more from intensified initial interventions to control the progression of COPD.
