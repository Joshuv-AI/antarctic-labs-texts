Integrating the Mystical Experience Questionnaire Into a Broader Psychometric Framework: English Validation of the Psychedelic Experience Scale and Comparison of Psilocybin and LSD Sessions Across Two Controlled Settings
Authors: Kurt Stocker, Matthias Hartmann, Frederick S Barrett, William A Richards, Nathan D Sepeda, Laura Ley, Anna M Becker, Friederike Holze, Matthias E Liechti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesFor English, the validated part of Psychedelic Experience Scale (PES48) is a four‐factor structure called the Mystical Experience Questionnaire (MEQ30). The other validated part of the PES48 consists of four more factors: two more mystical factors (paradoxicality and connectedness, which together with the MEQ30 form the MEQ40), and two more non‐mystical factors (visual experience and distressing experience). However, this latter four‐factor part of the PES48 has thus far only been validated for the German version of the PES48. We investigated whether the overall eight‐factor structure of the PES48 (which includes the MEQ30 four‐factor structure) can also be validated, and thus potentially be put to good use in English.MethodsData from 280 English PES measurements (145 different healthy participants) from four placebo‐controlled studies with low to high doses of psilocybin were included. The reliability of the eight subscales was evaluated using measures of internal consistency. The validity of the factor structure was assessed through model fit indices from confirmatory factor analysis. English results were then also compared with the German PES validation data set from Stocker et al. (2024).ResultsSix of the eight subscales (mystical, positive mood, transcendence of time and space, ineffability, connectedness, distressing experience) of the English PES48 show high internal consistency, one subscale (paradoxicality) shows good, and one (visual experience) acceptable internal consistency. Both MEQ models (MEQ30 and MEQ40) show similar fits (acceptable to good model fits). In English, both MEQ models show better fits than in German. All six MEQ40 scale means of the English data are higher compared to German data.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that the eight‐factor PES48 is also a valid psychometric tool in English. With the MEQ40 part of the PES48, one can measure mystical experience with a still wider conceptual breath than with the MEQ30. Furthermore, one can also measure non‐mystical visual and distressing states in an overall more comprehensive and broader conceptualization of the psychedelic experience. Higher MEQ40 scale means for the English than the German study participants could inspire future research into the role of setting in relation to mystical experience.Keywords: mystical experience, psychedelic experience, psychometrics, setting

Introduction

With data from randomized and mostly placebo‐controlled psychedelic trials, this article investigates whether the mystical experience questionnaire (MEQ30) (Barrett et al. 2015; MacLean et al. 2012) can be integrated into the broader psychometric framework of the validated part of the psychedelic experience questionnaire (PES48) (Stocker et al. 2024). In this manuscript, the term psychedelic experience refers to experiences induced by psychoactive substances that primarily act through agonism at serotonin (5‐HT) 2A receptors, including lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), psilocybin, mescaline, and N,N‐dimethyltryptamine (DMT) (Holze et al. 2024; Rickli et al. 2016). The MEQ—with the latest pre‐PES48 version being the MEQ30—is a well validated psychometric tool that has long been used to measure mystical experience as it can occur during acute psychedelic states (Barrett et al. 2015; R. R. Griffiths et al. 2006; MacLean et al. 2012; Pahnke 1969; Richards 1975).

The conceptualization of mystical experience in the MEQ has been variously criticized—for instance, as reflecting “Christian Bias” (Mosurinjohn et al. 2023, 1) or as “more adequately reflecting non‐Western traditions than Western theistic traditions like Christianity” (Cole‐Turner 2024, 1). Furthermore, a recent classification‐oriented review of altered or nonordinary states of consciousness has emphasized the diversity of phenomenological features associated with states labeled as “mystical,” leading to the suggestion that such states might alternatively be framed in terms of “significance,” alongside the remark that the term “mystical” is nebulous (Fort et al. 2025, 24).

While such reviews provide valuable syntheses across historical traditions and classification schemes, this assessment depends critically on how mystical experience is defined. In the classical tradition articulated by Stace (1960), the defining core of mystical experience is the transcendence of the subject–object dichotomy, whereas features such as bliss, paradoxicality, ineffability, noetic quality etc. are treated as peripheral characteristics that may accompany—but do not by themselves constitute—mystical experience proper. When this core–periphery distinction is made explicit, the term “mystical” is neither conceptually vague nor reducible to experiences that are merely subjectively significant or meaningful.

Up to the MEQ43 and its predecessors (R. R. Griffiths et al. 2006; Pahnke 1969; Richards 1975), this mystical core/periphery distinction was also clearly reflected psychometrically, with the subscales Internal Unity and External Unity corresponding to the core and the other subscales corresponding to the periphery. In the MEQ30 and MEQ40 (Barrett et al. 2015; MacLean et al. 2012; Stocker et al. 2024), however, this core/periphery distinction has become partially blurred, insofar as the subscale mystical conflates core elements (unity) with peripheral elements (sacredness, noetic quality).

Despite these conceptual issues, studies have consistently shown positive associations with such psychedelically occasioned mystical experience as conceptualized/measured with the MEQ (whether MEQ43 or MEQ30). This has been shown for example in relation to persisting positive effects on attitudes, mood, behavior, subjective well‐being, and life satisfaction in healthy participants (R. R. Griffiths et al. 2006; Schmid and Liechti 2018), and in relation to sustained symptom reduction in patients suffering from depression, anxiety, and addiction (Bogenschutz et al. 2015; Garcia‐Romeu et al. 2015; R. R. Griffiths et al. 2016; Holze et al. 2023; Ross et al. 2016). Therefore, while current and future psychometric work may further refine the measurement of mystical experience in philosophically and conceptually clearer ways (e.g., Taves et al. 2023), mystical experience as measured with the MEQ remains a valid and informative construct for clinical and psychometric research until such a refinement is achieved.

Recently, a section of a thematically rich questionnaire on psychedelic experience of the 1960s—the Psychedelic Experience Questionnaire/Scale 100 (PES100), also known as the States of Consciousness Questionnaire (SOCQ)—has been validated with a new factor structure (Stocker et al. 2024). This validated part of the PES contains 48 items (PES48), and consists of three main themes: extended mystical experience (6 factors), visual experience (1 factor), and distressing experience (1 factor). Extended mystical experience consists of the four factors of the MEQ30 (mystical, positive mood, transcendence of time and space, ineffability) (Barrett et al. 2015; MacLean et al. 2012), and two more newly established mystical factors (paradoxicality and connectedness). This validated 6‐factor mystical‐experience part (MEQ30 + paradoxicality + connectedness) of the PES48 is called the Mystical Experience Questionnaire 40 (MEQ40) (Stocker et al. 2024). That paradoxicality is an inherent part of the mystical experience has long been observed or proposed by scholars (James 1902, 417; Otto 1926, 63, Otto 1932, 47–48; Richards 2015, 26; Stace 1960, 304–305), and has, besides in the PES48 (Stocker et al. 2024), also found further recent psychometric support for psychedelics (Calder et al. 2024). Similarly, a deep sense of connectedness is widely regarded as part of the mystical experience (B. Griffiths 1982, 31; Otto 1926, 296, Otto 1932, 212; Richards 2015, 49–51), and has, besides in the PES48, also found further recent psychometric support for psychedelically induced states (Calder et al. 2024). Thus, a conceptual advantage of using the PES48 over the MEQ30 is that it incorporates the full MEQ40, allowing it to capture two additional facets of the mystical experience (paradoxicality and connectedness), as well as two other core dimensions of the psychedelic experience—visual and distressing effects—providing a broader measurement framework beyond mystical experience alone. Indeed, when looking at the main themes of the three most comprehensive questionnaires used to measure psychedelic experience—the PES100/48 (Richards 1975; Stocker et al. 2024), the 5‐Dimensional Altered States of Consciousness Scale (5D‐ASC; Dittrich 1985, 1998; Dittrich et al. 2010; Stocker et al. 2025; Studerus et al. 2010), and the Hallucinogen Rating Scale (HRS; Calder et al. 2024; Strassman et al. 1994)—it becomes apparent that psychedelic experience always seems to have at least these three common characteristics: positive experience (which can include mystical experience), distressing experience (e.g., anxiety and suffering), and perceptual experience (with visual perception playing the most dominant role). Therefore, this paper attempts to integrate the MEQ30 into a broader validated psychometric context of the psychedelic experience—the PEQ48—for use in English. Doing so offers the opportunity to more comprehensively and validly assess the psychedelic experience in English‐speaking populations. Given these potential advantages, we investigated whether the eight‐factor structure of the PES48 can also be validated in English.

Overall standardized and validated scales—including standardization and validation of the same measurement tools in different languages— are essential for ensuring comparability across studies and for enabling meta‐analytic syntheses in psychedelic and other nonordinary‐states‐of‐consciousness (NSC) 1 research. Recent initiatives such as the Altered States Database (ASDB) have already begun to systematically compile psychometric data for such purposes and to carry out such analyses using results of instruments such as the MEQ, 5D‐ASC, and HRS (e.g., Hirschfeld et al. 2023; Prugger et al. 2022). However, rather than on field‐wide standardization efforts, the present study focuses primarily on validating the English version of the Psychedelic Experience Scale (PES48) and on assessing its psychometric performance across datasets.

Method

Participants

280 English PES measurements of 145 healthy participants (mean age = 41.5, SD = 12.4, ranging from 22 to 69; 87 female) from four different psilocybin studies were included in the analysis (Carbonaro et al. 2018; R. R. Griffiths et al. 2006, 2011, 2018). For the just listed studies we included the use of the PES with the following high to low psilocybin doses (n refers to PES measurements): high (30 mg/70 kg; n = 155), moderate (20 mg/70 kg; n = 87), and low (10 mg/70 kg; n = 38). Not included in the analysis was the use of the PES in the just‐listed studies in the following conditions: first, when a very low psilocybin dose (below 10 mg/70 kg) had been used, and second, when not a classic psychedelic, but some other substance (dextromethorphan, methylphenidate hydrochloride, or placebo) had been administered. Furthermore, the English PES data set was compared with the 239 German PES data set from 140 healthy participants (mean age = 32.4, SD = 9.6, ranging from 24 to 64; 73 females) from the six different classic‐psychedelic studies (moderate to high doses of psilocybin and/or LSD) of the pooled study from Stocker et al. (2024).

Measures

Psychedelic Experience Scale (PES)

Psychedelic and mystical experience was assessed with the Psychedelic Experience Scale 100 (PES100) (Richards 1975; Stocker et al. 2024), also known as the States of Consciousness Questionnaire (SOCQ) (R. R. Griffiths et al. 2006). 48 items of the PES form the validated part of the PES (PES48) (Stocker et al. 2024). The PES48 contains the 40‐item Mystical Experience Questionnaire (MEQ40)—which consists of the MEQ30 (Barrett et al. 2015; MacLean et al. 2012) and the additional mystical factors connectedness and paradoxicality—as well as the two non‐mystical factors visual experience and distressing experience (Stocker et al. 2024).

Procedure and Data Analysis

The reliability of the factor structure was evaluated using measures of internal consistency, while its validity was assessed through model fit indices from confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω were computed as index of internal consistency. Cronbach's α assumes that all items contribute equally to the measurement (tau‐equivalence) and is influenced by the number of items, often overestimating reliability in homogeneous scales. In contrast, McDonald's ω accounts for differing item contributions using factor loadings, providing a more robust and accurate estimate of reliability, especially for heterogeneous scales. As model fit indices, we assessed normed Chi‐Square χ2/df, which evaluates the discrepancy between the observed and expected covariance matrices relative to the model's degrees of freedom, the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), which estimates how well the model approximates the data while accounting for model complexity, the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), which reflects the average standardized difference between observed and predicted correlations, and the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), which compares the fit of the proposed model to a null model (no relationships between variables), adjusting for sample size. Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω are based on polychoric correlations, and fit indices of the CFA are based on robust maximum likelihood estimation with Satorra‐Bentler correction (see Stocker et al. 2024).

Five of the six subscales of the English MEQ40 show high internal consistency (α and ω > 0.90), and the sixth subscale still shows good internal consistency (α and ω between 0.80 and 0.89) (Table 1). Discussion

Our findings suggest that the eight‐factor PES48 is a valid psychometric tool not only in German, but also in English for measuring mystical experiences more comprehensively than the MEQ30, while also incorporating non‐mystical visual and distressing effects into an overall more comprehensive and broader understanding of the psychedelic experience. Convergent validity between the MEQ and the 5D‐ASC (or its subscale structure: 11‐ASC) has already been robustly demonstrated in previous work (Liechti et al. 2017; r = 0.87 between MEQ30 and 5D‐ASC total scores) and in our own validation of the PES (Stocker et al. 2024). In that study, the PES subscales showed substantial convergence with their closest 11‐ASC conceptual counterparts—including PES paradoxicality and connectedness with 11‐ASC experience of unity (r = 0.69 and 0.59, respectively), PES distressing experience with 11‐ASC anxiety (r = 0.74), and PES visual experience with 11‐ASC elementary imagery and complex imagery (r = 0.48 and 0.42, respectively). The somewhat lower correlations in the visual domain are consistent with conceptual differences of the two instruments between open‐eye and closed‐eye visuals.

The similar model fit indices for the MEQ30 and MEQ40 suggests that the additional mystical subscales from the MEQ40 do not meaningfully impact model fit when compared to the MEQ30. This finding further supports existing scholarly and psychometric work establishing that paradoxicality (Calder et al. 2024; James 1902, 417; Otto 1926, 63, 1932, pp. 47–48; Richards 2015, 26; Stace 1960, 304–305; Stocker et al. 2024) and connectedness (B. Griffiths 1982, 31; Otto 1926, 296, Otto 1932, 212; Richards 2015, 49–51; Stocker et al. 2024) are also an inherent part of mystical experience.

The PES connectedness construct—as part of the mystical experience—was originally modeled after the qualitatively derived framework of Watts et al. (2017), which consists of being connected, acutely and post‐acutely, to the senses, to self, to others, to the world, and to a spiritual principle (Stocker et al. 2024, 85, Table 2). An important limitation of the PES connectedness factor, however, is that it currently lacks an item explicitly capturing connectedness to self. Watts et al. (2022) subsequently developed the Watts Connectedness Scale (WCS) based in their qualitative findings, designed to measure post‐acute feelings of connectedness in terms of the three factors: connectedness to self, to others, and to the world. The PES connectedness factor more closely resembles the original qualitative construct described by Watts et al. in that it focuses on acute feelings of connectedness and conceptually distinguishes being connected to the world and connectedness to a spiritual principle—both of which the WCS subsumes under “Connectedness to the world”.

That six of the eight subscales of the English PES48 show high internal consistency, and two either acceptable or good internal consistency, shows that the PES48 also in English can be used to measure mystical experience in a broader psychometric framework that also includes measuring visual and distressing experience. Thus, the PES48 is now also in line in English with all questionnaires that try to cover the psychedelic experience in comprehensive ways—the PES (Richards 1975; Stocker et al. 2024), the 5D‐ASC (Dittrich 1998; Stocker et al. 2025; Studerus et al. 2010), and the HRS (Calder et al. 2024; Strassman et al. 1994)—as each of these questionnaires at least cover these three basic themes: positive themes (such as mystical experiences), perceptual themes (such as visual experiences) and distressing themes (such as anxiety, suffering, isolation). Stocker et al. (2024, 96) have laid out in some detail some more specific psychedelic themes—which often can be viewed as subthemes of positive, perceptual, or distressing psychedelic experiences—covered or not covered with these three most comprehensive questionnaires used to measure psychedelic experience. The main observation was that each of the three questionnaires leaves out certain specific psychedelic themes that are covered in one or two of the other questionnaires. Thus, they pointed out that the answer of which questionnaire is recommended for use (in situations where not all three of these questionnaires can be used together) will depend on which themes should not be left out in the measurement of the acute psychedelic state for a given research or clinical context. We recommend the use of the PES48 over the 5D‐ASC or HRS when one wants to measure mystical experience in more comprehensive ways, but other than for that specific purpose we strongly recommend using both the PES48 and the 5D‐ASC—in its most updated validated form: the 3D‐ASCr (Stocker et al. 2025)—in clinical research and practice (see below). It is only the MEQ40 part of the PES (when contrasted with the 5D‐ASC and HRS) which has validated a larger number of scholarly proposed common characteristics of the mystical experience (James 1902, 380–382; Otto 1926, 50–70, Otto 1932, 38–53; Richards 2015, 26, 41–54; Stace 1960, 131–132; Stocker 2022, 332–336) as psychometric factors (Barrett et al. 2015; Barrett and Griffiths 2018, 409–410; Stocker et al. 2024).

Finally, we found that MEQ40 scale means of the English data were higher than in the German data, with the mystical subscale showing the largest difference and the ineffability subscale the smallest. Such higher MEQ scores in Johns Hopkins participants compared with University Hospital Basel participants have also been reported previously (Liechti et al. 2017, 1505). Prior work suggests that these differences are unlikely to reflect language‐related effects.

For example, in U.S. psilocybin studies, high‐dose psilocybin produced substantial MEQ30 scores, but inactive and active placebo conditions likewise yielded comparatively high ratings (Barrett et al. 2015). In contrast, in European laboratory studies, placebo, 3,4‐methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA), and methylphenidate produced only minimal increases in MEQ scores (Liechti et al. 2017; Schmid et al. 2014). Similar patterns in Europe have been observed in therapeutic settings, where active placebo administration resulted in only small MEQ increases (Gasser et al. 2014).

Taken together, these findings indicate that cross‐site differences in MEQ effect sizes could primarily reflect contextual and expectancy‐related factors—such as study setting, participant characteristics, preparation, and interpersonal support—rather than linguistic differences between questionnaire versions. Importantly, comparable cross‐laboratory differences have been observed across multiple instruments, including the MEQ and 5D‐ASC (Liechti et al. 2017), supporting the interpretation that these effects are design‐ and expectancy‐related rather than measure‐ or language‐specific. In contrast, PES scale means for visual and distressing experience—dimensions not previously assessed outside the Basel datasets—differed only marginally between the two settings in the present study. Overall, this means that English and German PES data can be pooled together in the future, as language seems not account for the differences in scores.

Although this is mere speculation, we think that the setting might play a crucial role in the observed differences in experienced mystical strength across the two language groups. The Johns Hopkins University laboratory (English data) is designed to resemble a welcoming living room, featuring a couch, a collection of books on spiritual and artistic themes, and nondescript artwork on the walls. In contrast, the University Hospital Basel laboratory (German data) has a more clinical, hospital‐like ambiance, with a standard hospital bed, plain white walls, a clock on the wall, and no books unless participants bring their own.

Given that studies have shown positive associations for psychedelically occasioned mystical experiences both for healthy individuals and for individuals suffering from certain mental health disorders (Bogenschutz et al. 2015; Garcia‐Romeu et al. 2015; R. R. Griffiths et al. 2006, 2016; Holze et al. 2023; Ross et al. 2016; Schmid and Liechti 2018), and given that in this study we have tentatively observed differences in experienced mystical strength between a living room‐like and a hospital room‐like setting, future research could investigate whether psychedelic experiences in living room‐like settings (compared to more clinical settings) might not only enhance mystical experiences, but might also be associated with related stronger post‐substance positive outcomes. These observations emphasize the need for carefully manipulating setting as a controlled variable in psychedelic clinical trials (Golden et al. 2022; Yaden et al. 2025).

We already noted that there are three major instruments aiming to capture the psychedelic experience rather comprehensively: the PES48 (Richards 1975; Stocker et al. 2024), the 5D‐ASC (Dittrich et al. 2006, 2010), and the HRS (Calder et al. 2024; Strassman et al. 1994). The 5D‐ASC (Dittrich et al. 2006, 2010) has recently been revalidated in a hierarchical framework that integrates the established 11‐ASC subscales (Studerus et al. 2010) into three higher‐order dimensions, conceptually aligned with Dittrich's original three core dimensions, while showing improved model fit compared with the original subscale‐free three‐dimensional formulation (3D‐ASCr; Stocker et al. 2025). We recommend administering both the PES48 and 3D‐ASCr whenever feasible—see Stocker et al. (2025) for a more comprehensive discussion on the matter—as these two instruments have been more consistently linked to clinically relevant outcomes than the HRS, while also differing in key aspects. For instance, insightfulness—an established 11‐ASC subscale that is also integrated within the revised 3D‐ASCr framework but not captured by the MEQ/PES—has been associated with improvements in depression (Carhart‐Harris et al. 2018), whereas ineffability—an established MEQ subscale retained within the PES48 but not captured by the 11‐ASC/3D‐ASCr—has predicted positive treatment outcomes in cancer‐related anxiety (Ross et al. 2016). Even in domains where these two instruments overlap—such as perceptual and distressing experiences—they meaningfully complement one another. For example, within the validated items, the PES48 visual factor uniquely captures complex open‐eye visuals (e.g., “38. With open eyes, seeing objects around you turn into great works of art”), whereas only a 11‐ASC/3D‐ASCr perceptual factor covers closed‐eyes visual scenes (“17 [39]. I saw entire scenes in total darkness or with my eyes closed”). Similarly, the PES48 distressing factor uniquely includes items such as suffering and despair (“13. Emotional and/or physical suffering; 16. Feelings of despair”), while only the 11‐ASC/3D‐ASCr distressing factors includes items reflecting impaired thought and volition (e.g., “10. [27] I was unable to make even the smallest decision; 36 [78]. I had the feeling that I no longer had a will of my own”).

Until future revisions or new instruments are developed that integrate these complementary aspects into a single, unified framework, we recommend using both the PES48 and 3D‐ASCr, with the HRS as an optional addition when time and resources permit—while noting that the clinical applicability of the HRS (Version 4) remains under evaluation and requires additional empirical confirmation (Calder et al. 2024, 11). Depending on research and therapy aims, these broad instruments can be complemented by more focused questionnaires such as the Emotional Breakthrough Inventory (EBI; Roseman et al. 2019), the Psychological Insight Questionnaire (PIQ; Davis et al. 2021), the Challenging Experience Questionnaire (CEQ; Barrett et al. 2016), the Ego Dissolution Inventory (EDI; Nour et al. 2016), or the Watts Connectedness Scale (WCS; Watts et al. 2022). Finally, given that qualitative research has identified phenomenological dimensions not yet adequately covered by current psychedelic psychometrics—such as insights into personal relationships (Belser et al. 2017) and peak experiences extending beyond classical mystical framings (Gasser et al. 2015)—future instruments should further integrate such qualitative insights.

Beyond psychedelic applications, future research could also assess the potential of the PES48 to characterize other forms of NSCs, both pharmacologically (e.g., dissociative substances) and non‐pharmacologically induced. Qualitative and phenomenological accounts suggest that the three experiential dimensions assessed by the PES48 may likewise capture central aspects of non‐pharmacologically induced NSCs, such as those occurring during intensive or advanced meditation (Kornfield 1979; Van Gordon et al. 2018, 2019), sensory deprivation (Kjellgren et al. 2008), or rhythm‐induced trance (Flor‐Henry et al. 2017; Kjellgren and Eriksson 2010). In our recent re‐validation of the 5D‐ASC (3D‐ASCr; Stocker et al. 2025), we proposed a comparable generalizability of the three core dimensions—positive, distressing, and perceptual effects—supporting the view that these phenomenological dimensions may extend across diverse consciousness‐modifying inducers and techniques (Dittrich 1985, 1998; Dittrich et al. 2006, 2010).

The PES48 provides a stand‐alone, psychometrically validated measure of psychedelically occasioned mystical experience that is integrated into a broader psychometric framework including a wider conception of mystical experience (by including mystical paradoxicality and connectedness). This measure also allows a direct contrast of psychedelic mystical states with psychedelic non‐mystical states such a distressing experience. We believe this measure to be a valuable contribution to the field, as it recapitulates a more comprehensive representation of the richness and diversity of psychedelic experiences within a single measure. The PES48 and its scoring key (for both English and German) can be found as a supplement in Stocker et al. (2024).
