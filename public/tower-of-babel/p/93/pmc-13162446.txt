Association of problematic short-video use with adolescent depression: a cross-sectional study and machine learning identification model construction
Authors: Zhihan Jiang, Tiejun Kang, Heng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProblematic short-video use (PSVU) is prevalent among adolescents and is increasingly recognized for its association with adverse mental health outcomes. This cross-sectional study aims to explore the association between PSVU and depression using SFPSVUS, and to develop an interpretable machine learning (ML) model for identifying adolescent depression.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted on data from 4000 adolescent participants. Multivariate Logistic Regression and restricted cubic spline (RCS) methods were used to assess the association between PSVU and depression. Feature variables were identified via Boruta feature selection and validated using Shapley Additive Interpretation (SHAP) methods. Nine ML models, including XGBoost, support vector machine (SVM), and random forest (RF), were developed to identify adolescents with depression.ResultsA total of 3816 adolescents were included. The SFPSVUS demonstrated acceptable reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.70) in the current sample. Multivariate Logistic Regression analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between PSVU and depression. RCS analysis indicated a significant nonlinear association between PSVU and adolescent depression. Six variables selected via Boruta feature selection were used to develop nine ML models, with the RF model demonstrating superior performance in identifying depression (AUC = 0.863) in the test set. PSVU emerged as an important feature in the model, alongside emotion regulation, non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) and sleep quality.ConclusionsPSVU shows a significant positive association with adolescent depression and serves as an important feature in ML identification models.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08024-x.Keywords: Problematic short-video use, Short-form problematic short-video use scale, Adolescent, Depression, Machine learning

Introduction

Concerns have been increasingly raised about the risks associated with excessive short-video use, particularly among adolescent populations [7, 36]. Adolescents are in a critical stage of brain development with immature self-control abilities, making them more vulnerable to the negative effects of excessive short-video use compared to adults [22, 35, 70]. Neurobiological evidence further indicates that gene expression patterns during adolescence represent a developmental period of heightened susceptibility to PSVU [16]. Following European expert recommendations to focus on functional impairment rather than usage frequency [15], this study adopts the term “problematic short-video use (PSVU)” to describe the psychosomatic impairment caused by users’ high-frequency short-video use.

The association between problematic internet or social media use and depression has long been a focus in adolescent mental health research [37, 38, 51]. A meta-analysis revealed a small yet significant positive correlation between adolescent social media use and depressive symptoms (k = 12 studies, r = 0.11, p < 0.01) with high heterogeneity (I²=95.22%) [21]. This substantial heterogeneity suggests that the relationship is likely to vary considerably depending on platform type and patterns of use. Therefore, moving beyond the generic concept of “social media” to investigate specific, algorithmically-driven, and highly immersive platforms such as short-video applications is warranted [62]. A cross-sectional study has reported significant positive correlations between PSVU and adolescent depression [61], while longitudinal research using cross-lagged panel network analysis has identified “Conflict” and “Sad mood” as key bridge symptoms forming mutually reinforcing cycles between PSVU and depression [42]. However, the lack of validated measurement tools has restricted the development in the related field [14]. For example, scales adapted from those measuring Internet or smartphone addiction are commonly used but lack validation specifically among adolescents, limiting the reliability of findings in this population. And its project may not be able to accurately capture the core characteristics of the short-video platforms [16, 56]. To address this gap, we developed the short-form problematic short-video use scale (SFPSVUS) based on the work of Zhang et al., a targeted tool designed to capture excessive use and its associated functional impairment in adolescents [68].

Adolescent depression arises from a complex interplay of multiple factors [8, 46]. The impact of PSVU on depression may not follow a simple linear pattern, but is likely moderated by individual adolescent characteristics (e.g., emotion regulation ability) and environmental factors (e.g., family structure) [69]. Traditional statistical methods, such as logistic regression, are limited in capturing such complex relational patterns and often fail to effectively identify potential nonlinear associations and interactions among variables [44]. Explainable Machine Learning (ML) overcomes these limitations [48]. Algorithms like Random Forest excel at automatically detecting nonlinear patterns and interaction effects from high-dimensional data [18]. More importantly, interpretation frameworks such as Shapley Additive Interpretation (SHAP) move beyond aggregate coefficients to quantify and visualize the specific, individualized contribution of each variable—including PSVU—to model identification [41]. This provides a transparent, data-driven assessment of PSVU’s unique value within a multifactorial risk profile, an insight that traditional methods are not designed to reveal [2].

This cross-sectional study aims to explore the association between PSVU and depression using SFPSVUS, and to develop an interpretable ML model for identifying adolescent depression.

Methods

Study population

The data for this cross-sectional study were collected between April 2024 and April 2025. The study employed a cluster sampling method, recruiting a total of 4,000 adolescents from five junior and senior high schools located in central and western China. Data were collected via questionnaires administered in class, with two trained researchers present to provide explanations and ensure the confidential and independent completion. To ensure data quality, the following measures were implemented. Participants were excluded if they provided ≥ 15 consecutive identical responses or completed the questionnaire in less than half the average response time. After applying these exclusion criteria, a total of 3,816 adolescents were included in the final analysis (95.4% of the initial sample).

Ethical approval (20230087) was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of School of Psychology, Northwest Normal University. Prior to data collection, informed consent was formally obtained from participating schools, students, and their legal guardians.

Data collection and measurement

Demographic characteristics: Participants reported their age (years), gender (0 = male, 1 = female), place of residence (0 = rural, 1 = urban), and three binary indicators: only child (0 = yes, 1 = no) [19], boarding (0 = yes, 1 = no), and left-behind (0 = yes, 1 = no). Specifically, Boarding referred to whether the student resided in school dormitories during the academic week, a common arrangement in many Chinese secondary schools. Left-behind children were defined as having one or both parents migrating for work to another city for at least six months in the past year, while the adolescent remained in their hometown under the care of other relatives (typically grandparents).

Short-form Problematic Short Video Use Scale (SFPSVUS)

The Problematic Short Video Use Scale was developed by Zhang et al., based on the Bergen Facebook Addiction Scale (BFAS) [68]. The initial version contained 35 items covering six dimensions: “User Needs,” “Social Interaction,” “Compensatory Expectations,” “Dependent Use,” “Excessive Use,” and “Inhibitory Control.” The SFPSVUS was derived from the original 35-item PSVUS by selecting the 6-item “Excessive Use” dimension. This considers the core criterion of behavioral addiction, which is excessive involvement in a particular activity. Fineberg et al., [15], Mihordin, [34], Ulvi, [50]. The dimension also captures core behavioral manifestations—preoccupation, loss of control, and neglect of daily activities—that are direct precursors to functional impairment [12], thereby providing a clinically meaningful indicator suitable for large-scale screening. In a previous study, we verified that SFPSVUS has good reliability and validity among Chinese adolescents [71]. In the current study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.70. This is acceptable for a scale with only a 6-item brief version [49] (Appendix A).

Patient health questionnaire (PHQ-9)

The PHQ-9 is a validated 9-item tool for assessing depressive symptoms, scored from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”) [39]. The scale demonstrates strong reliability and validity across diverse populations. A total score ≥ 10, which exhibits optimal diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity = 88%, specificity = 85%), was used to define depressive symptoms in the present study [25]. In the current study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.93.

Difficulty in emotion regulation scale (DERS)

The 36-item DERS assesses six dimensions of emotion dysregulation, including nonacceptance of emotional responses, goal-directed behavior challenges, impulse control difficulties, limited emotional awareness, restricted access to regulation strategies, and lack of emotional clarity [17]. Items are rated 1 (Almost Never) to 5 (Almost Always) on a Likert scale, with higher total scores indicating greater emotion dysregulation. For the categorical analysis, participants were classified into four levels based on their total score: ‘No Difficulty’ (score ≤ 51), ‘Mild’ (score 52–85), ‘Moderate’ (score 86–119), and ‘Severe’ (score ≥ 120) [26]. In the current study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.91.

Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI)

The PSQI, validated in Chinese populations, assesses sleep quality across seven domains: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, sleep medication use, and daytime dysfunction [5, 30]. Items are rated on a 4-point scale (1 = best to 4 = worst), with higher total scores indicating poorer sleep quality. For analysis, sleep quality was categorized into three levels based on established cut-offs: ‘Normal’ (PSQI ≤ 10), ‘Moderate’ (PSQI 11–15), and ‘Severe’ (PSQI 16–21). In the current study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.90.

Functional assessment of self-injury (FASM)

The FASM is a self-report measure of self-injury methods, frequency, and functions [32]. It evaluates 11 non-suicidal self-injurious (NSSI) behavior subtypes and 22 functional motivations, rated 0 (Never) to 3 (Often). A higher score indicates a higher overall frequency of NSSI behaviors. The Chinese version demonstrated strong internal consistency [43]. In the current study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.94.

Covariates

Confounding factors controlled for in this study included age, gender, only child, place of residence, boarding, left behind children, emotion regulation, NSSI and sleep quality. Empirical evidence indicates that these factors independently affect the risk of depression among adolescents [24, 54, 64].

Statistical analysis

Data preprocessing was conducted prior to all analyses. Potential univariate outliers in continuous variables were screened using the Interquartile Range (IQR) method. No significant outliers requiring adjustment were identified in the primary study variables. During data collection, questionnaire completeness was emphasized. The occurrence of missing data was minimal and was handled by listwise deletion, resulting in the final analytic sample of 3,816 complete cases. Subsequently, basic statistics were calculated to describe the overall distribution of variables, and correlation analysis was conducted to explore linear relationships between variables. Stratified analysis of baseline characteristics was performed according to quartiles of the SFPSVUS scores. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as proportion (percentage). Independent samples t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed continuous variables, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was employed to evaluate the relationship between PSVU and depression. To reduce bias from confounding factors, three models were constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age and gender), and Model 3 (adjusted for age, gender, only-child status, place of residence, emotion regulation, and sleep quality). p-values for trends were calculated using quartile levels. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression model with four knots was used to explore potential nonlinear relationships between PSVU and adolescent depression. Data preprocessing and statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3), Python, and DecisionLinnc(http://www.statsape.com). Specifically, R was used for data management, multivariate logistic regression, and RCS analysis. Python and DecisionLinnc were utilized for all ML model development, evaluation and visualization. All reported p-values were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Feature selection and model construction

Based on data from 3,816 adolescents, ML models were constructed to evaluate the ability of PSVU to identify adolescent depression. During model training and evaluation, all samples were randomly split into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio (random number = 100). In the training set, the Boruta algorithm—a wrapper-based feature selection method using random forests—was first applied to identify key features from 10 initial covariates. To address class imbalance, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was used to augment minority class samples in the training set, with 5 nearest neighbors and the random number was 100; oversampling was restricted to features selected by the Boruta algorithm. Based on the feature selection results, nine ML algorithms were used to develop identification models in the testing set: Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), Logistic Regression (LR), Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT), K-Nearest Neighbor Classification (KNNC), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). Advanced tree-based ensembles (RF, XGBoost, CatBoost, GBDT) were selected for their exceptional capability in automatically detecting complex nonlinear patterns and high-order interactions directly from data without the need for pre-specified parametric relationships. The LR model provides a critical benchmark against which the more complex models can be compared. The KNNC and SVM, along with simple DT, offer complementary approaches. KNNC relies on local similarity, SVM seeks an optimal separating hyperplane in high-dimensional space, and DT provides an intuitive, rule-based structure. The MLP, as a foundational neural network architecture, can capture subtle and complex relationships that might be overlooked by other model types [23].

To enhance model interpretability, the SHAP method was applied to quantify and interpret the importance of features selected by the Boruta algorithm, with feature validity further validated in the testing set. Model performance was comprehensively evaluated using multiple metrics, including Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), precision, recall, F1-score, specificity, and accuracy. These metrics together offered a thorough and multifaceted assessment of the models’ ability to identify adolescent depression.

Discussion

This study integrated multivariate logistic regression analysis with ML identification model construction to explore the relationship between PSVU and depression and to identify key influencing factors for adolescent depression. The RF model achieved a high AUC value in the test set (AUC = 0.863). SHAP value analysis revealed that sleep quality, emotion regulation, PSVU, and NSSI are the four core features for identifying adolescent depression [41].

First, regarding demographic characteristics, PSVU exhibited a complex pattern of gender differences. This study found a higher proportion of females in the higher PSVU quartile groups (e.g., Q3 and Q4). This aligns with the conclusion from most studies suggesting that “females are at higher risk for PSVU” (Miao [7, 31]). This may stem from females’ higher emotional sensitivity and their greater tendency to use short videos for emotion regulation and maintaining social relationships (e.g., interacting with friends through likes and comments) [10, 55]. This usage pattern, centered on emotional comfort and social connection, may lead to increased frequency of use. Interestingly, however, some studies indicate that males show a higher proportion in moderate and severe PSVU categories [33, 58]. This suggests a possibility that males might be more attracted to high-intensity entertainment, gaming content, and stimulating live streams on short-video platforms [52]. Their usage motivation tends more towards seeking novelty and sensory stimulation, which, once out of control, may more readily develop into unmanageable addictive behavior [29]. Differences in the specific dimensions emphasized by the PSVU assessment tools used across different studies might ultimately influence the observed gender difference results. Adolescents with more severe PSVU were more likely to be from urban areas and were more frequently non-boarders. This might be because urban adolescents have easier access to the internet, and non-boarders face less structured time management and a relatively more lenient home environment, thereby creating conditions for uncontrolled short-video use [27].

This study found that even after controlling for known high-risk factors (including emotion regulation and sleep quality), adolescents with high PSVU scores had a significantly increased odds of depression compared to those with low PSVU scores (OR = 6.262, 95% CI: 4.1954–9.5996). This association invites consideration of potential explanatory frameworks that integrate both individual and environmental factors [57]. For example, the I-PACE model proposed by Brand et al., provides an insightful framework for understanding the formation of PSVU [4]. Individuals with specific personality traits (e.g., neuroticism, impulsivity) and pre-existing psychological distress (e.g., emotion dysregulation) constitute the “susceptible soil” for PSVU [66]. When these vulnerable individuals are exposed to the meticulously designed “technological architecture” of short-video platforms—such as algorithm-driven infinite scrolling, personalized recommendations, and highly immersive audiovisual stimuli—it triggers intense affective (e.g., craving, pleasure, satisfaction) and cognitive (e.g., attentional bias, time perception) responses [9, 67]. The immediate pleasure or stress relief provided by each use acts as positive reinforcement, while the potential irritability and sense of emptiness upon cessation reinforce the behavior through negative reinforcement (i.e., escaping withdrawal distress) [20, 60]; Y. Zhang et al., [66]. Over time, the impulsive/reflexive system is continuously activated and strengthened, while the reflective/executive system responsible for self-control is relatively weakened, ultimately leading to a loss of behavioral control and forming a vicious cycle [40, 63]. Therefore, PSVU may represent a behavioral manifestation of underlying vulnerability within a compelling digital context, with its association to depression potentially rooted in shared affective, cognitive, and regulatory mechanisms [65].

Our findings are consistent with previous research. For instance, it might seem self-evident that sleep quality, emotion regulation, and NSSI are among the most important identifying factors for adolescent depression [45, 47, 53]. However, this principle was reaffirmed in our study within a comprehensive assessment framework that incorporated the evaluation of PSVU. On one hand, this indicates that, regardless of the presence of PSVU, our research confirms the significant importance of sleep, emotion regulation, and NSSI for adolescent mental health. Early assessment identifying poor sleep quality, impaired emotion regulation capacity, and NSSI behaviors can help mitigate the risk of substantial mental health issues in adolescents [1, 3, 6]. On the other hand, our model demonstrates that the combined assessment of sleep, emotion regulation, NSSI, and short-video use can further enhance the precision of identifying adolescents at high risk for depression. The descriptive statistical analysis in this study showed the presence of more severe emotion dysregulation, poorer sleep quality, and higher NSSI frequency among adolescents with high PSVU scores. It is noteworthy that sleep quality, emotion regulation, and NSSI are often discussed in previous research as potential important correlates of PSVU [11, 22, 28]. One cross-sectional study indicated a positive correlation between PSVU and sleep disturbances and NSSI [61]. Another study found that individuals with poor emotion regulation abilities are more likely to develop PSVU [59]. PSVU may serve as a measurable behavioral indicator reflecting the mental health risks of adolescents [33]. The co-occurrence pattern of PSVU with other risk factors deserves attention [13]. In the future, longitudinal studies will be needed to further examine the interactions and evolution trajectories between PSVU and these factors, and to explore whether incorporating it into a multi-dimensional assessment system can help identify high-risk adolescent groups earlier and more comprehensively.

In summary, this study identifies PSVU as a correlate of adolescent depression and provides an interpretable ML identification model framework. These findings underscore the relevance of digital media use in adolescent mental health assessment and set the stage for future research to test the predictive utility of PSVU screening.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, due to the cross-sectional survey design, causal relationships between PSVU and adolescent depression cannot be established. Future research needs to explore the causal relationships between PSVU and adolescent depression. Second, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce biases, including social desirability bias and recall bias, which could affect the accuracy of the measurements. Future studies should incorporate objective assessment tools (e.g., physiological indicators or behavioral observations) to complement self-report data. Third, the current analysis was restricted to a limited set of covariates. While we adjusted for key variables such as age, gender, and sleep quality, additional factors, including family environment, peer relationships, and academic stress, may influence the relationship between PSVU and adolescent depression. Expanding the range of covariates in future investigations will enhance the robustness of the findings. Fourth, the sample was exclusively collected from China, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other cultural contexts. Future research should prioritize cross-cultural adaptations and multinational samples to examine whether the observed patterns are consistent across diverse populations. This study utilized the SFPSVUS focusing on the excessive use dimension in large-scale screening. While this approach effectively captures core addictive behaviors, future studies employing the full problematic short-video use scale could provide a more comprehensive assessment of PSVU and further validate our findings. Furthermore, our ML model was developed and tested on a specific adolescent sample from China; its generalizability to other populations or temporal validation remains unproven. While SHAP values provide post-hoc insights into feature importance, they do not fully elucidate the causal pathways or mechanistic relationships between PSVU and depression. These highlight the need for cautious interpretation of the current findings and underscore directions for methodological improvements in subsequent studies.

Conclusion

PSVU shows a significant positive association with adolescent depression and serves as an important feature in ML identification models.
