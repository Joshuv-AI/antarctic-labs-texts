LINE-1 retrotransposition modulates metabolic stress and promotes cuproptosis resistance in lung squamous cell carcinoma by disrupting mitochondrial membrane homeostasis
Authors: Rui Zhang, Xiao Zhang, Wenjing Tian, Chenhui Wang, Lianmin Zhang, Hailong Wang, Pengpeng Liu, Guidong Chen, Jiajia Yi, Weijia Zhang, Jinpu Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) is one of the common pathological subtypes of non-small cell lung cancer, which poses significant treatment challenges due to poor response to conventional therapies and lack of effective biomarkers. Long Interspersed Nuclear Element-1 (LINE-1, L1) is a critical transposable element that has dramatically shaped the human genome and plays important roles in carcinogenesis and cancer progression. However, the functions of LINE-1 in LUSC still remain largely unknown.MethodsTo elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which L1-ATP8B1 (a typical high-frequency pathogenic LINE-1 chimeric transcript in LUSC) regulates mitochondrial dynamics and cellular metabolism in LUSC, we employed a combination of in vitro and in vivo experimental models. We systematically analyzed the effects of L1-ATP8B1 on mitochondrial membrane components, key protein expression, and the underlying molecular mechanisms governing its regulation of protein expression. Additionally, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of a combinatorial pharmacological strategy using the reverse transcriptase inhibitor Nevirapine (NVR) and the glutathione synthesis inhibitor buthionine sulfoximine (BSO) in LUSC xenograft models.ResultsOur findings demonstrate that L1-ATP8B1 reduces the accumulation of cardiolipin, a mitochondria-specific membrane phospholipid, thereby enhancing oxidative phosphorylation and cellular energy generation. Notably, L1-ATP8B1 disrupts mitochondrial membrane homeostasis by suppressing the expression of prohibitin 1 (a critical mitochondrial membrane protein) through a mechanism involving cardiolipin-dependent SUMOylation and TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination. Furthermore, L1-ATP8B1 promotes cuproptosis resistance in LUSC cells by increasing intracellular glutathione levels. Importantly, the combinatorial treatment with NVR and BSO effectively mitigated the growth of LUSC xenografts in vivo.ConclusionsOur findings reveal that L1-ATP8B1 plays a critical role in mitochondrial dysfunction and LUSC aggressiveness, suggesting it may serve as a promising prognostic biomarker and a novel therapeutic target for improving clinical outcomes in LUSC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07948-y.Keywords: Lung squamous cell carcinoma, LINE-1 Retrotransposition, Metabolic stress, Cuproptosis resistance, Mitochondrial dysfunction

Introduction

Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) is a subtype of non-small cell lung cancer that accounts for approximately 30% of all lung cancer cases, with a rising incidence globally [1]. The prognosis for LUSC patients remains poor, with a five-year survival rate of only 15 ~ 20% due to late-stage diagnosis and limited treatment options [2]. Current clinical therapies, including chemotherapy and targeted therapies, often face challenges such as drug resistance and adverse side effects, leading to suboptimal outcomes [3]. Recent studies have highlighted the critical role of mitochondrial dysfunction in cancer progression, particularly in LUSC, where altered energy metabolism and oxidative stress contribute to tumor aggressiveness [4, 5]. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying mitochondrial dysregulation in LUSC is essential for developing effective treatment strategies and improving clinical outcomes for patients afflicted by this aggressive disease.

To identify promising prognostic biomarkers and novel therapeutic targets for LUSC, we established a modified DeFuse algorithm - an analytical tool for detecting fusion transcripts - and subsequently applied it to systematically screen for Long Interspersed Nuclear Element-1 (LINE-1, L1) chimeric transcripts (LCTs) in LUSC [6, 7]. Through this approach, we successfully identified 13 high-frequency pathogenic LCTs that are recurrently detected in LUSC. LINE-1 retrotransposon is the only known active autonomous retrotransposable element in human, accounting for approximately 17% of the total genomic sequence [8]. Pan-cancer analysis of whole genomes showed that aberrant LINE-1 insertions may result in pro-tumorigenic outcomes including chromosomal rearrangements, genomic instability and insertional mutagenesis of tumor suppressor genes [9]. In multiple tumor types, the expression level of LINE-1 is significantly upregulated, which correlates with the proliferation and metastatic capacities of tumor cells [10, 11]. Additionally, the hypomethylated status of LINE-1 has also been utilized as a diagnostic biomarker for multiple types of cancers [12, 13]. Although recent studies have highlighted the role of LINE-1 in cancer biology, the underlying mechanisms by which LINE-1 influences cellular metabolism and mitochondrial integrity remain unclear.

LINE-1 contains two open reading frames (ORF1 and ORF2) [14]. ORF1 encodes an RNA-binding protein (ORF1p) with nucleic acid chaperone activity, and ORF2 encodes ORF2p, which has endonuclease and reverse-transcriptase activities. Taylor, et al. recently identified the structure of L1-ORF2p and elucidated the mechanism of LINE-1 ORFs in orchestrating the optimal processes of LINE-1 reverse transcription and targeted insertion during evolution, by activating LINE-1 within intron regions, which can then be transcribed into the adjacent exon to generate LCTs [15]. Many LCTs have been identified to contribute to carcinogenesis by altering the transcription and expression of target genes, mediating epigenetic regulation, and activating oncogenic signaling pathways [16]. For instance, the chimeric L1-MYC, as the first LCT to be discovered and reported, triggered the rearrangement and amplification of the oncogene MYC [17]. Inhibition of LCTs with reverse transcriptase inhibitors, a class of agents commonly used in HIV and HBV, has shown promising results in clinical studies and would affect tumor progression and metastatic dissemination [18]. Therefore, identification of the molecular mechanisms in regulating LCT-driven carcinogenesis might lead to the discovery of novel therapeutic targets for clinical applications.

L1-ATP8B1, a prominent tumor-specific LCT with high expression in LUSC tissues and significantly correlated with poor clinical outcomes in LUSC, interferes with the transcription and expression of ATP8B1 gene, which encodes a member of P-type cation transport ATPases and is responsible for maintaining membrane phospholipid homeostasis and mitochondrial dynamics [19, 20]. Furthermore, ATP8B1 is also involved in the transport of cardiolipin [21–23], a major lipid component on mitochondrion inner membrane that plays an important role in maintaining the structural integrity of electron transport chain (ETC) complex [24]. However, whether L1-ATP8B1 orchestrates LCT-driven mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic reprogramming to aggravate LUSC progression has not yet been fully disclosed.

In this study, we identify L1-ATP8B1 as a novel prognostic biomarker for unfavorable outcomes in LUSC, with considerable translational potential for non-invasive LUSC screening [25]. Mechanistically, we unravel a previously uncharacterized regulatory cascade: L1-ATP8B1 upregulation—cardiolipin depletion—prohibitin 1 SUMOylation/ubiquitination switch—cuproptosis resistance. Specifically, we demonstrated that L1-ATP8B1 disrupts mitochondrial membrane integrity, impairs lipid homeostasis, and compromises the stability of core mitochondrial proteins via employing systematic molecular biology experiments. Concurrently, L1-ATP8B1 modulates cellular energy metabolism and stress response pathways to promote LUSC oncogenesis. Notably, antioxidant phytochemicals from Myrtus communis L. fruit modulate mitochondrial oxidative stress and lipid metabolism [26], offering complementary non-synthetic strategies for cancer metabolic stress regulation. Furthermore, while post-translational modifications (PTMs) are well recognized to play pivotal roles in regulating metabolic enzyme activity and maintaining cellular homeostasis [26], our study firstly underscored the central role of LINE-1 in orchestrating PTMs to bridge protein stability and metabolic reprogramming.

Critically, combined pharmacological inhibition of reverse transcriptase and glutathione (GSH) synthesis effectively abrogates L1-ATP8B1-driven LUSC xenograft growth in vivo. Notably, the future development of L1-ATP8B1-targeted nanocarrier-based delivery systems holds great promise for enhancing therapeutic specificity and efficacy [27], addressing an unmet clinical need in LUSC treatment. Collectively, our findings establish L1-ATP8B1 as a key driver of LUSC progression and cuproptosis resistance through the cardiolipin/PHB1 axis, providing a strong preclinical rationale for the development of L1-ATP8B1-centered diagnostic strategies and combinatorial therapeutic regimens to improve clinical outcomes for LUSC patients.

Results

L1-ATP8B1 predicted poor clinical outcomes and correlated with mitochondrial dysfunction in LUSC patients

We previously identified pathogenic LCTs with high frequency in 448 LUSC samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset [6]. In the present study, we investigated the incidence of the top 13 LCTs and their associations with multiple clinicopathological characteristics in 190 LUSC samples from Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital (TJMUCH) cohort. Among these top-ranked LCTs, L1-ATP8B1 exhibited the highest detection rate (53%) in patients with short survival (≤ 40 months) compared to those with long survival (> 40 months, Fig. 1A). LUSC samples from the TJMUCH cohort were stratified into two groups (LUSCL1−ATP8B1+ and LUSCL1−ATP8B1−) based on L1-ATP8B1 expression levels. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis confirmed that high L1-ATP8B1 expression was significantly associated with poor overall survival in LUSC samples (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, we assessed the efficacy of L1-ATP8B1 as a diagnostic biomarker for LUSC. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis revealed area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.579 and 0.636 for 1-year and 3-year survival rates, respectively, indicating that L1-ATP8B1 possesses moderate discriminative ability for LUSC diagnosis (Fig. 1C).

The results of sequence analysis indicated that the detected L1-ATP8B1 is a chimeric sequence fragment generated by the transcription of LINE-1 in the intronic region into exon 3 of ATP8B1 (Fig. 1D). We further analyzed the associations between L1-ATP8B1 and other clinicopathological parameters. High L1-ATP8B1 expression was found to be associated with larger tumor size, lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis in LUSC patients (Fig. 1E). Additionally, L1-ATP8B1 expression correlated with patients’ smoking history and centrally located tumors. Collectively, these findings indicate that L1-ATP8B1 is predictive of adverse clinical outcomes and may serve as a novel diagnostic biomarker for LUSC.

Hypomethylation of the LINE-1 promoter has been reported to enhance the transcriptional activity of LINE-1, which in turn generates more active LCTs and thereby promotes tumor progression [28]. We therefore analyzed the correlation between the transcriptional levels of L1-ATP8B1 and the methylation levels of the LINE-1 promoter. Pearson linear regression analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between LINE-1 methylation levels and L1-ATP8B1 transcription (R = -0.610, P < 0.01, Fig. 1F). Among LINE-1 encoding proteins, L1-ORF1p is an RNA-binding protein with nucleic acid chaperone activity and has been recognized as a promising biomarker for multiple cancer types [13]. We thus examined the levels of L1-ORF1 and found that they were positively correlated with L1-ATP8B1 transcription (R = 0.673, P < 0.01, Fig. 1G), while negatively correlated with LINE-1 methylation levels (R = -0.565, P < 0.01, Fig. 1H). Furthermore, consistent with these transcriptional findings, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining results showed significantly higher L1-ORF1p expression in LUSCL1−ATP8B1+ group than in LUSCL1−ATP8B1− group (Figure S1).

We further identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) by comparing LUSCL1−ATP8B1+ and LUSCL1−ATP8B1− samples from TCGA dataset. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that multiple mitochondria-related biological processes (BPs) and cellular components (CCs) were enriched in LUSCL1−ATP8B1+ group, including mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I assembly, mitochondrial translational elongation, and mitochondrial electron transport from NADH to ubiquinone. These results suggested that L1-ATP8B1 may induce mitochondrial dysfunction in LUSC (Fig. 1I, left panel). Consistently, DEG enrichment analysis of samples from the TJMUCH cohort also demonstrated enrichment of mitochondrial metabolism-related pathways in LUSCL1−ATP8B1+ samples, indicating that L1-ATP8B1-mediated protumoral activity may be associated with mitochondrial metabolic disorders (Fig. 1I, right panel). Collectively, these findings indicate that hypomethylation-induced overexpression of L1-ATP8B1 triggers mitochondrial dysfunction, which in turn contributes to malignant progression and adverse clinical outcomes in LUSC.

L1-ATP8B1 facilitated oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and energy generation by reshaping cellular mitochondrial functions

We established an L1-ATP8B1-overexpressing LUSC cell line (H520-L1-ATP8B1) by transducing NCI-H520 cells with a recombinant lentivirus harboring synthetic core sequence of L1-ATP8B1. Control cells (H520-NC) were generated by transducing NCI-H520 cells with an empty vector lentivirus. The successful construction of these cell lines was verified using rolling circle amplification-fluorescence in situ hybridization (RCA-FISH) assays. RCA-FISH results demonstrated that endogenous chimeric L1-ATP8B1 could be detected in parental NCI-H520 cells but was barely detectable in the normal lung epithelial cell line BEAS-2B (Fig. 2A). Consistent with the overexpression design, H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells exhibited significantly higher levels of chimeric L1-ATP8B1 sequences compared with H520-NC controls (Fig. 2A). To assess the impact of L1-ATP8B1 on mitochondrial function, we first examined mitochondrial quantity and distribution in LUSC cells and normal lung epithelial cells using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Compared with H520-NC cells and normal BEAS-2B cells, H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells displayed a marked increase in mitochondrial density accompanied by a reduction in cristae density despite maintaining clear morphological integrity (Fig. 2B). These observations suggest that L1-ATP8B1-overexpressing cells may have enhanced energy production to support increased cellular growth and metabolism.

To further investigate L1-ATP8B1-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, we next measured the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) in two LUSC cell lines (NCI-H520 and SK-MES-1) using a Seahorse Bioscience XF-24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer. These parameters were used to assess the activity of OXPHOS and glycolysis, respectively. In L1-ATP8B1-overexpressing cells, the mean values of basal respiration and maximal respiration (key readouts of OXPHOS activity) were both significantly higher than those in control cells (Fig. 2C, upper panel). In contrast, no statistically significant difference in ECAR (a surrogate for glycolytic activity) was observed between the two groups (Fig. 2C, lower panel). These findings demonstrate that L1-ATP8B1 specifically enhances OXPHOS activity in LUSC cells, with no effect on cellular glycolysis.

Next, we investigated whether L1-ATP8B1 regulates mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP, ΔΨₘ) in LUSC cells. The JC-1 fluorescent dye accumulates in the mitochondrial matrix and forms red fluorescent aggregates, driven by the electrochemical potential gradient across the mitochondrial membrane. Here, JC-1 probe assays revealed that L1-ATP8B1 significantly maintained mitochondrial stability and increased MMP (Fig. 2D-E). Furthermore, we observed that L1-ATP8B1 also led to a marked reduction in the enzymatic activity of mitochondrial complex I (Fig. 2F).

Further experiments revealed that overexpression of L1-ATP8B1 in H520 cells resulted in a significant ~ 2-fold increase in intracellular ATP synthesis compared with control cells (Fig. 2G). Additionally, using MitoSOX Red fluorescent probe staining, we observed a marked elevation in intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in L1-ATP8B1-overexpressing cells (Fig. 2H). In subsequent functional inhibition assays, treatment with the reverse transcriptase inhibitor NVR significantly reversed the L1-ATP8B1-induced alterations in mitochondrial functions (Fig. 2D-H). Collectively, these mitochondrial function assay results demonstrate that L1-ATP8B1 enhances energy generation and promotes a high oxidative state in LUSC cells by modulating the activity of mitochondrial complex I.

We next investigated the role of L1-ATP8B1 in regulating the aggressive phenotypes of LUSC cells. Compared with control cells, H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells exhibited significantly increased proliferation rates and enhanced invasive capacity (Figure S2A-C). To determine whether L1-ATP8B1 promotes LUSC cell aggressiveness in a LINE-1-mediated endogenous reverse transcriptase-dependent manner, we performed functional assays using two approaches: treating H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells with the reverse transcriptase inhibitor NVR, or knocking down L1-ORF1p expression. As anticipated, both NVR treatment (Figure S2A-C) and L1-ORF1p silencing (Figure S2D-G) attenuated the enhanced proliferation and aggressiveness observed in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells. These findings were further validated independently in another LUSC cell line, SK-MES-1 (Figure S3). Collectively, these data demonstrate that L1-ATP8B1 reshapes mitochondrial function in a LINE-1-mediated endogenous reverse transcriptase-dependent manner, thereby enhancing OXPHOS activity and boosting energy production to drive the malignant behaviors of LUSC cells.

L1-ATP8B1 induced metabolic stress and disrupted mitochondrial membrane lipid homeostasis by interfering with the phospholipid transporter ATP8B1

To identify the pro-tumoral signaling pathways driven by L1-ATP8B1, we profiled 24 primary LUSC tissue samples using the nCounter® PanCancer IO-360™ Panel. Consistent with our observations in LUSC cell lines, the cell proliferation pathway was upregulated in LUSCL1−ATP8B1+ tissues compared with LUSCL1−ATP8B1− tissues, whereas the cell apoptosis and autophagy pathways were downregulated (Fig. 3A). Notably, the metabolic stress pathway was significantly activated in LUSCL1−ATP8B1+ samples (Fig. 3A) - a finding that aligns with our earlier observations of altered intracellular ATP generation and ROS production in L1-ATP8B1-overexpressing cells (Fig. 2G-H). We further performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to compare gene expression patterns between LUSCL1−ATP8B1+ and LUSCL1−ATP8B1− tissues. GSEA results demonstrated that the gene sets enriched in LUSCL1−ATP8B1+ tissues were primarily associated with several key biological processes: enhanced energy metabolism, elevated oxidative stress, and dysregulated transmembrane transporters. Additionally, phospholipid metabolism-related gene sets were downregulated in LUSCL1−ATP8B1+ tissues (Fig. 3B).

We have previously demonstrated that the formation of LCTs inhibits the transcription of their chimeric genes [6]. ATP8B1 is a membrane-localized phospholipid transporter protein. Here, to investigate whether LINE-1 chimerism affects ATP8B1 expression, we analyzed the correlation between L1-ATP8B1 and ATP8B1 levels in LUSC samples. The results showed a significant negative correlation between the mRNA levels of L1-ATP8B1 and ATP8B1 (R = -0.539, P < 0.01, Fig. 3C). Consistent with this transcriptional pattern, IHC staining revealed that ATP8B1 protein expression was significantly reduced in LUSCL1−ATP8B1+ group compared with controls (Fig. 3D). Collectively, these findings suggest that L1-ATP8B1 may induce metabolic stress by impairing the expression of the phospholipid transporter ATP8B1.

To further characterize the biological processes modulated by L1-ATP8B1, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells and their corresponding control cells. Transcriptomic analysis identified 1075 DEGs in total, with 539 genes upregulated and 536 genes downregulated relative to controls. Functional enrichment analysis of these DEGs revealed significant enrichment in regulation of epithelial cell proliferation, membrane potential modulation, and phospholipid metabolism-related events (e.g., phospholipid transporter activity, ATPase-coupled intramembrane lipid transporter activity, and regulation of membrane lipid distribution; Fig. 3E). These findings indicate that L1-ATP8B1 extensively remodels the cellular transcriptional landscape by systematically regulating phospholipid translocation and metabolism.

Given the established roles of ATP8B1 in regulating mitochondrial metabolism and membrane lipid distribution [20], we next employed ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) to analyze metabolites in mitochondria-enriched membrane fractions isolated from H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells and their corresponding control cells. This analysis revealed distinct differences in mitochondrial membrane metabolite profiles between the two groups. Using a screening threshold of |log2(FC)| > 1.5 and P < 0.05, we identified differentially accumulated metabolites, and surprisingly found that most of them showed a significant decrease in abundance (Fig. 3F-G).

Further classification analysis showed that the differentially accumulated metabolites were predominantly enriched in glycerophospholipids (GPs), accounting for 39.60% and 29.84% of total differential metabolites in the control and H520-L1-ATP8B1 groups, respectively (Fig. 3H). Additionally, glycerolipids (GLs), sphingolipids (SPs), prenol lipids (PRs), and saccharolipids (SLs) were each significantly downregulated in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells (Fig. 3I). A more detailed analysis of GP metabolites revealed that most of them were downregulated, including cardiolipin (CL), phosphatidylinositol (PI), and phosphatidic acid (PA) (Fig. 3J). To validate the mass spectrometry-based findings of altered mitochondrial membrane cardiolipin content, we used flow cytometry to quantitatively measure cardiolipin levels in the mitochondrial membranes of H520-L1-ATP8B1 and control cells. Cells were stained with 10-N-nonyl acridine orange (NAO) - a cardiolipin -specific fluorescent probe that binds exclusively to mitochondrial membrane cardiolipin and emits fluorescence, with signal intensity positively correlating with cardiolipin abundance. Flow cytometry results demonstrated a significant reduction in mean NAO fluorescence intensity in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells compared with control cells (Fig. 3K), which was highly consistent with the mass spectrometry-derived conclusion of decreased cardiolipin content. Collectively, these results confirm that L1-ATP8B1 disrupts mitochondrial membrane lipid homeostasis by specifically reducing cardiolipin accumulation, a process dependent on its interference with the expression and function of ATP8B1.

L1-ATP8B1 triggered mitochondrial dysfunction and aggressiveness of LUSC cells via suppressing mitochondrial membrane protein prohibitin 1 (PHB1) expression

Given L1-ATP8B1’s established roles in regulating mitochondrial metabolism and membrane lipid distribution, we performed comprehensive proteomic profiling of mitochondrial proteins from H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells and their control counterparts. A volcano plot was generated to visualize differentially expressed mitochondrial proteins (Fig. 4A). We further constructed a heatmap to illustrate global expression patterns, which showed that L1-ATP8B1 induced upregulation of multiple proteins involved in mitochondrial energy metabolism - forming a distinct red cluster. Notably, these proteins were significantly downregulated in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells treated with cardiolipin (Fig. 4B). Conversely, another set of proteins exhibited suppressed expression in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells (appearing as a blue cluster) and were upregulated following cardiolipin treatment (Fig. 4B). Of note, SUMO2 - a ubiquitin-like protein that covalently modifies target proteins to regulate their localization, stability, and interactions - was identified in this blue cluster.

To systematically identify key proteins with core regulatory roles in L1-ATP8B1-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and cellular physiology, we adopted a multidimensional screening strategy. Specifically, we constructed a Venn diagram integrating four protein sets: (1) differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells relative to controls; (2) proteins annotated to the “mitochondrion organization” Gene Ontology (GO) term (GO:0007005); (3) cardiolipin-binding proteins; and (4) differentially SUMOylated proteins. Analysis of this Venn diagram revealed a significant hierarchical correlation in the distribution of proteins across the four sets. Notably, PHB1 was the only molecule identified at the intersection of all four circles (Fig. 4C). This finding suggests that PHB1 may serve as a core regulatory factor that mediates the four biological processes outlined above.

We next compared PHB1 expression levels in LUSC samples via IHC staining, which showed a significantly lower protein expression in LUSCL1−ATP8B1+ tissues compared with LUSCL1−ATP8B1− tissues (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, subcellular localization analysis revealed that PHB1 protein expression was specifically reduced in the mitochondrial fraction of H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells. Importantly, this reduction in mitochondrial PHB1 could be reversed by treatment with the reverse transcriptase inhibitor NVR (Fig. 4E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that L1-ATP8B1 suppresses the expression of PHB1 in the mitochondrial compartment of LUSC cells.

PHB1 is an inner mitochondrial membrane protein that regulates multiple mitochondrial functions [29]. To assess the biological relevance of PHB1 in L1-ATP8B1-positive LUSC, we transfected H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells with a recombinant plasmid harboring the PHB1 coding sequence (generating H520-L1-ATP8B1-OV-PHB1 cells). As expected, L1-ATP8B1 overexpression reduced the enzymatic activity of mitochondrial complex I; however, ectopic PHB1 overexpression significantly rescued this reduced activity (Fig. 4F). Concomitantly, PHB1 overexpression also attenuated other L1-ATP8B1-induced mitochondrial functional abnormalities, including perturbations in MMP, ATP production, and intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 4G-I). These findings demonstrate that L1-ATP8B1 impairs mitochondrial complex I activity and induces mitochondrial dysfunction in a PHB1 deficiency-dependent manner.

Next, we investigated the role of PHB1 in L1-ATP8B1-driven aggressive phenotypes of LUSC cells. Using the CCK-8 cell proliferation assay, we observed a significant reduction in proliferation rate in H520-L1-ATP8B1-OV-PHB1 cells compared with H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells (Fig. 4J). Consistent with this, PHB1 overexpression also significantly inhibited the migratory and invasive capacities of H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells (Fig. 4K-L). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that L1-ATP8B1 induces mitochondrial dysfunction and promotes LUSC cell aggressiveness by suppressing the expression of the inner mitochondrial membrane protein PHB1 - an effect dependent on LINE-1-mediated endogenous reverse transcriptase activity.

L1-ATP8B1 accelerated PHB1 degradation via interfering the balance between cardiolipin-dependent SUMOylation and TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination

In H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells, we observed a significant reduction in PHB1 protein expression, which was rescued by cardiolipin treatment (Fig. 5A) - a finding that suggested post-translational modifications (PTMs) might contribute to L1-ATP8B1-induced aberrant PHB1 expression. To explore the mechanism underlying this reduced PHB1 expression, we assessed the levels of multiple PTM types in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells relative to control cells. Among the PTMs analyzed, the levels of acetylation, succinylation, and lactylation were not significantly altered by L1-ATP8B1 overexpression. In contrast, PHB1 ubiquitination was markedly increased in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells (Fig. 5B).

The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) plays a critical role in regulating protein degradation [30]. To determine whether L1-ATP8B1 mediates PHB1 degradation, we treated H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells with the proteasome inhibitor MG132. Following MG132 treatment, PHB1 protein expression in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells was significantly increased, reaching levels comparable to those in control cells. This result indicates that PHB1 is degraded via the UPS (Fig. 5C). We further confirmed this using co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays, which showed the enhanced binding of ubiquitin to PHB1 in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells (Fig. 5D). Previous studies have demonstrated an inverse correlation between protein ubiquitination and SUMOylation levels during PTMs. Given our earlier observation that SUMO2 expression was suppressed in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells, we next examined PHB1 SUMOylation levels in H520-L1-ATP8B1 and control cells. Consistent with the inverse ubiquitination-SUMOylation relationship, PHB1 SUMOylation was significantly reduced in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells; notably, this reduction was reversed by treatment with the reverse transcriptase inhibitor NVR (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these findings suggest that L1-ATP8B1 promotes PHB1 degradation by disrupting the balance between PHB1 SUMOylation and ubiquitination.

Next, to investigate whether cardiolipin regulates PHB1 SUMOylation, we treated H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells with exogenous cardiolipin. This treatment resulted in increased levels of SUMOylated PHB1 and a concomitant reduction in PHB1 ubiquitination (Fig. 5E). These findings suggest that L1-ATP8B1 may reduce PHB1 SUMOylation by decreasing cardiolipin content in the inner mitochondrial membrane; this reduction in PHB1 SUMOylation subsequently leads to increased PHB1 ubiquitination and, ultimately, enhanced PHB1 degradation.

Previous studies have reported that the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM21 regulates the ubiquitin-dependent degradation of PHB1 [31]. To determine whether cardiolipin modulates PHB1 ubiquitination and degradation in a TRIM21-dependent manner, we generated two recombinant constructs: one harboring the TRIM21 coding sequence (for TRIM21 overexpression) and a TRIM21-specific short hairpin RNA (shRNA) construct (for TRIM21 knockdown). These constructs were then transfected into H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells. Ubiquitination assays showed that TRIM21 overexpression increased PHB1 ubiquitination (Fig. 5F, lanes 1 vs. 3), whereas exogenous cardiolipin treatment significantly reduced TRIM21-induced PHB1 polyubiquitination (Fig. 5F, lanes 3 vs. 4). Consistently, TRIM21 knockdown decreased PHB1 ubiquitination (Fig. 5F, lanes 1 vs. 5), and this effect was further enhanced by cardiolipin treatment (Fig. 5F, lanes 5 vs. 6). To explore whether cardiolipin regulates the interaction between PHB1 and its E3 ligase TRIM21, we performed co-IP assays. These assays revealed a stronger interaction between PHB1 and TRIM21 in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells compared with control cells; notably, exogenous cardiolipin treatment markedly attenuated this binding (Fig. 5G). The transcriptome sequencing data showed no significant differences in the transcriptional levels of key SUMO enzymes between H520-L1-ATP8B1 and control cells (Figure S4). Collectively, these results demonstrate that L1-ATP8B1 impairs mitochondrial cardiolipin transport, which strengthens the PHB1-TRIM21 interaction and in turn accelerates PHB1 degradation by increasing PHB1 ubiquitination and decreasing PHB1 SUMOylation.

Building on the established roles of cardiolipin and TRIM21 in regulating PHB1 degradation and expression, we further investigated their involvement in L1-ATP8B1-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction in LUSC cells. Mitochondrial function assays revealed that treatment with exogenous cardiolipin or knockdown of TRIM21 in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells both resulted in increased activity of mitochondrial complex I (Fig. 5H). Additionally, these interventions (cardiolipin treatment or TRIM21 knockdown) led to restoration of MMP and intracellular ATP production in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells (Fig. 5I-J). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that L1-ATP8B1 accelerates PHB1 degradation and suppresses PHB1 expression by disrupting the balance between cardiolipin-dependent PHB1 SUMOylation and TRIM21-mediated PHB1 ubiquitination.

L1-ATP8B1 induced cuproptosis resistance via elevating GSH generation dependent on cardiolipin depletion

Based on the proteomic analysis results (Fig. 4A), we further performed functional enrichment analysis, which revealed the dysregulated proteins were primarily enriched in biological processes including copper ion detoxification, anion homeostasis, response to ROS, and stress response to metal ions (Fig. 6A). Notably, we observed differential expression of several cuproptosis-related proteins in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells, including metallothionein 1E (MT1E), ATP7A, and glutaminase (GLS) (Fig. 6B). GSH and metallothioneins (MTs) act as endogenous intracellular copper ion chelators: they bind copper ions to prevent the release of free copper ions, which otherwise induce cellular damage [32]. GSH synthesis is dependent on GLS activity, as GLS mediates the glutaminolysis of glutamine to glutamate - a key step in GSH biosynthesis. Meanwhile, ATP7A (and its homolog ATP7B) functions as ATPase-dependent copper transporters, responsible for exporting copper ions out of the cell [33]. We further analyzed intracellular copper concentrations, showing that H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells had significantly lower copper content than controls (Figure S5A). These data demonstrate that L1-ATP8B1-driven upregulation of copper chelation and efflux-related proteins synergistically contribute to reduced intracellular copper levels.

To assess whether L1-ATP8B1 confers protection against copper-induced toxicity, we treated H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells and their control counterparts with escalating concentrations of exogenous copper chloride (CuCl₂) or the copper-carrier drug elesclomol (ES). ES functions to chelate copper ions, facilitating their accumulation within cancer cells and thereby triggering copper-dependent oxidative stress that leads to cancer cell death. Notably, H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells displayed reduced sensitivity to these cuproptosis inducers in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 6C-D), suggesting L1-ATP8B1 might protect against copper-induced toxicity and induce cuproptosis resistance. The transcriptome sequencing data and Western blot assays indicated no significant changes in iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster proteins or Tricarboxylic Acid Cycle (TCA cycle) proteins in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells (Figure S5B-C). Collectively, these data indicated L1-ATP8B1 conferred cuproptosis resistance via reducing intracellular copper accumulation.

To investigate the roles of cardiolipin and PHB1 in L1-ATP8B1-mediated cuproptosis, we treated cells with exogenous cardiolipin or engineered them to overexpress PHB1, followed by assessment of the expression of key regulatory proteins involved in cuproptosis. Our results revealed that while several proteins were upregulated in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells, only GLS exhibited significant downregulation upon treatment with exogenous cardiolipin or PHB1 overexpression (Fig. 6E), indicating GSH generation might play a critical role in L1-ATP8B1-mediated cuproptosis dependent on cardiolipin depletion. Therefore, we further investigated the expression of genes involved in GSH biosynthesis, and found that GLS, glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC), glutamate-cysteine ligase modifier subunit (GCLM), and glutathione synthetase (GSS) were consistently upregulated in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells (Fig. 6F). We also observed a marked increase in the transcriptional level of c-JUN and STAT1—two well-characterized transcription factors of GLS—in H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells, indicating that L1-ATP8B1 likely promotes GLS transcription in an indirect manner (Figure S6).

Given the critical role of GSH in copper ion binding and the formation of copper-containing molecular chaperones - processes that prevent the release of free copper ions - we further treated cells with buthionine sulfoximine (BSO), an inhibitor of γ-glutamylcysteine synthetase (γ-GCS) that suppresses GSH biosynthesis. We observed that intracellular GSH levels were significantly elevated in L1-ATP8B1-overexpressing cells compared with control cells, and this elevation was reversed by BSO treatment (Fig. 6G). Additionally, both PHB1 overexpression and exogenous cardiolipin treatment also inhibited GSH production in these cells. To evaluate the roles of GSH, PHB1, and cardiolipin in modulating cellular responses to copper toxicity, we treated cells with a combination of ES and CuCl₂. The cell viability results showed that BSO treatment, PHB1 overexpression, and cardiolipin treatment each significantly attenuated the L1-ATP8B1- mediated resistance to cuproptosis (Fig. 6H). These findings consistently demonstrate that L1-ATP8B1 confers resistance to cuproptosis in LUSC cells by elevating intracellular GSH levels. Importantly, this L1-ATP8B1-mediated cuproptosis resistance can be reversed by exogenous cardiolipin treatment, PHB1 overexpression, or inhibition of GSH biosynthesis.

Synergetic pharmacological therapy with reverse transcriptase inhibitor and GSH synthesis inhibitor abolished L1-ATP8B1-induced growth of LUSC xenografts in vivo

To establish LUSCL1−ATP8B1+ xenograft models, we subcutaneously implanted H520-L1-ATP8B1 cells into NOD-SCID mice. These mice were then randomly assigned to receive one of the following treatments: NVR alone (50 mg/kg/day), BSO alone (150 mg/kg/day), or a combination of NVR and BSO (synergistic therapy). After 24 days of continuous treatment, the mice were euthanized, and xenograft tumors were excised. Tumor growth curve analysis revealed that both NVR and BSO monotherapies significantly inhibited xenograft growth; notably, the combined NVR/BSO therapy further reduced tumor growth compared with either monotherapy (Fig. 7A-B). Additionally, the combined therapy exhibited favorable safety profiles, as no significant body weight loss or mortality was observed in treated mice throughout the experimental period (Fig. 7C). To evaluate the effect of treatments on tumor metastasis, we excised lung tissues from the mice and performed hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining to identify metastatic lesions. The results showed that treatment with NVR alone, BSO alone, or the combined therapy significantly reduced the number of lung metastatic nodules compared with the untreated control group (Fig. 7D).

Given our previous demonstration that the formation of LCT suppresses ATP8B1 transcription while upregulating L1-ORF1p expression, we performed IHC staining to evaluate how our therapeutic regimens (NVR alone, BSO alone, or combined therapy) affected the expression of these molecules in xenograft tumors. IHC results showed that L1-ATP8B1-mediated downregulation of ATP8B1 was restored by NVR monotherapy or combined NVR/BSO therapy, whereas L1-ATP8B1-induced overexpression of L1-ORF1p was attenuated by BSO monotherapy or combined therapy (Fig. 7E). Since L1-ATP8B1 drives tumor progression through mitochondrial dysfunction dependent on PHB1 downregulation, we also assessed PHB1 expression in xenografts; notably, NVR monotherapy or combined therapy significantly restored PHB1 expression (Fig. 7E). Consistent with these molecular changes, Ki-67 IHC staining revealed a significant reduction in tumor cell proliferation, while TUNEL assays demonstrated a marked increase in tumor cell apoptosis in mice treated with NVR alone, BSO alone, or combined therapy (Fig. 7E-F).

Consistent with our in vitro findings in human LUSC cell lines, mice bearing L1-ATP8B1-overexpressing xenografts exhibited concurrent increases in intracellular ROS and GSH levels. Notably, these elevations were reversed by treatment with NVR alone, BSO alone, or the combined NVR/BSO therapy (Fig. 7G-H). Our results further demonstrated that the expression of key genes involved in GSH biosynthesis and cuproptosis regulation - including GLS, MT1E and ATP7A - was significantly upregulated in LUSCL1−ATP8B1+ xenografts. Importantly, NVR monotherapy or the combined therapy effectively suppressed the overexpression of these genes (Fig. 7I). Collectively, these data demonstrate that the combined pharmacological treatment with NVR and BSO inhibits tumor growth and metastasis in vivo. This therapeutic effect is mediated by suppressing L1-ATP8B1 activity and reversing cuproptosis resistance in mice bearing LUSCL1−ATP8B1+ xenografts.

Discussion

Metabolic reprogramming is one of the cancer hallmarks [34]. The current landscape of LUSC research highlights its unique metabolic characteristics with lipid metabolism disorders and mitochondrial dysfunction [2, 4, 35, 36]. Our findings indicate that LINE-1 retrotransposition has significant roles in influencing clinical outcomes and mitochondrial dysfunction in LUSC patients, establishing the first link among LINE-1, cardiolipin depletion, PHB1 SUMOylation-ubiquitination switch, and cuproptosis resistance. Accumulating evidence has confirmed that LINE-1 is involved in tumor initiation and progression, however, most studies have focused on its roles in regulating gene expression or inducing genomic instability [37], with no prior investigation into its role in modulating lipid metabolism or cell death modalities. Herein, we extend our previous work and expand the boundaries of knowledge regarding the biological functions of LINE-1, providing novel insights into the coordinated mechanisms underlying metabolic reprogramming and death evasion in tumors. Furthermore, a combined pharmacological regimen targeting LINE-1 and metabolic reprogramming provides robust preclinical rationale for the development of L1-ATP8B1-centered diagnostic modalities and combinatorial therapeutic strategies, thereby addressing the critical unmet clinical needs in the treatment of LUSC.

Given the formation process of LCTs could inhibit the transcription and function of its chimeric gene [6], L1-ATP8B1 appears to interfere with the expression of the phospholipid transporter ATP8B1. Since ATP8B1 is a phospholipid flippase belonging to the P4-type ATPase family, with the main function in the transmembrane transport of phospholipids, it affects the physical properties of the cell membrane, signal transduction, and cell viability by maintaining the asymmetry of cell membrane phospholipids [37], including phosphatidylserine (PS), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylcholine (PC), and cardiolipin [19–23]. Since co-IP assays in Fig. 5F demonstrated that cardiolipin supplementation had no discernible effect on either PHB1 expression or the level of SUMO1-conjugated PHB1 in control cells with intact cardiolipin homeostasis, sharp contrast was observed in cardiolipin-depleted L1-ATP8B1⁺ cells, where restoration of cardiolipin triggered a marked elevation in PHB1-bound SUMO1 levels., we therefore postulate that cardiolipin acts as a core driver of PHB1 SUMOylation. Notably, in addition to interference of the direct trafficking role of ATP8B1, LINE-1-induced mitochondrial stress might also indirectly contribute to cardiolipin reduction via ROS-mediated oxidation/degradation and inhibited synthesis [38–40]. Here our findings indicate the homeostasis of mitochondrial membrane cardiolipin plays a critical role in maintaining mitochondrial integrity and function. The molecular crosstalk between ATP8B1-mediated lipid trafficking and LINE-1-driven mitochondrial stress in regulating cardiolipin metabolism requires further investigation.

L1-ATP8B1-driven alterations in mitochondrial dynamics not only affects lipid metabolism, but also contributes to remodeling of the mitochondrial protein expression profile observed in LUSC cells. The suppression of PHB1 expression by LINE-1 further exacerbates mitochondrial impairment. PHB1 functions as a lipid scaffold to ensure the integrity and functionality of the mitochondrion inner membrane, participating in regulation of mitochondrial complex activity [41, 42]. It has been reported that PHB1, through its role as a mitochondrial chaperone, could assist cardiolipin to stabilize mitochondrial respiratory chain supercomplexes [43], as well as functions as a tumor suppressor in liver cancer [44]. This relationship underscores the significance of PHB1 in regulating mitochondrial health and cellular proliferation. SUMOylation and ubiquitination are two PTM machineries that act in a synchronic and competitive manner [45]. Herein, the accelerated degradation of PHB1 through the modulation of cardiolipin-dependent SUMOylation and TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination by L1-ATP8B1 illustrates a complex molecular mechanism that links mitochondrial dynamics to the phenotypic aggressiveness observed in LUSC.

Additionally, the induction of cuproptosis resistance via elevated GSH generation, dependent on cardiolipin depletion, further illustrates how L1-ATP8B1 alters the metabolic landscape of LUSC cells. Recent studies have demonstrated that copper ions play a crucial role in both physiological and pathological processes of cells, particularly in the initiation and progression of tumors [46]. Research findings indicate that dysregulated copper metabolism is closely associated with the development of multiple tumor types [32]. Excessive accumulation of copper can induce cell death, and it promotes the apoptosis of tumor cells by triggering oxidative stress and impairing mitochondrial function [47]. Since recent studies indicated copper ions could interact with the components of mitochondrial TCA cycle, resulting in Fe-S cluster protein loss and subsequent proteotoxic stress [47], we analyzed our transcriptome sequencing data to detect the transcriptional levels of Fe-S proteins and TCA cycle enzymes known to mediate cuproptosis. Notably, no transcriptional changes in Fe-S cluster proteins or TCA cycle proteins were detected in L1-ATP8B1+ cells. Complementary Western blot assays further verified that the protein expression profiles of core TCA cycle enzymes remained comparable between L1-ATP8B1+ cells and control cells, regardless of copper exposure (Figure S5). These data demonstrate that L1-ATP8B1-mediated cuproptosis resistance in LUSC cells is independent of TCA cycle/Fe-S cluster transcriptional or translational modulation. Instead, this phenotype is driven primarily by ATP7A-dependent copper efflux, with GSH-mediated copper chelation further reducing free intracellular copper. These dual mechanisms synergistically restrict the copper load required for cuproptosis, defining a non-canonical axis of L1-ATP8B1-elicited copper tolerance in LUSC.

In addition, to elucidate the transcriptional regulatory mechanism underlying GLS upregulation in L1-ATP8B1⁺ cells—given that LINE-1 itself lacks intrinsic transcription factor activity—we analyzed our transcriptome sequencing data by profiling four well-characterized transcription factors governing GLS expression, i.e. c-Myc, NF-κB1, c-JUN and STAT1 [48–51]. Our analyses revealed that while the transcriptional levels of c-Myc and NF-κB1 remained indistinguishable between L1-ATP8B1⁺ and control cells, c-JUN and STAT1 exhibited marked transcriptional upregulation specifically in L1-ATP8B1⁺ cells (Figure S6), indicating that L1-ATP8B1 likely promotes GLS transcription in an indirect manner. Notably, bioactive phenolics from Origanum species have been shown to modulate metabolic enzymes including GLS [52], highlighting the translational potential of plant-derived herbal components in regulating antioxidant defenses and offering avenues for natural adjunct therapies in mitochondrial-targeted cancer treatment. And it is deserved to further investigate the integrated regulation involved in cuproptosis, TCA cycle and GSH metabolism in L1-ATP8B1-driven cancer progression.

Furthermore, the pharmacological therapy combining a reverse transcriptase inhibitor and inhibition of GSH synthesis, successfully counteracted LINE-1 mediated growth in LUSC xenografts, suggesting that intersectional targeting of LINE-1 offers a promising therapeutic strategy. The integrated strategy represents a paradigm-shifting advancement in cancer molecular therapeutics, addressing two critical hallmarks of cancer progression. Mechanistically, this combinatorial approach exerts a dual synergistic effect: reverse transcriptase inhibitors abrogate the aberrant retrotransposon activation that underpin tumor cell proliferation and genomic instability [53], whereas depletion of GSH effectively induces oxidative stress-dependent cell death and even enhances cuproptosis in tumors [54]. Since we demonstrate L1-ATP8B1+ LUSC cells exhibit reduced cardiolipin content and elevated GSH levels, we might propose a multi-biomarker panel consisting of L1-ATP8B1 levels, cardiolipin contents, and GSH concentrations for LUSC stratification, which might enable more accurate identification of patients who are likely to benefit from therapies targeting LINE-1 and metabolic remodeling, filling the gap between basic LINE-1 research and its clinical translation.

To address the feasibility and toxicity concerns of our therapeutic strategy, we note that while reverse transcriptase inhibitors have not been clinically standardized for cancer treatment, their potential for repurposing is supported by growing preclinical evidence targeting LINE-1 retrotransposition in multiple malignancies [55]; notably, NVR has an established safety profile in antiviral therapy, with reported dose-limiting toxicities (e.g., mild gastrointestinal disturbances and fatigue) that are generally manageable and reversible, and its favorable pharmacokinetic properties including sufficient tissue penetration are well documented [56–58]. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that prolonged administration or combination regimens required for cancer treatment may increase the risk of off-target effect, highlighting the need for future clinical studies to prioritize dose de-escalation strategies and real-time function monitoring to define its safe and optimal dosage [28]. Regarding BSO, we fully recognize its inherent clinical limitations, including dose-dependent myelosuppression, gastrointestinal toxicity, oxidative stress-related organ damage, and limited success in prior cancer monotherapy trials [59]. In our study, we emphasize that BSO was used only as a mechanistic tool and proof-of-concept agent targeting GSH metabolism to dissect L1-ATP8B1-mediated copper death resistance, not as a standalone therapeutic. For clinical translation, replacing BSO with tailored analogs mitigates toxicity concerns, and phenolic-rich Glycyrrhiza glabra L. root extracts represent a promising natural alternative for GSH modulation [60], which can target GSH-dependent cuproptosis resistance in LUSC with low cytotoxicity. Combined with reverse transcriptase inhibitors like NVR, such natural compounds may synergistically enhance cuproptosis sensitivity by coupling oxidative stress regulation with LINE-1 retrotransposition inhibition, forming a low-toxicity combinatorial therapeutic strategy for LUSC.

In summary, this study systematically elucidates LINE-1’s multifaceted roles in LUSC and validates a targeted therapeutic strategy, yielding critical findings as follows: Clinically, LINE-1 expression predicts poor outcomes in LUSC patients and provides a rational basis for patient stratification in LINE-1-targeted therapy. Mechanistically, LINE-1 enhances OXPHOS/energy production by interfering with ATP8B1 to disrupt mitochondrial membrane homeostasis and reshape mitochondrial functions; it also drives mitochondrial dysfunction and LUSC aggressiveness via suppressing the mitochondrial membrane protein PHB1, and confers cuproptosis resistance by upregulating GSH generation. Importantly, a synergistic reverse transcriptase inhibitor and GSH synthesis inhibitor regimen effectively abrogate LINE-1-induced LUSC xenograft growth in vivo. Collectively, these findings establish LINE-1 as a key LUSC oncogenic driver via modulating mitochondrial function, lipid homeostasis, and cuproptosis resistance, highlighting the clinical translational potential of targeting LINE-1-associated pathways for LUSC therapy.

Materials and methods

Patient information

In total, tumor samples from 190 cases of LUSC patients were obtained from Cancer Biobank of TJMUCH, which were treated with partial lung resection surgery at Department of Lung Cancer of TJMUCH (Table 1). No prior treatments, including chemotherapy or radiotherapy, were given before lung resection surgery was performed. This project was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University (Approval No. Ek2021143) and written informed consent was obtained from the patients. All experiments were performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell lines and cell treatment

NCI-H520 and SK-MES-1 cells were purchased from Cellcook Co., Ltd. and verified via Short Tandem Repeat (STR) multi-amplification method. NCI-H520 cells were cultured in RPMI1640 (Gibco BRL). SK-MES-1 cells were cultured in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM, Gibco BRL). All media contained 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. NVR (TargetMol, Cat. No. T1595) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma Aldrich, Cat. No. 472301) to make stock solution and the final concentration of NVR was 350 µM. Cardiolipin (Sigma Aldrich, Cat. No. C1649) was diluted to the final concentration of 5 µM. BSO (TargetMol, Cat. No. T7708) was dissolved in PBS to make stock solution and the final concentration of BSO was 5 mM. CuCl2 (Sigma Aldrich, Cat. No. 751944) was dissolved in PBS to make stock solution and the final concentration of CuCl2 was 10 µM. Elesclomol (TargetMol, Cat. No. T6170) was dissolved in DMSO to make stock solution and the final concentration of elesclomol was 10 µM. For cell treatment assays, growth media were replaced with media containing the final concentration of NVR/cardiolipin/BSO/CuCl2+elesclomol after cells were seeded for 6 h. For MG132 treatment assay, cells were treated with or without 10 µM MG132 (Selleck, Cat. No. S2619) for 6 h before cells were harvested. Cell lines were routinely evaluated for Mycoplasma contamination. All experiments were completed less than 2 months after establishing stable cell lines or thawing early-passage cells.

Mouse models

NOD-SCID mice, which were 7 weeks old and weighed about 17–18 g, were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free animal facility. For xenograft experiments, 5 mice were assigned to each group; randomization was conducted using a computer-generated random number list, and blinding was applied to tumor measurement and subsequent data analysis. We obtained different NCI-H520 cells carrying constructed lentiviruses (OV-NC and OV-L1-ATP8B1) and resuspended 5 × 104 cells in 100 µL of PBS with Matrigel, followed by subcutaneous injection of the cells into the flanks of NOD-SCID mice. For drug treatment experiments, animals were subjected to treatment with NVR (50 mg/kg/day), BSO (150 mg/kg/day), or both. After model construction, the weights and tumor sizes of each mouse were monitored every 2 days. All animal protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments of TJMUCH (Approval No.: NSFC-AE-2021179). All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The Wistar IACUC guideline was followed in determining the time for ending the survival experiments (tumor burden exceeds 10% of body weight).

Lentivirus construction and transfection

For L1-ATP8B1 insertion lentivirus construction, the fragment of L1-ATP8B1 was amplified by PCR using cDNA of NCI-H520 cells as template. Then the amplified L1-ATP8B1 fragment was inserted into pHBLV-CMV-MCS-EF1-Puro lentiviral vectors (Hanbio Co., Ltd.) and the constructed positive plasmid was confirmed by DNA sequencing. The recombinant lentivirus with L1-ATP8B1 core sequence was generated by co-transfection in cells as previously described [6]. The recombinant lentiviruses overexpressing PHB1 and TRIM21 were purchased from Hanbio Co., Ltd. and infected into cells as previously described. Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) constructs for TRIM21, ATP8B1 and L1-ORF1 were inserted into the pHBLV-U6-MCS-CMV-ZsGreen-PGK-Puro vector and lentiviruses with different shRNA were generated by Hanbio Co., Ltd.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, RT-PCR and qPCR analysis were performed following the manufacturer’s protocol as previously described [6]. The sequences of primers are shown in Table S1. To confirm the bands detected in the PCR assay were the genes as predicted, we purified and sequenced the PCR products (Invitrogen). All experiments were performed in triplicate. Relative expression level of mRNA was calculated as 2−△Ct (△Ct = Ct target gene – Ct reference gene).

RNA library preparation, sequencing and enrichment analysis

Library preparation and sequencing steps were performed as previously described. Briefly, the libraries were sequenced on Illumina® (NEB) following manufacturer’s recommendations [6]. The RNA sequencing data has been uploaded to GEO database (accession number: GSE181043). Raw sequencing data was aligned to hg38 reference using STAR 2.5.3a [61] and HTseq 0.11.2 [62] was used to quantify the gene-sample expression profile. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was identified using limma voom [63] with cutoff criteria set as follows: false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (log2FC)| ≥ 1. KEGG and GO function enrichment analysis of the interested gene sets were performed using clusterProfiler package [64].

Gene expression (RNA) profiling: NanoString methodology

Gene expression analysis was also conducted with the NanoString nCounter gene expression platform (NanoString Technologies) as previously described [65]. Briefly, RNA was mixed in a 3′-biotinylated capture probe and 5′-reporter probe tagged with a fluorescent barcode. Probes and target transcripts were hybridized overnight. Hybridized samples were run on the NanoString nCounter preparation station by using a high-sensitivity protocol. The cartridge samples were scanned at maximum resolution by using the nCounter digital analyzer. Gene expression profile (GEP) scores were calculated as a weighted sum of normalized expression values for the genes.

Quantitative methylation-specific PCR analysis for the methylation levels of LINE-1 retrotranspositions

Genomic DNA was obtained and purified from frozen LUSC tissues in quantities sufficient for bisulfite treatment. Bisulfite conversion was carried out on 500 ng genomic DNA using EpiTect Bisulfite Kit (Qiagen), according to the manufacturer’s protocol as described previously [6]. The experiments were repeated at least 3 times.

RCA-FISH

The sections underwent deparaffinization using xylene, followed by dehydrated in ethanol, rinsed in 2×sodium chloride-sodium citrate (SSC) and digested using 25 mg/mL Protease K as described previously [66]. After digestion with restriction endonuclease, single-stranded target DNA sequences were exposed by lambda exonuclease. The padlock probe was hybridized and cyclized with T4 DNA ligase. To generate adequate quantities of DNA for the synthesis of FISH probes, rolling circle amplification (RCA) was employed to amplify the DNA. The padlock sequences are as follows: 5’-ggc tta aga aac ggc gcc ctc gca tca ata ccg atc atc tct tcc cct cgc atc aat acc gat cat cat tgc gct ttt cag acc-3’ (for LINE-1); 5’-tta tga aca caa aat tct tgt gct gcg aat agc cat cca ctc cat tct tct gcg aat agc cat cca. ctc cat acc acg aac aac ctc act-3’ (for ATP8B1).

Quantitative proteomics

The quantitative proteomic studies were performed by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. as previously described [67]. Briefly, the proteins extracted from samples (n = 3 per group) were digested and then the resulting peptides were desalted, reconstituted and analyzed by Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). The MS raw data for each sample were combined and searched using the MaxQuant 1.6.14 software for identification and quantitation analysis. DEPs were further filtered based on the following cutoff criteria: FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC)| ≥ 1.

Immunohistochemistry

All procedures were performed as described previously [6]. The details for antibodies we used here are in Table S2 and a biotinylated secondary goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (Santa Cruz, Cat. No. sc-2039) was combined with streptavidin-horseradish peroxidase (HRP) and a DAB staining kit (Maixin Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blot

The cells were collected in a cell lysate buffer with protease inhibitors. For mitochondrial extraction, it was extracted with a mitochondrial extraction kit (Beyotime Biotechnology). The protein was quantified using BCA Protein Assay Kit (Solarbio) and performed Western blot as previously described [68]. The details for antibodies we used are in Table S2 and all experiments were performed in triplicate.

Immunoprecipitation

Cell lysates were harvested using lysis buffer and rotated at 4 °C as previously described [69]. Lysates were clarified by spinning. Protein concentrations were measured using BCA standard curves (Pierce). Flag antibody (for binding to flag-PHB1, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to the protein lysate and samples were rotated overnight. Immunoprecipitation was carried out using the Invitrogen Dynabeads Protein G immunoprecipitation Kit. Lysates were next subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblot analysis. The experiments were repeated at least 3 times.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) assay

Cells were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and postfixed with 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4) and kaliumhexacyanoferrate (K3Fe(CN)6) in cacodylate buffer. Subsequently, the cells were dehydrated in a graded alcohol series and embedded into Epon resin (LX-112, Ladd Research). Ultrathin sections were collected on formvar-coated grids, counterstained with uranil acetate and lead citrate and visualized with transmission electron microscope.

Measurements of oxygen consumption and extracellular acidification

The rates of oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) in various cell lines were measured with a Seahorse Bioscience XF-24 extracellular flux analyzer, as detailed previously. Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well on Seahorse XF-24 polystyrene tissue culture plates, with 9 biological replicates per group. Inhibitors were used at the following concentrations: Oligomycin (1.5 µM), Carbonyl cyanide 4-trifuoromethoxy-phenylhydrazone (FCCP, 0.8 µM), Antimycin A (1.5 µM) and Rotenone (3 µM).

Cell proliferation assay

Cell Counting Kit 8 (CCK8) assay was used to detect proliferation ability. Cells were inoculated in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells/100µL/well. 10 µL CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. The cells were incubated at 37℃ for 2 h and the absorbance at 450/650 nm was measured by a microplate reader (Synergy HT). The mean values of the three replicates were calculated.

Cell apoptosis assay

Annexin-V-APC Apoptosis Assay Kit was used to detect cell apoptosis. After the cells were collected, they were washed with PBS and resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 105 cells/mL to prepare single-cell suspension. Subsequently, 5 µL Annexin-V and 5 µL APC were added to the cell suspension and the mixture was incubated in dark for 15 min. Fluorescent signals were obtained using flow cytometry (BD Biosciences). The experiments were repeated at least 3 times.

Wound healing assay

Cells were inoculated in 6-well plates. When the density reached 90 ~ 100%, draw a line with a 20 µl pipette tip on the monolayer cells to form “scratches”. Five fields were selected under the microscope at 0 h and 48 h after scribbling. The scratch distance was measured three times each time to take the average value. Cell migration rate = (0 h scratch distance − 48 h scratch distance) /0 h scratch distance ×100%. The mean values of the three replicates were calculated.

Trans-well invasion assay

The invasion ability was detected using Matrigel gel and Trans-well plate. The cells were inoculated in Matrigel and 200 µL serum-free RPMI-1640 at a density of 2 × 104 cells. The number of cells was counted from five fields randomly selected, and the average value of the three complex pores was calculated. The experiments were repeated at least 3 times.

Lipidomic profiling

Lipids were extracted from mitochondrial samples (5 biological replicates per group) using a methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE)-based method. Briefly, 100 µL of sample was mixed with 300 µL methanol and 1 mL MTBE, vortexed for 1 h, and then partitioned with 300 µL water. After centrifugation, the upper organic layer was collected, evaporated to dryness, reconstituted in 90% isopropanol/acetonitrileand analyzed by UHPLC-MS/MS. Raw data were processed in Progenesis QI for peak alignment, filtering and compound annotation against the LIPID MAPS® and KEGG databases. After missing value imputation and total sum normalization, multivariate analyses were conducted. Differentially abundant lipids were identified by |log2(FC)| > 1.5 and P < 0.05.

Cardiolipin detection

Cardiolipin levels were detected using NAO staining combined with flow cytometry. Briefly, cells were harvested and washed twice with cold PBS. Then, cells were resuspended in PBS containing 100 nM NAO (UElandy, Cat. No. N4002) and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, cells were washed twice with cold PBS to remove unbound dye. Fluorescent signals were obtained using flow cytometry. The experiments were repeated at least 3 times.

Copper ion detection

Copper ion concentration was determined using a commercial copper assay kit (Beyotime, Cat. No. S1075), following the manufacturer’s protocol with minor adaptations. Briefly, for cultured cells, after washing with PBS, adherent cells were detached using trypsin without EDTA, and all cells were pelleted. Cells were lysed on ice with BeyoLysis™ Buffer, homogenized, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. For the assay, appropriate volumes of samples and standards were aliquoted into a 96-well plate. After thorough mixing, the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 5 min, and the absorbance was measured at 580 nm. The copper ion concentration in the sample was calculated from the standard curve. Experiments were performed in triplicate.

Determination of intracellular ROS

ROS levels were detected using a ROS detection kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were collected, incubated with DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min, and then washed with serum-free medium. Flow cytometry was used to detect DCF fluorescence at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm. The experiments were repeated at least 3 times.

GSH detection assay

The levels of GSH were measured in cell or tumor lysates according to the instructions of the GSH and GSSG Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology). The experiments were repeated at least 3 times.

Measurement of cellular ATP levels

Cellular ATP levels were measured using the Enhanced ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells seeded in 12-well plate were rinsed with PBS and homogenized in ice-cold ATP lysis buffer. After centrifugation for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant of each sample was combined with equal volume of ATP assay buffer and then the level of chemiluminescence was immediately measured using Beckman Coulter. The experiments were repeated at least 3 times.

Assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)

JC-1 was used to measure the MMP according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Bioss). Cells in 6-well plates were incubated with JC-1 staining solution at 37 °C for 30 min and then washed with JC-1 buffer. Fluorescent signals were obtained using fluorescence microscopy. The experiments were repeated at least 3 times.

Mitochondrial complex activity assay

The activities of mitochondrial complex I were determined by the Micro Mitochondrial Respiratory Chain Complex I Activity Assay Kit (Solarbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The experiments were repeated at least 3 times.

TdT-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay

Apoptotic DNA fragmentation was examined using the One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay kit (Beyotime). Cyanine 3-labeled TUNEL-positive cells were imaged under a fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software following the manufacturers’ instructions. Measurement data are presented as median (interquartile range) and compared through the chi-square (χ2) test. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the least significant difference (LSD) tests. Pearson Linear Regression analysis was performed to assess the correlations between expression levels detected by qPCR. Cumulative survival curves were determined via the Kaplan-Meier method. Univariate survival analysis between different LCTs and the overall survival of LUSC patients was conducted through the two-sided log-rank test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.
