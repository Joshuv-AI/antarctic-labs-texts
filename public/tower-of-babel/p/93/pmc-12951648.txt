Planning self-care with patients with venous leg ulcers: a reflective lifeworld study of healthcare professionals
Authors: Natali Johnsson, Catharina Lindberg, Cecilia Fagerström, Hanna Tuvesson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionVenous leg ulcers affect 1% of the population worldwide and more than 3% of the older population. Recurrence rates range from 57–78% within one year after healing. In Sweden, the shift toward specialised primary care increases the need to support self-care planning to enhance healing and prevent recurrences. The study aimed to describe the phenomenon of planning self-care with patients with venous leg ulcers, as experienced by healthcare professionals.MethodsTwenty-four lifeworld interviews were conducted with healthcare professionals and analysed using reflective lifeworld research approach grounded in phenomenological philosophy.ResultsThe essential meaning of the phenomenon was shown as the ability to cultivate a caring relationship which opens the door to the patient’s lifeworld and creates an embodied space to share responsibilities for self-care planning with the patient. Developing caring conversations was a way to support learning and strive for collaboration and mutual understanding through the use of continuous motivational work.ConclusionsHealthcare professionals play a crucial role in involving patients in self-care planning. A collaborative approach, grounded in caring conversations and motivational support, is essential to prevent venous leg ulcer recurrence.KEYWORDS: Healthcare professionals, primary healthcare, phenomenology, reflective lifeworld research, self-care, venous leg ulcers

Introduction

Venous leg ulcers (VLUs) are a worldwide problem and account for around 70–80% of all leg ulcers. They affect approximately 0.3–3% of the global population (Finlayson et al., 2018; Kolluri et al., 2021). A VLU occurs as a result of chronic venous insufficiency, a condition caused by venous hypertension (Krizanova et al., 2023; Raffetto et al., 2020) that leads to blood pooling and microcirculatory changes in the lower extremities. The calf muscle pump plays a crucial role in venous return; impaired muscle function contributes to venous stasis, while venous insufficiency itself may further weaken the calf muscle performance, creating a vicious cycle that delays healing (Bradbury, 2010; Comerota & Lurie, 2015). A VLU typically develops on the inner side of the lower leg, between the ankle and the knee, and is characterised by a healing time of more than six weeks (Santler & Goerge, 2017).

VLUs are more prevalent in older people and have a significant impact on quality of life at both the personal, social and psychological level, as well as financially impacting healthcare services (Kolluri et al., 2021; Phillips et al., 2018; Weir & Davies, 2023). Healing is a challenge, and recurrence is common despite treatment, estimated at rates from 57% to 78% within 12 months after healing (Raffetto et al., 2020; Rocha et al., 2022). Therefore, special treatment and care are needed to prevent VLU recurrence (Bonkemeyer Millan et al., 2019; Stanek et al., 2023).

The majority of VLUs are treated in non-acute primary healthcare settings, such as healthcare centres and community home care (Batas, 2019; Bolton Saghdaoui et al., 2023). In these settings, patients receive treatment and support from healthcare professionals (HCPs) throughout the ulcer´s progression (Berenguer Pérez et al., 2019; Bolton Saghdaoui et al., 2023). Guidance is essential to prevent recurrence and alleviate suffering (Gomes et al., 2023; Stanek et al., 2023).

Patients with VLUs need therapeutic education to fully comprehend their chronic condition (Bobbink et al., 2020; Bouguettaya et al., 2023). Such education aims to enhance their understanding of the underlying causes and mechanisms of VLUs, as well as to develop the skills necessary for self-care (Bouguettaya et al., 2023; Brown, 2018). Research has shown that supporting patients with chronic conditions like VLUs can significantly reduce the recurrence rate, highlighting the importance of education and awareness in managing such conditions (Behairy & Masry, 2022; Gomes et al., 2023). Previous studies suggest that improved patient understanding of VLU diagnosis, treatment and post-healing management contributes to recurrence prevention (Atkin, 2019; Stewart et al., 2018). HCPs have a crucial role in providing information and supporting the practice of self-care activities (Hopkins, 2020).

The World Health Organisation defines self-care as ‘the ability of individuals, families, and communities to promote health, prevent disease, maintain health and cope with illness and disability with or without the support of a health worker’ (WHO, 2022). Previous studies have shown that self-care for VLUs might be a way to prevent recurrences and maintain health (Brown, 2018; Gomes et al., 2023; Hallas-Hoyes et al., 2021). In recent decades, there has been an increase in research into self-care as a means of improving and measuring health, to reduce the burden of VLU recurrence (Brown, 2018; Gonzalez, 2017; Suehiro et al., 2017; Žulec et al., 2022). Self-care activities, such as elevating the legs, performing skin care or compression therapy, keeping a healthy diet and being physically active, can be performed by patients on their own (Gonzalez, 2017; Hallas-Hoyes et al., 2021; Qiu et al., 2023). Effective self-care for VLUs requires collaboration between patients and HCPs who can provide tailored support and encouragement (Jones, 2019).

VLU treatment is demanding, and both HCPs and patients may need support with management, decision-making and planning strategies to prevent VLU recurrences (Weller et al., 2021). Research has shown that HCPs lack knowledge on clinical guidelines for VLUs (Goudy-Egger & Dunn, 2018; Weller et al., 2020). Therefore, there is a need for education on VLU management and how to support patients with VLUs (Goudy-Egger & Dunn, 2018; Kuhnke et al., 2019).

HCPs have encountered challenges in developing treatment and care strategies for VLUs because of their intricate characteristics and the intensive level of care they require (Cullen & Phillips, 2009). HCPs often perceive patients’ non-compliance with specialised treatments for VLUs, such as compression therapy, to be an obstacle (Cullen & Phillips, 2009; Perry et al., 2023). The time constraints and heavy workload of HCPs can lead to a lack of motivation for engaging patients in care planning (Cullen & Phillips, 2009). These challenges are exacerbated by a lack of support from organisations inside primary healthcare systems. Furthermore, inconsistent healing outcomes can lead to dissatisfaction and frustration among HCPs (Cullen & Phillips, 2009; Grey et al., 2020; Lindholm & Searle, 2016). Although self-care planning is an essential component of VLU treatment, there is a lack of research on the specific strategies that HCPs should use to develop an appropriate, personalised self-care plan for a patient with a VLU.

Healthcare settings often have routines and guidelines for treatment, but not for how to involve patients in VLU self-care (Weller et al., 2020; Weller et al., 2021). Research suggests that providing HCPs with checklists or tools may assist them in identifying patients’ needs that can be met through self-care and offering guidance to explore possibilities (Moore & Coggins, 2021). However, educating a patient while planning for self-care for VLU may be challenging (Bobbink et al., 2020).

Research on VLU self-care is scarce and there is insufficient evidence regarding how HCPs can cocreate self-care with patients living with a VLU. In order to initiate a self-care plan, both the HCP and the patient must take an interest, collaborating towards a shared goal. This study aims to describe the phenomenon of planning self-care with patients with VLUs, as experienced by HCPs.

Materials and methods

Study design

In this study, the phenomenon of planning for self-care with patients with VLUs was described through a reflective lifeworld research (RLR) approach, based on phenomenological ontology and epistemology as described by Dahlberg et al. (2008). RLR is grounded in phenomenological and hermeneutical philosophy and focuses on studying lived experiences and the characteristics of events in the lifeworld. The RLR approach means that researchers must ‘go to the things themselves’ and encounter human lived experiences in order to gain a deep understanding of a phenomenon (Dahlberg et al., 2008).

Setting and sample

The study was conducted in primary healthcare settings in the southeast of Sweden, including both rural and urban areas, where HCPs provide ulcer care and self-care support to patients with VLUs. Healthcare in Sweden is decentralised, provided by regions and municipalities. Patients with VLUs are generally managed in primary healthcare settings. There are sometimes specialist ulcer teams in a region or municipality, consisting of a district nurse, registered nurse, assistant nurse and general practitioner with competence in ulcer treatment (SBU, 2014). However, the majority of district nurses and registered nurses work independently within primary healthcare settings, most often in community healthcare or healthcare clinics. Managers at a total of 20 primary healthcare facilities were informed about the study’s aim and 15 of them agreed to their facility being included. The participants were verbally informed about the study by their managers. Those who expressed interest in participating were given contact details and could reach out to the researcher responsible for gathering data. The participants were then provided with information about the study. Prior to their interview, each signed an informed consent form. The study included HCPs (registered nurses, specialised district nurses and nurse assistants) working in primary healthcare settings, who had experience of treating VLUs. A total of 24 HCPs were recruited, 23 women and 1 man. They had between 2 and 32 years (mean = 11.2) of experience of working with patients with VLUs. The participants’ age ranged from 27 to 65 years (mean = 49.6).

Data collection

Data were collected through qualitative open lifeworld interviews (Dahlberg et al., 2008). In accordance with the RLR approach, as described by Dahlberg et al. (2008), interviews were used to describe the HCPs’ experiences. Two pilot interviews were conducted. These were included in the analysis and results because of the substantial content and suitability for the aim of the study. The interviews were conducted by the first author (NJ) between September and November 2020. As a consequence of the COVID-19 pandemic, in-person interviews were not feasible for all participants. Nine interviews were conducted in person, and fifteen were conducted by telephone. The interviews were conducted in a conversational form, beginning with the open-ended question ‘Could you tell me about your experiences of planning self-care together with patients with VLU?’ (Dahlberg et al., 2009). Follow-up questions were asked to gain deeper insight into the phenomenon. Probes were used to acquire a more profound understanding of the phenomenon. These included ‘Could you tell me more?’ and ‘Could you give an example?’ Interviews lasted between 19 and 74 minutes (mean = 40.3) and were recorded and transcribed verbatim. The in-person interviews were carried out at a home healthcare office (n = 7), at the participant’s home (n = 1), and in a private space at a public facility (n = 1), as chosen by the participants.

Data analysis

In the RLR approach, interviews should be conducted with an open and curious mindset towards the phenomenon being studied. The main core of RLR approach is the phenomenon being studied, which is explored through participants’ lived experiences (Dahlberg et al., 2008). Accordingly, this study aimed to describe the phenomenon of planning self-care with patients with venous leg ulcers, as experienced by HCPs. To allow the phenomenon to manifest itself, the researchers should stay open and bridled during the entire process, without making any assumptions. The analysis process in the RLR approach involves a back and-forth movement between the parts and the whole (Dahlberg et al., 2008). The analysis began with reading the interviews several times in order to understand and comprehend the phenomenon as a whole. The process continued with searching for meanings, where the text was approached to identify smaller meaning-bearing parts related to the phenomenon. These parts were examined through a reflective dialogue with an open attitude, while maintaining respect for the whole. The meanings were then clusterd based on similarities and differences. These clusters comprised the initial structure of the phenomenon that facilitated the revelation of its essential meaning. The clusters were subsequently interconnected to uncover a new whole—the essential invariant of the phenomenon—providing a broader understanding and describing the most essential meaning of the phenomenon. The essence of the phenomenon could be clarified further by identifying constituents that described its nuances and variations. During the analysis process, there was an emphasis on avoiding defining what was uncertain, so as not to deliberately determine a specific interpretation (Dahlberg et al., 2009). The analysis was conducted by the first (NJ) and last (HT) author, and the entire process was deliberated within the research group. A reflective attitude of openness and bridling was maintained throughout the analysis, ensuring that pre-understandings were continuously questioned rather than taken for granted. This meant actively holding back assumptions about HCPs’ roles and routines and returning to participants’ descriptions to let the phenomenon show itself. Continuous reflective discussions within the research group further enhanced transparency and validated meanings, ensuring that interpretations remained grounded in participants’ accounts rather than researchers’ expectations.

Ethical consideration

Approval for the study was granted by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (Reg. No. 2020-00965) in accordance with the Swedish Ethical Review Act SFS, (2003:460). When conducting the study, the ethical principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration (World Medical Association, 2013) and the Swedish Ethical Review Act were observed (SFS, 2003:460). The participants were provided with verbal and written information about the study before being asked to provide written consent. Each participant signed an informed consent form prior to the interview. They were apprised of their right to withdraw from the study at any point, without providing a reason, and they were assured that confidentiality would be upheld throughout the research endeavour. All participants voluntarily chose to participate in the study; no one withdrew.

Results

The essential meaning of the phenomenon

The findings present the essential meaning of the phenomenon of planning self-care with patients with VLUs, as experienced by HCPs, and is shown as an acquired ability to create and cultivate caring relationships with a patient, with the objective of opening the door to the patient's lifeworld. Self-care planning starts with creating an open, trusting atmosphere and arousing a patient’s willingness to share their life story and needs. Being aware of and understanding a patient’s knowledge can be the basis for enhancing the self-care planning process by creating an embodied space through caring conversations and promoting mutual understanding and reciprocal learning. Where work routines are halting, collaboration with a patient is supported by building own routines based on previous experiences. This is a prerequisite for self-care planning. Planning for self-care with a patient requires a deep understanding of self-care principles and strategies. This can be used to guide the patient through challenges, even when resources for VLU care are limited. Self-care planning with a patient also means bridging the gap between the patient's expectations of their ability to understand and perform self-care and their potential lack of motivation to follow the plan during ongoing VLU care. This opens for the possibility of a collaborative activity where the HCPs and the patient work together during the patient's treatment period, to create a mutual understanding of self-care and plan for later self-care activities. Encouraging the patient to participate in their own care and identify their own responsibilities for self-care planning, and sharing and designating responsibilities creates opportunities for continuous self-care that can develop over time. This can help the patient become more independent. Clearly designated responsibilities create a possibility to develop self-care strategies, evaluate them and produce a self-care plan. Self-care planning with a patient evolves over time and through relationships. Often, motivational support and extended collaboration within a healthcare team are necessary.

The following descriptions of constituents further elucidate the essential meaning of the phenomenon: Cultivating a caring relationship, Caring conversations as a way to support learning, Striving for collaboration and mutual understanding, Sharing and designating responsibilities and Continuous motivational work.

Cultivating a caring relationship

Self-care planning with a patient requires a caring relationship, cultivated through an HCP’s conscious mastery of various abilities. Such a relationship is essential for understanding patient needs and creating a supportive space for self-care planning. By using their experience, knowledge and skills, HCPs can develop an open and trusting space where the patient is encouraged to share their experiences and narrative about their life situation. This underpins a co-planning of self-care activities and facilitates the identification of suitable strategies to support them. Cultivating a caring relationship involves listening, interacting and acknowledging the patient’s whole lifeworld, going beyond focusing solely on the ulcer by getting to know the patient.

…by getting to know a person, you might understand them better…//they’ll dare to tell you things… you get an idea of their life outside the healthcare centre//…like maybe I can see…the opportunities for self-care and the obstacles to self-care. (Participant 1)

Cultivating a caring relationship involves inviting the patient into the self-care planning. It requires respect for patient integrity and self-determination, and empathy for the patient's situational awareness of living with a VLU. An open and accepting approach regarding the patient’s own choices and self-care decisions is used to develop a caring relationship and support self-care planning. The healing period often provides an opportunity to develop a close relationship that enables tailored self-care planning based on the understanding gained thereby.

…we’ve gained a very close relationship, because it’s been very slow-healing, this ulcer…//And then we get a chance to start…//with our communication and self-care. (Participant 2)

Caring conversations as a way to support learning

The caring conversation is described as a powerful resource to support learning and thus enable self-care planning with the patient. Through a caring conversation, and by using various conversational techniques, HCPs can initiate a dialogue and provide customised self-care advice.

HCPs describe communicating accessibility, tranquillity and a coaching approach as essential for supporting learning and preparing a patient to understand self-care planning. Caring conversations are used to create learning by providing specific information and support reflection that can contribute to the development of self-care planning encompassing various strategies.

…ask some questions…how should we dress this, do you want to do it yourself…//… if you’re focused on self-care.//explaining to the patient, showing… how to clean and dress the wound//asking … does that feel OK, do you think you can do it, shall we try that, we can do that in a week or a few days. (Participant 13)

A caring conversation opens for cooperation and discussion of self-care needs and activities. It serves as both a tool and a learning activity that can lead to insights about self-care planning.

…talking about…//how important it is that they are also involved and see what the ulcer looks like and what they can do to get an improvement…//…explaining that if you do this//… eat healthy and exercise … or that we need to use compression …to//get the ulcer to heal faster. (Participant 14)

A caring conversation helps to impart necessary knowledge to a patient and build collaborative self-care planning. Information alone does not ensure patient understanding or acceptance. During such conversations, HCPs must repeatedly reaffirm and explain the information related to a self-care plan to ensure that the patient understands it, as comprehension may vary between patients as may their individual needs.

…use a lot of education//…not just give them a bandage and assume that it’ll work out//…it requires… a lot of education//… practicing and learning how to do it and understanding the importance of always having it on and so forth. (Participant 2)

Striving for collaboration and mutual understanding

Striving for collaboration between HCPs and patients is the starting point for self-care planning. The goal is to obtain knowledge and understanding of the patient’s needs, which may be complicated by advanced age, multimorbidity or social isolation. Collaboration stimulates understanding of self-care. HCPs face challenges when patients have difficulty understanding the importance of self-care, potentially leading to non-compliance with the self-care plan.

…it’s really important…that the patient gets it, and that applies to both of us, that both the patient and I understand what the goal is and…and how we can achieve the goal, set up a plan, it’s really important…//following up all the time… (Participant 5)

Knowledge and understanding of VLU related to self-care planning are essential for building collaboration and mutual understanding. A lack of experience in self-care planning is a barrier to the development of collaboration and mutual understanding. Self-care planning is complex and requires the balancing different perspectives and preferences to enhance mutual understanding. When collaboration and mutual understanding are challenged, feelings may be evoked that require awareness in order to move forward.

… what am I doing wrong//do I need to back off a bit …//… why is he getting angry… it’s a lot of information…//having situational awareness//…being really cautious…//… you can’t say it doesn’t evoke feelings//…it would be wrong to say that it doesn’t//…//feel pretty weary… (Participant 6)

Even with the best of efforts, self-care planning can be challenging when routines for self-care planning are lacking, and more structured work routines are needed. HCPs’ work experience can serve as a solid foundation for promoting collaboration and mutual understanding when structured routines for self-care planning are lacking.

… there isn’t any structured plan, I guess it’s more ingrained experience//… what you need to think about, that you start from somewhere and then maybe I think about the structure anyway… (Participant 6)

Sharing and designating responsibilities

Planning self-care with a patient involves sharing and designating responsibilities between the HCPs and the patient, which can take time and requires experience. Self-care planning depends on a joint agreement about who is responsible for each activity. Throughout VLU care, the patient gradually receives information about sharing and designating responsibilities. Encouraging the patient to actively take responsibility and ownership of various self-care activities is crucial.

…it’s really important, because even if I, as a nurse, am there and have the responsibility for the primary ulcer, the patient also has…a responsibility for themself, a desire to get better. (Participant 23)

During ongoing care for a VLU, self-care planning involves continuous discussions and evaluations with the patient. Such evaluations show that there are sometimes obstacles to self-care planning, for instance when a patient lacks an understanding of who should be responsible for different parts of the plan and for compliance. Follow-up of self-care planning is the responsibility of the HCPs and results in new evaluations, which identify any new needs and adjustments required. Later, the patient and healthcare professionals share the responsibility for following up and developing a new self-care plan.

…it’s important to make sure that it really…when it’s self-care, then they take care of that on their own, but that you’re still following up and making sure that nothing has gotten worse. (Participant 15)

During VLU care, development of new self-care plans occurs gradually and may encounter organisational obstacles, such as time constraints and a workforce shortage, which can impede the sharing and designation of responsibilities for self-care planning.

Continuous motivational work

Self-care planning is necessary for patients living with VLUs, but they need support through motivational work. According to HCPs, such work needs to be continuous throughout the self-care planning process, to engage the patient in the different self-care activities. When sharing self-care information, a motivational attitude is necessary. Supporting the patient to stay motivated and committed is challenging and demands endurance and patience, especially when the patient struggles to maintain motivation.

…it takes a lot of motivational work…the patient isn’t easy to motivate for the care you want to give//…you have to be patient and move slowly//…so that they will be onboard... (Participant 6)

Working with other team members can facilitate self-care coordination and planning and provide valuable support in involving and motivating the patient. Continuous motivational work supported by the team can encourage the patient’s commitment to self-care and adherence to the self-care plan.

… it’s a form of teamwork too/[continuous motivational work]/… With the patient, me and the nurse and the doctor//…everything has to work in the surroundings…//(Participant 11)

Discussion

The aim of this study was to describe the phenomenon of planning self-care with patients with VLUs, as experienced by HCPs. The essential meaning shows that cultivating caring relationships required HCPs to use skills and knowledge that opened the door to each patient’s lifeworld. Caring conversations enhanced understanding and strengthened relationships, supporting self-care planning and facilitating collaboration and mutual understanding. This also enabled shared and designated responsibilities and allowed patients to take ownership of their self-care plan. Continuous motivational support and teamwork were needed, as planning self-care together with a patient with VLU changed over time and across relationships.

Planning self-care with patients with VLUs cultivating a caring relationship based on skills and knowledge that contribute to revealing each patient’s lifeworld. Research shows that interpersonal relationships with the patient are crucial for understanding their needs and developing knowledge in care practice, as it provides insight into their lifeworld (Ekebergh & Lindberg, 2020). Our findings revealed that the HCPs showed openness and acceptance of patients’ self-care decisions, creating a supportive environment for self-care activities. Furthermore, a previous study has shown that a caring relationship facilitates cocreation of suitable solutions for self-care planning, through the use of advanced skills and respect for the patient’s integrity, autonomy and dignity (Bright, 2022).

Our findings also highlight the importance of the HCPs’ adoption of an active and sensitive attitude when providing information in caring encounters with patients with VLUs. This is supported by previous research, indicating that patients emphasise effective communication and accessible information from HCPs (Törnvall & Wilhelmsson, 2010), while other studies point to communication gaps that hinder the patient engagement in self-care planning (Trouth, 2024). Caring conversations promoted accessibility, tranquillity and coaching, thereby preparing patients to use effective self-care strategies. Johnsson et al. (2024), is consistent with findings who emphasised communications skills as a way to empower patients, as well as research illustrating how caring conversations support explanation, engagement and empowerment.

Our findings indicated that mutual collaboration between HCPs and patients with VLUs enhanced understanding of self-care and mitigated challenges. However, lack of experience could hinder the development of a self-care plan. Previous research has shown that a lack of knowledge and expertise among HCPs may have a negative impact on patients (Docherty, 2020), and that inexperienced HCPs tend to focus on the ulcer treatment and may overlook valuable holistic information about the patient (Smith & Sharp, 2023). On the other hand, HCPs’ work experience can be used as a foundation for promoting collaboration and mutual understanding even if the routines and time for self-care planning are limited. Our study also points to organisational barriers, which may limit opportunities to engage in self-care planning. Addressing these challenges requires organisational support that promotes continuity, teamwork, and professional learning. Structured routines, clear guidelines and digital tools may further support documentation, follow-up, and patient engagement. These findings should be understood within the Swedish primary care context, where access to resources and home care services varies across rural and urban settings. HCPs should strive to improve their knowledge and skills, to empower and engage patients into self-care planning (Docherty, 2020).

The findings of our study suggested that self-care planning with patients with VLUs required both sharing and designating responsibilities. Encouraging a patient to take ownership of their self-care activities and educating them about VLU care and their role in their own care is important for the self-care planning to be tailored for that specific patient. These findings are supported by previous studies, which have shown that promotion of a self-care plan in everyday life can lead to increased independence and the development of tailored activities, enhancing the value of self-care (Kapp & Santamaria, 2017; Moore & Coggins, 2021; Wilde, 2020). To promote understanding between patients and HCPs, barriers and enablers must be identified, in order to ensure effective care, prevent misunderstandings and promote adherence to the plan. However, previous research highlights that sharing responsibilities for self-care is a challenge for HCPs. There is a need for awareness of the complexity that the practice of self-care may require (Brown, 2020). Nevertheless, another study has emphasised that self-care for VLUs may not be suitable for all patients. Certain patients may possess more capacity and willingness to participate in self-care than others. Not all patients have the capacity to be actively engaged in their healthcare planning, and preferences for participation may be influenced by age-related and broader socio-cultural factors (Dowsett, 2021). Such challenges may influence motivation and strengths related to being active in self-care planning, as also indicated in the present study. However, if self-care planning is genuinely adapted to a patient’s abilities, some degree of co-planning might be possible. Being able to identify reasonable goals and activities is likely important for ensuring that self-care planning is supportive.

Additionally, our findings showed that working with other team members could provide motivational support to a patient through coordination and engagement in following the self-care plan. Similarly, other research has shown that a multidisciplinary team can potentially support patients by showcasing healing progress and improving the self-care planning process (Smith & Sharp, 2023). Furthermore, patients may need continuous motivation to support and engage in the planned self-care activities, and encourage their performance of those activities (Moore & Coggins, 2021). In this study self-care planning was experienced as emotional demanding, particularly in situations of prolonged ulcer healing or difficulties engaging patients, which could challenge the continuity of caring relationships. Collegial and interprofessional support, shared responsibility, and organisational flexibility may help mitigate emotional fatigue, as shown in recent research on home HCPs’ experiences of caring relationships (Mathiesen et al., 2024).

Methodological considerations

The phenomenological approach was chosen to describe HCPs’ experiences of planning self-care with patients with VLUs. This study followed the RLR approach as described by Dahlberg et al. (2008) to ensure rigour and validity through openness and bridling. Throughout the research process, the authors continuously reflected on and bridled their pre-understandings to remain open and sensitive to how the phenomenon unfolded in participants’ accounts (Dahlberg et al., 2009). A possible limitation was that the majority of the participants were women, which may have influenced the findings by reflecting perspectives shaped by gendered experiences of care communication. However, this distribution mirrors the gender composition among HCPs in Swedish primary care, and the rich interviews contributed to describing the essential meaning of the phenomenon of planning self-care with patients with VLUs. No substantial differences were identified between the accounts of male and female participants. Nevertheless, a more gender-balanced sample might have generated additional perspectives. However, in line with phenomenological principles, generalisation in this context does not aim at representativeness but refers to the essential meanings that transcend individual variations (van Wijngaarden et al., 2017).

A further limitation is that some interviews were conducted by telephone due to the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have affected the outcome. However, telephone interviews have been found to generate a relaxed environment, giving participants space and encouraging them to express themselves honestly (Trier-Bieniek, 2012). Regardless of the data collection method, the interviews in this study yielded rich and nuanced data.

The phenomenological analysis was conducted using the RLR approach to describe the essential meaning of the phenomenon of planning self-care with patients with VLUs, based on the lived experiences of HCPs. This lifeworld-oriented approach was considered appropriate for describing these lived experiences, thereby enhancing the study’s validity (Dahlberg et al., 2008; van Wijngaarden et al., 2017). From a lifeworld perspective, this suggests that in clinical practice, HCPs should approach the patient as a person living with a VLU within a broader life context. Such an approach requires an open, dialogical stance and sensitivity to patients’ everyday experiences when planning self-care.

A strength of the study is that the entire research group has previous experience as nurses and researchers, and the large amount of data from the interviews provided rich phenomenological material. Throughout the analysis senior researchers supported the first author in maintaining phenomenon-oriented focus.

Conclusion

Healthcare professionals have a crucial role in supporting patients with VLUs to participate in self-care planning. The study showed that cultivating a caring relationship is essential to understanding a patient’s lifeworld and designing a self-care plan with the patient. Caring conversations can be used to promote learning and collaborate to achieve mutual understanding. Creation of an embodied space makes it possible to facilitate the sharing and designation of responsibilities and support needs with HCPs. Continuous motivational support and collaboration within a healthcare team are crucial for preventing VLU recurrence and ensuring adherence to a self-care plan, as planning and needs evolve over time. The findings highlight the importance of a caring relationship focusing on each specific patient while stimulating collaboration and learning to create a tailored self-care plan together. Such an approach might require improved work routines within primary healthcare in relation to self-care, with increased continuity and time for dialogue.

Implications

Planning self-care with patients with VLUs is important for the patients, the community and the primary healthcare system. Planning for self-care together promotes and enhances patient participation. The advantages for the patient could include prevention of prolonged suffering and promotion of independence and empowerment. Building on the findings of this study, future research could include patients’ perspectives and those of informal carers (e.g., family members or friends) to further develop the understanding of self-care planning as a shared and relational process. Such knowledge could inform the development of digital tools supporting both patients and HCPs in self-care planning.

These findings imply that clinical practice can be strengthened by approaching self-care planning as a relational and dialogical process. This means emphasising trust-building, adapting information through caring conversations, balancing perspectives to reach shared goals, clarifying roles to foster patient ownership, and sustaining engagement through encouragement and teamwork.
