Spinal cord stimulation for upper limb motor function in people with chronic post-stroke hemiparesis: a feasibility trial
Authors: Roberto M. de Freitas, Shovan Bhatia, Erynn Sorensen, Nikhil Verma, Erick Carranza, Scott Ensel, Luigi Borda, Amy Boos, Jeff Goldsmith, Lee E. Fisher, Daryl P. Fields, Marc P. Powell, Shane Gordon, Jeffrey Balzer, Robert M. Friedlander, George F. Wittenberg, Peter C. Gerszten, John W. Krakauer, Elvira Pirondini, Douglas J. Weber, Marco Capogrosso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic arm and hand hemiparesis is a major cause of disability after stroke. Unfortunately, standard-of-care rehabilitation falls well short of the high doses required to experience improvements. In this feasibility study, we explored the use of cervical epidural spinal cord stimulation (SCS) to assist motor function and provide meaningful functional improvements without the need of a high-dose rehabilitation program. Here we report the final outcomes of this study testing safety, feasibility and preliminary efficacy of cervical SCS as a neuroprosthetic approach for chronic post-stroke upper limb hemiparesis. We implanted seven participants with profound motor deficits (Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA) scores 15–35) using two leads implanted unilaterally in the cervical spinal cord for 4 weeks. No serious adverse events occurred. Under SCS ON, motor function immediately improved regardless of impairment severity (average +32% strength and +5.6 FMA points). Notably, three of seven participants with residual corticospinal connectivity to finger muscles improved hand/finger movement with SCS. Despite performing only 8.6 hours of motor activity (5.5 hours with SCS ON), participants improved by an average of +6.6 FMA points at the end of the study compared to baseline, and spasticity decreased in all participants. Although all benefited, our preliminary analysis indicates that spared sensory function may be a determinant of responsiveness to SCS. These findings provide preliminary evidence of the safety, feasibility and efficacy of cervical SCS for chronic post-stroke hemiparesis, suggesting its potential as a fully implantable neuroprosthetic solution for assisting upper limb function in daily living. ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04512690.

Main

Stroke is the leading cause of arm paralysis in the United States1,2, with approximately 400,000 individuals developing chronic arm paresis each year3–5. Affected individuals rank the recovery of arm and hand function as their first unmet clinical priority6. After stroke, the loss of corticospinal axons leads to a stereotypical manifestation of hemiparesis7,8, characterized by loss of strength (that is, loss of the ability to activate muscles); loss of dexterity (that is, loss of the ability to grade force and control digits); spasticity (that is, velocity-dependent resistance to passive stretch of joints)9,10; and intrusion of abnormal synergies (that is, abnormal co-contraction of muscles during voluntary movements).

Despite decades of research indicating that high-dose motor rehabilitation (100–120 hours in 4 weeks) can improve these symptoms11,12, the rehabilitation dose in clinical practice falls well short of these goals (approximately 7 hours in 4 weeks)13. Consequently, considerable research efforts have been historically directed toward the development of neuromodulation technologies that could catalyze the effects of rehabilitation14–16. That is, these technologies aim to induce lasting therapeutic effects by modulating the nervous system activity during motor training, resulting in post-intervention improvements in absence of stimulation (for example, non-invasive brain stimulation). Most notably, vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) was recently approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for use in individuals with post-stroke hemiparesis14. When combined with 27 hours of motor rehabilitation, VNS increased Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA) scores for upper limb motor function more than rehabilitation alone.

By contrast, we sought to design an intervention that could offer immediate and assistive improvements in motor function without the need for motor rehabilitation. Recent studies suggested that epidural SCS may improve strength17, dexterity17, spasticity18,19 and synergies20 in people with spinal cord injury and other motor disorders. Cervical SCS selectively activates dorsal root afferents that project to upper limb motoneurons21–23. Through this excitatory pathway, SCS can immediately facilitate voluntary movements even without rehabilitation24,25. In addition to assistive effects (that is, changes in function that occur immediately as SCS is turned ON), SCS also enables therapeutic effects over time, resulting in improvements of motor function when SCS is OFF26,27. Therefore, different from other neuromodulation technologies14,15, SCS combines these two interdependent effects to drive effective improvements24,26.

Following this rationale, we conducted a first-in-human clinical trial to prospectively characterize the assistive and therapeutic effects of epidural cervical SCS in chronic post-stroke hemiparesis (NCT04512690). Our goal was to investigate whether SCS is safe and feasible as well as obtain preliminary evidence of efficacy. Specifically, we sought to address four questions: (1) Is cervical SCS safe? That is, does stroke predispose patients to greater safety risks for surgical and stimulation procedures? (2) Is SCS feasible? That is, can SCS be delivered at intensities that improve motor function without causing discomfort? (3) To what extent does SCS affect the different symptoms of post-stroke hemiparesis, and are these effects retained if SCS is discontinued? (4) Are there determinants of responsiveness to SCS?

To address these questions, we followed FDA regulations for non-significant risk studies by limiting the SCS implant period to 29 days (21 CFR 812.3[d]). During this period, all participants were assessed daily with motor assessments—that is, participants did not undergo a formal motor rehabilitation protocol. Following FDA regulations, the leads were explanted after 29 days. Although this trial was designed to minimize risks, our vision is for SCS to be used as a permanent neuroprosthetic intervention, akin to deep brain stimulation (DBS) for Parkinson’s disease, alleviating post-stroke symptoms immediately as stimulation is turned ON. Accordingly, we focused on evaluating the assistive effects of SCS, comparing outcomes between SCS ON and OFF conditions.

We previously reported preliminary evidence of (1) the safety and (2) the feasibility of SCS in the first two study participants24. In the present study, we systematically addressed all four questions within a completed cohort of seven participants with a broader spectrum of stroke etiologies, lesion profiles, sex, handedness, races, age, years after stroke as well as motor and sensory impairments. We found the following: (1) To ensure safety, stimulation of the C3−C4 spinal segment should be avoided due to the risk of co-activating the phrenic nerve, which can interfere with normal breathing. (2) SCS can be delivered at effective intensities without causing discomfort, regardless of sex, age and race. (3) SCS improved strength, arm dexterity, movement synergies and spasticity, with maximal efficacy achieved when SCS is ON. Moreover, retention of motor improvements depends on continued use of SCS, supporting the use of SCS as a permanently implanted device. (4) Individuals with milder sensory impairments tended to show greater improvements in motor function, whereas baseline motor impairment did not affect responsiveness.

Results

We conducted a pilot study (NCT04512690) aimed at testing efficacy and safety of epidural cervical SCS in n = 7 individuals with post-stroke chronic hemiparesis (Fig. 1 and Extended Data Table 1; list of outcomes in Supplementary Table 1). We assessed the efficacy of cervical SCS by measuring (1) immediate assistive effects on motor function (that is, the difference between SCS ON and SCS OFF conditions (Supplementary Videos 1–6)); (2) therapeutic effects (that is, the difference in function between timepoints with SCS OFF (Supplementary Video 7)); and (3) effective improvement (that is, the difference between pre-implant baseline and SCS ON conditions (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Video 6)). All participants were surgically implanted with a pair of 8-contact SCS leads in the dorsal epidural space spanning from C3 to T1 spinal levels (Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 1a–g). Over 4 weeks, we conducted motor assessments with and without SCS (Fig. 1c and Extended Data Fig. 2). Notably, we did not implement a formal motor rehabilitation protocol (that is, participants did not follow prescribed routines of exercises). Instead, across 19 experimental sessions, participants engaged in assessments 5 days per week, amounting to 8.6 total hours of ‘motor activity’ (Supplementary Table 2). The lead electrodes were surgically removed at study end. The study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh institutional review board (IRB) (protocol STUDY19090210). Safety

We systematically documented all adverse events throughout the trial (Table 1 and Extended Data Table 2). We observed 14 mild adverse events and no moderate or severe adverse events. All adverse events resolved rapidly without sequelae. Most adverse events (11/14) did not have a definite relationship with the study, except for three adverse events in SCS01, SCS04 and SCS05. In particular, SCS04 experienced shortness of breath when SCS was delivered at high amplitudes (5 mA) using an electrode contact located between C3 and C4 spinal levels. This adverse event was immediately resolved by discontinuing stimulation. We think that this adverse event resulted from undesired stimulation of the phrenic nerve, likely interfering with diaphragm function28. In response, we reported this adverse events to the IRB and the FDA; as a precaution, we also modified our protocol to avoid stimulation of the most rostral cervical spinal levels. Specifically, stimulation of rostral contacts at high amplitudes (above 3 mA) was avoided when SCS was delivered with frequencies above 20 Hz (Extended Data Fig. 2). Notably, these contacts were still used when mapping the selectivity of each electrode with recruitment curves (Methods). Due to the rostro-caudal organization of motor neuron pools29, this modification limited our ability to recruit proximal muscles. Systematic calibration of SCS parameters

After implantation, we conducted a systematic procedure to find stimulation parameters that immediately facilitated (not induced) voluntary movements across multiple joints (Fig. 2a,b and Extended Data Fig. 2a−c). First, we identified contacts that selectively targeted muscles involved in specific movements using recruitment curves obtained with single-pulse stimulation with increasing amplitude (1 Hz, biphasic 200-µs square pulses, 0.1–10 mA) for each of the 16 contacts. Specifically, we identified a few contacts (usually two or three) that preferentially activated groups of shoulder, elbow, wrist and finger muscles. For each selected contact, we adjusted stimulation amplitude and frequency (typically 40 Hz or 60 Hz) by observing immediate changes in movement range of motion, strength and speed when SCS was ON24,25,27. Stimulation delivered with amplitudes that evoked paresthesia often resulted in optimal assistive effects. We then fine-tuned these contacts together, adjusting their stimulation amplitudes to target participant-specific impairments while minimizing interference between their effects on motor function (Extended Data Fig. 2c and Methods). For instance, in participants with pronounced flexor synergies, stimulation of contacts targeting elbow flexors (for example, biceps brachii) could exacerbate abnormal muscle co-activation, thereby hindering elbow extension. In these cases, we would reduce the amplitude of flexors contacts in favor of extensors contacts. These patient-specific factors were taken into account when calibrating SCS parameters using multiple contacts. Notably, participant feedback was crucial during these calibration procedures to ensure that stimulation caused no discomfort and produced immediate assistive effects, which was achieved for all participants. This procedure typically required 1−2 days of testing.

Assistive effects of SCS on strength

We expected an immediate increase in strength with SCS ON, as assessed by the torque produced during isometric maximum voluntary contractions (MVCs). Specifically, MVC was measured during shoulder and elbow flexion/extension using an isokinetic system (Computer Sports Medicine (CSMi), HUMAC NORM; Fig. 2b), and grip force was measured using a handheld dynamometer. On average, MVC increased during shoulder flexion (28%), elbow extension (35%), elbow flexion (34%) and grip (55%). Overall, the mean effect of SCS across joints was estimated as an increase of 32% in MVC strength (Fig. 2c). Notably, despite intersubject variability, all participants improved MVC strength in at least one joint during SCS ON compared to SCS OFF (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). It is worth noting that the absence of improvement in shoulder extension may be attributable to our safety-driven protocol modification after the adverse event observed in SCS04, which limited our ability to target shoulder muscles (Extended Data Fig. 1h,i). Taken together, these results demonstrate that SCS can increase strength regardless of impairment severity (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Impact of stimulation selectivity on strength outcomes

Selective activation of spinal segments was key to enable improvements in strength. For example, elbow extension torque was significantly increased in participant SCS04 when stimulation targeted the triceps using the 8R contact at the C7 spinal level (Fig. 2d–f). By contrast, elbow extension was hindered when stimulation targeted the biceps muscle using the 5R contact, which targeted more rostral (C5/C6) spinal levels (Fig. 2d–f). Pooling data across all participants allowed us to identify the spinal levels targeted by stimulation when participants showed improved strength at a given isolated movement (Fig. 2g,h). Specifically, using intraoperative electrophysiology data, we estimated which spinal levels were targeted by the contacts used to deliver SCS during MVC (Methods). Significant increase in shoulder flexion MVC strength was associated with activation of the C4 segment, and improvement in grip MVC was associated with activation of the T1 segment, consistent with motor pools innervation (Fig. 2g,h). These results support our hypothesis, indicating that the assistive effects of SCS depend on selective activation of sensory fibers that innervate muscle groups involved in the intended movement.

Assistive effects of SCS on arm dexterity

We quantified the assistive effects of SCS on arm dexterity by measuring changes in the quality of arm kinematics during a planar reach-and-pull task when SCS was turned ON (Fig. 3, Extended Data Figs. 4–6 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Specifically, the task was performed using an exoskeleton robot (KINARM; Kinarm) with antigravity arm support30 (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Methods). During the reach phase, participants were instructed to reach targets as straight as possible from a start position, focusing on movement control over speed (Fig. 3b,c). As exemplified in Fig. 3a,b,e,f, SCS resulted in straighter and smoother reaching trajectories (Fig. 3c), indicating increased dexterous reaching movements. Notably, all participants demonstrated improvement in arm reaching kinematics for at least one target with SCS ON (Extended Data Fig. 5a−i). We observed similar improvements for the pull phase (Extended Data Figs. 4a−d and 6a–i). Both reach and pull movements were significantly faster during the SCS ON condition (Supplementary Fig. 4). Notably, the area covered by the contralateral arm movement was reduced during SCS ON (Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating that these improvements are unlikely to be driven by overflow from the less affected arm. Moreover, these assistive effects of SCS on arm kinematics were observed irrespective of whether or not participants showed increased strength in elbow flexion or extension during stimulation (Extended Data Fig. 3), consistent with recent findings31. Assistive effects of SCS on abnormal synergies

To analyze the effects of SCS on muscle synergies, we compared the myoelectric activity—quantified as the root mean square (RMS) of the electromyogram envelope (eEMG)—of elbow flexors and extensors during the reach-and-pull task under SCS ON and OFF conditions. During the reach phase, myoelectric activity of the triceps brachii (TRI) and biceps brachii (BIC) muscles were amplified with SCS ON, whereas activity of the brachioradialis (BR) was suppressed (Fig. 3g,h). We then quantified agonist−antagonist co-contraction using activation ratios (TRI/BR and TB/BIC; Methods). We found that TRI/BR, not TRI/BIC, was significantly increased during SCS ON, indicating a reduction of abnormal agonist−antagonist co-contraction (Fig. 3i). Notably, the increase in the TRI/BR ratio was correlated with improvements in kinematics (Fig. 3j and Supplementary Table 6). During the pull, a similar pattern of EMG activation was also observed, although activity of TRI tended to decrease with SCS ON (compare to participant SCS02 as an outlier to this pattern in Extended Data Fig. 4e,f). In contrast to the reach phase, we found a significant decrease in TRI/BIC, not TRI/BR, which was significantly correlated with improvements in arm kinematics (Extended Data Fig. 4g,h). These changes in muscle agonist−antagonist co-contraction suggest that, besides its excitatory effects, SCS may also engage inhibitory mechanisms33–35. Together, these results suggest that SCS can modulate the activity of muscles acting at a single joint (elbow), engaging both inhibitory36 and excitatory mechanisms, thereby reducing abnormal muscle synergies and improving arm dexterity.

Effects of SCS on motor impairment scales: FMA and ARAT scores

The FMA is a validated clinical scale widely used to quantify impairment severity in individuals with post-stroke hemiparesis37,40. Therefore, we employed it to compare the effects of SCS (Fig. 4a–f and Extended Data Fig. 7) with previous studies11,14,15. During SCS OFF, we observed no significant change in FMA scores between baseline (pre-implant) and week 2, indicating weak therapeutic effects at the mid-study timepoint (Fig. 4c). By contrast, we measured a significant increase in FMA scores when SCS was turned ON (mean +4.57 compared to SCS OFF and +5.57 compared to baseline; Fig. 4c). These outcomes are consistent with the observed assistive effects of SCS on strength and arm dexterity, demonstrating that SCS can enable meaningful and immediate improvements. We then assessed changes in FMA scores at the end of week 4. At this timepoint, FMA scores with SCS OFF were higher than baseline (mean +5.14; Fig. 4c), indicating that participants gained improvements even with SCS OFF (that is, therapeutic effects). The effective improvement (that is, combination of assistive and therapeutic effects) was even greater (+6.57 with SCS ON versus baseline) (Fig. 4c). Surprisingly, effective improvement at week 2 (+5.57) accounted for 85% of the maximum improvement observed at week 4, suggesting that just 2 weeks of SCS intervention were sufficient to produce most of the functional gains (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 6). Consistent with these results, Action Research Arm Test (ARAT) scores at week 4 during SCS ON were significantly improved compared to baseline (+4.83) (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 7). It is worth noting that both FMA and ARAT scores assessed 4 weeks after lead explantation (that is, follow-up assessments) decreased relative to week 4 under SCS ON condition (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7), emphasizing that SCS should be continuously used for maximal efficacy. Taken together, these outcomes indicate that maximal efficacy of SCS on motor function is achieved when SCS is ON.

Effects of SCS on spasticity

Anecdotally, we noticed that participants experienced an immediate reduction in muscle tone when SCS was turned ON (Supplementary Video 8). When asked at the beginning of experimental sessions whether they had noticed any changes since a previous visit, participants often reported that these effects persisted after the experimental sessions (that is, SCS OFF). We then quantified the effects of SCS on spasticity using the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS; Fig. 4g,h)41,42. All participants had hand and arm spasticity at baseline. During SCS ON, five out of six participants showed an immediate reduction in MAS score that exceeded the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) threshold (>0.76) in at least one muscle group43 (Fig. 4g and Extended Data Table 3). Moreover, all participants experienced a clinically meaningful decrease in MAS scores from pre-implant (baseline) to week 4 (end of study) with SCS OFF in at least one muscle group (>0.76; Extended Data Table 3)43. Some participants, such as SCS03 and SCS07, showed significant reductions across multiple muscle groups. Notably, participants often reported that these changes impacted their daily lives. For example, participant SCS07 noted that reduced finger spasticity allowed her fingers to relax during manicure. These results indicate a strong therapeutic effect of SCS in reducing spasticity (Fig. 4h).

Potential determinants of SCS responsiveness

We conducted an exploratory analysis to identify potential determinants of responsiveness to SCS, using total FMA motor scores to quantify changes in motor function14–16. Particularly, the FDA recently approved VNS for motor rehabilitation in people with post-stroke hemiparesis based on reports showing a change of 5 points in the FMA motor scale. Therefore, we adopted a 5-point FMA threshold to distinguish between high-responders and low-responders, consistent with the MCID for the scale44. We categorized participants with ‘high’ (≥5) and ‘low’ (<5) responsiveness to SCS based on their FMA scores at week 4 with SCS ON relative to pre-implant (effective improvement). We then used their baseline FMA motor scores (Fig. 5a) and FMA sensory scores (Fig. 5b) to characterize their initial motor and sensory impairments, respectively. We also examined the association between responsiveness and residual anatomical integrity of the corticospinal tract (CST), quantified using fractional anisotropy (FA) of diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance images (Methods). Consistent with the weak association between baseline motor impairment and responsiveness, we found no clear relationship between CST integrity and effective improvement (Fig. 5c). Finally, no strong associations were found for most of the other participant characteristics, except for stroke type (Fig. 5d,e). Notably, the weak association between motor evoked potential (MEP) status and effective improvement on the FMA scale was also consistent with the above findings related to motor impairment and CST integrity. However, we observed that assistive effects on hand opening and finger individualization only occurred in the three out of seven participants who had residual finger movement or were MEP positive (MEP+) (Fig. 5f). Consistent with Fig. 4b, hand and finger improvements were predominantly observed as assistive effects of SCS (compare to HAND in Fig. 4b; Supplementary Video 1).

Functional motor performance

To assess the effects of SCS on functional motor performance, we administered clinical assessments and simulated daily living tasks. Participants showed significantly higher scores in the ARAT (Fig. 4) and the Box and Block Test (BBT) (Fig. 5g; assessed only on participants with residual hand function, SCS01 and SCS07) during SCS ON. Notably, participant SCS07 showed a negligible change in ARAT scores yet achieved a two-fold improvement in the BBT. Furthermore, we simulated daily living tasks, such as opening a lock, manipulating objects, drawing and three-dimensional reaching, in which participants showed improvements with SCS ON (Fig. 5h,i and Supplementary Video 6). Notably, these tasks captured aspects of functional performance that were not fully assessed by clinical assessments. For example, participant SCS08 demonstrated a notable increase in range of motion during a three-dimensional reaching task despite no improvement in ARAT scores (Fig. 5i). Notably, too, improvements in strength during the MVC task (Fig. 2) reflect the effectiveness of SCS in amplifying supraspinal drive into motor output yet did not translate into changes in function in more complex multi-joint movements. For example, the assistive effects on strength of participant SCS03 were relatively small (Extended Data Fig. 3), whereas this participant showed significantly large functional improvements compared to the others (Fig. 4f,g and Supplementary Video 5). Expanding our observation on the dissociation between the effects of SCS on elbow MVC and arm dexterity tasks, these results indicate that SCS modulates supraspinal neural drive in a task-dependent manner.

Discussion

We report the final results of a pilot clinical trial aiming to assess the safety, feasibility and preliminary efficacy of SCS in motor function and spasticity of seven participants with profound post-stroke motor deficits. Although we observed no serious adverse events, our assessments indicate that SCS should be delivered below the C3−C4 spinal segments to avoid respiratory complications. We showed that SCS produced significant motor improvement without causing discomfort regardless of sex, age or race and minimal physical exercise through a combination of assistive and therapeutic effects. Maximal efficacy was achieved with SCS ON, supporting the rationale for the use of SCS as a fully implantable system to be used ‘as needed’ in daily tasks (that is, not as a catalyst of motor rehabilitation). Finally, we observed that responsiveness to SCS was associated with sensory impairments, such that participants with milder sensory deficits showed greater motor improvements.

Neurostimulation was previously designed to catalyze effects of rehabilitation—that is, stimulation is intended to act in combination with rehabilitation to promote changes in function over time14–16. By contrast, we demonstrated that SCS operates through a different mechanism, immediately alleviating hemiparesis symptoms (that is, producing assistive effects). The combination of assistive and therapeutic effects resulted in significantly larger effect sizes over time. Notably, 85% of the improvement in FMA scores had already been achieved by week 2, when no significant therapeutic effects had been yet detected. Notably, follow-up FMA scores (SCS OFF) were significantly lower than those obtained with SCS ON at week 4, demonstrating the need for continued use of SCS. These findings suggest that SCS can achieve effect sizes similar to other effective neurostimulation approaches15, including FDA-approved VNS14, but with minimal exercise. Specifically, the total amount of active movement performed by our participants during motor assessments (8.6 hours) was, on average, at least three times lower than other trials14,15 and similar to outpatient standard of care (7 hours)13. At the same time, combining SCS with a high-dose motor rehabilitation program could lead to even greater improvements12,45,46. It is also worth noting that the cohort included in this clinical trial would be ineligible in most other interventional studies. For example, an appreciable proportion of the participants in our study were MEP negative (MEP−)15, did not have finger function12,14 and had low baseline FMA sensory scores (<6)14.

Although facilitation of hand and finger movements strongly depended on residual direct monosynaptic CST connections to finger muscles, this condition was not a prerequisite for motor improvements in strength, dexterity, abnormal synergies and spasticity. Indeed, our data suggested that spared sensory function, rather than motor function, was a potential determinant of responsiveness to SCS. Consistent with these results, improvements in arm strength and dexterity did not appear to depend on baseline motor impairments either. For example, although participant SCS02 did not regain finger function, she showed substantial improvements in strength, arm dexterity and spasticity, and participant SCS03 did not improve strength at elbow and grip and yet he improved in arm reaching dexterity and obtained the second best improvements in the FMA and ARAT.

Notably, whereas most therapies have been tested in individuals with ischemic stroke14,15,47, we found that SCS is also effective for those with hemorrhagic stroke. These results demonstrate that SCS is agnostic to the cause of CST damage, suggesting its rehabilitative potential in other neurological disorders48.

Furthermore, we observed consistent improvements in spasticity over time, with all participants showing significant reductions in MAS scores. Previous studies suggested that SCS may reduce spasticity in other neurological conditions49,50, but we were surprised to observe the size of the effects emerging over time. Although SCS is generally thought to excite neural circuits, effect on spasticity may reflect homeostatic adaptations after prolonged use of stimulation27. Notably, these outcomes support the potential of SCS as an alternative to standard (and often costly) spasticity treatments51, such as repeated botulinum toxin injections52, pharmacological treatments53 and implanted intrathecal baclofen pumps54. In comparison, we showed reduced MAS scores by an average of 2.1 points, compared to 1.4 points for intrathecal baclofen55 and 1.5 points for botulinum toxin56 (MAS ‘1+’ scores were converted to 1.5 for this comparison).

In the present study, we also analyzed the impact of stimulation parameters on effect size. We demonstrated that selective stimulation of appropriate spinal segments is critical for achieving meaningful assistive effects, which is an important consideration for testing non-invasive stimulation approaches57,58. For example, selective stimulation of caudal dorsal roots (C8/T1) was critical to facilitate hand function. In this study, we spent 1 week validating optimal stimulation configurations. However, the calibration process itself required only about 4 hours, which we think is reasonably practical for clinical use. Therefore, we envision a streamlined calibration workflow for clinical applications: intraoperative neuromonitoring teams can assist surgeons in optimizing lead placement and identifying selective contacts, and physical therapists can use available clinician programmers to efficiently fine-tune SCS parameters in implantable stimulators by evaluating immediate changes in motor function. Notably, all stimulation configurations used here to deliver SCS can be implemented with clinically available implantable pulse generators.

Limitations

This study is limited by four factors. First, the small sample size restricts the validity of our estimation of the effect size. However, the heterogeneity of our cohort points to the versatility of SCS in a broad stroke population. Second, the absence of a control group performing only motor assessments restricts the interpretation of our results with regard to therapeutic effects emerging over time. However, this limitation does not affect the bulk of our analysis, as most efficacy outcomes focused on the assistive effects of SCS. Even though we did not execute formal control for the therapeutic effects, the results of the VNS trial allow us to speculate on this point. Indeed, in the VNS study, control participants underwent a formal rehabilitation program of 27 hours that led to an average of +2.4 points on the FMA motor scale14. Using this value as reference, we observed a mean improvement of +5.14 FMA with SCS OFF after 4 weeks, despite minimal active movement (8.6 hours). Therefore, our results suggest that the observed therapeutic effects are largely attributable to the SCS intervention. Third, although evaluators were blinded to the stimulation conditions (SCS ON versus SCS OFF), participants were not (that is, SCS-elicited paresthesia). To address this limitation, we tested the effects of different contact configurations on voluntary motor output, as participants could not distinguish which contacts were being stimulated. We found that suboptimal stimulation configurations could even worsen motor output (compare to Fig. 2e), suggesting that motivation or placebo effects likely had minimal influence on outcomes. Finally, due to time constraints imposed by this non-significant risk investigation, we were not able to systematically assess the impact of SCS on functional activities for longer than 29 days. Future clinical studies should address these limitations by performing direct comparisons with controls in larger populations and longer time periods as well as measuring real-world arm use of participants.

In conclusion, our results suggest that SCS can be a feasible neuroprosthetic technology for treating movement dysfunctions and spasticity in post-stroke hemiparesis.

Methods

Study design and cohort

Trial and participant information

All experimental protocols were approved by the University of Pittsburgh IRB (protocol STUDY19090210) under an abbreviated investigational device exemption (IDE). The study protocol is published on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04512690), where it was registered on 13 August 2020. Eight participants were enrolled in the study; however, only seven completed the study. Specifically, participant SCS06 was withdrawn from the trial after being diagnosed with arrhythmia during preoperative assessments. All participants provided informed written consent according to the procedure approved by the University of Pittsburgh IRB, and participants were compensated for each day of the trial and for travel and lodging during the study period. Due to the small sample size in this single-arm study, sex-based analyses were not performed.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Individuals 21−70 years of age who had suffered from an ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke more than 6 months prior to the start of the study were eligible for participation. All individuals had hemiparesis affecting their upper limb (that is, residual arm and hand motor function) with baseline FMA scores for the upper extremity motor function between 7 and 40. Before the study, participants were screened with a rigorous medical evaluation. Candidates with severe comorbidities, previously implanted medical devices, claustrophobia, pregnant or breastfeeding were excluded from the study. Individuals were not receiving any anticoagulant, antispasticity or antiepileptic medications for the duration of the study period.

Study design and data reported

The goal of this pilot clinical trial was to evaluate the safety, feasibility and efficacy of SCS for improving motor function and spasticity in individuals with chronic post-stroke upper limb hemiparesis. The study was designed as a single-group, open-label, prospective study. Following FDA regulations for non-significant risk studies of implanted devices (21 CFR 812.3[d]), the SCS leads were implanted for 29 days under an abbreviated IDE approved by the University of Pittsburgh IRB. After 29 days, the SCS electrodes were explanted. Although we designed this clinical trial to minimize safety risks, our long-term vision is for SCS to be used as a neuroprosthetic device intended for continuous daily use rather than as a temporary intervention.

We designed our primary and secondary outcomes to primarily assess safety and obtain preliminary clinical and scientific evidence of both the assistive and long-term effects of SCS.

After screening and pre-study baselines, participants were implanted with clinical SCS leads. Starting from day 4 after implant, participants underwent scientific sessions five times per week, 4 hours per day, for a total of 19 sessions until explantation day. Tasks and measurements during the first week were focused on identifying optimal stimulation configurations that were then maintained for the remaining sessions. A custom stimulation system was used to deliver SCS exclusively during experimental sessions (more information about the custom system is provided in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Fig. 8). All experimental sessions, including baseline, during the period in which participants were implanted with SCS leads and during post-explantation phases, were conducted at the University of Pittsburgh. The implantation and explantation surgical procedures were performed at University of Pittsburgh Medical Center Presbyterian Hospital. Data were collected from March 2021 to April 2024.

In summary, the primary outcome of this study is safety, which was assessed by systematically reporting serious adverse events. Specifically, we consider the trial to be successful if there are no serious adverse events related to the use of the stimulation. We asked participants to report and rate, if present, any pain or discomfort that arises from the stimulation, with the goal of understanding if intensities required for motor effects are pain and discomfort free. The secondary outcome of this study is the efficacy of SCS for improving motor function. We assessed the efficacy of SCS in terms of (1) assistive effects, defined as the difference between SCS ON and SCS OFF conditions at any timepoint; (2) therapeutic effects, defined as the difference between timepoints for the SCS OFF condition; and (3) effective improvement, defined as the difference between pre-implant baseline and SCS ON condition at any timepoint. Specifically, we quantified the assistive effects of SCS on strength, by measuring MVC forces during isometric movements; dexterity, by analyzing arm kinematics during a planar reach-and-pull task; spasticity, using the MAS across multiple upper limb joints; and abnormal synergies, by comparing EMG signals of elbow antagonist and agonist muscles during a reach-and-pull task and subcomponents of the FMA for upper limb motor function. We also quantified the therapeutic effects emerging over time on spasticity, using the MAS. Moreover, we assessed the effective improvement enabled by SCS using total FMA motor scores, thereby enabling comparison of our effect sizes with those reported in the literature11,14,15. Finally, we also performed a battery of imaging and electrophysiology tests to assess motor and sensory impairments at baseline. Below, we detail the methods for each of the measurements reported in this trial. A detailed description of primary and secondary outcomes can be found in Supplementary Table 1 and on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04512690).

Safety

We systematically documented all adverse events using a log form provided by the University of Pittsburgh IRB and developed by the Education and Compliance Office for Human Subject Research. All adverse events were reported to the data safety and monitoring board (DSMB), as well as to the IRB, to determine whether or not they would be related to the clinical trial. The DSMB, composed of members independent of the study team, was validated by the University of Pittsburgh IRB and the National Institutes of Health, as the study was fully funded through a Brain Initiative UG3/UH3 grant. Throughout this clinical trial, we strictly followed all IRB directives for participant safety. For instance, after a study-related adverse event with participant SCS04, we rigorously adhered to IRB directives by reporting the event to the FDA and revising our stimulation protocol to ensure participant safety. This adverse event was also reported to and discussed with the DSMB members.

Intraoperative workflow/placement of leads

For specific information regarding the surgical procedure, see the Supplementary Methods.

Intraoperative recruitment curves

To confirm placement of each electrode lead and ensure activation of proximal arm muscles to distal hand muscles, we measured recruitment curves during the surgical procedure. Specifically, stimulation was delivered in a monopolar configuration using an intraoperative neuromonitoring system (Xltek Protektor32; Natus Medical). A subdermal return electrode was placed at the deltoid or upper back contralateral to the affected arm of the participant. For each SCS contact, stimulation pulses were delivered at 1−2 Hz with amplitude ramped up from 0 mA to 5 mA. Compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs) were recorded using subdermal needle electrodes bilaterally in both arms. EMG was often recorded from trapezius, anterior deltoid, medial deltoid, posterior deltoid, biceps, triceps, pronator teres, wrist flexors, wrist extensors, abductor pollicis and abductor digiti minimi. We recorded contralateral myoelectric activity to ensure that SCS did not induce crossover effects to the contralateral arm. Intraoperative electrophysiological data were manually extracted from proprietary files using EPWorks version 6.1.2.38 (Natus Medical).

Electrophysiology assessments

All electrophysiological data were collected using a custom MATLAB (MathWorks, R2020a/b) code interfacing with an NI Data Acquisition Board (PCI-6255) and an interprocess communication framework written in MATLAB (Dragonfly59) for synchronously recording stimulus pulse timing, EMG and experimental performance parameters.

MEP status

MEP status was used to assess the integrity of the CST after stroke60,61. To determine the MEP status, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) with a Magstim 2002 and a 70-mm-diameter figure-eight coil was applied at a 3 × 3-cm hotspots grid centered over the hand knob area of the primary motor cortex in the affected hemisphere. Participants were classified as MEP+ if peak-to-peak MEP amplitudes were ≥50 µV in either proximal arm muscles (that is, deltoid, biceps and triceps) and/or distal hand muscles (that is, abductor pollicis brevis, abductor digiti minimi and flexor digiti minimi) contralateral to the stimulated cortex. Participants were classified as MEP− if no MEPs could be evoked.

SSEP status

Somatosensory evoked potential (SSEP) status was used to assess the integrity of ascending sensory pathways60,62. Specifically, upper extremity SSEPs were acquired intraoperatively during lead placement through subdermal needle stimulation of the ulnar nerve in the affected arm and subsequently recording from the contralateral cortical hemisphere using scalp electrodes placed according to the standard international 10–20 system63. The cortical responses were specifically recorded from Fz-P4 and Fz-P3 electrode montages. Stimulation frequency was 3.11 Hz with a single pulse duration of 0.3 ms. Band-pass filters were set at 10–250 Hz with a maximum range window of ±2.5 mV. Participants were classified as SSEP positive (SSEP+) if the peak-to-peak SSEP amplitudes were ≥0.5 µV and as SSEP negative (SSEP−) otherwise.

Hand-opening ability

Hand-opening ability was assessed at baseline, prior to SCS leads implantation, using the FMA for upper extremity motor function (see below). Participants who scored >0 points on the finger mass extension subitem were considered to have hand-opening ability—that is, partial or full voluntary extension of the affected hand.

Imaging

Intraoperative fluoroscopy

Intraoperative anterior−posterior and lateral fluoroscopic images (C-arm) were used to guide accurate percutaneous placement of the SCS leads. These fluoroscopic images were used to estimate the relative position of the electrode leads along the cervical spinal cord and to develop a spinal segment map.

X-ray

Anterior−posterior and lateral X-rays were taken 1−2 weeks after implant to evaluate for lead stability. We used these X-rays to estimate the relative position of the electrode leads along the cervical spine (Extended Data Fig. 1).

Structural magnetic resonance imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was acquired using a 3T Prisma system (Siemens) using a 64-channel head and neck coil. A T1-weighted structural image was captured using a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo sequence (repetition time = 2,300 ms; echo time = 2.9 ms; field of view = 256 × 256 mm2; 192 slices; slice thickness = 1.0 mm; in-plane resolution = 1.0 × 1.0 mm2). Additionally, a T1-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequence was captured (repetition time = 5,000 ms; echo time = 390 ms; field of view = 256 × 256 mm2; 192 slices; slice thickness = 1.0 mm; in-plane resolution = 1.0 × 1.0 mm2) and was ultimately used to depict the lesions in Fig. 1d. Lesion segmentation was performed manually for each slice of the sequence using the MRIcron image viewer (Neuroimaging Tools and Resources Collaboratory (NITRC)); the resulting region of interest (ROI) was smoothed on all planes using a Gaussian smoothing kernel with full width at a half maximum of 2 mm. MRIcroGL (NITRC) was used to visualize and export the resulting segmented overlays.

Diffusion-weighted MRI for tractography

To assess the anatomical integrity of the CST, we estimated the asymmetry of FA between the lesioned and non-lesioned hemispheres using diffusion-weighted MRI. A 3T MRI scanner was configured to use a diffusion spectrum imaging scheme to acquire 257 diffusion samples. The maximum b value was 4,000 s mm−2, and the in-plane resolution and slice thickness were 2 mm. A diffusion sampling length ratio of 1.25 was used. Diffusion data were reconstructed using generalized q-sampling imaging (GQI) with an isotropic resolution of 2 mm. Restricted diffusion was quantified using restricted diffusion imaging64. Tensor-derived metrics were calculated, and a deterministic fiber tracking algorithm was used to reconstruct the CST fibers65. An ROI was placed in the midbrain, and a seed region was defined in Brodmann area 4 (BA4). For fiber tracking, we used an anisotropy threshold of 0.01 and an angular threshold of 65°. The step size was set to voxel spacing. Tracks with lengths shorter than 30 mm or longer than 300 mm were discarded. A total of 10,000 seeds were calculated for both hemispheres. Tracts were manually pruned after the execution of the algorithm. We then calculated the mean FA values for the left and right CST. Lead migration

Lead migration was identified by comparing intraoperative fluoroscopy images with post-implant X-rays as well as through electrophysiological assessments. Because X-ray and fluoroscopy images provide a planar perspective of the anatomical structures, they can produce distorted impressions of lead position relative to the spinal cord, depending on patient positioning and angle of image acquisition. Although these images can be used as an approximate indication of lead location, they cannot be fully relied upon to determine lead migration. In combination with imaging, we used electrophysiological assessments, including recruitment curves (see ‘Calibration of stimulation parameters’ section) and assistive effects of SCS on motor function, to detect lead migration. Specifically, discrepancies in the muscle groups activated by individual contacts together with changes in motor and sensory thresholds were used as indicators of possible lead migration. In this study, lead migration was determined only for participant SCS05 (Extended Data Table 2).

Calibration of stimulation parameters

We implemented an extensive protocol to develop and validate a systematic calibration procedure. Although this process was conducted throughout the first week after SCS implantation, the calibration procedure itself was completed during a single experimental session lasting about 4 hours. During this session, we obtained recruitment curves for each electrode contact by incrementally increasing stimulation amplitude while recording the evoked EMG responses from arm and hand muscles; this step required most of the session time (Extended Data Fig. 2a). The remaining time was dedicated to a systematic calibration procedure designed to identify optimal stimulation configurations (that is, contacts, pulse width, frequency and amplitude) while testing immediate assistive effects on motor performance (that is, SCS ON versus SCS OFF). After this calibration session during the first week, we tested and confirmed the calibrated stimulation parameters by comparing motor performance under SCS ON versus SCS OFF conditions during strength (MVC; see below for more details) and dexterity (reach-and-pull; see below for more details) tasks. For most participants, the stimulation parameters determined during the calibration session were used throughout the 4-week intervention, with only minor adjustments rarely required afterwards.

Recruitment curves

To evaluate the selectivity of SCS in recruiting individual muscles, we estimated recruitment curves by measuring the peak-to-peak amplitude of CMAPs as a function of stimulation amplitude. We used a wireless EMG system (Trigno; Delsys) to record the CMAPs from multiple upper limb muscles, such as trapezius, anterior deltoids, biceps brachii, triceps brachii, brachioradialis, abductor pollicis brevis and adductor digiti minimi. Stimulation was delivered to one contact at a time at 1−2 Hz, with amplitudes gradually increasing in steps from 0.2 mA up to 8 mA, or until the participant reported discomfort. Monopolar stimulation was applied using a return surface electrode placed over the iliac crest contralateral to the affected arm. For each contact on both leads, we measured the peak-to-peak amplitude of the CMAPs elicited at each stimulation amplitude, thereby obtaining recruitment curves that described a relationship between SCS amplitude and muscle recruitment (for example, Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 2).

Systematic procedure for SCS calibration

Following a systematic procedure, we calibrated the stimulation parameters of a set of contacts to facilitate voluntary movements across multiple upper limb joints. First, we used muscle recruitment curves to select contacts that selectively activated different muscle groups (for example, elbow flexors versus extensors). Specifically, we identified a few contacts (usually two or three, as shown in Extended Data Fig. 1) that preferentially activated muscles of the shoulder (anterior, medial and posterior heads of deltoid), the elbow (biceps brachii, triceps brachii and pronator teres), the wrist (extensor carpi radialis and flexor carpi radialis) or the fingers (abductor pollicis brevis and flexor digiti minimi brevis). For each selected contact individually, we tuned the stimulation amplitude and frequency while participants performed movements involving the joint(s) targeted by stimulation (for example, elbow flexion versus extension). For example, when testing caudal contacts targeting the C8/T1 spinal levels, we assessed hand function and grip strength. Stimulation amplitude was increased in a range between 0.2 mA and 8 mA and frequency between 40 Hz and 100 Hz. Pulse width was typically set to 200 µs. Starting from 40 Hz, frequency was increased only when increasing stimulation amplitude failed to produce satisfactory outcomes. After individually calibrating a set of contacts (usually two or three), we combined them with a personalized configuration for each participant. Stimulation amplitudes were readjusted when combining multiple contacts to minimize interference between their effects. Less frequently, we also adjusted the stimulation frequency across contacts. At the end of this calibration procedure, SCS was delivered below motor threshold but above sensory threshold (that is, not producing voluntary movements). Using these configurations, participants would feel paresthesia along the paretic limb during stimulation.

MVCs during isolated movements

MVC force produced during isometric movements of the shoulder and elbow joints was assessed using a robotic torque dynamometer (HUMAC NORM). During testing, the robot’s manipulandum was locked at a predetermined angle, and participants were instructed to exert their maximal voluntary force in either flexion or extension during 5 seconds. The joint under evaluation—shoulder or elbow—was aligned with the rotation axis of the manipulandum. To ensure isolation of single-joint motion, participants were stabilized using shoulder harnesses and joint-specific straps and braces according to HUMAC NORM’s recommended configurations. For example, during elbow strength testing, the upper arm and elbow were firmly supported against the chair back, and the forearm was held at a 90° angle to prevent movement. Moreover, the hand was often strapped to the manipulandum to reduce the contribution of grip during elbow and shoulder movements. Each movement was assessed over 5−6 repetitions, with a 10–15-second rest between repetitions. A 2-minute break was often provided after the third repetition. The maximum torque value from each repetition was considered for analysis. We used the HUMAC2015 version 15.000.0276 (HUMAC NORM) software environment for collecting joint torque data.

Grip strength was similarly quantified using a hydraulic hand dynamometer (Fabrication Enterprises, no. 12-0021). Participants were instructed to hold the device in a comfortable position and exert maximum grasping force for 5 seconds. The highest value from six repetitions was recorded and used for statistical analysis. Assistive effects of SCS on strength were assessed by comparing MVC force under SCS ON and OFF conditions within the same experimental session.

MVC testing was also performed on the unimpaired limb using the same procedures and joint configurations to evaluate for the degree of strength impairment across participants.

Selectivity index

We assessed the association between the motor neuron pool targeted by SCS during the MVC task and the corresponding improvements in upper limb strength. First, we mapped the spinal levels targeted by the SCS parameters used during the MVC task described above. We then estimated a selectivity index for each spinal level, quantifying the degree to which activation of that level was associated with increased MVC strength across each isolated movement.

Spinal segment maps

For each participant, we mapped the location of upper limb motor neuron pools across the cervical spinal levels, producing individual spinal segment maps. We used intraoperative fluoroscopic X-rays to identify the contacts positioned closest to the dorsal root fibers at each spinal level from C4 to T1. For each of these contacts, we analyzed intraoperative recruitment curves (see Methods for recruitment curves above) to identify the muscles activated at motor threshold—defined as the lowest stimulation amplitude delivered by each contact that evoked CMAPs with peak-to-peak amplitudes ≥50 µV. In some cases, multiple muscles were simultaneously activated at motor threshold amplitude. This process was repeated across all contacts. We then mapped the most selectively activated muscles (that is, those activated at motor threshold) across spinal segments (Extended Data Fig. 1a−g). Finally, we generated cohort-level spinal maps by overlapping the activated spinal segments for each muscle across all participants (Fig. 2g).

Selectivity index

A spinal selectivity index was developed to identify the spinal levels that contributed to increases in MVC force across all participants (Fig. 2g,h). We used X-ray images acquired at week 2 after implant to obtain an accurate estimation of the relative position of the leads along the spinal cord during the MVC task. We then identified the spinal levels with dorsal root fibers in closest proximity to the contacts used during the MVC task (that is, spinal levels most targeted by the contacts used). For each contact, we computed a selectivity index, defined as the ratio between the stimulation amplitude used during the MVC task and the motor threshold amplitude estimated for the targeted spinal levels (see Methods for spinal segment maps above). The selectivity index was computed for the stimulation configurations used during the MVC task of each isolated movement (shoulder flexion, shoulder extension, elbow flexion, elbow extension and grip). For each spinal level and movement, selectivity indices were averaged across all participants who showed a significant increase in MVC force (Extended Data Fig. 3). Finally, the selectivity indexes for each isolated movement were normalized by the sum across spinal levels to generate the heatmap in Fig. 2h.

Arm kinematics during reach-and-pull task

To assess the assistive effects of SCS on dexterity, we quantified arm kinematics during a planar reach-and-pull task. Specifically, we used a robotic augmented reality exoskeleton system (KINARM; Kinarm). Participants were secured in a modified wheelchair, and their arms were suspended in the exoskeleton to remove the effects of gravity66. The platform displayed virtual targets onto a dichroic augmented reality display in front of the individual that allowed them to visualize their hand position relative to the virtual graphics.

The assessment task consisted of two phases: reach and pull. At the beginning of each trial, the participant placed their hand on the starting position. A target was then presented, followed by an audio cue after a randomized 100–700-ms delay, indicating the start of the reach phase. During the reach phase, participants were instructed to reach as straight as possible toward the target, focusing on movement control over speed. A target was considered successfully reached if the participant held the hand position within a 0.5-cm radius of the target center for 500 ms. Upon successful reach, an audio cue indicated the end of the reach phase. If the participant was unable to reach the target, the assessment continued with the next trial. An audio cue was played indicating the start of the pull phase. During the pull phase, participants were instructed to return their hand to the starting position without any specific instructions about the movement performance. Each target was presented 5−10 times in random order. For each participant, appropriate targets were selected based on their individual range of motion. Dexterit-E 3.8 and 3.9 (Kinarm) were used for collecting arm kinematics.

During reach and pull phases, hand trajectories and velocity were analyzed using metrics that characterize movement straightness (path efficiency and deviation error) and smoothness (log dimensionless jerk and velocity peaks)67, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3). Self-reported assessments

At the beginning of experimental sessions, we systematically documented whether participants noticed any physical or mental changes since their previous visit using an IRB-approved form. Participants were encouraged to report any potential adverse events, such as pain, as well as any other changes that they observed, even if they thought these might not be related to the study.

Clinical assessments

FMA for upper limb motor function subcomponents

The FMA is a reliable and validated clinical scale widely used to quantify impairment severity in participants with post-stroke hemiparesis and their recovery after therapeutic interventions37,40. We, therefore, employed the FMA to enable comparison of our effect sizes with those reported in previous studies11,14,15. The FMA for upper extremity motor function consists of 33 items, each scored on a scale from 0 to 2, where higher scores indicate better motor execution (maximum score is 66). A trained occupational therapist (A.B.) conducted all assessments. Video recordings of the assessment were taken and reviewed by the same therapist to confirm results and ensure consistency across timepoints and stimulation conditions. If discrepancies were identified, the scores were updated accordingly. Discrepancies in scores were uncommon. The FMA was administered at baseline (pre-implant), week 2 post-implant, week 4 post-implant and follow-up (1 month post-explant) timepoints. At weeks 2 and 4, the FMA was conducted under SCS ON and OFF conditions within the same experimental session. The order of stimulation conditions was pseudorandomized, with a 5–10-minute rest break provided between them. The evaluator was blinded to the stimulation condition. An MCID of 5 points was used to interpret meaningful change14,44. Assistive effects of SCS were assessed by comparing total FMA motor scores between SCS ON and OFF conditions within the same session (weeks 2 and 4). Therapeutic effects were assessed by comparing FMA scores under SCS OFF across timepoints (baseline, week 2, week 4 and follow-up). Effective improvements were assessed as the difference in FMA scores between SCS OFF at pre-implant and SCS ON at weeks 2 and 4.

FMA motor subcomponents

For each participant, we also analyzed the scores of six subcomponents of the FMA motor across stimulation conditions and timepoints: flexor synergy (FS), extensor synergy (ES), movement combining synergy (MCS), movement out of synergy (MOS), wrist (WRIST) and hand (HAND). For visualization in web plots (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 7), the subscores for each participant were normalized to the maximum score of the corresponding subcomponent.

As a complementary analysis to assess the effects of SCS on the FMA motor subcomponents in the pooled cohort, we calculated the difference between the subscores across stimulation conditions and timepoints (Fig. 4b). Specifically, assistive effects of SCS were assessed by comparing FMA subscores between SCS ON and OFF at week 2, when assistive effects on total FMA motor scores were on average maximal and confounding influence from therapeutic effects was minimal. Therapeutic effects were assessed by comparing FMA subscores under SCS OFF between baseline and week 4, when therapeutic effects on total FMA motor scores were maximal. Effective improvements were assessed by comparing FMA subscores between SCS OFF at baseline and SCS ON at week 4, when effective improvement was maximal. Changes in subscores for each participant were normalized by the maximum score of each subcomponent. For example, an increase of 3 points in ES was normalized by 6, the maximum score for ES, yielding a normalized value of 0.5. These normalized subscores were then summed across all participants for analysis.

To assess the effects of SCS on abnormal synergies across joints, we focused on the MCS and MOS subcomponents of the FMA37–39. Specifically, MCS tasks involve joint movements that can still be performed within abnormal synergy patterns, often by combining components of the flexor or extensor synergies—for example, flexing the shoulder from 0° to 90° with the elbow extended. Because this movement is performed within a limited shoulder range of motion, it can still be executed despite the presence of flexor synergy. By contrast, MOS tasks require movements that cannot be accomplished within abnormal synergy patterns and, therefore, demand more isolated joint control—for example, flexing the shoulder from 90° to 180° with the elbow extended and the forearm pronated. Unlike the MCS example, this task requires full shoulder flexion across the extreme range of motion while suppressing both the elbow flexion and forearm supination inherent to abnormal flexor synergy. Therefore, MCS grades movements that can be executed despite abnormal synergies, whereas MOS requires overcoming these synergies to achieve more independent joint control. Together, MCS and MOS provide a graded measure of the ability to individualize joint movements beyond pathological coupling patterns observed after stroke.

FMA for upper limb sensory function

We used the sensory component of the FMA to quantify proprioception and tactile functions in the upper extremity. The sensory component of the FMA is assessed using the same score scale as the motor FMA components, with four items for proprioception and two items for tactile functions (maximum score of 12 points). A trained occupational therapist (A.B.) conducted all assessments after administering the FMA for motor function. That is, sensory FMA was administered at the same timepoints and under the same conditions as the motor FMA. Participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed throughout the evaluation. For proprioception testing, the therapist moved the participant’s affected arm/hand and asked them to mirror the movement with the non-affected arm/hand. For tactile testing, the therapist lightly touched the participant’s affected arm/hand using a piece of cotton and asked them to verbally identify where touch was felt, if touch was perceived. The FMA sensory scores used in this study were assessed from all participants before the implantation of the SCS lead electrodes (that is, baseline).

ARAT

The ARAT is an assessment of upper limb motor function that focuses on object interaction and manipulation. It consists of 19 task items scored from 0 to 3 (maximum score is 57), where 0 indicates that the participant is unable to perform the task and 3 indicates that the participant can perform it normally. These 19 task items are distributed across four categories: grasp, pinch, grip and gross movement.

The ARAT was administered at baseline (pre-implant), week 4 post-implant and follow-up (1 month post-explant) timepoints by a trained occupational therapist (A.B.). At week 4, the ARAT was conducted under both SCS ON and OFF conditions in pseudorandom order within the same experimental session. The therapist was blinded to the stimulation conditions, and a rest break (5–10 minutes) was given between conditions.

BBT

The BBT is a standardized assessment of arm and hand coordination. Participants are seated in front of a rectangular box divided into two square compartments by a wooden partition. One compartment contains a set of small wooden blocks (2.5 cm per side), whereas the other is initially empty and positioned ipsilateral to the affected limb. Participants are instructed to move as many blocks as possible, one at a time, from the full compartment to the empty one by crossing the wooden partition from above within 1 minute. The score corresponds to the total number of blocks successfully transferred, with each block counting as one point. Blocks that are dropped and bounced out of the target compartment are also counted as points. The BBT was conducted under both SCS ON and OFF conditions for participants SCS01 and SCS07 (Fig. 5g).

MAS

We used the MAS to assess the effects of SCS on upper limb spasticity. The MAS is a validated clinical tool with good test−retest reliability42 and is used to rate resistance to passive movement on a scale from 0 (no increased muscle tone) to 5 (marked rigidity). Of note, we converted the original 1+ score to 2 and rescaled the [2, 4] score range to [3, 5] for comparisons between our results and MCID values43. To minimize confounding effects of SCS and physical activity, the MAS was assessed by a trained occupational therapist (A.B.) at the beginning of experimental sessions. For each participant, we limited MAS assessments to joints with pronounced spasticity to reduce daily testing time. Overall, the MAS was assessed for shoulder external and internal rotators; shoulder abductors; shoulder flexors; elbow flexors and extensors; wrist flexors, supinators and pronators; and finger flexors.

Assistive effects of SCS on spasticity were assessed by comparing MAS scores between SCS ON and OFF conditions within the same experimental session. Both conditions were conducted at the beginning of the session. The stimulation conditions were pseudorandomized and blinded to the evaluator. These assistive effects were assessed at week 2 for participants SCS02 and SCS07; at week 3 for participants SCS04 and SCS08; and at week 4 for participants SCS01 and SCS05; and they were not assessed for participant SCS03. Therapeutic effects emerging over time on spasticity were quantified by comparing the average of the MAS scores at week 4 to baseline. An MCID of 0.76 was used to interpret meaningful change43.

Simulated daily living tasks

To assess changes in motor function, we simulated daily living tasks, such as opening a lock, manipulating objects, drawing and three-dimensional reaching, during SCS ON and OFF conditions. These tasks were performed according to participant preference (that is, they were not consistently conducted for all participants).

Surface EMG activity during reach-and-pull task

EMG recordings

To assess muscle activity during movement, surface EMG was recorded using a wireless EMG system (Trigno; Delsys). Up to 12 synchronized wireless sensors (Avanti Trigno; Delsys) were used to amplify, digitize and wirelessly transmit EMG signals to a base station unit. Each sensor sampled the analog signal at 1,925.925 Hz and applied a hardware band-pass filter of 20–800 Hz. Once the signals were received by the base station, they were converted back to an analog waveform and resampled at 8,000 Hz by a data acquisition system (National Instruments, PCI-6255) for synchronization with other task events. The Trigno system has a known, fixed wireless latency of 59.6 ms, which was considered for processing the EMG signals.

At the beginning of each experimental session, the arms and hands were cleaned using mildly abrasive skin preparation gel and isopropyl alcohol. Skin-safe adhesive was used to secure the EMG sensors to the participant’s arm. Depending on the muscles of interest for a particular experiment, we recorded from up to 12 individual muscles, such as trapezius, anterior deltoid, biceps brachii, triceps brachii and abductor digiti minimi. Sensors were then carefully removed at the end of each session.

Myoelectric activity during planar reach task

To analyze the effect of SCS on muscle activity patterns during the planar reach-and-pull task, we used EMG signals acquired from triceps brachii, biceps brachii and brachioradialis68,69. All EMG signals were band-pass filtered between 25 Hz and 500 Hz using a zero-lag, 5th-order Butterworth digital filter. We first segmented the EMG signals into reach and pull phases. We then calculated the RMS of the eEMG across SCS ON and OFF conditions for each muscle and movement phase (reach and pull).

Agonist–antagonist muscle activation ratio

We quantified the co-contraction of the triceps brachii (TRI), biceps brachii (BIC) and brachioradialis (BR) muscles during the reach-and-pull task using agonist−antagonist activation ratios: TRI/BR and TB/BIC. For each participant, reach target (or pull start) and stimulation condition (SCS ON and SCS OFF), we averaged the myoelectric activity of each muscle—measured as the RMS of the eEMG—and the corresponding agonist–antagonist activation ratios. Time on active movement

Although a formal rehabilitation protocol was not implemented, participants engaged in active movements during task assessments across study. We estimated the time spent on active movement under SCS ON and OFF conditions using video recordings from the experimental sessions and the length of data files. Task assessments included strength tasks using the HUMAC isokinetic system, reaching tasks using the KINARM exoskeleton robot and other functional tasks. Break intervals were not included in the time calculations.

Statistics

Bootstrapping

For independent comparisons at participant level, we performed a bootstrap analysis in which each condition was resampled separately, and the distribution of the difference in means was used to evaluate condition-level effects. For example, SCS ON versus SCS OFF conditions were compared for shoulder torque in participant SCS03 (Fig. 2b). For paired comparisons at the group level, we performed a bootstrap analysis by resampling the paired differences across participants and testing whether the distribution of these differences was significantly different from zero—for example, FMA scores compared between SCS ON and SCS OFF conditions across n = 7 participants in Fig. 4c. In both analyses, 10,000 bootstrap samples were used to construct empirical confidence intervals for quantities of interest. A 95% confidence interval was obtained by identifying the 2.5th and 97.5th quantiles for the resulting values. The null hypothesis of no difference in the mean was rejected if zero was not included in the 95% confidence interval. Bootstrap statistical analysis was performed only when at least five data points were obtained. All bootstrap analyses were performed using a custom code written in MATLAB R2022b (MathWorks).

For the total MVC change across joints, we performed the bootstrap analysis by resampling at the participant level, computing each within-joint mean, and then computing the average across joints. Resampling all values for each participant preserves the correlation across joints within a participant .

Linear correlations

Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine the linear relationship between the percentage change in agonist−antagonist muscle activation ratios (TRI/BIC and TRI/BR) and the corresponding percentage changes in arm kinematic metrics (path efficiency, deviation error, velocity peaks and log dimensionless jerk) enabled by SCS. Average values for the change in agonist−antagonist muscle activation ratios and arm kinematics were pooled across all participants and reach/pull targets. To reduce bias of extreme values when calculating the percentage increase in TRI/BR and TRI/BIC ratios, we excluded data points where the percentage increase of TRI, BR or BB across SCS ON and SCS OFF conditions exceeded 1.5 times the interquartile range (IQR)—that is, cases where values of TRI, BR or BIC in Fig. 3h and Extended Data Fig. 4f were above the upper whisker limit of box plots. All correlation coefficients and P values were derived using SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM, SPSS). Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.
