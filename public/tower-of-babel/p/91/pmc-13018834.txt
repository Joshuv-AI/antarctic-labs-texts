Metabolic profiles associated with fat loss in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Atsuhiro Higashi, Yasuaki Mizutani, Reiko Ohdake, Yasuhiro Maeda, Junichiro Yoshimoto, Sayuri Shima, Yusuke Seino, Akihiro Ueda, Mizuki Ito, Atsushi Suzuki, Hirohisa Watanabe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWeight loss is a substantial non-motor feature of Parkinson’s disease (PD) associated with worse clinical outcomes, but the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Thus, we investigated the mechanisms of PD-related weight loss by examining the correlation between body composition and various plasma metabolites.MethodsWe enrolled 91 patients with PD and 47 healthy controls between July 2021 and October 2023. Body composition was evaluated using bioelectrical impedance analysis. Plasma metabolite profiling was conducted via mass spectrometry, including short-chain and medium-chain fatty acids, Krebs cycle intermediates, ketone bodies and phospholipids. Subsequently, alterations in body composition in PD and their association with plasma metabolites were assessed.ResultsPatients with PD had lower body weight (p=0.003), body mass index (BMI; p=0.001) and body fat mass (p<0.001) compared with controls. Metabolomic analyses revealed that, in patients with PD, glycolysis and Krebs cycle markers (lactic acid and succinic acid) were reduced, while ketone bodies (acetoacetic acid and 3-hydroxybutyric acid), amino acid catabolism-related markers (2-hydroxybutyric acid and 2-oxobutyric acid) and acetic acid were elevated. Notably, in patients with PD, acetoacetic acid and 3-hydroxybutyric acid negatively correlated with BMI. Phosphatidylcholine (40:2) was also elevated in PD and showed higher levels in individuals at more advanced Hoehn and Yahr stages.ConclusionsPD-related fat loss was accompanied by a pattern of lower glycolytic activity and higher levels of lipid and amino acid metabolism-related metabolites, consistent with a potential shift in energy utilisation. These findings highlight metabolic pathways as potential targets for interventions to mitigate weight loss in PD.Keywords: MOVEMENT DISORDERS, PARKINSON'S DISEASE

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is frequently accompanied by weight loss, predominantly due to a reduction in fat mass rather than muscle mass. Altered lipid metabolism has been implicated in PD pathogenesis, supported by genetic studies, lysosomal dysfunction and changes in short-chain fatty acids. PD is characterised by mitochondrial impairment and glycolytic deficits, which contribute to reduced adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production. Metabolic alterations in lipid metabolism, in addition to the well-established mitochondrial dysfunction, have been increasingly implicated in the pathophysiology of PD.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study confirms previous observations that fat mass is preferentially reduced in patients with PD and further characterises the associated metabolic alterations through comprehensive plasma metabolomic profiling. Patients with PD exhibited reduced glycolysis and Krebs cycle activity, accompanied by elevated ketone bodies, amino acid catabolites and acetic acid. The significant negative correlation between ketone body levels and body mass index (BMI), along with their marked elevation in patients with lower BMI, supports a compensatory metabolic shift towards lipid utilisation. The findings highlight a specific bioenergetic signature underlying fat loss in PD.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

By elucidating the specific metabolic alterations linked to fat mass reduction in PD, this study provides potential biomarkers for detecting early metabolic imbalance. These insights may guide future research into the mechanisms of energy dysregulation and its role in PD progression. Clinically, assessing metabolic markers could help identify patients at higher risk of nutritional deterioration. The findings underscore the need to consider broader metabolic pathways when developing dietary and therapeutic strategies for PD.

Introduction

Weight loss in Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a prominent non-motor symptom linked to disease progression and reduced life expectancy.1 The loss is believed to stem from reduced energy intake, increased energy expenditure, disrupted homeostasis and treatment side effects.1 However, the precise mechanisms and effective treatments remain unclear.

PD-related weight loss primarily reflects reductions in fat mass rather than muscle mass,2 3 implicating alterations in lipid metabolism. Genome-wide association studies have linked lipid-related genes to PD pathogenesis,4 while mutations in lysosomal genes (GBA, SMPD1, ASAH1 and CTSD) disrupt lipid metabolism and α-synuclein clearance.5 Meanwhile, statin use can reduce the risk of PD,6 and cholesterol is associated with the body mass index (BMI), potentially affecting cognition.7 The observed alterations in short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in PD plasma and faeces8 9 further support a central role for lipid dysregulation in PD pathology and fat mass loss.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is another hallmark of PD, extending beyond neurons to peripheral tissues.10 11 These deficiencies, coupled with glycolytic pathway abnormalities,10 limit adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production. Under low glucose, cells shift to fatty acid β-oxidation, releasing fatty acids from lipid droplets via lipolysis and autophagy and metabolising them in mitochondria.12

Metabolic shifts from carbohydrates to lipids or ketone bodies occur in Alzheimer’s disease13 and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.14 Although less explored in PD, available evidence links impaired energy production to PD pathology.11 A similar shift may compensate for mitochondrial dysfunction, yet exacerbate fat loss.

In this study, we first examined the body composition in PD. We then measured plasma phospholipids, SCFAs, medium-chain fatty acids (MCFAs) and Krebs cycle-associated metabolites by mass spectrometry, alongside non-esterified fatty acids (NEFAs) as the indicators of energy utilisation.15 The obtained data may clarify the metabolic disturbances underlying PD-related weight loss.

Methods

Participants

We enrolled 91 consecutive patients with PD and 47 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HCs) at Fujita Health University Hospital during the period from July 2021 to October 2023. The patients with PD were diagnosed based on the criteria of the Movement Disorder Society.16 The HCs were selected based on cognitive normality (Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score) >25 and/or Japanese version of the Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination-Revised (ACE-R) score >89)17 18 without neurological or psychiatric disorders and no abnormal brain MRI findings.

Clinical evaluation

The patients with PD were assessed during the ‘on’ condition. Motor and non-motor symptoms were evaluated using the Japanese version of the Movement Disorder Society’s Unified PD Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS).16 Motor severity was determined using the Hoehn and Yahr (HY) scale and the Motor Examination part of the MDS-UPDRS (MDS-UPDRS Part III).16 Cognitive performance was assessed with the Frontal Assessment Battery, Japanese version of the ACE-R, MMSE and Japanese version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA-J). Details of additional assessments are provided in online supplemental document 1. The levodopa equivalent daily dose was calculated, considering opicapone and safinamide intake.19 Nutritional status was assessed using the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA). Based on the MNA score, participants were classified as normal (24–30 points), at risk of malnutrition (17–23.5 points) or malnourished (<17 points).20 The time difference between clinical assessment and specimen collection was shown in online supplemental document 1.

Body composition analysis

Body composition was assessed using an InBody 770 multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) device (InBody Japan, Tokyo, Japan) as described previously.21 The following body composition components were evaluated: body water content (L), protein content (kg), mineral content (kg), muscle mass (kg) and body fat mass (kg). Height (m) and body weight (kg) were measured at the same time, and BMI was calculated as weight/height squared (kg/m2). To ensure consistency with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), previously used for body composition analysis in PD,2 16 patients with PD underwent both BIA and DXA (Prodigy; GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) between September 2023 and March 2024. The mean (SD) interval between methods was 5.75 (8.69) days (range, 0–34). BIA and DXA showed close alignment for body fat mass (p<0.001, rs=0.964) and muscle mass (p<0.001, rs=0.679) (online supplemental figure 1).

In addition, the diagnosis of sarcopenia was retrospectively made based on the revised criteria of the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (2019),22 using handgrip strength and skeletal muscle mass index measured by BIA, both assessed on the same day in patients with PD. In the HC group, however, handgrip strength data were not available, and thus, retrospective assessment of sarcopenia could not be performed.

Blood sample preparation

To obtain plasma, blood samples were collected by venipuncture using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid blood collection tubes after a fasting period of >6 hours. After collection, the samples were centrifuged at 1500×g for 10 min, and 500 µL aliquots of the plasma supernatants were transferred into polypropylene tubes, promptly frozen and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. The storage period from sample collection to analysis was summarised in online supplemental document 1.

Measurement of plasma SCFAs, MCFAs and other organic acids

The concentrations of SCFAs, MCFAs and Krebs cycle-associated organic acids in plasma were determined by ultra high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS). The method used for these measurements is described in the online supplemental document 2. Specifically, the following 17 organic acids were measured: two SCFAs (acetic acid and isobutyric acid); three MCFAs (caprylic acid, capric acid and lauric acid) and 12 Krebs cycle-associated metabolites (lactic acid, pyruvic acid, acetoacetic acid, 3-hydroxybutyric acid, 2-hydroxybutyric acid, 2-oxobutyric acid, citric acid, isocitric acid, 2-ketoglutaric acid, succinic acid, fumaric acid and malic acid). We also assessed the lactic acid/pyruvic acid ratio.

Measurement of plasma NEFAs

To assess the body lipolysis rate from adipose tissue,23 we measured the plasma NEFA levels using a Lab Assay NEFA Kit (Wako Chemical, Osaka, Japan).

Measurement of plasma phospholipids

Phospholipid concentrations were determined by UHPLC-MS/MS. The method used for these measurements is described in online supplemental document 3.

Statistical analysis

We carried out the statistical analyses using JMP software V.16 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05 for all tests. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the sex distribution between the two groups. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. For continuous variables that did not meet the assumptions of normality, comparisons between two groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Spearman’s rank correlation test was used to assess the correlations between continuous values, including the body composition components and the plasma metabolite levels.

To further explore the metabolic alterations associated with PD-related weight loss, we stratified patients with PD into two subgroups according to the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition criteria: a low BMI group (BMI<18.5 if aged <70 years or <20 if ≥70 years) and a normal BMI group.24 We then compared plasma metabolite concentrations across three groups: HC, normal BMI PD and low BMI PD, using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Steel–Dwass post hoc comparisons.

To correct for multiple comparisons, we used a false discovery rate (FDR) correction, with the cut-off value for FDR set at 0.05. Phospholipids were evaluated by creating volcano plots using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (www.metaboanalyst.ca),25 where unadjusted p<0.05 and fold change ≥1.5 were used as thresholds. FDR-adjusted p values (q<0.05) were also examined to confirm statistical significance.

Results

Participant characteristics

As shown in table 1, age and sex distribution did not differ significantly between groups. The PD group had lower ACE-R, MoCA-J and MMSE scores (all p<0.001) and higher scores on the Geriatric Depression Scale-15 (p<0.001) compared with HCs. The HbA1c levels were within normal range, and total cholesterol levels were significantly lower in PD. Additionally, diabetes and dyslipidaemia were less common in PD, but medication use did not differ.

Associations of plasma metabolites with body fat mass and BMI in the PD and HC groups

Table 3 summarises the correlations between the plasma metabolites and body fat mass or BMI in the PD group. 3-Hydroxybutyric acid and acetoacetic acid were negatively correlated with body fat mass (3-hydroxybutyric acid: rs = −0.230, p=0.028 and acetoacetic acid: rs = −0.303, p=0.004) (figure 2A,B), but neither of these correlations remained significant after FDR correction. Both metabolites were also negatively correlated with BMI (3-hydroxybutyric acid: rs = −0.352, p=0.001 and acetoacetic acid: rs = −0.372, p<0.001) (figure 2C,D), and these correlations remained significant after FDR correction (q=0.006 and q=0.005, respectively). Those patterns were not observed in the HC group (online supplemental table 2). In contrast, in the HC group, 2-hydroxybutyric acid was positively correlated with body fat mass (rs=0.296 and p=0.044) and lactic acid, pyruvic acid and isocitric acid were positively correlated with BMI (all p<0.05), but none of these correlations remained significant after FDR correction.

Metabolite profiles in PD subgroups by BMI status

The stratified analysis revealed several significant differences in plasma metabolites across the HC, low BMI PD and normal BMI PD groups (online supplemental table 3). Notably, 3-hydroxybutyric acid and acetoacetic acid were markedly elevated in the low BMI PD subgroup compared with both HC and normal BMI PD (both p<0.01, Steel–Dwass) (figure 3A,B). In contrast, the glycolysis-related marker—the lactate/pyruvate ratio—was significantly lower in both the normal BMI PD and low BMI PD groups compared with the HC group (figure 3C). Furthermore, 2-oxobutyric acid, a byproduct of amino acid catabolism, was significantly higher in both PD groups than in the HC group (figure 3D). These findings suggest that patients with PD with lower body mass exhibit a more pronounced metabolic shift towards lipid utilisation.

Volcano plots (figure 5) revealed a significant increase in phosphatidylcholine (PC)(40:2) in the PD group only (fold change, 1.647, log2=0.720). Associations of plasma phospholipids with body fat mass and BMI in the PD and HC groups

The correlations of phospholipids with body fat mass and BMI are shown in online supplemental table 6. Except for lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), none of the phospholipid classes were significantly associated with body fat or BMI after FDR correction in either group. However, in the HC group, several LPC species (and total LPC) were positively correlated with body fat and BMI even after FDR correction. These relationships were not observed in the PD group.

Body composition and metabolic alterations according to disease severity in PD

We further examined PD-related body composition and metabolic alterations stratified by HY stage. In our PD cohort, the distribution of HY stages was HY1 (n=8), HY2 (n=31), HY3 (n=36), HY4 (n=14) and HY5 (n=2). Advanced-stage patients (HY4–5; n=16) showed lower BMI and body fat mass, and higher ketone bodies, 2-oxobutyric acid and PC(40:2) (online supplemental table 7). These differences were largely preserved after FDR correction, although ketone bodies lost significance in Wilcoxon tests and succinic acid in the analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). When the HY stage was treated as an ordinal variable, the Jonckheere–Terpstra test confirmed that all alterations identified in the present PD analyses changed significantly with increasing disease severity (online supplemental table 8). In HY1 patients, direct comparison with HCs showed significance only for PC(40:2). For the other parameters, effect size analysis indicated moderate changes in BMI, body fat mass and lactic acid, although CIs overlapped zero (online supplemental table 9).

Nutritional status adjustment on PD-associated alterations

MNA scores were significantly lower in the PD group (p<0.001) (online supplemental figure 3). The distribution of nutritional status categories also differed significantly between groups (χ² test, p<0.001) (HC: normal=40, at risk=6 and malnourished=1; and PD: normal=42, at risk=36 and malnourished=13). ANCOVA adjusted for MNA scores confirmed that group differences in body fat mass, lactic acid, lactate/pyruvate ratio, 2-oxobutyric acid, succinic acid and PC(40:2) remained (online supplemental table 10). Within the PD group, MNA scores correlated positively with BMI and body fat mass and negatively with ketone bodies (acetoacetic acid and 3-hydroxybutyric acid) and PC(40:2) (online supplemental table 11, online supplemental figure 4).

Discussion

In this study, weight loss in PD was mainly due to reduced body fat, while muscle and other components remained relatively preserved. Metabolic profiling revealed impaired glycolysis (lower lactic acid and lactic acid/pyruvic acid ratio) and Krebs cycle activity (lower succinic acid), accompanied by higher ketone bodies (acetoacetic acid and 3-hydroxybutyric acid) and amino acid catabolism-related metabolites (2-hydroxybutyric acid and 2-oxobutyric acid). Plasma acetic acid levels were also higher. These results suggest a compensatory shift towards lipid and amino acid utilisation due to impaired carbohydrate metabolism, which may underlie the preferential loss of body fat in patients with PD. However, the causal relationship remains speculative. Alternative explanations include reduced caloric intake, increased energy expenditure related to motor symptoms or dopaminergic medication effects. Future longitudinal studies with comprehensive dietary intake and energy expenditure measurements are needed to clarify these metabolic shifts.

Lower lactic acid and lactic acid/pyruvic acid ratio limit pyruvic availability for oxidative phosphorylation.27 Meanwhile, reduced succinic acid, a key Krebs cycle intermediate,28 suggests mitochondrial dysfunction because its deficiency is linked to greater PD severity.29 Conversely, ketone bodies were higher, indicating increased reliance on fatty acid β-oxidation.14 30 These correlated with BMI and NEFAs, suggesting that the reliance on fatty acids for energy may accelerate adipose depletion, contributing to weight loss. In addition, stratifying patients with PD based on BMI revealed that individuals in the low BMI PD group exhibited the most prominent alterations in energy metabolism, particularly marked by elevated levels of these ketone bodies. This supports the hypothesis that a compensatory reliance on alternative energy substrates accompanies weight loss in PD. Complementing the BMI-stratified findings, the HY stage-stratified analysis demonstrated stepwise changes in body composition and metabolism along disease progression, indicating that metabolic alterations intensify with advancing PD. In addition, ANCOVA adjusted for MNA scores confirmed that group differences in fat mass, lactic acid, lactate/pyruvate ratio, 2-oxobutyric acid, succinic acid and PC(40:2) remained. This reinforces that disease-intrinsic abnormalities, such as impaired glycolysis and tricarboxylic acid cycle activity, are not fully attributable to nutritional status. Within PD, MNA correlated positively with BMI and body fat and negatively with ketone bodies, suggesting that poor nutritional status aggravates the metabolic shift and highlighting the potential role of dietary interventions to attenuate fat loss and metabolic dysregulation. Furthermore, 2-hydroxybutyric acid and 2-oxobutyric acid levels were higher, suggesting greater amino acid catabolism, paralleling the enhanced fatty acid β-oxidation, as evidenced by their clustering with ketone bodies in the correlation matrix. Acetic acid was also higher and may be produced in the liver to counteract metabolic stress, thereby supporting acetyl-coenzyme A generation through alternative pathways.31 Collectively, this study suggested the energy metabolic shifts observed in PD.

Although fat loss was evident, muscle mass did not differ between patients with PD and HCs in our cohort. Consistently, the prevalence of sarcopenia in the PD group was 8.8%, similar to or lower than 11% reported in the general older population.26 These findings may reflect the relatively moderate disease stage (mean HY stage, 2.7) of the participants who were able to stand for the BIA. Previous studies have indicated that sarcopenia becomes more pronounced in later or advanced stages of PD.32 33 Therefore, early intervention may be critical, especially as fat mass reduction often manifests prior to advanced disease, potentially exacerbating metabolic stress and influencing the disease trajectory.

Based on our phospholipid analysis, PC(40:2) was the only phospholipid exhibiting higher levels in PD. PC(40:2) is composed of various fatty acids, such as oleic acid (18:1), erucic acid (22:1), linoleic acid (18:2) and behenic acid (22:0). From a mechanistic perspective, the fatty acids comprising PC(40:2) have distinct effects. Oleic acid, despite neuroprotective properties, can exacerbate α-synuclein toxicity by promoting its membrane binding and aggregation.34 Erucic, linoleic and behenic acids stabilise membranes but may also promote oxidative stress,35 neuroinflammation36 or neurodegenerative processes.37 Collectively, these features suggest potential neurotoxic properties of PC(40:2) in PD pathogenesis. Consistently, our results demonstrated higher PC(40:2) levels in advanced HY stages, suggesting that phospholipid dysregulation may reflect or contribute to PD progression. Notably, in the HC group, levels of certain LPC species were positively correlated with body fat mass and BMI, yet this relationship was absent in the PD group. One possible explanation is that altered phospholipase A2 activity, or other lipid-remodelling enzyme activity, impairs normal lipid turnover in PD, further contributing to pathological changes.38

Interestingly, we also observed increased acetic acid levels in the plasma of patients with PD, which could reflect gut–blood barrier dysfunction and/or a compensatory hepatic response to energy stress.9 31 These findings suggest that acetic acid in plasma, which is not entirely derived from intestinal metabolites, may serve as an additional substrate for the Krebs cycle under conditions of impaired glucose metabolism.

This study has several limitations. First, while the sample size was sufficient to detect group differences, it may not fully represent the broader PD population. Second, the cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of causality between metabolic changes and disease progression. Moreover, the moderately sized cohort, reflecting a relatively moderate disease stage (mean HY stage 2.7), may limit the generalisability of our findings. Therefore, larger and longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether these alterations precede PD symptoms or result from disease progression and to validate the findings in more representative PD populations. Third, the validity of body composition measurements using BIA was assessed only in the PD group, based on a comparison with DXA. Although strong correlations were observed between the two methods for both body fat and muscle mass, BIA tended to overestimate fat mass and underestimate muscle mass. Similar trends have been reported in validation studies of BIA in healthy adults,39 suggesting that these biases are inherent to the method rather than specific to PD. Therefore, the impact of such measurement bias on the interpretation of body composition changes in the PD group is likely minimal. Fourth, although UHPLC-MS/MS provided comprehensive plasma metabolite profiles, no validation using tissue samples was conducted. Fifth, although the study identified elevated PC(40:2) levels in patients with PD, the specific molecular species comprising PC(40:2) were not quantified. Sixth, the potential effects of therapies, such as imeglimin,40 which target mitochondrial function and glucose metabolism, and dietary interventions should be explored in animal models and clinical trials to validate their impact on PD-related metabolic imbalances. Finally, this study did not assess mitochondrial proxy markers in blood, such as platelet-derived measures. While mitochondrial dysfunction is central to PD pathophysiology, relevant circulating markers were not assessed and warrant future evaluation.

In conclusion, patients with PD exhibited decreased body fat mass, leading to weight loss, alongside metabolic changes suggestive of impaired glycolysis and Krebs cycle function, compensated by increased lipid and amino acid utilisation. These shifts may be associated with reductions in body fat mass, and changes in phospholipids were also associated with clinical parameters. Further research is needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms and evaluate therapeutic strategies targeting these metabolic pathways in patients with PD.
