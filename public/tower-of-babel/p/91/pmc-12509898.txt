Translesion Synthesis DNA Polymerase Gene Expression Is Impacted by Age and IGF-1 in Epidermal Human Skin
Authors: Stanley D. Rider, Madison S. Owens, Craig A. Rohan, Jeffrey B. Travers, Michael G. Kemp
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Regions of sun-exposed skin are prone to carcinogenesis in geriatric individuals and exhibit a reduced ability to remove mutagenic UV photoproducts from DNA. To determine whether geriatric skin may be more reliant on translesion synthesis (TLS) DNA polymerases to replicate unrepaired UV photoproducts, we examined the expression of TLS polymerases in the epidermis of young and geriatric individuals. Although significant differences were not observed between these 2 age groups, the expression of the TLS polymerase genes POLH, POLI, POLK, REV1, and REV7 were found to be inversely correlated with the skin aging biomarker gene COL1A1 but not with S100A7 among geriatric individuals. We further examined the effect of UVB exposure on TLS polymerase expression and found that mRNA levels of POLI, POLK, and REV1 were elevated in UVB-irradiated geriatric skin relative to those in young adult skin. Consistent with prior studies linking reduced insulin-like growth factor-1 production and signaling with altered UVB responses, studies with cultured keratinocytes and geriatric skin indicated that deficient insulin-like growth factor-1 signaling is associated with a stronger UVB-dependent induction of REV1. In summary, this work suggests that alterations to TLS polymerase gene expression in UVB-irradiated geriatric skin should be considered in future studies of skin cancer in human subjects.

Introduction

UVB wavelengths of sunlight induce the formation of photoproducts in DNA (Cadet et al, 2015) that are potentially mutagenic if not removed by the nucleotide excision repair (NER) machinery (Kemp, 2019; Sancar, 2016). Complete loss of NER leads to the rare skin cancer–prone disease xeroderma pigmentosum (DiGiovanna and Kraemer, 2012). However, altered NER activity may also contribute to sporadic skin cancers among the general population owing to interindividual differences in NER gene expression caused by environmental, occupational, and dietary factors. Furthermore, most nonmelanoma skin cancers occur in individuals aged >65 years (Kraemer, 1997), and several studies have shown the skin of older individuals to be slower at removing UV photoproducts from DNA than the skin of young adults (Freeman, 1988; Lewis et al, 2010; Xu et al, 2000; Yamada et al, 2006).

UVB radiation can induce both mutagenesis and cell death. However, because there is no evidence that geriatric individuals display increased skin cell lethality such as sunburn after UVB or sun exposure, geriatric individuals may be more reliant on DNA damage tolerance pathways to deal with unrepaired UV photoproducts in DNA. Several DNA damage tolerance pathways exist in human cells (Chang and Cimprich, 2009), which include the use of translesion synthesis (TLS) DNA polymerases that can incorporate nucleotides across damaged DNA templates (Sale, 2013). However, although TLS polymerases promote cell viability after exposure to UVR and other DNA damaging agents, they have a reduced fidelity and therefore elevate the likelihood of introducing incorrect nucleotides during DNA synthesis. The utilization of TLS polymerases on damaged templates is thought to involve the monoubiquitination of the replicative clamp protein proliferating cell nuclear antigen, which serves as a biochemical signal to recruit TLS polymerases to sites of DNA damage (Jansen et al, 2007; Kanao and Masutani, 2017). Interestingly, a prior study showed that the epidermis from UVB-irradiated geriatric skin displayed elevated levels of proliferating cell nuclear antigen monoubiquitination relative to UVB-exposed young adult skin (Hutcherson et al, 2021), suggesting that geriatric skin is more reliant on this mutagenic DNA damage tolerance pathway to replicate unrepaired UV photoproducts in DNA and thus to survive UVB exposures.

Furthermore, the expression of some TLS polymerases, including polymerase eta that is mutated in the variant form of xeroderma pigmentosum (ie, XP-V) (Masutani et al, 2008), is induced by exposure to UVR or other genotoxins (Lerner et al, 2017; Liu and Chen, 2006). Whether the expression of TLS polymerases is altered in geriatric skin relative to that in young adult skin under either naïve or UVB-exposed conditions has not previously been examined. Thus, in this study, we used skin biopsies from geriatric and young adults to examine the relative expression of TLS polymerases relevant to UVB exposure and DNA damage tolerance. We find altered expression of a subset of TLS polymerases in these 2 groups after UVB exposure and provide evidence using keratinocytes in vitro and skin biopsies ex vivo that insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), which is known to be expressed at lower levels in geriatric skin (Lewis et al, 2010) and to impact various aspects of the cellular DNA damage response (Fernandez et al, 2015; Kemp et al, 2017a; Kuhn et al, 1999; Lewis et al, 2010, 2008; Loesch et al, 2016), contributes to these differences.

Results

We next examined 2 additional genes whose expression has been correlated with aging. COL1A1 expression is reported to be lower in aged human skin (Bigot et al, 2012; Fisher et al, 2008; Mine et al, 2008; Spandau et al, 2012), whereas psoriasin (S100A7) expression is regulated by telomere dysfunction in aging skin cells (de Castro et al, 2014). However, neither locus showed significant differences in steady-state transcript levels between the young and geriatric groups (Figure 1b). This may indicate that our model of aging, on the basis of chronological age, was incomplete or that the variation among people was too large to detect a difference within the small sample size of our study population.

Nonetheless, we then explored the relationships between the expression of COL1A1 or S100A7 and the TLS polymerase genes within each of the 2 age groups by performing linear regression analyses of the different TLS polymerase genes with COL1A1 and S100A7. TLS expression levels were not significantly correlated with those of S100A7 in either the young or the older adults or with COL1A1 in the young adults (Figure 1c and e). However, in striking contrast, the expression of most of the other TLS polymerase genes (with the exception of PRIMPOL and REV3L) was negatively correlated with COL1A1 expression among the geriatric individuals (Figure 1c and d), such that higher expression of the TLS gene (lower ΔCt value) was associated with lower expression of COL1A1 (higher ΔCt value). Thus, we conclude that although basal TLS polymerase gene expression is not significantly different between young and older adults, lower expression of the skin-aging gene COL1A1 within geriatric individuals is associated with increased expression of most of the TLS polymerases. Furthermore, these data may support the “hinged linear spline” model of skin aging, in which skin cell replacement time (which is dependent on cell proliferation) is unchanged in young people (aged <50 years) but undergoes a significant linear increase (takes longer to replicate and replace cells) in those aged >50 years (Grove and Kligman, 1983).

Expression of several TLS polymerases is elevated in UVB-irradiated geriatric skin relative to that in young adult skin

Because TLS polymerase gene expression may be impacted by exposure to UVB radiation or other genotoxic agents, we performed a second study in which we obtained skin punch biopsies from young and geriatric adults, exposed the skin to 400 J/m2 UVB (approximately 1 minimal erythemal dose), and then incubated the skin ex vivo in a 37 °C water bath for 24 hours prior to epidermal harvesting and RT-qPCR analysis. Similar to the results in Figure 1a, no significant differences in TLS polymerase gene expression were noted in the nonirradiated skin between the 2 age groups (Figure 2). However, when we compared the expression of the TLS polymerases in the UVB-irradiated biopsies from the 2 age groups, we found that several of the genes (POLI, POLK, REV1, and REV3L) displayed higher expression (lower ΔCt values) in the geriatric adults than in the young adults (Figure 2). Loss of IGF-1 signaling results in enhanced UVB-dependent induction of TLS polymerase genes in quiescent keratinocytes in vitro

Previous studies have shown that reduced IGF-1 signaling in geriatric skin in vivo, skin explants ex vivo, and keratinocytes in vitro is correlated with altered cellular and DNA damage responses to UVB radiation (Fernandez et al, 2015; Kemp et al, 2017a, 2017b; Lewis et al, 2010, 2009, 2008; Loesch et al, 2016; Spandau et al, 2012; Travers et al, 2013). Thus, to extend our studies to keratinocytes in vitro and investigate the role of IGF-1 signaling on TLS polymerase gene expression, we treated HaCaT keratinocytes with a small-molecule antagonist of the IGF-1 receptor (IGF-1R) and then examined the impact of this inhibition on the expression of the TLS polymerases after UVB exposure. As shown in Figure 3a, the expression of several of the TLS polymerases, including POLH, POLI, POLK, and REV3L, were induced 2- to 5-fold after the exposure of the subconfluent cells to UVB radiation. The other genes displayed either no change after UVB exposure or exhibited lower levels of expression. Traditional 2-dimensional in vitro keratinocyte culture systems utilize subconfluent populations of rapidly proliferating cells in which 30–40% of cells are in S phase at any given time. However, this situation may not accurately mimic the situation with keratinocytes in vivo. Thus, to more closely represent the conditions of keratinocytes in the epidermis in vivo, we grew HaCaT cells to confluence and then maintained the cells in low serum to limit cell proliferation, which results in 1% or less of cells undergoing S phase at any given time (Khan et al, 20221). Using these cell culture conditions, we then repeated our experiments with the IGF-1R inhibitor and UVB radiation. In contrast to the situation with subconfluent proliferating HaCaT cells (Figure 3a), we found that IGF-1R inhibition was associated with a greater UVB-dependent induction of most of the TLS polymerases (other than PRIMPOL and REV7) in the confluent, nonproliferating HaCaT cells than in the subconfluent, proliferating cells (Figure 3b). Thus, these results indicate that loss of IGF-1 signaling results in elevated mRNA expression of TLS polymerases after UVB exposure.

Injection of IGF-1 into geriatric skin leads to a reduced UVB-dependent induction of REV1 mRNA in geriatric skin

To extend our work on IGF-1 signaling from cultured keratinocytes to human skin, skin punch biopsies from geriatric individuals were injected with either PBS or IGF-1 and then exposed to a single, low fluence of UVB radiation (400 J/m2). The biopsies were next incubated in a 37 °C water bath for 24 hours. RNA was then isolated from the epidermis for RT-qPCR analysis. As shown in Figure 4, biopsies injected with IGF-1 showed a reduced UVB-dependent induction of several of the TLS polymerase genes, although only REV1 reached a statistically significant change. Discussion

Given the relevance of TLS DNA polymerases to mutagenesis and cellular responses to DNA damage induced by UVR, understanding how the 2 major contributors to skin carcinogenesis (UV exposure and age) impact TLS DNA polymerase expression may provide new insights into understanding the origins of skin cancer in geriatric individuals. Although significant differences in the basal expression of these enzymes were not observed between young adult and geriatric skin (Figure 1), our data indicating that UVB-irradiated geriatric skin expresses higher levels of several TLS polymerase (POLI, POLK, and REV1) than UVB-irradiated young adult skin (Figure 2) suggests that geriatric skin may be more dependent on these DNA polymerases to deal with DNA damage induced by UVB exposure. Moreover, our data showing that IGF-1/IGF-1R signaling in cultured keratinocytes in vitro (Figure 3) and skin ex vivo (Figure 4) impacts TLS DNA polymerase expression and particularly REV1 support a growing body of evidence that reduced signaling through this pathway contributes to abnormal UVB DNA damage responses and skin cancer risk in geriatric skin (Kemp et al, 2017b).

However, there are several limitations to our work, including the small numbers of human subjects used in our study, in vitro incubation of the biopsies after UVB exposure, and our analysis of only mRNA (and not protein) levels of these gene products. Thus, future studies involving greater numbers of participants, a broader range of ages, and analysis of gene expression at the protein level could be beneficial to understand how age impacts TLS polymerase expression. Similarly, we used only a single, low fluence of UVB radiation, which appeared to have only a modest effect on gene expression. Thus, future studies using chronic exposures to UVB radiation, solar-simulated light, or even sunlight could reveal greater insights into how age and UVB exposure impact TLS DNA polymerase expression and function in the skin. Finally, our studies with UVB in this study involved the in vitro incubation of the biopsies after UVB exposure, which may not mimic what takes place in intact skin in vivo. It could be beneficial in the future to expose human subjects to UVB radiation in vivo and then obtain biopsies or to compare gene expression in regions of sun-exposed and protected skin to better define the effect of UVR on TLS polymerase gene expression in the skin.

Another issue that remains to be addressed is the mechanism through which IGF-1/IGF-1R signaling impacts multiple TLS DNA polymerase genes. The activation of tumor suppressor protein p53 after UVB exposure is known to be altered by disrupted IGF-1 signaling (Alkawar et al, 2020; Lewis et al, 2008), and both POLH and POLI are known to be regulated by canonical and noncanonical p53 function (Biber et al, 2020; Castaño et al, 2024; Hampp et al, 2016; Ihle et al, 2021; Lerner et al, 2017; Liu and Chen, 2006). Thus, there may be diverse cell signaling pathways affected by IGF-1 signaling that lead to altered TLS DNA polymerase expression. Because dermal wounding and skin rejuvenation methods, such as fractionated laser resurfacing, increase IGF-1 signaling and protect against actinic neoplasia (Frommeyer et al, 2021; Spandau et al, 2021), it will be interesting to determine how these strategies impact the expression of TLS polymerases.

Finally, our in vitro cell culture studies show a specific role for IGF-1 signaling in the UVB-dependent induction of TLS DNA polymerases in nonreplicating quiescent cells. Although the canonical function of these enzymes occurs at replication forks, TLS polymerases also function outside of S phase where they may perform DNA synthesis at single-stranded DNA gaps generated by NER (Sertic et al, 2018). Thus, it is possible that the impact of reduced IGF-1 signaling is greater in quiescent stem cells in the skin, which could have implications for the origins of mutagenesis and skin carcinogenesis.

Materials and Methods

Skin biopsies

Skin punch biopsies (5 mm) were obtained from the lower back/upper buttocks region after written, informed consent and anesthetization of adult volunteers with Fitzpatrick types I–III skin and ranged in age from 21 to 87 years. This human subject research protocols abided by the Declaration of Helsinki principles for informed consent and was approved by the Wright State University Institutional Review Board. The first study involved skin biopsies from 12 young (aged <30 years; 8 male, 4 female) and 12 geriatric (aged >65 years; 6 male, 6 female) subjects that were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen after collection and collected as part of a prior study (Cvammen et al, 2024). To examine the impact of IGF-1 and UVB exposure, additional biopsies (2 for young adults, and 4 for geriatric adults) were obtained from additional human subjects comprising 12 young (6 male and 6 female) and 12 geriatric (10 male and 2 female) subjects. To test the effect of IGF-1, the 5-mm biopsies were injected into the dermis with 50 μl of either PBS or IGF-1 in PBS (100 μg/ml), followed by incubation of the samples at 37 °C for 30 minutes before further treatment. The procedure for UVR made use of injected samples that were unexposed or that were exposed to 400 J/m2 UVB irradiation (approximately 1 minimal erythemal dose) using a pair of Analytik Jena UVP XX-15M UVB bulbs calibrated at 290 nm. Each biopsy sample was then placed into a single well of a 96-well plate with 50 μl of EpiLife culture medium (Gibco) and incubated in a 37 °C water bath for 24 hours with the epidermis exposed to air and the dermis submerged in the medium. Samples were then snap frozen and stored at −80 °C prior to collection of the epidermis. To separate the epidermis from the dermis, frozen biopsies were submerged in 75 °C water for 10 seconds, followed by submersion in ice cold water for 10 seconds. A 4-ml dermal curette was used to remove the detached epidermis from the dermis for RNA isolation.

RT-qPCR

Epidermal samples were homogenized in TRIzol reagent (Ambion) using a 1.5-ml pestle (Wilmad-LabGlass), extracted with chloroform, and centrifuged for 10 minutes at 10,000g to obtain the upper phase containing RNA. The upper phase was combined with an equal volume of 70% ethanol prior to RNA isolation. RNA-containing cell culture lysates were processed using a RNeasy Plus Micro kit (Qiagen) to obtain purified RNA. RNA (400–1000 ng) was treated with DNase and reverse transcribed with the Quantitect reverse transcriptase kit (Qiagen), according to the manufacturer's instructions. qPCR was performed using an Azure Cielo 6 real-time PCR machine and TaqMan Fast Advanced master mix with ∼40 ng cDNA in each 10 μl reaction. Taqman primer and probe sets (Thermo Fisher Scientific) included B2M: Hs00187842_m1, COL1A1: Hs00164004_m1, S100A7 (PSOR1): Hs01923188_u1, PrimPol: Hs00923572_g1, POLH (eta): Hs00197814_m1, POLI (iota): Hs00969214_m1, POLK (kappa): Hs00211965_m1, Rev1: Hs01019768_m1, Rev3L: Hs00161301_m1, and Rev7 (MAD2l2): Hs01057448_m1.

Cell culture

Actively dividing HaCaT cells were plated at a density of approximately 30% confluence in a 6-well dish and maintained in DMEM with high glucose (Cytiva) and 10% fetal bovine serum (fetal clone III, Cytiva) and penicillin/streptomycin. Actively dividing cells were then treated 24 hours after plating with either vehicle (DMSO) or 10 μM AG538, with the final DMSO amount consisting of 1/1000 of the media volume. After 2 hours, cells were exposed or not to 200 J/m2 UVB. Dividing cells were harvested 24 hours after treatment. To induce quiescence prior to drug and UVB treatments as described earlier, cells were grown to confluence and then incubated in DMEM containing 0.5% fetal bovine serum for a minimum of 2 days. Quiescent cells were also harvested 24 hours after UVB exposure. Cell cultures were lysed in RLT plus buffer (Qiagen) for RNA isolation using 350 microliters of RLT for each well of a 6-well dish.

Mathematical and statistical analyses

RNA expression level data are presented using dot plots for actual data points and the mean ± SD. D’Agnostino and Pearson and Anderson–Darling tests showed that the ΔCt were normally distributed. Pearson's correlation coefficient (r) was used to evaluate the association of the TLS polymerase with either COLA1 or S100A7 as determined by linear regression. Differences in RNA expression levels between young and geriatric groups were evaluated using a 2-tailed, unpaired Student's t-test. For the analysis of TLS polymerase gene expression in non and UVB-irradiated skin biopsies from young and geriatric adults, 2-way ANOVA with Fisher’s least significant difference posthoc test. Gene expression changes in cultured HaCaT cells were analyzed using a 2-way ANOVA with Fisher’s least significant difference posthoc test for multiple comparisons. Wilcoxon signed-rank test for matched pairs was used to compare fold changes in gene expression between biopsies injected with PBS and those injected with IGF-1. GraphPad Prism (version 10.0) (GraphPad Software) was used for all analyses, and a P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics Statement

Experiments with human skin used discarded skin from routine panniculectomies of surgical patients who provided written, informed consent at Wright State Physicians, which was approved by the Wright State University Institutional Review Board.
