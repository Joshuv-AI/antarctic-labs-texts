The impact of high standard farmland construction on China’s agricultural resilience
Authors: Feiwu Ren, Zihan Xia, Yi Huang, Jiangtao Chi, Gai He, Yanwei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-standard farmland construction (HSFC) is a crucial strategy for strengthening China’s agricultural infrastructure and promoting agroeconomic growth. Agricultural resilience (AR)—the capacity of the agricultural system to maintain core functions and recover from external shocks—is a key indicator of sustainable rural development. This study develops an index system to measure AR using the Drivers-Pressures-State-Impacts-Responses (DPSIR) framework and panel data from 28 provinces (2011–2022). Employing fixed-effects and mediation models, we find that HSFC significantly enhances AR—especially in major grain-producing and northeastern regions—while the effects are weaker in the west and statistically insignificant in the east and non-major grain-producing regions. HSFC improves AR mainly by boosting labor productivity and increasing farmers’ incomes, with income growth as the primary driver. However, by accelerating rural urbanization, HSFC may also contribute to population outflow, which can hinder resilience gains. This research demonstrates the value of the DPSIR approach for systematically exploring how HSFC affects AR through productivity, income, and urbanization mechanisms, and offers policy insights for targeted resource allocation and sustainable agricultural management.Keywords: Agricultural resilience, DPSIR, High-standard farmland, Food security, Rural developmentSubject terms: Environmental economics, Sustainability, Socioeconomic scenarios

Introduction

Climate change, extreme weather events, and geopolitical instability are increasingly threatening global food systems. In this context, resilience—the ability of systems to maintain function amid shocks —has become a central concept in agricultural research. According to Folke’s theory, agricultural resilience (AR) can be interpreted as the ability of an agricultural system to maintain key functions under external disturbances1. While agricultural resilience is a global concern, it holds heightened significance in China due to the country’s vast population2, strained resource base, and growing exposure to climate-related risks3.

Farmland improvement plays a critical role in China’s agricultural development. Since 2004, the No. 1 Central Document has identified agricultural development as a top policy priority for 21 consecutive years, consistently emphasizing farmland improvement as a key focus area4. High-standard farmland construction (HSFC) is a central initiative under this strategy. Defined as the upgrading of existing farmland into a modern agricultural infrastructure network—including irrigation systems, drainage facilities, and rural road networks5—HSFC forms the foundation for enhancing national food security.In addition to its infrastructure significance, HSFC also serves as an important tool for agricultural governance. It contributes to poverty alleviation and rural economic development, thereby strengthening agricultural resilience6. Existing studies have examined the impacts of HSFC on rural environments7–9, food production5,10, and poverty reduction9,11. However, certain research gaps remain. Firstly, despite being a vigorously promoted agricultural policy in China, there is a lack of systematic assessments of the overall effects and mechanisms of HSFC on agricultural resilience. Secondly, amid escalating food security risks, there is a notable scarcity of quantitative studies exploring agricultural improvement from an external macro perspective. Finally, with the labor force declining due to population aging, agricultural crises faced from the farmers’ perspective may have been overlooked.

To address these questions, this study first constructs an evaluation index system for agricultural resilience based on the Drivers–Pressures–State–Impacts–Responses (DPSIR) framework. As a systematic analytical tool, the DPSIR model offers a clear structure, rigorous logic, and distinct hierarchical layers, enabling the effective identification of interactions between human activities and environmental systems. Compared with the traditional Pressure–State–Response (PSR) model, DPSIR incorporates two additional components—Drivers and Impacts—which expand the causal chain and allow for a stepwise analysis of how external drivers exert pressures on the agricultural system, alter its state, produce specific impacts, and subsequently elicit adaptive responses. This makes DPSIR particularly well-suited for capturing the complex dynamics of agricultural systems under socioeconomic and environmental transformations.Second, this study utilizes data on HSFC to conduct an in-depth analysis of the mechanisms through which HSFC influences agricultural resilience, thereby enriching the analytical perspective on agricultural development. Third, the study introduces a theoretical model from the perspective of farmers, who play a dual role within the agricultural production and management systems. It explores how HSFC affects agricultural resilience through mechanisms such as productivity improvement, income enhancement, and urbanization.

Against the backdrop of global climate change and increasing natural disasters, enhancing agricultural resilience has become a key strategy for safeguarding food security and stabilizing rural economies. The innovation of this study lies not only in the integration of the DPSIR framework, but also in its detailed investigation of the mechanisms linking HSFC and resilience. The findings provide valuable policy insights to support high-quality and sustainable agricultural development in China.The rest of the article is organised as follows: the second part is the literature review, the third part is the theoretical analysis and hypotheses, the fourth part is the research methodology and data, the fifth part is the empirical results, and the last part is the conclusion and discussion.

Literature review

Resilience theory and the Drivers-Pressures-State-Impacts-Responses (DPSIR) framework

Introduced into ecology by Canadian ecologist C.S. Holling in the 1970s12, the concept of resilience has since expanded beyond its initial scope, encompassing fields such as urban studies. Early resilience research focused on how systems absorb shocks while maintaining core functions13. However, scholarship has emphasized resilience’s role in enabling systems to renew, reorganize, and evolve14—elements essential for sustainable development but previously underexplored15. This represents a conceptual shift toward a multidimensional understanding of resilience.

The Organisation for Economic and Co-operative Development (OECD) later evolved the stress model into the Pressure-State-Response (PSR) model, which was subsequently expanded by the European Environment Agency (EEA) through the integration of drivers and impacts16. This enhancement facilitates the identification of causal connections between human and natural systems and supports the evaluation of progress towards sustainable development17,18. The DPSIR framework emerged as a valuable tool, linking conceptual inquiry across social and natural sciences and offering a systematic way to describe interactions between the environment and human activities19. The framework’s ability to merge knowledge from various domains and underscoring its potential to bridge science, policy, and management.

DPSIR’s application in fields ranging from water resources20–22 and ecology23,24 to food security25,26, urbanization27, and regional development illustrates its broad utility28,29. Its adaptability to various research contexts reinforces the framework’s relevance, particularly in studying agricultural resilience (AR), where the interaction between human activities and the natural environment plays a pivotal role. In this interaction, the development of agriculture acts as a driver of human activities, while human activities and changes in the environment exert pressures on the development of agriculture, which lead to changes in the state of agricultural development and consequently impacts. Finally, humans can further respond and adapt agriculture based on these impacts. As such, DPSIR offers a comprehensive perspective by incorporating human agency into the analysis, thereby enriching the discourse on AR—a field poised for further exploration.

Agricultural resilience

Focusing on AR offers a proactive alternative to conventional, defensive risk management strategies. The concept of resilient agriculture is increasingly becoming a focal point within agricultural development and risk management discourse. A growing body of literature has explored AR from diverse perspectives. Initially, concerning the conceptualization and quantification of AR, Liu et al. introduced a TOPSIS evaluation model, using weighted Martens’ distance and grey correlation analysis (MTS-GRA-TOPSIS), to assess the resilience of the Comprehensive Regional Agricultural Soil and Water Resource Systems (CRAWSR)30. Furthermore, Hao and colleagues formulated a tripartite model—encompassing economic, social, and environmental dimensions—and established 16 indicators for evaluating sustainable agricultural development capacity5. Studies have also examined AR determinants, especially land resources and policy factors. Lin contends that crop diversification constitutes a viable and cost-effective strategy for bolstering AR, offering farmers a range of scales and modes for both sustainability and economic gain31. Similarly, Bowles et al. observed that, under optimal conditions, diversified crop rotations led to a notable increase in maize yields, and even under adverse conditions, they significantly mitigated yield losses during dry years32. Additionally, research by Webb et al.33 and Michler et al.34 elucidates the interplay between land degradation, climate change, and AR, highlighting the urgent need for adaptive policies and conservation agriculture practices amidst environmental challenges. Notwithstanding the valuable insights offered by existing literature on measures of AR, which predominantly concentrate on static dimensions of agricultural development, there is a discernible gap in incorporating forward-looking stances on risk resilience and response mechanisms. Moreover, these studies often focus on the impact of climate35,36, farming practices37,38, and infrastructure39–41 on agricultural resilience, but there is a lack of research based on the farmers’ perspective. Compared to these external factors, farmers are often the most active agents in achieving agricultural sustainability goals42, their ability to navigate and adapt within this intricate and dynamic sector is crucial for fostering an economically and environmentally resilient agricultural framework34,43.

Farmland improvement and high-standard farmland construction (HSFC) in China

HSFC plays a central role in improving agricultural infrastructure in China. This process entails various activities such as land development, soil enhancement, water management, improvement of rural road networks, and the preservation of farmland and ecological systems. These efforts aim to address challenges such as the fragmentation of arable land, the inadequacy of water management facilities, and the poor quality of agricultural land. They also aim to enhance agricultural resilience and promote sustainable development44. Previous research has predominantly concentrated on the construction, advantages, and issues associated with HSFC. Wen Song et al. investigated the decision-making and execution processes of HSFC projects in administrative villages, focusing on the assessment of arable land quality and the organization of construction efforts45,46. Pu et al. evaluated the impact of ten select projects in Liaoning Province through on-site surveys and remote sensing, showing that HSFC significantly improves the stability of grain production during droughts47. Utilizing Chinese provincial data and a continuous difference model, Ye et al. demonstrated that HSFC policies notably enhance agricultural total factor productivity, though with delayed effects48. Moreover, Peng et al. highlighted that HSFC can markedly decrease rural poverty by 7.4%11. In contrast, Hao et al. reported that such projects can substantially increase grain yield by mitigating yield loss due to droughts and floods and by upgrading medium- and low-yield plots5. While extensive studies have been conducted on the production benefits of HSFC, a systematic examination of its multidimensional impacts on agricultural development remains lacking.

Existing research on AR primarily focuses on the production or ecological dimensions, often overlooking the integration of resilience with risk resistance and response, as well as the broader examination from a farmers’ and macro-perspective. This gap limits theoretical development and calls for more rational and applicable evaluation frameworks. Meanwhile, specialized investigations into the synergies between the development of HSFC and AR, particularly concerning their effects and underlying mechanisms, remain scarce. A thorough exploration of these topics is crucial not only for ensuring the stability and vitality of agricultural advancement in China but also for invigorating the sector’s intrinsic growth motivations and reshaping its geographical development patterns to serve as a catalyst for progress. Utilizing panel data from China’s provincial administrative regions between 2011 and 2022, this study employs fixed-effects and mediation-effects models to analyze the influence of HSFC on AR and the mechanisms driving this impact. This endeavor aims to offer scientifically grounded recommendations for fostering high-quality agricultural development amid an increasingly challenging development context.

Theory and hypothesis

Research theory

Theory of production factors

Farmers play a critical role in enhancing agricultural resilience (AR). Classical economists Adam Smith and David Ricardo were among the first to systematically formulate and analyze the theory of production factors, emphasizing the contributions of labor, land, and capital to production. According to Smith’s theory, labor specialization increases productivity, a foundation for improving agricultural output49. Ricardo extended the analysis to include the diminishing marginal returns of land in its contribution to labor production50. From the perspective of factor theory, HSFC enhances AR by improving labor productivity and the efficiency of resource allocation51,52. Furthermore, farmers’ roles are manifested in their ability to adapt to market changes, embrace new technologies and practices, and innovate in product development53. In contemporary agriculture, farmers face the intricate challenge of balancing production efficiency with sustainability and ecological harmony. Analyzing the role of farmers through the lens of factor theory not only illuminates their contributions to agricultural production via labor and resource management but also sheds light on their innovative and adaptive strategies in addressing contemporary agricultural challenges.

Incentive theory

Incentive theory, a fundamental tenet of microeconomics, examines what motivates individuals to undertake certain actions. Incentive theory suggests that increased returns from HSFC motivate farmers to invest more, enhancing productivity and AR54. Adams’ equity theory articulates that individuals assess their input-to-reward ratio (e.g., effort, skill versus pay, recognition), comparing it to that of their peers55. Perceived disparities in this ratio can lead to decreased effort or an increased pursuit of rewards56,57. Offering economic incentives to farmers could motivate them to enhance their agricultural investments, such as purchasing seeds, fertilizers, and advanced farming equipment58. Such augmentations in inputs can lead to improved agricultural productivity and crop quality, thereby contributing to the overarching development of the sector59.

Based on the above theories, we develop a framework (see Fig. 1), where HSFC influences AR directly and indirectly via productivity, income, and urbanisation. HSFC directly enhances agricultural output through infrastructure improvements, while also promoting labor productivity and income growth—two crucial channels that reinforce AR. Meanwhile, urbanisation, though partially driven by HSFC-induced labor release, may undermine AR by reducing the rural labor pool and response capacity to risks. These causal pathways underpin the hypotheses proposed in “Hypothesis” section and form the basis for our mediation model.

Theoretical framework.

Hypothesis

Food production increase effects

Enhanced AR generally indicates a stable and sustainable food supply, as indicated by prior studies1,31,60. The development of premium-quality farmland encompasses an integrated approach that addresses land improvement, road access, and water management infrastructure61. Initiatives such as HSFC development facilitate agricultural enhancements by consolidating fragmented farmlands, thereby promoting large-scale cultivation that streamlines smallholder farming into contiguous, high-quality land parcels62. This development not only augments the soil’s fertility and productive depth but also mitigates prominent constraints on cropland quality63. Furthermore, upgrading farm road networks supports agricultural mechanization and modernization, fostering an uptick in food output64.

In rural areas, crucial public provisions like irrigation and drainage systems bolster agricultural productivity and food availability by enhancing water resource management. Moreover, these systems allow rural labor to shift from farming to other economic activities by enhancing the efficiency of labor usage. Additionally, they mitigate the impact of environmental calamities on capital and labor inputs, thus contributing to the expansion of the agrarian sector and associated industries.

Farmers’ income-generating effects

Growth in AR is also reflected in increased incomes for farmers. Evidenced by multiple research findings, the inception of rural infrastructure like transportation not only boosts productivity and economic expansion but also advances income levels, thereby diminishing poverty – an effect delineated as the trickle-down phenomenon65. Upgrading irrigation systems also empowers farmers to better handle adversities, which cumulatively improves rural living standards and perpetuates equilibrium in farmland use66. Investments in agricultural consolidation and research and development amplify production aptitude and efficacy67 while nurturing regional agrarian economic prosperity. Simultaneously, farmland management initiatives conserve labor, enabling the reallocation to non-farming sectors, thus elevating income potentials.

Population urbanisation

The increase in AR is often accompanied by the contradiction between agricultural development and urbanisation68. After the reform and opening up, the allure of city life—with better employment, education, and healthcare—has lured substantial rural labor to urban centers69. This exodus diminishes the agricultural labor pool’s aptitude, posing a formidable challenge to the country’s agronomy70. Agriculture is fraught with uncertainties, including climatic variations, pests, and disasters. The labor shift impairs farmers’ capacity to respond to such adverse events promptly71. Furthermore, the economic disparity between urban earnings and agricultural incomes may prompt farmers to overlook risks and neglect infrastructure maintenance in rural areas, imposing latent perils on agricultural continuity.

Based on the above statements, we formulate the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1

HSFC bolsters AR.

Hypothesis 2

HSFC reinforces AR via the impact on boosting food production.

Hypothesis 3

HSFC reinforces AR via the impact on boosting farmers’ incomes.

Hypothesis 4

While HSFC can stimulate urbanisation, the latter may impede the growth of AR.

Core explanatory variable

The core explanatory variable is the area of HSFC. In 2017 and earlier, China’s HSFC was jointly handled by the departments of finance, land, water conservancy, and development and reform. In 2018, due to the national institutional reform, China established the Department of Agriculture and Rural Affairs to oversee rural and agricultural development, and the construction of HSFC has been led by the newly-established administrative departments of agriculture and rural areas in each region since that year. As a result, data for 2018 are in a missing state, and the China Financial Yearbook has suspended the inclusion of data on HSFC for 2018 and later years. By collaborating with agricultural and rural departments across provinces and central municipalities, we gathered data on HSFC for 2019–2022. To address the missing data for 2018, we adopted the following approach: First, for provinces where detailed HSFC plans for 2018 were available, we used the approved and publicly announced plans to approximate the missing values. For regions where such plans were unavailable, we applied linear interpolation to estimate the missing 16 data points. Although linear interpolation is a widely used data processing technique, it may introduce certain biases. To assess the potential impact of interpolation on our results, we conducted a comparative analysis of the data before and after interpolation, as shown in Table 2. The mean of the original dataset (including interpolated values) was 158.054 (95% confidence interval (CI): [138.2, 177.9]), while the mean excluding interpolated values was 156.825 (95% CI: [136.2, 177.4]). The substantial overlap between the confidence intervals and the 95% CI for the mean difference [–7.5, 10.1], which includes zero, suggests that the effect of interpolation on the mean estimate was smaller than the natural variability of the data. Furthermore, interpolation did not systematically alter the statistical conclusions regarding the central tendency of the variable. Therefore, the use of interpolation to estimate missing data is considered methodologically robust. Following data imputation, we aggregated the annual high-standard farmland dataset for each province in China. We applied a logarithmic transformation for computational convenience.

Mediating variables

The high-standard farmland policy is a key national strategy aimed at safeguarding food security. It plays a crucial role in consolidating and enhancing comprehensive food production capacity, with broad and far-reaching impacts on agricultural production, ecology, and other related areas. Given that existing high-standard farmland primarily serves agricultural industrial development and food security, this study, based on agricultural production and management systems and in alignment with the hypotheses discussed in “Theory and hypothesis” section, selects labor productivity (LP), population urbanization (PU), and rural resident income (RRI) as mediating variables. Referring to the existing literature, we define LP as the gross output of agriculture, forestry, animal husbandry, and fishery per primary-sector worker, divided by the number of people employed in the primary sector73. PU is defined as the ratio of urban to total permanent residents74,75; and RRI is defined as the log-transformed per capita disposable income of rural households (LN_RRI)76,77.

Existing studies have demonstrated that local government policies and regulatory frameworks play a crucial role in shaping agricultural development78,79. Therefore, it is essential to account for government intervention in agricultural research. Secondly, agricultural development is also shaped by the local industrial structure80, which highlights the need to consider the upgrading of industrial structure and the state of agricultural industry development in the area. Additionally, the level of agricultural production is linked to the degree of land urbanization in the region. In areas with higher levels of land urbanization, agricultural development space is more likely to be constrained81,82. Finally, local agricultural development is closely correlated with agricultural research investments83. Heterogeneity analysis

To align with the evolving grain production landscape, in 2001, China categorized the nation into 13 major grain-producing regions, facilitating the optimization of geographical resource advantages by major grain-producing areas. While benchmark regression reveals the overarching impact of HSFC on AR, capturing regional disparities in the industrial specialization remains challenging. To address this, our analysis categorizes the 28 samples into major grain-producing regions vs. non-major regions, conducting a heterogeneity analysis. The findings, outlined in Table 7, showcase the differences between major grain-producing regions and non-major regions in columns (1) and (2), respectively. From a regional perspective, HSFC did not significantly promote AR in the eastern region, which may be attributed to the region’s industrialization level, where agricultural activities are no longer dominant. The northeastern region benefited most from HSFC, likely due to its fertile black soil, historical focus on grain production, and policy prioritization. The central region followed, supported by moderate industrial-agricultural integration. In contrast, the western region showed the weakest effect, constrained by labor outflows, harsh geography, and fragmented land tenure systems92. These findings suggest that HSFC strategies must be tailored regionally. In the northeast, policies should focus on sustaining soil health and scaling up precision agriculture. In the central region, integrating HSFC with rural industrial upgrading and agro-tech diffusion may yield optimal results. For the western region, a more holistic policy package is essential, combining HSFC with social safety nets, labor retention incentives, and adaptive land-use reforms to prevent underutilization or abandonment of improved land.

Robustness tests

Following methodologies from previous research93,94, this study used four strategies for robustness testing, and the results of each robustness test are presented in Table 7: shortening the sample period, lagging the core explanatory variable by one period, and adding control variables (Proportion of road area (PRA) and Proportion of the primary industry in GDP (PPIGDP)), as shown in Models 1–3 in Table 5. Specifically, lagging the core explanatory variable—HSFC—by one period helps mitigate potential endogeneity concerns, particularly reverse causality between HSFC and agricultural resilience. The rationale is that HSFC construction may not immediately influence AR within the same year, and any reaction from local governments to contemporaneous AR trends could bias results if not controlled for. The temporal lag thus enhances the causal inference of our estimation. Additionally, we excluded 16 data points with missing high-standard farmland data, and the regression results are presented in column (4) of Table 8. The robustness test shows that the construction of HSFC continues to exhibit a strong correlation with agricultural resilience, suggesting that the results of this study are robust.

The results of indirect effects illustrate that the development of HSFC positively influences LP, PU, RRI, and AR (see Table 8; Fig. 2 Mediation model diagrams). It is evident that LP and RRI enhance AR, whereas PU adversely affects it, corroborating our hypothesis. Specifically, the establishment of HSFC boosts labor productivity by augmenting agricultural infrastructure and ameliorating substandard agricultural conditions, as noted by Rajkhowa and Baumüller and Kijek et al., with farmers being the primary beneficiaries95,96. An increase in labor productivity and food production mitigates the effects of agricultural risks, such as natural disasters, thereby significantly bolstering AR. Conversely, PU significantly impacts AR by triggering a rural exodus, which reduces the agricultural labor force and impairs the capacity to respond to agricultural risks. This demographic shift exacerbates the vulnerability of rural areas, as the rural population ages, and younger workers migrate to urban centers. As a result, farming activities are less resilient to environmental shocks such as droughts and floods. Policymakers should focus on addressing these demographic shifts by implementing rural revitalization strategies, including providing better incentives for farmers to remain in rural areas, improving rural infrastructure, and supporting labor market transitions that align with agricultural development needs. This demographic shift challenges the sustainability of Chinese agriculture, driven by high-quality agricultural workers seeking better-paying opportunities outside farming and the difficulties in replenishing the rural labor force with youthful workers. This situation underscores the existing dilemma in Chinese agricultural development. Our results suggest that enhancing rural resident income (RRI) has a more pronounced impact on improving AR than increasing labor productivity (LP). This can be attributed to the fact that higher incomes enhance the capacity of rural households to invest in agricultural improvements, such as adopting new technologies and diversifying income sources, which directly bolster agricultural resilience. In contrast, increasing labor productivity, while important, may have limited effect if it does not directly translate into greater economic opportunities or stability for farmers. Consequently, it is crucial to augment subsidies for the agricultural population to counterbalance the adverse effects of population urbanization on AR.

Discussion and conclusions

Direct effects of High-standard farmland construction (HSFC) on agricultural resilience (AR)

The direct effect results indicate that HSFC is becoming an increasingly important driving force for enhancing AR in China, with robustness tests supporting this finding. HSFC provides a solid foundation for high-quality agricultural development by creating contiguous farmlands with well-developed infrastructure. Additionally, systematic improvements in agricultural land quality, agricultural roads, water conservancy, and power infrastructure have significantly optimized land use, effectively achieving land protection and improving land concentration97. Secondly, major grain-producing regions, as designated by the State Council in 2001, serve as the principal contributors to China’s national grain security due to their superior land quality and larger agricultural labor force98, which ensures the efficient operation of HSFC. In contrast, non-major grain-producing regions, which often prioritize industrial development such as manufacturing, energy, and services, exhibit weaker agricultural development foundations. Thirdly, the northeastern region showed significant benefits from HSFC, followed by the central and western regions, while the eastern region showed no significant improvement in AR. The eastern region is not classified as a major grain-producing region and is dominated by secondary and tertiary industries99, whereas the central region benefits from favorable climatic conditions conducive to HSFC implementation100. The western region, with weaker agricultural and economic foundations, should prioritize addressing population and income issues while focusing on agricultural development101. The northeastern region, with its strong agricultural resource advantages102, should be prioritized for HSFC investments. This provides a theoretical basis for the targeted and differentiated implementation of HSFC.

Mechanism of hsfc’s effect on AR

The mediation effect indicates that HSFC promotes agricultural resilience by increasing labor productivity and raising farmers’ incomes. The Netherlands, as a leading agricultural nation, has made significant strides in improving agricultural infrastructure. As a global model for agricultural modernization, the Netherlands boasts world-leading irrigation and water management technologies103. Through efficient irrigation systems and precise water resource management, the Netherlands can address extreme weather events such as droughts, improving agricultural productivity104. Furthermore, the Netherlands has implemented land reclamation and soil improvement technologies to enhance land quality, ensuring high agricultural yields105. However, HSFC also has the potential to induce rural labor outflow due to population urbanization, which could undermine agricultural resilience. Since HSFC increases agricultural productivity and reduces labor input for the same grain output, the Cobb-Douglas production function suggests that the elasticity coefficients of capital and labor directly determine the growth rate106–108. This frees rural labor from agricultural tasks, optimizing labor time allocation. The time and labor saved can be redirected to higher-income activities. However, this could delay responses to adverse factors such as pests, floods, or droughts, thereby weakening agricultural resilience. Additionally, the mass migration of labor to urban areas leads to local talent loss and rural hollowing. In such “hollow villages,” rural poverty may deepen, potentially offsetting the income increase resulting from HSFC, creating a masking effect. In contrast, the U.S., one of the world’s largest agricultural producers, offers valuable lessons with its policies that support farmers in maintaining agricultural production. U.S. agricultural policies primarily include price support, income support, and crop insurance109,110. Price support calculates subsidies based on farmers’ income and market price fluctuations, while income protection provides compensation for income volatility, especially during natural disasters or market downturns. Crop insurance helps farmers cope with losses from natural disasters and market price fluctuations. In China, agricultural subsidies primarily focus on production-based subsidies, which are fixed and often distributed based on land area. Larger agricultural operations benefit more, while small-scale farmers with fragmented landholdings may not fully benefit111. Furthermore, while there are many types of subsidies, some are weakly linked to farmers’ actual incomes or production risks112, resulting in a “scattergun” approach that does not adequately focus on income protection and risk hedging. Additionally, limited insurance options and low compensation standards offer inadequate protection against extreme weather and price volatility, resulting in low farmer participation113. This lack of comprehensive protection causes farmers to leave agriculture in search of more stable and higher incomes. Therefore, despite the significant success of HSFC, balancing urban and rural development remains a critical issue in agricultural development.

Conclusion

By applying the DPSIR framework, this study constructs an index system to evaluate AR in China. Using fixed-effects and mediation models, we find that HSFC significantly enhances AR, especially in major grain-producing and northeastern regions. The positive effects occur mainly through improvements in labor productivity and, more importantly, increased farmers’ incomes. Conversely, HSFC-driven rural urbanization may reduce the agricultural workforce and offset resilience gains. Our findings confirm the mechanisms proposed in our theoretical framework, while highlighting that urbanization has a negative impact on AR—suggesting the need for policy measures that address rural population outflow when promoting HSFC. Integrating demographic considerations into future infrastructure policy will be crucial for sustaining agricultural resilience.

Theoretical contributions

This research integrates the DPSIR model into agricultural infrastructure studies from an external macro perspective, building on resilience theory. It establishes an AR indicator system and expands the scope of AR research. Additionally, employing the theory of production factors, it constructs a theoretical framework centered on the agricultural production and operation system from the farmers’ perspective. Through econometric methods, it systematically examines how HSFC influences AR, employing both a dual fixed-effect model and a mediation effect model. This approach extends the conceptual avenues for exploring AR.

Management recommendations

In light of China’s specific conditions, we suggest the following strategies:

First, increase the development of HSFC and enhance land quality: The results indicate that HSFC significantly improves labor productivity and farmer income, which is conducive to rural revitalization and agricultural modernization. Therefore, HSFC standards should be refined and tailored to regional agricultural conditions.

Policy support should be strengthened to encourage local governments and relevant departments to actively participate in HSFC. Furthermore, a stable funding mechanism for HSFC should be established to ensure long-term financial investment, improve fund utilization efficiency, and ultimately enhance agricultural resilience.

Second, shift agricultural subsidy strategies to improve farmers’ income from agriculture. Experimental findings indicate that improving farmers’ income more significantly fosters AR than enhancing labor productivity does. Increasing farmers’ income boosts their willingness to farm and, based on the opportunity cost theory, makes staying in rural areas more appealing compared to urban employment, as it reduces the opportunity costs involved by not requiring them to forsake their familiar lifestyles and social ties for better pay, thus aiding in counteracting the reduced agricultural productivity linked to labor migration. International examples, such as those from the Netherlands and the U.S., show that modern agricultural infrastructure—through precision irrigation, land reclamation, and mechanization—significantly boosts agricultural productivity and resilience. In China, the challenge of rural-urban disparities and agricultural population loss should be addressed by adopting policies that balance agriculture and urbanization, transitioning from a “production support” model to one that integrates “income protection + risk management + green development.” This policy shift, employing market tools and precision design, will protect farmers’ interests while improving agricultural competitiveness and sustainability.

Third, optimize agricultural factor allocation and accelerate rural human capital accumulation. HSFC should focus not only on material infrastructure but also on enhancing human capital. During the HSFC process, efforts should be made to train new professional farmers, improving their agricultural production, management, and business skills. By enhancing farmer education and providing agricultural technical training, farmers can better adapt to modern agriculture, promoting agricultural industrialization and scaling up. Furthermore, HSFC involves various sectors, including agriculture, rural development, finance, and water conservancy. Future policies should combine agricultural mechanization, sustainable development, and farmer education to promote high-quality agricultural development. International experiences, such as those from the Netherlands, offer valuable insights. The Netherlands’ policies that promote positive interactions between agricultural production and population movement, through subsidies, land transfer policies, and farmer training, ensure the stability and resilience of agricultural production. China can draw lessons from such successful international experiences to balance rural-urban development, mitigate the negative impacts of rural labor outflow, and promote high-quality, sustainable agricultural development.

Fourth, design targeted institutional strategies to mitigate rural depopulation and hollowing. While HSFC enhances productivity, it may unintentionally accelerate rural–urban migration, weakening agricultural resilience in the long term. Policymakers should consider integrated rural revitalization measures, such as (1) promoting rural entrepreneurship programs that attract youth to return and settle in villages; (2) implementing differentiated land-use policies that link land rights security with active participation in agriculture; and (3) establishing collaborative platforms between local governments and agricultural cooperatives to support smallholder engagement and retention. These measures can help stabilize the rural labor force and ensure the long-term sustainability of HSFC outcomes.

Limitations and prospects

This study provides an in-depth analysis of the impact and mechanisms of HSFC on agricultural resilience. Nonetheless, several areas warrant further improvement: First, the research scope should be expanded. Due to data reliability and availability, this study primarily analyzes data at the provincial level, with limited research on regional differences. Future studies should conduct more granular analyses at the municipal or district (county) level, particularly focusing on regional disparities in agricultural resilience. Expanding the research scope would provide data support for local governments to develop more precise HSFC policies. Second, there is insufficient research on the interactions between agricultural resilience indicators. This study constructed an agricultural resilience indicator system based on the DPSIR model, but interactions between the indicators may influence the results. Future studies could employ techniques such as structural equation modeling (SEM) to capture interactions between indicators and better assess the multifactorial nature of AR.

Third, this study does not account for certain uncertainties, such as policy changes and global trade conflicts, which may have a potential impact on agricultural resilience. Additionally, the ecological sustainability of agriculture, such as soil health and water management, warrants further exploration. Future research should incorporate these factors into the agricultural resilience analysis framework to provide a more comprehensive assessment of HSFC’s role in promoting agricultural sustainable development.

Lastly, while this study adopts fixed-effects and mediation-effect models to control for unobserved regional heterogeneity, potential endogeneity remains a concern. For instance, regions with inherently higher AR may attract more HSFC investment, thus inflating the estimated effect. As a mitigation strategy, we lagged the HSFC variable by one period in the robustness checks, which helps reduce reverse causality and affirms the directional plausibility of the observed effects. Nevertheless, future research should employ stronger identification strategies—such as instrumental variable (IV) approaches or difference-in-differences (DiD) models leveraging exogenous policy shocks—to further validate causal claims.
