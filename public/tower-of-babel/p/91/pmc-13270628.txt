Deciphering epigenetic crosstalk in multiple myeloma pathogenesis and treatment
Authors: Shamila D. Alipoor, Mariusz Shrestha, Aijun Liu, Hong Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple myeloma (MM) is a plasma cell malignancy that originates in the bone marrow (BM) and is characterized by the clonal expansion of Bcell–derived plasma cells producing abnormal monoclonal immunoglobulins. Despite significant therapeutic advances that have improved patient outcomes, drug resistance remains a major obstacle to effective disease management. Genetic and epigenetic heterogeneity are key features of MM that drive disease onset, progression, and therapeutic resistance. Both early and advanced stages of MM exhibit global and locus specific DNA methylation abnormalities that silence tumor suppressor genes and disrupt key regulatory pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin, JAK/STAT, and apoptotic signaling. Altered activity of histone modifiers, including methyltransferases (e.g., EZH2, MMSET) and deacetylases (e.g., HDAC4, HDAC6), promotes chromatin remodeling and myeloma cell survival, representing promising therapeutic targets. Dysregulated long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), such as MALAT1 and MIAT1, further contribute to genomic instability and drug resistance. Emerging epigenetic therapies targeting DNA methyltransferases, HDACs, and bromodomains show promise in overcoming therapeutic resistance and improving patient outcomes. In this article, we summarize current insights into the central role of epigenetic alterations including DNA methylation changes, histone post-translational modifications, and lncRNA dysregulation in MM pathogenesis, discuss their mechanistic and clinical implications, and highlight therapeutic opportunities emerging from these discoveries.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a malignant plasma cell disorder that accounts for approximately 10% of all hematologic malignancies and represents the second most common hematologic cancer worldwide [1]. MM arises from the clonal proliferation of malignant plasma cells within the bone marrow (BM), leading to the excessive production of monoclonal immunoglobulins, known as M-proteins or light chains, which accumulate in the serum and urine. Together with progressive BM infiltration [2], this abnormal protein production results in end-organ damage, including anemia, renal insufficiency, osteolytic bone lesions, and hypercalcemia. Despite significant therapeutic advances, MM remains largely incurable [3, 4]. The disease predominantly affects older adults, with a median age at diagnosis of approximately 60 years, and is associated with an unfavorable prognosis, as reflected by a 5-year survival rate of about 48.5% [5].

The development of MM follows a multistage process that begins with precursor conditions such as monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) [6], an asymptomatic premalignant plasma cell disorder characterized by elevated levels of defective immunoglobulins. Although MGUS does not require treatment, it carries a measurable risk of progression to MM [7]. Over time, MGUS may evolve into smoldering multiple myeloma (SMM) and eventually symptomatic MM, with reported annual progression rates ranging from 1% to 10% [7].

MM progression is driven by a complex interplay of genetic and epigenetic alterations, including chromosomal translocations, copy number abnormalities, and recurrent mutations in key genes such as KRAS [8], NRAS [8], TP53 [9, 10], and DIS3 [10, 11].

Epigenetics refers to heritable yet reversible modifications that regulate gene expression and cellular phenotype without altering the underlying DNA sequence [12]. During MM, epigenetic mechanisms disrupt normal patterns of gene expression and promote disease progression, driving advancement to later stages [13]. The BM microenvironment (BME) further supports genetic and epigenetic changes in MM growth and survival. Epigenetic crosstalk between malignant cells and the BME shapes disease outcomes [14, 15].

To date, significant therapeutic advances have been made using epigenetics modulators in MM. Recent preclinical studies have identified the importance of histone methyltransferase G9a and DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) in the pathogenesis of MM [16]. Dual inhibition of G9a and DNMTs synergistically reduced cancer cell viability and induced apoptosis, highlighting a promising epigenetic based therapeutic approach [17]. Single-cell sequencing is revealing the epigenetic heterogeneity and clonal evolution across the diverse cellular components of tumor sections including malignant, immune, and stromal cells, enhancing our understanding of tumor progression and therapy response [18]. This knowledge ultimately supports decision making aimed at preventing irreversible organ damage in MM. In this review we summarized and discussed the recent updates of the epigenetic mechanisms and biomarkers in different stages of MM and the role they play in the progression of disease.

Epigenetic characterization of MM

On a genetic level, MM cells exhibit a high degree of clonal diversity both within and between patients [19]. This heterogeneity classifies MM into two main cytogenetic subgroups: hyperdiploid and non-hyperdiploid. The hyperdiploid subtype is characterized by additional copies of specific odd numbered chromosomes ( 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 15, 19, and 21) and is generally associated with a more favorable prognosis [19]. In contrast, the non-hyperdiploid group is primarily defined by chromosomal translocations such as t(11;14), t(4;14), t(6;14), t(14;16), and t(14;20). Some of these abnormalities, including t(11;14) and t(6;14), correlate with favorable outcomes, whereas others, such as t(4;14), are linked to poor prognosis [20, 21].

At the epigenetic level, numerous alterations have been described, including aberrant DNA methylation, dysregulated histone modifications, and altered expression of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) (Table 1) [22]. These epigenetic changes can disrupt key signaling pathways commonly implicated in MM pathogenesis, such as Wnt/β-catenin [23, 12], JAK/STAT [24, 25] and p53 [26]. In the following sections, we discuss these mechanisms in greater depth and highlight potential epigenetic biomarkers associated with MM progression.

Aberrant DNA methylation: related epigenetic biomarkers in MM

DNA methylation primarily involves the addition of methyl groups to cytosine bases at CpG dinucleotides [27]. This modification is generally associated with chromatin compaction and transcriptional repression by recruiting methyl-binding proteins that promote the assembly of histone deacetylases into repressive chromatin-remodeling complexes [28].

Aberrant DNA methylation in MM comprises two mechanistically distinct patterns: widespread global hypomethylation and focal, locus-specific hypermethylation, both of which are hallmarks of disease development and progression [29].

Global hypomethylation contributes to genomic instability and widespread transcriptional dysregulation rather than direct gene-specific effect. Hypomethylation of repetitive elements such as LINE1, Alu, and SAT alpha has been linked to chromosomal instability, with particularly low methylation levels observed in non-hyperdiploid and high-risk translocation groups such as t(4;14) [30]. This epigenetic alteration is already evident at the premalignant MGUS stage, persists in newly diagnosed MM, and progressively worsens with advancing disease, correlating with genomic instability, disease progression, and poor prognosis [31] (Table 1). Consistent with these observations, global methylation levels decline progressively with advancing disease [32], a pattern strongly supported by large-scale datasets such as the MMRF CoMMpass study, which demonstrate substantially lower global CpG methylation levels in MM cells compared with normal plasma cells and B cells [32].

Locus specific, DNA hypermethylation predominantly targets CpG islands within promoter regions of tumor suppressor genes, such as p15, p16 [33], TP53 [34], SOCS1, and DAPK [35], as well as enhancer regions of B-cell transcription factor genes like BCL11A, BATF, and PAX5 [36]. This focal hypermethylation silences genes and disrupts key developmental and tumor-suppressive pathways. Notably, these patterns are stage-specific: SOCS1 and DAPK hypermethylation emerge in MGUS, whereas changes in p16, SHP1, and CDH1 appear only in advanced MM, progressively intensifying to peak in plasma cell leukemia and correlating with reduced overall survival [37] .

MM cells also exhibit highly variable DNA methylation across patients, as revealed by advanced sequencing approaches such as enhanced reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (eRRBS) [38]. These variations, termed epiallele shifts, are associated with poorer treatment responses [38]. As MM progresses, particularly at relapses, malignant cells acquire additional stochastic methylation changes, notably at bivalent promoters regulating developmental genes. Under normal conditions, these promoters are maintained in a poised state through a balance between repressive H3K27me3 and activating H3K4me3 histone marks; disruption of this balance promotes disease progression toward more aggressive stages [38]. In MM and other B-cell malignancies, aberrant DNA hypermethylation can replace histone-mediated repression at regions that are typically unmethylated in normal precursor cells. This epigenetic switch leads to stable silencing of key regulatory genes, reducing cellular plasticity and locking cells into a malignant state [39]. Increased stochasticity in DNA methylation further enhances myeloma cell adaptability, facilitating resistance to environmental stress and therapy, contributing to disease progression and therapeutic refractoriness over time [40].

Gene-specific hypo- and hyper-methylation events also serve as significant biomarkers in MM. Hypomethylation of oncogenes such as JAG2, a positive regulator of the Notch signaling pathway, and ABC transporters, which facilitate drug efflux, contributes to enhanced tumor growth and treatment resistance [41]. Additionally, several hypermethylated genes are directly linked to treatment response. SPARC [42] and TGFBI [13] are associated with increased sensitivity to chemotherapy, while RASD1 methylation correlates with dexamethasone sensitivity a corticosteroid commonly used in MM therapy [43].

Overall, both global and gene specific DNA methylation patterns represent valuable prognostic and predictive biomarkers in MM. Integrating these epigenetic alterations into clinical practice may enhance risk stratification, improve prognostication, and inform therapeutic decision making, thereby enabling more personalized treatment strategies for patients with MM. Histone modifications and chromatin remodeling

Histone modifications comprise covalent chemical changes, including methylation, acetylation, and phosphorylation, that occur predominantly on histone tail residues. These modifications alter chromatin structure by changing the affinity of histones for DNA and other associated proteins, thereby influencing chromatin compaction and accessibility to transcription factors. Histone acetylation generally relaxes chromatin and promotes transcription, whereas specific histone methylation marks can either activate or repress gene expression, depending on their genomic location and biological context [44]. In MM, aberrant expression of histone modifying enzymes, including histone methyltransferases (HMTs), histone deacetylases (HDACs), and histone acetyltransferases (HATs), disrupts transcriptional balance to promote aberrant gene expression programs that drive malignant transformation and disease progression [45] (Table 1).

Histone methyltransferases (HMTs) and demethylases

EZH2 is an HMT associated with MM that is part of the Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2). This complex mediates transcriptional silencing by catalyzing trimethylation of lysine 27 on histone H3 (H3K27me3), a repressive chromatin mark associated with gene inactivation. EZH2, the catalytic subunit of PRC2 [46], exhibits progressively increased expression during MM progression, from MGUS and smoldering stages to overt plasma cell leukemia. EZH2 overexpression is associated with disease progression and poor prognosis, whereas its inhibition induces apoptosis in MM cells [47]. Mechanistically, EZH2 forms a repressive complex with AP2α that silences tumor suppressor genes, such as EMP1, through H3K27 trimethylation [48]. Pharmacological targeting of EZH2 has shown promising results in preclinical models [46]. Inhibition of EZH2 not only reduces myeloma cell proliferation and osteolytic bone lesions but also restores tumor suppressor gene expression [48].

The recurrent translocation t(4;14) in MM results in overexpression of the Multiple Myeloma SET domain protein (MMSET), also known as NSD2. MMSET encodes a histone methyltransferase that catalyzes di- and trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 36 [49]. In t(4;14) + MM, MMSET overexpression leads to a genome wide increase in H3K36me2 and, concomitantly, a reduction in the repressive histone mark H3K27me3. This shift promotes a more open chromatin configuration that favors oncogenic gene activation [49]. Mechanistically, MMSET-mediated H3K36 dimethylation interferes with the binding and methyltransferase activity of the PRC2 complex, including EZH2, which is responsible for catalyzing H3K27 trimethylation on the same histone tail. Collectively, these epigenetic alterations disrupt chromatin architecture, diminish H3K27me3 mediated repression, and drive oncogenic transcriptional programs in t(4;14) + MM [50, 51].

SETD2 is frequently dysregulated and plays a critical role in maintaining myeloma cell survival. This histone methyltransferase catalyzes trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 36 (H3K36me3), a mark associated with transcriptional elongation and RNA processing. Loss or inhibition of SETD2 in MM cell lines reduces H3K36me3 levels and impairs cell proliferation, underscoring its essential function in sustaining malignant growth [52].

A recent study by Lyu et al. identified a histone modification-related gene signature with strong prognostic value in MM. This signature comprising SUZ12, KAT2A, AURKA, BUB1, SUV39H2, UTY, and PCGF5, includes key regulators of histone modification, and epigenetic control in MM. Functionally, these genes are associated with pathways governing cell cycle progression, cellular proliferation, and immune regulation, underscoring their contribution to disease progression and risk stratification [53].

In MM, SUV39H2 functions as a histone methyltransferase responsible for trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 9 (H3K9me3), a mark associated with transcriptional repression and heterochromatin formation. Its overexpression is associated with poor prognosis and chemoresistance in MM [54].

PCGF5, a component of the Polycomb Repressive Complex 1 (PRC1), mediates monoubiquitination of histone H2A and cooperates with PRC2 mediated H3K27 methylation to promote gene silencing. Dysregulation of these histone modifying enzymes disrupts chromatin organization, leading to aberrant cell proliferation, evasion of apoptosis, and drug resistance [55].

Likewise, the lysine demethylases KDM6A (UTX) and its Y‑linked homolog UTY remove repressive histone H3K27me3 marks, thereby promoting activation of genes involved in immune recognition and antigen presentation [56]. Through this mechanism, KDM6A acts as a tumor suppressor by maintaining tumor immunogenicity and facilitating T-cell–mediated immune surveillance; loss of this function contributes to impaired immune recognition and increased disease aggressiveness [56]. KDM3A is another histone demethylase that drives MM pathogenesis by erasing repressive H3K9 methylation, thereby activating a KLF2–IRF4 transcriptional circuit essential for myeloma cell survival and proliferation. This axis is further sustained by a positive feedback loop involving hypoxia inducible factor 1 (HIF-1) and the long noncoding RNA MALAT1, reinforcing glycolytic metabolism and tumor persistence. Notably, silencing KDM3A is toxic to MM cells, underscoring its oncogenic role [57].

Histones can also be methylated on arginine residues, a modification catalyzed by protein arginine methyltransferases (PRMTs) [22]. PRMT4, also known as coactivator-associated arginine methyltransferase 1 (CARM1), is overexpressed in MM. Pharmacological inhibition of PRMT4 using small molecules such as EZM2302 and TP-064 markedly suppresses tumor growth, supporting its relevance as a therapeutic target in MM [58, 59]. PRMT5, another key arginine methyltransferase, is frequently upregulated in MM CD138 + plasma cells and is strongly associated with poor survival outcomes [60]. The selective PRMT5 inhibitor EPZ015666 has demonstrated potent anti MM activity by reducing proliferation and inducing apoptosis in myeloma cells [60].

Histone deacetylases (HDACs) and acetyltransferases (HATs)

This group plays a critical role in MM progression by removing acetyl groups from histones, promoting chromatin condensation and transcriptional repression [61]. Dysregulated HDAC activity in MM fosters tumor cell proliferation and resistance to therapy, with specific isoforms such as HDAC3 and HDAC6 implicated in survival signaling, protein degradation pathways [62], and modulation of the BME [57]. Collectively, these alterations underscore the central role of HDACs in coordinating both intrinsic myeloma cell survival mechanisms and extrinsic interactions within the tumor microenvironment.

HDAC4 is upregulated through MM progression and its knockdown induces apoptosis and autophagy, especially under conditions of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress [63]. The microRNA miR-29b has been identified as a functional antagonist of HDAC4, highlighting cross regulation between noncoding RNAs and histone modifying enzymes [63]. HDAC6 promotes protein quality control via aggresome formation and forms a positive feedback loop with MYC to drive oncogenesis [64]. SIRT6, a class III HDAC, accelerates myeloma progression by maintaining the transcription of DNA damage related genes, and its loss promotes anti myeloma responses. In contrast, HDAC11 interacts with IRF4 to support plasma cell differentiation and survival, and its inhibition disrupts this process, thereby reducing MM cell survival [65].

CBP and p300, as essential HATs, maintain IRF4 expression, which in turn stabilizes the oncogenic transcription factor MYC and sustains MM cell growth [66]. Inhibition of CBP/p300 disrupts this regulatory loop and exerts anti-MM effects by simultaneously repressing IRF4 and MYC expression [66].

Collectively, these findings link aberrant histone acetylation and deacetylation to multiple hallmarks of MM. Histone modifications and their roles as epigenetic biomarkers across different stages of the disease are summarized in Table 1; Fig. 1.

Non-coding RNAs

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), including long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs) play an important role in the regulation of gene expression and act as epigenetic modulators in MM (reviewed in [15]). Transcriptome profiling has revealed widespread dysregulation of ncRNA networks in malignant plasma cells, which profoundly influence disease progression, and therapeutic resistance [67].

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs)

LncRNAs exert epigenetic control in MM through diverse mechanisms, including miRNA sponging and direct interactions with chromatin-modifying complexes like PRC2. lncRNAs can regulate gene expression by acting as “sponges” for microRNAs (miRNAs). By binding to miRNA response elements (MREs), they sequester miRNAs and prevent them from repressing their mRNA targets. This competitive interaction alleviates miRNA-mediated suppression and can consequently drive epigenetic changes through modulation of epigenetic miRNAs (epimiRNAs) [68]. Additionally, lncRNAs can directly interact with chromatin modifying complexes, such as PRC2 or histone methyltransferases to alter epigenetic patterns and influencing transcriptional activity.

Among lncRNAs, Metastasis Associated Lung Adenocarcinoma Transcript 1 (MALAT1) is a well-characterized oncogenic lncRNA frequently dysregulated in MM and has been identified as biomarkers of poor prognosis of the disease [69]. Treatment with bortezomib (BTZ), a proteasome inhibitor widely used in MM therapy, enhances MALAT1 expression in MM cell lines. Inhibition of MALAT1 re-sensitizes these cells to BTZ and other chemotherapeutic agents by impairing DNA repair pathways. Furthermore, MALAT1 interacts with key miRNAs, including miR-1271-5p, miR-509-5p, and miR-29b, whose upregulation leads to reduced EZH2 expression and altered histone methylation. Interestingly, MALAT1 itself is targeted by miR-125b, which is downregulated in MM [70].

There are also other lncRNAs reported with epigenetic roles in MM ( Table 1). NETA1 regulates miR-214 and miR-193a, influences proliferation, and mediates resistance to dexamethasone - a glucocorticoid widely used in MM therapy- via DNA repair and Wnt/β-catenin pathway modulation [71].

Taiana et al. demonstrated that the long non- coding RNA NEAT1 is highly expressed in MM plasma cells compared with normal cells. NEAT1 expression was independent of molecular subtypes, cytogenetic features, and clinical outcome, indicating that its upregulation is a common characteristic of MM rather than a prognostic marker. These findings suggest a potential functional role for NEAT1 in myeloma biology [72].

UCA1 promotes JAK/STAT signaling by downregulating miR-331-3p and targeting miR-1271-5p [70]. HOTAIR is an oncogenic long non-coding RNA that interacts with PRC2 and lysine specific demethylases to regulate epigenetic modifications. In MM, HOTAIR promotes dexamethasone resistance by enhancing cell survival and proliferation through activation of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, representing a potential diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target [73]. Regarding tumor cell proliferation, lncRNA Protein Disulfide Isomerase Family A Member 3 Pseudogene (PDIA3P) enhances c-Myc transactivation and modulates pentose phosphate pathway signaling, thereby facilitating cancer cell survival [74]. The lncRNAs H19 and SMILO suppress proliferation by sponging miR-29b; however, they also contribute to tumor maintenance and drug resistance in MM [75, 76].

The oncogenic lncRNA Antisense Noncoding RNA in the INK4 locus (ANRIL) promotes chemoresistance via EZH2 mediated PTEN silencing [77]. Similarly, dysregulated lncRNAs Plasmacytoma Variant Translocation 1 (PVT1) and STAT3-Induced Long Noncoding RNA 18 (STAiR18) interact with epigenetic complexes such as PRC2 and are associated with histone methylation, reinforcing repressive gene expression programs in MM [78]. lncRNAs such as PRC2-Associated LncRNA Regulating UPR in MM (PLUM) facilitate PRC2 complex assembly, enhance EZH2 activity, and modulate chemoresistance [79]. Finally, TP73 Antisense RNA 1 (TP73-AS1), also known as KIAA0495, is progressively downregulated during MM development, potentially through mechanisms extending beyond promoter methylation, underscoring the complexity of lncRNA mediated epigenetic regulation in MM [80].

MicroRNAs (miRNAs)

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are short non-coding RNAs (~ 19–25 nucleotides) that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by inducing degradation or translational repression of target mRNAs [81]. In MM, miRNAs orchestrate multiple pathogenic mechanisms such as immunomodulation, tumor microenvironment remodeling, DNA methylation, genomic instability, and treatment resistance [82]. These small RNAs can act as oncogenes or tumor suppressors, representing both prognostic biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets [15].

In MM, epigenetic regulation is intricately linked with miRNA expression and function [82]. Several epimiRNAs, have been identified to both influence and be influenced by epigenetic modifiers such as DNMTs and HDACs [82]. These miRNA–epigenetic modifier interactions constitute a reciprocal regulatory circuit that is vital for MM pathogenesis and therapeutic response [82]. The epigenetic role of miRNAs in the pathogenicity of MM has been comprehensively reviewed previously [15, 82]. For example, promoter hypermethylation silences tumor suppressor miRNAs, miR-34c and miR-152, correlating with disease progression [83, 84]. As discussed earlier, the miR-29 family directly targets HDAC4, promoting apoptosis and enhancing drug sensitivity in MM cells [63]. Specifically, MiR-29b is frequently downregulated in myeloma cells and regulates critical pathways involved in cell proliferation, apoptosis, epigenetic modulation, and interaction with the BME [85, 86]. Restoration of miR-29b expression has been shown to inhibit myeloma cell growth and enhance sensitivity to anti myeloma agents, supporting its therapeutic potential [87, 88]. Moreover, miR-29b has been proposed as a promising candidate for miRNA based therapeutic strategies in MM [89].

miR-410 is highly expressed in newly diagnosed and relapsed MM and correlates with advanced disease stages and poor OS by suppressing the transcription factor KLF10 and activating the PTEN/PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway [90]. Similarly, miR-135b and miR-642a enhance the malignant phenotype in MM by targeting DEP domain-containing mTOR-interacting protein (DEPTOR), a negative regulator of mTOR, promoting undifferentiated cell states and worse patient outcomes [91]. Silencing of miR-340-5p [92] and miR-28-5p [93] via promoter hypermethylation is an early, epigenetically driven event in MM development, linking DNA methylation to miRNA dysregulation. miR-181a and miR-20a are embedded within epigenetic circuits where their expression and downstream effects influence chromatin-modifying complexes and transcriptional networks. miR-181a is significantly upregulated in MM and functions as a novel oncogene by promoting cell proliferation, migration, and tumor growth. Mechanistically, miR-181a regulates key cell cycle proteins such as CCND1 (cyclin D1), thereby facilitating progression through the G1/S checkpoint [94]. Additionally, miR-181a expression is influenced by the BME and may be involved in epigenetic regulatory networks that modulate gene expression essential for MM pathogenesis. Knockdown of miR-181a leads to cell cycle arrest and increased apoptosis, indicating its critical role in MM tumorigenesis [95].

Similarly, miR-20a is an oncomiR that contributes to proteasome inhibitor resistance through its epigenetic regulatory effects on pathways involved in cell survival and proliferation [96]. It has been shown that miR-20a promotes MM cell proliferation and migration while inhibiting apoptosis by directly targeting the tumor suppressor PTEN. This interaction activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, contributing to MM progression. Conversely, inhibition of miR-20a increases PTEN expression, resulting in suppression of this pathway and reduced MM cell growth and migration [96].

Our group has shown that dysregulation of the EZH2/miR-138 axis contributes to drug resistance in MM by downregulating the tumor suppressor RBPMS. Overexpression of EZH2 in drug resistant MM cells leads to repression of miR-138 via H3K27me3 mediated silencing, which in turn suppresses RBPMS expression and promotes resistance. Restoration of miR-138 or inhibition of EZH2 reverses drug resistance and sensitizes MM cells to agents such as bortezomib [97].

Aurora kinase A (AURKA) is a serine/threonine kinase essential for mitotic progression and chromosome segregation. In MM, AURKA is frequently overexpressed and contributes to increased cell proliferation, genomic instability, and disease malignancy. Our studies reveal that miR-137 is frequently downregulated in MM due to promoter methylation and functions as a tumor suppressor by directly targeting AURKA. Loss of miR-137 results in elevated AURKA expression, enhancing chromosomal instability and chemotherapeutic drug resistance. Overexpression of miR-137 restores drug sensitivity, decreases chromosomal aberrations, and induces apoptosis via the p53/ATM/Chk2 pathway, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target to overcome drug resistance and genomic instability in MM [98].

miR-34a acts as a tumor suppressor microRNA in MM by directly targeting the oncogenic protein Myristoylated Alanine-Rich C Kinase Substrate (MARCKS) [84]. Overexpression of this oncogene is implicated in drug resistance and progression of MM [99]. We have demonstrated that overexpression of miR-34a reduces MARCKS protein levels, leading to decreased proliferation and increased apoptosis of MM cells. Importantly, inhibition of the miR-34a/ MARCKS axis resensitizes MM cells to chemotherapeutic agents, overcoming drug resistance. These findings highlight miR-34a’s potential as a therapeutic agent to restore drug sensitivity and inhibit tumor progression in refractory MM cases [84].

Furthermore, the prognostic value of miRNAs in MM progression is increasingly recognized [100–104]. For example, upregulated miR-20a and miR-148a levels are associated with relapse-free survival (RFS) [105], while decreased serum levels of miR-25, miR-16, and miR-30a correlate with poor OS and disease severity [102]. Moreover, miRNAs such as miR-744 and let-7e are linked with disease stage and remission duration, showing their clinical utility as prognostic biomarkers [104].

Research into miRNA-based interventions and combined epigenetic therapies continues to expand promising new avenues for effective MM treatment.

Epigenetic mechanisms in bone marrow microenvironment (BME)

The BME plays a central and dynamic role in driving the progression of MM from its precursor states, MGUS and SMM, to overt malignancy [14]. This complex niche is composed of numerous cellular constituents, including mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), osteoblasts, osteoclasts, endothelial cells, and diverse immune cells, together with non-cellular components such as cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and an intricately organized extracellular matrix (ECM), which collectively shape the BME [14].

Throughout disease evolution, the BME undergoes profound remodeling, characterized by cytokine deregulation, expansion of immunosuppressive cell populations, and restructuring of the extracellular matrix. These changes establish a specialized, tumor-supportive microenvironment that attenuates effective immune surveillance and promotes malignant plasma cell survival and proliferation [106].

The immune milieu within the BME similarly undergoes substantial epigenetic rewiring, facilitating the expansion and activation of immunosuppressive populations such as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and regulatory B lymphocytes. These cells undergo epigenetically driven changes that enhance the production of immunosuppressive cytokines and chemokines, further compromising anti-tumor immune responses and supporting dysregulated plasma cell survival and proliferation [14].

Furthermore, aberrant DNA methylation of homeobox genes in mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) compromises osteoblast differentiation, thereby contributing to the hallmark bone disease of MM [107]. In addition, histone deacetylation of key transcription factors, including runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), a master regulator of skeletal development, further suppresses osteogenesis, remodeling the niche in a manner that favors tumor growth [108, 109].

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) laden with bioactive proteins, adhesion molecules, and even histones [110], contribute significantly to intercellular communication and niche remodeling, amplifying tumor-permissive signaling cascades within BME. Among the most prominent epigenetic mediators of the cross talk are exosomal miRNAs, which orchestrate communication between tumor cells and multiple BME compartments(reviewed in [15]).

Comparative analyses of exosomal miRNA profiles in serum from MGUS, SMM, and MM patients reveal dynamic shifts during disease progression [111]. Tumor-suppressive miRNAs, including miR-125a, miR-28, and miR-92a, progressively declined [111, 112], while oncogenic and immune-evasive miRNAs accumulate. These changing miRNA profiles foster a microenvironment that promotes angiogenesis, proliferation, and immune escape [111, 113]. Stromal cell–derived exosomes differ markedly between healthy donors and MM patients, with the latter lacking protective miRNAs like miR-15a, miR-16, and miR-152, instead carrying RNAs that promote tumor survival and therapy resistance [114]. Tumor plasma cells secrete exosomal miRNAs such as miR-21, miR-146a, and miR-182 into BME, which in turn amplify pro-survival signals via IL-6 production and activation of Wnt/PI3K/AKT pathways [15, 115].

Various BME cell subsets act as main collaborators in this exosome-driven crosstalk. TAMs secrete exosomes enriched with miR-223 and miR-365 that drive angiogenesis, immune suppression, and chemoresistance [15]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), released exosomal miRNAs like miR-20a, miR-21, and miR-181 while can be reprogrammed upon receiving tumor-derived exosomal miRNAs such as miR-105 and miR-122, and further promote tumor proliferation and inhibit apoptosis [96, 116]. Similarly, MDSCs expand and exhibit enhanced suppressive capacity through uptake of exosomal miRNAs including miR-21 and miR-155, which activate STAT3 signaling and mitigate T cell function [117]. MM-derived exosomes deliver miR-146a and miR-155 to mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), inducing cytokine secretion that supports MM cell migration. Furthermore, miR-214 downregulation enhances macrophage polarization to the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype via JAK/STAT signaling [81]. Regulatory B cells also increase during disease progression [118], influenced by epigenetic modification driven in part by miR-155, contributing to immune tolerance [81, 119].

Importantly, drug resistance in MM also involves tumor microenvironment interactions, including EV secretion and adhesion mediated by factors such as SORT1 and LAMP2, which impact immunomodulatory drug efficacy [120].

Non-coding RNAs like piRNA-823 contribute epigenetically to tumor progression and poor prognosis by regulating chromatin silencing [121]. The abnormalities of piRNAs, representing 20–30% of exosomal small RNAs, contribute to epigenetic repression in MM by recruiting DNMT3a/3b and silencing tumor suppressors such as p16INK4A [122] .

The accumulation of tumors promoting epigenetic regulators, with loss of tumor suppressive mechanisms, underpins malignant progression and contributes to therapy resistance. Targeting the wide spectrum of epigenetic changes offers a promising therapeutic avenue to disrupt the pathological crosstalk between plasma cells and the BME, ultimately aiming to improve outcomes for patients with MM (Tables 2 and 3).

Epigenetic mechanisms driving drug resistance in MM

Epigenetic dysregulation and miRNA imbalance, particularly involving epigenetic miRNAs (epi-miRNAs), are key drivers of drug resistance across different stages of MM [82]. Our investigations revealed that the epigenetic–miRNA axis tightly controls cellular survival and immune evasion pathways that are predictive of treatment outcome [82]. We have shown that EZH2-mediated histone methylation suppresses anti-tumor miRNAs, such as miR-138 and miR-137, contributing to resistance against proteasome inhibitors and chemotherapeutic agents [97]. EZH2 silences miR-138, resulting in depression of RBPMS, a critical effector promoting proliferation and drug resistance [97]. Similarly, epigenetic silencing of miR-137 induces resistance through upregulation of AURKA, which enhances chromosomal instability and cell-cycle progression [98]. In line with these findings, we observed that pharmacologic targeting of EZH2 restores drug sensitivity and suppresses multiple oncogenic signaling cascades [46].

In addition to histone modifications, we identified multiple miRNA-mediated mechanisms governing drug resistance within the tumor microenvironment. We demonstrated that miR-101-3p overcomes bone-marrow stroma-induced chemoresistance by suppressing BIRC5 (survivin), to induce apoptosis [135]. Furthermore, re-expression of miR-155 targets CD47 and TNFAIP8, promote phagocytosis and apoptosis and effectively reverses drug resistance [81]. We also identified a regulatory link between the p53–miRNA network and therapeutic response, in which p53-dependent miRNAs control apoptotic and DNA-repair targets; disruption of this axis contributes to MM pathogenesis and treatment failure [136]. Moreover, epigenetic regulation of immune-checkpoint components, including PD-L1, contributes to MM immune escape and drug refractoriness [137].

Recent studies further highlight that combined inhibition of EZH2 and DNMT1 disrupts epigenetic gene silencing, reactivating pro-apoptotic and tumor-suppressor genes and sensitizing MM cells to therapy, underscoring significant synergy between these epigenetic regulators [138].

Additionally, the long non-coding RNA PLUM, which is upregulated in relapsed MM, interacts with EZH2 to stabilize and enhance PRC2 complex activity, contributing to increased H3K27 trimethylation and epigenetic silencing of tumor-suppressor genes critical for drug response. Targeting this axis with antisense oligonucleotides restores drug sensitivity [79].

Moreover, DNA hypermethylation alterations can drive treatment resistance following CAR T-cell therapy in MM. Silencing of the target antigen GPRC5D through hypermethylation enables tumor cells to escape immune attack by anti-GPRC5D CAR T cells, leading to relapse and resistance [139].

Therapeutic targeting of these networks through epigenetic enzyme inhibition, miRNA restoration, or modulation of immune-checkpoint expression offers promising strategies to overcome chemoresistance and improve patient outcomes [82] (Tables 2 and 3).

Current and emerging epigenetic therapies, cutting-edge discoveries and technological advances

Epigenetic drugs targeting DNA and histone modifications

Preclinical studies using murine models of MM have provided compelling evidence for the therapeutic potential of epigenetic-based interventions. Traditional murine systems, including syngeneic and xenograft models, have been widely used to investigate disease biology and drug efficacy. However, these models often fail to fully recapitulate the complexity of human MM, particularly with respect to tumor microenvironment interactions. More advanced platforms, such as humanized mouse models incorporating human bone or stromal components, have demonstrated improved capacity to mimic disease progression and therapeutic responses observed in patients. These models provide a more physiologically relevant context for assessing novel agents, especially those targeting microenvironment dependent signaling pathways. Nonetheless, limitations remain, including technical complexity, and incomplete immune system representation, underscoring the need for continued refinement of translational platforms [140].

FDA approved DNA methyltransferase inhibitors (DNMTis) such as 5-azacytidine (AZA) [141] and decitabine (DAC) [142], originally developed for myelodysplastic syndromes, have demonstrated anti-myeloma activity in both in vitro and in vivo models. These agents induce cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis in MM cells, in part through reactivation of tumor suppressor genes such as CDKN2B (p15) and CDKN2A (p16) [143, 144](Table 3).

In murine models of MM, DAC treatment reduces tumor burden, decreases monoclonal antibody secretion, and improves survival without significant toxicity [145]. In addition, DAC modulates transcriptional programs involved in immune regulation and cell-death–related pathways [143]. AZA selectively inhibits proliferation of both drug-sensitive and drug-resistant MM cells [146] and exhibits synergistic anti-myeloma effects when combined with chemotherapeutic agents such as doxorubicin, bortezomib, and dexamethasone [146]. Collectively, these findings support a role for DNMTis as promising components of combinatorial treatment strategies in MM, although further preclinical and clinical investigation is required.

Targeting histone post-translational modifications (PTMs) has also emerged as a key epigenetic strategy in cancer therapy, particularly in MM. Histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACis) are central to this approach. HDACis exert pleiotropic effects essential for MM cell biology and have demonstrated clinical benefit in relapsed MM, with ongoing trials exploring their combination with novel agents to overcome drug resistance (reviewed in [147, 128] ) (Table 3). FDA-approved pan-HDAC inhibitors such as Panobinostat [129], as well as selective HDAC6 inhibitors including WT161 [148], ricolinostat (ACY-1215), and citarinostat [149] employ diverse mechanisms to suppress MM cell growth (Table 3).

Selective inhibition of HDAC6 is particularly promising due to its role in the unfolded protein response and cellular stress pathways that are critical for plasma-cell survival. These inhibitors disrupt aggresome-mediated protein degradation and downregulate oncogenic transcription factors, including c-MYC and IKZF1/3, to promote MM cell death [148].

Chromatin regulators and novel epigenetic targets

Additionally, bromodomain and extra-terminal domain (BET) inhibitors exhibit significant anti-MM activity by suppressing MYC expression [150, 151]. BET inhibitors show efficacy both as monotherapy and in combination, representing novel therapeutic options for MM [152].

Histone methyltransferases are emerging as important epigenetic targets for the management of MM. Small-molecule inhibitors of EZH2 have been developed and show antitumor activity in EZH2-dependent cancers both in vitro and in vivo, including B-cell malignancies. EZH2 blockade reactivates tumor-suppressor programs and represses key myeloma oncogenes, resulting in MM cell death in cell lines and xenograft models [153]. Clinical development of EZH2 inhibitors (e.g., tazemetostat/EPZ-6438) is ongoing across hematologic and solid malignancies (NCT01897571) [154]. Preclinical studies further show that EZH2 inhibitors can act synergistically with proteasome inhibitors and other myeloma drugs to overcome resistance. In this context, several compounds (e.g., GSK2816126) have entered early-phase clinical testing in diverse tumors, including MM (NCT02082977) [155].

Beyond EZH2, inhibitors of G9a/GLP (EHMT2/EHMT1), which catalyze histone H3 lysine 9 dimethylation (H3K9me2), such as A-366, BIX-01294, and UNC-0638, have demonstrated anti-leukemia and anti-myeloma effects in preclinical models [156].

The MMSET (NSD2) methyltransferase represents another promising therapeutic target in MM. shRNA-mediated knockdown of MMSET impairs tumor growth in vitro and in vivo, and the recent development of MMSET inhibitors such as LEM-06 opens new avenues for testing MMSET-directed therapies [157] (Table 3).

Two key histone mono-ubiquitination marks, H2AK119ub1 and H2BK123ub1, regulate DNA repair. Notably, H2AK119ub1, catalyzed by the PRC1 complex, marks transcriptionally repressed chromatin and sites of DNA damage [158, 159] (Table 3).

BMI-1, a core PRC1 subunit, is essential for tumor growth and cancer stem cell maintenance across various cancers, making it an attractive therapeutic target [160]. the selective BMI-1 inhibitor PTC-209 has demonstrated potent anti-myeloma activity by reducing MM cell survival and growth, overcoming stromal cell-mediated drug resistance, and modulating the myeloma microenvironment through inhibition of osteoclast development and reduced osteoblast formation in vitro [161]. PTC-209 also shows synergistic and additive anti-MM effects when combined with pomalidomide and carfilzomib. Experimental data indicate that PTC-209 inhibits BMI-1, leading to decreased PRC1 ubiquitin ligase activity and a global reduction in H2AK119ub1 marks. Furthermore, combination treatments of PTC-209 with EZH2 and BET inhibitors demonstrate synergistic anti-MM effects in vitro [161, 162].

miRNA-based therapeutic strategies in MM

In parallel with advances in experimental models, increasing attention has been directed toward the therapeutic potential of miRNAs in multiple myeloma. Beyond their established diagnostic and prognostic value, miRNAs have been explored as therapeutic agents or targets, with preclinical studies demonstrating that restoration of tumor-suppressive miRNAs or inhibition of oncogenic miRNAs reduces myeloma cell viability, induces apoptosis, and enhances sensitivity to existing therapies [163, 164]. Although clinical translation of miRNA-based therapies remains challenging due to issues related to delivery, stability, and off-target effects, recent advances in nanoparticle-based delivery systems and chemical modifications have improved their translational feasibility [140, 165, 166].

miRNA-based therapeutics, using miRNA mimics to restore tumor-suppressive miRNAs or antagomirs to inhibit oncogenic miRNAs, represent an expanding area in cancer therapy and show promising preclinical activity in MM [99] (Fig. 2). Ectopic expression of tumor-suppressor miRNAs, such as miR-125b, miR-34a, miR-15a, and miR-16, reduces MM cell growth in vitro and suppresses tumor growth in mouse models by targeting oncogenic pathways involving IRF4, BCL2, CDK6, and NOTCH1, and by modulating the bone marrow microenvironment. Conversely, inhibition of oncomiRs (e.g., antagomirs targeting the miR-17-92 cluster or miR-106b-25) also produces anti-myeloma effects [167]. However, translation to the clinic continues to face delivery and stability challenges. Our group and others have identified several epigenetically regulated miRNAs (epi-miRNAs) that represent potential therapeutic targets to overcome drug resistance in MM [46, 82, 84, 96–98]. miRNAs are vulnerable to nucleases and therefore require efficient, tumor-selective delivery. Nevertheless, advances such as locked nucleic acid (LNA) chemistry and improved nanoparticle carriers have enhanced stability and in vivo activity. For example, LNA-based miR-221 inhibitors show improved efficacy and safety in preclinical MM studies [168]. Because individual miRNAs regulate multiple targets and pathways, miRNA therapeutics have the potential to simultaneously disrupt several oncogenic circuits in MM, making them powerful yet technically demanding anti-cancer agents.

In the context of myeloma bone disease, miRNA-based therapies show significant translational promise. Dysregulated miRNAs modulate osteoclast activation, osteoblast suppression, and inflammatory signaling within the BME. In particular, targeting oncogenic miRNAs such as miR-21 not only inhibits MM progression but also interferes with Th17 driven osteoclastogenesis, a key mechanism underlying myeloma associated bone destruction [169]. Furthermore, advances in miRNA delivery and therapeutic design have strengthened the feasibility of translating miRNA based approaches into clinical strategies aimed at preventing or mitigating bone disease in patients with multiple myeloma [170].

Conclusion

The integration of epigenetic alterations with underlying genetic heterogeneity underscores the complex biology and clinical variability of MM. Epigenetic dysregulation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis, progression, and therapeutic resistance of MM. Both global and locus specific alterations in DNA methylation, aberrant histone modifications, and profound imbalances in non-coding RNAs drive disease evolution and foster therapy resistance. The intricate interplay among DNA methylation enzymes, histone modifiers (such as EZH2, MMSET, and HDACs), and non-coding RNAs (including lncRNAs and miRNAs) orchestrate gene expression programs and cellular plasticity, enabling MM cells to adapt and persist within the BME. EVs further serve as potent mediators of intercellular communication within the BME.

The dynamic epigenetic interplay between malignant plasma cells and the BME not only drives tumor progression and immune evasion but also offers novel opportunities for therapeutic intervention. Epigenetic therapies, including DNMT inhibitors, HDAC and HAT inhibitors, bromodomain blockers, and miRNA based strategies, are demonstrating growing clinical potential to overcome resistance and improve patient outcomes, particularly when used in combinatorial regimens. Emerging targets such as EZH2, MMSET, PRMTs, BMI-1, and key non-coding RNAs (including MALAT1, NEAT1, and ANRIL) are further broadening the therapeutic landscape of MM. Further exploration of epigenetic biomarkers, regulatory mechanisms, and their interaction with the microenvironment is critical for advancing precision medicine and developing more effective, individualized strategies for MM management.
