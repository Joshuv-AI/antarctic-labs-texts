Limb-bud and heart (LBH) promotes expansion and survival of esophageal adenocarcinoma cells
Authors: Sydnee Fo, Heng Lu, Lei Chen, Dunfa Peng, Zheng Chen, Wael El-Rifai, Farah Ballout
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEsophageal Adenocarcinoma (EAC) is an aggressive malignancy with poor prognosis. The rising incidence of EAC in the past few decades underscores the need to better understand the molecular features of EAC. The role of the transcriptional co-factor Limb-Bud and Heart (LBH), implicated in embryonic development and Wnt signaling, remains unclearly characterized in gastrointestinal cancers. Given the importance of gastroesophageal reflux disease and acidic bile salts (ABS) exposure in EAC tumorigenesis and progression, we investigated the role of LBH in EAC carcinogenesis.Hypothesis/objectivesWe hypothesized that LBH promotes tumor progression in EAC by enhancing proliferation and survival, particularly under reflux-like conditions.MethodsLBH expression levels were compared between normal and EACs using publicly available databases, patient samples, and cell lines. Functional roles were assessed using siRNA knockdown and overexpression models in EAC cell lines, followed by BrdU incorporation, colony formation, luciferase reporter assays, qRT-PCR, and western blot analysis of pathway markers.ResultsLBH expression was significantly elevated in EAC tissues and cell lines compared to non-neoplastic esophageal tissues. Knockdown of LBH reduced proliferation, viability, and colony formation, while overexpression had opposite effects. BrdU assays confirmed decreased proliferation with knockdown and increased proliferation with overexpression. Mechanistically, LBH upregulated the Wnt signaling pathway. LBH depletion suppressed β-catenin, cyclin D1, and Wnt transcriptional activity, while overexpression induced activation of these pathways. ABS treatment induced β-catenin and cyclin D1 expression and increased Wnt activity, and these effects were diminished with LBH knockdown. At the transcriptional level, LBH depletion decreased mRNA expression of proliferation-associated markers (PCNA, FOXM1, and CCND1), confirming its role in driving tumor growth. Importantly, LBH knockdown also induced apoptosis and suppressed cell survival signaling. Western blot analysis showed decreased BCL-xL and BCL2 levels with increased PARP cleavage. Similarly qRT-PCR showed reduced BCL2 and BCL-xL, and increased BIM, consistent with heightened apoptosis and reduced survival.ConclusionLBH plays an important role in activation of signaling pathways that promote cell proliferation and survival in EAC. These findings warrant further investigation for testing the therapeutic potential of targeting LBH in EAC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12935-026-04404-1.

Introduction

Esophageal carcinoma is a disease in epidemiologic transition. There are two main types of esophageal carcinomas globally: squamous cell carcinoma (SQCC) which remains the most common histological type and adenocarcinoma that has become the leading histological subtype in the Western world [1]. Since the 1970s there has been a significant upturn in the incidence of esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC), for which the most common predisposing factor is the genotoxic effects of acidic bile salts in chronic gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) [2]. Malignant transformation during EAC carcinogenesis is associated with signaling effects of chronic reflux, leading to a sequence of histopathological changes: from normal specialized intestinal metaplasia, known as Barrett’s esophagus (BE) to low- and high-grade dysplasia toward invasive adenocarcinoma [3]. EAC patients’ frequently present with advanced disease at diagnosis (stage 3 or 4) with a 5-year overall survival rate at approximately 20% which drops close to 5% in patients with advanced stages [4]. Therefore, a better understanding of the biological and molecular characteristics of EAC is essential for improving diagnostic accuracy, prognostic assessment, and the development of effective therapies for this lethal disease.

The limb-bud and heart (LBH) gene is a highly conserved transcription co-factor in vertebrates implicated in embryonic development [5]. Growing evidence suggests that embryonic development and tumorigenesis have similar molecular mechanisms. Although LBH’s function in adult tissues is not well understood, its structure presents a high dgreee of disorder, suggesting that this conformational plasticity might affect how LBH regulates transcriptional activity. Multi-cancer analysis reveals universal association of oncogenic LBH expression with DNA hypomethylation and Wnt [6]. It was previously observed that LBH acts as a target gene in the Wnt pathway to inhibit mammary epithelial differentiation and promote Wnt- induced tumorigenesis [7]. LBH overexpression in several cancer types correlates with poor prognosis.

In this study, we investigated the role of LBH in EAC under conditions mimicking GERD and assessed its functional role in various models. Our results indicate that LBH mediates proliferation and survival of EAC cell lines. This effect was mediated by Wnt/beta catenin and cyclin D1. In addition, we showed that knocking down LBH induced apoptotic cell death in EAC cells. Taken together, these findings suggest that LBH plays an important role in promoting EAC tumorigenesis and cancer cell survival.

Methods

The key resources for reagents, kits, and primer sequences can be found in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Cell culture

FLO-1 and OE33 cells were a gift from Dr. David Beer from the University of Michigan. The SK-GT-4 cell line was provided by Dr. Xiaochun Xu (MD Anderson, Houston, TX). OE33 cell line was maintained in RPMI 1640 media (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID: SCR_008452, Waltham, MA USA, cat. No. 11875093) complemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID: SCR_008452, cat. No. A3160402) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID: SCR_008452, cat. No. 15070063). OE33 cells were treated with ABS for 20 min followed by a 3-hour recovery period in RPMI medium. The FLO-1 and SK-GT-4 cell lines were maintained in DMEM media (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID: SCR_008452, Waltham, MA USA, cat. No. 11995073) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were grown at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% carbon dioxide. Cells are tested monthly for Mycoplasma contamination using a PCR-based detection kit (SouthernBiotech, RRID: SCR_019250). Cell line identity was routinely verified every six months through Labcorp’s Cell Line Authentication Services, with the most recent authentication performed on August 27,2025.

Acidic bile salts treatment

Bile salts cocktail was prepared by mixing equivalent amounts of glycocholic acid (GCA), taurocholic acid (TCA), glycodeoxycholic GDCA), glycohenodeoxycholic acid (DCDCA) and deoxycholic acid (DCA). Cells were treated with 200 micromolar of bile salts in acidic culture media (pH 4.0) for 20 min followed by 3 h recovery in regular media.

Protein extractions

RIPA lysis buffer (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, RRID: SCR_008987; sc-24948) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors was used for protein extraction. After homogenization, the samples was incubated on ice for 10–15 minutes with intermittent vortexing to facilitate complete lysis. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 mintes at 4 °C to remove cellular debris and unbroken cells. The resulting supernatant, which contains the solubilized proteins, was carefully transferred to a clean microcentrifuge tube. Four fold (4x) LDS buffer was added to the tube, vortex, and set to boil at 85 °C for 10 minutes. Protein concentration was determined using a protein quantification assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Western blot analysis

The protein concentration was quantified using the DC Protein Assay (Bio Rad) following the manufacturer’s protocol (Bio- Rad Laboratories, RRID: SCR_008987, CA, USA). Cells were previously lysed using RIPA lysis buffer and an equal amounts of protein lysate, prepared by adding 4x Laemmli sample buffer and heating at 85 °C for 10 min, were separated by 10% SDS PAGE for 90 min at 100 V and then transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (Bio-Rad laboratories, RRID: SCR_008426, CA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Following primary antibody incubation, membranes were then washed three times with 1X TBST and incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Target proteins were visualized using the Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID: SCR_008452) and chemiluminescent images were captured and analyzed with the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, RRID: SCR_008426, CA, USA).

Quantitative real- time RT-PCR analysis

According to the manufacturer’s protocol, total RNA was extracted using RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat No.74104). The iScript cDNA synthesis kit (BioRad) was used for cDNA synthesis. Primers were designed using Primer3 online software (http://bioinfo.ut.ee/primer3-0.4.0/primers/) and synthesized by Integrated DNA technologies. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction were performed using an iCycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, RRID: SCR_008426). All reactions were preformed in triplicate, and the expression was calculated and normalized the CT value of the housekeeping gene (HPRT). The primer sequences are provided in supplementary Table 1.

Transfection

For transient knockdown of LBH, si-Ctrl or si-LBH was transfected into cells using LipoJet reagent purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (RRID: SCR_008987, CA, USA; sc-29470). For transient overexpression of LBH, a mammalian expression plasmid or empty vector was transfected into cells using PolyJet reagent. LBH si-RNAs were obtained from Dharmacon (L-020067-00-0005; Lafayette, CO, USA) and Thermo Fisher Scientific (RRID: SCR_0008452; Catalog# 1299001).

Annexin V apoptosis

Annexin V apoptosis detection Kit (BD Pharmingen) was used for apoptosis analysis. Briefly, OE33 and SK-GT-4 cells were seeded in 6 well plates, followed by transfection with control and LBH siRNA the next day (concentration for OE33 40nM and SK-GT-4 80nM). Cells were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 1X binding buffer then transferred to a 5 mL flow tube. Cells were then stained with annexin-V and Sytox red, gently vortexed and incubated in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. Apoptotic cells were analyzed with florescence-activated cell sorting using a flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson).

BrdU assay

BrdU assay was performed according to the manufacturers’ protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID: SCR_008452, Waltham, MA USA, cat. No. B23151). Briefly, 200 mg of BrdU was dissolved in 32.5 mL of anhydrous DMSO, and 10 uL of this solution was diluted in 10 mL of tissue culture medium. The cultured cells were labelled with BrdU labeling solution and incubated at 37 degrees Celsius for 2 hours. After incubation the labelling solution was removed and washed twice with PBS. Cells were then with 3.7% formaldehyde in PBS for 15 minutes at room temperature, followed by 3 washes for 2 min with PBS. The PBS was removed and 1 mL of Triton X-100 permeabilization buffer was added to each well then incubated for 20 min at room temperature. The permeabilization buffer was removed and 1 mL of 1 N HCL was added and incubated on ice for 10 min. 1mL of 2 N HCL was then added and incubated at room temperature for 10 minutes. 1 mL phosphate/citric acid bufferpH7.4 was added and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Finally, the wells were washed 3 times with Triton X-100 permeabilization buffer (2 min per wash). Slides were mounted and images were obtained using confocal microscopy.

ATP Glo

Cells were transfected with either si-Ctrl/si-LBH or vector/LBH mammalian expression plasmid. Cells were split 24 h after transfection and seeded into 96 well plates at a density of 1,000 cells per well. Cell viability was measured using the CellTiter-Glo Cell Viability Assay (Promega, RRID: SCR 006724, Madison, WI, USA) and changes in absorbance were recorded in a FluolarStar luminescence microplate reader (BMG Labtech, RRIC: SCR 025024, Orthenberg, Germany).

Colony assay

OE33 and SK-GT-4 cells were transfected with control siRNA or si-LBH. Forty-eight hours after transfection, cells were seeded at a density of 1000 cells/well in 6-well plates and cultured at 37 °C for another two weeks. Each experiment was set up in triplicates. Cells were stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution and images were analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.53k, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Luciferase reporter assay

Cells were transfected with either si-Ctrl or si-LBH using Lipojet Transfecting reagent and with empty vector or LBH overexpressing vector using Polyjet DNA transfecting agent. The next day, cells were co-transfected with 1 µg each of the pTOP-FLASH (Tcf/Lef reporter) and pFOP-FLASH (mutated Tcf/Lef binding site) plasmids (Addgene) using polyjet DNA transfecting agent. pTOP/pFOP are a pair of luciferase reporter plasmids designed to assess Wnt/β-catenin signaling activity. The pTOP (or TOPflash) plasmid contains wild-type binding sites for the TCF/Lef transcription binding sites that drive luciferase expression in proportion to pathway activation. In contrast, the pFOP (or FOPflash) plasmid carries mutated TCF/Lef binding sites and serves as a negative control, reflecting nonspecific or background luminescence [8]. Cells were treated with acidic bile salts (200 μm; pH 4) for 20 min and allowed recovery for 3 h in complete medium forty-eight hours after transfection. Cells were lysed with 1X luciferase passive lysis buffer and luciferase activity was measured on a FLUOstar OPTIMA microplate reader (BMG LABTECH, Cary, NC) after adding the luciferase regent (Pormega). Luciferase activity was determined by by calculating the TOP/FOP ratio.

Bioinformatics analysis of public databases

GSE13898 public database of EAC patients was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (RRID: SCR_005012; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds). Bioinformatics analysis of GSE13898 database (64 EAC, 15 BE, and 28 NE) was performed in the R environment (version 4.1.3). Boxplots were drawn using R software.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Prism, RRID: SCR_002798, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons between two independent groups were assessed using Student’s T-test, while differences among three or more groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc correction. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Unless otherwise stated, all biochemical experiment were performed in triplicate.

Results

LBH is upregulated in EAC

We analyzed LBH expression in EAC compared to normal samples in public datasets. Analysis of the GEO dataset, GSE138898, that contains both normal esophagus (NE) and pre-neoplastic BE samples, showed that LBH mRNA expression is significantly higher in EAC tissues compared to NE and BE samples (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1A). To validate these results, we analyzed LBH protein expression in cell lines representing non-neoplastic NE (Het1A), non-dysplastic BE (BAR10T, BART, CPA), BE with high-grade dysplasia (CPB), and EAC (SK-GT-4, FLO-1, OE33, OE19) cell lines. Western blot analysis showed that LBH is more abundant in the high-grade dysplasia- and EAC-derived cell lines than in the NE and non-dysplastic BE cell lines (Fig. 1B). Consistently, LBH expression was markedly increased in a panel of de-identified human EAC tissues when compared to their matched normal tissues (Fig. 1C). The transcriptomic data aligned with protein level findings suggest that LBH expression is upregulated in dysplastic and neoplastic EAC tissue samples.

LBH promotes proliferation of EAC cells

After showing that LBH is upregulated in EAC, we investigated its role in cell growth, proliferation, and viability. Using a colony formation assay, the LBH knock down in EAC cell lines significantly suppressed the colony formation ability (Fig. 2A and B) and decreased the cell viability of OE33 and SK4 cancer cells, as measured by the CellTiter ATP-Glo assay (Fig. 2C and D). Conversely, overexpression of LBH in FLO1 cells, which express relatively low endogenous LBH, significantly increased cell viability (Fig. 2E). Taken together, these findings indicate that LBH promotes proliferation and viability of EAC cells. Furthermore, the knockdown of LBH decreased the expression of Cyclin D1, a proliferation marker, and overexpression induced its upregulation (Fig. 2F). Because LBH can act as a target gene for the WNT pathway [5], we used the pTOP-FLASH/pFOP-FLASH reporter assay to measure the Wnt/β-catenin transcriptional activity. LBH knockdown significantly reduced the Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation, while overexpression reversed this effect (Fig. 2G and H). Taken together, these results suggest that LBH regulates proliferation and survival of EAC cells, possibly through Wnt/β-catenin signaling.

ABS exposure induces LBH expression

To determine whether LBH is induced by ABS in reflux conditions, we used an ABS cocktailtreatment to mimic GERD conditions observed in patients. Cells were transiently exposed to ABS (200 μm, pH 4.0) for 20 min followed by recovery in complete medium for 3 hours. Western blots analysis showed a consistent increase in the protein levels of LBH in BE and EAC cell lines (Fig. 3A). Immunofluorescence staining of LBH in OE33 cells with or without ABS treatment further confirmed the induction of LBH under reflux conditions (Fig. 3B). Moreover, exposure to ABS induced β-catenin phosphorylation at Ser552, an activating phosphorylation site that triggers β-catenin nuclear translocation and signaling [9], and cyclin D1 expression in OE33 and SK4 cells; however, this induction was lost with the knockdown of LBH (Fig. 3C and D). Immunofluoresence staining of phosphorylated β-catenin (Ser552) in OE33 cells showed high expression of nuclear β-catenin under ABS conditions which was lost with LBH knockdown (Fig. 3E). Similarly, induction of Wnt/β-catenin transcriptional activity was dependent on LBH expression in response to ABS exposure (Fig. 3F). BrdU incorporation assays in OE33 and FLO-1 cells showed a significant decrease in proliferative capacity following LBH knockdown (Fig. 4A). Conversely, LBH overexpression enhanced proliferation (Fig. 4B). This was further confirmed by analyzing FOXM1 and CDK1 protein levels (Fig. 4C and D) and by measuring the mRNA expression of the proliferation markers PCNA, FOXM1, and CCND1, which showed reduced expression following knockdown of LBH (Fig. 4E and F).

LBH silencing in EAC cells induces apoptotic cell death

We next investigated the impact of LBH on the survival of EAC cells by measuring apoptotic cell death. Annexin V staining showed that LBH knockdown induced a significant increase in apoptotic cell death in OE33 and SK4 cells. This effect was also observed in response to ABS, denoting the role of LBH in protecting against the cytotoxic effects of ABS (Fig. 5A and C). In line with these results, LBH knockdown was associated with PARP cleavage, upregulation of BIM and a reduction in BCL-2 and BCL-XL protein levels, which was prominent under ABS conditions (Fig. 5B and D). Similarly, qRT-PCR analysis showed a reduced expression of the antiapoptotic genes BCL-2 and BCL-XL and increased expression of the proapoptotic gene BIM, following LBH knockdown in OE33 and SK4 cells (Fig. 5E and F). Together, these findings indicate that LBH silencing promotes apoptotic cell death.

Discussion

This study focused on identifying the expression and molecular function of LBH in EAC. Although it was first discovered in vertebrate embryogenesis, LBH has received increasing attention in cancer research. It been reported that similar signaling mechanisms may coexist during embryonic development and tumorigenesis [10].

In this study, we demonstrate that LBH is overexpressed in EAC cell lines and human tissues. We also report induction of LBH in response to reflux conditions and a significant role for LBH in activating cancer cell proliferation and survival. Consistant with our findings, LBH has been shown to act as an oncogenic driver in many cancers. LBH was shown to be highly expressed in Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and associated with stem-like, metastatic phenotypes [11]. LBH was reported to drives proliferation and invasion through activation of PI3K-AKT and integrin/FAK/Akt pathways in Gastric and hepatocellular carcinomas [12]. In addition, LBH overexpression has also been correlated with WNT/Integrin pathway activation and poor outcomes in colorectal, pancreatic, liver, bladder, and brain cancers [6]. Together, our results expand the current knowledge about LBH and supports its role in promoting the growth and survival of EAC cells.

After establishing the biological role of LBH in EAC, we explored its molecular function within these cells. A study by Lindley et al. showed that LBH inhibits the proliferation of CNE1 cells in nasopharyngeal carcinomas by causing G1/S arrest [13]. In breast cancer, LBH is considered an oncogene directly regulated by the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and LBH overexpression leads to a more aggressive basal differentiation of breast cancer [14]. Yu et al. demonstrated that LBH promotes the proliferation and invasion of gastrointestinal carcinomas by upregulating the Integrin/FAK/Akt pathway [5]. In our study, we found that LBH positively regulated cell growth possibly through regulation of CDK1, FOXM1, and cyclin D1. Cyclin D1 is, a key cell cycle regulator that plays a major role in cancer cell proliferation and tumor growth in several cancer types, including breast cancer, colon, and lung by regulating the cell cycle and exerting oncogenic functions [15]. Cyclin D1 acts as a sensor for growth factors and other mitogenic signals, translating them into a cellular proliferate response. The high expression of cyclin D1 drives unchecked cellular proliferation promoting tumor growth, and playing a central role in the pathogenesis of cancer [16]. Our results suggest that LBH promotes the proliferative capacity of cancer cells, possibly by supporting cell cycle progression via cyclin D1.

LBH acts as a target gene for the WNT pathway to inhibit mammary epithelial differentiation and promote WNT induced tumorigenesis [7]. LBH overexpression in aggressive basal-like breast cancers correlated with elevated WNT pathway activity [7]. Additional studies have demonstrated that LBH is co-expressed with components of the Focal Adhesion (FAK)/Integrin signaling pathway in gastric cancer [5, 12], where it can modulate Integrin expression in both activating and inhibitory ways [5, 17]. Importantly, gene signatures reflecting LBH–WNT–Integrin co-expression strongly predict poor patient survival in colorectal, gastric, esophageal, and pancreatic cancers [6]. This positive association between LBH and the WNT and Integrin/Focal Adhesion pathways indicates functional linkage. Multiple mechanisms have been described through which Integrins can initiate WNT signaling [18], while WNT signaling can, in turn, activate Integrin/Focal Adhesion cascades [19]. Genes that function within the same mechanistic network often display coordinated expression and mutual regulation. Considering that LBH is a direct WNT/β-catenin target during epithelial development and in many cancers [6], and that it enhances FAK–PI3K–AKT signaling in gastric cancer models [5], LBH likely mediates crosstalk between the WNT and integrin signaling pathways and might actually regulate WNT/ β-catenin expression. Here we show that LBH knockdown reduced β-catenin activation, while overexpression reversed this effect. The pTOP/pFOP reporter assay, commonly used as a measure of the WNT/β-catenin/TCF transcription complex activity [20], showed LBH-dependent activation of the transcription activity. Aberrant activation of this pathways leads to the accumulation of β-catenin promoting transcription of many oncogenes. Notably, cyclin D1 is known as a direct transcription target of β-catenin in cancer [21]. While direct negative feedback of LBH on the WNT pathway has not been clearly established, our results show that LBH’s regulation of cell fate and proliferation may indirectly shape WNT pathway activity.

The prevalence of EAC continues to increase rapidly despite advances in therapy. The etiology of EAC remains unclear but has been linked to a variety of risk factors, with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) being considered a major risk factor for the development of BE and its progression to EAC. Esophageal inflammation and injury caused by GERD induce cytokine signaling and cell proliferation, leading to accelerated EAC development [22]. We observed a consistent upregulation of LBH protein levels across EAC cell lines upon exposure to ABS, which mimics GERD conditions, suggesting that LBH is responsive to reflux-linked stimuli. Importantly, ABS exposure also induced LBH-dependent expression of β-catenin and cyclin D1 with increased Wnt/β-catenin transcriptional activity. LBH knockdown reversed these effects, implying its requirement for activation of Wnt signaling under reflux conditions.

After evaluating the role of LBH in cell proliferation through the Wnt pathway, we further explored the effect of LBH on EAC cells by measuring programed cell death. Annexin V staining revealed that silencing LBH markedly increased apoptosis, both under basal conditions and following ABS treatment. This indicates that LBH plays a role in cancer cell survival, and its depletion sensitizes EAC cells to programmed cell death, especially under reflux-like stress conditions. We further confirmed apoptosis on the protein level by assessing key markers including PARP, a nuclear enzyme that is activated in response to DNA damage to promote repair or induce apoptosis [23]. When it is cleaved it indicates that the cell is undergoing apoptosis. BCL-2 family of proteins (BCL2 and BCL-xL) that function mainly as anti-apoptotic factors thus promoting cell survival [24]. The knockdown of LBH suppressed the anti-apoptotic markers, BCL2 and BCL-xL, and induced apoptotic markers such as BIM and PARP cleavage. This effect was particularly pronounced in cells exposed to ABS, contributing to the maintenance of cell survival during reflux-associated stress. Collectively, these findings support the role of LBH in promoting the survival of cancerous cells.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides insight into the biological, functional, and mechanistic roles of LBH in EAC cells proliferation and survival, demonstrating its role in activation of b-catenin and anti-apoptotic signaling, protecting against the genotoxic lethal effects of reflux conditions in EAC. Understanding these interactions may uncover novel therapeutic targets, as disrupting LBH-mediated transcriptional programs could impair the survival advantage conferred on EAC cells under reflux-associated stress.
