Management of proximal humerus fractures: a review of contemporary treatment options
Authors: Erick M. Marigi, Cristobal E. Lopez, Ian M. Marigi, Micah Nieboer, Jenni Tangtiphaiboontana, Elizabeth P. Wellings, Rodrigo de Marinis, Jonathan D. Barlow, Joaquin Sanchez-Sotelo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proximal humerus fractures are frequent injuries, representing the third most common fracture in geriatric patients. Due to the variety of fracture patterns, patient profiles, and subspecialty training of treating physicians, management of these fractures continues to garner debate. Nonoperative treatment remains the mainstay for most fractures; however, operative treatment has increased over the last decade. The purpose of this article is to provide a contemporary review of the clinical evaluation, classification, and management of proximal humerus fractures.

Proximal humerus fractures (PHFs) are frequent, with a reported incidence of 161 per 100,000 people and an estimated 2,750,000 fractures per year in the United States by 2030.30 They are the third most common fracture in elderly patients, typically occurring in the context of osteoporosis.11,31 As such, these fractures are typically seen among individuals over 60 years of age with a female to male ratio of 2:1.8,9,30

Unlike other fractures associated with osteoporosis, PHFs often affect older adults and can truly affect subsequent independence following the injury.8,30 Common complications of PHFs include posttraumatic stiffness, avascular necrosis (AVN), post-traumatic arthritis, malunion, or nonunion, and often resulting in chronic pain and functional limitations.6 Not uncommonly, patients are affected by more than one complication which can have compounding effects. As a whole, PHFs result in an extended recovery period and substantial economic burden not only for patients, but also for the healthcare system.23 As such, ongoing efforts are being made to standardize the evaluation and management of these fractures.29,45,58

Due to the wide range of possible fracture patterns and the diversity in patient profiles, treatment remains a topic of ongoing debate and controversy.29,41,58 Although most of these fractures in older adults are managed nonoperatively, there has been a recent increase in the indication for surgery.50,58 Examples include utilizing intramedullary nailing for fractures extending into the humeral shaft and use of the reverse total shoulder arthroplasty for complex fracture patterns.27 Moreover, when surgical management is selected, patient outcomes can differ substantially depending on the characteristics of the fracture and the surgery performed.37,41,43,62 Therefore, multiple attempts have been sought to achieve consensus, such as new classifications,17 consensus studies,29,58 and higher level clinical studies. The objective of this review is to summarize our current approach and management of PHFs based on the best available evidence.

Evaluation

History

As most patients with PHFs are geriatric, assessing their prefracture functional status and identifying comorbidities that may pose a barrier to surgery and rehabilitation are key. This begins with an evaluation of the patient's hand-dominance and functionality based on activities of daily living and prior use of assistive devices. Given its strong association with these fractures, the comorbidity of primary or secondary osteoporosis should also be investigated and properly managed.30 For younger patients, sports participation and occupation represents another important factor to account for when deciding the most appropriate treatment alternative. Alcohol, tobacco, and ilicit substance use should be queried for risk stratification.

Physical examination

During the physical examination, concomitant injuries should be assessed, particularly other osteoporotic fractures (hip, distal radius, or vertebral).30 Once other injuries have been ruled out, a targeted assessment of the affected limb should be carried out. Obvious deformities may indicate an associated dislocation or diaphyseal involvement. Although neurovascular injuries are more commonly associated with dislocated PHFs, a neurological assessment should always be conducted, especially for sensory and motor compromise of the axillary nerve and brachial plexus.28 While a thorough examination can be limited by pain, assessing sensation on the lateral deltoid region and extension of the shoulder against gentle resistance is typically tolerated. A distal examination of motor and sensory function including finger, wrist, and if possible, elbow function is important to assess. Typically, the evaluation of shoulder function and range of motion is not routinely performed due to pain at the initial encounter.28 At 2-3 weeks from injury, pain and swelling have typically decreased and some patients may have early callus and stability at the fracture site to tolerate further shoulder physical examination with less pain.

When assessing vascular status, it is important to acknowledge that the presence of a distal pulse does not exclude an associated vascular injury.19,56 Therefore, if there is a high clinical suspicion additional tests in the form of a brachial-brachial index or computed tomography angiography scan should be considered even in the presence of symmetric distal pulses. Clinical indicators of possible vascular involvement include a very medialized shaft fragment, an expanding hematoma, refractory hypotension without an evident cause, and concurrent brachial plexus injury.19,56

Diagnostic imaging

The initial imaging study consists of shoulder radiographs in 3 views: true anteroposterior (Grashey), scapular axial (Y-view), and Velpeau axillary.51 A traditional axillary view is not recommended, as it requires abduction, which will cause pain and carries the potential to displace the fracture. Initial imaging is typically obtained in the emergency room in a supine position which can often exaggerate the fracture deformity and displacement. Therefore, subsequent x-rays obtained in follow-up should be done in an upright or standing position to best determine the position of the fractures during the healing process.

The radiographic assessment should evaluate articular congruence (dislocation or subluxation), intra-articular involvement, translation of the humeral head to the shaft, angulation patterns (varus-valgus), the presence of associated comminution, and any displacement of the fracture. Additionally, it is important to identify possible associated glenoid fractures as well as pre-existing conditions such as osteoarthritis or rotator cuff arthropathy. In fractures with greater tuberosity displacement, the potential for impingement should be evaluated on both the anteroposterior view (for subacromial impingement) and the Velpeau view (for posterior glenoid rim impingement).51 It is important to remember that most of the times, radiographs are obtained in internal rotation (in the context of sling use), which tend to overestimate valgus.21

Considering the aforementioned variability inherent to radiographic evaluation, the use of computed tomography (CT) with 3 dimension (3D) reconstruction is extremely useful in the evaluation of PHF.58 This is particularly true in complex fractures, as it allows for a more accurate assessment of the pattern, aids in decision-making, and in cases requiring surgical intervention, it facilitates planning and execution.58

A recent Delphi study conducted to evaluate consensus on the management of PHFs, evaluated (among other variables) surgeon's agreement regarding obtaining 3D CT images.58 The level of consensus on the utility of 3D CT reconstruction was good, including its value for surgical planning, displacement severity, and detailed assessment of humeral head and tuberosity involvement. Of note, 81% of the participants chose not to complement their evaluation with CT in elderly patients with comorbidities presenting with a 2-part varus displaced fracture, for which most of them agreed to treat nonoperatively.58

Classification

The classification system developed by Neer continues to be commonly used due to the relative ease to classify fractures on plain radiographs and familiarity amongst treating clinicians.44 This system anatomically divides the proximal humerus into 4 parts: the humeral head, greater tuberosity (GT), lesser tuberosity (LT), and humeral shaft. Fractures are classified based on the number of fragments that define separate parts and to consider a part as involved, there must be a displacement of at least 1 cm or an angulation of 45°.44 A later clarification on his own classification system published by Neer highlighted the arbitrary nature of these criteria.44 Later studies evaluating Neer classification performance found a worrisome intra- and interobserver reliability.5,24

There are multiple limitations associated with the Neer classification.7,24,25,33 Although it is the most known, it was not originally designed to guide therapeutic decision-making, and displacement criteria were set arbitrarily, as previously mentioned.44 Additionally, the original schematic fracture patterns do not always correspond to the morphology observed in PHFs in practice, as demonstrated in a morphologic analysis by Hasan et al.20 In their study, these authors analyzed PHF patterns in 3D CT scans and concluded that these fractures tend to follow repetitive patterns related to rotator cuff insertions. Furthermore, they found that the GT does not behave as a single large fragment but rather tends to divide into 2 parts corresponding to the supraspinatus and infraspinatus insertions.20 Additionally, the study identified a high incidence of intra-articular fractures, though with a low rate of humeral head involvement (typically articular fragments associated with tuberosity fractures), as well as a low likelihood of fractures at the bicipital groove due to its high bone density.20

The Hertel classification,22 later modified to the head, greater tuberosity, lesser tuberosity, and shaft classification,53 is a purely descriptive system emphasizing the vascularization of the humeral head to predict AVN, and was not designed for therapeutic decision.22 In terms of agreement, variable performance has been reported, from moderate to good interobserver agreement.36,53 The Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen/Orthopedic Trauma Association classification incorporates both the division into parts and the presence of intra-articular involvement.42 This is useful as it uses consistent terminology and principles to other fractures classified by Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen/Orthopedic Trauma Association. One study reported moderate inter-observer correlation, with a Kappa index of 0.57 to 0.59.57 However, like other classifications, it is not specifically designed to guide management.25,33,42

Mayo-Fundación Jiménez Díaz (Mayo-FJD) classification

The Mayo-Fundación Jiménez Díaz (Mayo-FJD) classification is based on fracture pattern recognition, introducing a new morphologic approach to PHFs.14,17 This classification has been reported to provide almost perfect intraobserver and interobserver agreement using both radiographs and CT scan.17 This classification categorizes PHFs into 3 main groups: isolated tuberosity fractures (lesser or greater); fractures with severe involvement of the humeral head due to impaction, split, or fracture-dislocation; and fractures in which the humeral head remains largely intact but is fractured at the anatomical or surgical neck.17 These 3 groups encompass 7 distinct patterns. Additionally, in cases where there is involvement of the humeral head or surgical neck, the tuberosities may remain intact, have a single fractured tuberosity, or involve both tuberosities.17

Some limitations of the classification include the preference for a CT for complete evaluation of the fracture pattern. Additionally, there are limited data correlating the fracture patterns to long term outcomes. There is one retrospective cohort study by Tafoya-Arreguín et al55 where 149 patients with PHFs initially managed nonsurgically were followed for a minimum of 24-months. They observed that type 5 fractures (valgus impacted) showed the best outcomes (Constant 76.7), while type 9 (disengaged neck) represented the worst results (University of California Los Angeles 9.9) and a 100% conversion rate to reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA). Furthermore, on multivariate analysis, they noted that the Mayo-FJD fracture type was the strongest independent predictor of functional outcome (P < .0001). Therefore, the authors concluded that the Mayo-FJD classification can serve as reliable predictor of both functional outcomes and the necessity for conversion surgery.55 At the present moment, the authors are in the process of building on the existing literature aiming to correlate long term clinical outcomes to fracture types in order to create more utility of the classification.

Surgical neck (SN) fractures

In these cases, the primary fracture line is located at the surgical neck, and the humeral head often maintains its anatomical axial orientation with the glenoid. The tuberosities may or may not be involved. These fractures carry a high risk of nonunion when there is complete lack of contact between the diaphysis and the remaining proximal humerus (5 – 10%), making them the PHFs with the highest incidence of nonunion.46 Additional risk factors for nonunion include smoking, malnutrition, immunosuppression, and fractures through pathologic bone.17

Isolated fracture of the tuberosities

These fractures are usually, though not exclusively, secondary to glenohumeral dislocation, with the GT typically affected in anterior dislocations and the LT in posterior dislocations. Less frequently, fractures of the GT can be seen as a result of a direct impact to the proximal humerus. When resulting from a dislocation without other associated fracture lines, they are classified as tuberosity fractures, and displacement is assessed after reduction. Management depends on the degree of displacement, considering that these fractures can be functionally equivalent to a rotator cuff tear. Malunion of these fractures can restrict range of motion, particularly in cases of superior displacement (due to subacromial impingement) and medial displacement (due to shortening of the rotator cuff's functional length).14

Varus posteromedial (VPM) fractures

These fractures involve comminution and collapse of the posteromedial calcar. This fracture pattern results in caudal and medial displacement (varus) along with posterior inclination (retroversion) of the humeral head. One or both tuberosities may be fractured, with the GT typically displacing medially and posteriorly.14 Due to impaction at the fracture site, nonunion is less common; however, malunion can lead to a reduced range of motion.14,17

Valgus (VL) fractures

In these cases, the humeral head is impacted laterally, leading to fragmentation of the tuberosities. Typically, the GT fractures into 2 fragments: the anterior fragment remains continuous with the LT, while the other fragment displaces posteriorly. The impaction of the humeral head results in significant loss of metaphyseal bone stock laterally, particularly in osteoporotic patients. This is especially relevant in surgically treated cases, as the bone defect may present a challenge after fracture reduction. These fractures have a higher risk of malunion and nonunion due to their inherent instability, a risk that further increases if there is also involvement of the medial periosteal hinge.17 AVN is seen more frequently in this type of fractures despite nonoperative or surgical treatment.14,16

Humeral head fractures

This group includes 3 fracture patterns. If there is a fracture of the humeral head with diaphyseal dissociation and dislocation, it is classified as head dislocation (HD). If the humeral head is impacted by at least 40%, it is categorized as head impaction (HI). If the head is fractured into 2 or more parts, it is classified as head split (HS). As in previous cases, the tuberosities may or may not be involved.

This group is generally associated with a poor prognosis without surgery, with a high risk of residual instability, glenohumeral maltracking, and early post-traumatic arthritis.17 Most require surgical management, with a tendency toward rTSA due to its more predictable outcomes.28,33,43

Based on findings of Foruria et al, categorizing fractures with a complete loss of contact between the humeral head segment and the diaphysis can be challenging, as they could be classified as SN, VPM, or VL fractures.17 To address this issue, the disengaged neck (DN) fracture category was introduced. With this modification, the authors also developed a new framework that provides a therapeutic purpose to the classification.17 They determined that surgery should be considered in cases of HS, HI, HD, and DN, while in other cases—GT, LT, SN, VPM, and VL—the indication for surgery will depend on the degree of displacement.17

Management of PHFs

Much controversy has surrounded the treatment of PHFs in recent years due to new evidence on how to approach evaluation, rehabilitation protocols, and surgical techniques.29,41,58 Consequently, new management approaches have been sought, including updated classifications such as the previously mentioned Mayo-FJD; however, further studies will be necessary to determine the clinical impact of these strategies.

Although conservative management can lead to good results in a considerable number of cases, surgery is key to avoid certain complications mentioned above derived from specific fracture patterns. Moreover, studies with many patients comparing treatments and showing similar outcomes present various shortcomings. For example, Rangan et al (PROximal Fracture of Humerus Evaluation by Randomisation - trial Number 2 [PROFHER trial]) and Launonen et al have many methodologic selection bias related to case complexity and selection bias.32,47 The PROFHER study excluded nearly 80% of the initially collected cases, and did not include cases managed with rTSA, which highlights the need to consider their results with caution.47 The authors aim to address these limitations with the PROFHER-2 randomized controlled trial that compares the clinical and cost-effectiveness of rTSA vs. hemiarthroplasty vs. nonoperative treatment in patients with 3- and 4-part PHF. This study would likely provide major clinical insight into the treatment of these injuries and help guide shared decision making with patients.48

Indications for surgical treatment are heterogeneous across the literature probably due to the diversity of fracture types and patients. Such complexity has not been fully captured by the available classification methods and operative management criteria. This was reflected in a Delphi consensus, where agreement was reached in certain specific areas, but many discrepancies persisted.58 Nevertheless, there are fracture characteristics that can guide the choice of treatment, considering the advantages and disadvantages of each option.

Nonoperative treatment

The vast majority of PHFs cases are amenable to nonoperative treatment, which would be recommended in up to 85% of cases according to one study.2 However, it is important to note that, in the United States, Sabesan et al found that the proportion of patients treated conservatively decreased from 65% to 59% between 2004 and 2012.50 This might be due to refined surgical techniques and continual improvements of implants such as locked plating, intramedullary nails, and the rTSA.27 In general, nonsurgical treatment is preferred for fractures without clear surgical indication, or in patients with multiple comorbidities, low functionality, or advanced age. Favorable outcomes have been reported in terms of residual pain, as assessed by the visual analog scale (67% without pain, 27% with scores between 3 and 6, and 6% with scores above 6), and nonunion rates ranging from 3% to 10% (higher in SN fractures).14

The mainstay of nonoperative treatment consists of immobilization of the arm in a neutral position or external rotation for 3-6 weeks. The type of immobilization device recommended varies between a simple sling, with or without arm restriction, and a shoulder immobilizer in external rotation. Although there is no consensus on the optimal type of immobilization, use of a conventional sling with the hand resting on the abdomen may lead to healing with the humeral shaft in internal rotation and/or reduced contact between the humeral head and the GT.26,51 This can be avoided by using a shoulder immobilizer in neutral or slight external rotation.26,51 Jin et al conducted a cadaveric study evaluating the effect of immobilization position and the forces involved in SN and GT fractures.26 In SN fractures, the deforming force exerted by the pectoralis major, which pulls the shaft anteriorly and medially, was minimized in internal rotation. In fractures involving the greater tuberosity, the deforming forces acting on the GT were better counteracted in abduction and neutral rotation, as mentioned previously.26

Regardless of the immobilization tool, the authors prefer to avoid excess support of the elbow which will negate the positive reductive effects of gravity on the fracture. This is best accomplished by using a shoulder immobilizer with an arm and wrist cuff or a cuff and collar. While this may rest the arm in a slightly internally rotated position, in our practice the continual gravitational pull has been recognized as more important in this setting. Alternatively, when using a simple sling or shoulder immobilizer with an abduction pillow, care must be taken to allow the elbow to rest in the deepest part of the sling as caudal from the shoulder as the patient will allow. The goal is to avoid using the straps of the sling to push the elbow cephalad and mitigate the positive effects of gravity on fracture reduction.

From the beginning, immobilization should be removed periodically to perform range of motion exercises for the elbow, wrist and hand. Some surgeons recommend pendulum exercises during the second or third week for simpler fracture patterns that are at least length and alignment stable. Regarding rehabilitation, formal physical therapy is initiated in the fourth week with passive exercises, and active exercises being introduced a few weeks later based on the patient's progression.38 In a prospective randomized trial, Martínez et al compared 1-week versus 3-week immobilization in patients with nonoperatively treated PHFs, including mostly displaced one-to four-part fractures. Among the 111 patients who completed follow-up, no significant differences were found in pain, functional outcomes, or complications. These results support the use of a shorter, 1-week immobilization period.38 Further studies may better refine immobilization period, and the type of device to be used for each case.

Close radiographic follow-up is essential to detect secondary fracture displacement that may shift the treatment recommendation from nonoperative to surgical management. Displacement risk should be assessed based on the patient age and the extent of initial fracture displacement.15,39 SN fractures have a higher risk of inherent fracture instability and should be monitored with weekly radiographs during the first 3 to 4 weeks. In nondisplaced or minimally displaced fractures, serial imaging is generally reserved for cases with comminution.39 In general, we recommend obtaining radiographs at 1 or 2 weeks after injury during orthopedic consultation, 6 weeks, and 12 weeks.

Surgical treatment

The surgical indications of PHFs continues to garner debate with wide variability in the published guidelines to date.17,28,29,43,58 For certain fractures: open fractures, fractures with associated vascular injuries, fracture-dislocations, and head split fractures, operative treatment is often indicated. For all other patterns, the authors prefer to use the Mayo-FJD classification to guide indication.

For DN fractures or fractures with severe humeral head involvement (HD, HS, or HI), surgery is almost always recommended.17,29,43 For other fracture patterns, the indication will depend on the degree of displacement.17 The criteria used are based on degrees of displacement associated with poor outcomes following conservative treatment.14 It should be noted that each case must be assessed alongside patient-specific factors (age, functionality, comorbidities, hand dominance, occupational, or sports activity).

For isolated tuberosity fractures, surgical treatment is considered by most when medial displacement exceeds 5 to 10 mm (depending on the patient's functional demands), or when there is substantial posterior-superior displacement in the case of the GT.12,41,49 In young patients and athletes, some studies have recommended surgery for very little amounts of displacement (3 mm).12,49 Surgical treatment is also generally considered for SN, VL, or VPM fractures that display a humeral head displacement greater than 33% relative to the diaphysis.10 Regarding angulation, there is not enough evidence to determine a specific angle that would require surgical correction. However, studies using a 45° threshold defined by Neer observed a higher percentage of nonunion in greater angulations.10,44,60 In addition to these criteria, fractures that become increasingly displaced during follow-up may also represent an indication for surgery.

Surgical techniques

Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning

Closed reduction and percutaneous fixation involves the use of percutaneous K-wires and/or screws to address the fracture. Advantages include reduced intraoperative bleeding, lower risk of infection, and the preservation of soft tissues.61 However, it is only feasible for a small subset of select patients with amenable patterns, such as valgus impacted fractures. Disadvantages include an elevated risk for hardware migration, screw cutout, malunion, and nonunion which can be common especially in osteoporotic bone.13,35

El-Alfy described a modification of Kapandji's “palm tree technique,” achieving the best results in patients with 2-part fractures impacted in valgus.13 Baig et al published a technique for compromised tuberosities in which, in addition to closed reduction and fixation with K-wires, an anchor was added to secure the GT and prevent its displacement during follow-up.1 Since its use is generally limited to specific cases, the literature reports a small number of patients and short follow-up periods. Therefore, the selection of this technique should be made cautiously, but could be a good alternative in patients with good bone quality and no major head-shaft instability.51,61

Intramedullary nailing

Intramedullary nails offer the primary advantage of protecting soft tissues by utilizing minimally invasive approaches. Concerns regarding rotator cuff injuries can be mitigated with an approach through an alternative entry point through Nevaiser's portal and use of small-diameter straight nails.34 Fracture patterns amenable to nailing tend to be 2- to 3-part fractures, with reported healing rates approaching 99%.59 These high healing rates with IM nailing must be balance against the relatively higher percentage of malunion (estimated as approximately 20%) compared to plates, and a loss of reduction approaching 25% % in 3- to 4-part fractures.59 Additionally, a reoperation rate over 15% has been reported, primarily due to proximal nail migration.59 Nonetheless, these outcomes should be interpreted with caution, as there may always be some component of malunion without opening a fracture, but this may not be clinically significant. Additionally, many of these reported complications were reported with earlier-generation implants.

Current third-generation nails incorporate multiple design improvements aimed at enhancing stability and reducing failure rates.40 These include nylon or polyethylene bushings and threaded holes to secure proximal screws and prevent migration, locking spiral blades to increase the bone–implant interface, blunt screw tips to avoid intra-articular penetration, and more divergent screw trajectories to optimize tuberosity fixation.40

Intramedullary nails can be a suitable option for displaced SN fractures without tuberosity involvement, as they allow fracture reduction and fixation while minimizing soft tissue disruption. Another potential indication is a minimally displaced surgical neck fracture associated with a displaced tuberosity fragment. In such cases, the nail provides stabilization of the neck, facilitating a more stable reduction and fixation of the tuberosity.51

Compared to plate fixation, intramedullary nailing offers advantages such as reduced intraoperative bleeding, soft tissue preservation and shorter surgical time.59 In comparative studies, plates yield similar results when treating 2- to 3-part fractures.59 However, in 4-part fractures, plates seem to be associated with better reduction, although the overall complication rates remain similar.18,41

Open reduction internal fixation (ORIF)

Locked plates offer several advantages, including direct reduction of the tuberosities, preservation of the cuff footprint and joint space, and compatibility with various augmentation techniques. The most important aspect of an ORIF is the quality of the reduction, ensuring an adequate tuberosity reduction, avoiding varus, and providing medial calcar support are critical. To further improve outcomes, additional techniques have been incorporated: rotator cuff sutures through the plate to augment fixation, intramedullary or extramedullary strut bone grafts to support fractures with substantial impaction, calcar locking screws to enhance medial stability, and augmentation of screw fixation into the humeral head with bone cement.3,54

In 2020, a retrospective study from the Mayo Clinic evaluated 173 PHFs treated with locking plate fixation in patients over 60 years of age, with a mean follow-up of 6.1 years.3 The overall complication and failure rate were 44% and 34%, respectively, increasing with patient age and fracture complexity. Despite this, the reoperation rate remained relatively low at 11%, as most complications, particularly AVN, were managed nonoperatively. The most frequent indication for reoperation was screw cut-through secondary to humeral head collapse or secondary fracture displacement.3 The average time to complication onset was 16 months, supporting the need for clinical and radiographic follow-up of at least 18 months. Intramedullary nailing and plate fixation are both considered for fractures that do not meet criteria for shoulder arthroplasty, though there is no clear consensus on which technique is superior.4,45,62 Guidelines have been proposed based on fracture patterns, although the scientific evidence to support them remains pending.4,51

One problem that has been identified with periarticular locking plates for PHFs is screw penetration through the humeral head, which may even lead to glenoid bone loss if the screws penetrate through the head and into the glenoid. Contemporary proximal humerus plates have been designed so that the proximal screws are not oriented orthogonal to the plates and directed to the location of the glenoid, but angled in a manner that prioritizes tuberosity fixation and head fixation through the tuberosities. Reports on the outcomes of internal fixation using these contemporary plates are needed in order to determine whether they really represent an improvement.

Hemiarthroplasty

Before the wide adoption of rTSA, hemiarthroplasty was the treatment of choice for unreconstructible fractures. Outcomes are completely dependent on placement of the prosthetic humeral head in the correct height and version and tuberosity healing. Unfortunately, tuberosity healing has remained unpredictable in the setting of hemiarthroplasty, particularly in elderly patients with osteopenia and those with small and/or multifragmented greater tuberosity fractures.28,37,41 Currently, rTSA is most commonly preferred for fractures requiring arthroplasty, but hemiarthroplasty is still considered an option for very young patients under 40 with a severely compromised humeral head, especially when the tuberosities are fractured in single large fragments.37 Other scenarios would be isolated HI, unreconstructible head split or anatomic neck fractures with intact tuberosities.37,43 It may also be indicated for patients requiring joint replacement whose glenoid bone stock is not suitable for the glenoid component or a rTSA, or in cases of sequelae in which the humeral head is nonviable and the tuberosities healed in a reasonable position (e.g., AVN).37,43

rTSA

rTSA has become an increasingly utilized surgical option for managing complex PHFs, particularly in elderly patients with fracture patterns unsuitable for ORIF or hemiarthroplasty.43 When comparing rTSA to ORIF in patients over 65 year old, there have been comparable functional outcomes but a higher reoperation rate following ORIF.33,43 Similar findings were reported in the network meta-analysis by Zheng et al, which highlighted the lower need for revision surgery with rTSA compared to other surgical options.62

Indications include HS, HI involving more than 40% of the articular surface, and HD with one or both tuberosities fractured.43 In cases of displaced VPM and VL fractures, where treatment decisions are less straightforward, rTSA is generally recommended when tuberosities are fractured in combination with severe comminution, poor bone quality, deltoid tuberosity index, advanced age (typically over 65-70 years), or an unstable humeral head fragment.17,28,43,52 The timing of rTSA, whether performed acutely or delayed for fracture sequelae, remains debated. Most studies favor acute intervention, which seems to be associated with better functional outcomes and lower complication rates, although some studies report no significant differences.28,33,43

Sound tuberosity repair and the use of fracture-specific implants is recommended, as most studies demonstrate improved outcomes when the tuberosities are repaired, despite variability in healing rates.33,43 Techniques such as supraspinatus release may be employed to reduce tension on tuberosity repairs and minimize displacement.43 Regarding implant selection, no definitive clinical advantage has been demonstrated with cemented stems over cementless fixation or with more vertical humeral inclination angles; therefore, these decisions remain at the discretion of the surgeon.43

Conclusion

Treatment of PHFs continues to lack clearly defined indications for nonoperative versus operative treatment. Furthermore, when surgery is pursued, there is no clear consensus on the optimal technique or fixation strategy. In each case, patient-specific factors, fracture morphology, and functional demands challenge broad treatment algorithms. However, this review highlights the importance of a thoughtful shared decision-making exercise between the surgeon and patient. When internal fixation is selected, key principles include an anatomic reduction, robust tuberosity fixation, calcar support, bone grafting for severe comminution in valgus fractures, and selective consideration of augmentation techniques; contemporary plates designed with tuberosity fixation as a priority may surpass the outcomes obtained with traditional locking plates. In cases managed with a rTSA, tuberosity reconstruction, adequate deltoid tensioning, and implant stability are essential to achieving favorable outcomes.
