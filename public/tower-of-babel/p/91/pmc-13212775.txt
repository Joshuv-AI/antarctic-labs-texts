The Market for Monoclonal Antibodies: Trends, Challenges, and Opportunities
Authors: André Soares Motta-Santos, Kenya Valéria Micaela de Souza Noronha, Leonardo Costa Ribeiro, Jeffrey Gow, Khorshed Alam, Mônica Viegas Andrade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study examines the monoclonal antibody innovation landscape through patent activity and market data.MethodsA dedicated dataset was constructed by linking multiple sources (Antibody Society, Orange Book, Ark Patent Intelligence, US Patent and Trademark Office, PATSTAT, Purple Book, US Veterans Affairs, US-FDA, and ORBIS), covering patents registered between 1986 and 2019. Data analysis comprised six components: (i) a general description of the dataset, (ii) an examination of monoclonal antibody patents and approval trends, (iii) an analysis of therapeutic indications, (iv) a characterization of patent holders and producers through descriptive and network analyses, (v) an assessment of shareholder influence, including common-ownership patterns, and (vi) an evaluation of monoclonal antibody prices.ResultsThe dataset included 63 monoclonal antibodies, 1732 unique patents, 89 active pharmaceutical ingredients, 34 producers, and 214 therapeutic indications, of which 36.5% were single indication, while the average number of secondary indications was 3.78. Roche, Johnson & Johnson, Eli Lilly, Amgen, and Novartis led reference medicine production, while Amgen and Pfizer were notable in biosimilars, with only five companies producing both. Shareholders such as BlackRock, UBS, Vanguard, and JPMorgan exerted strong market influence in monoclonal antibody production. Prices were extremely high, averaging US$127,430 per course or year (s = US$142,509), with 42.1% of monoclonal antibodies priced above US$100,000.ConclusionsWhile monoclonal antibodies have transformed modern medicine and improved safety and effectiveness, their persistently high prices, reinforced by market concentration and financial investor influence, raise serious concerns for equity and social welfare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40259-026-00774-0.

Key Points

We assembled and analyzed a unique dataset (1975–2019) linking patents, approvals, ownership, and pricing for 63 monoclonal antibodies.Innovation and production are highly concentrated among a few firms, reinforced by common institutional investors.Monoclonal antibody prices remain extremely high, with over 40% above US$100,000 per year, raising critical concerns for access and equity.

Introduction

Pharmaceutical companies have continuously evolved over the past two centuries in response to exogenous shocks and the need to balance the interests of various interacting agents, such as consumers, profit-seeking firms, regulatory agencies, universities, politicians, and government-funded research centers [1, 2]. Compared to other industries, pharmaceutical companies invest heavily in research and development (R&D) and set high prices for their inventions [3–7]. They must continuously innovate to meet the changing healthcare needs of an aging population [3, 8–10]. Over the years, new challenges have emerged and companies have successfully absorbed the biotechnology revolution, scientific advances, and socioeconomic progress. The accumulated knowledge allowed for the precise targeting and rational design of new pharmaceutical compounds [1], but with high social and individual costs [11–18]. Amongst these high-cost revolutionary technologies are monoclonal antibodies (mAbs).

Monoclonal antibodies are immunoglobulins produced by a single B-cell clone line. They are preparations of antibodies in which every molecule is identical in its amino acid sequence. Their structure contains two heavy and two light polypeptide chains connected by a disulfide bond, resulting in a Y-structure. The Y-structure includes a variable region (Fab), dependent on the antigen targeted by the mAb, and a constant region (Fc), responsible for linking the antibody to immune cells [19]. Each specific mAb is expected to have the same antigen recognition sites, affinity, interactions, and effects [20]. The most frequently discussed indications are hematologic malignancies, solid tumors, and autoimmune disorders. In recent decades, other uses have been discovered, such as hypercholesterolemia, asthma, osteoporosis, inflammatory bowel disease, allograft rejection, drug reversal, and infectious diseases [20–22]. Most mAbs can be used to treat multiple diseases and have at least one application for cancer [23].

The market for mAbs stands out even within the broader pharmaceutical sector, generating substantial revenues for their developers and producers [24–26]. The first mAb approved for human use by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) was Muromonab-CD3 (Orthoclone OKT3®; Janssen-Cilag) in 1986 [19, 22], but the market did not take off at this point. The murine nature of mAbs was associated with immunogenic reactions [19, 22]. The mAb approval rate increased drastically after the deposit of patents protecting methods to obtain chimeric, humanized, and human mAbs from the 1990s onward [20, 22, 27–31]. The first chimeric mAb, abciximab, was approved for clinical use in 1994. Two of the most successful chimeric mAbs, rituximab and infliximab, were approved just a few years later (1997 and 1998, respectively). Around the same period, the first humanized mAb (daclizumab, 1997) was registered. The first blockbuster of this group was approved the following year (trastuzumab, 1998). The first human mAb was adalimumab, approved in 2002, which quickly became a huge success. By 2022, at least 144 mAbs had been approved or were under review by the US FDA and the European Medicines Agency [32]. In 2021, the global market for mAbs was valued at US$185.5 billion [33]. This is a remarkable figure for a relatively small number of molecules when compared with the total pharmaceutical market that reached US$1.48 trillion in 2022 [10, 34].

The large revenues are associated with the high prices of mAbs. Between 1997 and 2016, the average annual price of FDA-approved mAbs was approximately US$96,731 (median = US$58,968), with 32% of them exceeding US$100,000 per year [35]. From an economic perspective, there are a few possible explanations for these prices. Initially, the mAb market faces higher barriers to entry than the chemical drug market [36]. Biopharmaceutical products incur additional development and production costs because of their complexity and dependence on living systems [28]. Another key feature of the mAb market is the absence of true generics. While generic drugs are chemically identical to their reference products, it is virtually impossible to produce an identical mAb in different companies once the original patent has expired. Instead, biosimilars have been developed. Biosimilars are biologic molecules highly similar to an already approved reference product, though minor structural differences, such as post-translational modifications, may remain [20]. Furthermore, the market penetration of reference mAbs is stronger than that of chemical drugs. For example, Remicade® (infliximab) sales decreased only by 15% after the emergence of the first biosimilars between 2015 and 2017 [23]. In addition, most mAbs still lack competitors in their respective markets, although some biosimilars and me-too drugs already exist [22].

The purchase of mAbs is usually unfeasible at the individual level [22, 32, 37]. In countries with public healthcare systems, these drugs may be subsidized, and many have been listed for the treatment of a wide range of diseases [20–22, 31, 38–44]. Despite their clinical benefits, the high prices of mAbs pose a serious threat to social welfare [45, 46]. Decisions on whether and how to fund these therapies involve complex trade-offs, requiring input from regulatory agencies, health technology assessment bodies, healthcare professionals, and policymakers. These players must balance considerations of clinical effectiveness, budget impact, and equity, while also navigating pressures from patients, physicians, and manufacturers [47–50]. Yet, the allocation of resources to mAbs may displace funding that could generate greater health gains elsewhere in the system [51–55]. Against this backdrop, understanding the market for mAbs—covering innovation, production, and pricing—is essential for appropriate decision making. This article contributes to the literature by characterizing the landscape of the mAb market through an analysis of patent activity and market data. To our knowledge, this is the most comprehensive characterization of the mAb market to date. In the mAb market, previous studies have applied patent analytics to examine innovations, primarily using descriptive approaches [56–61]. Regarding pricing studies, there is an interesting work on the subject [35]. However, it focuses on mAb prices, not its relationship with market characteristics.

Methods

This is a descriptive analysis based on a comprehensive, unprecedented, and dedicated dataset created by the authors to study the market for mAbs. This work focuses on data from the USA. There are two main reasons for that choice. (i) First, relevant data are relatively easy to access through government open databases and the authors had some previous experience with them. (ii) Second, given the size and global influence of the US pharmaceutical market, data from the US Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) serve as a robust proxy for worldwide patent activity [59, 62, 63]. However, before analyzing the markets, it is necessary to define them in terms of their geographic and product dimensions.

Relevant Markets

The delimitation of a relevant market refers to the process of identifying the set of economic agents (consumers and producers) that effectively respond to changes in price, quantity, and quality within an industry [10]. The geographic dimension defines the territorial boundaries of the market, including the location and reach of all relevant sources of supply. The dimension of the product encompasses goods that consumers consider substitutes, based on their characteristics, price, and intended use [10, 64].

Regarding the geographic dimension, since the emergence of large pharmaceutical companies in the last century, the scope of pharmaceutical markets has expanded globally [8]. Despite the global nature of pharmaceutical production and innovation, the distribution of drugs across countries is constrained by patents and national regulations. Patents are territorial, granting the holder exclusive rights only within the countries in which they are registered. Consequently, medicines cannot be freely supplied across borders to bypass higher prices in certain markets. This prevents arbitrage, whereby drugs sold at lower prices in one country could otherwise be imported into higher-priced markets. The TRIPS Agreement establishes minimum standards for intellectual property protection across member countries of the World Trade Organization, strengthening territorial control of patented medicines and affecting both market access and pricing [65–67].

The pharmaceutical market can be defined at different levels with respect to the dimension of the product. (i) The broadest level of aggregation is the pharmaceutical market. However, as drugs generally exhibit low cross-elasticity (i.e., they are rarely substitutes), this definition has limited practical use [1, 68]. (ii) An alternative approach is to define the market by indication [68], in which case two products are considered part of the same market if they are used for the same therapeutic purpose. This approach has the drawback of grouping different drug classes together, which can be problematic because drugs from distinct classes can act as both complements and substitutes depending on the context. (iii) A third possibility is to define the market by drug classes. Drug classes can be determined based on various criteria. The most common is the mechanism of action, which describes how a drug produces its intended effect on the body. Drugs with different mechanisms of action may exhibit synergistic or additive effects. Negri et al. [10] suggest that drug classes can be considered equivalent to relevant markets. Although this approach avoids mixing different classes, it introduces another limitation: drugs within the same class may act as substitutes for one indication but not for others.

To address these limitations, the market can be defined by (iv) class indication [69]. In this approach, drugs within the same class are considered competitors only if they are used for the same therapeutic purpose. Although conceptually precise, this definition may complicate the analysis in practice. Regulatory agencies often categorize the market according to drug classes, such as the World Health Organization’s Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification [70] or the Brazilian Chamber for the Regulation of the Pharmaceutical Market (CMED) classification of relevant markets [71]. As a result, many indicators have already been calculated at the drug class level and would need adjustment for the more granular class-indication categories. Furthermore, many pharmaceutical products require prescriptions, which limits the consumer’s choice. Consequently, certain acceptable substitutes, such as me-too drugs or drugs from different classes but with similar indications, may be excluded from the competitive process at the microlevel. These drugs could be substitutes from the perspective of the health system, but individual consumers cannot freely choose between them. (v) The simplest way to define a market is by agent, maximizing disaggregation. However, this level of detail can restrict the general applicability of the analysis results. Ultimately, the appropriate level of market aggregation depends on the objective of the analysis. The aim of this study is to study the market for mAbs. It would, therefore, be a specific aggregation of a group of drug classes.

Dataset Construction

The dataset was constructed by linking the previously described sources (Fig. 1). The list of mAbs approved by the FDA and their international non-proprietary names was obtained from the Antibody Society website [32, 81, 82]. These international non-proprietary names were then used to retrieve the USPTO patent numbers from the IQVIA database [72]. Patents are an exclusive right granted to the author of an invention. They are technolegal documents through which governments provide innovators with protection against imitation, as part of a social contract, provided that their inventions are novel, non-obvious, and useful [67, 83, 84]. The standard patent protection term is 20 years, after which the knowledge enters the public domain [66, 67, 85]. Patents are the most measurable output of the innovation process [86]. To file a patent, innovators must disclose information about the invention, inventors, and holders. Therefore, patent registries are good, reliable, and rigorous sources of data to study innovation [60, 62, 84, 87–92].

Schematic overview of the construction of the database of monoclonal antibodies approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) between 1986 and 2019. USPTO US Patent and Trademark Office

IQVIA patent numbers were used to retrieve metadata from the USPTO subset of PATSTAT [63, 74]. The metadata retrieved at this stage included application numbers, country codes, filing dates, publication dates, International Patent Classification codes, USPTO identification numbers, patent types, priority patent application numbers, and their names, countries, and types of patent holders. The patent holder types include: (i) companies; (ii) individuals; (iii) government non-profit organizations; (iv) universities; and (v) hospitals. Priority patents are the first patent deposited to protect an invention worldwide [93]. The date of the priority deposit marks the beginning of intellectual property rights protection, after which inventors might seek protection in multiple countries. The set of multiple publications protecting the same technology domestically or in different countries is called a patent family [94, 95]. The priority patent numbers were then searched in PATSTAT, which includes other patent offices, and their metadata and patent families were retrieved. The data obtained at this stage included International Patent Classification codes, application numbers, country codes, and filing dates. All these data were incorporated into the database.

The Antibody Society database [32] also provided data on the type of immunoglobulin, targets, the type of mAb, the first indications, and approval dates. The Purple Book [75] provided data on biosimilars, producers, brand names, and approval dates. The VA website [77] provided data on practiced pharmaceutical prices on the US pharmaceutical market. Prices were collected for the first indication. The FDA website [79] provided data on indications of each mAb. Finally, (viii) ORBIS [80] was used to collect data on companies, their global ultimate owners, and shareholders. The database consisted of data from 1986 to 2019: 1986 marks the year in which the first mAb, muromonab-CD3, was approved for human use by the FDA [19, 22], and 2019 represents the cut-off year of the IQVIA database available to the authors for this study. The list of antibodies and their main characteristics is available in Table 1 of the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Holders were reclassified in the database to incorporate groups of companies and their subsidiaries (Table 2 of the ESM). All mAbs identified through the described method were included in the analysis. No mAbs were excluded based on their application or use.

Data Analysis

A descriptive approach was applied to characterize the mAb market. First, a general description of the dataset was provided. Second, mAb patents and the time progression of approval of new mAbs by the FDA were discussed, along with mAb indications and their progression over time. Third, the patent holders and mAb producers were characterized by descriptive and network analyses. The main players in the production market were identified. Fourth, the shareholders of these players were identified, and a breadth network analysis was conducted to evaluate their influence over the market. A common ownership analysis was carried out to identify shareholders that commonly invest together in the same companies. Finally, the prices of mAb were discussed. The discussion of mAb prices included prices in 2025. Two price analyses were conducted. The first analysis is the price per milligram of the drug. The second is the cost of 1 year of treatment or one course of the drug in case it is less than a year. When applicable, hypothesis testing was performed using the Student’s t test or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. A 5% significance level was accepted. All data analyzes were performed in R [96].

Network Analysis

For the breadth analysis, two networks were built. First, a directed network of producers and molecules was built to show the characteristics of the production market. The network flows from producers (in blue) to molecules (in pink). The second was a directional network between shareholders (in yellow) and companies (in blue). Companies that are also shareholders were colored orange. This network flows from shareholders to companies. Four indicators were calculated: degree centrality, out-degree centrality, in-degree centrality, and hub scores. As the network is weakly disconnected, other indicators such as diameter, density, connectivity, and transitivity are unhelpful and were not reported for these networks.

Degree centrality refers to the number of direct edges of a vertex. The assumption is that the more connections there are in a vertex, the more important it is to the network. For node i, the degree centrality can be calculated by Eq. (1), in which Gi↔j is the value of the edge between the vertices i and j (0 or 1) and N is the number of vertices in the network. Degree centrality can still be divided between in-degree and out-degree centrality. In-degree centrality is the number of edges received by a vertex (Eq. 2), in which Gi←j is the binary value of the edge from j to i), and out-degree centrality is the number of edges flowing from a vertex (Eq. 3), in which Gi→j is the binary value of the edge from i to j) [62]. In the producers’ network, out-degree centrality refers to the number of molecules produced by a company and the in-degree centrality refers to the number of companies producing a molecule. In the shareholders’ network, out-degree centrality refers to the number of companies in which a shareholder has interest, and in-degree centrality refers to the number of shareholders of a company.

Hub scores are specifically applied to directed networks and focus on vertices that point to many important vertices (called authorities). Thus, hub scores emphasize a vertex’s role as a connector or distributor of influence [97]. In the producers’ network, a company with a high hub score is one that produces many relevant drugs within the mAb market. An assumption that drugs that have more producers are associated with more demanding markets was made. In the shareholders’ network, investors with high hub scores are those who have interest in more powerful companies. The hub scores are calculated by Eq. (4), where hi is the hub score of vertices i, aj is the authority score of vertices j, the sum runs over all vertices j such that there is a directed edge from vertex i to vertex j (i.e., i points to j), and E is the set of directed edges. Simultaneously, the authority scores aj are defined by Eq. (5). This means the authority score of j depends on the hub scores of vertices pointing to it, and the hub score of i depends on the authority scores of vertices it points to. The scores are computed iteratively until convergence [97].

Common Ownership Analysis

Common ownership occurs when multiple firms are partially owned by the same investors [69, 98]. This structure allows shareholders to diversify risk by investing in multiple competing firms, effectively promoting self-insurance [1, 10, 99–102]. The first part of the common ownership analysis is made up of an undirected network between shareholders to identify those with higher eigenvector centrality. Eigenvector centrality measures the influence (i.e., status or prestige) of a vertex in an undirected network. It infers that a vertex is more important if it is connected to other highly central vertices. The interpretation of the results is similar to the hub scores. However, hub scores are specific to the directed network.

Formally, the eigenvector centrality can be calculated by Eq. (6). Let E be the set of edges, where {i,j}∈E indicates that vertices i and j are connected. The sum runs over all neighbors j of vertex i (with i≠j). Each term in the sum represents the centrality of neighbor j, denoted as CE(j), and the total is scaled by a constant factor α. The vector CE=CE1,⋯,CENT is the solution to the eigenvalue problem A×c=ev×c, where A is the adjacency matrix of the graph, c is the eigenvector of centralities (i.e., CE(i) for all vertices i), and ev=α-1 is the eigenvalue. In practice, the igraph package in R [96, 103] calculates eigenvector centrality by constructing the adjacency matrix and computing its principal eigenvector, which yields the centrality score for each vertex. These values are normalized so that the highest score is 1. In economic terms, a higher eigenvector centrality indicates a vertex that is connected to other highly influential vertices.

Other metrics used in the undirected network were degree centrality, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, density, and transitivity. The CD and its variations evaluate how many connections a vertex makes (i.e., power), as previously explained. Betweenness centrality and closeness centrality consider the placement of a vertex. Betweenness centrality measures how often a vertex lies on the shortest paths between two other vertices (Eq. 7), in which Gjk is the number of shortest paths between vertices j and k, and Gjk(i) is the number of those paths that pass through i, with i≠j≠k). This measure is particularly important for identifying vertices that serve as potential intermediaries or bridges within the network [62]. Shareholders with high betweenness centrality should be important in terms of flux of information and influence in the network for their capacity to connect different groups. They can intermediate contracts and agreements, especially between firms that do not have many shareholders in common or shareholders that do not invest simultaneously in many companies (common owners).

Closeness centrality measures the proximity of a vertex to all other vertices in the network (Eq. 8), where d(i,j) is the shortest distance between vertices i and j, N is the total number of vertices in the network, and i≠j). One limitation of betweenness centrality is that a vertex may have a high betweenness centrality while still being relatively distant from most other vertices in the network. Closeness centrality, in contrast, provides information about how easily a shareholder can transmit influence and information throughout the network without relying much on intermediaries [62, 69]. Therefore, it also reflects strategic positioning in the market, but from a different perspective. In this way, betweenness centrality and closeness centrality serve as complementary indicators.

The diameter is the shortest path length between the two most distant vertices in a network. In this work, the diameter represents the worst-case scenario for transmitting information and influence through the undirected network. Density is defined as the proportion of observed connections relative to the maximum possible number of connections (Eq. 9) for directed networks or Eq. (10) for undirected networks, where L is the number of actual connections in the network and N is the number of nodes) [62, 104].

The same equation can be used for C3,C4,⋯,CW just by adjusting the companies that should be found together. Results

The database includes 63 mAbs: two are murine (amino acid sequences from mice), nine are chimeric (about ~65% human and ~35% murine), 27 are humanized (~95% human sequences), and 25 are human.

mAb Patents, Indications, and Regulatory Approval Progression

The 63 mAbs were associated with 1732 unique patent identification numbers. Most patents were associated with drugs, but not necessarily with new drugs. They were described as “New Use Related to Main Indication” (N=395), “Molecule Patents” (N=328), and “Dosing Regimen/Administration Conditions” (N=173) [Table 4 of the ESM]. As the same PIN could be related to more than one mAb, there are 2640 PIN-mAb sets in the database. The average number of patents associated with each mAb was 41.9 (s=61.1, minimum=1, median=21, maximum=387). There were 718 unique priority patents in the database. Because one priority might be associated with more than one mAb, there are 1175 priority-mAb sets in the database. The average number of priorities per mAb is 18.6 (s=26.1, minimum=1, median=9, maximum=158).

Figure 2 presents the general and priority patent filing years for the mAb market. There were very little patent deposits before the mid-1990s. From this point forward, the number of patents and their priorities started growing. Despite the difference in magnitude, curves a and b have very similar shapes, suggesting that the period between priority and progeny filling is not very long. The curves peak in 2002 and 2003 for mAb general patents and priority patents. The deposit of new patents associated with the 63 mAbs in the dataset began decreasing in the 2010s. The data for the approval date on new mAbs correlate nicely with patent deposit data. Figure 3 shows that there was very little activity in the market for mAbs in the 1980s, as expected from the patenting activity, and started increasing in the 2000s. The research and approval of new mAbs increased over time. The reduction in patent activity is consistent with the fact that patents are typically registered long before drugs reach the market and that the data are right-censored; that is, although the approval of new mAbs continued, the patent sample only extends until 2019.

The number of indications is associated with the number of different (sub)markets of a molecule, as previously explained. More indications should be associated with more useful drugs and, in general terms, higher demand. The 63 mAbs in the dataset were associated with 214 indications, an average of 3.4 per molecule (s=3.19). The number of indications varied between 18 (pembrolizumab) and 1 (various). Some of the mAbs with more uses were pembrolizumab (18), nivolumab (11), bevacizumab (10), adalimumab (9), and rituximab (9). The most common first indication fields were oncology/hematology (30 mAbs) and immunology (20); i.e., molecules were first developed for diseases in these fields. Regarding the 214 indications identified, the majority were also in oncology/hematology (47.2%) and immunology (36.9%) [Table 1].

Figure 4 shows the evolution of the indications of the 63 mAbs in the database over time; the number of mAb indications increased over time and became more diverse. About 36.5% of the sample had only one indication at the moment of data collection. The average number of secondary indications for molecules with more than one indication was 3.78 (s=3.29), varying from 1 (various) to 17 (pembrolizumab). The average time delay between the first and last indications was 5.64 years (s=5.60). The maximum time delays were 21.8 years for rituximab, 18.5 years for daclizumab, 17.5 years for omalizumab, 16.3 years for bevacizumab, and 13.5 years for adalimumab. Therefore, it is possible to confirm that research continues after the first approval of a mAb and more uses are identified. Sometimes, these new uses are in fields different from the original indication. Monoclonal antibodies with uses classified in more than one field are eculizumab (3), rituximab (2), denosumab (2), dupilumab (2), mepolizumab (2), alemtuzumab (2), and ofatumumab (2).

Patent Holders and mAb Producers

Data on the holders were available for 1703 mAb general patents. These patents were associated with 928 holders, including 51 companies, 18 universities, three hospitals, eight non-profit organizations, and 848 individuals. A patent can be associated with more than one holder, and the same holder may appear multiple times in the database. The total number of patent holders varied between 1 and 23 (x¯=2.7, s=3.1). Data on the holders of 445 priority patents were available. The priorities are associated with 241 unique holders: 32 companies, eight government non-profit organizations, two hospitals, 185 individuals, and 14 universities. The total number of holders per priority varied between 1 and 14 (x¯=1.8, s=1.9). Priorities are statistically associated with fewer holders than general patents (p<0.001).

mAb Prices

Of the 63 mAbs in the database, the VA database reported prices for 58. The missing mAbs were abciximab, daclizumab, obiltoxaximab, olaratumab, and raxibacumab. Abciximab, daclizumab, and olaratumab have been withdrawn from the market. Obiltoxaximab and raxibacumab have very limited applications, as both are used exclusively for anthrax therapy. Ofatumumab prices had to be removed from the sample because of inconsistency. It had price values of US$0.01 for some presentations in the dataset. Therefore, the final sample consisted of 57 mAbs.

The price per milligram varied widely between agents, with a range from US$0.957 to US$131,709. The mean was US$3396, but the median was only US$17.9, indicating a right-skewed distribution. After removing outliers, the mean price per milligram was US$30.2 (s=37.3, median=12.2). The price per milligram is important because it is partly influenced by the technologies used to produce mAbs. Although there are some differences, the core technology for producing mAbs is largely similar across agents. All of them are manufactured using immortalized mammalian cell lines. Therefore, the substantial differences in price per milligram are more likely explained by market characteristics rather than by production technology. For reference, the price of 1 mg of gold today (30 September, 2025) is US$0.121. The cost per mg of some common medicines in the VA database is much lower: paracetamol (500-mg tablet), US$0.00003332; ibuprofen (400-mg tablet), US$0.0001230; omeprazole (20-mg tablet), US$0.001667; and linezolid (2-mg/mL injectable, currently one of the most expensive antibiotics), US$0.03635. Therefore, mAbs are objectively expensive.

However impressive, this analysis does not inform about the costs of treatment with mAbs. In more useful terms, the cost of treatment per course or year was US$127.430 on average (s=US$142,509, minimum=US$2,540, maximum=US$642,782, median=US$69,831). Twenty-four mAbs (42.1%) had prices above US$100,000. Therefore, mAb treatments purchase is unfeasible at the individual level, especially in low- and middle-income countries. Reference drugs were available for all 57 mAbs. Only ten mAbs had biosimilars in the price database (from 2025). Therefore, 47 markets were monopolies if the market was divided by agent. Considering only the mAbs that have biosimilars, the average prices of the reference drugs is higher than the prices of biosimilars US$80,160 versus US$56,026. Biosimilars are priced at about 70% of the price of the reference drug. However, the difference was not proved significant because of the small sample sizes. Nonetheless, it was observed that mAbs for cancer treatment are significantly more expensive than others (p<0.001).

Discussion

Our dataset included 63 mAbs, about half of the nearly 120 mAbs already approved by the FDA by 2022 [32]. There were 1732 and 718 unique general patents and priority patents associated with these mAbs, respectively. The average number of patents and priorities associated with each mAb was 41.9 (s=61.1) and 18.6 (s=26.1). The large number of priority patents per mAb is an indication of the complexity of such molecules; i.e., many innovations from various knowledge fields are necessary to create a single molecule. Another possible explanation is patent thicketing, whereby a company constructs a dense web of patents around each drug to increase the likelihood that some patents will withstand legal challenges or to escalate litigation and licensing costs for competitors [105]. Frakes and Wasserman [105] found that the volume of patents appears to be heavily associated with the threat of competition in the biological drug market. The number of patents that biosimilars must face before entering the market is much larger than that for small-molecule generics.

We also observed that the number of new patents associated with the 63 mAbs began to decline in the 2010s. This trend can be easily explained. First, there is a natural reduction in new patents related to each mAb once the initial priority patent loses its protection. Second, the sample is subject to right-censoring in 2019. In addition, as expected, a temporal gap was observed between the peaks of the curves for patent filings (innovation) and approvals (production). Typically, pharmaceutical patents are filed before clinical trials, which means that it can take several years for a newly patented molecule to reach the market; 80% of patentable inventions in the pharmaceutical industry are patented, suggesting the firms’ reluctance to rely on trade secrets [106]. Consequently, the long interval between patent filing and market launch shortens the effective period of exclusivity for pharmaceutical companies compared to firms in other industries [85, 107]. To mitigate this reduced exclusivity window, companies have adopted a range of strategies, including evergreening and reverse payments [1, 36, 69, 105, 108–111].

Evergreening refers to the practice of obtaining multiple patents covering different aspects of a single invention to extend market exclusivity and deter competition [110]. This can sometimes involve only minor changes compared to the original patent [36]. Frakes and Wasserman [105] found evidence of evergreening in the biological drug market by analyzing indicators related to the timing and novelty of subsequent patent applications. Because of evergreening, patent challenges have become quite common, especially for high-sales drugs. The strategy of frequent challenging patent protection is known as prospecting. It has also increased with time, but with limited efficacy in reducing the lifetime of patents. Prospecting seems to work as a countermeasure to restore the practical life of patents to nominal terms [109].

The use of evergreening by pharmaceutical companies is highly controversial. It is a strategy for companies to build a strong patent portfolio, but has been associated with the delay in the entry of new competitors into the market and the increase in healthcare costs [110]. However, a much more complex problem is the extension of patents through reverse payments [36, 111]. Pharmaceutical companies may want to retain market dominance by paying competitors not to enter the market until a specific date. Entry induces losses to the innovative companies and gains to imitative companies that are highly asymmetric. An innovative company loses much more than an imitative company profits. Therefore, the joint benefit for the brand and the generic in preventing entry is clearly positive [69].

The 63 mAbs in the database were associated with 89 active pharmaceutical ingredients, 34 producers, and 214 therapeutic indications. Of the mAbs, 36.5% were only used for a single indication and the average number of secondary indications was 3.78. A recent study showed that pharmaceutical companies use a strategy to approve a drug for a specific condition, the one most likely to be accepted by regulators, and then request approval for other health conditions, generating significant budget impacts on the health system when these drugs are listed [112]. This trend has been observed by other authors and has also influenced the sales of mAbs worldwide. The best-selling drug in 2023, for example, was pembrolizumab (Keytruda®: US$25 billion; Merck & Co). Pembrolizumab was approved for the first time by the FDA, in 2014, for advanced melanoma. Since then, Merck has expanded its applications. It is now used to treat about 18 diseases [78, 79, 113].

The most common patent holders in our database were companies. Roche Group had the largest number of mAb general patents in the database and a relevant number of priority patents. Other important companies in our analysis were AbbVie, Amgen, Novartis, GlaxoSmithKline, Biogen, and BMS. One of our previous analyses has already shown that the market for mAb innovations is moderately concentrated on general patents and unconcentrated on priority patents. Most importantly, it is significantly more concentrated than the market for chemical drug innovations [7]. We found that some companies central to the development of mAbs worldwide were Roche, PDL, City of Hope, Celltech, AbbVie, Amgen, Novartis, GlaxoSmithKline, Biogen, BMS, Regeneron, J&J, and AstraZeneca. However, interestingly, the most relevant patents in this analysis are associated with methods and procedures for obtaining mAbs, not with molecules themselves [7].

The analysis of the main patent holders aligns reasonably well with the main manufacturers of drugs. We observed in our dataset that Roche, J&J, Eli Lilly, Amgen, and Novartis led the production of reference drugs, while Amgen and Pfizer were notable in biosimilars, with only five companies producing both. These observations are generally in agreement with the scientific literature. In 2017, seven companies dominated the mAb market, accounting for 87% of sales. They were (i) Genentech (Roche Group) with 31% and 11 molecules; (ii) AbbVie with 20.0%; (iii) J&J with 13.6%; (iv) BMS with 6.5%; (v) Merck Sharp & Dohme with 5.6%; (vi) Novartis with 5.5%; and (vii) Amgen with 4.9% [23]. It is important to note that many companies might be associated with large market shares because of their production of molecules in more concentrated classes, an analysis that is not present in the network. Therefore, the data should not align perfectly; nevertheless, in general, the most innovative companies are also among the companies with the largest market shares. It is also quite clear that the mAb innovation and production activities are based on a few high-income countries.

Compared to other industries, pharmaceutical companies invest heavily in R&D, which carries a significant risk, and charge relatively high prices for their new technologies [8, 9, 34, 69]. In 2022, the pharmaceutical industry topped the European Union’s Industrial R&D Investment Scoreboard, with an investment of US$213 billion in absolute terms or 16.5% of their net sales [114]. The average capitalized cost of bringing a new drug to market is estimated at US$2.6 billion (in 2013 US dollars), with the development time from inception to marketing between 10 and 12 years [4, 115]. These costs are often reflected in the monopoly pricing of new technologies, enabled by patent protections, once a new drug reaches the market [116]. New molecule entities typically yield stable returns on investment [101]. However, the continued need for substantial R&D investments throughout the development process possibly makes the pharmaceutical industry susceptible to financialization and common ownership dynamics [69, 99, 117, 118].

Financialization refers to the process by which corporate strategies become subordinate to the accumulation of financial capital [101, 118]. In this context, pharmaceutical companies manage innovation portfolios and trade products to maximize shareholder value [99, 117]. Investment decisions are thus increasingly influenced by stock market performance rather than operational efficiency or public health priorities. Authors suggest that financialization has reshaped the business model of pharmaceutical companies, leading to a greater focus on “blockbuster medicines,” which are products that generate annual sales exceeding US$1 billion [101]. Common ownership occurs when multiple firms are partially owned by the same investors [69, 98]. This structure allows shareholders to diversify risk by investing in multiple competing firms, effectively promoting self-insurance [1, 10, 99–102]. In addition, common investors hold shares in both innovative (e.g., J&J, Pfizer, Eli Lilly, Amgen, and Novartis) and imitative companies (e.g., Teva, Sandoz, and Aurobindo) [98].

Our shareholder analysis points to BlackRock, UBS, The Vanguard Group, JPMorgan, and Varma Mutual Pension Insurance as the most influential shareholders in the production network. These are notably very large institutional investors. Anecdotal and empirical evidence suggest that institutional investors actively and passively engage with corporate boards and management to influence strategic decisions in pharmaceutical companies [69, 86, 98, 119–121]. The greater their stake in a company, the more influence they have over decisions [102]. Although large institutional investors own at most 5–8% of a single company, this is usually enough to position them among the top investors, granting them privileged access to decision-making processes [98, 99].

The most relevant common owners in the production network seem to be BlackRock, UBS, JPMorgan, Varma Mutual Pension, and The Vanguard Group. The common ownership analysis aligns nicely with the shareholder analysis. Ownership structures in the pharmaceutical industry raise concerns about the shifting locus of control, from healthcare stakeholders to financial entities, which may prioritize capital market interests over public health needs. Strategic behaviors such as mergers, acquisitions, and monopoly formation to maximize stock prices, in turn, could destabilize industrial ecosystems and inflate market prices [101, 122]. In addition, large pharmaceutical corporations often operate through hierarchical structures with multiple subsidiaries. When companies in the same industry are owned by common shareholders, competitive dynamics may be undermined [98, 99, 102, 123]. Subsidiaries of a single parent company typically avoid direct competition, and similar behavior may be expected between firms with overlapping ownership [98]. As a result, structured control over pharmaceutical companies may influence the allocation of R&D resources, innovation trajectories, commercialization timing, and drug pricing [118].

Researchers have studied the phenomenon of common ownership in the pharmaceutical market [69, 98, 99, 120]. Banal-Estañol et al. [69] investigated ownership networks among active US pharmaceutical firms between 2004 and 2014, revealing a dense and growing network among R&D-intense companies, while generic firms exhibited a sparser and declining network. In 2014, BlackRock Inc. was the biggest shareholder in the three largest pharmaceutical companies in the USA, namely J&J, Merck, and Pfizer, alongside other shared investors. Common ownership may facilitate strategic partnerships, allowing companies to collaborate in R&D and share technologies [119, 124], but might also be associated with reduced market competition [98, 125–127]. While prevalent across industries [99], common ownership may pose challenges to innovation and market dynamics. However, it is important to highlight that the level of coordination among such shareholders is currently unknown.

The companies with higher common ownership ratios in our analysis are some of the same companies that have already been cited as key market players [7, 23]. The surprise in this sample was Emergent BioSolutions, a company owned by some large investors, such as BlackRock, The Vanguard Group, State Street, and others. There appears to be a relationship between common ownership and firm-level outcomes such as innovation and production. Firms with higher common ownership ratios may benefit from improved information flows, reduced competitive pressure, or improved strategic alignment among shareholders, factors that could, in turn, strengthen their capacity to innovate and coordinate production [119, 124].

The prices in the mAb market were found to be extremely high, averaging US$127,430 per course or year (s = US$142,509), with 42.1% of mAbs priced above US$100,000, making them largely unaffordable at the individual level. These findings are consistent with the study by Hernandez et al. [35] that observed that the average annual price of FDA-approved mAbs was approximately US$96,731 (median=US$58,968) between 1997 and 2016. In their sample, 32% of the mAbs exceeded prescription costs of US$100,000 per year. The hypothesis raised by Hernandez et al. [35] that mAbs are generally very expensive technologies is confirmed by our study. In population terms, even the cheapest mAb treatment is too expensive to be purchased out-of-pocket for most of the population in low- to middle-income countries. Another hypothesis raised by Hernandez et al. [35] is that mAbs for cancer are more expensive than others. In fact, when we compared the mAbs categorized as “Oncology/Hematology” with the others, we also confirmed this result (p<0.001).

The current scenario of the mAb market is characterized by low competition [7, 22]. We found that most mAb markets on our database (82.5%) were monopolistic at the agent level. This high concentration has also been confirmed by other studies for drug classes. In Brazil, concentration levels for six classes of antineoplastic mAbs have been reported [71]: Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) values were 0.784 for anti-CD20 antibodies, 0.886 for anti-vascular endothelial growth factor/vascular endothelial growth factor receptor, 0.747 for anti-human epidermal growth factor receptor 2, 0.775 for anti-epidermal growth factor receptor, 0.471 for anti-programmed cell death protein 1/programmed death-ligand 1, and 0.366 for other classes. High market concentration is a well-known driver of high prices at the class and agent levels [7].

Monoclonal antibodies are effective and relatively safe solutions in most markets they enter. However, their applications are mostly very specific. Therefore, it is a surprise to see some of them among the best-selling drugs worldwide. In 2019, rituximab (Rituxan®: tenth, US$6.54 billion, Roche), infliximab (Remicade®: 15th, US$5.03 billion, J&J), and trastuzumab (Herceptin®: 12th, US$6.08 billion, Roche) were among the top 20 best-sellers worldwide in 2019. Adalimumab (Humira®: first, US$19.73 billion in 2019, AbbVie) was the best-selling drug in that year [24]. In 2023, Humira® was the second best-selling drug worldwide (US$14.4 billion), but rituximab, trastuzumab, and infliximab were no longer among the top 20. Despite the decline of these mAbs, others appeared on the best-selling lists, such as dupilumab (Dupixent®: sixth, US$11.6 billion, Regeneron and Sanofi), ustekinumab (Stelara®: eighth, US$10.9 billion, J&J), nivolumab (Opdivo®: ninth, US$10 billion, BMS and Ono), daratumumab (Darzalex® and Darzalex Faspro®: tenth, US$9.7 billion), rizankizumab-rzaa (Skyrizi®: 14th, US$7.7 billion, AbbVie), and ocrelizumab (Ocrevus®: 16th, US$7 billion, Roche). The high prices of mAbs is likely the best explanation for their commercial success.

Some limitations of our work are worth mentioning. (i) The simplistic categorization used for the fields of mAbs might not be optimal. We roughly used the same categories as Hernandez et al. [35] to improve comparability. However, as many diseases require multidisciplinary attention, there is some degree arbitrariness in their classification. (ii) Data on worldwide sales of mAbs were largely lacking and untrustworthy in the literature. Many companies did not report these data on their 10-K or 20-F forms [128] or even on their websites or public reports. Therefore, we were unable to analyze the concentration on the market for mAbs. (iii) The prices of the drugs were an estimate based on first indication protocols and the prices declared by the VA [77]. Therefore, annual costs may vary beyond what was described here. (iv) Analyzing mAb prices was a complicated task because of the multiplicities of presentations. In general, to get some results, we chose to consider only the first indications. Data on formulations built for specific purposes, different from the first indication, were removed from the dataset. (v) Annual doses were estimated for an adult with 70 kg and 1.70 m, unless the drug is specifically designed for children. Even then, there were some exceptions. For example, price data for alemtuzumab were only available for Lemtrada®. Therefore, the first indication substituted for multiple sclerosis. (vi) When two medicines in the exact same technology, presentation, pharmaceutical form, and dose had different prices, the lower value was considered. It is illogical for a buyer to purchase the most expensive medicine. (vii) Starter kits were also removed from the analysis because they serve a very specific and market-oriented purpose. (viii) Data on the shares of each shareholder in the companies were missing from the ORBIS database. Therefore, we were only able to conduct a breadth-oriented analysis rather than a depth-oriented analysis. (ix) Monoclonal antibodies for rare diseases were included in the analysis. Because many of these medicines are high priced, they may have influenced the overall price levels. However, this potential effect was reasonably controlled by reporting both the mean and the median values.

Conclusions

Monoclonal antibodies have transformed modern medicine, offering unprecedented therapeutic advances across a growing range of diseases. Their clinical impact is undeniable, with significant gains in safety and effectiveness; however, their persistently high prices raise serious concerns about equity and social welfare. Our analysis shows that mAb prices are objectively high. Moreover, drug prices appear to be shaped by multiple factors, including disease severity, rarity, availability of alternative treatments, presence of biosimilars on the market, level of innovation (reference drug or biosimilar), package size, administration technology (e.g., prefilled syringes, vials, pens), demand, production scale, market power and concentration, and common ownership. However, it remains questionable whether R&D or production costs play a significant role in determining the final price of these technologies. Innovation in the mAb sector has been prolific but remains heavily concentrated within high-income countries and a limited number of companies, many of which share common ownership through major financial investors. This concentration of innovation, production, and financial influence underscores the need for a closer scrutiny of market dynamics to ensure that the benefits of mAbs are more equitably distributed throughout the world. This work was an unprecedented attempt to characterize the market for mAbs from multiple perspectives and to draw conclusions about the trends, challenges, and opportunities it presents.
