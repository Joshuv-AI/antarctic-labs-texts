The longitudinal relationship of young children’s mental health with parental mental health and demographic factors during the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Natalie DeRoche, Mara Z. Vitolins, Joseph Skelton, Edward H. Ip, Callie L. Brown
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We aimed to assess the relationship of early child mental health measured through the Pediatric Symptom Checklist-17 with parental mental health and demographic factors over a two-year period during the COVID-19 pandemic. This study was a secondary analysis of survey data from a parental feeding practices study. Survey data were collected at baseline from 321 parents between February 2020 and January 2021 and two additional times over the next two years. Separate mixed effects multilevel regression models were used to examine the relationship over time between children’s mental health with child sex and race/ethnicity; parent depression, anxiety, and stress; and household income and food insecurity. Higher parent depression, anxiety, and stress scores were associated with worsening mental health for their children. Male children had higher inattentive PSC-17 subscale scores compared to female children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-025-00292-5.

Introduction

Early childhood, defined as birth to age eight [1], is a period of rapid social, cognitive, and behavioral development and change, rendering the mental health of young children highly susceptible to environmental influences [2]. These influences include parental mental health [3], race [4, 5], socioeconomic status [6, 7], and food insecurity [8]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, a host of additional factors were shown to have a negative impact on childhood mental health [9]. Many situations outside of the control of children (e.g., lockdown, virtual learning) added change and stress to their everyday lives. School closures negatively influenced children’s behavior and mental health due to the lack of structure, lack of peer connection, decreased family meals, increased screen time, and increased time in stressful home environments [10]. Children who experienced longer periods of virtual learning were found to have increased stress and decreased well-being compared to counterparts who had less virtual learning [11], and conversely, in-school preschool attendance was shown to be a protective factor against negative COVID-19-related mental health impacts [9]. Because of school closures and lockdowns, children experienced decreased physical activity and increased screen time, worsening their ability to cope with pandemic stressors and increasing their anxiety [12]. Additionally, many services and activities that families relied on for early childhood education and behavioral health services remained closed for long periods and some never re-opened, while others switched to virtual options [13]. As a result of these new changes, young children were found to have a significant increase in emotional reactivity, aggressive behaviors, and internalizing behaviors, such as anxiety during lockdown periods [14, 15].

Childhood mental health has also long been shown to be related to parental mental health [16], and findings during the COVID-19 pandemic reinforced this [17]. Positive parent–child interactions and less parental mental health distress serves as a protective factor for children, while increased parental stress and worsening parenting mental health were predictive of worsening child mental health [18, 19]. A systematic review found that children’s mental health was associated with both pandemic-related stressors and worsened mental health of parents. Another study found that parental stress and loneliness during the pandemic led to an increase in internalizing and externalizing symptoms in children, which was stronger in families from marginalized communities [18]. Poor parental well-being during the pandemic was found to lead to harsher parenting styles, which increased mental and behavioral health problems in children [20]. These studies were limited, however, by small sample sizes, cross-sectional data, and/or a focus on older, school-aged children.

Additionally, many of the necessary social supports that families, particularly low-income families, rely on were put on pause or stopped during this time, increasing the stress and anxiety families faced. For example, school meals are necessary for relieving the burden of food insecurity for many families. Although in March 2020 the United States government issued a nation-wide waiver to allow all schools to provide meals for children [21], during 2020–2021, there was a 4.7% and 30.7% drop in breakfast and lunch participation rates, respectively, from previous years [22].

While demographic and socioeconomic factors, food insecurity, and parental well-being have all been shown to impact early childhood mental health, it is unclear how these factors affect children’s mental health over time with the additional stressors of the pandemic. While there has been research that focused on the initial effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on parental and childhood mental health, few studies have examined the changes in early childhood and parental mental health longitudinally throughout the pandemic. The current study aims to address this gap. Our objectives were to (1) assess change in parent mental health (depression, anxiety, and stress) and child mental health (via the Pediatric Symptoms Checklist [PSC]-17) over a 2-year period during the COVID-19 pandemic; and (2) assess the association of pediatric mental health with: parental mental health (depression, anxiety, and stress) and with demographic and socioeconomic factors (parent education, child sex, child race/ethnicity, household income, and household food insecurity) over a two-year period during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Participants

We conducted a secondary analysis of data originally collected for a longitudinal study of parent feeding practices. 335 children between the ages of 3–5 years old were recruited for the original study from four pediatric clinics affiliated with a single institution in the Southeastern United States. These clinics consisted of an urban clinic, an urban pediatric residency continuity clinic, a suburban clinic, and a rural clinic. Children born prematurely (< 34 weeks’ gestation) or with a birth weight of < 2500 g were excluded from the study. Additionally, children with intellectual or developmental delays and children with medical conditions affecting weight or diet were excluded, as the data for the current study were collected as part of a larger study of parent feeding practices. If families had multiple children who qualified for the study, only the youngest child was approached for participation. The child and their caregiver were enrolled as a dyad. Caregivers had to be at least 18 years old, speak English, and be the child’s legal guardian (for the remainder of the manuscript, “caregivers” will be referred to as parents).

Procedure

Before participating in the study, parents received an explanation of the purpose and protocol of the research, were given an opportunity to ask questions, and then gave written consent. Baseline demographic, socioeconomic, and mental health survey data for both parents and children were collected in person at the baseline visit between February 2020 and January 2021. Parents completed the surveys at their child’s medical appointment, either on paper or using REDCap on a tablet. Parents also had the option to have the surveys read to them, however, no parents utilized this method. Parental reports of young children’s mental health symptoms have been found to be reliable [23], particularly for reports of externalizing symptoms, such as acting out, unwillingness to share and fighting [24, 25]. When discrepancies occur, they are most likely due to parental under-reporting of internalizing symptoms, such as feelings of hopelessness and worrying, because these symptoms are more difficult to observe [24]. The reliability of parental report of children’s mental health symptoms compared to children’s self-reported symptoms decreases as children age and become adolescents [26, 27].

Cross-sectional survey data were collected at three different time points (including baseline data) using the same survey questionnaires. After baseline data were collected, data were collected at approximately one-year intervals two additional times over the next two years (Year 1 follow-up data were collected between February 2021 and December 2021 and Year 2 follow-up data were collected between February 2022 and January 2023). Data was collected from families at three different times (Baseline, Year 1, and Year 2). Follow-up surveys were collected either via email, in which parents completed the survey on REDCap, or by phone, in which research staff read the parents the questions and recorded responses into REDCap. Participants received a gift card for participating in the study ($10, $15, and $20 at baseline, Year 1, and Year 2, respectively.) The Wake Forest University School of Medicine Institutional Review Board approved this study. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Measures

Demographic and socioeconomic factors

The child’s age, sex (male or female) and race/ethnicity (categorized in this analysis as non-Hispanic white, Non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and Other) were reported by the parent. Parent demographics were self-reported, including relationship to the child (mother, father, grandparent, other), age, and level of education (less than high school degree, high school degree or equivalent, some college but no degree, associate’s/ bachelor’s/ master’s or greater). Parents also reported their household income (less than $20,000, $20,000–$40,000, $40,000–$60,000, greater than $60,000). Food insecurity, defined as, “a household-level economic and social condition of limited or uncertain access to adequate food” [28], was assessed using The Hunger Vital Sign, a validated screening tool that is both sensitive and specific for identifying food insecurity [29, 30]. The Hunger Vital Sign consists of two questions which evaluate access to food over the last 12 months; responses include ‘often true’, ‘sometimes true’ or ‘never true’. A family is considered positive for food insecurity if they answer ‘often true’ or ‘sometimes true’ for one or both questions.

Child mental health

Child mental health and overall psychosocial functioning was evaluated using the Pediatric Symptom Checklist (PSC-17), which has strong internal consistency (α = 0.91). The PSC-17 is a validated and reliable 17-item instrument that measures internalizing behaviors, externalizing behaviors, and inattention [31–33]. This assessment provides a total score and subscale scores in these three areas. The internalizing subscale score measures symptoms of anxiety and depression, the externalizing subscale score measures conduct problems, and the inattention subscale score measures concentrating problems. Response choices to each item include ‘never’, ‘sometimes’, and ‘often’. The total score is obtained by summing the scores across the 17 items, and subscale scores are calculated by summing subsets of items for the externalizing, internalizing, and inattention subscales. A total score greater than or equal to 15 or a score greater than or equal to 7 on the inattention or externalizing subscale or greater than or equal to 5 on the internalizing subscale is considered a positive screen for behavioral/ emotional problems and poor psychosocial functioning [33].

Parent depression

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a validated measure that is widely regarded as the gold standard for depression screening in adults, was used to assess depressive symptoms (Cronbach α = 0.8 in validation study, 0.88 in this sample) [34]. Parents self-reported frequency (‘not at all’, ‘several days’, ‘more than half the days’, or ‘nearly every day’) to questions involving feeling down, changes in sleep, appetite or energy, and thoughts of death or suicide. Responses for the 9 items were summed to provide a total score (range 0–27). Scores between 5 and 9 are classified as mild depression; 10–14 as moderate depression; 15–19 as moderately severe depression; and 20–27 as severe depression. In this study, moderately severe and serve depression were considered a positive screen for depressive symptoms.

Parent anxiety

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item Scale (GAD-7) was used to evaluate anxiety. The GAD-7 is considered both sensitive and specific as a measure of anxiety levels in the past two weeks [35]. Parents could respond ‘not at all’, ‘several days’, ‘more than half the days’, or ‘nearly every day’ in response to questions concerning worrying, anxiety, restlessness and irritability. Responses are summed across the 7 items to provide a total score (range 0–21) with a score of 5–9 indicating mild anxiety, 10–14 indicating moderate anxiety, and 15–21 indicating severe anxiety. In this study, scores in the moderate and severe anxiety ranges were considered a positive screen for anxiety. The GAD-7 has been validated among diverse populations of US adults and has high internal consistency (Cronbach α = 0.92 in original study, 0.90 in this sample) and good test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation = 0.83) [35, 36].

Parent stress

The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) is a validated 10-item questionnaire used to measure stress. Parents answered ‘never’, ‘almost never’, ‘sometimes’, ‘fairly often’, or ‘very often’ in response to questions concerning how often they had feelings such as being in control, overwhelmed, nervous, confident in ability to cope, or unable to cope over the past month [37]. Item scores for the 4 positively stated items were reverse coded and then item scores were summed across the 10 items to obtain a total score (range 0–40 with a higher score indicating more stress). The PSS has been validated in diverse US populations and has high internal consistency (Cronbach α = 0.89 in original study, 0.82 in this sample) and test–retest reliability scores range from 0.55 to 0.85 [37, 38].

Timing of baseline data collection

Given that the baseline data collection occurred at varying points of the COVID-19 pandemic, we created an indicator variable to designate time of recruitment and baseline data collection. The following time points were designated: pre-pandemic phase 0 (February 2020—March 17, 2020), Phase 1 easing period (June 1, 2020—September 3, 2020), and Phase 2 easing period (September 4, 2020—February 2021). Of note, no data collection occurred during the period of March 18, 2020—June 3, 2020 due to stay at home orders and our institutional research COVID-19 guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Power calculation for this secondary data analysis was based on a simulation method that used observed data to set up parameters such that the study’s specific design (e.g., repeated measurements), sample size, and variability were accounted for. We selected PSC total score as outcome and parental stress (PSS) as fixed effect, and subject as random effect. The goal is to provide realistic information about the minimum effect size that could have been detected at 80% power. The calculation was based on n = 1000 runs, and each run was assessed using the Kenward-Roger test [39] for the detection of significant effect. The detectable effect size was found to be 0.25, a small to moderate effect size by Cohen’s criterion [40]. The power analysis was performed using the R package simr [41].

Summary statistics were used to describe the participant characteristics, including describing the prevalence of child and parent mental health problems over time. Differences in continuous mental health scale scores (PHQ total, GAD total, PSS total, PSC-17 total, and PSC-17 subscale scores) over time were assessed using an unadjusted linear regression with the year variable. Differences in categorical mental health scale scores (moderate and severe depression and anxiety, positive total PSC-17 score and subscale scores, and overall positive PSC-17 screen) over time were assessed using chi-square with the year variable. In bivariable analyses, separate mixed effects regression models for repeated measurements were used to examine the relationship between change in children’s mental health (separate outcome variables of total score and the 3 subscales of the PSC-17) with parent depression, anxiety, and stress. In multivariable analyses, separate mixed effects regression models for repeated measurements were used to examine the relationship between change in children’s mental health (separate outcome variables of total score and the 3 subscales of the PSC-17) with other variables identified a priori, including child sex, and race/ethnicity; parent age, education, depression, anxiety, and stress; household income and food insecurity; and timing of baseline data collection. Linear mixed models in Stata, utilizing full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation, assume that the data is missing at random, and can handle missing data on the outcome variable without listwise deletion. Potential interaction terms were tested between food insecurity and parent mental health variables; they were not significant and were not included in the final model (data not shown). To assess for collinearity of parent mental health variables, a correlation matrix was run, and all correlations were < 0.8. Additionally, we ran the same multivariable models as linear regression models (without the random effect of participant) and evaluated Variance Inflation Factors; all were < 5, indicating acceptable collinearity. Therefore, all parent mental health variables were included in the final models. Given the multiple comparisons (four child mental health outcomes), using the Bonferroni correction with an alpha of 0.05, a p-value of < 0.012 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using StataSE v14 (College Station, TX).

Results

Baseline demographics

Of the 335 children recruited and with baseline data collected for the original study, complete data were collected at a subsequent year (either year 1 or year 2) from 321 parent/child pairs and this represented the participants included in this study; 170 parents completed surveys at year 1 and 228 parents completed surveys at year 2. Children’s ages ranged from 3 to 5 years (mean = 4.4, SD = 0.84) at the time of recruitment and 50.8% were female. Children were racially and ethnically diverse (39.8% Non-Hispanic Black, 38.3% Non-Hispanic white, 17.3% Hispanic). The majority of the parents in the study were mothers (87.5%) and the average age of the parents was 30.9 years (SD = 6.5). Families were diverse in terms of both parental education and household income. Just under 10% of families had food insecurity (Table 1). Parent’s mental health

At baseline, 8.3% of parents had a positive screen on the GAD-7 indicating moderately severe (score 10–14) or severe anxiety (score greater than 15) (Table 2). On the PHQ-9, 8.9% of parents at baseline had scores between greater than or equal to 15, indicative of a positive screen for moderate or severe depression. At baseline, the level of perceived stress among parents was 19.9 (out of 40) on the PSS. The prevalence of positive screens on the PHQ-9, GAD-7, and PSS were not significantly different at Year 1 and Year 2. Parent mental health did not significantly change over time (Table 2, p > 0.1 for all).

Multivariable analyses

In unadjusted analyses, mixed effects regression models for repeated measurements were used to examine the relationship between change in children’s mental health (separate outcome variables of total score and the 3 subscales of the PSC-17) with parent depression, anxiety, and stress. Each one-point increase on the parent stress scale (indicating more stress) was associated with a 0.13 higher PSC-17 total score (β 0.13, 95% CI 0.07–0.19) and 0.03–0.05 higher subscales scores on the internalizing, externalizing, and inattention subscales (Table 3). Higher parent anxiety score was significantly associated with the child’s higher total, internalizing, and inattention PSC-17 scores. Discussion

The prevalence of adult and child mental health problems stayed relatively consistent throughout the 3-year period of the COVID-19 pandemic. Higher parent depression, anxiety, and stress scores were associated with higher total PSC-17 score and internalizing, externalizing, and inattention subscale scores for children. Additionally, male children had higher inattentive subscale scores compared to female children.

Our finding that parental mental health problems, indicated by greater reported stress, anxiety and/or depression symptoms, were associated with children’s mental health difficulties, as reflected in higher total and PSC-17 subscale scores, is consistent with prior research [16–18]. Children of parents with higher depression, stress and anxiety have been found to be more likely to develop maladaptive attachment styles, behaviors and social problems [42], consistent with the emotional and behavioral problems reflected in the PSC-17 scores recorded in this study. This observed relationship between children’s mental health and parental depression is consistent with other studies in which parental depression has been widely shown to correlate with a child’s psychological problems, so much so that reduction/management of parental depression (excluding post-partum depression), has been shown to correlate with a decrease in child’s mental health symptoms/severity [43].

Other factors such as social isolation, financial challenges, and difficulty of raising a young child during the pandemic likely influenced the parent’s self-reported stress [9]. For example, researchers found that women who had lost their jobs or who had difficulty with childcare during the Covid-19 pandemic showed larger increases in depression and anxiety over time than those not experiencing these difficulties [44]. While these socioeconomic factors likely contributed to the significant association between higher parental stress and elevated child’s total PSC-17 and all three subscale scores, they were not specifically assessed in our study. Therefore, we were unable to determine whether these factors may have directly influenced children’s mental health, rather than indirectly affecting it through the impact of increased parental negative mental health symptoms.

Our study did not show a statistically significant association with household food insecurity, household income, or parent education. This was surprising, as these factors have previously been shown to negatively influence children’s mental health through increased familial hardships, decreased protective factors, and increased distressing environments [45]. The burden of food insecurity is also felt most strongly by low-income families. While the COVID-19 pandemic saw many job layoffs, non-profit closures, and lack of access to school meals [46], there was also an increase in federal benefits for families (such as the child tax credit and increased Supplemental Nutrition Assistance (SNAP) benefits, which may have offset other income losses for lower income families and have been protective against worsening mental health changes.

Compared with girls, boys in this study had a significantly higher inattention subscale scores. During this two-year period in the pandemic, there were less activities both in-school and out of school for children to get exercise, play, and stimulation, so it is possible that this led to increased concentration difficulties at home. Since the PSC-17 was filled out by the parent, the higher score may be related to parental perceptions. These results are consistent with research showing that attention problems are more likely to be identified in boys than girls because boys are more likely to depict traditional, outward problems with attention, such as fidgeting [47].

While we do not have pre-COVID mental health data to compare the results of our study to, a large study in 2018, found that 3.9% of people reported symptoms of psychological distress on the Kessler 6 Psychological Distress Scale [48]. In April 2020, 13.6% of this group reported psychological distress [48]. It is likely that a similar increase in mental health symptoms would have been seen in our population as well. The lack of significant changes in the incidence of mental health in children and their parents throughout this two-year period during COVID was surprising. We expected that mental health problems would have been highest in the first year because of the increased stressors of lockdown and initial phases of the pandemic and would have slowly decreased by year 2. Because of the many fallouts of the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic (deaths of loved ones, financial burdens, layoffs, online learning), it is reasonable, however, that the incidence in mental health problems in kids and adults stayed steady throughout those two years and did not fall as the incidence of cases of COVID-19 began to drop. It is also possible that the mental health symptoms were worse during the time period that data collection for this study was paused (March-June 2020) and that our baseline data collection missed the worst of the mental health symptoms during lockdown. Alternatively, it is possible that the many supports put into place during COVID-19, such as the child tax credit and increased Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) benefits, were effective buffers and prevented worsening of parent and child mental health problems. Given these results, we encourage pediatric providers to screen for parent and child mental health at yearly well-child visits. Community health worker or provider follow-up is then needed to connect families identified as having mental health issues to the appropriate resources.

There are several limitations to this study. First, this study was a secondary analysis of data collected from a study investigating parental-child feeding practices. During the original study, we excluded children with intellectual or developmental disabilities, children with medical conditions affecting feeding, children born prematurely, and parents who did not speak English, While our sample population was racially, ethnically, and socioeconomically diverse, these population exclusions limit the generalizability of the current study. For example, previous research has found that parents of children with developmental disabilities [49] and/or children with complex medical conditions [50–52] often report significantly higher levels of depression and anxiety than parents of typically-developing children. Excluding this population potentially restricted our upper range of anxiety and depression scores as well as the percentage of parents reporting moderate-to-severe anxiety and/or depression. In addition, non-English-speaking residents in the United States often have higher levels of depression, stress, and anxiety as a result of factors such as resettlement, limited access to care, and previous trauma [53]. Thus, excluding these groups limits the generalizability of our findings.

Second, mental health assessments for both parents and children were self-reported by parents. This may have led to parents under- or over-reporting their own or their child’s behavioral and mental health symptoms. Some factors influencing the parental report of mental health symptoms include cultural norms, young children’s difficulty with communicating emotions to parents [54], parenting style [24], and mental health literacy [55] These factors are difficult to control in self-reported data, possibly resulting in some same-source bias in our data.

Third, while attempts were made to collect follow-up mental health data for parents and children in both year 1 and year 2, not all families reported data at all three time points, and many families had data from baseline and only one subsequent year. This made it more difficult to track yearly trends and draw conclusions about longitudinal changes throughout this two-year period. Additionally, we did not collect pre-pandemic mental health data on all participants. This would have beneficial to determine if prevalence of mental health problems increased at the beginning of the pandemic and remained subsequently high throughout it. Baseline data, collected between 2/2020 through 1/2021, captured different phases of the pandemic. While the time of baseline data collection variable was not significantly associated with child mental health outcomes in the multivariable model, it is still possible that the month that a parent completed the baseline surveys could have affected the baseline results. Because of the lack of pre-pandemic data, mental health symptoms in this population cannot be directly attributed to the COVID-19 pandemic. Other factors, including parental marital status, parental addiction, household size, adverse childhood experiences, and familial support were not collected in this study, but could have influenced both parent and child reported mental health symptoms. Finally, this study did not include a mechanistic investigation, and there is a possibility of unmeasured confounding.

Conclusions and future directions

Parent mental health concerns (including depression, anxiety and stress), were associated with worse child mental health. These findings can be generalized to other English-speaking parents with typically-developing children without significant medical conditions. The incidence of adult and child mental health problems stayed relatively consistent throughout the 2-year period of the COVID-19 pandemic. Future directions include evaluating long-term child mental health outcomes of screening for and intervening on parent and child mental health problems in the clinical setting. Efforts to improve children’s mental health should also consider assessing the mental health of parents and caregivers, and family-focused interventions should be considered and evaluated.
