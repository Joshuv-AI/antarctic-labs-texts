Fructose 1-phosphate inhibits mannose phosphate isomerase to suppress hepatocellular carcinogenesis
Authors: Yongqiang Wang, Xiangyang Zhang, Ningning Wang, Huimin Jiang, Ningning Liang, Chenxi Du, Chunzhao Yin, Rui Li, Lili Zhang, Qiaochu Tu, Jingwen Lv, Haoran Ma, Xiaodong Xu, Xinran Kong, Xin Chen, Guijun Liu, Shiting Chen, Hualing Xu, Jun Qin, Shengxian Li, Yongzhen Tao, Shan Zeng, Hong Shen, Marcus D. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fructose consumption increases the risk of obesity-related metabolic diseases and some cancers, but its role in hepatocellular carcinogenesis (HCC) remains controversial. Animal studies suggest that high fructose promotes HCC, whereas human data fail to support the positive link between fructose intake and elevated risk of liver cancer. Moreover, fructose metabolism is progressively attenuated in HCC with the loss of key fructolytic enzymes, including fructose-1,6-bisphosphate aldolase B (ALDOB). Here, we report that fructose suppresses HCC through fructose 1-phosphate (F1P)-mediated inhibition of mannose phosphate isomerase (MPI) in the context of ALDOB deficiency. Transcriptomic and metabolic flux analyses using human HCC cells and tissues revealed that liver cancer cells retain a significant ability to metabolize fructose despite the downregulation of fructolytic genes, with ALDOB showing the earliest and most pronounced suppression compared with GLUT2 and KHK. Dietary supplementation with 10% fructose suppressed HCC in liver-specific Aldob knockout mice. Further spatial and single-cell transcriptomic analyses of clinical HCC samples revealed the spatiotemporal dynamics of fructolytic gene expression and identified subsets of cancer cells that retain fructose uptake and phosphorylation capacity (SLC2A2⁺/KHK⁺) but lack ALDOB expression. Upon fructose exposure, accumulated F1P binds to and inhibits MPI, reducing protein N-glycosylation and triggering apoptosis due to maladaptive ER stress. We further performed virtual high-throughput screening of FDA-approved and clinical-trial drugs and identified ebselen as a potent MPI inhibitor. Taken together, the results of our study reveal a novel mechanism by which dietary fructose inhibits HCC through the F1P-MPI axis, suggesting a therapeutic strategy targeting metabolic vulnerabilities in cancer.

Introduction

HCC, the predominant type of liver cancer, is an aggressive malignancy characterized by significant metabolic rewiring, poor prognosis, and highly heterogeneous therapeutic responses.1 As the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, HCC imposes a staggering global health burden. Oncogenic transformation of normal hepatocytes to malignant cells is driven by viral infections, especially hepatitis B virus, or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH).2 For the past several decades, excessive fructose intake has emerged as a major dietary driver in the pathogenesis of MASH.3,4 The increasing global prevalence of a Westernized diet, heavily relying on high-fructose corn syrup, has been correlated with a parallel rise in MASH-associated carcinogenesis, making the interplay between dietary macronutrients and the risk of various cancers a subject of intense scientific research.

While previous studies have linked high fructose consumption to the incidence of various metabolic diseases5–7 and some gastrointestinal cancers, such as colorectal cancer (CRC),8–10 the role and underlying mechanisms of fructose metabolism in HCC remain controversial. This contention largely stems from the stark disparity between controlled murine models and human epidemiological observations. Although some animal studies have shown that fructose may promote HCC by enhancing protein O-GlcNAcylation11 or inflammation-driven lipogenesis12 via the gut–liver axis, recent findings suggest that fructose may also exert context-dependent antitumor effects. For example, fructose induces leptin production in adipocytes through mTORC1 activation, thereby enhancing CD8⁺ T-cell–mediated immune responses.13 However, previous studies reporting these pro-tumorigenic effects observed in animal models often utilize supraphysiological doses of fructose, which may fail to accurately recapitulate the nuanced metabolic environment within the complex human physiological milieu. On the one hand, the NIH AARP Diet and Health study carried out by the National Cancer Institute of the United States, the largest prospective cohort study on the relationship between diet and cancer risk, revealed an inverse association between fructose intake and liver cancer risk in women, while in men, fructose intake showed a weak inverse association with all cancers.14 A recent study on the effects of metabolism-related lifestyles and clinical risk factors on digestive system cancers in approximately 200,000 subjects in East Asian populations revealed an inverse causal link between sweet taste and HCC risk.15 On the other hand, an analysis of data from the European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition cohort of 477,206 participants from 10 European countries revealed a positive association between HCC incidence and artificially sweetened soft drinks, but not sugar-sweetened ones.16 This paradoxical lack of positive correlation with natural sugar-sweetened beverages further complicates the narrative, suggesting that hepatic fructose metabolism during malignant transformation is far from a straightforward oncogenic driver.

We and others reported that the progressive downregulation of genes important for fructose metabolism during the progression of HCC, including fructose-1,6-bisphosphate aldolase B (ALDOB)2,17, and demonstrated that this deficiency led to metabolic reprogramming of glucose, which promoted cancer cell proliferation through the insulin receptor pathway18, the PI3K-Akt19, and the pentose phosphate pathways (PPP).17 This epigenetic or transcriptional silencing of ALDOB represents a critical evolutionary juncture for the developing tumor. By shunting carbon flux away from glycolysis and toward PPP, malignant cells successfully generate copious amounts of NADPH and ribose-5-phosphate requisite for mitigating oxidative stress and sustaining rapid proliferation. Moreover, recent studies suggest that compared with normal hepatocytes, malignant cells have a limited capacity for fructose metabolism.20,21 In particular, cancer cells of melanoma, breast cancer, and cervical cancer are unable to metabolize fructose readily due to the lack of expression of ketohexokinase-C (KHK-C). Thus, dietary fructose appears to promote extrahepatic tumor growth through interorgan transfer of various lipid species, including lysophosphatidylcholines (LPC).20 Intriguingly, however, while primary hepatocytes can readily metabolize fructose, HCC cells almost completely lose the capability to utilize it as a fuel, highlighting a profound metabolic shift during malignant transformation. Consequently, the role and underlying mechanisms of fructose metabolism during oncogenic transformation in HCC remain elusive, leaving several knowledge gaps.

In this study, we found that genes involved in fructose metabolic pathways, such as GLUT2 (SLC2A2), KHK, and ALDOB, are significantly downregulated but not completely lost in human HCC tissues compared with peripheral normal tissues. Interestingly, ALDOB appears to be downregulated earlier and more markedly than GLUT2 and KHK during HCC progression, suggesting HCC cells may retain the capacity to metabolize fructose, presumably in the early stages. Consistently, metabolic tracing experiments using universally labeled 13C-fructose or 13C-glucose found decreased fructolysis but increased glycolysis in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues. Next, we performed spatial transcriptomic and single cell RNA-seq, and identified subsets of malignant hepatocytes/hepatoma cells that retained SLC2A2 and KHK expression but lacked ALDOB expression (SLC2A2+KHK+ALDOB-). To mimic the differential downregulation of ALDOB during human HCC progression, we further employed liver-specific ALDOB-KO mouse models of HCC. We found that dietary fructose suppresses HCC through F1P-mediated inhibition of MPI. This inhibition suppresses protein N-glycosylation, thereby triggering endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and subsequent apoptosis. Targeting this pathway with the clinical drug ebselen highlights a potential therapeutic strategy for HCC.

To assess the expression patterns of genes related to fructose and glucose metabolism in HCC, we analyzed a dataset from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (LIHC) cohort22 and reported that compared with normal tissues, tumor tissues with increased expression of SLC2A1 (GLUT1), HK1/2, GPI, PFKL, ALDOA and TPI1 exhibited upregulated glycolysis and polyol pathway activity (Supplementary Fig. 1a). In contrast, genes involved in fructose metabolism, including SLC2A2 (GLUT2), KHK, ALDOB, and TKFC, were consistently downregulated (Fig. 1b). Next, we performed Western blot analysis on paired HCC tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues (n = 42) and observed significant decreases in GLUT2, KHK and ALDOB expression and more pronounced loss of ALDOB expression in tumor tissues than in KHK tissue (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 1b and Supplementary Table 1). Similarly, tissue microarray analysis (TMA) of human HCC tissues (n = 182) revealed that ALDOB and KHK levels were much lower in HCC tumor tissues than in paired peripheral normal tissues, with a more prominent decrease in ALDOB than in KHK (Fig. 1d–f and Supplementary Tables 2–3). Interestingly, ALDOB and KHK gene expression in HCC tissues decreased in a grade-dependent manner from normal (N) to advanced (G4) grades. Compared with the N stage, ALDOB started significantly decreasing in G1, whereas KHK started significantly decreasing in G2 (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d).

To explore the metabolic consequences of differential gene expression in HCC, we performed metabolic flux analysis using universally labeled 13C6-glucose or 13C6-fructose on primary mouse hepatocytes and HCC cell lines to track metabolites involved in glycolysis and the TCA cycle (Fig. 1g). We found that primary hepatocytes metabolize fructose more readily than glucose does (Fig. 1h), whereas HCC cell lines, such as HepG2 (Fig. 1i), Huh7 (Supplementary Fig. 1e), and Hepa1-6 (Supplementary Fig. 1f), prefer glucose over fructose but can still metabolize fructose. Next, we performed similar tracing experiments using paired fresh human HCC tumor tissues (T) and peripheral normal tissues (N) and reported that compared with peripheral normal tissues (N), tumor tissues presented increased glycolysis and TCA levels and that they retained a significant ability to metabolize fructose, albeit at lower levels (Fig. 1j, k).

Taken together, these data indicate that despite the downregulation of fructolytic genes, HCC cells maintain their fructose-metabolizing capacity, with a more pronounced decrease in ALDOB than in KHK or GLUT2 within the same tumors.

Dietary fructose suppresses HCC progression in mice with Aldob deficiency

Given that fructose is ubiquitous in modern diets and cannot be avoided by patients with HCC, the early and pronounced loss of ALDOB in human HCC is therefore of particular clinical relevance. This clinical scenario, in which a partial blockade of fructose metabolism occurs, may result in a situation reminiscent of hereditary fructose intolerance (HFI).23,24 To further investigate this, we generated mice with hepatocyte-specific Aldob knockout (KO), and ALDOB KO did not significantly alter the expression of other genes in the fructose and polyol pathways (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Following exposure to the carcinogen diethylnitrosamine (DEN)/carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) to induce HCC, half of the mice were supplemented with 10% w/v fructose in the drinking water for 16 weeks, a concentration below that typically applied in high-fructose diet models (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d).11,13,20 At this dose, fructose supplementation had no significant effect on body weight, blood glucose, tumor incidence, tumor size, or circulating serum alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase (ALT and AST) levels in WT mice, in contrast to the pronounced metabolic and tumor-related alterations observed in KO mice (Fig. 2a–c and Supplementary Fig. 2e–h). Consistent with previous reports, loss of ALDOB reduced circulating glucose levels and markedly increased both the number and size of liver tumors, while having no significant effect on body weight or circulating ALT/AST levels (Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Fig. 2d–g).17,18,25 Strikingly, exposure to 10% w/v fructose suppressed HCC formation in the KO mice, in which only rare, small tumors could be detected in the livers of the Aldob-KO mice treated with fructose (Fig. 2a–c). F1P accumulation impairs HCC growth in vitro and in vivo

To determine whether fructose inhibits HCC by modulating tumor metabolism, we initially performed nontargeted metabolomics on mouse HCC tissues to systematically assess fructose-induced metabolic alterations, which revealed that hexose phosphate species were among the most prominently altered metabolites. However, owing to the isobaric nature of these metabolites, their specific identities (e.g., F1P versus F6P) could not be resolved by untargeted analysis alone. Therefore, hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) was used to successfully separate the isobaric hexose phosphate species (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b), as previously described.10,12,20 Our data revealed that F1P was the most significantly upregulated metabolite, whereas ATP, ADP, and AMP levels did not significantly change in the livers of Aldob-KO mice upon fructose exposure (Fig. 2d, e and Supplementary Fig. 3c, d), which suggests that fructose-induced ATP depletion is unlikely to play a major role in tumor suppression.

Given the low basal levels of KHK and ALDOB in various HCC cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 3e),26 we engineered a stable cell line with enhanced expression of KHK-C in Huh7 cells (Huh7KHK-C) to mimic mice with Aldob deficiency (Supplementary Fig. 3f, g). As expected, treating Huh7KHK-C cells with fructose led to rapid accumulation of F1P and inhibition of cell proliferation (Fig. 2f, g). These effects were reversed by pharmacological inhibition of KHK by the KHK inhibitor PF-06835919, suggesting a causal role for F1P (Fig. 2f, g). We reproduced these results in a xenograft model in which Hepa1-6Khk-c cells were implanted subcutaneously into nude mice (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 3g–j). In this context, fructose supplementation significantly attenuated tumor formation, whereas KHK inhibition abolished this effect. Furthermore, we observed strong inhibition of DEN-induced tumor formation in fructose-fed KO mice, which was completely reversed by KHK inhibition (Fig. 2i, j). In these tumors, F1P accumulation correlated with tumor suppression (Fig. 2k). Interestingly, we performed Mendelian randomization analyses on data from genome-wide association studies (GWASs) and reported a reversed association between single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) affecting F1P levels and the risk of developing HCC (Supplementary Fig. 3k). This aligns with another Mendelian randomization study in which a sweet taste was associated with a decreased risk of HCC development.15 Moreover, the levels of F1P in mouse HCC tissues were significantly negatively correlated with the number of tumors (Fig. 2l).

These data suggest that upon fructose exposure, the increase in F1P levels due to ALDOB deficiency is toxic to developing HCC tumors.

F1P disrupts protein N-glycosylation and triggers maladaptive ER stress

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which dietary fructose inhibits HCC via F1P accumulation, we performed a transcriptomic analysis using Huh7KHK-C cells exposed to fructose. We detected significant enrichment of genes associated with the unfolded protein response (UPR), ER stress, and N-glycosylation pathways in fructose-treated Huh7KHK-C cells with F1P accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Similarly, the expression of genes involved in ER stress and apoptosis was significantly upregulated in the Huh7KHK-C cells and tumor tissues of the KO mice treated with fructose (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). Western blot analysis revealed significant upregulation of major ER stress markers, such as phosphorylated protein kinase-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (p-Perk), activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), and C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP), and the apoptosis marker cleaved caspase-3, in the HCC tissues of the KO mice fed fructose-supplemented drinking water compared with those fed a normal diet (Fig. 3a). The upregulation of ER stress and apoptosis was readily reversed by KHK inhibition in Huh7KHK-C cells (Fig. 3b, c). We used transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to examine ER structure in mouse tumor tissues and Huh7KHK-C cells exposed to fructose. Because ER homeostasis is tightly linked to protein folding and N-glycosylation, we next examined global N-glycosylation. Protein N-glycosylation can be probed by using Concanavalin A (Con A) lectin blotting, which is specific to mannose residues linked through α-1,2-glycosidic bonds in protein N-glycosylation, and whose glycan modifications can be cleaved by PNGaseF, confirming their N-glycosylation status (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f).27 Using these methods, we found globally reduced protein N-glycosylation in tumor tissues from Aldob-KO mice fed 10% w/v fructose compared with that in control mice (Fig. 3f). In addition, we observed reduced protein N-glycosylation levels in Huh7KHK-C cells upon fructose treatment, which was largely reversed by KHK inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 4g).

To identify specific N-glycosylated proteins affected by F1P accumulation in HCC, we analyzed highly abundant N-glycosylated proteins in HCC tumor tissues compared with adjacent nontumor tissues using the ProteomeXchange database (PXD016406).28 This analysis revealed that the expression of CD147 (BSG), SE1L1 (SEL1L), and PSAP also markedly increased at the gene level in the LIHC database (Supplementary Fig. 5a–d). Furthermore, the N-glycosylation of CD147, SEL1L and PSAP was confirmed in Huh7KHK-C cells (Supplementary Fig. 5e). shRNA-mediated knockdown of CD147, SEL1L, or PSAP led to reduced growth in Huh7 cells, suggesting that these glycosylated proteins are important for the proliferation of HCC cells (Supplementary Fig. 5f–i). Additionally, we detected a marked decrease in the N-glycosylation of CD147, SE1L1 and PSAP in the KO mice fed a 10% fructose diet (Fig. 3g). These alterations were also observed in HCC cell models with F1P accumulation and were reversed by the KHK inhibitor (Supplementary Fig. 5j). To investigate whether F1P-mediated N-glycosylation defects are specific upstream drivers of the maladaptive integrated stress response (ISR) and subsequent apoptosis, we treated Huh7KHK-C cells with fructose, glucose or KHK inhibitor (KHKinh) for 48 h to mimic prolonged stress exposure. Fructose or fructose + glucose treatment robustly induced the expression of GADD34, BIP, CHOP, and ATF4 along with cleaved PARP. Consistent with these findings, fructose treatment also led to profound N-glycosylation defects, as evidenced by a global reduction in ConA binding and the hypoglycosylation of CD147, SEL1L, and PSAP. All these effects were completely abolished by KHKinh (Supplementary Fig. 5k–l).

To further examine the contribution of N-glycosylation to human HCC outcomes, we performed a Kaplan‒Meier survival analysis of the LIHC dataset. These data demonstrated that high expression of N-glycosylation genes alone or in combination with low-fructose metabolism genes was associated with poorer survival than low expression (Supplementary Fig. 5m, n).

Together, these data demonstrate that F1P accumulation in HCC disrupts protein N-glycosylation, leading to ER stress and apoptosis.

To elucidate fructose metabolic dynamics during hepatocarcinogenesis, we first identified hepatocytes in our in-house scRNA-seq dataset of surgically resected HCC tissues on the basis of canonical marker gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b, Supplementary Tables S4–5). We then inferred large-scale copy number variation (CNV) profiles using inferCNV to further stratify these hepatocytes into normal and malignant populations (Supplementary Fig. 7c–e). Subsequent pseudotime analysis performed on these defined malignant cells and normal hepatocytes using the Monocle algorithm demonstrated a downregulation of these fructolytic genes during hepatocyte malignant transformation (Fig. 4d–f and Supplementary Fig. 7f–i). Notably, compared with KHK and SLC2A2, ALDOB loss occurred earlier and to a greater extent (Fig. 4d–f and Supplementary Figs. 7, 8).

Despite this global downregulation, subsets of malignant hepatocytes retained expression of SLC2A2 and KHK but lacked ALDOB (SLC2A2+KHK+ALDOB-), indicating a preserved capacity for fructose uptake and phosphorylation but an impaired ability to cleave F1P. These cells were consistently detected across multiple datasets (in-house data, HRA001748 and GSE151530; Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 8a–d, f–i) and were enriched in pathways related to ER stress and protein N-linked glycosylation (Fig. 4h, Supplementary Fig. 8e, j and Supplementary Table 6), mirroring the features observed in Aldob-deficient mouse tumors upon fructose exposure (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 4).

Collectively, these spatial and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) revealed that fructolytic genes are progressively downregulated during HCC development, with ALDOB showing the earliest and most pronounced suppression. This deficiency may render early-stage tumors susceptible to fructose-induced ER stress and glycosylation defects.

We then performed molecular docking and dynamics simulations on the binding of F1P to MPI and determined that Arg295 is a critical residue for the interactions between the phosphate group of F1P and Arg295 (Fig. 5c). Arg295 is also the key binding site of MPI with F6P (Supplementary Fig. 9b), and its mutation to histidine (R295H) in patients leads to congenital disorders of glycosylation (CDG). Mutation of Arg295 to histidine strongly reduced the capacity of MPI to bind to both F1P and F6P, highlighting the critical role of this residue (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 9c–d). To experimentally validate these binding interactions, we conducted microscale thermophoresis (MST) experiments and reported that compared with F6P, F1P has a higher binding affinity for MPI, with Kd values of 9.83 ± 5.44 μM and 24.62 ± 16.79 μM, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9e, f), whereas F1P failed to bind to the MPI mutant R295H (Supplementary Fig. 9g). Importantly, in vitro enzymatic activity assays revealed that F1P inhibited MPI activity in a dose-dependent manner, whereas structurally similar compounds, such as fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (FBP) and the intracellularly abundant metabolite lactate, did not affect MPI activity (Fig. 5e). Treatment with F1P, or with fructose in the presence of KHK, led to substantial MPI inhibition (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 10a), further supporting the role of F1P as a direct MPI inhibitor and its production depending on the KHK-mediated phosphorylation of fructose. Conversely, fructose treatment of MPI mutant R295H cells did not elicit ER stress, protein N-glycosylation defects, or cell death (Supplementary Fig. 10b–d). Next, we used 13C6-labeled glucose to trace the metabolic pathways affected by F1P accumulation in two separate cell lines (Huh7KHK-C and Hepa1-6Khk-c) (Fig. 5g) and reported that F1P derived from fructose significantly reduced the flux of 13C6-labeled glucose to M6P and GDP-mannose but increased the levels of labeled F6P (Fig. 5h–k and Supplementary Fig. 10e–i).

Given the inhibitory relationship between F1P and MPI, we hypothesize that mannose treatment would bypass this inhibition. Mannose can be phosphorylated to M6P, which can subsequently form GDP-mannose without the need for MPI. To test this hypothesis, we treated Huh7KHK-C cells with physiologically relevant concentrations of mannose (50 µM), which reversed F1P-induced ER stress, dilation, and apoptosis, similar to KHK inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). In the DEN/CCl4-induced HCC model, we further confirmed that the fructose-induced attenuation of tumorigenesis was readily reversed by exposure to 0.3% w/v mannose in the drinking water without significantly affecting the body or liver weight (Fig. 5l, m and Supplementary Fig. 11c–e). Consistently, mannose treatment readily alleviated ER dilation (Fig. 5n), reduced the expression of ER stress- and apoptosis-related proteins (Fig. 5o), and restored the increased N-glycosylation of SE1L1, CD147, and PSAP (Supplementary Fig. 11f). Although mannose supplementation did not lower F1P levels or the AMP/ATP ratio, it significantly restored M6P and GDP-mannose levels, suggesting that the tumor-suppressive effect of F1P in HCC cells is mediated primarily through the inhibition of MPI (Supplementary Fig. 11g–j).

Together, these data indicate that fructose-derived F1P competitively binds to and inhibits MPI, leading to disrupted N-glycosylation, ER stress, and apoptosis.

MPI is a potential therapeutic target for HCC

To further validate the clinical relevance of MPI-mediated ER stress and protein N-glycosylation in the context of fructose metabolism in HCC, we analyzed paired HCC tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues from 42 patients and observed increased N-glycosylation of proteins, such as SE1L1, CD147, and PSAP (Fig. 6a, b, Supplementary Fig. 12a, b and Supplementary Table 1), and reduced F1P levels in tumor tissues (Fig. 6c) and elevated levels of M6P and GDP-mannose (Fig. 6d, e). Correlation analyses revealed that MPI protein abundance was positively correlated with N-glycosylation, whereas F1P levels were negatively correlated with N-glycosylation levels. Surprisingly, F1P levels were slightly negatively correlated with MPI protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 12c–e). Inspired by the tumor-suppressive effect of F1P on HCC through its interaction with MPI, we explored the therapeutic potential of MPI for HCC. Interestingly, compared with normal tissues, MPI is upregulated in many cancer types, including HCC (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 13a, b). As expected, knocking down MPI reduced the flux of 13C6-labeled glucose to N-glycosylation precursors, such as M6P and GDP-mannose (Fig. 6g, h), and the changes in the levels of nonlabeled M6P and GDP-mannose were reversed by mannose treatment (Fig. 6i). Moreover, genetic knockdown or chemical inhibition of MPI by ML089 in Huh7 cells suppressed cell proliferation, which was reversed by mannose supplementation (Supplementary Fig. 13c–e). These effects were recapitulated in in vivo subcutaneous tumor formation models in nude mice (MPI knockdown, Supplementary Fig. 13f–i; ML089, Supplementary Fig. 13j–l). Similarly, using a RAS and c-Myc dual-driven spontaneous mouse HCC model based on the Sleeping Beauty transposon system (Supplementary Fig. 14a), we observed reduced HCC formation and progression with MPI knockdown, which was reversed by 0.3% mannose supplementation (Fig. 6j–k and Supplementary Fig. 14b–h). Importantly, the tumor phenotypes were consistent with the protein markers associated with severe ER stress and disturbed glycosylation of N-glycoproteins (Fig. 6l and Supplementary Fig. 14i).

Together, these findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting MPI to inhibit HCC progression.

Ebselen targets MPI and inhibits HCC

To further explore the therapeutic potential of targeting MPI for HCC, we conducted a virtual high-throughput screening of ~2000 FDA-approved and clinical trial drugs, followed by 2D similarity screening using Tanimoto similarity against the TargetMol-Drug Repurposing Library. This combined approach identified ebselen as an effective MPI inhibitor, and prioritized based on its structural similarity to, and pharmacological tractability relative to, the known MPI inhibitor ML089. Ebselen also demonstrated additional anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and cytoprotective properties,37,38 further underscoring its therapeutic promise. Interestingly, molecular docking and simulations revealed strong binding of ebselen to MPI at Arg295 and Gln110 (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Fig. 15a), and the R295H mutation in MPI markedly impaired binding (Fig. 7b). Enzymatic activity assays confirmed the dose-dependent inhibition of MPI, with an IC50 of 0.12 μM (Fig. 7c). Furthermore, treatment of Huh7 cells with ebselen significantly reduced the flux of glucose to M6P and GDP-mannose (Fig. 7d). In vivo studies demonstrated that ebselen effectively suppressed HCC progression in nude mouse xenografts (Supplementary Fig. 15d–f). Consistent results were observed in spontaneous liver tumor models treated with ebselen without systemic toxicity (Fig. 7e, f and Supplementary Fig. 15g–m), and this effect was accompanied by increased ER stress (Fig. 7g) and disrupted N-glycosylation (Supplementary Fig. 15n). Notably, oral administration of 0.3% mannose largely reversed the tumor-suppressive effects of ebselen by alleviating ER stress and restoring protein N-glycosylation (Fig. 7e–g and Supplementary Fig. 15d–n). Furthermore, ebselen synergized with sorafenib to achieve enhanced suppression of HCC growth in a nude mouse model (Fig. 7h and Supplementary Fig. 15o–p). Additionally, ebselen strongly inhibited the growth of multiple cell lines with high MPI expression across several cancer types (Supplementary Fig. 15q–t).

As a glutathione peroxidase (GPX) mimetic, ebselen is a promiscuous drug with strong antioxidant properties that may inhibit ferroptosis. To exclude the possibility that the antitumor effect of ebselen is confounded by these generic properties, we performed a cell proliferation assay under ferroptotic conditions and found that the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) or the general antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC) failed to rescue the cell growth inhibition caused by ebselen (Supplementary Fig. 16a–f). To provide further evidence that the PERK-mediated ISR drives ebselen-induced apoptosis, we found that inhibition of ISR by the ISR inhibitor ISRIB significantly reversed ebselen-induced growth inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 16g), similar to the classic inducer of ER stress-mediated cell death, tunicamycin (Supplementary Fig. 16h–i). These data strongly support the conclusion that ebselen specifically targets MPI to cause UPR activation and ER stress to mediate apoptosis (not ferroptosis), independent of its generic antioxidant properties.

By exploring the metabolic vulnerability of cancer cells, we report that ebselen suppresses HCC through the selective targeting of MPI-mediated ER stress and apoptosis, suggesting a novel therapeutic strategy.

Discussion

HCC is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Although epidemiological data support a positive correlation between dietary intake of fructose and increased risks of some human cancers, the impact of dietary fructose on HCC remains poorly defined.8,13 In this study, we revealed an unexpected tumor suppressive role of dietary fructose in human HCC and a mouse model under conditions of ALDOB deficiency (Fig. 7i). We found that fructolytic genes (SLC2A2, KHK, ALDOB) were differentially downregulated during the progression of HCC, with ALDOB showing the earliest and most marked suppression. This progressive silencing reflects a dynamic evolutionary pressure during HCC, wherein the malignant cells actively remodel their metabolic machinery to secure bioenergetic advantages. Given the widespread presence of dietary fructose, this early ALDOB loss presents a clinically relevant metabolic vulnerability in HCC. Using liver-specific Aldob KO mice, we unexpectedly reported that dietary fructose inhibited HCC progression. This effect was mediated by the accumulation of F1P, which induced maladaptive ER stress and tumor cell death. Unlike transient or mild ER stress, which tumors frequently exploit to enhance their survival and maintain protein homeostasis through the UPR within the ER, the insurmountable F1P-driven stress drives malignant cells toward apoptosis. Spatial and single-cell transcriptomic analyses of clinical HCC samples revealed the spatiotemporal dynamics of fructolytic gene expression, identifying subsets of cancer cells that retain fructose uptake and phosphorylation capacity (SLC2A2⁺/KHK⁺) but lack ALDOB expression. These cells were enriched for ER stress and glycosylation-defect signatures. Mechanistically, F1P directly binds to MPI at Arg295, suppressing glucose-derived N-glycosylation and activating ER stress in HCC cells. Notably, MPI was upregulated in human HCC, and both pharmacological and genetic inhibition of MPI significantly impaired tumor progression. This metabolic vulnerability was further exploited by screening an FDA-approved drug library, and ebselen was identified as a potent MPI inhibitor that mimics the ability of F1P to suppress HCC in vitro and in vivo.

Although many studies have linked high fructose consumption to the incidence of gastrointestinal diseases,3,6,8,10 the role of fructose metabolism in HCC remains controversial. Animal studies largely support the tumor-promoting effects of fructose through various mechanisms, such as enhancing O-GlcNAcylation or inflammation-driven lipogenesis via the gut‒liver axis.11,12 Notably, a majority of these studies relied upon supraphysiological doses in rodent models to emulate the consequences of a Westernized diet rich in high-fructose corn syrup. Furthermore, these pro-tumorigenic models heavily emphasize systemic alterations, such as perturbations in the intestinal microbiome, compromised gut barrier integrity, and the subsequent translocation of endotoxins into the portal circulation, which collectively foster a highly inflammatory and pro-neoplastic hepatic niche. On the other hand, human data from epidemiological studies have yet to validate these pro-oncogenic effects of fructose. One study reported a negative association between fructose intake and liver cancer risk in women.14 Intriguingly, emerging data demonstrate that liver cancer cells have significantly downregulated fructose pathways, including the loss of key metabolic enzymes, such as KHK and ALDOB,17,21 although the upstream factors and consequences of this metabolic reprogramming remain largely unknown. As such, a recent study reported that most cancer cells were unable to use fructose readily as a nutrient and proposed a cell nonautonomous mechanism to account for this tumor-promoting phenomenon through interorgan transfer of various lipid species generated from liver.20

Our study also highlights the tumor suppressive role of F1P and the clinical potential of targeting MPI in multiple HCC mouse models. Mechanistically, F1P accumulation led to significant upregulation of ER stress markers and caused ER dilation in tumor tissues, which resulted in reduced protein N-glycosylation and altered N-glycosylation of key proteins, including SEL1L, CD147, and PSAP (Fig. 4). The aberrant glycosylation of CD147 is particularly noteworthy, given its well-established role in promoting extracellular matrix degradation, tumor angiogenesis, and metastatic dissemination. On the other hand, we identified MPI, a critical metabolic node orchestrating glucose and mannose metabolism in cancer cells, as a potential metabolic vulnerability. The inhibition of MPI by F1P disrupts the isomerization of glucose-derived F6P to M6P, impairing protein N-glycosylation (Figs. 5, 6). Prior studies have demonstrated that MPI inhibition not only blocks mannose-derived M6P entry into glycolysis but also leads to M6P accumulation, suppressing glucose-derived glycolysis,39–41 highlighting MPI as a promising therapeutic target. We further reported that the FDA-approved drug ebselen, a potent MPI inhibitor that binds at Arg295, dramatically suppresses HCC. Because ebselen possesses a well-characterized safety profile in humans, its translation into clinical oncology trials could be markedly accelerated. Interestingly, ebselen showed synergistic effects with sorafenib in suppressing HCC growth and demonstrated significant inhibitory effects across multiple cancer cell lines with high MPI expression (Fig. 7). Notably, although we performed in vitro enzymatic assays using millimolar F1P, our analysis of mouse and human HCC tissues indicated that F1P can accumulate to submillimolar to millimolar levels, especially in the context of ALDOB deficiency and fructose exposure. Furthermore, in our current model, fructose exposure-induced ATP depletion appeared to play a limited role in the antitumor effects of F1P (Figs. S3D and S11H).

Although our current study offers paradigm-shifting insights into the tumor-suppressive effects of fructose in HCC, several limitations remain to be addressed. First, the Aldob knockout mouse model recapitulates complete and constitutive gene loss, which likely oversimplifies the gradual and heterogeneous downregulation observed in human HCC. Consequently, the upstream factors governing the spatiotemporal suppression of fructolytic genes during HCC tumor progression remain to be resolved. A previous study revealed that F1P binds to PKM2 and induces its monomerization and nuclear translocation to activate HIF1α signaling.9 We speculate that hypoxia may drive HCC cell progression, enabling these cells to utilize low-dose fructose in the tumor microenvironment, while F1P accumulation could establish an adaptive feedback loop under hypoxic conditions. This intriguing interplay suggests that within the severely hypoxic cores of solid hepatic tumors, minimal fructose availability might paradoxically confer a survival advantage until the tipping point of ALDOB suppression is reached, after which it becomes lethal. Second, whether the known antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects of MPI may also contribute to its therapeutic efficacy remains to be studied. Future clinical research is warranted to test these antitumor properties in humans.

In conclusion, our work characterized metabolic vulnerability in HCC driven by ALDOB deficiency, and targeting F1P-mediated MPI inhibition with the FDA-approved drug ebselen offers an immediately actionable therapeutic strategy for HCC.

Materials and methods

Mouse models

All mice were pathogen-free and housed in an SPF animal facility at the Shanghai Institute of Nutrition and Health (SINH), Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China. The protocols for all mouse experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and the Animal Committee of SINH.

Liver-specific Aldob knockout male mice (Aldobf/f, WT; Aldobf/f +Alb-Cre, KO) were generated and fed a fructose-free diet modified from TestDiet AIN-93G as previously described.17 A primary liver cancer mouse model was established using DEN/CCl4-induced or DEN-induced methods as previously described17 and is outlined in the extended figures. The mice were fed control (sterile water) or 10% fructose drinking water (w/v) at the 12th week. On the day of sacrifice, the mice were fasted for 6 h (8 A.M. to 2 P.M.) and anesthetized with Avertin prior to euthanasia. The livers and tumors were promptly excised, weighed, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. Note: owing to the aggressive tumor formation in the DEN/CCl4 model, only larger tumors (>2 mm diameter) were counted, whereas all tumors were counted in the DEN model.

For the in vivo KHK inhibition experiment following the scheme of the DEN/CCl4-induced primary liver cancer mouse model, the KO mice were fed control (sterile water) or 10% fructose drinking water (w/v) at the 12th week. Moreover, vehicle (5% DMSO, 40% PEG 400, 5% Tween 80, 50% ddH2O) or the KHK inhibitor PF-06835919 was administered (oral gavage daily) to the mice at a dose of 50 mg/kg body weight.42

A primary liver cancer model was established in 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice using hydrodynamic injection as previously described.43 Briefly, mice were injected with a plasmid mixture (3 μg Sleeping Beauty transposase, 15 μg c-Myc, and 15 μg nRAS in 2.5 mL sterile 0.9% NaCl solution) and monitored for up to 45 days before euthanasia. The mice were fed control (sterile water) or 10% fructose drinking water (w/v) on the seventh day. Moreover, vehicle (5% DMSO, 40% PEG 400, 5% Tween 80, 50% ddH2O) or the MPI inhibitor ebselen (Shanghai MCE, HY-13750, dissolved in vehicle) was administered (oral gavage daily) to the mice at a dosage of 20 mg/kg body weight.

For the subcutaneous xenograft model, 1.5 × 10⁶ Hepa1-6 cells stably expressing Khk-c and either nontargeting shRNA (sh nc) or sh-Mpi (Supplementary Table 9) were subcutaneously injected into the right dorsal region of 4-week-old male athymic nude mice. After 25 days, the mice were euthanized, and the tumors were excised and weighed. The experimental procedures were conducted as previously described.44

Human subjects

Clinical tissues for microarray and immunohistochemistry analysis were collected in our previous study, and their clinical information is shown in Supplementary Tables 2–3.44

Cryopreserved tumor and precancerous tissues from 42 HCC patients were obtained from Shanghai Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital in our previous study44 and used for Western blot and metabolic analysis. Clinical information is included in Supplementary Table 1.

Fresh HCC tissues for metabolite labeling and single-cell RNA sequencing were collected from HCC patients who were hospitalized at Xiangya Hospital of Central South University, Changsha, China. This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (Approval No. 202103218). All sample collections were performed with the informed consent of the HCC patients. Clinical information is detailed in Supplementary Tables 4–5.

Cells and culture conditions

HEK293T, Huh7, HepG2, LM3, Hepa1-6, 786-O, C42, MCF7, and HT29 cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, unless otherwise specified. The initial glucose concentration was 25 mM, and the culture medium was replaced every 24 h. Mycoplasma contamination was tested biweekly. For fructose treatment, the cells were cultured in medium supplemented with 10 mM D-fructose (Sigma‒Aldrich) for the indicated durations.

Mouse primary hepatocyte collection

Primary hepatocytes were isolated from male C57BL/6 mice using a modified two-step perfusion method as previously described.44 Briefly, after anesthesia, the liver was perfused via the IVC with perfusion buffer, followed by collagenase digestion. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm strainer and purified using a Percoll gradient. Isolated hepatocytes were cultured in hepatocyte-1 medium (DMEM, 10% FBS, 2% penicillin/streptomycin) on collagen-coated 6-well plates at 5 × 105 to 1 × 106 cells per well. After 4 h of culture, the medium was changed to hepatocyte-2 medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin) for further culture.

Plasmid construction, lentiviral transduction, and generation of stable cell lines

pLKO-shRNA and scramble shRNA sequences were obtained from Mission RNAi. MPI, KHK, and ALDOB cDNAs were used as templates and inserted into the PCDH vector using the 2× Super Kfx MasterMix and SoSoo Cloning Kit. The primer information is detailed in Supplementary Table 6.

Lentiviruses were produced in 293T cells by cotransfection with psPAX2 and pMD2. G, along with the respective plasmids, using Lipofectamine 3000. After 48 hours of transfection, the cell supernatants were collected. To generate stable cell lines, cells were infected with the supernatants in the presence of 10 μg/ml polybrene. Selection was performed with puromycin for at least 4 days (Huh7, 2–4 μg/mL; Hepa1-6, 4–6 μg/mL).

MPI enzymatic assay

His-MPI or His-MPIR295H and His-G6PD were expressed and purified from E. coli and tested in different treatment groups for MPI enzymatic activity using a coupled enzymatic reaction.41 Briefly, samples were mixed in buffer containing 40 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 6 mM MgCl2, 5 mM Na2HPO4/KH2PO4, 20 μM PGI, 50 μM G6PD, and 2 mM NADP+. After the enzymatic reaction was initiated by the addition of 2 mM M6P, NADPH/H+ production was assessed by measuring the optical density at 341 nm for 1 h at room temperature.

Bulk RNA sequencing and transcriptional profiling

Bulk RNA sequencing was performed as previously described.17 Total RNA was isolated from Huh7 cells, and quality checked using a NanoDrop One. Library preparation and sequencing were performed by Majorbio Biotech using the Illumina platform. Data analysis was performed using featureCounts (v.2.0.0) and DESeq2 in R (v.4.0.3).

Statistical analysis

The data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 9. Unless otherwise specified, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups. Detailed statistical methods and sample sizes for each experiment are provided in the corresponding figure legends.
