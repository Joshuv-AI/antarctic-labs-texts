Linking bathythermal habitat selection to management of a migratory freshwater fish
Authors: Richard T Kraus, Matthew D Faust, Scott Colborne, Christopher S Vandergoot
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFor migratory fishes, habitat selection in dimensions of temperature and depth may be jointly used to define the bathythermal niche. Seasonal and long-term changes in the availability of bathythermal habitat can cause behavioral responses that have consequences for managing interjurisdictional fisheries that target migratory fishes. Management of such fisheries typically relies on standardized surveys to provide knowledge of stock status, yet changes in fish behavior may complicate interpretation of survey results. For example, changes in bathythermal habitat selection could uncouple fish availability from surveys designed to intercept migrating fish in predefined bathythermal habitats.MethodsWe applied electronic tagging methods to Lake Erie walleye (Sander vitreus) to address spatial management and stock assessment questions regarding bathythermal habitat. Specifically, we sought to determine if bathythermal habitat use varied in relation to body size, season, and time of day, with a particular focus on how these may influence availability to fisheries-independent surveys conducted during September-November.ResultsAs predicted, bathythermal habitat distribution fluctuated substantially throughout the year, was most limited during spawning months, and most expansive during fall migration. During the summer stratified months, walleye primarily selected epilimnetic habitats, despite the prevailing notion that colder hypolimnetic waters would be preferred during this time.ConclusionsWhile our results did not support a previous hypothesis that smaller fish were more available to assessment survey gears, our results did support previous assertions that walleye were more active and available to suspended gill nets during late crepuscular periods in fall. Considering uncertainties in water quality and long-term warming trends, our case study could improve decisions regarding spatial management of this species in the context of independent water quality management objectives.Keywords: Great lakes, Acoustic telemetry, Spatial fishery management, Percidae

Background

The environmental conditions that shape the behaviors and habitat of fishes include foundational variables of depth and temperature, which when combined form the bathythermal niche. The bathythermal niche represents the depth of habitat at which suitable temperatures can be found, which involves active habitat selection by fish based upon bioenergetic needs, ontogeny, and competition [1, 2]. In this way, both depth and temperature can be thought of as interacting resources [3], which may be in limited supply during some seasons or in some areas [4, 5]. Further, the bathythermal niche of fishes is subject to modification by climate change, a novel stressor that may potentially alter ecosystems globally [6–8].

The volume and location of bathythermal habitat for fishes can change both horizontally and vertically over seasonal and longer time scales [9, 10], affecting how fishers decide where to fish and how fishery managers make decisions about the quantity and location of fishing effort. As ectotherms in a rapidly warming climate, zoogeographic distributions of some species have shifted north [11–14], but many species are subject to geological constraints and some must adapt in-place [15–17]. Freshwater fishes, particularly those in lakes, are prime examples of species that may face geological constraints on movements in response to anthropogenically driven thermal conditions. Understanding population adaptation to climate warming at a local scale, relevant to resource management, can be complicated by seasonal and diel patterns of thermal structure in aquatic environments because critical limitations on a fish’s niche may occur only briefly [18–22]. Recent advancements in biologging are providing opportunities to study bathythermal habitat use directly to potentially capture responses to short-term critical events and establish baseline conditions for monitoring future trends in freshwater habitats [23–26].

The need to understand changes in bathythermal fish habitat is especially important in the most southern of the Laurentian Great Lakes, Lake Erie. Historically and currently, Lake Erie represents one of the most productive freshwater fisheries in the world, equal to or surpassing total combined commercial landings of all the other Laurentian Great Lakes [27]. Due to high nutrient loadings (cultural eutrophication), shallow geomorphology, and low residence time of water in Lake Erie, long-term changes in thermal structure are an ongoing concern for achieving water quality goals, sustaining fisheries, and restoring/conserving collapsed populations of native fishes [28–30]. Water temperatures have steadily increased over several decades, leading to higher maximum temperatures and shorter winters / longer summers – trends that are forecasted to continue [4, 31–34]. Increasingly warmer and shorter winters may disrupt egg development and reproduction of spring spawning species [35], and longer hotter summers depress the thermocline and increase hypoxia, reducing the volume of hypolimnetic summer refugia for cool and coldwater species [4, 30].

Currently, one of the most economically valuable and culturally important fisheries in the Great Lakes is for walleye, Sander vitreus. Predicted impacts of a warming ecosystem for walleye have been manifold with negative effects acting through changes in seasonal cycles of temperature [36–38], biotic interactions [39], and patterns of fishing effort [40], emphasizing an interest to understand and monitor key variables for sustainable resource management [41]. Walleye habitat selection may be altered by seasonal hypolimnetic hypoxia [9] and temperature preferences [25]. Bioenergetic models applied to smaller freshwater systems predict that warming conditions could increase annual walleye production as long as fish could survive summer temperatures [42]. Alternatively, application of regional-scale climate models predicted variable effects on habitat quantity in Lake Erie, ranging from declines in the western end to increases in the eastern end [43].

Walleye are a migratory species in the Great Lakes, spawning on shallow reef, shoal, or tributary habitats and moving toward deeper cooler areas during summer. In Lake Erie (Fig. 1), the largest populations exhibit this migration as a cyclical west-east cross-lake movement [44]. Walleye occupy a wide range of temperatures in Lake Erie, primarily representative of the epilimnion [45]. Annual thermal cycles are one important driver of cross-lake migrations [46] and bioenergetic growth performance [47]. Lake depths change from relatively shallow in the west to deep in the east. The distances moved by walleye are thought to vary with fish body size (i.e., total length = TL), and, to a lesser degree, sex, such that larger individuals (> 550 mm TL) and females spent more time in the eastern end of the lake and at lake depths > 13 m (note: in contrast, fishery managers define walleye habitat as areas < 13 m) [48–50]. Bounded by lake depth, bathymetric habitat selection estimated from electronic tags from large walleye (> 550 mm TL) indicated a strong affinity for demersal habitats [51]. Demersal habitat selection combined with evidence from fishery-independent gill net surveys highlighted that, by targeting depths that are too shallow in the water column, population assessments may under-sample larger/older individuals and overestimate exploitation and mortality [51]. While this previous example examined only vertical habitat selection, additional tagging studies could build off Gorman et al. [51] to address related questions about seasonal distribution, temperature, and behaviors of smaller/younger adults (< 550 mm TL).

A warming environment in Lake Erie would inherently shift the location and availability of preferred thermal habitat for walleye with unknown consequences to bathymetric habitat selection and adaptation to warmer conditions. Simultaneous measurement of both depth and temperature currently occupied by walleye is needed to predict changes in habitat selection. As bathythermal habitat selection changes, long-established population survey designs may become less effective at representing stock demographics, for example if fish niches shift to occupy greater depths than the current survey targets [52]. Currently, agency-specific fisheries-independent gill net surveys provide age-specific relative abundance of Lake Erie’s walleye population that directly informs a statistical catch-at-age model [41, 53]. Nets are fished during September-November at depths that hang from 1.8 m below the surface to 3.6 m or sometimes are set on the lake bottom. Fisheries managers are interested in understanding how biases in demographic information could be exacerbated if bathythermal habitat use also varies with fish body size.

Building on previous telemetry investigations, we implanted data logging acoustic telemetry tags in walleye to determine if bathythermal habitat use varied in relation to body size, season, and time of day. We tagged fish from a broader size range to be able to compare large (> 550 mm TL) and small adults (< 550 mm TL), and we parsed two years of detections and biologging data into seasonal stanzas (overwintering, spawning, summer feeding migration, fall return migration) and diel periods (night, dawn, day, and dusk) to be able to compare size effects on bathythermal habitat selection. Similar to Gorman et al. [51], we hypothesized that body length would be positively associated with deeper habitat depths throughout the year and the use of cooler metalimnetic or hypolimnetic temperatures during summer stratification. We further hypothesized that smaller fish would spend more time in shallower depths, where they would be more vulnerable to fishery-independent gill nets. Due to the previously described demersal behavior [51], we anticipated that seasonal migration across the full range of lake bathymetry would be reflected in a cyclical seasonal pattern with deeper habitat use during summer periods when the population has shifted east toward deeper areas of Lake Erie available in the central and east basins of the lake. Fall migration was of interest in the context of bathythermal habitat use because it coincides with cooling and seasonal turnover across the lake. Thus, we expected that larger fish would be positioned farther east than smaller fish and that overall mean longitude would decrease through the fall migration period. Because walleye are thought to be more vertically active during crepuscular periods [54, 55], we also modeled diel patterns to test the hypothesis that fish would spend more time near the surface near dusk and dawn hours, a rationale used to develop overnight fishery-independent gill net survey methods currently used across Lake Erie [56]. Our results provide further insight into how walleye behavior, specifically selection of bathythermal habitat, may influence population assessment and management in Lake Erie.

Methods

Acoustic telemetry of tagged fish

To obtain data on walleye bathythermal habitat use, we implanted acoustic telemetry data storage tags (model V13TP-ADST; mean volume = 17 mL, mean mass in air = 16 g; Innovasea, Inc., Nova Scotia, Canada) in n = 48 walleye on 5 April 2021. Fish were captured with short-term (< 1 h) gill net deployments at a known spawning area near Toussaint Reef in Ohio waters of Lake Erie (Fig. 1). We did not aim to select fish based upon size or sex: only based on what was available in the catches. As a consequence, nearly all fish were male (except two females) and ranged in TL from 440 to 628 mm (mean = 522 mm, s.d. = 50 mm). Although numerous areas support spawning populations of walleye throughout Lake Erie, the reef complex where fish were collected and tagged for this study is believed to the most productive location contributing to the lake-wide population [44], thus, we assumed that these fish are broadly representative of the population. Tags were surgically implanted into the peritoneum, following accepted guidelines for animal care [57] and previous successful studies on walleye and other fishes [58–62], although we briefly describe the surgical process below. Once captured, fish were held in large flow-through tanks that received a fresh supply of lake water. Walleye were transferred to a surgical cradle and electrically immobilized [63]. Tags were inserted via a small (~ 3 cm) incision on the fish’s ventral side, which was closed with 2 or 3 absorbable monofilament sutures [48, 59]. During surgeries, gills were irrigated with a hose to provide oxygenated water. Once a surgery was completed, fish were transferred back to a recovery tank until release, generally less than 15 min or until fish had regained equilibrium.

Before being released, walleye also received an external lock-on loop tag (210 mm overall length, 1.8 mm diameter) [64] with a message indicating a $100 USD reward for return of the internal transmitter. This high reward system was critical in recovering tags to retrieve the detailed depth and temperature records stored in the internal memory. We did not formally estimate tag reporting rate [65], but assumed that the high reward used was sufficient to elicit 100% reporting of either found or harvested tags [66]. The internal tag was slightly positively buoyant (mean mass = 16.2 g, mean volume = 17 mL, mean density = 0.95 g/mL), but not so buoyant as to affect the fish’s movements [67], such that if a fish died, the tag would float to the surface and potentially wash ashore. The tags were programmed to take measurements at 2-minute intervals by alternately recording either temperature or pressure, producing a continuous archive of data available if the tags were retrieved.

The tags were also capable of transmitting alternating coded acoustic signals of temperature and pressure at a random uniform interval between 180 and 300 s (240 s nominal delay), for up to 584 days, from the tagging date (5 April 2021). Transmitted detections of tagged fish were obtained from the Great Lake Acoustic Telemetry Observation System (GLATOS) [68], which is a network of autonomous passive acoustic receivers throughout the Great Lakes (Fig. 1). Prior to analysis, acoustic receiver data were corrected for clock drift and filtered to remove potential false detections that can result from signal collisions or incomplete signal transmissions [69, 70]. Tags were recovered by fishers (n = 9) and beachcombers (n = 5), and these were sent to the manufacturer to obtain the full data logs, which had a similar potential data recording capability that was never fully realized due to the shorter duration of fish at liberty prior to recapture. The archived data on the tags were continuous as compared with the data obtained from the acoustic telemetry receiver network, which only recorded depth or temperature whenever a tag transmission was detected by passive acoustic receivers. Due to the irregular detection intervals of the telemetered data, continuous archival data from the subset of n = 14 recovered tags (29% of released tags) were analyzed separately as described below.

Seasonal and diel depth estimation

To evaluate whether habitat depth varied with body length, we first developed a linear model to predict tag depth as a function of month, diel period and their interaction effect, where individual fish was modeled as a random effect using the lme4 package in R [71]. Diel period was determined using the suncalc package in R [72]. Crepuscular periods were defined as between twilight and either sunrise or sunset, based upon a central geographic coordinate (42.05°N, 81.50°W) in the middle of the central basin of Lake Erie. Accounting for monthly and diel main effects was necessary to account for previously described depth variation for walleye from Lake Erie [51]. The same model was applied separately to both the detection data and the data logs from recovered tags. From the detection data model, random effects coefficients, which represented an individual fish’s depth deviation from the global/population-averaged mean (after accounting for month and diel period), were regressed on total length (at time of tagging) for each month of the fall return migration period from September through November. We focused on this period because it is when fishery-independent gill net surveys are conducted by fishery management agencies to assess demographic composition and relative abundance of walleye stocks in Lake Erie. We excluded the data logs from recovered tags from this part of the analysis because of the limited and variable sample size of individuals.

Size-based return migration

The gill net assessment that informs walleye population status in Lake Erie is assumed to occur while walleye are moving east-west during an annual cross-lake return migration [46, 73], and fishery managers have had a long-standing interest in how migration timing varies with body length due to the size selectivity of the survey gears used [74, 75]. Lake Erie is oblong in shape with the long axis oriented approximately east-west; therefore, longitude is a reasonable metric for following walleye seasonal movements across the lake. Thus, we conducted an ANCOVA (analysis of covariance) of mean monthly longitudes of detections on fish total length at tagging from September (the onset of destratification) through November when the return migration is typically thought to have finished. Similar to the depth estimation, we excluded the data from recovered tags from this part of the analysis due to the limited fraction of continuously recorded data that could be matched with a receiver location.

Depth-temperature associations

To understand how temperature related to depth, we developed kernel density plots for four seasonal stanzas. The stanzas were: spawning (“Apr-May” = March 15th to May 15th ), summer stratification (“Jun-Sep” = May 15th to September 30th ), fall-turnover and stock assessment (“Oct-Nov” = October and November), and overwintering (“Dec-Mar” = December to March 15th ). The seasonal divisions were adjusted from strict monthly divisions because of historical peak walleye spawning dates (15 Mar to 15 May) in Lake Erie [76, 77]. Because temperature and depth information could not be transmitted at the same time, we binned the data by whole meter and degree Celsius, and subsequently calculated a daily residency index for each combination of depth and temperature for each individual fish. The daily residency index was based upon the method of Kessel et al. [78], which was calculated as the fraction of days that an individual fish was detected in a bathythermal bin divided by the total days detected in all bathythermal bins. This created a matrix in each stanza with a single residency index (or blank value) for each depth-temperature bin. To quantify the bathythermal conditions where fish spent most of their time, kernel density distributions were calculated using residency indices as weights. Estimation of weighted kernel density distribution was accomplished with the kde2d.weighted function of the ggtern R package [79]. For graphical comparison of residency indices with recent historical epilimnetic temperature distributions, surface temperature data from 1995 to 2023 were summarized into quantiles (from GLSEA2 [80]). Only monthly surface water temperature values were available from this source, and thus there was only an approximate match with the tag detection data. We conducted graphical comparisons of residency indices with the metalimnion depth range (15–17 m) [81] during summer stratification and survey gill net depths during fall return migration (nets extending from 1.8 to 3.6 m in Michigan and Ohio, and across variable depths in Ontario) [82]. Separately, for the recovered tag data, we compared marginal mean depth and temperature for each combination of month and diel period from mixed-effects modeling with individual fish as a random effect.

Mapping seasonal bathythermal habitat

To visualize available bathythermal habitat, we used lake bathymetry and seasonal mean surface water temperatures maps from GLSEA2 to map inter-decile areas from tag data. For the temperature map data, an interpolated daily surface temperature product was averaged for each stanza during the tag deployments to provide a more precise match with tag detection data. For depth, we calculated inter-decile ranges of lake depths where detections occurred (weighted by the number of detections), which provided a bathymetric boundary of preferred areas in each season. Because some receivers were suspended (0.5 to 2 m above the bottom) in the water column, lake depths were estimated from bathymetry [83] using receiver coordinates. We separately calculated inter-decile ranges of tag temperatures (also weighted by number of detections) to define seasonal area of surface temperatures that represented areas preferred by walleye. Maps of Lake Erie with each area were examined for overlap to view seasonal changes in bathythermal habitat preferences of walleye. To understand whether walleye tended to occur within areas of predicted preferred habitat, we plotted residency indices of detection data against the available habitat.

Vertical movements

To test the previous hypothesis that walleye increase vertical movements during crepuscular periods and become more available to suspended gill nets [56], we calculated the rate of change in depth (i.e., vertical speed) for each 4-minute recording interval from recovered tag data. Because tags were recovered on various dates, data logs varied in length, resulting in sample sizes of individuals that varied across months. We then applied the same model used for depth and temperature to fit vertical speed data and estimate marginal mean velocity in both the up and down directions.

Model evaluation

For all the linear mixed-effects models (with either detections or data logs), fit was evaluated by plotting residuals against time, explanatory power was evaluated with fixed-effect and conditional R2 coefficients of determination using the MuMin package in R [84], and the importance of random effects was evaluated with likelihood ratio tests. Marginal means from all the models were evaluated for significance through inspection of 95% confidence interval overlap. Other comparisons were evaluated with traditional t-statistic or F-statistic tests of significance at alpha = 0.05.

Results

Detection data summary

Acoustic tag detection data resulting from the passive receiver array in Lake Erie were filtered for potential false detections to produce a data set with N = 421,081 time-stamped detections from all 48 tagged fish. Each track was inspected for evidence of mortality, but all showed evidence of activity (movement among several receivers that were 10s of kilometers apart) up until the end of the track and thus were included in the analyses. Based upon spacing of receivers and previous short-term detection probability tests with walleye [85], the average maximum time between detections was expected to be on the order of hours to a few days. Short duration tracks were not censored from the data, and except for cases of recovered tags, were possible unreported recaptures. Periods at large ranged from 7 to 594 days (ending 13 April 2021 to 11 November 2022; mean = 356 days), and all fish were detected on multiple stations, ranging from 14 to 77 (mean = 43) per individual. At the time of tagging, fish total lengths (440 to 628 mm; mean = 522 mm, s.d. = 50 mm) were consistent with the typical size range of adult walleye in Lake Erie at that time.

Tags that were recovered from recaptured fish or found on shorelines (n = 14) were successfully processed to retrieve data logs producing a data set with N = 1,582,038 timestamped measurements of either temperature or depth (after data were censored for periods when the tags were not at-large in free-swimming walleye). Data records for individual fish ranged in duration from 36 to 584 days (ending 7 June 2021 to 10 November 2022; mean duration = 278 days), providing diel information from a variable number of fish (8 to 14 individuals per month).

Size effects on depth and return migration

In stark contrast with our hypothesis, fish total length had almost no predictive power for either mean tag depth or mean longitude (Fig. 2). Our linear mixed effects model of observed depths explained moderate variability (main effects R2 = 0.448) with an additional 10% explained by random effects of individual fish (conditional R2 = 0.551). The full interaction model with diel period and month was superior to a no-interaction model (log-likelihood = -462,003, Chi-square = 2,136.2, d.f.= 33, p < 0.001), and the random effects term was deemed necessary based upon likelihood ratio test (likelihood ratio = 32,963, d.f.= 1, p < 0.001). Accounting for month and diel period interaction, depth deviations of individual fish were constant across a size range of fish from 440 to 660 mm (F-statistic = 0.399, denominator d.f. = 46, p = 0.53). Similarly, the full interaction model of fish total length with month (September, October, and November only) and mean longitude was superior to both the no-interaction model (log-likelihood = -7,425.8, Chi-square = 261.5, d.f.= 2, p < 0.001) and the one without random effects (likelihood ratio = 28,151, d.f.= 1, p < 0.001), but the total length main effect was not significant (t-statistic = -0.585, Satterthwaite’s d.f. = 3.1, p < 0.563). A shift westward (a reduction in longitude of 1.3 degrees) from September to November was significant (t-statistic = -14.6, Satterthwaite’s d.f. = 18,920, p < 0.001) and evident from the plots (Fig. 2). Still, overall, the interaction model to explain longitude from fish total length accounted for a trivial amount of variability (main effects R2 = 0.065).

Seasonal bathythermal habitat comparison

Walleye occupied a wide range of bathythermal conditions, which shifted seasonally with prevailing epilimnetic temperatures (Fig. 3). The weighted kernel density estimates accounted for the variation in detections across individual fish, emphasizing tendencies for walleye to select a biased subset of bathythermal conditions. For example, during winter’s unstratified conditions, the center of mass of their bathythermal distribution was at 6 m and squarely within the interquartile range of recent typical surface temperatures (Fig. 3, Dec-Mar). While tag depths were only slightly shallower (ca. 5 m) than winter, during spawning months the center of mass of bathythermal distribution skewed toward warmer conditions with many observations recorded > 75th percentile of available temperatures (Fig. 3, Apr-May). During post-spawn summer months when stratification develops, the bathythermal distribution’s center of mass shifted deeper to ca. 13 m, but also exhibited a concentration of detections in cooler shallower conditions, representing early season pre-migration habitat (Fig. 3). While most of the summer stratified data were representative of epilimnetic conditions, the distribution also skewed toward deeper cooler conditions with many observations deeper than the metalimnion up to 23 m (Fig. 3, Jun-Sep). During fall turnover and walleye return migration months, the ranges of depths and temperatures were like stratified months, but the bathythermal distribution center of mass was shifted shallower (ca. 5 m) and toward the 25th percentile (ca. 11 °C) of available temperatures (Fig. 3, Oct-Nov).

The majority of bathythermal distribution was deeper than the depths targeted by interagency fall gill net surveys (nets stretched from 1.8 to 3.6 m target depths). Further, three tags were detected on receivers deployed at standard gill net set locations and these tags were substantially deeper than depths fished by the gill nets (mean fish depths: 7.5 to 15 m; Fig. 3, Oct-Nov). These detections were opportunistic as the survey gill nets were only set overnight.

Available bathythermal habitat area

Comparison of inter-decile area of bathymetric habitat area revealed strong seasonal changes with varying degrees of overlap with the detection data (Fig. 4; Table 1). Most of the lake was suitable as measured by either bathymetry, surface temperature or both, except for winter and spawning months (Fig. 4; Table 1). During spawning months, habitat areas were primarily restricted to the western end of the lake. Depth and temperature habitat areas overlapped in a small area (1957 km2, Table 1) around the islands, from the mouth of the Detroit River, and immediately adjacent to a few locations with very high residency indices on receivers placed on spawning reefs (Figs. 1 and 4). The distribution of detections during winter was similar to that during spawning months, but the bathythermal habitat area was more expansive, covering most of the western end of the lake (6786 km2, Table 1; Fig. 4). The most expansive habitat areas occurred during summer and fall months (Fig. 4; Table 1). During summer, bathythermal overlap occurred only in the central and eastern portions of the lake, whereas during fall months the overlap also occurred in the western portion (Fig. 4). Patterns of detection of walleye were similar between summer and fall, with many sites having moderately high residency in areas that were predicted to be suitable only thermally (central portion of the lake, Fig. 4) or bathymetrically (western end of the lake during summer, Fig. 4). While detections were widespread during summer and fall, there was a tendency for residency indices to be higher along the southern shore (Fig. 4). Detections of walleye in areas outside of bathythermal overlap areas are subject to interpretation but represent situations where fish may be selecting habitats based upon other variables (e.g., shallow spawning substrate on western reefs) or where they can find local microscale habitats with more suitable bathythermal conditions (e.g., suspended near the metalimnion in the middle of the lake).

Diel vertical behaviors

Analysis of the more detailed continuous data logs from the recovered tags provided additional insights to the hypothesis that diel vertical behavior may influence walleye catches in gill nets suspended near the surface. Prominent monthly changes in depth emphasized that fish were shallowest during spawning months of April and May (between 4 and 6 m mean depth) and deepest during stratified months of July, August, and September (between 12 and 17 m mean depth; Fig. 5). On average, fish depth was constant across diel periods (Fig. 5A) with a small tendency to be deeper during daytime when the mean depth was shallowest (e.g., April) and shallower during daytime when the mean depth was deepest (e.g., July and August). Variation in tag depth across diel periods was typically less than the reported accuracy of the tag pressure sensor (± 0.9 m), thus little significance can be attributed to these differences. Most important, data logs from recovered tags emphasized that fish spent almost no time at depths targeted by suspended gillnets, i.e., < 4 m (Fig. 5A). Some explanation for the apparent inefficacy of suspended gill net surveys was illustrated by the estimation of vertical speed, which exhibited strong diel and monthly variation (Fig. 5B). Vertical distances moved in either direction were small, typically − 1.5 m (down) to 1.8 m (up) as summarized by the 90th percentiles. As estimated by the 99th percentile, the maximum vertical movements were 6 m (either direction). Vertical speed was highest overall during July, August, September, and October, and during these months it increased significantly during crepuscular periods and to a lesser degree at night (Fig. 5B). Due to the 4-minute time step and depth accuracy, vertical speed estimates underestimate true speed but are reflective of increased activity that could result in availability to suspended gill nets: this is explored further in the discussion. Lake depths tended to be deeper than tag depths (Fig. 5C), simply illustrating that fish were suspended but close to the bottom. There was a small tendency for lake depths to decrease during some diel periods, which reflected a temporary nearshore shift toward shallower lake depths (Fig. 5C). Mean tag temperatures were reflective of typical annual cycles for Lake Erie and showed almost no diel variation, except for a small increase during daytime in July and August (Fig. 5D).

Discussion

In bathythermal dimensions, our results emphasized walleye as an epilimnetic coolwater species in Lake Erie. This result appeared counter to the assumption that walleye would occupy the hypolimnion [86, 87] during summer stratification unless hypoxia was present; however, hypoxia seasonally occurs and is irregularly distributed across the central basin [88, 89]. Including during 2021 and 2022, normoxic hypolimnetic habitat is abundant in Lake Erie during most years [30, 90]. Bias toward occupying warmer temperatures in colder months and cooler temperatures in stratified months helps to define how thermal habitats change seasonally and understand how these relate to long-term warming trends in the Great Lakes [4, 32, 91]. Because our results show walleye skew towards warmer temperatures (around 10 °C) during colder seasons, warmer winters in Lake Erie would not appear to present occupancy limitations as much as during summer when a habitat squeeze could occur [92]. In a scenario with warmer water temperatures, there would be less coolwater habitat (< 20 °C) in the epilimnion during summer. This would indicate that fish would either move to find cooler temperatures or spend more time in the hypolimnion. As increased temperatures reduce oxygen saturation, exclusion of walleye from the hypolimnion during seasonal hypoxia [87, 93] would be increasingly problematic. This phenomenon is called the oxythermal squeeze and has been extensively characterized for many other species [92, 94–96], particularly piscivores, such as striped bass (Morone saxatilis) in reservoir systems that may concentrate with prey when conditions are moderate or be forced into unsuitable habitats without prey [19, 97]. Similar to lake whitefish (Coregonus clupeaformis) in Lake Erie, eastward migration of walleye to avoid hypoxia would likely occur, but at some point, walleye could rely on increased prey consumption to overcome the bioenergetic demands of inhabiting super-optimal temperatures [19, 50, 98]. This possible future scenario could have unknown consequences for spatial management of walleye by causing them to spend more time in eastern portions of the lake that are outside of the current quota management zone (Fig. 1).

Combining previous work that characterized demersal habitat use of Lake Erie’s walleye [51] with epilimnetic habitat use observed in this study contributes to an explanation for why migration routes tend to follow the shoreline perimeter of the lake [46, 48]. During stratified months, walleye were often near areas where the thermocline intersects the bottom (depicted as the metalimnion, 15–17 m, in Fig. 4). Across a broad range of lake depths during other months they also tended to occur at depths similar to lake depths at receiver locations. Thus, the combination of being associated with the thermocline in areas where the thermocline is similar to lake depth results in walleye spending more time along the perimeter of the central basin where depth isopleths are 15–17 m. While this result was expected based upon previous depth telemetry research, the hypothesis that demersal habitat use varies with size or age [51] was not supported by our results. Using a broader size range of tagged fish than the previous study, small fish (< 550 mm TL) did not occupy shallower depths than large ones (> 550 mm TL), leaving an unresolved question about how effective suspended gill net surveys are for assessing walleye stocks. Also, unresolved is whether females (of which our sample only included two) would exhibit different bathythermal habitat selection than males. The additional information on vertical speed from data logs from recovered tags pointed toward an alternative hypothesis that changes in diel activity increase the availability of fish to suspended gill nets during crepuscular periods, especially during late summer and fall. This evidence from tagging supports Isbell and Rawson’s [56] earlier assertions that diel activity shifts fish toward the surface during crepuscular periods [refer to: 55], supporting the basis of current gill net population assessment methods.

Because the sampling rate of the data loggers we used would tend to underestimate the vertical speed changes, crepuscular changes in vertical activity are likely much greater than we observed. The changes in depth required for walleye to become available to suspended gill net surveys approaches one atmosphere of pressure (ca. 10 m in fresh water), and thus presents a challenge for physoclistous fishes to regulate swim bladder volume [99]. Most vertical movements in our data were less than one atmosphere, but due to underestimation bias from the recording interval, we can infer those bouts of movement toward the surface typically lasted less than 4 min and involved a greater vertical distance than we could quantify. Considering buoyancy regulation in physoclistous fishes, rapid vertical changes that return to the starting depth can be accommodated, whereas an abrupt decrease in depth could lead to barotrauma [100]. Excursions to shallower depth with compensatory return to the starting depth are evidenced by the mirror-image results for movement in the up and down directions (Fig. 5B). We further suspect that these rapid movements represent foraging behavior, which would help to explain why walleye catches correlate with bycatch of potential prey in suspended gill nets and trap nets [74, 101]. The lack of size effects on habitat depth points towards an additional hypothesis that variation in diel vertical activity is constant with fish size, but more work would be beneficial to investigate this hypothesis.

Habitat models for walleye are of interest to understand the efficacy of fishery-independent surveys, and the results of this work demonstrated how bathythermal habitat shifts seasonally across the entire lake. Although these seasonal changes help to explain why walleye tend to migrate east after spawning [46], our results also highlighted time-areas in which other dimensions of habitat use warrant investigation. Some considerations are (1) how forage availability and hypolimnetic hypoxia might explain why walleye occur in supra-optimal temperatures (> 20 °C) during stratification [87], (2) how bathythermal habitat occupation during spawning is influenced by habitat requirements of eggs and larvae [102, 103], and (3) how other dimensions of habitat might explain the tendency to occupy specific regions of the lake. On this last point, consider the residency indices that were more frequent and higher values along the southern shoreline (Fig. 4): these areas are heavily influenced by turbidity from the Maumee and Sandusky River plumes, whereas the northern half of the lake is influenced by the relatively clear Detroit River plume (Fig. 1) [81]. Because walleye have an optical sensory system that is highly sensitive to light, they may prefer to spend time in turbid areas with lower lux [54, 55, 104]. Testing this optical habitat hypothesis to better understand walleye habitat selection and migration in Lake Erie is an area of ongoing work.

Conclusions

Although depth and temperature are fundamental to habitat selection of aquatic ectotherms, many other abiotic and biotic factors shape the migration behavior of fishes [105]. We acknowledge above that optical conditions, spawning habitat selection, and distribution of prey have been investigated previously for walleye [106, 107]. Changes in the location of suitable bathythermal habitat may also affect other components of the ecosystem, for example by temporarily releasing benthic macroinvertebrates from predation in shallow areas that are too warm for brook char, Salvelinus fontinalis [108]. The ability to achieve broader goals of improved water quality, such as a reduction in nutrient inputs and hypoxia in Lake Erie, is also likely to have consequences for spatial management of fisheries. There is a growing understanding of how fish may move across jurisdictional boundaries in response to deteriorating environmental conditions [109]. What will happen if conditions improve is an open question because of the potential for fish stocks to be exposed to new levels of exploitation through behavior responses that allow them to occupy different spatial management units [110]. As fish seek out depths with their preferred temperatures under a warming climate, there is concern about hyperstability of stocks (where fishery catches are maintained due to concentration of individuals in spite of a declining population) both for walleye specifically and fishery resources in general [111]. In the Great Lakes and other aquatic ecosystems, the interplay between anthropogenic effects – either positive (e.g., nutrient reductions) or negative (e.g., cultural eutrophication) – are likely to influence migratory fish behavior and fisheries [112]. Because habitat selection of migratory fishes is the result of responses to manifold variables, the application of electronic tagging technologies may become essential for successful integration of water quality and fishery management.
