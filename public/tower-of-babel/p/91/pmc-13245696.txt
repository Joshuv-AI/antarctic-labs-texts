Neglected Minds in Neglected Spaces: Probable Common Mental Disorders and their Determinants among Married Women in Deprived Urban Communities of Uttarakhand, India: A Mixed-Methods Study
Authors: Santosh Kumar, Sapna Negi, Basavaraj Shankar Yankannavar, Bhavna Singh, Nilanjan Roy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Women residing in urban slums are disproportionately affected by common mental disorders (CMDs), often due to socioeconomic adversity and limited access to healthcare. In India, mental health among married women in deprived urban settings remains underexplored.Objectives:To assess the prevalence of probable CMDs and explore psychosocial determinants influencing mental health among married women residing in the urban slums of Uttarakhand.Materials and Methods:A mixed-methods study was conducted from January to March 2022 among married women aged 18–45 years living in the slums of Dehradun and Haridwar districts. Quantitative data were collected using the WHO self-reporting questionnaire (SRQ-20) through a cross-sectional survey (n = 250), while qualitative insights were obtained from 15 in-depth interviews. Descriptive statistics, Chi-square tests, and logistic regression analyses were performed using SPSS v25. Thematic analysis was used for qualitative data.Results:The prevalence of probable CMD was 22%. Somatic symptoms such as poor sleep, being tired all the time, poor digestion, and stomach discomfort were significantly associated with CMD (P < 0.005). Older age (>35 years) and low monthly household income (<₹ 10,000) were significant predictors of probable CMD. Qualitative findings highlighted poor living conditions, financial insecurity, and intimate partner violence as major stressors. Emotional support from family and religious coping emerged as key stress mitigators.Conclusion:Psychosocial distress is prevalent among married slum-dwelling women and is shaped by age, poverty, and gender-based violence. Integration of mental health services into primary care and targeted community interventions is crucial to address CMDs in this vulnerable group.

INTRODUCTION

Health is a fundamental human right, irrespective of an individual’s social, economic, or cultural background. The conditions in which people live profoundly influence their health, with access to clean and safe surroundings playing a crucial role. In many developing countries, rapid urban migration has become a common phenomenon, driven by the pursuit of economic opportunities. This steady movement toward cities not only reshapes urban landscapes but also raises important questions about equitable access to health-supportive environments.[1] These migrants mostly include poor laborers who cannot afford proper accommodation and thus reside in informal settlements, resulting in the development of slums. Thus, these slums are the byproduct of rapid and unplanned urban development and are mushrooming every day. From 2014 to 2018, the slum population climbed from 23% to 24% of the world’s urban population, resulting in over 1 billion urban poor. These slums are most prevalent in three regions of the world: Eastern and South-Eastern Asia, harboring 370 million inhabitants, Sub-Saharan Africa, with 238 million, and Central and Southern Asia, hosting 226 million.[2] These figures are largely attributed to India and China, which account for 36% of the world’s population.[3] In India, approximately 14 million urban slum dwellings accommodate over 65 million individuals, comprising nearly 25% of the country’s urban population.[3] The poor living conditions and persistent challenges to securing a stable livelihood in these slums have a profound impact on the physical and psychological health of their residents. These environments often lack basic infrastructure, sanitation, and access to healthcare, exacerbating vulnerability and deepening health disparities within urban populations.[4]

According to the Global Burden of Diseases 2019, mental illnesses affect women more than men since women are more prone to have psychological illnesses and are less likely to seek or obtain mental health services.[5] Various studies in India have reported that, besides poverty, common mental disorders (CMDs) are consistently linked to the female gender.[678910] The term common mental disorders (CMDs) refers to neurotic and non-psychotic conditions, most notably depression and anxiety disorders, which are frequently encountered in community and primary healthcare settings.[11] Women residing in slums are more likely to experience CMDs as a result of the gender hierarchy.[12] The prevailing male dominance often compels women to shoulder the full burden of domestic responsibilities, leaving little space for them to acknowledge or express psychological distress.[9] Further, due to the absence of targeted policies addressing the needs of seriously ill women, this exacerbates the pervasive stigma surrounding mental illness within Indian society.[13]

In India, especially in Uttarakhand, this crucial public health issue has received relatively scant attention. Given the poor integration of public health, it is perplexing to provide psychological health care in a primary care setting. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to determine the prevalence of CMDs among married women aged 18–45 who lived in the slums of Haridwar and Dehradun District. This study explored women’s understanding of mental health and factors affecting the mental health of women living in selected slums of Uttarakhand, India.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mixed-method research was undertaken in the urban slums of Uttarakhand’s Haridwar and Dehradun districts. The data was collected over a period of two months, from January 2022 to March 2022. The study included married women aged 18–45 years residing in the selected slum areas.

Quantitative phase

The cross-sectional survey was conducted in the year 2022 within the slums of Dehradun and Haridwar. A multistage cluster random sampling technique was employed. In the first stage, urban slums in Dehradun and Haridwar districts were listed using municipal corporation records. In the second stage, a total of 10 slum clusters (five from each district) were selected using probability proportional to size sampling. Each slum cluster was defined as a geographically contiguous settlement unit recognized by local authorities. In the third stage, households within each selected cluster were chosen using systematic random sampling. From each selected household, one eligible married woman was randomly selected using a simple random method. Sampling continued until the required sample size was achieved.

Assuming a prevalence of CMDs among married women in urban slums to be 22.1%,[9] with an absolute precision of 7.5%, a 95% confidence level, and a design effect of 2, the required sample size was calculated to be 236. After accounting for an anticipated non-response rate of 10%, the final sample size was estimated to be 260. The sample size calculation was performed using OpenEpi software (v3.01). Of the 260 eligible women approached, 250 consented to participate, yielding a response rate of 96.2%. Ten women declined participation due to time constraints and a lack of willingness to participate. No systematic differences were observed between respondents and non-respondents.

To assess the presence of CMDs among participants, the self-reporting questionnaire-20 (SRQ-20),[14] a standardized screening tool developed by the WHO was used. This 20-item questionnaire is specifically designed for use in primary care settings to screen for non-psychotic mental health conditions. Compared to other psychiatric screening tools, the SRQ-20 is cost-effective, easy to administer, and has demonstrated strong face and criterion validity. Its simplicity and clarity make it particularly suitable for use by field workers in community-based settings.[14] In this study, a cut-off score of >10 on the SRQ-20 was used to indicate probable CMD.[15] While several Indian studies have used lower cut-offs (≥7 or ≥8), higher cut-offs have also been applied in low-resource community settings to improve specificity and reduce false positives. Given the socio-cultural context, low literacy levels, and risk of symptom over-reporting in vulnerable populations, a higher threshold was selected to improve diagnostic specificity. However, it is acknowledged that the choice of cut-off significantly influences prevalence estimates, and future studies should validate optimal SRQ-20 cut-offs in local languages and cultural contexts. Also, SRQ-20 is a screening instrument and not a diagnostic tool; the findings represent probable CMD rather than confirmed psychiatric diagnoses.

To analyze the quantitative data, IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 25) was used. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and proportions, were computed to summarize the characteristics of the study population. To explore associations between variables, Chi-square tests were initially conducted to assess the relationship between somatic symptoms and the presence of CMDs. Subsequently, a stepwise multivariable binary logistic regression approach was adopted to examine the association between CMDs and various sociodemographic factors. Variables with P < 0.20 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariable model. The final model was derived using backward likelihood ratio elimination based on statistical significance, model fit, and theoretical relevance supported by the literature.[6916] Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance statistics. All variables had VIF values <2 and tolerance >0.5, indicating the absence of significant multicollinearity. Somatic symptoms were not included in the multivariable model as predictors because they represent clinical manifestations of CMD rather than independent sociodemographic determinants. Including symptom variables would lead to conceptual overlap and overadjustment bias. Therefore, only upstream sociodemographic and structural determinants were included in the regression model.

Qualitative phase

Purposive sampling was used to select married women who were slum inhabitants for in-depth interviews. Data collection was continued until thematic saturation in the interviews was attained, resulting in a total of 15 completed interviews. After the study objectives were clearly communicated and formal informed consent was obtained, each participant was interviewed individually, ensuring privacy and confidentiality. Using a semi-structured interview guide, the participants were asked about their understanding of mental health, the daily factors impacting their mental well-being, and the coping mechanisms they employ. With the participants’ consent, all interviews were recorded digitally using a voice recorder and afterward transcribed in the native language and then translated into English. Interviews were taken by trained personnel, each of whom lasted for 7–15 min. Thematic analysis, following Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework, was used for qualitative data analysis. Transcripts were coded inductively and iteratively, with codes grouped into categories and themes. To ensure rigor, peer debriefing was conducted among the research team, reflexive memos were maintained, and analyst triangulation was used during coding. Although interviews were brief (7–15 min), thematic saturation was achieved due to focused interviewing and homogeneity of the study population. A convergent mixed-methods design was used, wherein quantitative and qualitative data were collected concurrently, analyzed independently, and integrated during interpretation. Integration occurred through triangulation of findings, where qualitative themes were used to explain and contextualize quantitative associations. This approach followed the Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study guidelines.

Ethical approval

The AIIMS, Rishikesh institutional ethics committee gave its clearance for the study’s ethical conduct. IEC no: No. 102/IEC/EM/2021. Confidentiality was maintained, and participants were recruited after obtaining consent. Participants reporting suicidal ideation were immediately referred to the nearest primary health center and district mental health services. Field investigators were trained in psychological first aid and crisis response. Confidential referral pathways were established with local health facilities and ASHAs to ensure follow-up support.

RESULTS

Quantitative findings

Overall, 250 eligible women participated in the study. The mean age was 40 years (±8.9 SD), with slightly more than half the women (67.6%) above 35 years of age [Table 1]. The majority of them (89.6%) belong to the Hindu religion. Only 15.6% were illiterate, and nearly half (42.4%) had attained education up to the primary level. About 76.4% were housewives by profession, whereas the rest were engaged in menial jobs of housekeeping, labor, and farming. About 72% of them had a monthly family income below Rs. 10,000 [Table 1].

Qualitative findings

A total of 15 women participated in-depth interview. From content analysis, three categories emerged: 1. Understanding of the mental health concept; 2. Factors leading to mental distress; and 3. Stress mitigators [Figure 2].

Concepts and determinants of mental health of married women living in slums

Category 1: Understanding of mental health concept

The participants described a mentally healthy person as one who is not suffering from any illness and seems happy in his/her life. Almost all of them perceived that a mentally healthy person has a harmonious life and healthy relationships with her husband and in-laws.

“I think mental health means someone is not suffering from any illness and is seemingly happy” (38-year-old woman)

Because public health initiatives focusing on mental well-being are quite rare in India, many of them were unfamiliar with the provision of primary healthcare services for CMDs, while others considered mental health issues to be a minor issue that can be resolved on their own or does not require professional assistance. This could explain why the informants did not consider seeking mental health services.

“I don’t think there is any solution for tension or stress.” (41-year-old woman)

“I suggest that before going [to see] a doctor for…depression… maybe you just have to.… Things are much better if you [try] the natural way …” (38-year-old woman)

Instead, most informants believed that mental health treatment is only effective in severe circumstances, such as when an individual is oblivious of himself or his surroundings and poses a threat to himself or others.

“If that person is mad and attacks others or himself, then he/she may be sent to a mental hospital...” (46-year-old woman)

Category 2: Factors leading to mental distress

Three factors emerged as major factors influencing participants’ mental health: (1) access to basic needs, (2) future insecurity, and (3) intimate partner violence.

Access to basic needs

Basic necessities include food, housing, clothing, education, and healthcare. However, the slum dwellers struggle to meet their basic needs adequately. Because the majority of them are migrants from Bihar and West Bengal, they are unable to afford land due to exorbitant rent prices, so they live in kaccha houses. According to the interviews, they used water from a common water supply line for drinking and cooking, but only for a limited time. Most of the houses were made of soil, tin, grass, and straw, and they were extremely small, filthy, and stuffy, making them vulnerable to physical dangers. Furthermore, the slum dwellers revealed that their residents have been demolished several times in the past due to the establishment of plants/factories, Kumbh fairs, or elections, and that they may be replaced in the future, which often horrifies them.

“We were removed for construction of Godown. We earlier had houses made up of tin sheds but they were demolished by the government. Few got lands whereas others didn’t. We can be removed from here too. This is their land they can do anything. Someone was saying that they are going to make a Plant here, we have got 5 months time period. Then we will be removed. Where will we go?” (32-year-old woman)

Furthermore, their sanitary systems were deplorable. The majority preferred open defecation near riverbanks or railway lines. Female participants expressed dismay about going out in the open for defecation due to the threat of wild animals, sexual violence against women, and the discomfort of traveling at night/during broad daylight or during the rainy season.

We don’t have latrines at home, so we go in open. There are lot of issues in going in open. This is a forest area. One or two women can’t go. We usually go in Pair of three or four. The alcoholics keep roaming around that why we go in groups. At night, we have to hold the nature’s call, until it’s not very urgent.” (36-year-old woman)

Financial insecurity

The vast majority of the women’s husbands worked mostly in the unorganized sector (laborers, house helpers, rickshaw pullers, drivers, factory workers, rag pickers, and shopkeepers). Women typically were responsible for taking care of household duties. The employment of their husbands was also unpredictable due to the weather, whereas many were suspended during the coronavirus pandemic. As a result, a lot of them cited financial hardship as the main source of stress.

There is only one person to earn and four to eat. What will a person do? How will he fulfill all needs”? (25-year-old woman)

One of the women expressed her distress over not being able to help her husband earn more income for her children.

“I am worried only above my daughters. We don’t have enough money. If I could have got some work to do, I can earn something so that they can study well.” (24-year-old woman)

Intimate partner/domestic violence

More than half of the wives reported that their husbands drank regularly and excessively. A few of the wives of heavy drinkers also reported intimate partner abuse, which had a major negative effect on their psychological well-being. One of the interviewees who revealed having suicidal thoughts was a victim of intimate partner abuse. She explained how her marital problems and her husband’s intimate violence were induced by financial issues.

“My husband is a labourer. We lived happily. But since COVID came, he had no work to do. So, he started drinking at home. I tried to stop him several times, but he said he is stressed. One day, we had heated arguments. And in anger, he slapped me hard. It happened only once.” (24-year-old woman)

A 25-year-old married woman whose husband beat her regularly revealed how her in-laws and husband physically and emotionally abused her for not having a male child:

“ have three daughters. My husband and mother-in-law want a boy. She keeps taunting me that if I can’t give a boy, she will send me back to my mother and got another bride. My mother lives alone. She doesn’t have money. What will I do? This gives me a lot of stress”. (25-year-old woman)

However, none of the women acknowledged turning to the legal system for assistance when faced with violence from an intimate partner or other family members.

Category 3: Stress mitigators

When stressed, most participants pursued social assistance from family members (e.g., parents, brothers/sisters, or neighbors). Participants’ social networks suggested them how to deal with their prevailing stressors. For example, one participant stated:

“I share with my parents, and they consoled me. I keep saying that i am tired of this life, so they tell me you have two children, you should think about them.” (24-year-old woman)

The majority of them also revealed that they rely on spiritual powers to relieve stress by offering prayers. Participants saw it as the ultimate method for redirecting their focus away from their difficulties and giving them a positive perspective on life.

“I keep praying to God to keep my family healthy and provide us food every day. We can do nothing else but pray.” (29-year-old woman)

DISCUSSION

This mixed-method study voiced concerns about the increasing likelihood of psychological disorders among married women living in urban slums of India. Women who have an alcoholic husband, are victims of intimate or domestic violence, or have a low family income and limited access to basic necessities, are more vulnerable to CMD. At the same time, when a variety of more temporal stressful events (such as budget troubles and unemployment) hit, this could lead to chronic CMDs in females. In our sample, some women reported suicidal thoughts, highlighting the seriousness of CMDs. All of the study’s female participants, including those who had suicidal thoughts, did not refer to seeking expert psychological assistance. Instead, they rely on a range of mitigating variables, such as social support from family, friends, and community members, as well as trust in a higher power, to help them cope with mental agony. Given the criticality of addressing psychological issues among Indian women, these findings point out possible avenues for psychosocial interventions for married slum-dwelling women.

According to the findings of this study, 22% of married women living in Uttarakhand slums have a CMD. This was discovered to be consistent with several Indian studies. Study by Panigrahi et al.[9] reported 22.1% CMD prevalence among women in Bhubaneswar slums, whereas Nair et al.[6] reported 44% prevalence among South Indian slum-dwelling women, which is higher than our study. Somatic symptoms are a significant predictor of CMD. Female depressed patients reported more somatic symptoms in the form of tiredness, appetite, and sleep problems than men in research done in North India,[17] and there was a statistically significant difference. This matches the findings of our study perfectly. Given the high prevalence of CMD and its linkage with somatic symptoms in community settings, general practitioners and lay health workers must be trained to detect and manage CMDs at the primary level.[6]

Women’s mental health can be improved or harmed by social determinants. These factors include gender, educational level, age, socioeconomic level, family income, relationship status, occupation, religion, location of residence, and so on.[4] Our study’s results showed that women with poor family incomes above the age of 35 years were at an increased risk of developing CMD than their younger counterparts. The qualitative findings of our study also reported that women who were housewives or had unemployed husbands were more stressed and had future insecurities. Panigrahi et al.[9], Khan and Flora[18], and Kiran et al.[16] provide evidence in favor of the positive association of CMD with age. The effects of low family income have also been studied by Kiran et al.,[16] Ashe and Routray[19], and Khan and Flora.[18] According to a systematic review, the most important risk factors for poor mental health in women are low income, poverty, and socioeconomically disadvantaged position. Therefore, providing women and the members of their homes with significant social support and employment avenues can actually boost their mental health.

A further element that was discovered to be connected to mental health was a lack of basic necessities. Participants in our study cited the lack of toilet facilities, decent housing, water, and sanitary conditions as distressing factors. This finding is consistent with research conducted in the slums of Mumbai.[8] These scholars concluded that different people may be influenced by slum hardship in diverse ways, likely to result in psychological distress via different “mechanisms.” As a result, it is vital to theorize CMD among slum-dwelling women as a result of environmental and social factors rather than just from a medical standpoint.[8]

In India, especially in marginalized communities, violence by spouses and in-laws is a deeply ingrained public health and human rights issue. In our study, many women reported that their husbands or in-laws had threatened them or physically abused them, which had an adverse effect on their mental health. Numerous studies have found this to be coherent. In a qualitative study of slum dwellers in Bangalore, Travasso et al.[20] found that women having an alcoholic partner and experiencing domestic violence are more likely to have detrimental and prolonged depressive episodes and contemplate suicide. According to Ellsberg et al.,[21] Women who have been victims of intimate partner abuse at least once in their lifetime have much more mental distress, suicidal thoughts, and attempts than women who have not. Women are abused everywhere, but those who live in urban slums encounter unique challenges when trying to access healthcare and social services. Because of a lack of knowledge about domestic violence support services and the stigma associated with violence and psychiatric illness, the suffering women are compelled to stay in abusive relationships without reaching out for help.

Although only age and income remained statistically significant in the multivariable model, the strong univariate associations between SRQ-20 symptom domains and CMD indicate substantial clinical relevance. The symptom burden reflects somatization patterns of psychological distress, which are clinically meaningful for primary care screening and early identification strategies in low-resource settings.

An individual’s mental health is greatly influenced by their social support. Our study’s inferences show that social support and spirituality serve as protective factors against CMD. This was determined to be consistent with a study by Travasso et al.,[20] which found that social support from peers and family was an indicator of lower anxiety and depression. Similar to this, Panigrahi et al.[9] suggested that women’s participation in religious or social obligations has a positive impact on their psychological well-being.

Implications and recommendations

The outcomes of this study highlight the urgent need to address the psychosocial determinants contributing to the high prevalence of CMDs among married women residing in urban slums. The findings suggest that family-related stressors, including marital discord and domestic violence, are significantly associated with poor mental health outcomes. Therefore, interventions aimed at strengthening family support systems, promoting marital counseling, and providing legal and psychosocial support to victims of domestic abuse should be prioritized. Economic vulnerability emerged as another critical factor, pointing to the importance of expanding livelihood opportunities through community-based skill development programs and self-help groups. Such initiatives not only improve household income but also enhance women’s autonomy and psychological resilience. Given the shortage of specialized mental health professionals in India, there is a compelling case for integrating task-sharing approaches by training lay health workers to deliver low-intensity, evidence-based psychological interventions at the community level. Increasing mental health awareness and reducing stigma through culturally sensitive campaigns, facilitated by local health workers and community leaders, could further promote early help-seeking behavior. Moreover, the enforcement of existing legal protections against domestic violence must be strengthened, alongside reforms to ensure responsive and gender-sensitive support services. Overall, a comprehensive, multi-sectoral strategy that integrates mental health services with social and economic development efforts is essential to effectively address CMDs among slum-dwelling women.

Limitations

Our study also comes with some practical limits. This study’s cross-sectional design could only pinpoint associations and not temporal relationships, or potentially causal links, between variables and mental health. These findings cannot be extrapolated to a wider group of women, such as older women, widows, divorcees, separated women, or women who are not married, as it was conducted among married women in the age range of 18–45 years. Due to the fact that the data were self-reported, biases like recall or social desirability were possible. Besides this, the demonstrated prevalence of 22% is based on an application of the SRQ screening instrument. It is crucial to note that this statistic represents a screening result that indicates probable CMD rather than a diagnostic estimate. As a result, this prevalence is likely to be overestimated and may require further diagnostic evaluation for precise diagnosis of CMD presence. While participants with probable CMD were referred to health facilities, the study could not assess follow-up or treatment outcomes due to systemic limitations. However, it is crucial to note that the study’s effectiveness in adequately meeting the needs of participants may still be influenced by a variety of circumstances, including the availability and quality of services given by community health workers.

CONCLUSION

This study provides critical insights into the mental health challenges faced by married women in urban slums. A multi-pronged strategy encompassing psychosocial support, legal protection, livelihood opportunities, and mental health integration at the primary level is essential. Therefore, when creating various strategies and programs aimed at promoting the mental health of slum women, policymakers should consider these factors. Participants with CMD were encouraged and supported to report to the nearest district and community health center to seek counseling and medical services. A comprehensive report has been sent to the Chief Medical Officer of the district to strengthen the community mental health program. Improved career opportunities or skill development initiatives could assist them in becoming self-sufficient, thereby improving their social and economic status. NGOs can potentially play a significant role in preventing violence and improving the mental health of women because they are better equipped to comprehend issues at the community level. Community health nursing officer at the health and wellness center located in urban slum areas can create awareness campaigns aimed at the slum community about the importance of mental health and its correlates would be beneficial. Domestic violence policies must be strengthened. Primary physicians or lay health workers may be employed for community-based psychiatric interventions.
