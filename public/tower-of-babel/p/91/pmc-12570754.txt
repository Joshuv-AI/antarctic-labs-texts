In vitro biomimetic development of artificial dental calculus: a proof of concept study
Authors: Jae-Kook Cha, Kyeong-Won Paeng, Seung Ha Yoo, Chae Hyun Lee, Yoon-Hee Kwon, Ui-Won Jung, Baek-Il Kim, Hoi-In Jung, Magda Feres, Mariano Sanz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo develop an in vitro model to generate dental calculus through a standardized process of biofilm mineralization, and compare it with natural human dental calculus .MethodsThe proposed in vitro model consisted of two staged procedures: first biofilm formation and then its mineralization. After a 72-hour pre-culture, biofilms of Streptococcus mutans were formed on a glass dish in artificial saliva with 1% sucrose for 3 h. Then the calcification process consisted of incubating the sample in supersaturated mineral solution for 21 h. These steps were repeated for 5 days. The samples were then divided into 3 groups based on the different processes; (i) mineralization only, (ii) biofilm formation only, (iii) biofilm formation and mineralization. Obtained samples were compared morphologically with human supragingival calculus using scanning electron microscopy, quantitative light-induced fluorescence, and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy. Confocal laser scanning microscopy was used to assess bacterial distribution and viability, and X-ray diffraction analysis was performed to evaluate the crystallinity of the mineral deposits. A solubility test was also conducted to assess structural stability.ResultsThe artificially developed calculus exhibited similar characteristics to human dental calculus including a layered structure with bacterial embedded in a porous mineral matrix, and comparable ratio of inorganic components. No weight loss was observed before or after the solubility test.ConclusionArtificial calculus synthesized through this novel biofilm mineralization model demonstrated structural and chemical characteristics consistent with early-stage human dental calculus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07050-7.

Background

Bacterial biofilms are linked to the aetiology of periodontal diseases, since their growth and maturation on dental surfaces trigger the host inflammatory immune response characterized by upregulated cytokines and chemokines that lead to chronic inflammation and to tissue destruction - if the inflammatory process is not resolved [1, 2]. Therefore, regular oral biofilm removal is the essential strategy to maintain periodontal health. During calculus formation, the non-mineralized matrix becomes entrapped and subsequently mineralized with calcium phosphate, resulting in a calcified biofilm. Dental calculus not only serves as a passive retention site for biofilms but also plays an active role in the progression of periodontal disease. Its porous and rough surface facilitates bacterial adhesion and protects the microbial community from mechanical removal and host defense mechanisms. Additionally, calculus-associated biofilms can sustain an inflammatory response, leading to continuous destruction of both soft and hard tissues if not adequately managed [3]. Recent studies have also highlighted that the mineralized structure of calculus may promote a dysbiotic shift in the microbial community, contributing to persistent inflammation and disease progression [4].

In clinical practice, biofilm removal is carried out in conjunction with dental calculus elimination, as the calculus’s rough surface serves as a reservoir for the accumulation of oral bacteria. Given the critical role of biofilm and calculus accumulation in the pathogenesis of various oral diseases, the development of robust models for biofilm and calculus is essential. These models help advance our understanding of biofilm formation, maturation, and removal, which are crucial for the development of effective preventive and therapeutic strategies.

In vitro static biofilm models, using representative bacterial strains from the subgingival environment, demonstrated how bacterial colonization and maturation resemble in vivo biofilm formation [5, 6]. Subsequently, the development of dynamic biofilm models employing a bioreactor and fluid dynamics to mimic salivary flow has allowed for a better understanding of these mechanisms in a simulated human oral environment. This model has been utilized in various experiments, such as investigating the effect of biofilm on dental implant surfaces [7], and studying the role in development of dental caries lesion [8].

While the development of artificial biofilm models has been actively studied in recent years, the development of modern artificial calculus models remains scarce. While in vitro models of artificial dental calculus development were previously reported [9, 10], these early models relied on human saliva and plaque as the bacterial source, requiring continuous delivery of substances for biofilm growth and calcification at specific rates through complex devices. More recently, Borah et al. (2014) developed an experimental dental model to investigate the inorganic aspect of calculus formation using dog saliva with added concentrations of ions such as magnesium, calcium chloride, and potassium phosphate in a water bath. The study revealed that carbonated hydroxyapatite, rather than calcite as previously believed, was the primary component of dental calculus [11]. However, this model focused solely on the inorganic elements and did not address the microbial composition of dental calculus.

Building on these findings, it is essential to recognize that the formation of dental calculus is a dynamic process that begins with the calcification of non-mineralized biofilm - comprising oral bacteria, host proteins, and DNA - when saliva becomes supersaturated with calcium and phosphate ions. As bacterial metabolism progresses, the biofilm transitions into calculus, strengthening its attachment to the tooth surface and capturing intraoral particles [3]. To fully understand this transition from biofilm to calculus, the development of a robust in vitro model that explores both the mineralization process and the microbial components of biofilm is crucial [12].

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to develop an experimental model that integrates biofilm formation and mineralization, simulating the natural transition from biofilm to calculus observed in the human oral environment. As a proof-of-concept study, the hypothesis was that by repeatedly inducing cycles of biofilm formation and mineralization, it would be possible to experimentally create artificial calculus exhibiting key characteristics such as a similar morphological and chemical structure to natural calculus, as well as low solubility in water.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines. This experimental model is set up in two processes. The process begins with the production of an artificial biofilm, followed by its subsequent mineralization. For the mineralization process, a double-jar system was used, in which one jar is placed inside the other to replicate the human oral cavity (Fig. 1). Using a water circulation bath, triple distilled water was continuously circulated in the external jar to maintain the temperature at approximately 36.5 ℃, (MaXircu CR-8, DAIHAN Scientific, South Korea), while the mineral solution was filled in the inner jar. To replicate the average salivary flow rate in the oral cavity (30mL/h), a magnetic bar and magnetic stirrer (PC420, Corning) were used to maintain this solution flow rate [13]. To stabilize the position of the sample, a sterilized aluminum stent was located in the inner jar.

Schematic of the artificial calculus formation using a double-jar artificial mouth model

As a positive control, supragingival human calculus was collected using a sterile curette from 5 systemically and periodontally healthy adults (aged ≥ 18 years) with thick calculus deposits on the lingual surfaces of mandibular anterior teeth. All participants had not undergone professional scaling for at least six months before sampling. The calculus was collected from sites exhibiting gingivitis without radiographic evidence of alveolar bone loss. Written informed consent was obtained (Institutional Review Board of the Yonsei University Dental Hospital, Approval No. 2–2020-0092). The collected calculus samples were immediately fixed in Karnovsky’s fixative for further processing. For analysis, the harvested calculus was processed and trimmed into approximately 2 × 2 mm specimens to ensure consistent sample size across evaluations.

In vitro calculus formation

Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans) strain

In this study, S. mutans ATCC 25,175 (acquired from the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology) was cultured and diluted 1:1 with 80% glycerin (Duksan, Korea) and stored at −80 ℃.

Biofilm formation

Standard microscope glass slides (76 × 26 × 1 mm, pre-cleaned, plain; Marienfeld Superior™, Germany, Part No. 101432) were cut into 1 × 1 cm pieces using a precision diamond glass cutter (Silberschnitt® 555.0, Bohle Group, Germany) for use as substrates for biofilm formation. Before use, the slides were cleaned by sequential sonication in 70% ethanol and deionized water, followed by autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min. Glass dishes were immersed in 1.5 mL of sterile artificial saliva (composed of 2.2 g gastric mucin, 0.381 g NaCl, 0.213 g CaCl₂·2 H₂O, 0.738 g KH₂PO₄, 1.114 g KCl, pH 7.0, and distilled water up to 1000 mL) for 4 h to form an acquired pellicle before the biofilm development phase. As part of the pre-culture process, 800 µL of artificial saliva and 800 µL of Todd Hewitt yeast extract broth (THYE, Difco) were mixed, and the total sucrose concentration was adjusted to 1% (1.6 mL of solution A). Then, 200 µL of incubated S. mutans adjusted to 10⁷ CFUs/mL was inoculated into the mixture. This inoculum was incubated for 72 h at 37 ℃ in a 10% CO₂ environment to allow sufficient biofilm development on the glass surface. Once the pre-culture phase was completed, the glass samples were transferred to a freshly prepared solution A and incubated at 37 ℃ in a 10% CO₂ environment for an additional 3 h [14].

Mineralization process

The glass samples with formed biofilm were placed on sterilized metal stent and immersed in 150 ml of mineral solution containing Musin 5 g, NaCl 4.92 g, CaCl2 0.28 g, KH2PO4 0.07 g, MgCl2 0.21 g, NaHCO3 0.84 g, Urea 0.2 g and 1000 ml of distilled water, with the pH adjusted to 9.5 using KOH [11]. Mineralization was conducted in a double-jar system maintained at 36.5 ℃, where the inner beaker held the mineral solution and the outer container circulated triple-distilled water using a recirculating water bath incubator (MaXircu CR-8, DAIHAN Scientific, South Korea) to maintain a constant temperature. Continuous stirring at 250 rpm was applied using a magnetic stirrer and magnetic bar to ensure uniform mineral distribution. The mineral uptake phase lasted 21 h at 36.5 ℃. The biofilm formation (3 h) and mineral uptake cycle (21 h) was repeated five times, with the mineral solution refreshed and pH measured after each cycle. After the final cycle, the glass samples were rinsed three times with 1 mL of distilled water using an up-and-down motion to remove excess mineral solution and loosely attached biofilm. To prevent unintended mineralization during the pre-culture phase, artificial saliva, containing trace amounts of calcium and phosphate, was excluded from the medium in conditions designed to form biofilm without mineralization.

Quantitative light-induced fluorescence

To detect biofilm formation, Quantitative light-induced fluorescence (QLF; Q-ray, AIOBIO, Seoul, Korea) was used. For each group, six samples were photographed. Photographs under white light were first taken for the visual observation, then the pattern of the bacterial growth and its byproduct production was observed under blue light, which can detect fluorescence from biofilm (Canon EOS 550D, Japan). The imaging process was conducted inside a sterile clean bench to maintain aseptic conditions. Photographs were taken at 7 time points for each sample: before the experiment, after the pre-culture phase, and after the completion of each mineral uptake cycle.

In addition to qualitative imaging, green fluorescence intensity characteristic of S. mutans was quantitatively measured from the blue light images using standardized image analysis software (ImageJ, National Institutes of Health, USA). The fluorescence values were recorded and analyzed to evaluate bacterial growth and activity over time.

Scanning electron microscope, energy dispersive spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction

Morphological and elemental analyses were conducted using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), respectively. For SEM analysis, samples were fixed in Karnovsky’s fixative (2% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) for 24 h, then rinsed in phosphate buffer for 10 min. Post-fixation was performed with 1% OsO₄ for 2 h, followed by dehydration through a graded ethanol series (50–100%), with each step lasting 10 min. The samples were subsequently dried using a critical point dryer (LEICA EM CPD300), coated with platinum using an ion sputter coater (LEICA EM ACE600), and observed under a field emission electron microscope (MERLIN, ZEISS).

For each group, four samples were analyzed by SEM. Horizontal-view images were taken from the center of each glass disc to observe morphological characteristics. In contrast, vertical-view images were acquired from the central area near the disc edge to measure the thickness of the deposits.

EDS analysis was performed to assess the elemental composition of the sample surfaces and to compare artificial calculus with human dental calculus. A total of five samples from the MU group, three from the BF group, four from the BF + MU group, and five human calculus samples were included in the analysis. The same sample preparation protocol used for SEM was applied, except that carbon coating was used instead of platinum. Elemental measurements were performed using an energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS/Flat-Quad, Bruker). Components originating from the background glass, fixatives, or coating materials were excluded from the analysis. The elements analyzed included calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P), which serve as primary indicators of mineralization; magnesium (Mg), associated with variations in crystallinity; and sulfur (S), a trace element that may influence mineralization and biofilm stability, as reported in previous studies [15].

Additionally, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted to assess the crystallinity of the artificial calculus samples. Measurements were performed on BF + MU group samples using a powder X-ray diffractometer (Ultima IV, Rigaku, Tokyo, Japan) over a 2θ range of 10° to 90°, with a scanning speed of 1° per minute. The data were analyzed to detect crystalline phases or amorphous patterns.

Confocal laser scanning microscope

A confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) was used to analyse the amount and distribution of bacteria and extracellular polymeric substances (EPS). For EPS staining, dextran-conjugated Alexa Fluor was diluted to 0.1 mg/mL (10 µM) in distilled water and filtered using a syringe filter. The prepared dye solution was added to solution A during each biofilm formation cycle, with the dye solution comprising 1/9 of the total volume of the solution A, with or without bacterial inoculation. Before image acquisition using the LSM 700 camera (Carl Zeiss, Germany), the glass samples were immersed in 200 µL of standard fluorescent nucleic acid stain (SYTO 9) for 15 min, which was diluted in distilled water at a ratio of 3:1000. Images were captured using Zen software (Carl Zeiss) [16]. Four samples per group were used for this analysis.

To analyze bacterial colony and EPS formation, four samples were used for each group. For the BF group, regions of interest (ROIs) were randomly selected based on the even distribution of green (bacterial) and red (EPS) signals. For the BF + MU group, areas with the highest bacterial colony density and areas with the least bacterial growth were selected separately for analysis. The MU group was excluded from this analysis due to the absence of bacterial inoculation.

Solubility

The samples were completely dried for 24 h in a drying oven (C-DOD, Chang Shin Scientific Co., Busan) at 37 ℃, and their weight was measured to confirm the amount of substance formed. To compare the solubility of each group, the previously weighed, completely dried samples were immersed in 1 mL of distilled water at 37 ℃ for 72 h, then dried again for re-weighing. Four samples per group were analyzed to calculate the average weight.

Statistical analysis

All Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Quantitative data are presented as means with standard deviations (mean ± SD). Due to the small sample size and the non-normal distribution of data, non-parametric tests were selected. Group comparisons were initially conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. When significant differences were detected, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction (p < 0.017).

Results

Quantitative light-induced fluorescence

The white and blue light-induced fluorescence images of each group are shown in Fig. 3, illustrating the gradual change in bacterial growth at the end of each experimental cycle. Under white light, the glass from the MU group was covered by a semi-transparent white layer without any fluorescence when exposed to QLF.

Images of the QLF analysis. QLF imaging was performed under both white and blue light following each cycle of biofilm formation and calcification. Scale bars = 5 mm

In both the BF and BF + MU groups, where S. mutans was inoculated, fluorescence indicating bacterial clusters and byproducts was observed under blue light. Both groups displayed clusters of bacterial colonies and byproducts in a chalky white form. The main difference was that the BF + MU group exhibited denser white clusters of S. mutans colonizing the glass, whereas the BF group showed more scattered byproducts.

Additionally, under white light, the BF + MU group demonstrated a progressive increase in light intensity over time, reflecting the accumulation of biofilm and mineralized byproducts. In contrast, the BF group showed an initial increase in light intensity during the early experimental cycles (Day 1–2), followed by a gradual decline, potentially indicating biofilm dispersion or reduced biomass stability over time. Both the BF + MU group and the BF groups exhibited a decrease in green fluorescence intensity compared to the pre-culture phase, maintaining lower and relatively stable fluorescence levels throughout the subsequent experimental cycles. The detailed quantitative data are provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Scanning electron microscope, energy dispersive spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction

In SEM analysis, the calculus samples collected from humans was used as the reference comparison as a (Fig. 4). In the MU group, no bacterial colonization was detected, and only an irregular mineralized surface was observed. The BF group showed loosely connected chains of cocci-shaped bacteria, while the BF + MU group exhibited densely packed cocci with long chains on a mineralized surface. The human calculus sample showed compact clusters of bacteria in various shapes, including cocci, rods, and spirals on a mineralized, lamellated surface. Compared to human calculus, the BF + MU group displayed morphological similarities, with a mixture of mineralized and non-mineralized features and microbial clusters on a less lamellated surface.

In the vertical-view, each group showed thick layers of material on the glass, except for MU group, where there was only 4.63 ± 0.63 μm of material layer. The thicknesses on the glass from BF and BF + MU group were 608.60 ± 48.40 and 219.33 ± 42.29 μm, respectively. The BF showed the greatest thickness compared to the MU and BF + MU groups. The overall comparison revealed a significant difference in vertical thickness among groups (p < 0.05), but post-hoc pairwise comparisons did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.017).

In the MU group, EDS analysis did not detect magnesium or sulfur, and only small amounts of calcium (0.36 ± 0.35%) and phosphorus (0.61 ± 0.75%) were identified. In the BF group, only phosphorus (1.28 ± 0.46%) was detected, with no detectable calcium, the key element for mineralization. In contrast, the BF + MU group showed significantly higher levels of calcium (6.79 ± 2.61%) and phosphorus (5.05 ± 1.72%). For comparison, the human calculus group exhibited even higher levels of calcium (23.37 ± 7.78%) and phosphorus (9.59 ± 1.78%). Based on these results, the calculated calcium-to-phosphorus (Ca/P) molar ratios were as follows: 0.14 ± 0.20 for the MU group, 1.02 ± 0.08 for the BF + MU group, and 1.79 ± 0.50 for the human calculus group.

The detailed elemental compositions, including Ca, P, Mg, and S weight% and the Ca/P molar ratios based on atomic%, are provided in Supplementary Figure S3. A significant difference in the Ca/P molar ratio was found across the groups (p < 0.05), no significant difference between the BF + MU group and human calculus groups. In contrast, a significant difference was observed between the MU and human calculus groups (p < 0.008).

XRD analysis of the BF + MU group samples revealed an amorphous pattern with no distinct crystalline peaks, indicating limited crystallinity in the artificial calculus. Detailed XRD data are provided in Supplementary Figure S4.

Solubility

The solubility for each group is illustrated in Fig. 6. The MU group resulted in a smaller amount of artificial calculus compared to the other two groups and showed no change in weight after the solubility test. The BF group exhibited the greatest product formation, with a weight of 26 mg. Discussion

This proof-of-concept study developed a standardized in vitro model to replicate key characteristics of human dental calculus by mimicking the natural transition from biofilm to calculus, incorporating both organic and inorganic components. The pre-mature artificial calculus, created through repeated biofilm formation and mineralization cycles, demonstrated key similarities to human calculus, including clustered bacteria within a lamellated structure and comparable chemical composition. Additionally, comparisons of macro- and micro-morphological structures, elemental composition, and solubility supported the reproducibility and relevance of the experimental model.

CLSM and SEM analyses demonstrated that the BF + MU group exhibited densely packed cocci-shaped bacteria embedded within a lamellated mineralized structure, a key morphological feature commonly observed in human dental calculus. In contrast, the BF group displayed loosely connected bacterial chains with limited mineral deposition. This structural difference may be attributed to the presence of mineral components in the artificial saliva used in the BF + MU group, which likely promoted a more compact and stabilized bacterial colonization environment. These findings were further supported by EPS fluorescence analysis. Specifically, the BF + MU group showed increased EPS signal intensity, suggesting enhanced metabolic activity and byproduct accumulation associated with the stabilized bacterial colonization facilitated by mineral-based scaffolds. The CLSM results were consistent with SEM observations, revealing densely distributed bacterial cells and EPS in the BF + MU group, whereas the BF group exhibited a more diffuse and scattered distribution.

In the EDS analysis, calcium and phosphorus were detected at relatively higher levels in the BF + MU group compared to the MU and BF groups. The calculated Ca/P molar ratio in the BF + MU group was 1.02, which falls within the range reported for human dental calculus (1.0–2.09) [17, 18]. Although this value was lower than that observed in the human calculus group (1.79 ± 0.50), it is consistent with values reported for early-stage or pre-mature calculus in previous studies [19]. These findings align with a study by Abraham et al., which demonstrated that the Ca/P ratio increases progressively as calculus matures, starting from values similar to those observed in our BF + MU group. Furthermore, a recent study by Wang et al. [20] reported a Ca/P ratio of 0.67 in incompletely mineralized dental plaque, supporting the interpretation that the artificial calculus in the BF + MU group represents an early stage of mineralization.

The physical stability of the pre-mature artificial calculus was further assessed through solubility testing. The BF + MU group demonstrated only minimal weight loss, indicating a relatively stable mineralized structure. In contrast, the BF group exhibited a greater reduction in weight, likely due to the absence of a mineralized matrix, which may have resulted in higher solubility and reduced structural integrity. While statistical significance was not observed after Bonferroni correction, the overall trend suggests that mineralization contributed to enhanced structural resilience in the BF + MU group.

The strength of this study model lies in its simplicity and standardization, achieved by excluding natural saliva and employing a well-controlled and reproducible system [10, 11, 21]. This approach minimized variability associated with differences in salivary composition, microbial diversity, and environmental factors such as pH. S. mutans was selected as the sole bacterial species due to its well-characterized capacity to form robust biofilms, rapidly produce extracellular matrix, and initiate early plaque development critical for mineral deposition [22, 23]. Its long-standing use as a model organism in biofilm research further ensured experimental reproducibility [22, 23]. The use of a single, well-defined strain allowed for effective matrix formation while avoiding the complexity introduced by multispecies interactions.

Another distinctive aspect of the standardization in this experimental model is the use of glass plate to replicate the calculus formation process. In previous models, natural tooth or root surfaces were commonly used as the platform for plaque and calculus development [10, 11]. While these surfaces closely mimic oral conditions, they introduce variability due to structural and compositional difference, such as surface roughness and mineral content, which can influence biofilm development and mineralization. Other models, like those using hydroxyapatite-coated plastic pegs, complicate quantitative analysis due to challenges in defining consistent regions of interest [21]. In contrast, the glass plate ensures uniform surface characteristics, enabling reproducible biofilm formation and mineral deposition across samples. Its smooth, non-porous nature facilitates the observation and analysis of biofilm morphology and mineralization patterns.

Additionally, previous models required prolonged incubation periods ranging from 2 to 5 weeks to achieve mineralization [9, 10], whereas the present model successfully induced early-stage mineral deposition within 10 days. This accelerated process was facilitated by the inclusion of mucin and urea in the mineral solution. Mucin contributed to biofilm matrix stabilization and enhanced bacterial adhesion, while urea, metabolized by urease-producing bacteria, released ammonia and elevated the local pH, promoting calcium phosphate precipitation [10, 11, 21, 24]. Through repeated cycles of biofilm formation and mineral uptake under controlled conditions, the model was able to reproducibly simulate rapid mineralization without the need for additional chambers or frequent solution changes.

To explore the potential applications of this experimental model, it is important to investigate how anti-calculus agents affect both the quantity and characteristics of calculus formation. Previous studies have emphasized the importance of assessing such agents under well-controlled in vitro conditions [21, 25, 26]. This model offers a reproducible platform to assess whether artificially formed calculus responds similarly to natural human calculus when exposed to inhibitory treatments. By observing the effects of these agents on mineral deposition and structural changes, this model may provied valuable insights into the mechanisms of calculus inhibition. These findings could further support the utility of this model for the development and preclinical of new anti-calculus therapies.

This model presents several notable limitations. It utilized a single bacterial species, S. mutans, selected for its well-characterized biofilm-forming capacity and its role in early plaque development. While this approach enhanced model standardization and reproducibility, it did not capture the complexity of the multispecies microbial environment involved in natural calculus formation. Additionally, although the BF + MU group produced artificial calculus exhibiting structural and chemical features resembling early-stage human calculus, the relatively short mineralization period likely resulted in pre-mature rather than fully matured deposits. This interpretation is further supported by X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Supplementary Fig. S4), which indicated limited crystallinity consistent with early-stage mineralization.

To further enhance the biological relevance of the model, future studies should incorporate standardized multispecies biofilms, including early and late colonizers as well as periodontal pathogens from the orange and red complexes [27, 28]. Additionally, the model should be modified to simulate subgingival calculus formation, which differs from supragingival calculus in terms of microbial composition, crystal characteristics, and environmental exposure. Beyond expanding microbial diversity, future investigations should include advanced structural, chemical, and behavioral assessments, such as crystalline maturity (via XRD analysis), solubility characteristics, and responsiveness to anti-calculus agents, to comprehensively validate the model’s biological applicability. Furthermore, to support more complete crystal maturation, future studies may consider extending the mineralization period, provided that experimental reproducibility and feasibility are maintained.

Nevertheless, a key strength of the present study lies in the introduction of a simplified and standardized in vitro model that, for the first time, replicates the natural transition from biofilm to dental calculus through repeated cycles of biofilm formation and mineralization. This model successfully reproduced the structural, chemical, and solubility characteristics of human dental calculus and offers a valuable platform for advancing the understanding of calculus formation mechanisms and for evaluating anti-calculus treatments under controlled experimental conditions.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, including the use of a single bacterial species and the incomplete crystallization observed in the artificial calculus, we successfully developed a standardized in vitro model that replicates the biofilm formation and mineralization processes. The resulting artificial calculus reproduced key morphological, chemical, and behavioral characteristics of early-stage human dental calculus. This proof-of-concept model provides a valuable platform for future investigations into calculus formation mechanisms and for evaluating anti-calculus treatments under controlled conditions.
