Patient Comfort and Acceptance of Different Isolation Techniques for Silver Diamine Fluoride Application: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Shilpa Shetty Naik, Rachna Sharma, Divya Shetty, Shirin Chavan, Mayuri Gorule, Rhea Shah, Sanpreet Singh Sachdev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEfficient isolation is critical for effective silver diamine fluoride (SDF) application in pediatric dentistry. However, conventional methods like cotton roll isolation may be uncomfortable and compromise cooperation. Introduction

Silver diamine fluoride (SDF) is a topical agent composed of 38% SDF solution, equivalent to approximately 5% fluoride, in a colorless liquid with a pH of 10. It has gained significant attention in pediatric dentistry due to its ability to arrest or slow the progression of carious lesions without the need for mechanical excavation, thereby preserving sound tooth structure.1,2 SDF also promotes the remineralization of demineralized dentin, making it particularly valuable for minimally invasive treatment in young, anxious, or uncooperative children.1,2 According to the 2017 clinical practice guidelines by the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD), SDF is endorsed as a nonrestorative treatment option in specific clinical situations where traditional restorative methods may not be feasible.1

However, a notable drawback of SDF is its potential to cause dark staining on the teeth and transient irritation or discoloration of the oral mucosa and gingiva. These stains, although usually self-limiting and healing within 1–2 days, can lead to esthetic concerns and soft-tissue discomfort if the agent inadvertently contacts surrounding tissues.3 Therefore, achieving effective isolation of the treatment area during SDF application is essential, not only to enhance clinical efficacy but also to improve patient comfort and prevent undesirable tissue reactions.4

Traditionally, cotton rolls and saliva ejectors have been used for moisture control in pediatric dentistry. Cotton rolls serve to absorb saliva and maintain a relatively dry field, which helps in the better adhesion and effectiveness of various dental materials.4,5 While simple and noninvasive, they offer limited protection against contamination and do not prevent SDF from coming into contact with surrounding soft tissues.

Rubber dam isolation (RDI) is considered the gold standard in moisture control. It provides optimal access and visibility of the operative field, significantly reduces contamination, and protects the patient from accidental aspiration or ingestion of dental materials.6,7 Despite these advantages, traditional RDI presents significant challenges in pediatric patients. Placement of the metal clamp can be painful, often necessitating local anesthesia, and may lead to gingival trauma due to the clamp engaging tooth undercuts and compressing soft tissues.8 Moreover, the clamp's sharp edges and the sudden force required for placement may cause soft-tissue damage, particularly in younger children.8 These factors often make rubber dam use impractical in children with dental anxiety or poor cooperation.9

To overcome these limitations, newer alternatives have been developed. The OptraDam, a flexible, anatomically preformed rubber dam system, eliminates the need for metal clamps and can be easily positioned in the oral cavity. This design not only reduces placement time but also minimizes pain and gingival trauma in pediatric patients.10,11 Additionally, the OptraDam has been shown to lower anxiety levels in both children and clinicians due to its simplified placement and reduced invasiveness.9

Another innovative isolation method is OpalDam, a light-cured resin barrier that can be directly applied to the gingiva and cured in place.12 It creates a protective seal around the tooth, allowing effective isolation in procedures where conventional dams are not tolerated. OpalDam is especially useful in young children due to its ease of application and comfort during use.12

Considering the clinical importance of SDF and the necessity of child-friendly isolation techniques, it becomes essential to evaluate the comfort and acceptance of various methods from the child's perspective. Hence, the present study aimed to evaluate and compare the comfort and acceptance of three different isolation techniques—cotton roll, OpalDam, and OptraDam—during the application of SDF in primary mandibular molars in children.

Materials and Methods

The present randomized clinical trial (RCT) was conducted in the institutional Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry at a School of Dentistry in Navi Mumbai, clinical trial registry number: CTRI/2025/02/081336. Study was conducted over a period of three months from April 2025 to June 2025. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review and Ethics Board under the approval number IREB/2024/PEDO/24. The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and followed all institutional and national ethical guidelines. Prior to the commencement of the trial, detailed information regarding the procedure, objectives of the study, and potential risks, including the possibility of black discoloration of the treated teeth, was explained to the parents or legal guardians of all eligible children. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant's caregiver, confirming their voluntary agreement to enroll their child in the study.

Eligibility Criteria

Children between 5 and 9 years of age presenting with moderate carious lesions in primary mandibular first or second molars were screened for eligibility. Only children without any known allergy to silver compounds or latex, and whose parents were willing to accept posttreatment discoloration associated with SDF application, were considered for inclusion. Children were excluded if clinical or radiographic evaluation revealed pulpal involvement of the carious lesions, or those with systemic illnesses, developmental or psychological disorders, or those undergoing long-term pharmacological treatment that could influence their behavioral or pain responses during the dental procedures.

Sample Size Determination and Randomization

The sample size for the study was calculated using the G*Power software, version 3.1.2, by assuming an effect size of 0.5, a power of 80%, and a significance level of 0.05. Based on this calculation, a minimum of 13 participants per group was deemed necessary to detect statistically meaningful differences. Anticipating a 10% attrition rate, the final sample size was fixed at 39 children. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated sequence to allocate participants into one of the three study groups. Allocation concealment was ensured through the use of sealed, opaque envelopes, which were opened only at the time of the clinical procedure.

Clinical Procedure and Isolation Techniques

All children underwent thorough oral prophylaxis before the initiation of treatment to ensure a clean operative field. This included mechanical removal of plaque and supragingival scaling where required. The clinical intervention involved the application of 38% SDF to carious lesions under isolation using one of three techniques: cotton roll, OpalDam, or OptraDam. In the cotton roll group, standard absorbent rolls were placed buccally and lingually around the targeted tooth along with a saliva ejector to aid moisture control (Fig. 1). For children in the OpalDam group, a 4–6 mm thick layer of the resin barrier was applied directly onto the attached gingiva and approximately 0.5 mm onto the dry enamel surface to ensure an effective seal around the tooth (Fig. 2). This layer was then light-cured for 20 seconds using a scanning motion to form a durable physical barrier. Children assigned to the OptraDam group were managed using the clamp-free rubber dam system, which was positioned according to the manufacturer's protocol, allowing atraumatic placement without the need for local anesthesia (Fig. 3).

For all participants, 38% SDF was applied using a sterile bent microsponge brush. The applicator was first dipped into the SDF solution, and excess liquid was removed by dabbing it against the inner surface of a plastic dappen dish. The SDF was then applied to the carious lesion for a duration of at least one minute, following which the area was gently dried with a stream of compressed air. Isolation was maintained for a minimum of three minutes following application to maximize therapeutic effectiveness and to minimize soft-tissue exposure to the medicament. A high volume suction system and petroleum jelly were employed during the procedure across all groups to maintain standardization and to prevent mucosal staining or contamination.

Operator Calibration and Blinding Protocol

To ensure uniformity and eliminate interoperator variability, all procedures were performed by a single calibrated pediatric dentist trained in the application of each isolation technique. Postoperative evaluations, including pain assessment and patient satisfaction scoring, were conducted by two independent pediatric dentists who were blinded to the type of isolation method used during the procedure. These evaluators did not participate in the treatment phase of the study and were unaware of group allocation at the time of assessment.

Assessment of Pain and Patient Satisfaction

Postoperative discomfort was evaluated using two validated pediatric pain scales: the Wong–Baker FACES Pain Rating Scale (WBFPS) and the Faces Pain Scale–Revised (FPS-R), both of which depict cartoon facial expressions representing varying levels of discomfort and allow children to communicate their pain intensity effectively and reliably within their cognitive ability range.13,14 These visual tools were administered immediately following the procedure by trained examiners blinded to the group assignment of each child.

In addition to pain scoring, each child was assessed for subjective acceptance and overall comfort associated with the assigned isolation technique through a structured, prevalidated questionnaire designed specifically for pediatric populations. The questionnaire was administered in a face-to-face format immediately after treatment, ensuring that the responses reflected the child's fresh procedural experience. The first section of the questionnaire recorded demographic details such as age, gender, and prior exposure to dental isolation procedures. The second section included six items designed to evaluate specific dimensions of the child's experience with the isolation system used during SDF application.

Each question was scored on a 0–10 numerical rating scale, where “0” represented the absence of the described sensation or discomfort and “10” represented the maximum intensity of the experience. The first item asked the child to rate the degree of annoyance experienced during placement of the isolation system. The second item evaluated whether the isolation triggered a gag reflex during treatment. The third item explored the perception of fluid leakage into the oral cavity during the procedure. The fourth question assessed whether the isolation caused noticeable stretching of the mouth, cheeks, or lips. The fifth item measured the sensation of pressure exerted on the tongue by the isolation system. The final item in the questionnaire evaluated overall comfort during the procedure, wherein a higher score (closer to 10) indicated greater comfort and ease of tolerance, contrasting with the discomfort related directionality of the preceding items.

Statistical Analysis

All data were compiled in Microsoft Excel and exported to the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 11.5 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, were calculated for each parameter. To determine statistically significant differences within and between groups, paired Student's t-tests and repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) were performed. A significance threshold was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses. The data analysis strategy ensured robust comparisons of the isolation techniques with respect to pain perception and subjective patient acceptance.

Results

A total of thirty-nine children were enrolled in the study, with thirteen participants allocated to each of the three isolation groups: Cotton roll, OpalDam, and OptraDam. The ages of the participants ranged from 5 to 9 years, with a mean age of 6.92 ± 1.07 years across the sample. The gender distribution comprised 22 males (56.4%) and 17 females (43.6%), with comparable representation across all three groups. All participants completed the intervention and subsequent assessments without any dropouts or protocol deviations. No adverse events or complications were reported during the isolation or SDF application procedures.

Pain Perception Based on WBFPS

The WBFPS scores demonstrated clear differences in perceived discomfort among the three isolation techniques.13 As shown in Table 1, the highest mean pain score was recorded in the cotton roll group (mean = 4.00 ± 1.41), followed by the OptraDam group (mean = 2.54 ± 2.11). The lowest mean pain score was observed in the OpalDam group (mean = 1.38 ± 1.50), indicating greater patient comfort with this method.

Statistical comparison using one-way ANOVA revealed that the differences in pain scores among the three groups were statistically significant (F = 7.708, p = 0.002). Post hoc analysis using Tukey's test further confirmed a significant difference between the cotton roll and OpalDam groups (p = 0.001), suggesting that children experienced significantly less pain with OpalDam isolation compared to cotton roll. However, the differences between cotton roll and OptraDam (p = 0.087) and between OpalDam and OptraDam (p = 0.209) were not statistically significant, indicating a relatively comparable level of comfort between the latter two techniques. These findings highlight that clamp-free and resin-based isolation techniques, particularly OpalDam, are better tolerated by pediatric patients in terms of pain perception during SDF application.

Patient Satisfaction

Patient-reported satisfaction scores varied significantly across the three isolation groups in all six evaluated domains, as presented in Table 2. The cotton roll group consistently recorded the highest mean scores in Questions 1 through 5, indicating a greater degree of discomfort. These included annoyance during placement (Q1: 7.00 ± 1.29), gag reflex stimulation (Q2: 6.38 ± 2.18), sensation of fluid leakage (Q3: 6.08 ± 1.50), stretching of oral tissues (Q4: 3.00 ± 0.91), and pressure on the tongue (Q5: 3.38 ± 1.12). In contrast, the OpalDam group reported the lowest scores in each of these domains, reflecting a higher level of comfort, with mean scores ranging from 0.69 to 2.08. The OptraDam group demonstrated intermediate values between the other two groups.

In terms of overall comfort (Q6), the mean satisfaction score was significantly higher in the OpalDam group (8.31 ± 1.80), followed closely by OptraDam (8.08 ± 1.50). The cotton roll group again showed the lowest comfort rating (3.23 ± 1.69), further reinforcing its lower acceptance among pediatric participants.

One-way ANOVA revealed statistically significant intergroup differences for all six questions, with p-values less than 0.05. The most significant differences were observed in Questions 1 through 3 and Question 6 (all p < 0.0001), suggesting that clamp-free and resin-based isolation methods were better tolerated by children compared to traditional cotton roll isolation. These findings support the preference for newer isolation systems, particularly OpalDam, in reducing discomfort and improving overall patient experience during procedures such as SDF application.

Comparison between the cotton roll and OptraDam groups also revealed statistically significant differences for most domains. Specifically, significant improvements in patient satisfaction were noted for Questions 1–3 (p < 0.0001) and Question 5 (p = 0.004), as well as for overall comfort in Question 6 (p < 0.0001). However, no statistically significant difference was found for oral tissue stretching (Q4, p = 0.150) between cotton roll and OptraDam, suggesting a comparable experience for that specific parameter.

When comparing OpalDam and OptraDam, no statistically significant differences were observed in any of the six questions. The p-values for these comparisons ranged from 0.150 to 0.974, indicating that both newer isolation techniques were similarly well tolerated by pediatric patients. These results reinforce the superior acceptability of OpalDam and OptraDam over the cotton roll method, with OpalDam offering the most consistent improvements in patient-reported comfort across all evaluated domains.

Discussion

The findings of the present RCT clearly indicate that replacing traditional cotton roll isolation with either a light-cured resin barrier (OpalDam) or a preformed clamp-free rubber dam (OptraDam) significantly enhances the comfort and acceptance of pediatric patients undergoing SDF application. Among the three methods, OpalDam yielded the lowest mean Wong–Baker FACES pain score and consistently outperformed the other techniques in patient-reported domains such as gag reflex, fluid leakage, soft-tissue stretching, tongue pressure, and overall comfort. This improvement can be attributed to the barrier's ability to form a precise, atraumatic seal without mechanical pressure or mucosal displacement, while simultaneously protecting adjacent soft tissues from the caustic and staining properties of SDF.

These results are in agreement with previous evidence demonstrating superior patient experience with clamp-free isolation systems. In a crossover clinical trial, Mahima et al. found that OptraDam significantly reduced Venham anxiety scores compared to conventional rubber dam use in children aged 6–12 years, especially when audiovisual explanation preceded the procedure.15 While adults have also expressed high tolerance for RDI, the heightened sensory sensitivity and lower distress thresholds of children justify the pursuit of less intrusive alternatives such as clamp-free isolation techniques.16

Contrary to common assumptions, previous randomized trials have shown that clamp-based rubber dams may not necessarily increase procedural anxiety in pediatric patients when applied under appropriate conditions. Shahzan et al. reported no statistically significant difference in Animoji scale scores between children isolated with cotton rolls and those treated under a rubber dam.17 A subsequent study by Shahzan et al. similarly found equivalent levels of post-procedural anxiety between the two methods.18 Reinforcing these findings, Kucukyilmaz et al., in a split-mouth RCT involving 100 children, observed no significant difference in either subjective pain scores or objective physiological stress indicators, such as heart rate and blood pressure, between cotton roll and rubber dam groups.19 Interestingly, the study also noted lower operator stress during rubber dam use, suggesting added procedural efficiency.

However, the poorer performance of cotton rolls in our study likely stems from the distinct clinical demands of SDF application. The metallic taste of the medicament, its propensity to stain soft tissues, and the need for prolonged and absolute moisture control create a procedural context in which even minor leakage or soft-tissue contact can trigger heightened discomfort in children. Clamp-free methods, by circumventing both mechanical trauma and fluid seepage, offer a more appropriate and tolerable solution for such indications.

Furthermore, the issue of clamp-related discomfort remains a persistent challenge in pediatric dentistry. In a meta-analysis of 16 randomized trials, it was revealed that neither topical nor infiltrative anesthesia, behavioral guidance, audiovisual distraction, nor electronic dental anesthesia could reliably mitigate the pain associated with clamp placement in children.20 This supports the notion that mechanical redesign, eliminating the clamp entirely, is a more effective approach than attempting to pharmacologically mask its adverse effects. Our findings, wherein OpalDam produced significantly better comfort scores than cotton rolls under otherwise identical conditions, reinforce this concept.

The evidence for increased procedural efficiency and improved comfort with alternative isolation methods is further strengthened by studies involving integrated suction–retractor systems. Alhareky et al. demonstrated that the Isolite system significantly reduced chair time and improved patient preference compared to the rubber dam, with 69% of children favoring its use.21 A similar pattern was observed in a prospective study by Bagher et al., where DryShield showed strong patient preference despite some operational noise.22 Although these systems differ in design from OpalDam and OptraDam, the shared attributes of non-clamp-based retention and tissue protection appear to be the common denominator in enhancing pediatric tolerance.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of the present study. The relatively small sample size, although statistically powered for medium effect detection, precluded subgroup analysis by variables such as age, prior dental experience, or anxiety traits. The use of self-reported pain and satisfaction scales immediately following treatment introduces a degree of subjectivity and potential recall bias. Future studies should consider incorporating physiological stress markers, delayed assessment points, and larger, more diverse pediatric cohorts to strengthen the generalizability of the results. Moreover, the procedures were confined to primary mandibular molars; further research is warranted in maxillary quadrants and in children with special healthcare needs or behavioural challenges.

Despite these limitations, the evidence from this study strongly supports the use of clamp-free isolation systems such as OpalDam and OptraDam as effective, well-tolerated alternatives to conventional cotton roll isolation for SDF application in children. Taken together with previous findings that clamp-based rubber dams do not necessarily exacerbate anxiety and that adjunctive behavioral and anesthetic strategies fail to reliably mitigate clamp-related pain, these results advocate for a paradigm shift toward atraumatic, child-centered isolation strategies in minimally invasive pediatric dentistry.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this RCT, clamp-free isolation methods markedly improved the pediatric experience during SDF application. OpalDam produced the lowest pain scores and the highest satisfaction across all comfort domains, while OptraDam also outperformed conventional cotton roll isolation, underscoring the clinical value of eliminating metal clamps and minimizing soft-tissue exposure. These findings support adopting resin-barrier or preformed clamp-free dams as the preferred isolation approach for minimally invasive caries management in young children, with the potential to enhance cooperation, reduce chairside stress, and improve overall treatment efficacy. Further multicenter trials with larger cohorts and objective stress markers are warranted to confirm these benefits across diverse clinical settings and procedural contexts.
