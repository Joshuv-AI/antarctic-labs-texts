An RNA damage response network mediates the lethality of 5-FU in colorectal cancer
Authors: Jung-Kuei Chen, Karl A Merrick, Yi Wen Kong, Anita Izrael-Tomasevic, George Eng, Erika D Handly, Jesse C Patterson, Ian G Cannell, Lucia Suarez-Lopez, Aaron M Hosios, Anh Dinh, Donald S Kirkpatrick, Kebing Yu, Christopher M Rose, Jonathan M Hernandez, Haeun Hwangbo, Adam C Palmer, Matthew G Vander Heiden, Ömer H Yilmaz, Michael B Yaffe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Summary5-fluorouracil (5-FU), a major anti-cancer therapeutic, is believed to function primarily by inhibiting thymidylate synthase, depleting deoxythymidine triphosphate (dTTP), and causing DNA damage. Here, we show that clinical combinations of 5-FU with oxaliplatin or irinotecan show no synergy in human colorectal cancer (CRC) trials and sub-additive killing in CRC cell lines. Using selective 5-FU metabolites, phospho- and ubiquitin proteomics, and primary human CRC organoids, we demonstrate that 5-FU-mediated CRC cell killing primarily involves an RNA damage response during ribosome biogenesis, causing lysosomal degradation of damaged rRNAs and proteasomal degradation of ubiquitinated ribosomal proteins. Tumor types clinically responsive to 5-FU treatment show upregulated rRNA biogenesis while 5-FU clinically non-responsive tumor types do not, instead showing greater sensitivity to 5-FU’s DNA damage effects. Finally, we show that treatments upregulating ribosome biogenesis, including KDM2A inhibition, promote RNA-dependent cell killing by 5-FU, demonstrating the potential for combinatorial targeting of this ribosomal RNA damage response for improved cancer therapy.Keywords: 5-FU, RNA damage, ribosomal RNA, ribosomal protein, 5-FU-based chemotherapy

Introduction

Cancer chemotherapy involves the use of drugs that preferentially kill or inhibit the growth of cancer cells. These drugs can be generally classified into alkylating agents that directly damage DNA bases or cross-link DNA strands, antimetabolites that block DNA/RNA synthesis, topoisomerase inhibitors and anti-tumor antibiotics that interfere with DNA replication and cause single- or double-strand DNA breaks, and mitotic inhibitors that impair the segregation of chromosomal DNA during cell division.1,2 Despite intensive efforts to understand the determinants of response to chemotherapeutics, it is frequently difficult to predict which patients will respond to which drugs, how to best combine different DNA-damaging agents with each other, or whether small-molecule inhibitors of the DNA damage response (DDR) will affect patient outcomes.3,4,5 Therefore, most combination chemotherapy regimens currently used in clinical practice have originated from empiric observations and clinical trials often designed based on non-overlapping toxicities.6

One of the earliest chemotherapy drugs administered to human patients with cancer, including those with cancers of the gastrointestinal tract, was the antimetabolite 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), first described in 1957.7 This agent has long been postulated to derive its clinical benefit from its incorporation into DNA and its inhibition of the enzyme thymidylate synthase (TYMS), which is required for deoxythymidine triphosphate (dTTP) production and DNA synthesis.8 In modern clinical practice, 5-FU is usually administered in combination with platinum-based DNA-crosslinking agents or DNA-damaging topoisomerase inhibitors. Genomic alterations induced by these latter two drug classes activate the DDR, a broad signaling network that arrests the cell division cycle, recruits DNA repair machinery to the lesions, and controls the onset of cell cycle re-entry, senescence, or programmed cell death.9,10

The combination of oxaliplatin or irinotecan with 5-FU and leucovorin (denoted FOLFOX and FOLFIRI, respectively) has shown modest efficacy in the treatment of patients with advanced stage gastrointestinal malignancies, particularly pancreatic and colorectal cancer (CRC), exceeding that observed with 5-FU plus leucovorin alone.11,12 Oxaliplatin causes intrastrand and interstrand DNA crosslinks between purine bases.13,14 Irinotecan is a camptothecin analog that forms a ternary complex with topoisomerase I and DNA, resulting in single- and double-strand DNA breaks.15 5-FU, present in both the FOLFOX and FOLFIRI regimens, has multiple activities. Following cell entry, 5-FU is converted to several active metabolites, including 5-fluorodeoxyuridine monophosphate (5-FdUMP), 5-fluorodeoxyuridine triphosphate (5-FdUTP), and 5-fluorouridine triphosphate (5-FUTP). 5-FdUMP potently inhibits substrate binding of deoxyuridine monophosphate to TYMS, resulting in the depletion of dTTP pools, impaired DNA synthesis, and replication fork stalling. The 5-FdUTP metabolite itself can be directly incorporated into DNA, contributing to DNA damage that requires base excision repair.16

Despite distinct mechanisms of action of oxaliplatin and irinotecan, their clinical efficacy is indistinguishable when combined with 5-FU.17 One explanation could be that 5-FU disruption of deoxyribonucleotide triphosphate (dNTP) pools compromises repair of irinotecan- or oxaliplatin-induced DNA lesions, or that incorporation of 5-FdUTP during DNA repair enhances lethality induced by DNA crosslinking or double-strand breaks. However, 5-FU also results in the incorporation of 5-FUTP into RNA, affecting the processing and maturation of multiple RNA species.18 Whether these damaged RNAs significantly contribute to 5-FU cytotoxicity in mammalian tumors or are cleared from RNA pools with negligible cytotoxic effects remains a topic of active debate. Recent papers continually emphasize important roles for 5-FU-mediated DNA damage,19 and the current clinical use of the deoxynucleoside 5-fluorodeoxyuridine (floxuridine) for hepatic CRC metastases via hepatic artery infusion20 reflects the belief that major clinical benefits of 5-FU derive from its DNA-damaging deoxy metabolites.

We examined 5-FU interactions with oxaliplatin and irinotecan to explore synergy and identify targeted agents to enhance the response. We unexpectedly found the primary cytotoxic mechanism of 5-FU in CRC cell lines and primary human CRC organoids involves an RNA damage-induced apoptotic pathway. We show that 5-FU cytotoxicity results from the disruption of ribosome biogenesis, causing nucleolar restructuring, lysosomal degradation of ribosomal RNA, and proteasomal degradation of ubiquitinated ribosomal protein subunits, promoting apoptotic cell death in both p53 wild-type (WT) and mutant cell lines. Modulating the rate of ribosome biogenesis using nutrients, small-molecule inhibitors, or genetic manipulation alters the sensitivity of CRC cells to 5-FU-induced cell death and demonstrates that targeting ribosome biogenesis pathways may improve anti-cancer treatment options.

Results

5-FU does not enhance the efficacy of oxaliplatin or irinotecan in killing CRC cells, nor display synergy in human clinical trials

To examine the cell killing efficacy of oxaliplatin and irinotecan in the treatment of CRC, we screened 11 well-established CRC cell lines for sensitivity to these drugs, and to 5-FU (Figures 1A and 1B). Relative drug sensitivities, compared by integrating the area under the dose-survival curves (AUC) (Figure 1B), revealed a wide range of responses. Some cell lines were uniformly sensitive to all three drugs (i.e., SW48, HCT116, and LoVo), others were moderately resistant (i.e., HT-29 and GP5d), while still others showed varied resistance to one or two agents, likely recapitulating clinical responses seen in a heterogeneous tumor population.

Intra-strand DNA crosslinks formed by oxaliplatin are excised by the nucleotide excision repair pathway or bypassed by translesion synthesis, while inter-strand crosslinks are repaired primarily by the Fanconi pathway and/or homologous recombination. DNA breaks generated by irinotecan inhibition of topoisomerase I are repaired primarily by single-strand annealing, homologous recombination, or non-homologous end joining. All these repair mechanisms require DNA polymerases and dNTPs for efficient repair.21,22 Depletion of the free dNTP pool by 5-FU-mediated inhibition of TYMS and/or inappropriate incorporation of 5-FdUTP into DNA might synergistically enhance the cell death caused by oxaliplatin or irinotecan at one or more doses (Figure 1C). We therefore measured cell survival in response to combination treatment with irinotecan and 5-FU or oxaliplatin and 5-FU. Surprisingly, combining 5-FU with oxaliplatin or irinotecan resulted in sub-additive loss of viability in nearly all cell lines examined (Figures 1D, 1G, and S1A). Conversely, we observed clear additivity between another nucleotide-depleting drug, hydroxyurea, with irinotecan or 5-FU, suggesting that 5-FU’s cytotoxic activity might be independent of its effects on deoxynucleotide pools (Figures 1E and 1F). Furthermore, focusing on the apoptotic response, there was typically minimal benefit of the 5-FU-containing drug combination beyond that of the DNA-damaging drugs alone, and even examples of antagonism between the drugs where addition of 5-FU decreased cell death induced by oxaliplatin or irinotecan treatment (Figures 1H and S1B–S1H).

To test whether this lack of synergistic killing seen in colon cancer cell lines was reflected in the clinical response of human patients with colon cancer, we used publicly available data and calculated the expected additivity of progression-free survival (PFS) or time to disease progress (TTP) for patients treated with these drugs individually23,24 or in combination and compared this to the observed PFS or TTP from publicly available FOLFOX or FOLFIRI trials25,26 (see STAR Methods).27 Analysis of this clinical trial data revealed, at best, an additive but non-synergistic effect for the combination of oxaliplatin with 5-FU (Figure 1I), and a sub-additive effect for the combination of 5-FU and irinotecan (Figure 1J). Taken together, these results indicate that the cytotoxic activities of 5-FU minimally enhanced the DNA damage-induced cytotoxicity conferred by these agents and are instead consistent with the beneficial effects of combination chemotherapy arising from patient-to-patient variability rather than drug additivity or synergy.28

5-FU-dependent cytotoxicity in mammalian CRC cells is primarily dependent upon its incorporation into RNA

5-FU’s primary mechanism of action has been classically ascribed to TYMS- and DDR-dependent activities. However, our results suggested that 5-FU’s DNA damage effects may not fully explain its efficacy. Indeed, we observed that TYMS mRNA expression across the CCLE cell line panel showed no correlation with 5-FU sensitivity (Figure S2A), and its expression was unable to distinguish responders from non-responders in clinical trials of FOLFIRI or FOLFOX for CRC (Figures S2B and S2C).29,30 Furthermore, maximal TYMS inhibition in our cell lines occurred at concentrations of 5-FU that do not induce apoptosis in short-term assays or affect proliferation over 72 h (Figures S2D–S2G). We therefore examined how CRC cells utilize 5-FU relative to uracil to better understand the global effects of 5-FU on nucleic acid metabolism. Uracil and 5-FU are metabolized by the same pyrimidine salvage pathway enzymes leading to their accumulation in both RNA and DNA (Figure 2A).18 Recent reports suggest that regulation of this pathway is important, since perturbations leading to greater 5-fluorouridine monophosphate (5-FUMP) increase 5-FU’s potency.31,32,33 Importantly, 5-FUMP is not committed to RNA incorporation, since the downstream metabolite, 5-fluorouridine diphosphate, is metabolized to 5-fluorodeoxyuridine diphosphate by ribonucleotide reductase, leading to its incorporation into DNA. Thus, the importance of 5-FU’s nucleic acid effects could be a combination of the drug’s metabolic fate and the consequences of its incorporation into DNA, RNA, or both. Using 14C-labeled 5-FU, we observed that far more of the drug accumulated in RNA than DNA, consistent with previous results.34 This was qualitatively and quantitatively distinct from how cells use 14C-labeled uracil (Figure S2H), which was distributed approximately equally between RNA and DNA (presumably as thymidine). However, significantly more radioactivity was detected in RNA from 14C-5-FU-treated cells. Furthermore, 5-FU treatment decreased the amount of 14C-uracil incorporated into both RNA and DNA, but did not qualitatively alter the distribution of labeled uracil, suggesting that 5-FU has an inherent priority over uracil for RNA incorporation.

Although these experiments show 5-FU is prioritized for RNA incorporation, it remained unclear if RNA is the relevant target responsible for the drug’s lethality, or merely a non-productive sink. To address this, we inhibited RNA synthesis or DNA synthesis and measured 5-FU-mediated apoptotic cell death. Given that rRNA represents the majority of cellular RNA and 5-FU’s previously described ability to impair rRNA biogenesis,35,36 we compared an RNA polymerase I (RNA Pol I)-specific inhibitor CX-5461,37,38 with that of aphidicolin, an inhibitor of replicative DNA polymerases. As shown in Figure S2J, pre-treatment with CX-5461 caused dramatic resistance to 5-FU-induced apoptosis, while blocking DNA replication with aphidicolin unexpectedly enhanced 5-FU-induced cell death. These altered apoptotic effects upon 5-FU treatment directly correlated with its incorporation into RNA; CX-5461 pre-treatment caused a ∼50% decrease in 5-FU incorporation into RNA, while aphidicolin pre-treatment increased 5-FU incorporation into RNA by ∼30% (Figure S2K). Furthermore, the enhanced apoptosis following aphidicolin pre-treatment was eliminated by blocking 5-FU incorporation into RNA by co-pretreatment with both aphidicolin and CX-5461 (Figures S2J and S2L).

Interestingly, oxaliplatin treatment significantly reduced the incorporation of 14C-5-FU in RNA, suggesting the observed sub-additivity of 5-FU/oxaliplatin combinations might result from blocking 5-FU’s RNA-dependent effects similar to CX-5461 (Figures S2M and S2N). This is consistent with the recent finding that oxaliplatin can act as a potent inhibitor of rRNA transcription, which significantly contributes to its mechanism of action.39 Notably, CX-5461 treatment did not suppress the ability of 5-FU to inhibit TYMS or activate the DDR as measured by Chk1 phosphorylation at S345 residue (Figure S2L), indicating that RNA Pol I inhibition does not block the uptake of 5-FU or its DNA damage effects. Thus, treatments that increase 5-FU incorporation into RNA result in greater cytotoxicity, whereas those preventing the drug’s RNA incorporation cause resistance to cell death.

It has been shown that TYMS knockdown sensitizes cells to 5-FU treatment,40 consistent with TYMS as a direct 5-FU drug target. Consistent with this, we observed a modest increase in cell death after 5-FU treatment following TYMS knockdown, particularly at lower levels of 5-FU treatment where the amount of death was small. However, at all 5-FU doses, drug-induced cell death was inhibited by CX-5461 treatment even when TYMS was knocked down (Figures S2O and S2P). Therefore, although 5-FU can cause DNA damage, the drug’s efficacy in mammalian CRC cell killing appears to be primarily dependent upon its incorporation into RNA.

5-FU downstream metabolites biased toward RNA or DNA incorporation reveal that RNA damage more potently induces CRC cell death

To better dissect phenotypic consequences of DNA- and RNA-dependent 5-FU effects, we used metabolites downstream of 5-FU—5-fluorouridine (5-FUR) and 5-fluoro-2′-deoxyuridine (5-FdUR)—that are strongly biased toward specific incorporation into RNA or DNA, respectively (Figure 2A).34 In agreement with these distinct metabolic fates, 5-FUR treatment only very weakly activated the DDR, assessed by phosphorylation of Chk1 and Chk2, whereas both 5-FU and 5-FdUR treatment resulted in robust activation (Figure 2B). In addition, 5-FdUR induced much higher activation of γH2AX and robust phosphorylation of RPA32 at Ser33, a specific marker of replication stress, compared to 5-FUR (Figures S3A and S3B). Despite its inability to activate the DDR, 5-FUR was a far more potent inducer of apoptosis compared to 5-FdUR in both p53 WT and isogenic p53-null cell lines (Figures 2C and 2D). Furthermore, despite causing equivalent stabilization of p53, 5-FUR treatment resulted in a far more robust accumulation of PUMA and poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) cleavage than 5-FdUR (Figure 2E).

Notably, cell death was dramatically reduced if cells were pre-treated with CX-5461 prior to 5-FUR administration (Figure 2F). In contrast, CX-5461 treatment did not block the modest amounts of apoptosis caused by 5-FdUR treatment. Interestingly, RNA Pol III inhibition using ML-6021841 also reduced 5-FUR-induced apoptosis (Figure 2G), albeit to a lesser extent than CX-5461. CX-5461 was recently shown to also inhibit topoisomerase II42,43 and cause DNA damage-induced apoptosis in neuroblastoma cells, which overexpress Top2B at high levels. However, in contrast to Top2A, colon cancers do not overexpress Top2B44 and the doses and conditions of CX-4561 treatment in our experiments did not activate the DDR (Figure S2L). To further confirm that the inhibitory effect of CX-5461 on cell death from 5-FU and its metabolites resulted from RNA Pol I inhibition, we used 1 ng/mL of actinomycin-D, a dose that preferentially inhibits Pol I activity.45 This treatment mitigated cell death induced by 5-FU and 5-FUR, but not by 5-FdUR (Figure 2H). To further demonstrate that RNA incorporation is required for cell death, we replaced the 5′-OH of 5-FUR with a methyl group (5-F-5′dUR) or removed the 3′-OH of 5-FUR (5-F-3′dUR), modifications which impair 5-FUR’s incorporation into RNA. Both 5-F-5′dUR and 5-F-3′dUR treatment had little effect on cell viability relative to 5-FUR (Figures 2I, S3C, and S3D), indicating a dependency on RNA incorporation. Taken together, these results suggest a model in which 5-FU incorporation into RNA, and subsequent RNA damage effects, more potently induce cell death in CRC cells compared to 5-FU’s DDR-dependent effects.

Most chemical or genetic perturbations reported to date that increase tumor cell sensitivity to 5-FU focused on enhancing the drug’s DNA damage effects.19,40,46 We therefore examined Chk1 inhibition or TYMS down-regulation on 5-FUR versus 5-FdUR treatment responses. Inhibition of Chk1 significantly enhanced the response to 5-FdUR in both p53 WT and null cells while having little effect on more potent response of these cells to 5-FUR (Figures 2J and 2K). Likewise, TYMS knockdown enhanced cell sensitivity toward 5-FdUR more than to 5-FUR (Figures S3E and S3F), further confirming that 5-FUR and 5-FdUR are biased toward RNA and DNA damage effects, respectively, and that known 5-FU synergistic treatments have primarily targeted the DNA damage-dependent mechanism of action of the drug.

5-FUR specifically triggers ubiquitination of multiple ribosomal proteins

Unlike the well-studied 5-FU-DNA damage response, relatively little is known about the signaling events downstream of 5-FU-RNA damage. To explore this, we performed a comprehensive mass spectrometry-based analysis of the phospho-SQ/TQ proteome, total phospho-proteome, and ubiquitin-proteome using quantitative 11-plex TMT mass spectrometry in CRC cells treated with 5-FdUR or 5-FUR (Methods, Figure 3A). To detect the most direct effects of each compound, we chose an acute time point (6 h) following accumulation into RNA and DNA, respectively, that correlated with increased p53 accumulation in 5-FUR-treated cells and KAP1 phosphorylation in 5-FdUR-treated cells (Figure 3B). We focused on post-translational modifications uniquely or preferentially enriched in one treatment or the other, hypothesizing that such changes would most likely explain differences in the phenotypic effects of these two 5-FU metabolites.

The DNA damage response kinases ATM, ATR, and DNA-PK preferentially phosphorylate SQ/TQ motifs,47 and p-SQ/p-TQ motif antibodies have previously been used to enrich substrates of these kinases after ionizing radiation and other genotoxic treatments.48 As shown in Figures 3C and 3D, 5-FdUR treatment resulted in marked upregulation of SQ/TQ phosphopeptide abundance, whereas 5-FUR had far more muted effects. Likewise, while both treatments caused changes to the total phospho-proteome, relatively few changes were specific to 5-FUR-treated cells compared to 5-FdUR (Figures 3E and 3F). Notably, the limited set of proteins whose phosphorylation levels were uniquely increased by 5-FUR treatment include a ribosomal protein (RPL12) and an RNA pseudouridine synthase (RPUSD2), both of which play significant roles in ribosome biogenesis. In contrast, many of the phosphorylated proteins containing 5-FdUR-modulated sites participate in DNA repair and replication, consistent with strong activation of the DDR and replication stress by this compound (Figure S4).

Strikingly, KGG enrichment identified many ubiquitinated peptides following 5-FUR treatment and far fewer following 5-FdUR treatment (Figure 3G). Nearly all significantly enhanced ubiquitinated peptides following 5-FUR treatment came from 40S to 60S ribosomal proteins (Figures 3H and 3K). This effect was specific to 5-FUR, as 5-FdUR treatment resulted in fewer differentially ubiquitination events, most of which occurred on proteins involved in the DDR (Figure 3L). Furthermore, nearly all the ribosomal proteins detected by mass spectrometry had at least one lysine that was differentially ubiquitinated following 5-FUR treatment, suggesting that the ribosome is a key signaling hub for 5-FUR effects. Although total changes in proteome were small at this time point, there was a significant decrease in the levels of ribosomal proteins in cells treated with 5-FUR compared to 5-FdUR (Figures 3I and 3J).

Overall, these data indicate that while there are some common alterations in the total and modified proteome induced by treatment with these two 5-FU metabolites, potentially reflecting some interconversion of RNA and DNA-damaging metabolites, each compound displayed unique consequences on cellular signaling, likely leading to differences in phenotypic effects.

5-FUR causes proteasome-dependent, but not lysosome-dependent, degradation of ribosomal proteins

To examine whether the decrease in ribosomal protein levels following 5-FUR treatment reflects loss or redistribution of intact ribosomes compared to 5-FdUR, we used sucrose gradient centrifugation to profile ribosomes in 5-FUR and 5-FdUR-treated cells. As shown in Figure 4A, 5-FUR treatment eliminated both the polysome and monosome fraction and depleted the levels of individual 40S and 60S subunits. In contrast, 5-FdUR treatment primarily shifted translation-proficient polysomes to monosomes and free ribosomal subunits with no decrease in total levels of ribosomal RNA. These findings suggest that 5-FUR treatment causes ribosome degradation, while 5-FdUR interferes with translation but does not alter total ribosome abundance. Furthermore, this 5-FUR-induced loss was specific to ribosomes, since no changes were observed in the levels of markers for other subcellular organelles (Figure 4B).

In response to various stimuli including starvation, oxidative stress, and inhibition of translation by mTOR inhibitors, ribosomes are known to undergo a specific autophagic process, ribophagy, involving ubiquitination- and lysosomal-dependent degradation.49 To examine whether ubiquitination of ribosomal proteins and loss of total ribosomes by 5-FUR reflected ribophagy, HCT116 cells were co-treated with 5-FUR together with either chloroquine, which inhibits autophagy by blocking lysosome-autophagosome fusion,50 or with the proteasome inhibitor MG132. Surprisingly, chloroquine co-treatment further decreased ribosome abundance compared to 5-FUR treatment alone (Figure S5A) and decreased the levels of ribosomal proteins (Figures 4C and S5B). In contrast, co-treatment with MG132 partially rescued the levels of these ribosomal proteins (Figure 4C), indicating that 5-FUR-induced ubiquitination of ribosomal proteins results in their degradation through a proteasome-dependent pathway rather than through lysosome-dependent ribophagy. 5-FU, the parent drug of 5-FUR, also caused degradation of ribosomal proteins, while treatment with 5-FdUR did not (Figure S5C).

This autophagosome-independent degradation of ribosomal proteins suggests that, in CRC cells, 5-FU and 5-FUR exert their primary effect during ribosome biogenesis rather than causing stress-dependent autophagy of pre-existing ribosome pools, consistent with previous findings related to 5-FU-induced cell death in yeast,36 and in mammalian cells.51,52 To further examine this, cells were stained for nucleolin, an abundant ribosome biogenesis protein that serves as a marker of nucleolar morphology.53 As shown in Figures 4D and 4E, treatment with 5-FU and 5-FUR, but not with 5-FdUR, disrupted the normal nucleolar architecture resulting in the appearance of small punctate nucleolin foci and nucleolar necklaces within 6 h of treatment, and large nucleolar aggregates by 24 h, consistent with nucleolar restructuring and defect in ribosome biogenesis.54

5-FUR induces lysosomal-dependent autophagy of ribosomal rRNA

We next examined the effect of 5-FU, 5-FUR, and 5-FdUR on total levels of ribosomal RNA. Consistent with impaired ribosome biogenesis, 5-FU and 5-FUR, but not 5-FdUR treatment, caused a 20%–30% decrease in 18S and 28S rRNA in whole-cell extracts (Figure 5A). In contrast, 5-FdUR treatment actually slightly increased 18S and 28S rRNA abundance, likely by inhibiting cell division leading to increased cell size (Figure S6A). Similarly, 5-FUR, but not 5-FdUR, significantly decreased the amount of 18S, 28S, and 45S rRNA when analyzed by qPCR (Figure 5B).55

Autophagy is a well-recognized mechanism for degrading damaged molecules in addition to intact organelles and macromolecular structures.56 Although not involved in degrading ribosomal proteins after 5-FU or 5-FUR treatment (Figure 4C), we wondered whether autophagy degraded the 5-FU-damaged ribosomal RNA. During autophagy, microtubule-associated protein 1A/1B-light chain 3 (LC3) is converted to a phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) membrane-localized form (LC3-II), initiating the formation and lengthening of autophagosomes, which later fuse with lysosomes to degrade cargo.57 The localization and post-translational modification of LC3 was therefore examined following 5-FU and 5-FUR treatment. As shown in Figures 5C and 5D, 5-FUR, but not 5-FdUR, resulted in bright LC3-positive puncta and clusters, with appearance of an LC3-II immuno-reactive form (Figure 5D, compare lane 3 with lanes 1 and 5). Next, flux through the autophagy pathway was measured by quantifying increases in LC3-II accumulation 3 h following chloroquine addition, which blocks lysosome-autophagosome fusion.58 Autophagic flux was significantly increased ∼2-fold by treatment with 5-FUR, but not 5-FdUR (Figure 5D). Treatment with the parent compound 5-FU caused a similar ∼2-fold increase in autophagic flux, mirroring that seen with 5-FUR, similar but slightly less than the increase observed with rapamycin, a positive control (Figure S6B).59

To further examine the role of autophagy in rRNA degradation, we targeted ATG5, a key protein required for the conjugation of LC3-I with PE to form membrane-associated LC3-II.60 Using 3 distinct small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), ATG5 knockdown blunted the decline in 45S, 28S, and 18S ribosomal RNAs following treatment with 5-FUR (Figure 5E). Likewise, chloroquine treatment also decreased 5-FUR-induced declines of 45S, 28S, and 18S ribosomal RNAs (Figure S6C). In addition, 5-FUR caused the accumulation of RNA-containing bodies that localized within autophagosomes as revealed by monodansylcadaverine staining (Figure 5F). Taken together, these data indicated that 5-FUR treatment targets ribosomal RNAs to autophagosomes for destruction by increasing autophagic flux, while targeting ribosomal proteins to the proteasome for degradation.

To explore the contribution of rRNA degradation to 5-FUR-induced tumor cell death, cells were treated with 5-FUR ± chloroquine to inhibit autophagosome-lysosome fusion. Chloroquine co-treatment caused a ∼2-fold increase in cell survival (Figures 5G and S6D). Co-staining for cPARP1 and cCaspase-3 revealed a similar ∼3-fold decrease in 5-FUR-induced apoptosis following chloroquine treatment (Figure 5H). Similarly, siRNA knockdown of ATG-5 also enhanced cell viability and reduced apoptotic cell death following 5-FUR treatment (Figures 5I, 5J, and S6E). Thus, the inhibition of autophagy following 5-FUR treatment reduces apoptotic cell death.

KDM2A depletion enhances ribosomal RNA damage and synergizes with 5-FU to promote CRC tumor cell death

Since 5-FU-induced cell death appeared to occur primarily through fluorouridine incorporation into rRNA, inducing an RNA damage response with distinct modes of RNA and ribosomal protein degradation, we hypothesized that co-treatments that upregulate rRNA transcription might enhance 5-FU cytotoxicity in cancer cells. Cells were therefore treated with 5-FUR in the presence or absence of glucose, a well-known regulator of rRNA transcription.61 Indeed, cells cultured in DMEM containing glucose, which enhanced nascent RNA transcription measured by nucleolar EU incorporation, displayed significantly increased cytotoxicity to 5-FUR (Figures 6A and 6B) compared to cells cultured in DMEM lacking glucose. In contrast to rRNA levels, no changes were observed in total mRNA levels (Figure S7A). Conversely, inhibition of mTORC1, a kinase complex that positively regulates ribosome biogenesis,62 greatly decreased the cytotoxicity to 5-FUR (Figure S7B).

KDM2A, a recently described Jumonji domain-containing lysine demethylase, negatively regulates rRNA transcription by demethylating H3K36 on the rDNA promoter.63,64,65,66 Treatment of cells with daminozide, a selective KDM2A small-molecule inhibitor,67 enhanced nascent RNA transcription and increased both 5-FUR toxicity and apoptotic cell death (Figures 6C–6E). Furthermore, depletion of KDM2A using siRNA resulted in similar enhancement of 5-FUR-induced cytotoxicity and apoptosis (Figures 6F and 6G). To further test whether KDM2A depletion enhanced cell death from 5-FU-induced RNA damage, but not DNA damage, we compared responses of control and KDM2A-depleted HCT116 cells to 5-FU, 5-FUR, or 5-FdUR. KDM2A depletion sensitized HCT116 cells to 5-FU and 5-FUR, but not to 5-FdUR treatment (Figures 6H, 6I, and S7C). To further demonstrate that upregulation of rRNA transcription, rather than other cellular processes affected by KDM2A depletion, was responsible for sensitization to 5-FU-induced RNA damage, KDM2A depletion experiments were performed in the presence or absence of low-dose actinomycin-D to block RNA Pol I activity. As shown in Figures 6J and S7D, the increased 5-FUR-induced apoptosis following KDM2A knockdown was completely abrogated when Pol I-dependent transcription was blocked. These data further implicate impaired ribosome biogenesis as a major mechanism for 5-FU-mediated cytotoxicity and suggest that therapeutic targeting of rRNA transcription pathways can enhance 5-FU therapeutic responses.

Sensitivity to RNA and DNA damage metabolites varies in a tissue-specific manner

To more broadly explore the relative cytotoxic effects of the RNA and DNA-damaging metabolites of 5-FU across a panel of CRC cell lines, we measured the GI50 (dose required for 50% inhibition of proliferation) for 5-FUR and 5-FdUR and calculated the log2(GI50 5-FUR/GI50 5-FdUR) ratio as a metric of metabolite sensitivity for each cell line. A ratio of zero indicates equal sensitivity toward the two compounds whereas a negative or positive number indicates greater sensitivity for 5-FUR or 5-FdUR, respectively. In every CRC cell line tested, we found that 5-FUR was a more potent inhibitor of cell viability than 5-FdUR (Figure 7A), indicating that CRC cells are broadly more sensitive to 5-FU’s RNA-damaging effects.

To determine how cells respond to these compounds across different tumor types and tissues of origin, we leveraged publicly available dose-response data from the NCI-60 cancer cell line panel68 and calculated the same relative metabolite response metric. Substantial variability in the relative sensitivity of cell lines from different tumor types to the RNA and DNA damage effects of 5-FU metabolites was seen across the NCI-60 panel, which, surprisingly, occurred in a tissue-specific manner (Figure 7B). Cell lines from cancers in which 5-FU has historically shown greatest clinical efficacy—colon, breast, and ovarian69—were almost always more sensitive to 5-FUR than 5-FdUR, whereas cells from other tumor types lacking strong clinical 5-FU responses either showed a mixed response, greater sensitivity to 5-FdUR, or no overall selectivity. These findings indicate that while CRC cells are particularly sensitive to 5-FUR, this phenomenon is not universally true across cancer cells from other tissues. Further, this suggests that tumor types clinically responsive to 5-FU treatment appear to be particularly sensitive to its RNA-damaging effects.

Gene expression analysis of patient tumor samples suggests that 5-FUR sensitivity is a predictor of clinical response to 5-FU-based therapy

To elucidate gene expression patterns associated with 5-FU RNA and DNA damage sensitivity, RNA expression data from the NCI-60 cell line panel were correlated with the metric of relative sensitivity to 5-FUR and 5-FdUR (Figure 7C). Genes, ranked by their Pearson correlation coefficients, were analyzed using gene set enrichment analysis.70 This revealed that expression of genes promoting RNA Pol I-dependent transcription strongly correlated with the greatest differential sensitivity to 5-FUR relative to 5-FdUR (Figure 7D and Table S1), providing independent validation of our earlier results. Furthermore, the genes highly expressed in our analysis of 5-FUR-sensitive cells were specifically downregulated in a 5-FU-resistant gastric cancer cell line (Figure S7E), indicating resistance to 5-FU is associated with loss of sensitivity to the RNA-damaging metabolite 5-FUR. In contrast, gene sets associated with 5-FdUR sensitivity, the DNA-damaging metabolite, were related to cell proliferation and the cell cycle (Table S1), consistent with primary effects of 5-FdUR mediated through the DDR.

We next analyzed the expression of POLR1A, encoding the RNA Pol I catalytic subunit, across The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) samples for which both tumor and adjacent normal tissue data were available. This revealed that tumor types in which 5-FU is currently a frontline therapy component generally exhibited both elevated expression of POLR1A compared to other tumor types, and had statistically significantly increased expression of POLR1A within the tumor relative to their normal tissue counterpart (Figure S7F). This tumor-specific POLR1A upregulation was not observed in tumor types in which 5-FU has no clinical utility.71 Notably, in both kidney and thyroid tumors, the expression of POLR1A gene is actually lower in tumor cells than normal non-cancerous tissue.

Finally, we asked whether these data could provide insights into patient responses to 5-FU-based therapies. To identify gene expression signatures predicting clinical response to FOLFOX, Tsuji et al. analyzed pre-treatment tumor biopsies and measured basal gene expression profiles.30 Patients were subsequently classified as responders or non-responders to treatment, allowing those authors to derive a strongly predictive gene signature in non-responders. We found that their predictive signature for non-responders was highly enriched in genes that anti-correlate with sensitivity to 5-FUR versus 5-FdUR, suggesting that patients whose tumors fail to respond to 5-FU-based therapies are likely resistant to 5-FU’s RNA damage effects (Figure 7E).

5-FU-induced RNA damage is a more potent inducer of cell death in patient-derived CRC organoids than 5-FU-induced DNA damage

To further demonstrate that RNA damage is primarily responsible for the dominant cytotoxic effects of 5-FU treatment in CRC, we compared the relative responses of three patient-derived CRC primary tumor organoids to 5-FUR and 5-FdUR. Consistent with our finding in CRC cell lines, 5-FUR was a much more potent inducer of tumor cell death in all three CRC tumor organoids (Figures 7F–7H). Nucleolin staining demonstrated that treatment with 5-FU and 5-FUR, but not 5-FdUR, induced nucleolar restructuring, resulting in smaller necklace-like nucleolin foci within 6 h of treatment (Figure 7I), consistent with impairment of ribosome biogenesis.

Discussion

Motivated by the unexpected finding of a lack of synergistic or even additive anti-tumor responses to clinically used 5-FU-based combination chemotherapy in a systematic study of human clinical trial data and a panel of CRC cell lines, we found using systems pharmacology, phospho- and ubiquitin proteomics, cell biology, and biochemical validation, that apoptotic cell death induced by 5-FU in CRC cells is primarily mediated by its effects on ribosomal RNA leading to impaired ribosome biosynthesis, rather than through its effects on DNA replication or direct DNA damage. Furthermore, by analyzing differential sensitivity to RNA- and DNA-damaging metabolites of 5-FU in the NCI-60 dataset, we discovered that RNA damage-induced cell death is particularly over-represented in human cell lines from tumor types where 5-FU has clinical utility. In addition, correlating human gene expression data from patients who responded or failed to respond to 5-FU-based therapies demonstrated that this RNA damage phenomenon appears to control the observed clinical response in patients with CRC treated with 5-FU-containing regimens. Consistent with this, the RNA-damaging metabolite of 5-FU more potently induced cell death in CRC patient-derived organoids than the DNA-damaging metabolite, and phenocopied the nucleolar restructuring from impaired ribosome biogenesis seen upon 5-FU treatment. We expect that capecitabine, a prodrug metabolized into 5-FU, primarily kills CRC cancer cells through this same RNA-damaging ribosome biogenesis mechanism.

Similar to our findings, Milano and colleagues reported no increase in apoptotic cell death in WiDr CRC cells when oxaliplatin was added to 5-FU and folinic acid treatment, using a mitochondrial membrane permeability assay.72 There are many possible reasons for the sub-additive loss of viability when 5-FU is included in combination therapies if its primary mechanism of action in CRC cells is TYMS inhibition, such as inhibition of replication following irinotecan or oxaliplatin treatment, which would alleviate the need for high dNTP levels, or slowing replication after dNTP depletion to allow cells more time to repair irinotecan- or oxaliplatin-induced DNA lesions. However, Figure S2P shows that TYMS knockdown had no effect on the ability of CX-5461 to suppress 5-FU-induced cell death. Furthermore, oxaliplatin treatment was recently shown to inhibit Pol I-dependent rRNA transcription,39 consistent with our finding that oxaliplatin treatment resulted in reduced incorporation of 5-FU into RNA (Figure S2M). Taken together, these data support a dNTP-independent mechanism for the lack of synergy seen with 5-FU and these DNA-damaging agents.

It is important to stress that experiments implicating the RNA incorporation of 5-FU as a significant contributor to cytotoxicity were first performed over 40 years ago.73,74 Mechanistic studies in flies and fission yeast show that 5-FU can affect RNA processing and the RNA exosome,75,76 while large-scale chemogenomic screens and other studies in budding yeast indicated that 5-FU’s mechanism of action could involve an RNA-dependent mechanism.36,77 Similar RNA processing defects have also been reported in mammalian cells.35,51,52,78,79,80,81,82,83 However, the relative importance of these RNA effects on the drug’s clinical efficacy remains an active area of debate and ongoing investigation, given the ability of 5-FU to activate the DDR, and its well-accepted mechanism of TYMS inhibition.18,19 The purported importance of DNA damage as a cytotoxic mechanism in human cells has been highlighted by many studies showing that 5-FU’s cytotoxicity can be enhanced by targeting its DNA damage effects (e.g., via TYMS knockdown, Chk1 or Rad51 inhibition, or overexpression of N-methylpurine-DNA glycosylase).40,46,84,85 Our work, using a broad panel of CRC cell lines, CRC organoids, the NCI-60 collection, RNA and DNA metabolites of 5-FU, and gene expression data from human patients with CRC, however, indicates that DNA damage perturbations are likely secondary effects of the drug in clinically relevant settings. 5-FU’s primary efficacy in clinically relevant tumors is instead dictated by ribosomal RNA damage and defective ribosome biogenesis, which can be enhanced by modulating rRNA transcription to enhance tumor cell death.

Our findings implicating the disruption of ribosome biogenesis accompanied by lysosomal rRNA destruction and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis of ribosomal proteins as the primary mechanism for 5-FU-induced cell death are strongly supported by a recent study from Nordlund and colleagues who reported that RNA modification pathways were major contributors to the cytotoxicity of 5-FU,81 but differ from those of Therizols et al., who claimed that 5-FU treatment resulted instead in the generation of functional 5-FU-containing ribosomes which preferentially translate a set of survival genes.86 The reason for this discrepancy is unclear, although, like us, Therizols et al., also observed a marked decrease in the levels of 18S and 28S rRNA after 5-FU treatment, which we showed is accompanied by the disruption of nucleolar morphology and a decrease in ribosomes. Furthermore, we showed that activation of rRNA transcription by inhibiting KDM2A significantly enhanced the sensitivity of CRC to the RNA damage effects of 5-FU. Intriguingly, KDM2A is a multi-functional lysine demethylase that is overexpressed in a variety of cancer types, including gastric, colon, breast, and non-small cell lung cancer, and thought to contribute to tumor metastasis, in part through Erk1/2 activation.87,88,89,90 Whether its role as a suppressor of rRNA transcription contributes to its pro-metastatic behavior is unclear.

In the unperturbed cell state, ribosome abundance is highly regulated at the level of biogenesis and turnover to meet cellular demands under various physiologically conditions.91 Ordinarily, ribosome turnover is regulated through the lysosomal/autophagy pathway (ribophagy), which is enhanced by nutrient starvation or mTOR inhibition.91,92 In contrast, when ribosomal proteins accumulate in stoichiometric excess over the rRNA, Lam et al. showed that they were degraded by ubiquitin-dependent proteolysis.93 Here, we report that 5-FU RNA damage induces a hybrid response in which the fluorinated RNAs are targeted to the lysosome for degradation, while the excess ribosomal proteins are ubiquitinated and targeted to the proteosome. The molecular mechanism through which this hybrid degradation process activates the apoptotic response remains to be determined but suggests that the integrity of structured RNAs (i.e., rRNAs and possibly tRNAs as well) is likely being monitored by some type of quality control apparatus.

Importantly, our findings should not be interpreted, in any way, as invalidating the clear clinical utility of FOLFOX and FOLFIRI chemotherapy regimens, both of which have shown marked improvement in outcomes for a selected subset of patients with CRC,11,12 likely as a consequence of patient-to-patient variability.28 Instead, our results reveal the importance of the RNA damage mechanism for 5-FU-induced cell death in colon cancer cells, which could be further leveraged by additional drug combinations that specifically target ribosome biogenesis pathways.

Limitations of the study

Overarching limitations of the study are highlighted here. Our demonstration that RNA damage during ribosome biogenesis is the major mechanism for the cytotoxic anti-cancer effects of 5-FU in CRC treatment protocols is based on proteomic, cell biological, and biochemical studies in cultured cells and human tumor organoids and correlates with data from human patients with CRC, but has not been directly demonstrated in human CRC tumors using pre- and post-treatment biopsies. We do not yet know the molecular quality control mechanism by which 5-FU-containing RNAs are sensed, triggering nascent ribosome degradation. Elucidating the signaling pathways linking autophagy of damaged ribosomes to cell death also awaits further examination. Nonetheless, this study sheds insights into better mechanisms for clinical utilization of 5-FU, both as a single agent and in combination with DNA-damaging chemotherapies.

Experimental model and study participant details

HT55, SW48, GP5d, Colo678, Colo320DM, HCT116, and HT-29 cells were grown in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin. HCT15, DLD1, and Colo205 cells were grown in RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin. Method details

Immunoblotting

After the indicated treatment, cells were collected with their media, pelleted by centrifugation, and washed 2x PBS. Cell pellets were then lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with phosphatase inhibitors (PhosSTOP, Roche) and protease inhibitors (cOmplete protease inhibitors, Roche). Lysate concentrations were determined by BCA assay, and equal amounts of protein were loaded in each lane of the SDS-PAGE gel. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, blocked with Odyssey Blocking Buffer (Licor) diluted 1:1 with PBS. Antibodies were diluted in Odyssey Blocking Buffer mixed 1:1 with PBS-T. Primary antibodies were typically incubated with membranes overnight at 4°C, and secondary antibodies were applied for 1–2 h at room temperature. After antibody incubations, membranes were washed 3x with PBS-T. Western blots were visualized Licor Odyssey Imager, and analyzed using Image Studio software (Licor). For autophagy flux measurement, HCT116 cells were treated with 80 μM 5-FU, 2 μM 5-FUR, 2 μM 5-FdUR or 0.2 μM rapamycin for 15 h, then treated with or without 20 μM chloroquine (CQ) for an additional 3 h. LC3-II was normalized to actin, and autophagic flux calculated by subtracting the value of normalized LC3-II in the presence of CQ by that without CQ. Autophagy flux of DMSO-treated cells was set to 1, and the remaining samples normalized accordingly.

Flow cytometry

After the indicated treatment, ∼3 x 106 cells were collected, fixed, and stained as described.95 Briefly, treated cells were collected by trypsinization and mixed with their growth media to ensure that detached or loosely attached cells were also harvested. Cells were then pelleted by centrifugation, washed with PBS, and fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Cells were washed twice with PBS +1% BSA, resuspended in ice-cold methanol, and stored at −20°C until further processing. For antibody staining, cells were pelleted by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS-T + 1% BSA, and, if using, incubated with the unconjugated primary antibody for >1 h at room temperature while mixing. Fixed cells were then washed twice with PBS-T + 1% BSA, and stained with appropriate secondary antibody and/or a fluorphore-conjugated primary antibody typically overnight at 4°C while mixing. Cells were then washed twice with PBS-T + 1% BSA, once with PBS +1% BSA. Induction of apoptosis was determined based on positive staining for cleaved-caspase 3 (BD biosciences, cat: 559565, secondary antibody conjugated with Alexa 488) and/or Alexa 647-conjugated cleaved-PARP (BD biosciences, cat: 558710).

Detection of 14C-5-FU and 14C-Uracil in RNA and DNA

For cell labeling experiments, 24 h after 1x106 cells were plated in a 6-well dish, 0.6 μCi of [2-14C]-Uracil (56 mCi/mmol) or [2-14C]-5-FU (48 mCi/mmol) was added to each well containing 1 mL of media. Cells were collected at the indicated times. Human clinical trials analysis

Kaplan-Meier plots of Progression-Free Survival (PFS) or Time To Progression (TTP) for previously untreated metastatic colorectal cancer treated with 5-FU/LV, oxaliplatin, irinotecan, FOLFOX, and FOLFIRI were extracted and digitized from published clinical trials (Table S2).23,24,25,26 The trial of oxaliplatin monotherapy reported median PFS (4.1 months) and median duration of response (7.2 months) among 24% of patients with response,23 which was fitted by a 2-parameter Weibull survival function. Clinical drug additivity was defined as the addition of progression-free survival times (PFS) or times to disease progression (TTP), while correcting for schedules of CT scanning used to measure progression. We used a two-step procedure to simulate the PFS distribution under drug additivity. First, a virtual patient cohort was generated by sampling from a joint distribution of monotherapy responses with a correlation supported by experimental data. Second, each virtual patient’s response to two drugs (A, B) was calculated as the sum of PFS or TTP times for the individual drugs (PFSAB = PFSA + PFSB – first scan time). For example, if a hypothetical patient X had 4 months PFS in response to drug A, and 7 months PFS in response to drug B, and the first CT scan for assessing disease progression was obtained at 1 month, then under drug additivity, the expected PFS time for patient X treated with A + B would be 4 + 7–1 = 10 months. The Cox proportional hazard method was used to calculate hazard ratios between the additivity predictions and the observed PFS/TTP for patients treated with the drug combination. 5-FU/LV and irinotecan have sub-additive effects on time to progression (hazard ratio for observed versus expected efficacy = 1.25, 95% CI [1.03, 1.51], Cox proportional hazard model). Note that HR = 1 does not mean no efficacy, it means as effective as expected. 5-FU/LV and oxaliplatin have additive effects on Progression-free survival (hazard ratio for observed versus expected efficacy = 1.08, 95% CI [0.86, 1.36]. Additional details of the methods are described in.27

Proteome and PTM quantification sample preparation

Lysis buffer containing 9 M urea, 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES (pH 8.0), one complete-mini (EDTA free) protease inhibitor (Roche), and one phosphatase inhibitor (PHOSstop) tablet (Roche) were added to cell culture plates in order to lyse the cells. Protein concentrations were then determined by Bradford assay and then disulfide bonds were reduced by incubation with 5 mM DTT (45 min, 37°C). This was followed by alkylation of cysteine residues by 15 mM IAA (30 min, RT Dark) which was quenched by the addition of 5 mM DTT (15 min, RT Dark). Initial protein digestion was performed by the addition of LysC (1:50 enzyme:substrate ratio) followed by incubation at 37°C for 3 h. Samples were then diluted to 1.5 M urea with 50 mM HEPES (pH 8.0) before the addition of Trypsin (1:25 enzyme to substrate ratio) and incubation overnight at 37°C. The next day, the resulting peptide mixtures were acidified and desalted via solid phase extraction (SPE; SepPak, Waters). Following desalting samples were separately enriched for phosphorylated peptides using TiO2 enrichment. Flow through of this enrichment was saved for ubiquitylome and global proteome analysis. Enriched phosphorylated peptides were then desalted and resuspended in 200 mM HEPES (pH 8.5) and labeled with tandem mass tags (TMT, Thermo Fisher Pierce) according to the manufacture instructions. After 1 h of labeling the reaction was quenched by the addition of 5% hydroxylamine and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Labeled peptides were then mixed, acidified, and purified by SPE. Labeled and combined phosphopeptides were then enriched for peptides bearing the ATM/ATR motif utilizing a PTMscan (Cell Signaling Technology) kit. Enriched ATM/ATR peptide were desalted before nLC-MS/MS analysis. Flow through from the ATM/ATR enrichment was desalted before separation into 96 fractions by offline basic-reversed phase chromatography. Fractions were then combined into 12 fraction pools which were analyzed by nLC-MS/MS. For ubiquitylome and global proteome analysis flow through from the TiO2 enrichment was desalted before enrichment with the PTMscan Kgg remnant motif antibody (Cell Signaling Technology) as previously described.96 This resulted in 6 Kgg fractions which were desalted before nLC-MS/MS analysis. Flow through from the Kgg enrichment was was desalted before separation into 96 fractions by offline basic-reversed phase chromatography. Fractions were then combined into 24 fraction pools of which 12 were analyzed by nLC-MS/MS.

Proteome and PTM quantitative nLC-MS/MS analysis

For nLC-MS/MS analysis, peptides were separated using a Dionex UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano Proflow system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for ATM/ATR and Kgg experiments or a NanoAcquity UPLC (Waters) for global proteome and phosphorylation experiments. A gradient of 2% buffer A (98% H20, 2% ACN with 0.1% formic acid) to 30% or 35% buffer B (98% ACN, 2% H20, 0.1% formic acid) with a flow rate of 450 or 500 nL/min was used to separate peptides over a 25 cm capillary column (100 μm I.D.) packed with Waters nanoAcquity M-Class BEH (1.7 μm) material (New Objective, Woburn, MA). Samples were analyzed using an Orbitrap Fusion or Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer, (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA). For all analyses the SPS-MS3 method was implemented for improved quantitative accuracy.97,98 For all experiments, intact peptides were surveyed in the Orbitrap and the top 10 peptides were selected for fragmentation. For Kgg experiments +2 precursor ions were not selected for fragmentation as previously described.96 For global proteome, global phosphoproteome, and ATM/ATR experiments fragments were analyzed in the ion trap while Kgg experiments utilized the Orbitrap for MS2 analysis. Quantitative MS3 scans selected the 8 most abundant fragment ions from the MS2 spectrum and fragmented them at high energy (HCD, 55 NCE) to produce reporter mass ions.

Proteomic and PTM data analysis

Assignment of MS/MS spectra was performed using the MASCOT search algorithm to search against all entries for Homo sapiens (human) in UniProt (downloaded June 2016). A search of all tryptic peptides (2 missed cleavages) was performed and a precursor tolerance of 50 ppm was used to limit the number of candidate peptides, while a 0.8 Da tolerance was used to match MS/MS data collected in the ion trap. Static modifications included TMT on the N-terminus of peptides and lysine residues (+229.16293 for TMT) and cysteine alkylation (+57.0215), while variable modifications included methionine oxidation (+15.9949) for all experiments, phosphorylation of S/T/Y (+79.9663) for global phosphorylation and ATM/ATR experiments, and gg remnant (+114.0429) for ubiquitylome experiments. Peptide spectral matches were filtered to a 2% false discovery rate using a target decoy approach scored with a linear discrimination analysis algorithm before filtering to a 2% false discovery rate at the protein level as previously described.99 Quantitative values were extracted and corrected for isotopic impurities using Mojave.100 Additionally, quantitative events with a precursor purity <0.5 (±0.25 Da) or sum intensity <30,000 were discarded before quantitative values were normalized and converted to “relative abundance” values using custom scripts coded in R. For quantitative analysis, peptide spectral match level data was summed to the peptide level and peptide level data was summed to the protein level. Peptides showing significant differences in drug-treated cells compared to DMSO-treated cells were highlighted in the volcano plot. Peptides classified as “specific” represented the enrichment or depletion of peptides in cells treated with either 5-FdUR or 5-FUR compared to DMSO. For example, peptide intensity with log2 (5-FUR/DMSO) > or <1 is classified as 5-FUR specific peptide. Peptides classified as “drug A > drug B” represented the enrichment or depletion of peptides in cells treated with both 5-FdUR and 5-FUR compared to DMSO, and the enrichment or depletion in one drug is 2-fold more than another drug. For example, peptide intensity with log2 (5-FUR/5-FdUR) > 1 is classified as 5-FUR > 5-FdUR peptide in the volcano plot. For each comparison a Student’s t test (two tailed, unequal variance) was used to calculate a nominal p-value. Data were further visualized in Spotfire (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA). Ubiquitinated proteins were analyzed by STRING101 and visualized using Cytoscape102 (Figures 3K, 3L and S4).

Computational analysis of NCI-60 cell line drug response

Dose-response data for 5-FUR and 5-FdUR treatment in the NCI-60 cell line panel was downloaded from the NCI’s Developmental Therapeutics Program website. Percent growth inhibition values were averaged when multiple measurements were reported for a given compound concentration in the cell line being examined. Dose-response curves were fit using Prism, and absolute GI 50 measurements were extracted for each cell line treatment. Quality of the curve fit was determined R2 > 0.70, and cell lines in which either compound did not fit this criteria were excluded from further analysis. Further, if a GI 50 could not be accurately determined for a compound due to the GI 50 being outside of the reported dose range (i.e., the lowest concentration of a compound gave greater than 50% inhibition or the highest concentration gave less than 50% inhibition), then this cell line was also excluded from further analysis. To assess relative sensitivity of the remaining cell lines to 5-FUR and 5-FdUR, we calculated the log2(5-FUR GI 50/5-FdUR GI 50). For the cell lines that passed our data quality filters above, we downloaded gene expression data collected by microarray from the NCI CellMiner website.68 The Z score of both gene expression data and log2(5-FUR GI 50/5-FdUR GI 50) were calculated, and the Pearson correlation coefficient between the expression of each gene and the relative sensitivity metric was calculated using MeV.94 These correlation coefficients were then used as a pre-ranked list for Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). Reported statistics are based on gene list permutations.

Cell survival assays

103 colorectal cancer cells were plated per well in 96 or 384-well plates. 24 h after plating drugs were added to the media at the indicated concentrations, and viability was measured 48 or 72 h after drug addition by either CellTiter-Glo (Figures 2I, S2D–S2G, S3E and S3F), MTT (Figures 6A, S3C, and S7B), SYTO60 staining (Figure 2J) or Resazurin (Figures 1A, 1B, 1G, and S1 only as indicated). Percent viability is normalized to vehicle treated control wells. Dose-response curves were fit using Prism, and the area under the curve (AUC) and GI 50 values for response to the compounds were determined when indicated. For dose response matrices, the expected viability was determined based on a Bliss Independence model of drug interaction,103 and relative synergy was determined based on comparing the expected viability to that which was observed. Negative, 0 and positive numbers indicate antagonism, additivity, and synergy effect, respectively. For Trypan Blue exclusion viability test, cell sample was diluted 1:1 with 0.4% Trypan Blue solution and incubated for 1–2 min at room temperature. The unstained cells were then counted under light microscope.

Colony formation assay

HCT116 cells treated with different siRNA were counted and 400 cells were plated in a six-well plate. Cells were exposed to different dosages of drugs and then incubated at 37°C for 10 to 14 days until colonies could be visualized under a light microscope. The colonies were stained with 20% ethanol solution containing 0.5% crystal violet. After gentle washing, the colonies were counted and normalized to control siRNA-treated cells.

Immunofluorescence

2x105 cells seeded on 6-well containing one poly-L-lysine coverslips (BD Biosciences) were fixed with 10% neutral buffered formalin solution (Sigma) for 15 min and extracted with PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma) for 10 min at room temperature. After blocking with 5% BSA (Sigma), samples were incubated with indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Samples were then washed and incubated with secondary antibodies plus DAPI (Thermo Scientific) for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were mounted onto glass slides with Fluoromount mounting medium (SouthernBiotech) and visualized by FV1200 (Olympus) confocal microscope. For live cell microscopy with monodansylcadaverine (MDC) staining, cells seeded on 35 mm glass-bottom dishes (In Vitro Scientific) were stained with SYTO RNA select green fluorescence (ThermoFisher) for 20 min at 37°C, washed and followed with 50 μM monodansylcadaverine (Sigma) plus Hoechst33342 (Invitrogen) for 30 min at 37°C. After wash with PBS, the stained cells in FluoroBrite DMEM (ThermoFisher) were analyzed with FV1200 (Olympus) confocal microscope.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from 3x106 cells using RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen). The RNA concentration was determined using a Nanodrop ND1000 (Nanodrop Technologies). RNA (1 μg) was reversely transcribed to cDNA with SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen) using Random hexamer (Invitrogen) as primer. Real-time PCR was performed on a StepOnePlus instrument (Applied Biosystems) using SYBR green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). The qPCR conditions were as follows: 95°C for 10 min, 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 1 min. This was followed by melting curve analysis to confirm singlely amplified product: 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 1 min, 0.05 °C/s increment from 60°C to 95°C, and hold at 95°C for 15 s. The results were analyzed with StepOne real-time PCR software.

Ribosome profiling

Sucrose density gradient centrifugation was used to separate ribosomes into polysomal and subpolysomal fractions. Cells were pre-treated with 0.1 mg/mL cycloheximide for 5 min at 37°C. After washed with PBS containing 0.1 mg/mL cycloheximide, Cells were lysed with lysis buffer (15 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 0.3M NaCl, 15 mM MgCl2, 1 mg/mL heparin, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1 mg/mL Cycloheximide and 80U/ml RNase inhibitor) according to cell number (1.5x107/mL). After centrifugation at 13,000 g for 15 min, the supernatants were loaded onto a 10–60% sucrose gradients in lysis buffer without Triton X-100. The gradients were sedimented at 38,000 rpm for 2 h using an SW41 Ti rotor at 4°C (221,777 g). Fractions were collected and analyzed with a Gradient Master fraction collector system (Biocamp).

18S/28S rRNA detection

Total RNA was extracted from 3x106 cells using RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen). The RNA was separated using standard formaldehyde denaturing gel for northern blot. Gel was stained with GelRed Nucleic Acid Staining dye (Biotium) and visualized under UV light. 18S and 28S rRNA were quantified by Fiji ImageJ software.

Measurement of new RNA synthesis

1 x 105 HCT116 cells were seeded on coverslides in 6 well dishes. After the indicated culture or treatment conditions., cells were treated with 1 mM EU for 1h, then fixed, permeabilized and detected using the Click-iT RNA Imaging Kit (Invitrogen) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Imaging was performed using confocal microscopy (above) and EU incorporation quantified using Fiji ImageJ software. Data were averaged using all cells within a single field with ≥10 fields for each condition.

Organoid drug responses assay

For drug dose response assays, organoids were dissociated to single cells and plated in Matrigel plugs 10 μL per well, each containing 2000 cells. Cell number was quantified using resazurin reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) (0.05 mg/mL final concentration) applied to the organoid plugs after 5 days of drug treatment. The organoids were incubated with resazurin for 4 h, and red fluorescence was quantified by a plate reader spectrophotometer 560ex/590em (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

Organoid immunofluorescence

Organoids were washed once with PBS and then fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at RT and stored in PBS at 4°C. Organoids were permeabilized with PBS/3% BSA containing 1% Triton X-100 for 4h at 4°C and then stained with primary antibody against nucleolin (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology) overnight in PBS/3% BSA solution. After a PBS wash for 10 min x3, organoids were stained with secondary Ab overnight in PBS/3% BSA and then with 5 μg/mL DAPI (Thermo Scientific) in PBS for 1 h at 4°C. Imaging was performed using an Olympus FV1200 confocal microscope. The size of nucleolin-staining foci in ≥100 cells per condition was quantified and is shown as a violin plot (Figure 7I).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism using a two-tailed unpaired t test. Results are displayed as mean ± SEM or mean ± SD in graphs. Significance is represented as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001, NS, not significant (p > 0.05). Biological replicates are indicated in the figure legends.
