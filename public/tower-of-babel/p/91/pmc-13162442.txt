Lactococcus cremoris decreases virulence‑associated properties, and modifies the protein profile of foodborne pathogen Campylobacter jejuni in biofilm
Authors: Blaž Jug, Maja Šikić Pogačar, Aleš Berlec, Anja Klančnik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Campylobacter jejuni, the most common foodborne pathogen, relies on adhesion, invasion, and biofilm formation for successful virulence and persistence in the food-processing environments. In this study, we investigated the effects of the transient bacterium with probiotic properties Lactococcus cremoris on C. jejuni virulence via an adhesion and invasion assay using human colon adenocarcinoma Caco-2 cells, culturability via colony-forming units, and proteomic adaptation in co-culture via label-free quantitative mass spectrometry. Caco-2 cell adhesion and invasion assay revealed that L. cremoris decreases C. jejuni adhesion (by 50%) and invasion (by 92%), particularly when inoculated prior to the pathogen. In mature (48 h old) dual-species biofilms, C. jejuni culturability decreased by 88%, while L. cremoris culturability remained unaffected, compared to single-species biofilms. Proteomic analysis of biofilm cells showed metabolic reprogramming in C. jejuni, with increased levels of proteins related to the TCA cycle, stress response, amino acid and nucleotide metabolism, chemotaxis, and energy production. The most upregulated proteins included the tungsten transporter TupA, LuxS, Dps, and hydrogenase subunits HydAB, indicating adaptations to nutrient limitation and oxidative stress. Nevertheless, the decreased culturability of C. jejuni suggests it is overwhelmed under the competitive conditions of dual-species biofilms. Our findings demonstrate that L. cremoris exerts strong antagonistic effects on C. jejuni, restricting its virulence, and triggering significant metabolic shifts in dual-species biofilms. These results highlight the potential of L. cremoris as a preventative strategy against C. jejuni adhesion, colonisation, and transmission.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13099-026-00827-3.

Introduction

Lactococcus cremoris (formerly Lactococcus lactis subsp. cremoris) is a Gram-positive, spherical, non-motile, non-sporulating, facultative anaerobic lactic acid bacterium extensively utilised in dairy fermentation processes and biotechnological applications, including genetic engineering. The species is considered safe for use in food applications and has been included in regulatory safety assessments [1, 2].

Beyond its technological relevance, certain L. cremoris strains have been described as transient bacteria with probiotic properties, meaning that they do not permanently colonize the gastrointestinal tract but may exert beneficial effects during transient passage. Darby et al. (2019) [3] identified L. cremoris ATCC 19,257 as a highly efficacious beneficial bacterium exhibiting cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory effects in Drosophila and murine models, while Luerce et al. (2014) [4] demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects of L. cremoris on human colon adenocarcinoma Caco-2 cells.

Lactic acid bacteria, including L. cremoris, can modulate pathogen behaviour by influencing virulence-associated phenotypes such as adhesion, motility, and biofilm formation. These effects are mediated through multiple mechanisms, including local pH reduction, production of fermentation by-products, secretion of bacteriocins, and competition for nutrients and adhesion sites [5, 6]. In line with this, L. cremoris has demonstrated inhibitory activity against several foodborne pathogens. For example, Boodhoo et al. (2023) [7] reported that oral administration of L. cremoris NZ9000 significantly reduced necrotic enteritis lesion scores in chickens infected with Clostridium perfringens. In addition, specific L. cremoris strains have been shown to exert antagonistic effects against Salmonella Typhimurium, Listeria monocytogenes and Enterococcus faecalis [8 – 10].

C. jejuni is a Gram-negative, microaerophilic, highly motile curved rod bacterium with characteristic bipolar flagella and is the leading cause of bacterial gastroenteritis, primarily due to its association with contaminated poultry products [11]. Genomic analyses of C. jejuni have revealed a pathogen uniquely adapted to survival in complex environments. Unlike many bacteria, C. jejuni lacks an extensive number of genes for carbohydrate or amino acid degradation but compensates with an abundance of transport systems, enabling it to scavenge essential nutrients from its surroundings [12]. This metabolic reliance on external amino acids, citric acid cycle intermediates, and monosaccharides highlights its dependency on nutrient-rich environments and interactions with other microorganisms, thereby positioning C. jejuni as a highly specialized gastrointestinal pathogen [13]. Its pathogenicity is associated with biofilm formation, which aids its survival and transmission in food chains [14]. Although C. jejuni is a microaerobic bacterium, it is capable of forming biofilms under aerobic conditions, a trait that significantly contributes to its persistence in food-processing environments and along the food chain [15]. In complex biofilms, C. jejuni can benefit from metabolic by-products and protection provided by neighbouring species. Its transport systems aid nutrient uptake, while biofilm cohabitants buffer environmental stresses, making these mutualistic interactions vital in hostile or nutrient-poor conditions [16, 17].

Extensive research has also documented the capacity of lactic acid bacteria to inhibit C. jejuni. Neal-McKinney et al. (2012) [18] demonstrated that several Lactobacillus species, including L. acidophilus, L. crispatus, L. gallinarum and L. helveticus, inhibited C. jejuni growth in vitro and reduced intestinal colonisation in broiler chickens. Similarly, Wine et al. (2009) [19] showed that L. helveticus R0052 significantly reduced invasion of the well-characterised C. jejuni strains 81–176 and 11,168 in human intestinal epithelial cell lines.

Despite the extensive characterisation of Lactobacillus–C. jejuni interactions, corresponding data for L. cremoris remain scarce. Given that L. cremoris is ubiquitous in fermented dairy products consumed worldwide, and that C. jejuni is frequently associated with poultry, understanding their potential interactions is of considerable relevance for food safety.

The aim of this study was to investigate the effects of the transient bacterium with probiotic properties L. cremoris on foodborne pathogen C. jejuni at key links in its spread cycle such as infection of new hosts and persistence in the food-processing environments through 48 h old mature biofilm (hereafter referred to as biofilm; [20]) using both phenotypic and molecular approaches. We first examined how the presence and inoculation sequence of L. cremoris affect C. jejuni adhesion to and invasion of human colon adenocarcinoma Caco-2 cells, i.e. key traits associated with virulence. Next, we assessed the effects of L. cremoris on the culturability of C. jejuni in dual-species biofilms using colony-forming unit (CFU) counts. Finally, we analysed the proteomic profile of C. jejuni cells from single- and dual-species biofilms using label-free quantification (LFQ) mass spectrometry (MS) to identify C. jejuni biofilm-associated adaptations when co-cultured with L. cremoris.

Methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

C. jejuni NCTC 11,168 and L. cremoris NZ9000 (which does not produce bacteriocin nisin) were stored at − 80 °C in 20% glycerol (Kemika, Croatia) and 80% Mueller Hinton (MH) broth (Oxoid, UK). C. jejuni was grown on Karmali agar (Oxoid, UK) supplemented with a selective additive (SR0167E, Oxoid, UK) at 42 °C for 24 h under a microaerobic atmosphere (87% N₂, 10% CO₂, 3% O₂). Then cultures were transferred to MH agar and incubated again under identical atmospheric conditions for 24 h at 42 °C. L. cremoris was grown on MH agar for 24 h at 37 °C under a microaerobic atmosphere. Bacterial concentrations were prepared by resuspending the biomass from the agar plates in MH broth and measured with a spectrophotometer (Perkin Elmer, USA) at 600 nm, with an optical density of 0.1 corresponding to an approximate cell density of 10⁷ cells/mL. A 100-fold dilution was prepared, and 0.1 mL of each culture was used.

Caco-2 cell cultures and C. jejuni adhesion and invasion assay

Caco-2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), supplemented with 5% (v/v) foetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA), 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (all from Merck, Germany) as previously described [21]. Cells were routinely passaged at approximately 80% confluency (after 3–4 days). Following trypsinisation, cells were counted using a haemocytometer and resuspended to a concentration of 4 × 10⁵ cells/mL. A volume of 100 µL of the cell suspension was seeded into each well of a 96-well tissue culture plate, resulting in a final density of 4 × 10⁴ Caco-2 cells per well.

Plates were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ to allow the formation of confluent monolayers, which was confirmed by light microscopy. Prior to bacterial inoculation, culture medium was replaced and monolayers were washed three times with fresh antibiotic-free DMEM.

Bacterial inocula were prepared to achieve a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1:100 (Caco-2 cells: bacteria), corresponding to a bacterial concentration of 4 × 10⁷ cells/mL. For co-incubation experiments, C. jejuni and L. cremoris were applied at a 1:1 ratio. Three treatments were applied. In Treatment I, bacterial inocula (1 mL) in DMEM without antibiotics were added simultaneously to Caco-2 monolayers. In Treatment II, Caco-2 monolayers were first inoculated with C. jejuni and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ to allow early adhesion, after which L. cremoris was added and incubation continued for an additional 2 h. Treatment III mirrored Treatment II, except that L. cremoris was added first, followed by the addition of C. jejuni and further incubation. All treatments were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂, a condition commonly used for Caco-2 infection models and compatible with C. jejuni adhesion and invasion [22–24]. After incubation, Caco-2 monolayers were washed twice with DMEM to remove non-adherent bacteria.

To determine the total number of adherent and intracellular C. jejuni cells, monolayers were lysed using Triton X-100, and bacteria were quantified by plating serial dilutions on Karmali selective agar, as described in Sect. "Quantification of culturable cells in biofilms using the CFU method". To quantify intracellular bacteria, an additional gentamicin protection step was included: after washing, Caco-2 monolayers were incubated with DMEM containing 100 µg/mL gentamicin (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) for 1 h to eliminate extracellular bacteria, followed by washing, cell lysis, and bacterial enumeration as described above. The number of adherent C. jejuni cells was calculated by subtracting the number of intracellular bacteria from the total. All adhesion and invasion experiments were performed in biological and technical triplicates.

Quantification of culturable cells in biofilms using the CFU method

The experiment followed the protocol described by Klančnik et al. (2017) [25]. Nunc 96-well polystyrene microtiter plates (Thermo Fisher, USA) were used as an abiotic model surface. For the formation of both single- and dual-species (1:1 ratio) biofilms, selected bacterial inocula were prepared in MH broth (Oxoid, UK) and added to each well to a final volume of 200 µL and incubated at 37 °C under microaerobic conditions for 24–48 h. The pH of the spent medium was measured (Mettler Toledo, USA), and then the spent medium was discarded. Biofilms were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove non-adherent cells, while adherent cells were removed by sonication from the surface and were resuspended in fresh PBS. Serial 10-fold dilutions were then prepared, from which aliquots (10 µL) were plated onto Karmali agar with selective supplements for C. jejuni quantification and GM17 for L. cremoris quantification. Plates were incubated under optimal conditions for each bacterium for 24 h, after which colonies were counted, and CFU/mL was calculated. Results were reported as mean log₁₀ CFU/mL ± standard deviation (SD), based on three independent biological and technical replicates.

Proteomic analysis

Biomass preparation

After 48 h of incubation at 37 °C under microaerobic conditions on MH agar plates, biofilms were washed with PBS (Oxoid, UK) and carefully scraped from the agar surface using an inoculation loop (Golias, Slovenia). The collected biomass was then centrifuged twice at 5,000 g for 5 min, with supernatant removal and the addition of fresh PBS between centrifugations. The resulting pellet was stored at − 80 °C for later proteomic analysis.

Protein extraction, purification, and quantification

Protein extraction was conducted as described by Ramić et al. (2023). In short, biomass samples were resuspended in lysis buffer (7 M urea, 2 M thiourea, 5 mM dithiothreitol, and 2% CHAPS) and then exposed to three cycles of rapid freezing in liquid nitrogen and thawing. Sterilized zirconia glass beads (Carl Roth, Germany) were then added, and mechanical homogenization was performed using Bullet Blender (Next Advance, USA). Following centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris, the resulting supernatant was collected, protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad, USA), and 25 µg of protein per sample was transferred to Nanosep microcentrifuges with 30 kDa Omega filters (Pall). The filters were used for buffer exchange, removal of low‑molecular‑weight contaminants (salts, detergents, lysis reagents), and concentration of the total protein sample prior to enzymatic digestion. Protein digestion was performed with Lys-C (1:100, w/w) for 2 h at 37 °C, followed by dilution with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate to decrease urea concentration to 1 M. Subsequent trypsin digestion (1:25, w/w) was conducted for 16 h at 37 °C. Proteolysis was terminated by adding 20% acetonitrile and 10% formic acid.

Lysed peptides were desalted on a C-18 column, eluted using 2 × 50 µL of 0.1% formic acid in 60% acetonitrile, and concentrated to 20 µL. Peptides were separated by nano liquid chromatography (Thermo Scientific Vanquish Neo) coupled in line with a mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific Exploris 480) equipped with an electrospray source (Thermo Scientific NanoSpray Flex NG ion source). Peptides were trapped onto a precolumn (Thermo Scientific µPAC Trapping Column) using solvent A (0.1% formic acid, MS grade, Fischer Scientific). Peptides were separated on an analytical column (Thermo Scientific µPAC NEO HPLC Column, 50 cm NanoLC) using a 75 min linear gradient of 0–45% of solvent B (0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile, MS grade, Fischer Scientific) at a flow rate of 500 nL/min. The raw data were acquired on a mass spectrometer in data-dependent acquisition mode. Full-scan MS spectra were acquired on the Orbitrap (scan range of 300–1800 m/z, resolution of 90,000, automatic gain control target of 3 × 106, and maximum injection time of 25 ms). The most intense peaks were selected for higher-energy collision dissociation fragmentation for a total cycle time of 1.5 s at 30% normalized collision energy. Higher-energy collision dissociation spectra were acquired on the Orbitrap at a resolution of 15,000, automatic gain control target of 2 × 105, and maximum injection time set to automatic. Charge inclusion was selected for ions with charge states of 2–6. The dynamic exclusion was set to automatic.

Tandem mass spectra were searched using MaxQuant (version 2.5.0.0) against a UniProt-reviewed C. jejuni database containing 1,625 proteins and common contaminants (database accessed on 28 February 2025). During database searches, it was considered that cysteines are fully carbamidomethylated (+ 57,0215, statically added), methionines are fully oxidized (+ 15,9949, dynamically added), and all N-terminal residues and lysines are acetylated (+ 42,0106, dynamically added). Two missed cleavages were permitted. Peptide mass tolerance was set at 50 ppm and 0.02 Da on the precursor and fragment ions, respectively. Protein identification was filtered at a false discovery rate of < 1% and were accepted when supported by at least two peptides in total (razor + unique peptides), with unique peptides mapping exclusively to a single protein and razor peptides assigned according to the MaxQuant protein grouping algorithm. Quantification was performed using LFQ with default settings and the “match between runs” feature enabled.

The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE [27] partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD069146.

Quantitative proteomics and statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted on log2-transformed LFQ intensity values from three biological replicates. A Student’s t-test was performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 365). For proteins not detected across all replicates, missing values were imputed from a Gaussian distribution centred around the lowest observed intensity using the Perseus software (Cox Lab, Germany). Obtained p values were ranked and evaluated for a false discovery rate with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Proteins with a q value of ≤ 0.05 and an absolute log₂ fold change of > |0.57| were considered statistically significant [28]. Cytoscape 3.10.3 (The Cytoscape Consortium, USA) was used for visual representation of constructed networks of differentially expressed proteins, which were searched in a STRING database for C. jejuni subsp. jejuni NCTC 11,168 = ATCC 700,819 organism with a 0.8 confidence (score) cutoff and a maximum of 10 additional interactors. Afterwards, the network was clustered using the Markov Cluster Algorithm (MCL) (value of four) implemented in the Cytoscape plugin clusterMaker to determine clusters and functional interactions. For each cluster, the built-in functional enrichment was used to obtain enriched terms of molecular and biological functions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and graphical representation were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.5 (GraphPad Software, USA). To assess changes in the numbers of culturable bacterial cells, the mean logarithmic values of cells in dual-species biofilms were compared to those in single-species biofilms using one-way ANOVA with the Brown-Forsythe test for unequal variances was followed by Dunnett’s T3 post hoc test comparing each dual‑species condition to the corresponding single‑species control. This same method was applied to evaluate the number of adhered and invaded C. jejuni cells in the Caco-2 cell assay. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

L. cremoris modulates C. jejuni adhesion to and invasion of Caco-2 cells

We assessed the effects of L. cremoris on C. jejuni adhesion to and invasion of Caco-2 cells, a commonly used in vitro model for studying Campylobacter-host interactions [21, 29]. In addition to simultaneous inoculation of both bacteria, we further examined whether a 1 h pre-incubation of either bacterium prior to the addition of the other would substantially alter C. jejuni adhesion to and invasion of Caco-2 cells.

First, the adhesive and invasive capacities of L. cremoris and C. jejuni were assessed. Single-species and simultaneous dual-species inoculations were carried out for 2 h of incubation with Caco-2 cells. Compared to C. jejuni, L. cremoris showed a higher number of adherent cells but no invasive ability, as expected (Fig. 1; Suppl. Table 1). In the dual-species inoculations, only C. jejuni was quantified as it was the target of this study (Fig. 1; Suppl. Table 1). C. jejuni adhesion was decreased by L. cremoris, both when L. cremoris was simultaneously inoculated with C. jejuni and after L. cremoris was pre-incubated with Caco-2 cells for additional 1 h prior to C. jejuni inoculation. Conversely, C. jejuni adhesion was not altered after C. jejuni was pre-incubated for additional 1 h prior to L. cremoris inoculation. These results regarding adhesion are similar to those regarding invasion. The simultaneous inoculation of both bacteria decreased C. jejuni intracellular numbers by 43%. However, C. jejuni pre-incubation with Caco-2 cells for additional 1 h prior to L. cremoris inoculation gave C. jejuni time to significantly invade epithelial cells by more than 97% compared to 2 h single-species inoculation. Conversely, L. cremoris pre-incubation for additional 1 h before C. jejuni inoculation significantly decreased C. jejuni invasion by 92%, compared to the 2 h single-species control.

These results demonstrate that the sequence of bacterial inoculation affects both adhesion and invasion. Specifically, adding L. cremoris prior to C. jejuni greatly decreases C. jejuni adhesion to and invasion of Caco-2 cells. By contrast, adding C. jejuni additional 1 h prior to L. cremoris enhances the invasive ability of C. jejuni but does not affect the total number of adherent cells.

No significant differences between the CFU/mL counts of L. cremoris cells in single- and dual-species biofilms were observed. This indicates that the presence of C. jejuni does not affect the biofilm-forming capacity of L. cremoris under the tested microaerobic conditions (Fig. 2; Suppl. Table 2).

However, the numbers of culturable C. jejuni biofilm cells were significantly decreased (by 88%) in dual-species biofilms compared to single-species biofilms. This indicates that co-culture with L. cremoris inhibits C. jejuni culturability under the tested environmental conditions. The initial pH of the MH broth was 7.19 ± 0.03. After incubation, the pH of the dual-species supernatant decreased to 6.75 ± 0.03, which was slightly, but not significantly, lower than the pH of the C. jejuni single-species supernatant (6.84 ± 0.02). By contrast, the single-species L. cremoris supernatant exhibited a significantly lower pH of 6.49 ± 0.02.

Proteomic profiling of C. jejuni in single- and dual-species biofilms

To explore the molecular adaptations of C. jejuni during interspecies interactions, we performed LFQ MS analysis of the expressed proteome of single- and dual-species biofilms. Differentially expressed proteins were categorized into functional groups using the Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) classification system to identify shifts in metabolic and cellular processes of dual-species C. jejuni biofilms. In addition, we conducted functional enrichment analyses to uncover biological processes modulated in C. jejuni in response to co-culture with L. cremoris.

Functional categorization of differentially expressed C. jejuni proteins based on COG analysis

In this study, we compared the proteomic profiles of C. jejuni from single- and dual-species biofilms (co-cultured with L. cremoris) after 48 h of growth to identify protein-level changes associated with interspecies interactions. A total of 79 C. jejuni proteins were differentially expressed in the dual-species biofilms, of which 76 were upregulated and only 3 were downregulated, relative to the single-species C. jejuni biofilms (absolute log₂ fold change > |0.57|; q value < 0.05; Suppl. Table 3).

Proteomic analysis of C. jejuni in dual-species biofilms compared to single-species biofilms revealed 88 differentially expressed protein functions (Fig. 3, Suppl. Table 4). These were classified according to their COG functional classification, indicating broad effects of L. cremoris co-culture on various biological processes. Importantly, most of these functional shifts were associated with upregulated proteins, while only a small subset of functions corresponded to downregulated proteins. This indicates that C. jejuni primarily responds to the dual-species environment through enhanced activity of cellular functions (Fig. 3).

The most represented category of differentially expressed proteins was energy production and conversion (COG C), comprising 20% of the upregulated proteins. This increase likely reflects elevated energy demands required to support the other upregulated pathways, including the transport and metabolism of amino acids (COG E, 13%) and nucleotides (COG F, 7%). These changes suggest enhanced biosynthetic and repair activities of C. jejuni that may support adaptation to the conditions of dual-species biofilms. Such broad metabolic adjustments are further indicated by the upregulated transport and metabolism of carbohydrates (COG G, 5%), coenzymes (COG H, 6%), and lipids (COG I, 5%). Moreover, increased posttranslational modification and protein turnover (COG O, 8%) implies a stress response, and changes in cell wall biogenesis (COG M, 5%) and signal transduction (COG T, 6%) suggest modifications in intercellular communication.

By contrast, downregulation was limited to energy production and conversion (COG C, 1%), transcription (COG K, 1%), and signal transduction mechanisms (COG T, 1%). These limited downregulations reinforce the overall trend of upregulation and metabolic activation of C. jejuni in dual-species biofilms.

Cytoscape-based functional grouping and enrichment analysis of differentially expressed C. jejuni proteins from dual-species biofilms

For a more detailed analysis, we used Cytoscape [30] in combination with the MCL algorithm to organize differentially expressed proteins into functional interaction clusters. Using a high-confidence STRING interaction cut-off score of 0.8, we identified seven densely connected protein clusters with elevated mutual interaction scores compared to the rest of the network (Fig. 4). Each cluster was analysed for enriched molecular and biological functions using the integrated functional enrichment tool of Cytoscape (Table 1).

Discussion

The experimental approaches employed in this study were designed to capture distinct but biologically relevant ecological niches of C. jejuni. We aimed to define the effects of the transient bacterium with probiotic properties L. cremoris on key links in C. jejuni’s spread cycle such as persistence in the food-processing environments through biofilms, and infection of new hosts through Caco-2 assay. Proteomic analyses were therefore performed on biofilm cells to characterise adaptive responses linked to persistence and survival rather than epithelial invasion. Although as stated, both represent distinct biological processes, several physiological traits, including motility, adhesion, chemotaxis and stress adaptation, contribute to both phenotypes. Previous studies have reported associations between biofilm formation capacity and virulence-related traits in Campylobacter spp.; however, these relationships are strain-dependent [33, 34]. In this study, adhesion and invasion assays therefore show early host-pathogen interactions, while biofilm formation reflects adaptive responses linked to environmental persistence. Accordingly, the proteomic profiles obtained under biofilm-forming conditions should not be interpreted as direct determinants of epithelial adhesion or invasion but rather as reflections of metabolic and stress-related adaptations associated with biofilm persistence.

Our findings clearly demonstrate that L. cremoris modulates key virulence traits of C. jejuni, specifically adhesion to and invasion of Caco-2 cells. Notably, simultaneous inoculation of both species significantly decreased C. jejuni adhesion and invasion, suggesting interference with early host-pathogen interactions. These results are consistent with previous reports indicating that beneficial or commensal bacteria can limit pathogen colonisation through competitive exclusion, niche displacement, and disruption of the attachment to host cell receptors [35].

Notably, nearly 1 log₁₀ more L. cremoris cells than C. jejuni cells adhered to Caco-2 cells. This is in agreement with the observed decrease in adherent C. jejuni cells after the simultaneous inoculation of both bacteria. The sequence of inoculation also played a key role. Preincubating Caco-2 cells with L. cremoris significantly decreased C. jejuni adhesion and invasion. This suggests that L. cremoris may interfere with C. jejuni adhesion by occupying critical binding sites on the epithelial surface. However, potential effects on epithelial barrier integrity, such as modulation of tight junctions or transepithelial resistance, cannot be assessed using the gentamicin assay applied in this study. By contrast, pre-incubation with C. jejuni significantly increased its invasion, most likely because of the additional time available for adhesion, invasion, or modulation of the epithelial surface. The observed effects of L. cremoris should be interpreted within the context of an in vitro model. While the findings suggest a potential role for L. cremoris as a transient competitor limiting C. jejuni exposure, confirmation of probiotic efficacy and safety would require in vivo validation in appropriate host models.

The pronounced decrease (88%) in C. jejuni culturability in dual-species biofilms suggests that L. cremoris alters the microenvironment of C. jejuni. Probiotic strains are known to secrete various substances, such as lactic acid, diacetyl, acetaldehyde, acetate, hydrogen peroxide, and bacteriocins. These substances can disrupt the biofilm matrix and decrease the culturability of pathogens [36–38]. However, we did not directly quantify secreted antagonistic factors under our experimental conditions, therefore mechanistic explanations remain hypothetical and should be addressed in follow-up work. Certain probiotics may inadvertently support the growth of pathogenic bacteria in the gut by providing essential carbon sources (e.g. lactic acid) or alternative precursor molecules (e.g. diacetyl), which can be utilised for riboflavin production and thereby enable iron uptake [39, 40]. The pH of the dual-species supernatant was only marginally lower than that of C. jejuni, indicating that acidification alone does not account for the observed decrease in C. jejuni culturability. To further investigate the underlying mechanisms, we performed a comprehensive proteomic analysis, aiming to uncover specific factors responsible for this antagonistic interaction.

Proteomic clustering revealed a strong enrichment of proteins involved in central carbon metabolism, energy production and stress response, suggesting metabolic adaptation of C. jejuni in dual-species biofilms. The most significant proteomic changes were observed in energy production and conversion, comprising 20% of the upregulated proteins. Notably, several critical tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) enzymes were upregulated, including FumC, AcnB, IcdM, and SucD, indicating a fully engaged and efficient respiratory chain, optimized for energy extraction from available resources. The upregulation of AcnB could also be a direct response to acidic stress imposed by L. cremoris, as AcnB increases acid consumption by the TCA cycle [41]. The upregulation of SdhABC links the TCA cycle with respiratory metabolism, as it is the only enzyme that participates in the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation.

Parallel to these aerobic enzymes, components of hydrogen metabolism were also upregulated. Ni-Fe hydrogenase (HydA, HydB) enables the utilization of molecular hydrogen as an electron donor. Hydrogen can serve as an abundant source of electrons, supporting energy metabolism and growth of C. jejuni in low-oxygen, nutrient-limited environments [42]. Beyond its metabolic role, hydrogenase activity appears to confer respiratory protection to enzymes that are sensitive to reactive oxygen species, helping C. jejuni tolerate oxidative stress [43]. Our results showed a 28.9-fold increase in hydrogenase in dual-species biofilms, indicating its crucial role. Importantly, the deactivation of hydrogenase has been associated with a significant reduction in the bacterium’s ability to adhere to and invade both human and avian intestinal cells [44, 45].

The highest fold change in protein levels was observed for the tungstate-binding protein TupA, with a 32.1 log₂-fold increase. TupA functions as the periplasmic component of a high-affinity ATP-binding cassette transporter system that is essential for tungstate uptake. This enables C. jejuni to incorporate tungsten into periplasmic formate dehydrogenase [46], which is a major energy-generating enzyme. It is particularly important under low-oxygen conditions, in which formate is available as a fermentation by-product from surrounding microbes or host environments [47]. Kassem et al. (2012) [45] showed that mutants lacking formate dehydrogenase activity have diminished motility, biofilm formation, oxidative stress resistance, and colonisation ability.

S-ribosylhomocysteine lyase (LuxS) was the second most expressed protein, with a 31.0 log2-fold increase. Elvers and Park (2002) [48] linked it to quorum sensing through its role in the production of autoinducer-2 (AI-2), a signalling molecule involved in interspecies bacterial communication and biofilm formation. However, recent studies challenge its role as a true quorum sensing regulator. Ramić et al. (2022, 2023) [26, 49] demonstrated that AI-2 levels correlate linearly with bacterial cell concentration, suggesting its production of AI-2 is a passive by-product of metabolic activity rather than a quorum sensing mechanism. These findings shift the focus of LuxS from quorum sensing to its essential metabolic function of homocysteine production and methyl recycling in bacteria. This activity is crucial for maintaining intracellular methionine and S-adenosylmethionine pools, which fuel essential methylation reactions and support virulence and host colonisation [50]. This emphasizes the need for more nuanced interpretations of its role in C. jejuni under nutrient-limited and competitive conditions such as complex biofilms. Accordingly, LuxS upregulation in our dual-species biofilm is interpreted mainly as increased demand for methyl recycling under nutrient-limited and competitive conditions, rather than as evidence for LuxS driven interspecies signalling.

Due to its complex nutritional requirements and limited metabolic capabilities, C. jejuni relies heavily on scavenging amino and keto acids from its environment, e.g. the host, neighbouring gut microbes, or the biofilm matrix itself [51]. These compounds feed directly into the central metabolism of C. jejuni, and their utilization is critical for survival. Consistent with this, several proteins involved in amino acid biosynthesis and uptake were found to be upregulated in this study.

Proteins related to the acquisition and metabolism of aspartate (AspA, AspB), serine (SdaA, GlyA), and glycine (GlyA), and the conversion of glutamate to glutamine (GlnA) were also upregulated. These amino acids are abundant in MH broth [52] and are known to promote growth [53], as they can enter the TCA cycle after deamination [54]. Components of the LIV transporter system, such as LivK (responsible for the uptake of branched-chain amino acids, particularly leucine) and other related transporters (such as IlvE) were also upregulated. Together, these findings indicate that C. jejuni adopts a metabolically flexible, nutrient-scavenging strategy to survive in dual-species biofilms.

In addition to metabolic adjustments, a significant upregulation of proteins involved in protein folding, repair, and degradation was determined. Proteins that were most upregulated were associated with oxidative stress defence and included HtrA (a periplasmic chaperone-protease; [55, 56]), Dps (an iron-binding protein that prevents the generation of hydroxyl radicals via the Fenton reaction; [57]), Rrc (Rubrerythrin-like protein as hydrogen peroxide reductase; [58]), MdaB (a quinone reductase; [59]), and an essential global response regulator CosR (a OmpR-type response regulator [60]). that has been implicated in the control of oxidative stress defence, energy metabolism and motility, suggesting a broader regulatory adaptation under biofilm-associated conditions [15, 60, 61].

The upregulation of the chaperone trigger factor (Tig) and heat-shock proteins DnaK and HtpG shows the need for proper folding of newly synthesised or stress-denatured proteins and proteolysis of irreversibly misfolded proteins [62]. The upregulation of TrxA (a thioredoxin involved in the oxidative stress response and acid stress) and the hypothetical protein Cj0706 [31] may reflect the antagonistic influence of L. cremoris. The periplasmic protein p19 (iron transporter) was also upregulated, and iron acquisition is tightly linked to the resistance of C. jejuni to oxidative stress [63]. Furthermore, the upregulation of motility and chemotaxis proteins (e.g. FlgL, CheA, CheY, Tar) indicates a response related to nutrient scavenging, avoidance of negative stimuli, and organised biofilm formation [64].

Only a few downregulated proteins were identified, which, however, play an integral role in cellular processes such as transcription (RpoC) and chemotaxis (Cj0262c). Our findings are in line with those of Reid et al. (2008), who reported a similar transcriptomic response in C. jejuni NCTC 11,168 under acidic stress conditions.

Our proteomic analysis highlights a metabolically active state of C. jejuni during co-culture with L. cremoris. The upregulation of proteins involved in energy production, amino acid transport, and nucleotide metabolism reflects an adaptive strategy to survive in a competitive dual-species biofilm environment [65]. The upregulation of proteins involved in stress responses (acidic and oxidative), chemotaxis, flagellar assembly and posttranslational modifications further reflects an intensified need for environmental sensing and adaptation under the competitive conditions of dual-species biofilms. Despite these versatile adaptations, the significant decrease in culturability suggests that these responses are not sufficient to preserve C. jejuni culturability within the dual-species biofilm.

Conclusions

The observed reduction in C. jejuni adhesion and invasion suggests that L. cremoris may contribute to limiting early host exposure when present prior to pathogen contact, consistent with the concept of competitive exclusion. In parallel, the pronounced reduction in C. jejuni cultivability in dual-species biofilms indicates a potential role in limiting pathogen persistence in complex microbial communities. These findings support the notion that L. cremoris could be relevant both as a transient bacterium with probiotic properties and as a protective competitor in biofilm-associated environments, such as food-processing surfaces. The observed effects of L. cremoris should be interpreted within the context of an in vitro model. While the findings suggest a potential role for L. cremoris as a transient competitor limiting C. jejuni exposure, confirmation of probiotic efficacy and safety would require in vivo validation in appropriate host models.

Proteomic analysis revealed that C. jejuni upregulates its transport and acquisition of essential nutrients, chemotaxis, flagellar assembly, and acidic and oxidative stress responses under competitive conditions with L. cremoris in biofilm. These changes are indicative of metabolic and physiological adaptation under competitive biofilm-associated conditions rather than direct determinants of pathogenicity.
