Development and validation of an affordable freehand scanning 3D ultrasound reconstruction system with visual positioning
Authors: Jiaojiao Ma, Zuxi Huang, Liangkai Wang, Xuejiao Yu, Yanghua Zhang, Heng Xie, Bo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThree-dimensional (3D) ultrasound provides more intuitive, detailed, and comprehensive diagnostic information. The primary objective of this study was to develop affordable, lightweight, and wide-field 3D ultrasound probes which is a critical challenge for expanding the accessibility and utility of this technology.MethodsWe describe an affordable freehand scanning 3D ultrasound reconstruction system. The system utilizes a QR code film attached to the skin surface for positioning. During each ultrasound scan, a lightweight camera fixed on the probe records the QR codes within the scanning area. Based on QR code features, a skin map is constructed, and ultrasound images and film image information are preprocessed. The calibration relationship between the ultrasound probe and camera field of view is determined. Finally, comprehensive algorithms such as ORB-SLAM positioning are used for three-dimensional image reconstruction.ResultsThrough hand-scanning tests, the results show that the scanning speed is 15 mm/s, and when the image acquisition frame rates are 10 Fps/s, 20 Fps/s, and 30 Fps/s, the maximum cumulative error of ORB-SLAM algorithm positioning does not exceed 2%, meeting the positioning requirements of the intelligent skin texture image reconstruction system for thyroid ultrasound. Additionally, the effectiveness of three-dimensional reconstruction is verified through model volume testing.ConclusionThe intelligent skin texture image reconstruction system for ultrasound can achieve 3D image reconstruction, displaying lesion features and the spatial positions of surrounding tissues in three-dimensional space, thereby assisting ultrasound doctors in making accurate and rapid diagnoses.Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Introduction

Three-dimensional (3D) ultrasound surpasses two-dimensional (2D) imaging with its comprehensive view and spatial understanding, enhancing diagnosis by revealing subtle abnormalities and enabling precise quantitative analysis. Its data powers virtual reality and 3D printing for advanced applications, and it engages patients with a more intuitive understanding of their anatomy [1]. Furthermore, AI and machine learning can leverage 3D ultrasound data for automated tasks, boosting efficiency and accuracy [2]. Despite the advantages of 3D ultrasound, current probes are hindered by high costs, bulky designs, and limited field of view, necessitating further research for more accessible and effective solutions. Creating affordable, lightweight, and wide-field 3D ultrasound probes is a critical challenge for expanding the accessibility and utility of this technology [3]. This requires a multidisciplinary approach involving advancements in materials, manufacturing, transducer technology, and software algorithms. By addressing these challenges, we can unlock the full potential of 3D ultrasound, transforming it into a powerful tool for healthcare professionals worldwide.

Ultrasound probes are indispensable for freehand 3D ultrasound imaging and image-guided interventions. Typically, optical or electromagnetic (EM) trackers are utilized to track the probe’s freehand motion with real-time sub-millimeter accuracy. Nonetheless, these tracking methods often necessitate costly and cumbersome equipment. Moreover, optical trackers demand a direct line of sight between the optical system and markers on the probe, while EM trackers are susceptible to interference from ferromagnetic materials, presenting challenges for their clinical adoption. In pursuit of more cost-effective alternatives, several low-cost probe localization techniques have been explored, occasionally incorporating inertial sensing technology. For instance, structured light has been employed to estimate the probe’s orientation on the skin’s surface [4]. Furthermore, systems reminiscent of optical mice have been introduced, which track skin features from a short distance to ascertain the probe’s two-dimensional translation along the skin’s surface [5]. Another method involves probe tracking by affixing a specialized strip with high-contrast markers to the scanning area, a technique well-suited for pre-determined scanning paths [6]. As the probe’s position is determined in relation to the patient’s body, these methods exhibit robustness against rigid patient movements. A lightweight camera mounted on the probe captures skin features while acquiring two-dimensional ultrasound scans. Utilizing the skin feature video, a skin map is established, and the probe’s pose corresponding to each camera frame is estimated.

In this paper, we expand upon our previous work in two aspects. Firstly, we design a specific thin film material to achieve skin surface image reconstruction based on a thin film attachment method. This film material, serving as standard information for optical pattern recognition, should possess image features that can clearly distinguish the XY coordinate direction of the probe movement (Fig. 1). This ensures positioning and differentiation of the acquired local images, thereby guaranteeing the accuracy of the reconstructed images. Prior to ultrasound scanning, the film is simply affixed smoothly to the designated area corresponding to the ultrasound scanning region, allowing for normal ultrasound scanning of the examination site. During this process, both two-dimensional ultrasound images and local images of the film are obtained simultaneously. Secondly, after image acquisition, the ultrasound images and film image information are preprocessed, and the calibration relationship between the ultrasound probe and the camera field of view is determined. Finally, comprehensive algorithms such as ORB-SLAM localization are used for three-dimensional image reconstruction. The system performance is evaluated through tests such as freehand scanning and model volume measurements.Fig. 1Ultrasound probe coordinate system definition. Backend Server:

The volume rendering platform based on 3D slicer receives the DICOM files processed by the server to form a 3D model.

Key Workflow:

The physician scans the target area with the probe-camera device, while the QR code film provides real-time positional reference.

The ultrasound image and optical data are transmitted to the backend server for SLAM-based positioning, and the positioning information and ultrasound image are packaged into a standard DICOM format file.

The 3D reconstruction software stitches 2D slices into a volumetric model, enabling multi-planar visualization and quantitative analysis.

QR code film design

We printed the QR code on a polypropylene film, which has good softness and can fit well with human skin without affecting the attenuation of ultrasound. To maximize ORB feature extraction robustness, the QR code film (Fig. 3B) incorporated:

Physical dimensions (1.4 mm/pixel): Optimized through iterative testing—smaller codes ( < 1.2 mm) generated insufficient features under the camera’s resolution, while larger ones ( > 2.0 mm) reduced the effective field of view and feature density. The 1.4 mm design achieved optimal balance, yielding 800 stable features per frame for precise SLAM operation (Fig. 4). High-contrast textures patterns: Geometric elements (e.g., checkerboard grids) with sharp edges and corners were embedded to generate strong ORB feature points. These patterns enhanced detection stability under varying lighting conditions [7].

Camera calibration

This study employs a checkerboard-based Zhang’s calibration method for high-precision binocular system calibration [10]. The workflow comprises five key steps:

Data Acquisition: A 9 × 6 checkerboard pattern was captured in 15 stereo image pairs (Fig. 6) at varied poses (translation/rotation/tilt) to ensure sufficient spatial coverage. The board was positioned to occupy the entire field of view with uniformly distributed orientations, enhancing parameter observability.Fig. 6Binocular Calibration Image Set. Corner Detection Refinement: A two-stage detection approach was implemented. Coarse detection: Initial corner localization via OpenCV’s findChessboardCorners. Sub-pixel refinement: Further optimized using cornerSubPix, achieving detection errors < 0.08 pixels.

Single camera calibration: Based on Zhang Zhengyou’s calibration method, the left and right cameras are calibrated with internal parameters. By minimizing the reprojection error, the camera internal parameter matrix, distortion coefficient and external parameter matrix are solved.

Binocular calibration: Combine the image corner data of the left and right cameras, optimize the relative position relationship of the binocular system through stereo calibration, calculate the rotation matrix R and translation vector t of the right camera relative to the left camera, and further optimize the stereo correction parameters.

Accuracy verification: Use the calibration results to perform stereo correction (Bouguet algorithm) on the image pair, check the epipolar alignment effect, and evaluate the calibration accuracy through the 3D reconstruction error. Finally, retain the calibration results with a reprojection root mean square error (RMSE) less than 0.5 pixels.

Feature extraction and matching

We utilized the ORB (Oriented FAST and Rotated BRIEF) feature detector and descriptor generation algorithm, which demonstrates excellent computational efficiency and robustness [11].

The FAST (Features from Accelerated Segment Test) algorithm was employed as a lightweight yet efficient corner detection method in our system. To ensure sufficient key point detection density in resolution-limited images, we adopted a relatively low threshold of 7. This empirical value was determined through systematic testing: while the conventional threshold of 20 failed to extract the required 800 feature points under our imaging conditions (leading to tracking failures), reducing the threshold effectively improved detection density.

For feature description, we implemented the BRIEF (Binary Robust Independent Elementary Features) algorithm, which generates compact binary feature vectors by comparing 256 predefined point pairs around each key point. Since BRIEF descriptors inherently lack rotation invariance, we incorporated the well-established intensity centroid method [12] to estimate key point orientation. A Gaussian image pyramid with 8 levels was implemented to enable multi-scale feature detection. Each pyramid level contains incrementally downsampled and Gaussian-filtered versions of the original image, effectively reducing noise while preserving structural information. The 8-level configuration represents a conventional choice in the field; while varying pyramid depth could marginally affect computational efficiency, our experiments demonstrated negligible impact on overall system performance.

The number of extracted features per frame was set to 800 after careful consideration of both computational constraints and application requirements. Our system operates at 30 fps with a probe scanning speed of 15 mm/s (consistent with conventional ultrasound examination speeds). This parameter represents an optimal balance between real-time processing (exceeding 800 features caused computational delays) and tracking robustness (fewer features increased tracking failure rates). Empirical testing confirmed that 800 features provided the best compromise for our operational conditions.

Visual mapping and pose estimation

We adopt the standard ORB-SLAM2 framework for real-time visual SLAM based on a binocular camera setup (baseline B = 5 cm, focal length f = 23 cm). A sparse 3D point cloud map is initialized during system startup using stereo images. Initial camera poses are estimated via the Perspective-n-Point (PnP) algorithm with RANSAC, based on matched feature points. As the system proceeds, camera motion is tracked through ORB feature detection and optical flow-based matching between frames.

To correct for accumulated drift over long scanning sessions, loop closure detection is enabled using the Bag-of-Words (BoW) method built into ORB-SLAM. The estimated camera trajectory provides the basis for ultrasound probe localization and 3D reconstruction of the scanned anatomy. For further algorithmic details and formulas, please refer to Appendix A.

Real-time processing:

To satisfy the need for real-time processing, a distributed approach was adopted. ORB-SLAM runs on a high-performance server, while image acquisition and preprocessing are conducted on the terminal device. Multi-threading techniques were used to optimize the algorithm’s execution efficiency.

Error control:

In the design and placement of the QR code, the size of the feature points and the feature textures were optimized to enhance feature point detection and matching. This improved the accuracy and robustness of the pose estimation.

Software system construction

The software system developed in this study is designed to enable real-time acquisition, synchronization, and 3D reconstruction of portable ultrasound images and optical tracking data. The system adopts a distributed front-end and back-end architecture and is divided into three core modules: (1) data acquisition and transmission, (2) image processing, and (3) 3D reconstruction and visualization. These modules operate in coordination via unified timestamps and network protocols, ensuring real-time performance and scalability across a variety of portable ultrasound imaging scenarios (Fig. 7).Fig. 7Data workflow diagram of the 3D ultrasound reconstruction system

Data workflow diagram of the 3D ultrasound reconstruction system

Data acquisition and transmission module

This module is implemented as an Android-based mobile application built upon the Philips Lumify portable ultrasound platform. Its primary function is to synchronously acquire ultrasound images and reference camera images, enabling spatiotemporal alignment of multimodal data. Key implementations include:

SDK Integration and Synchronization: By integrating the Philips Lumify SDK and camera SDK, frame-level synchronization is achieved at the image acquisition layer, with synchronization errors controlled within 10 milliseconds.

Low-latency Transmission: Image data is transmitted via local Wi-Fi or 5 G networks. The transmission latency is kept below 30 milliseconds, ensuring inter-frame temporal misalignment is within a single frame.

Data Packaging and Transmission: Acquired images are encoded using base64 and attached with a unified timestamp before being uploaded to the server. This enables precise synchronization and decoding at the processing end.

Image processing module

Deployed on the server side, this module is responsible for receiving uploaded data, estimating camera pose, and integrating spatial information with image content. It consists of the following components:

Data Decoding: Incoming data packets from the mobile device are decoded to extract raw ultrasound and camera images.

Pose Estimation: The ORB-SLAM algorithm is applied to the camera images to estimate the position for each frame through feature extraction and matching.

Data Integration and Standardization: The estimated pose is fused with the corresponding ultrasound frame to generate standardized medical imaging files in DICOM format. Custom private DICOM tags are used to embed SLAM-derived spatial coordinates into the file headers, enabling compatibility with standard PACS systems.

To ensure processing efficiency, a pipeline scheduling mechanism is employed to process each frame in real time—decoding, localization, and DICOM integration occur sequentially but continuously, minimizing system response time.

3D Reconstruction and visualization module

This module is developed based on the open-source medical imaging platform 3D Slicer, with added network interfacing capabilities for real-time DICOM input. Its main functionalities include:

Data Retrieval: The module continuously listens for incoming DICOM files from the server through a network interface.

3D Reconstruction and Slice Visualization: Using spatial information embedded in the DICOM metadata, the system reconstructs 3D ultrasound volumes from 2D image sequences. Users can simultaneously view both the 3D volume and corresponding 2D slices, improving diagnostic interpretability.

Fast visualization workflow: Through tight integration with the image processing module, the system supports real-time data transmission. After the scan is completed, the 3D model can be generated immediately without the need to transmit data again, providing fast feedback for clinical decision-making.

The QR code map is fixed on the base of the linear stage, while the stereo camera is rigidly mounted to the ultrasound probe using a custom bracket. The results show that the ORB-SLAM-based tracking algorithm can maintain sufficient localization accuracy and motion consistency under practical conditions. In particular, the higher frame rate reduces the inter-frame error and cumulative drift to a certain extent. These findings suggest that the system can meet the precision requirements for 2D-plane scanning scenarios such as thyroid skin surface texture reconstruction.

However, since the experimental setup was limited to planar motion on the XY axis, this study does not yet fully assess localization performance in the Z direction or under complex 3D trajectories. Future experiments with a more comprehensive 3D motion platform will be conducted to evaluate full 6-DoF pose accuracy and robustness in depth-varying scanning scenarios.

Discussion

The development of a portable, affordable, and wide-scan-range 3D ultrasound probe is critical to overcoming the current limitations of ultrasound technology and expanding its accessibility. Such devices can bring advanced imaging capabilities to underserved and remote areas where traditional ultrasound systems are difficult to implement [13]. This improvement has the potential to significantly enhance healthcare outcomes by enabling early detection and diagnosis of various diseases. A portable probe can be used at the bedside or in home settings, offering patients a more convenient and comfortable scanning experience, thereby reducing anxiety and improving overall satisfaction. A wide scanning range allows coverage of larger anatomical regions in a single pass, reducing the need for repeated scans and accelerating the diagnostic process. Furthermore, affordable 3D ultrasound solutions open new opportunities in telemedicine, point-of-care diagnostics, and emergency medicine, supporting the development of personalized healthcare and improving clinical efficiency [14].

The system presented in this study uses a binocular camera for visual tracking, leveraging stereo vision to obtain depth information and improve pose estimation accuracy. This approach enhances object detection, distance estimation, and 3D perception, which are essential in applications ranging from medical imaging to robotics. The success of this method, however, depends heavily on the robust detection and tracking of QR code features. It was observed that an inappropriate QR code size could negatively impact system performance—too small leads to poor resolution, while too large narrows the field of view. After calibration, a set of QR codes matching the camera parameters was selected to balance accuracy and system responsiveness.

The use of ORB-SLAM for real-time dense visual SLAM was motivated by its robustness, real-time performance, capability to generate dense maps, modularity, loop closure detection, support for multiple camera types, and widespread adoption in research. Experimental results confirmed that under scanning speeds of 15 mm/s and image acquisition frame rates of 10, 20, and 30 fps, the system maintained a maximum cumulative positioning error below 2%, which is acceptable for our thyroid ultrasound imaging application. Higher frame rates led to increased positioning accuracy, supporting more frequent pose updates and reducing accumulated error. This highlights the importance of choosing appropriate frame rates for optimal system performance in dynamic scanning tasks.

Both the fishbone model and the human thyroid model experiments demonstrated the effectiveness of the 3D reconstruction approach. However, certain limitations were observed. In the fishbone model, due to its surface material properties, ultrasound reflections led to noticeable image artifacts, particularly ghosting at boundary regions. Similarly, in the thyroid model experiment, the curvature of the neck surface introduced reconstruction deviations compared to the actual anatomical structure. Despite this, the system successfully performed volume measurements of embedded nodules, demonstrating a valuable clinical capability.

Nevertheless, several limitations of the current system remain. First, precision evaluation was only performed in the 2D plane; accuracy in the full 3D space has not yet been systematically assessed. Second, the ORB-SLAM module, while effective, still offers significant room for improvement, especially in map optimization and loop closure detection, which were not tested in this study. Third, the change in the probe’s posture during the scanning process will affect the positioning accuracy, the extent of which has not been evaluated. In terms of reconstruction, repeated scanning paths can cause image ghosting, and scanning speed strongly affects the reconstruction quality. These issues highlight the need for future research in trajectory optimization, motion compensation, and image post-processing.

Additionally, while the current system demonstrates high cost-effectiveness and robustness to rigid patient motion, further miniaturization and image quality enhancement are essential for broader clinical adoption. Future efforts should focus on developing a more compact, higher-resolution 3D ultrasound system with improved stability across different environments and compatibility with various probe types. These advancements will be crucial for enhancing the robustness, scalability, and clinical utility of portable 3D ultrasound solutions.

Conclusion

We have presented a highly cost-effective and miniature-mobile system for free-hand 3D ultrasound, which localizes the probe by tracking skin features and is therefore robust to rigid patient motion. Results from freehand scans on three body parts show that the system could potentially be an alternative to conventional tracking devices for freehand 3D ultrasound with a much lower cost and higher portability.
