Iatrogenic large coronary artery fistula treated with coronary artery stenting in an 8-year-old child: a case report
Authors: Sidra Yoosannaf, Masood Sadiq, Ahmad Usaid Qureshi, Muhammad Muzaffar Mahmood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIatrogenic coronary artery fistula, although a rare complication after cardiac surgery, may lead to haemodynamic instability and requires immediate recognition and management. Percutaneous coronary intervention despite its technical challenges is a viable intervention in selected paediatric cases.Case SummaryWe report a case of an 8-year-old child who had cardiac surgery for TOF with pulmonary atresia. The procedure was complicated by a large iatrogenic fistulous communication from left anterior descending artery to right ventricle, ventricular arrhythmias and low cardiac output state. He was salvaged with emergency percutaneous coronary intervention using a covered stent that was successfully deployed across the origin of the fistula, resulting in complete closure of the abnormal communication while preserving normal antegrade coronary flow.DiscussionCoronary artery stenting is a technical challenge in paediatric population owing to small calibre vessels. Introduction

Iatrogenic coronary artery fistulas (CAF) are well-recognized complication of chest trauma and invasive cardiac procedures.1 Majority of iatrogenic post-surgical CAF are small with no haemodynamic significance and close spontaneously.1 Large fistulas, though rare, require immediate revascularization to prevent myocardial infarction or ventricular arrhythmias. In adults, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) using a covered stent is an established treatment option for coronary artery perforation and fistulas.2 Coronary intervention in infants and young children using stents poses unique technical challenges limiting its use. Coronary stents carry short term procedural challenges due to small calibre (2 mm or less) of coronary vessels in infants and young children as well as long term risks of stent failure in a growing child.3 Despite such challenges, coronary artery stenting can be lifesaving, offering rapid restoration of coronary flow. We present an 8-year-old boy with an iatrogenic coronary laceration leading to CAF, after surgical correction for Tetralogy of Fallot with pulmonary atresia, treated with emergency PCI using covered stent. To our knowledge, this is the first instance of using a covered stent in a young child for iatrogenic coronary artery fistula to right ventricle during Tetralogy of Fallot with pulmonary atresia.

Case presentation

An 8-year-old boy with Tetralogy of Fallot, pulmonary atresia, single large outlet ventricular septal defect (VSD), balanced ventricles and a large patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) supplying good size confluent branch pulmonary arteries presented with worsening cyanosis and shortness of breath. He was diagnosed at 2 months of age but lost to follow-up owing to family migration to a different region. He had worsening cyanosis over the years, grade IV digital clubbing and required frequent squatting to catch his breath after minor physical activity. He was adequately built with height 117 cm (-1z), weight 18.5 kg (-1.8z) and surface area 0.78 m2. Detailed work-up reaffirmed atretic pulmonary valve, a small native main pulmonary artery (MPA), well-formed confluent branch pulmonary arteries, and discrete stenosis at left pulmonary artery (LPA) origin at site of PDA insertion which was arising from the under surface of a left aortic arch. As planned, he underwent semi-elective open-heart surgery where VSD was closed with a Dacron patch using interrupted pledgeted 5/0 Prolene sutures. Infundibular obstructive muscle bundles were resected, pulmonary annulus site excised and a competent valved right ventricle (RV) to MPA tract established using right atrial appendage (RAA). The MPA and LPA origin were augmented with an autologous fresh pericardial patch. Per-operative and immediate post-operative echocardiography revealed no residual VSD, a peak gradient of 12 mmHg across the RVOT and no pulmonary regurgitation.

On the first post-operative day, the patient deteriorated after attempted weaning from ventilatory support, developing frequent premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) followed by haemodynamic instability and features of low cardiac output syndrome. There was strain pattern on electrocardiogram septal as well as lateral leads with junctional ectopic tachycardia (Figure 1A). Mechanical ventilatory support was stepped up. A 2-D echocardiogram revealed interventricular septal stunning with borderline preserved biventricular systolic function. Colour doppler study showed a fistulous communication from proximal left anterior descending (LAD) artery to RV. Coronary steal was deemed significant due to strain pattern on ECG and considered the reason for developing PVCs. With a differential between congenital coronary artery fistula or iatrogenic coronary perforation making fistulous tract into RV cavity, an urgent coronary angiography was planned. Vascular access was achieved through right femoral artery with a 6F radial sheath. Patient was anticoagulated with 100 unit per kg unfractioned heparin and ACT was maintained around 230 s, lower than usual recommendations due to recent cardiac surgery. Selective LMCA was performed which showed dual LAD system with perforation of the lesser LAD (Short LAD) draining into RV (Figure 2A, Supplementary material online, Video S1). It was differentiated from simple perforation with no local staining or contrast collection in soft tissue. It was further differentiated from congenital coronary artery fistula by the absence of any fistulous tubular tract and drained directly into RV. The vessel mainly supplied septal perforators while the main LAD (Long LAD) showed a dominant supply. The rapid contrast extravasation into RV through fistula from lesser LAD was causing steal from main LAD.

Given the patient’s critical condition and small distal coronary artery calibre, bypass graft or direct surgical repair were impractical. An emergency PCI was planned after a detailed discussion and written consent by the parents. In absence of Intravascular Ultrasound (IVUS), the distal segment of the lesser LAD was wired using a Runthrough NS® (Terumo, Japan) coronary guidewire under fluoroscopic guidance. A contrast injection through the micro-catheter confirmed distal wire position in the true lumen. Initial balloon inflations did not stop the perforation. With ongoing significant coronary steal and haemodynamic instability, a 2.5 × 15 mm drug-eluting PK Papyrus® (Biotronik AG, Bülach, Switzerland) covered stent was deployed across the perforation. (Figure 2A, B). The stent was deployed at nominal pressure (7 atm), avoiding higher deployment pressures to minimize risk of stent induced injury to the distal small calibre vessel. Contrast injection showed no further extravasation or shunt. While the distal end of the stent appeared under-expanded, in view of small distal calibre vessel, post-dilation of the stent was not performed. (Figure 2B). Dual antiplatelet therapy was initiated with Aspirin (5 mg/kg/day OD) and Clopidogrel (0.2 mg/kg/dose OD). The patient’s haemodynamic status improved and he was successfully extubated on the second post-procedure day having stable rhythm. Patient was discharged in stable condition from hospital three days later.

Echocardiography at 1 and 8 months follow-up demonstrated normal biventricular systolic function (LV EF 72%), unobstructed right ventricular outflow, normal interventricular septal movement and no residual coronary fistula (see Supplementary material online, Video S9). ECG showed sinus rhythm with normal QRS complex morphology and no evidence of ischaemic changes (Figure 1C). At 8 months follow up, patient was doing well with no symptoms.

Discussion

Iatrogenic coronary artery injury after cardiac surgery in paediatric age group is a well-documented complication typically occurring in children having congenital heart defects with pulmonary stenosis requiring right ventricle outflow tract (RVOT) resection.4–6 The lesion in our case was novel in itself as it was a partial laceration of rare coronary artery branching distribution and the rent was directed into RV cavity rather than extravasating externally. A good spatial awareness of coronary vessels considering multiple anatomical variations passing close to RVOT is of utmost importance to avoid such adverse events in patients undergoing resection.

The clinical presentation ranges from being completely asymptomatic to severe haemodynamic instability. First septal perforator branch from LAD is known to be at risk of resection however, usually it does not cause significant steal, muscle ischaemia or arrhythmias and seals off within 48–72 h after surgery. In our case, the laceration involved Lesser/Short LAD, part of dual LAD system, and resulted in haemodynamic instability likely as a result of coronary steal phenomenon compromising blood supply to Main/Long LAD.

In the absence of guidelines for the management of such patients, treatment needed to be individualized based on haemodynamic status and availability of equipment and expertise. While a well-established treatment modality in adults, PCI is rarely performed in children, owing to exceedingly rare indications as well as technical concerns in this age group. In particular, inserting stents in small calibre vessels in a growing child pose risk of stent thrombosis due to small diameter in the short-term and stent to vessel size-mismatch resulting in stent mal-apposition, migration and thrombosis in the long-term. Despite these technical concerns, PCI in congenital heart disease has been performed in stenotic coronaries usually post-coronary button transfer in arterial switch operation with good short-term results,7 in patients with Kawasaki disease and those with coronary artery stenosis secondary to familial hypercholesterolaemia. Re-intervention rate in small case series is as high as 60% within 5 years.8 Poor outcomes have been reported for children younger than 3 months, receiving coronary stents.9 The use of dual-antiplatelet therapy as well as statins vary from case to case with no clear recommendations.10 In our case, statins were not used as coronary arteries were inherently not diseased. Our case shows that in select paediatric patients with coronary perforation, not amenable to surgery, PCI with a covered stent is a viable option.

Conclusion

Iatrogenic coronary artery fistula in children, though very rare, can be catastrophic and requires immediate recognition and management. PCI using a covered stent is a viable salvage option for lesions not amenable to surgery in critically sick children.
