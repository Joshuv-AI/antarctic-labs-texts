Pathways of urban green space in shaping population aging across China via spatial spillover and non-linear threshold effects
Authors: Xiaoning Zheng, Yujue Wang, Dongmei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChina is undergoing an unprecedented demographic transition, with population aging posing significant urban governance challenges. As a core component of green infrastructure, urban green spaces (UGS) are hypothesized to support elderly populations through complex spatial and non-linear mechanisms. This study integrates mediation analysis, the Spatial Durbin Model, and threshold regression to examine these relationships. The findings reveal that UGS significantly promotes elderly concentration through two distinct pathways. UGS enhances public service capacity, generating an amenity-pull effect that drives the spatial sorting of the elderly across administrative boundaries. This mechanism provides a robust justification for the observed spatial spillover effects in regional aging patterns. UGS mitigates the ecological burden, a path characterized by non-linear dynamics. The relationship between UGS and aging is moderated by a multi-dimensional threshold mechanism involving both UGS density and environmental carrying capacity. The results emphasize that in an era of high population mobility and regional integration, UGS serves as a strategic resource for collaborative governance. Policymakers should transition from isolated urban planning to cross-boundary ecological coordination, leveraging these spillover and threshold dynamics to achieve balanced regional development and support the Healthy China 2030 strategy.Keywords: Urban green space, Aging, Mediation Analysis, Spatial Durbin model, Threshold regression model, China

Introduction

Population aging represents one of the most significant global social transformations, carrying profound socioeconomic and health implications [1]. In response, governments worldwide are implementing a series of countermeasures, including social security and healthcare reforms [2]. However, many developing nations face the dual challenge of growing old before getting rich [3].

Since officially entering an aging society phase in 2001 with an elderly population share of 7.1%, China has undergone a persistent and accelerating demographic transition. By the conclusion of 2023, the proportion of citizens aged 65 and above had risen significantly, demonstrating profound spatial heterogeneity across the nation in Fig. 1. These evolving demographic patterns underscore the urgent need for a rigorous investigation into how urban ecological infrastructure influences regional population structures and aging trends.

Evolution of demographic aging and old-age dependency ratio in China

Urban green spaces (UGS) shape demographic patterns by influencing residential preferences, lifestyle choices, and the spatial distribution of older adults [4]. UGS significantly influence aging-related demographic dynamics. Insufficient or poorly managed green spaces may exacerbate environmental issues [5]. Expanding and improving green spaces may accelerate the concentration of elderly populations in cities. By enhancing urban attractiveness and providing environments conducive to recreation, wellness, and social interaction, green spaces encourage older adults to remain in or relocate to cities, thereby reinforcing urban aging trends [6]. Despite growing research on urbanization and aging, systematic analyses examining UGS as a driver of population aging remain limited.

Despite growing attention to this issue, important knowledge gaps remain, notably the frequent neglect of spatial heterogeneity in UGS and the lack of systematic exploration into the nonlinear or threshold effects of UGS on aging. The underlying transmission pathways remain under-examined. This study specifically examines how the interplay between public service optimization and the mitigation of ecological burdens mediates the impact of UGS on demographic structures. Clarifying these mechanisms is essential for building age-friendly environments, optimizing resource allocation, and alleviating the pressures of an aging population.

To fill these gaps, this study investigates the relationship between UGS and aging outcomes in China using prefecture-level panel data covering 300 cities from 2001 to 2023. By constructing a multi-dimensional mediation framework, we explore how UGS triggers an amenity-pull effect on the elderly through the dual channels of Public Service (PS) and Ecological Burden (EB). Utilizing spatial Durbin and threshold regression models, we evaluate both the spatial spillover effects of UGS and the nonlinearities in its influence on demographic aging under the constraints of environmental carrying capacity. By utilizing a continuous panel dataset and spatial econometric tools, we investigate whether UGS construction influences regional aging structure through health pathways and population migration mechanisms. This clarification is essential for providing scientifically grounded evidence for urban planners.

The contributions of this research are threefold. It advances the theoretical literature by uncovering the amenity-pull mechanism through which UGS reshapes demographic structures. By constructing a multi-dimensional mediation framework, we identify how UGS expansion attracts and retains elderly populations through the dual pathways of PS optimization and EB mitigation. This study provides an evidence base for the design of spatially-sensitive policies and urban planning. We reveal that UGS is a strategic spatial resource with significant cross-regional spillover effects, necessitating coordinated regional governance for age-friendly development. Third, we identify a double nonlinearity in the relationship between UGS, environmental constraints, and demographic aging. Specifically, we find that the impact of UGS itself is nonlinear, characterized by a marginal increasing effect once green coverage reaches a critical mass. Furthermore, we pinpoint a second threshold effect regarding the city’s environmental carrying capacity: an environmental red line for the Ecological Burden. Beyond this threshold, the intensifying industrial externalities neutralize the positive aging-friendly efficacy of greening. This highlights the necessity of balancing ecological expansion with carbon constraints to effectively manage the evolving spatial distribution and structural transformation of aging populations, thereby fostering a sustainable demographic-environmental balance.

Literature review

UGS encompassing parks, forests, wetlands, greenbelts, and other vegetation-covered areas within metropolitan environments, has been increasingly acknowledged as a pivotal determinant of population health, particularly in the context of demographic aging [7, 8]. As the proportion of older adults continues to rise globally, with China facing one of the most profound and rapid transitions, understanding how UGS contributes to physical, psychological, and social well-being has become a critical research frontier.

A major dimension of UGS lies in its capacity to regulate the ecological environment and reduce harmful exposures. Vegetation moderates urban airflow dynamics, alleviating health risks associated with respiratory diseases and heat-related illnesses [9]. Empirical evidence demonstrates that urban greening improves air quality and enhances overall life satisfaction [10]. These findings suggest that UGS serves as a vital environmental buffer. Public green areas provide accessible and safe venues for low-intensity exercise, and recreational activities that improve cardiovascular fitness, mobility, and overall physical resilience. By lowering concentrations of PM2.5 and PM10, urban vegetation directly reduces the incidence of respiratory and cardiovascular complications [11]. These studies indicate that UGS is not only a leisure amenity but also an integral determinant of physical health outcomes among older adults.

Psychological and social dimensions represent another pathway through which UGS contributes to healthier aging. Contact with natural environments has been shown to alleviate stress, reduce depressive symptoms, and enhance cognitive restoration [12]. Moreover, parks and green areas provided essential venues for social interaction during the COVID-19 pandemic, when social distancing disrupted conventional networks [13, 14]. These findings emphasize the dual psychological and social mechanisms through which UGS enhances quality of life and prolongs functional independence in later life.

Research indicates that UGS exhibit significant distributional inequities, with affluent neighborhoods faring substantially better than marginalized communities [15]. Older adults often struggle to equitably access these health benefits [16], exacerbating health inequalities through green space disparities.

Despite substantial progress, several gaps remain evident in the literature. Existing research often treats urban greening as a static environmental benefit, overlooking its role as a dynamic driver of geographic population sorting. Methodologically, the focus on localized physical and psychological outcomes frequently ignores the inter-regional migration dynamics that reshape city-level demographic structures. While the direct health benefits of UGS are well-documented, there is insufficient exploration of the underlying pathways specifically how public service optimization and the mitigation of ecological burdens synergistically attract and retain elderly cohorts. These limitations highlight the necessity of adopting longitudinal datasets and advanced econometric models to systematically capture the amenity-led spillovers and the nonlinear constraints imposed by environmental carrying capacity. By addressing these gaps, this study moves beyond individual-level health metrics to uncover the macro-level mechanisms through which UGS expansion alters the trajectories of population aging.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, UGS influences aging outcomes through five primary pathways: environmental regulation, physical health promotion, psychological restoration, social cohesion, and climate change adaptation. These pathways reinforce the city’s environmental quality and public health resources, enhancing its appeal as a survival amenity for older adults. By improving localized habitat conditions and fostering healthy aging, UGS triggers amenity-led migration dynamics characterized by two distinct effects: the retention of local seniors and the attraction of inter-regional retirement migrants. These pathways inform the following hypotheses, which guide the empirical analysis.

Multi-dimensional pathways of UGS on aging

Urban green space plays a crucial role in promoting the physical health of older adults. By providing accessible environments for outdoor activities, green spaces reduce sedentary lifestyles, enhance cardiovascular fitness, and mitigate the prevalence of chronic diseases. At the same time, green vegetation improves local air quality by filtering pollutants and lowering exposure to harmful particles, which directly benefits the respiratory health of vulnerable populations. Together, these mechanisms highlight the essential role of UGS in safeguarding physical health as populations age [17]. Yet beyond its direct health benefits, the expansion of UGS may also reshape demographic structures. Enhanced living environments and superior health resources in urban areas serve as critical determinants for the residential choices of the elderly, fostering a concentration of senior cohorts and reshuffling regional demographic compositions [18]. Such ecological improvements not only bolster the physical well-being of local seniors but also amplify a city’s magnetic pull on retirement migrants from other regions. Consequently, the disproportionate concentration of older adults in high-quality green areas reflects the profound demographic shifts driven by urban ecological development. As a result, the relative share of older adults in the overall population may increase, reflecting the demographic consequences of UGS development.

UGS expansion serves as a catalyst for broader urban infrastructure optimization. High-quality green spaces are typically integrated with age-friendly facilities, such as accessible walkways, outdoor fitness zones, and proximity to healthcare centers [19]. By augmenting these institutional supports, UGS enhances a city’s service-led pull effect. This superior service capacity specifically targets the needs of elderly cohorts who prioritize healthcare accessibility and social welfare over economic opportunities, thereby increasing the aging proportion in ecologically well-equipped cities.

UGS plays a critical role in mitigating environmental stressors, such as filtering air pollutants and regulating the urban heat island effect [20]. For the elderly, who are more vulnerable to respiratory and cardiovascular risks, the reduction of this ecological burden acts as a health stabilizer that prolongs life expectancy and lowers mortality rates. Consequently, by alleviating environmental health risks, UGS fosters a supportive habitat that encourages elderly residents to remain and retirees to relocate, leading to a qualitative and quantitative shift in the population structure.

Therefore, UGS goes beyond providing environmental amenities; it systematically alters local demographic compositions by improving institutional and ecological conditions.

Beyond physical health, UGS also contributes substantially to the psychological and social well-being of the elderly. Access to green parks helps reduce stress, alleviate depressive symptoms, and combat loneliness, key challenges that are increasingly mitigated through public health programs and community centers integrated into these ecological spaces. In addition, the collocation of public service facilities within these green parks fosters opportunities for social interaction and community building, strengthening social cohesion and buffering the negative psychological effects of isolation among older adults. These findings suggest that UGS serves as a critical public health resource not only by promoting mental resilience but also by reinforcing social ties in aging communities [21]. At the same time, the benefits of UGS are not confined within administrative boundaries. Improvements in environmental quality, living conditions, and health resources in one city can influence migration flows and reshape demographic structures in neighboring regions. As a vital component of urban infrastructure, UGS expansion often augments a city’s public service capacity, creating a powerful service-pull that attracts elderly cohorts seeking better healthcare accessibility and institutional support. These interregional dynamic underscores the possibility that UGS exerts spatial spillover effects on demographic aging.

Furthermore, the role of UGS in the era of climate change cannot be overlooked. With rising global temperatures and the intensification of urban heat islands, older adults are disproportionately exposed to heat-related health risks [22]. UGS can effectively mitigate these risks by regulating microclimates, reducing ambient temperatures, and supporting climate adaptation. However, the benefits of UGS may not increase linearly; instead, they may demonstrate threshold effects, where health improvements become more pronounced once green space availability surpasses a certain level. Moreover, spatial spillover effects imply that the benefits of UGS extend beyond local communities, influencing aging outcomes in neighboring areas. These insights suggest that the demographic effects of UGS are likely to be nonlinear. At lower levels of green coverage, the capacity of UGS to influence population aging may be limited. Yet once a critical threshold is reached, the ecological and health benefits become strong enough to alter migration dynamics and amplify the concentration of older adults. This indicates that the relationship between UGS and demographic aging is characterized by a marginal increasing effect rather than a simple linear association.

The demographic efficacy of UGS is inextricably linked to a city’s overall environmental carrying capacity. While green infrastructure provides essential ecosystem services, its capacity to support an aging-friendly environment is not infinite; instead, it is constrained by the ecological burden of the urban system. In industrializing urban centers, high levels of carbon emissions and environmental pollution represent a significant structural strain that can undermine the survival amenities provided by green space [23]. These insights suggest that the regulatory effect of the ecological burden on the UGS-aging nexus is characterized by pronounced nonlinearities. At lower levels of ecological stress, UGS effectively functions as a health stabilizer that enhances the physiological resilience of the elderly. However, once the Ecological Burden surpasses a specific tipping point, the intensification of environmental pressure and respiratory risks may overwhelm the localized cooling and filtering effects of green vegetation. Under such conditions, the amenity-pull of UGS becomes insufficient to counteract the negative externalities of pollution, rendering the positive association between greening and aging insignificant. This indicates that the relationship between UGS and demographic aging is not only governed by the scale of greening itself but is also strictly contingent upon the city’s environmental threshold.

Hypothesis 4b: The impact of UGS on China’s aging population is constrained by the city’s environmental carrying capacity. That is, once the EB exceeds a critical threshold, the environmental pressure will neutralize the ecological benefits of greening, rendering the positive association between UGS and aging insignificant.

The literature indicates that UGS drives the concentration of elderly populations and reshapes urban demographic structures through multiple pathways environmental, psychological, social, and climate adaptation while also revealing methodological and policy-related limitations. This study integrates these perspectives, laying the groundwork for exploring how UGS in China act as a catalyst for the amenity-pull effect, thereby increasing the size of the elderly population and altering local aging trajectories. By identifying the mediation of public services and the constraints of ecological burdens, this research offers valuable insights for urban planning and public policy aimed at balancing ecological development with demographic sustainability.

Methodology

Model construction

This study constructs a comprehensive empirical framework integrating Two-way Fixed Effects, mediation analysis, the Spatial Durbin Model, and Panel Threshold Regression to systematically investigate the impact of UGS on population aging. The Two-way Fixed Effects model serves as the baseline specification to identify fundamental correlations while controlling for city-specific and year-specific fixed effects. And the mediation effect model is introduced to decode the internal transmission pathways. Spatial Durbin Model is implemented to quantify regional spillover effects, while the PTR model is utilized to identify potential nonlinearities and phased shifts in the UGS-aging relationship. By synthesizing these models, this study offers a holistic perspective that differentiates between direct and indirect effects while tracking their dynamic variations across different developmental stages of green infrastructure.

To rigorously capture the spatial interdependencies of UGS and its impact on population aging, this study constructs two types of spatial weight matrices according to the methodological framework proposed by Janatabadi and Ermagun [24]. The first specification is the Geographic Distance Matrix (), in which the elements are defined based on the inverse of the squared great-circle distance between city centroids. The underlying logic adheres to the First Law of Geography, which posits that closer spatial units exert more significant mutual influences. Within the context of this study, represents the spatial decay of environmental amenities, based on the assumption that the amenity-pull and health-promoting effects of UGS are most pronounced between geographically proximate cities.

The second specification is the Economic Distance Matrix (), which integrates geographic distance with economic output to account for the fact that demographic flows and service-sharing are often driven by socio-economic linkages rather than mere physical proximity. By incorporating the absolute difference in average per capita GDP during the study period from 2001 to 2023, this matrix assumes that cities with comparable levels of economic development exhibit higher interaction intensity. This formulation is particularly effective for capturing the competition for public services and the migration patterns of the elderly population between economically synchronized urban centers. To maintain the stability of the econometric estimation and facilitate the interpretation of spatial lag coefficients, all matrices are row-sum normalized. This procedure ensures that the spatial lag of a variable represents a weighted average of its values in neighboring cities, thereby enhancing the reliability of the empirical findings.

Panel threshold regression model

To further investigate the nonlinear and phased impacts of UGS on the aging population, this study employs the Panel Threshold Regression model developed by Hansen [25]. We initially set UGS as the threshold variable to identify potential structural breaks in the UGS-aging relationship across different stages of green infrastructure development.

Recognizing that the marginal benefits of greening may be contingent upon environmental contexts, we extend our analysis by employing EB as an alternative threshold variable. This allows us to explore whether the effectiveness of UGS in attracting or retaining the elderly population shifts significantly under varying levels of environmental pressure. By utilizing these two respective threshold analyses, we provide a more nuanced understanding of how the internal scale of greening and external environmental constraints separately shape the demographic landscape.

To account for regional interdependencies, the Spatial Durbin Model results are analyzed to capture cross-city spillover effects. We present the threshold effect results to elucidate the nonlinear, phased characteristics of both the scale effects of urban greening and the constraint effects of the ecological environment. This section conducts robustness tests to ensure the reliability of the empirical results.

Historical greening levels provide the physical and planning foundation for current UGS provision, ensuring a high correlation between the instrument and the endogenous variable. Discussion

Rethinking the impact of UGS on aging in China

Since the onset of the 21st century, the expansion of UGS in China has progressed in tandem with an accelerating demographic transition, revealing deep-seated spatial interactions between ecological transformation and aging structures. Our Spatial Durbin Model results indicate that UGS does not merely function as a localized environmental amenity but triggers a significant amenity-led sorting process that transcends city boundaries. By augmenting PS capacity, UGS generates a spatial pull effect that attracts elderly populations from neighboring regions, while simultaneously mitigating the EB to foster a supportive habitat for physiological resilience.

This empirical positive correlation between UGS and aging rates should not be misinterpreted as UGS worsening the demographic crisis; rather, it underscores that high-quality green infrastructure provides a selective advantage in attracting and retaining elderly individuals seeking a superior quality of life. This relationship is characterized by distinct nonlinearities. The threshold regression analysis confirms a scale effect where the demographic benefits of UGS intensify significantly only after reaching a critical tipping point, yet remain contingent upon an environmental constraint an ecological red line of carbon emissions. These findings validate UGS as a proactive and structurally complex force in fostering a resilient, aging-friendly society.

UGS is not simply an environmental amenity; it is also a mechanism of resource redistribution. In places where green infrastructure is scarce, the demographic consequences are often severe: declining populations, deteriorating health conditions, and a higher concentration of the elderly and vulnerable groups [35]. This process can be described as selective sedimentation, where green-rich cities attract mobile, productive cohorts, leaving behind the immobile, dependent, and elderly [36]. The outcome is an uneven demographic map marked by rapid and spatially unbalanced aging.

The threshold regression analysis provides further nuance, demonstrating that the association between UGS and population aging is nonlinear and stage-dependent. In the initial stages of greening, the demographic pull is relatively weak. However, once UGS surpasses a critical scale effect threshold, its predictive capacity for elderly concentration intensifies rapidly, reflecting a transition from quantitative landscape expansion to qualitative improvement in urban livability.

Our findings reveal that this ecological benefit is contingent upon a boundary constraint of carbon emissions. Once the EB exceeds the tipping point, the positive association between UGS and aging disappears, neutralized by environmental pressure. If unchecked, high-emission cities may fall into a structural lock-in, where even aggressive greening efforts fail to attract or retain populations, leaving these regions resistant to demographic revitalization.

UGS influences aging through the synergistic effects of spatial sorting, institutional provision, and ecological constraints: green-rich cities leverage a superior PS capacity to attract mobile elderly cohorts; meanwhile, fragmented ecological investments in high-burden areas fail to mitigate the EB, resulting in a pattern of amenity-led sedimentation where the immobile elderly are concentrated in underdeveloped, resource-scarce regions.

Therefore, UGS shapes the aging landscape through three distinct mechanisms: the demographic mechanism of amenity-led sorting, the institutional mechanism of enhanced public service provision, and the ecological mechanism of carbon-constrained health benefits. Without a coordinated strategy to bridge the green-carbon divide, these spatial disparities will continue to worsen regional imbalances in China’s demographic transition.

Policy implications

China’s population aging should be viewed as a systemic outcome shaped by the interplay of UGS development, socioeconomic transformation, and institutional adaptation. The empirical findings of this study suggest that while the expansion of UGS is a pivotal instrument for supporting an aging-friendly society, its efficacy is governed by complex spatial spillovers and non-linear threshold effects. Addressing the regional imbalances identified in our analysis requires a coordinated, multi-dimensional policy framework that integrates spatial, demographic, and ecological governance. A regional coordination mechanism must be established to manage the amenity-led sorting and spatial spillovers revealed by the SDM analysis. Since UGS generates a significant pull effect that attracts elderly populations across city boundaries via the PS pathway, policy interventions should prioritize the equitable distribution of high-quality green infrastructure. To mitigate the selective sedimentation of the immobile elderly in ecologically deprived areas, fiscal transfers and ecological compensation should be utilized to subsidize greening projects in peripheral regions [37]. By balancing the spatial allocation of ecological assets, planners can prevent the demographic polarization that arises from uneven amenity-pull, thereby fostering a more inclusive and spatially balanced aging landscape.

Ecological governance must be differentiated based on the environmental carrying capacity and the carbon-constrained threshold. Our results indicate that the positive association between UGS and aging is strictly contingent upon an EB threshold. For cities exceeding this critical tipping point, policymakers should prioritize industrial emission reduction and carbon mitigation prior to or in tandem with landscape greening. In these high-burden contexts, blind greening without concurrent pollution control may result in inefficient resource allocation, as the health benefits of UGS are neutralized by excessive environmental pressure. Therefore, a dual-track strategy integrating carbon-lowering initiatives with ecological restoration is essential for cities to cross the environmental red line and unlock the demographic benefits of green spaces.

Urban planning should adopt a strategic scaling approach to surmount the UGS scale-effect threshold. Given that the predictive impact of UGS on elderly concentration intensifies significantly only after reaching a critical coverage level, municipal governments should avoid fragmented and small-scale greening efforts. Instead, they should focus on the construction of interconnected green networks and large-scale ecological corridors that provide sufficient service density to influence demographic structures [38]. By ensuring that UGS development surpasses the efficiency tipping point, cities can maximize the qualitative transformation of their demographic landscapes, ensuring a sustainable transition toward a resilient and healthy aging society.

The governance of aging in the context of UGS expansion requires a paradigmatic shift toward a holistic, life-course-oriented framework. Aging should be seen not only as a late-life challenge but as a cumulative process shaped by spatial exposure to ecological environments, access to healthcare, and intergenerational mobility. Only through institutional innovation, spatial coordination, and ecological redistribution can China mitigate the structural risks of population aging while aligning with broader objectives of rural revitalization, common prosperity, and sustainable development.

Research limitations and future prospects

First, constrained by prefecture-level city panel data, it fails to uncover micro-level heterogeneity and dynamic mechanisms. Future research should employ finer-grained microdata and longer time series to deepen our understanding.

Second, while the model controlled for social, economic, and environmental variables, it did not account for soft factors such as cultural perceptions and environmental awareness. Future research should integrate qualitative and quantitative methods to explore the moderating effects of these contextual variables.

Third, while China’s experience holds reference value, differences in institutional frameworks and developmental stages across nations necessitate future cross-national comparisons to examine the universality and particularity of the relationship between UGS and aging populations in diverse contexts.

Overall, future research should strive to develop a more nuanced, interactive, and comparable framework for understanding population aging in the context of ecological and spatial transformations. This would not only deepen academic insights but also provide richer empirical guidance for policymaking in demographic management and environmental planning in both developed and developing economies.

Conclusions

Based on a panel dataset of 300 prefecture-level cities in China from 2001 to 2023, this study utilizes the Spatial Durbin Model and threshold regression to systematically analyze the impact of UGS on population aging and its underlying mechanisms. The main findings are as follows:

UGS serves as a significant positive predictor of elderly population concentration. The results indicate that the expansion of UGS significantly enhances the amenity-pull of urban areas for the elderly, acting as a proactive driver for aging-friendly environments. The empirical evidence confirms a robust positive correlation between green space coverage and the share of the elderly population, suggesting that optimized ecological landscapes provide superior habitats that validate the theory of amenity-led demographic sorting.

The dual mediating roles of PS and EB. The mechanism analysis reveals that UGS influences demographic structures through two primary channels. Specifically, UGS translates ecological assets into demographic attractions by augmenting Public Service capacity, thereby enhancing the institutional support available to the elderly. Simultaneously, UGS modifies the aging landscape by mitigating the Ecological Burden, which reduces environmental stressors and improves the overall livability of the urban habitat. These two variables act as critical intermediaries, bridging the gap between urban greening and regional demographic shifts.

Service-driven cross-regional pull and spatial spillovers. Further analysis of the Public Service pathway shows that the impact of UGS transcends administrative boundaries. The SDM confirms significant spatial spillover effects, where high-quality green spaces and superior public services in a city generate a pull effect on neighboring regions, attracting elderly cohorts. This cross-regional demographic movement driven by service-pull reveals the deep-seated spatial interdependence of population aging.

Evolutionary stages based on dual thresholds of UGS and EB. The relationship between UGS and aging is characterized by distinct nonlinearities and stage-dependent features. The predictive impact of green space is relatively weak when coverage is low; however, once UGS surpasses a critical tipping point, its positive association with elderly concentration intensifies rapidly. The benefits of greening are constrained by the city’s environmental carrying capacity. Once the Ecological Burden, measured by carbon dioxide emissions, exceeds the threshold, overwhelming industrial environmental pressure neutralizes the ecological benefits of UGS, rendering the positive association between greening and aging insignificant.

This study advances theoretical and empirical understanding of the UGS–aging nexus by moving beyond linear assumptions and uncovering the dual mechanisms of spatial diffusion and nonlinear amplification. The findings provide a strategic policy framework for targeted interventions, including optimizing UGS allocation, enhancing regional coordination, and adopting phased governance to rectify demographic imbalances caused by uneven ecological resource distribution, thereby achieving sustainable coordination between population structures and ecological transitions.
