Exploring the status and influencing factors of nurses’ digital health literacy: a scoping review
Authors: Caihua Ye, Deliang Hou, Minna Pikkarainen, Bocan Chen, Na Qin, Shaona Lin, Xinjun Jiang, Vivien Xi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe utilization of digital health technologies (DHT) among nurses remains notably low, primarily due to limited digital health literacy (DHL). Enhancing DHL among nursing staff is essential for improving healthcare delivery. Despite its importance, a comprehensive synthesis of existing literature on this subject is still lacking.ObjectiveThis scoping review aims to summarize the current status, measurement instruments, and strategies to enhance nurses’ DHL, while identifying and categorizing influencing factors within a structured framework.MethodsA scoping review was conducted following a systematic search in PubMed, Ovid, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, WanFang, CNKI, and VIP from inception to January 2025. Supplementary manual searches were updated in November 2025 to identify additional eligible studies published between February and November 2025, and the final eligibility cutoff for inclusion was November 2025.ResultsA total of 21 studies were included, predominantly conducted in high-income and upper-middle-income countries. The reported formats and levels of nurses’ DHL varied considerably, generally indicating low proficiency. Only four general measurement tools were identified for assessing nurses’ DHL. Factors associated with DHL among nurses were categorized into three levels based on the social ecological model: individual, interpersonal, and organizational. Furthermore, included studies suggested a model-based mediating role for DHL at the individual level, with higher DHL linked to multiple positive outcomes. Only one study proposed a training program aimed at improving nurses’ DHL; however, its effectiveness remains unverified.ConclusionsResearch on nurses’ DHL is still emerging. There is an urgent need to develop a nursing-specific conceptual model, a standardized assessment framework, and dynamic measurement tools responsive to evolving Web 2.0 and Web 3.0 technologies. The integrated framework of influencing factors derived from the social ecological model provides a theoretical foundation and practical guidance for nursing leaders and researchers to develop targeted, multilevel interventions aimed at enhancing nurses’ DHL.RegistrationOpen Science Framework website (OSF.IO/N9HWQ).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04532-z.Keywords: Nurse, Digital health literacy, eHealth literacy, Influencing factors, Scoping reviews

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) recognizes digital health technologies (DHT) as pivotal in enhancing five critical areas: patient care, management and monitoring, prevention, disease surveillance, and e-learning [1]. Nurses constitute the largest professional group within the global healthcare framework [2] and play an essential role in integrating DHT into clinical settings. A survey revealed that 94 nurses implemented 102 different digital technologies in their practice [3]. Despite this, research indicates nurses’ engagement with DHT remains predominantly low [4, 5], mainly due to inadequate digital health literacy (DHL) [5, 6]. Notably, the WHO has integrated DHL into its public health strategies [7], recognizing it as essential to improving health outcomes and advancing health equity [8]. Given nurses’ critical role in DHT implementation, enhancing their DHL is imperative.

With the rapid advancement and widespread adoption of DHT, individuals require an integrated set of competencies known as DHL. DHL refers to an individual’s ability to seek, understand, evaluate, and apply health information and technologies within digital health environments [9]. In contrast, eHealth literacy primarily emphasizes the ability to locate, comprehend, and critically evaluate health information from online or electronic sources, with a particular focus on internet-based contexts [10]. As a newer and broader concept, DHL expands beyond eHealth literacy by encompassing the comprehension, utilization, and active engagement with various DHT, such as telehealth, mobile health applications, and virtual reality platforms [1, 11]. Therefore, DHL more accurately reflects the essential competencies required by individuals, particularly nurses, to effectively navigate today’s increasingly complex digital healthcare environment.

DHL was positively associated with nurses’ acceptance of digital health tools. Chen et al. [12] reported that DHL was significantly associated with nurses’ attitudes towards artificial intelligence (AI) applications and was also indirectly related to attitudes via perceived value and trust in technology. Additionally, DHL can alleviate the adverse effects of information anxiety on nurse’ core competencies [13]. Nurses hold a critical role within healthcare systems, and their DHL enables patient empowerment by facilitating patients’ understanding and use of health information for active health management [14]. These findings underscore the multifaceted importance of nurse’ DHL. However, the current state of nurses’ DHL is concerning. For example, a survey involving multiple healthcare professional groups revealed that as many as 47.5% of nurses had inadequate DHL levels [15]. Moreover, nurses often lead digital health interventions provided to patients [16], and insufficient DHL among nurses could negatively influence patient safety and care quality [17]. Therefore, comprehensively exploring factors influencing nurses’ DHL is imperative.

In recent years, the influencing factors of nurses’ DHL have gradually drawn scholarly attention. For example, Cho et al. [18] identified health-promoting behaviors as protective factors for DHL among nurses, whereas information anxiety was reported as a barrier [13]. However, the current body of research remains exploratory, typically examining only a limited number of variables and lacking a systematic analysis of mechanisms underlying DHL in nursing contexts. Importantly, existing studies and literature reviews often consider healthcare providers broadly, frequently integrating multiple professional groups into analyses without specifically focusing on nurses as frontline professionals actively utilizing DHT [19]. Although one review reported generally high eHealth literacy levels among healthcare professionals, identifying sociodemographic characteristics, previous technology experience, and health-related behaviors as significant influences [20], its sample included 11 different professional groups. This makes it difficult to precisely depict nurses’ DHL status. Additionally, current DHL assessment instruments were mainly developed and validated in general populations or among patients [21], and limited evidence exists regarding their suitability and validity for nursing populations. Furthermore, effective interventions designed to enhance nurses’ DHL have not yet been systematically investigated. Given DHL’s emerging status within nursing, no specialized literature review has yet synthesized existing research on this topic comprehensively. Scoping reviews are particularly useful for mapping current evidence on specific topics, identifying literature gaps, and proposing future research directions [22]. Therefore, conducting a scoping review to systematically synthesize research on nurses’ DHL is urgently required.

The social ecological model is widely recognized as a theoretical framework that elucidates how health behaviors are influenced by factors across multiple dimensions [23]. Previous studies have applied this model in scoping reviews to systematically identify and categorize multilevel influencing factors, subsequently forming analytical frameworks [24]. Guided by the five-level structure of the social ecological model (individual, interpersonal, community, organizational, and policy levels) [23], this study aims to systematically map current research on nurses’ DHL, including assessment tools and intervention strategies, and specifically identify influencing factors to develop an integrated analytical framework. This approach will provide a robust theoretical foundation and practical guidance for future research, policy-making, and targeted interventions.

This scoping review aims to address the following research questions: 1) What is the scope and current reporting status of research regarding nurses’ DHL? 2) Which instruments have been utilized to assess DHL among nurses? 3) What factors associated with nurses’ DHL and related outcomes have been identified? 4) Which interventions or strategies have been reported to enhance nurses’ DHL?

Method

Design

A scoping review was conducted to systematically identify, map, and synthesize existing evidence concerning nurses’ DHL. The review was performed and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist [22]. The protocol for this review was prospectively registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF) (10.17605/OSF.IO/N9HWQ) prior to study initiation.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in major electronic databases, including PubMed, Ovid, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, WanFang, CNKI, and VIP, from inception to January 2025. Additional manual searches, including reference list screening and targeted search engine queries, were updated in November 2025 to identify further eligible records published between February and November 2025. The final eligibility cutoff for inclusion was November 2025. The search terms were iteratively developed by the research team, combining controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) and free-text keywords related to nurses and DHL. No restrictions on study design were applied during the search phase. For instance, the PubMed search strategy was as follows: ((“Nurses”[Mesh]) OR (nurse OR nursing OR clinical nurse OR registered nurse OR nursing professional OR nurse practitioner)) AND (digital health literacy OR DHL OR eHealth literacy OR e-Health literacy OR electronic health literacy). Equivalent search strategies were adapted for other databases according to their indexing terms and search syntax. The complete database-specific search strategies for both English and Chinese databases are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were guided by the Participant-Concept-Context framework [22]. Specifically, the criteria were: 1) Participant: Eligible participants included nurses holding a valid professional license or certification, such as Licensed Practical Nurses, Registered Nurses, Advanced Practice Registered Nurses, or Nurse Practitioners. Additionally, studies featuring mixed participant groups (e.g., nurses alongside physicians or other healthcare professionals) were included only if both the number of nurses and DHL-related outcomes specific to the nurse subgroup were explicitly reported. No minimum proportion of nurses was required for mixed samples; eligibility depended solely on clearly reported nurse-specific data. 2) Concept: Studies were required to explicitly address DHL or eHealth literacy, defined as the ability to acquire health information from digital sources and translate this information into practice [10, 11]. 3) Context: Included studies encompassed various practice environments of nurses, such as hospitals, community-based settings, and outpatient clinics. 4) Types of evidence sources: Primary research studies, including cross-sectional, longitudinal, qualitative, and intervention studies, were included. Multiple evidence types were considered to facilitate a comprehensive synthesis of research on nurses’ DHL.

The exclusion criteria were: 1) Irrelevant topics: Studies not explicitly focused on DHL or eHealth literacy, such as those addressing general digital literacy, digital competence, digital capabilities, or telemedicine skills without an explicit DHL component. 2) Ineligible participants: Studies involving nursing students or non-nurse populations (e.g., patients or the general public). 3) Ineligible study types: Review articles, conference papers, and study protocols. 4) Articles for which the full text was inaccessible. 5) Publications in languages other than English or Chinese. 6) Duplicate publications.

Study selection

All retrieved references were imported into NoteExpress (version 3.9.0) for automated deduplication. The deduplication process involved comparing records based on publication year and title, disregarding case sensitivity, punctuation, and whitespace, with a similarity threshold set at 85% to identify duplicates. Two researchers then independently screened the literature in two stages according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. First, titles and abstracts were reviewed to exclude clearly irrelevant studies. At this initial screening stage, topic relevance was manually assessed based on the predefined conceptual definitions of DHL and eHealth literacy, without relying on automated algorithms or keyword filters. Second, full-text articles were evaluated for eligibility. Any disagreements encountered during screening were resolved through discussion, with a third researcher consulted if consensus could not be reached.

Data extraction

Two researchers independently extracted data from the included studies using a predefined and pilot-tested Excel form. This extraction form was developed based on the study’s objectives and relevant key domains, and refined through iterative team discussions. Extracted variables included: 1) study characteristics (authors, country/region, year of publication, study design); 2) participant characteristics (sample size, description of participants, professional roles); 3) DHL assessment details (instrument name, version, scoring methods, and reported reliability); 4) reported DHL status or outcomes; 5) influencing factors identified; 6) components of interventions. Any discrepancies during data extraction were resolved through reviewer discussions, with adjudication by a third reviewer if agreement could not be reached.

Data synthesis and analysis

Due to the substantial heterogeneity among included studies, a quantitative synthesis was not conducted. Instead, a descriptive synthesis supported by tables and visualizations was performed to systematically summarize findings relevant to each research question. This descriptive approach enabled comparative assessment of results from diverse study designs and outcomes without statistical aggregation. Structured tables were utilized to clearly display key study characteristics and principal findings.

Following data extraction, information regarding factors influencing nurses’ DHL was systematically collated and compared across studies. Two reviewers independently examined and categorized these influencing factors using a data-driven charting method rather than qualitative thematic analysis. Any disagreements regarding categorization were resolved through discussion to achieve consensus, ensuring analytical rigor and consistency. Subsequently, the categorized influencing factors were mapped onto the five levels of the social ecological model, constructing a theoretically informed, multilevel analytical framework [23].

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 3003 records were retrieved. After removing duplicates and screening titles, abstracts, and full texts, 2989 articles were excluded. Additionally, seven studies identified through manual searching were added, resulting in 21 eligible studies included (Fig. 1).

The PRISMA flow diagram for study selection

These selected studies originated from 13 countries with diverse economic statuses: two from low-income countries (Ethiopia [15, 25]), one from a lower-middle-income country (Vietnam [26]), nine from upper-middle-income countries (China [12, 13, 27–30], Turkey [31], Jordan [32], and Iran [33]), and nine from high-income countries (Korea [18, 34], Denmark [35, 36], Japan [37], Italy [38], Greece [39], Saudi Arabia [40], and Cyprus [41]). These studies, published between 2018 and 2025, involved a total of 8044 nurses, with individual study samples ranging from 39 to 2540 participants. Most studies employed a cross-sectional design, while one utilized a longitudinal design [35], and another conducted an e-Delphi survey [41]. Reported status of nurses’ DHL

Twenty studies utilized various instruments to assess nurses’ DHL, demonstrating substantial heterogeneity in reporting formats (Table 1). Among studies employing the eHEALS, four distinct reporting methods were identified: seven studies reported total mean scores ranging from 21.89 to 32.70 [18, 26, 27, 31, 33, 37, 39]; two studies provided standardized mean scores of 3.83 [12] and 3.60 [40]; two studies reported qualification rates of 38.81% [30] and 55.19% [29]; another two studies reported high-level DHL prevalence rates of 52.5% [15] and 73.9% [25]; and one study reported a median total score of 32 [28]. Using the DHL Instrument, Zhao et al. [13] found an overall mean DHL score of 2.46, whereas Shudayfat et al. [32] presented each subscale score as a percentage of the theoretical maximum (55.3%–90.6%). Using the eHealth Literacy Questionnaire, Palshof et al. [36] reported mean scores across seven dimensions ranging from 2.41 to 3.18. Similarly, in a longitudinal study, Kayser et al. [35] assessed DHL at baseline and three-month follow-up, with dimension mean scores ranging from 2.57 to 3.40 at baseline and 2.59 to 3.38 at follow-up. Using the Health Literacy Survey Digital, Comparcini et al. [38] reported median scores of 100, 1.83, and 87.5 for its three dimensions.

Instruments for nurses’ DHL

eHealth literacy scale

The eHealth Literacy Scale (eHEALS), developed by Norman et al. [42] in Canada in 2006 based on the Lily model [10], is currently the most widely utilized instrument for assessing nurses’ DHL, appearing in 15 of the reviewed studies. The scale comprises eight items rated on a five-point Likert scale, yielding total scores ranging from 8 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge, confidence, and skills in locating, evaluating, and applying electronic health information to solve health-related issues. The original validation study reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.88, indicating good internal consistency reliability. The scale has subsequently been translated and implemented in various countries and regions worldwide. However, the eHEALS was initially designed for general health consumers rather than healthcare professionals, inadequately capturing the complexities inherent in nurses’ clinical environments. Moreover, the scale was developed during the Web 1.0 era, whereas the current internet landscape has evolved significantly (Web 2.0). Therefore, future research should aim to refine and update the eHEALS, ensuring its alignment with contemporary digital health contexts and nurses’ professional needs.

Digital health literacy instrument

Two studies employed Digital health literacy instrument (DHLI) to assess nurses’ DHL [13, 32]. The DHLI, developed by van der Vaart et al. [43] in the Netherlands in 2017, provides a comprehensive evaluation of DHL skills relevant to both Web 1.0 and Web 2.0 contexts, with a particular emphasis on practical competencies associated with Web 2.0. The instrument consists of seven dimensions and includes an innovative combination of objective performance-based items and self-reported measures within each dimension, enhancing measurement objectivity. Responses are recorded using a four-point Likert scale, with higher scores reflecting higher levels of DHL. The original validation study reported good internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.88). Subsequently, the DHLI has been translated into multiple languages and applied internationally [44]. Compared to the eHEALS, the DHLI integrates interactive competencies typical of the Web 2.0 era, thus offering broader applicability and greater relevance to contemporary digital environments. However, the DHLI remains a general instrument designed for the public, lacking content tailored specifically to the clinical responsibilities and professional contexts of nursing. Furthermore, its structural validity and cross-cultural suitability for nursing populations have not been empirically established. Future research should explore the applicability of the DHLI among nursing professionals across diverse cultural and regional contexts.

eHealth literacy questionnaire

Two studies applied the eHealth Literacy Questionnaire (eHLQ) to evaluate nurses’ DHL [35, 36]. Developed by Kayser et al. [45] in 2018 based on a systematic and inductively derived eHealth Literacy Framework [46], this questionnaire aims to comprehensively capture key competencies required for effectively understanding and utilizing electronic health technologies and digital health services. The eHLQ comprises seven dimensions and 35 items rated on a four-point Likert scale, with higher total scores indicating greater DHL proficiency. The instrument has demonstrated good composite reliability (coefficients ranging from 0.75 to 0.87) and has been translated into multiple languages [47]. Compared with other assessment tools, the eHLQ evaluates not only the individual-level competencies emphasized by the eHEALS and DHLI but also incorporates individuals’ experiential interactions with DHT and related service systems. It thus integrates evaluations across three levels: the individual, the system, and their interactions. However, the eHLQ remains a general instrument, with limited application specifically among nursing populations, and its suitability for nurses across different regions warrants further empirical validation.

Health literacy survey digital

Only one included study utilized the Health Literacy Survey Digital (HLS19-DIGI) to assess nurses’ DHL [38]. The HLS19-DIGI was developed as part of the European Health Literacy Survey, conducted from 2019 to 2021 under WHO leadership, aiming to evaluate and strengthen health literacy across diverse international populations [48]. Although based on the DHLI [43], the HLS19-DIGI aligns more closely with established general health literacy concepts and models [49]. Compared to the DHLI, the HLS19-DIGI introduces a dimension focused specifically on comprehending digitally accessed health information, expanding the coverage of digital health resources. The instrument comprises three subscales: HLS19-DIGI HI (8 items, Cronbach’s alpha = 0.89), HLS19-DIGI INT (2 items, Cronbach’s alpha = 0.80), and HLS19-DIGI DD (2 items). Currently, the HLS19-DIGI has been translated into 13 languages and validated in surveys involving 28,057 participants across 13 countries, demonstrating robust psychometric properties [11]. However, as a generic tool developed for general populations, large-scale reliability and validity testing specifically among nurses remains lacking. Thus, its applicability within nursing-specific contexts requires further examination.

Overall, the absence of nurse-specific DHL assessment instruments represents a significant measurement gap, highlighting the necessity of developing and validating context-sensitive tools tailored specifically for nursing practice.

The multifaceted role of nurses’ DHL

Factors associated with nurses’ DHL

Individual-level factors

Several individual-level characteristics were consistently associated with nurses’ DHL across included studies. Older age [33], higher educational attainment [33, 38], and higher levels of palliative care knowledge, attitudes, and practices [28] were positively associated with greater DHL. Nurses reporting prior experience in accessing online health information (OHI) or participation in eHealth literacy training also had higher DHL scores [37]. Moreover, professional attributes, such as superior nursing performance [34], showed positive associations with DHL [38] (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Psychological and behavioral characteristics were also frequently identified as correlates of DHL. Characteristics positively associated with DHL including confidence in using OHI for health education [37], digital habits and attitudes [38], and health-promoting behaviors [18, 31, 34], particularly in stress management, spiritual growth, and health responsibility. Conversely, stress [27] and information anxiety [13] were negatively correlated with DHL (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Interpersonal- and organizational-level factors

At the interpersonal level, three studies consistently indicated that positive interpersonal relationships were associated with higher nurses’ DHL [18, 31, 34]. At the organizational level, two studies explored related factors. Kritsotakis et al. [39] reported that supportive nursing practice environments were positively associated with DHL, particularly through collegial nurse-physician relationships and nurse participation in hospital affairs. Conversely, Isazadeh et al. [33] observed lower DHL among nurses working in busier hospital environments (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Outcomes associated with nurses’ DHL

Two studies investigated outcomes associated with nurses’ DHL. Chen et al. [12] reported that DHL was positively associated with nurses’ attitudes toward AI usage and was also linked to these attitudes via technology trust and perceived value. Ramadan et al. [40] found that nurses’ DHL was positively associated with alert response performance and system optimization behaviors, with additional indirect effects via perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use in path model (Fig. 3).

The path model-based mediating role of nurses’ digital health literacy and its associated outcomes

Reported model-based mediation of nurses’ DHL

A limited number of studies also explored mediating roles of DHL within specific psychosocial relationships. One study reported that DHL had a partial mediating effect in the path model between stress and anxiety [27], while another indicated that DHL showed mediation in the model for the association between information anxiety and nursing core competencies [13] (Fig. 3).

Enhancing strategies of nurses’ DHL

Only one study developed strategies specifically aimed at improving nurses’ DHL. Efthymiou et al. [41] conducted two rounds of e-Delphi consultations involving 20 experts from five different countries to create an eHealth literacy curriculum tailored explicitly for nursing students and staff. The curriculum included modules addressing general concepts of health literacy and eHealth literacy, practical techniques for identifying patients with limited health literacy, managing readability and feasibility issues, navigating eHealth challenges, and integrating health literacy principles with patient safety. This curriculum has undergone preliminary pilot testing with nursing personnel via the elily2.eu online platform [50]. However, the curriculum primarily emphasizes geriatric care and does not sufficiently address the needs of nurses working in other specialties. Additionally, although developed through literature review and expert consensus, it lacks an explicit theoretical underpinning and does not directly target known influencing factors of nurses’ DHL for intervention. Importantly, no empirical evaluation has been conducted to confirm the effectiveness of this curriculum; thus, its ability to significantly enhance nurses’ DHL remains uncertain. Moreover, specific details regarding the intervention’s format and duration were not provided. Additional, further empirical studies are required to examine its effectiveness and generalizability to diverse nursing settings.

Discussion

This scoping review systematically mapped the current global literature on nurses’ DHL and integrated influencing factors across multiple levels using the social ecological model to develop an analytical framework. Findings indicate that nurses generally possess insufficient levels of DHL worldwide. Moreover, a substantial gap exists regarding validated assessment instruments: only four generic tools have been employed to date, and no measurement instruments specifically reflecting the unique contexts and demands of nursing practice currently exist. Importantly, factors related to nurses’ DHL occur at individual, interpersonal, and organizational levels. The review also highlighted a mediating role of DHL in the path model at the individual level, which was closely associated with nurses’ attitudes and clinical behaviors. However, targeted interventions designed specifically to enhance nurses’ DHL remain extremely limited. These findings highlight critical gaps in theoretical development and practical implementation within existing literature and hold urgent implications for nursing practice, education, and policy-making. Therefore, there is an immediate need to develop nursing-specific DHL assessment tools aligned with nurses’ professional roles and responsibilities, as well as to design systematic, multilevel intervention strategies guided by an ecological framework.

DHT have become widely integrated into clinical nursing practice, with evidence suggesting potential benefits for nurses’ workloads and enhance care quality [51]. However, their effective utilization relies heavily on nurses having adequate levels of DHL. This review indicates that relevant research has gradually emerged only since 2018, with existing evidence consistently highlighting insufficient DHL among nurses. This finding contrasts with studies on healthcare professionals as a whole [19, 20], which typically report higher DHL levels. Analysis suggests that such studies often aggregate multiple professional groups, including physicians, pharmacists, and nurses, into combined analyses. Physicians and other healthcare professionals generally possess stronger foundational digital skills and higher educational attainment [52], potentially masking nurses’ true DHL status. This discrepancy underscores the importance of specifically developing and enhancing DHL among the nursing workforce. Additionally, the current lack of standardized measures for assessing nurses’ DHL is particularly concerning. The included studies employed varied indicators, such as qualification rates, proportions with high proficiency, and mean scores, complicating comparisons and synthesis across studies. This lack of standardization not only impedes meta-analyses but also restricts accurate DHL evaluation. Despite these limitations, the synthesis clearly demonstrates generally inadequate DHL among nurses, emphasizing the urgent need to establish a standardized assessment framework that aligns with the specific demands of the nursing profession.

This review identified only four instruments currently used for evaluating nurses’ DHL, all of which are generic scales primarily focused on individual-level assessments that do not adequately reflect the influence of nursing-specific work environments [39]. However, our analysis suggests that nurses’ DHL is associated with factors identified at individual, interpersonal, and organizational levels. Consequently, existing tools may fail to fully capture the complexity of nurses’ DHL in actual clinical practice. Recently, some studies have begun utilizing assessment instruments developed in the Web 2.0 era to measure nurses’ competencies in online interactions and co-creation of health information [13, 38]. Nevertheless, these instruments’ applicability to nursing populations remains limited, as they lack large-scale reliability and validity testing and have not been adapted for cross-cultural contexts. Additionally, emerging frameworks for assessing DHL in the Web 3.0 context have recently appeared [53], suggesting potential directions for future instrument development. More importantly, current generic scales generally exclude essential technology-interaction competencies increasingly critical in nursing practice, such as remote patient monitoring and mobile health applications [54]. Furthermore, DHL, as a dynamically evolving concept [9], has yet to be integrated within a comprehensive conceptual model specifically tailored to nursing professionals. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a nursing-specific DHL conceptual model aligned with nurses’ roles and tasks, and subsequently, to establish standardized assessment tools integrating Web 2.0 and Web 3.0 features and encompassing competencies related to emerging DHT.

This review finds that nurses’ DHL is associated with multiple factors and outcomes. It may serve as a model-based mediator at the individual level and is positively associated with various outcomes. Additionally, by adopting the social ecological model, this study developed an integrated framework to illustrate factors related to nurses’ DHL. Existing evidence predominantly emphasizes individual-level factors, including sociodemographic attributes, knowledge and experience, professional roles, attitudes, behaviors, and psychological states, consistent with prior research conducted among general populations [55, 56]. In addition, interpersonal interactions and the nursing work environment were identified as important factors associated with DHL [39]. Notably, approximately 44.4% of nurses reported a lack of systematic digital technology training and indicated that their institutions lacked dedicated technical support teams [3]. This highlights the necessity for hospitals to strengthen organizational support and cultivate supportive practice environments. However, few studies have explored interpersonal and organizational factors, while community- and policy-level influences remain unexplored. Evidence has shown that community resources, such as public facilities, are associated with higher DHL among older adults and vulnerable groups [57]. Therefore, future research should extend analytical perspectives to incorporate all five levels of the social ecological model, thereby comprehensively examining multilevel mechanisms. Additionally, most existing evidence derives from small-scale, cross-sectional studies, which fail to capture dynamic changes over time due to the lack of longitudinal data. Therefore, longitudinal studies combined with qualitative methods are urgently needed to deepen understanding of interactions among multilevel factors shaping nurses’ DHL. These efforts will provide robust evidence for developing multidimensional, personalized strategies to enhance nurses’ DHL.

Enhancing nurses’ DHL aligns not only with the WHO’s Global Strategy on Digital Health 2020–2025 [7] but also should be integrated into nursing education systems and healthcare policy agendas. However, this review finds that interventions specifically aimed at enhancing DHL among nurses are extremely limited, with no high-quality evidence confirming their effectiveness [41]. In contrast, studies targeting older adults, individuals with chronic conditions, and younger populations have shown that personalized interventions can significantly improve DHL [58]. These interventions encompass various modalities, including online courses on digital competencies and health information appraisal, small-group toolkits covering basic digital operations and information retrieval, and hybrid training combining group workshops and individual coaching [58]. Such approaches offer valuable insights transferable to the nursing context. However, existing interventions predominantly emphasize individual skill training [59], neglecting the embedded, context-specific nature of DHL within clinical settings. Given this study’s identification of influencing factors across individual, interpersonal, and organizational levels based on the social ecological model, future interventions should shift from narrow skill-oriented approaches towards comprehensive, multilevel coordinated support strategies.

This study, however, has several limitations. First, studies with mixed-professional samples were included when nurse-specific outcomes were explicitly reported to maximize the available evidence base. Although this approach increased the comprehensiveness of the review, the proportion of nurses varied across studies, and findings derived from studies with smaller nurse subgroups may be less stable and representative and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Second, the literature search was restricted to eight widely used English and Chinese databases, potentially excluding relevant research published in other languages or indexed in regional databases. Nevertheless, additional relevant studies were retrieved through supplementary manual searches. Moreover, the studies included in this review displayed significant heterogeneity, preventing a quantitative synthesis or meta-analysis regarding nurses’ DHL levels and related factors. Finally, this review employed only a scoping review methodology to summarize existing evidence, and did not include a rigorous quality appraisal of the primary studies.

Conclusion

This scoping review synthesized global evidence on nurses’ DHL, demonstrating that research in this area remains preliminary and that nurses generally exhibit inadequate DHL. Available measurement instruments are limited in number and predominantly generic, failing to adequately represent the diverse digital competencies nurses require in clinical practice. This review proposed an integrated analytical framework based on the social ecological model, capturing factors related to DHL at the individual, interpersonal, and organizational levels, and highlighting DHL’s multifaceted associations within this structure. However, strategies designed specifically to enhance nurses’ DHL remain extremely limited, with a notable lack of empirical evidence regarding their effectiveness. Considering these critical gaps, future research urgently needs to: 1) develop a conceptual model and measurement framework specifically tailored to nurses’ DHL; 2) create assessment tools incorporating Web 2.0 and Web 3.0 features relevant to nursing practice contexts; 3) employ longitudinal designs and qualitative methodologies to comprehensively explore multilevel interaction mechanisms; and 4) design and rigorously evaluate systematic, multilevel interventions based on an ecological perspective. This review offers a theoretical foundation and practical guidance to address these research gaps and to facilitate the advancement of nurses’ DHL.
