Proteolytic dysfunction in bovine digital dermatitis caused by Treponema spp. and the therapeutic use of non-antimicrobial matrix metalloproteinase inhibitors
Authors: Priyoshi Lahiri, Daniel Young, Regan Penner, Kyle Lesack, Angelica Petersen Dias, Rita Hannawayya, Jeroen De Buck, Karin Orsel, Morley D. Hollenberg, Francis Johnson, Lorne Golub, Joseph Scaduto, Antoine Dufour, Eduardo R. Cobo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Digital dermatitis (DD) is a painful hoof disease in cattle, closely linked to Treponema spp., for which the pathogenesis remains poorly understood and effective treatments are lacking. This study identifies a persistent pro-inflammatory proteomic signature in untreated, non-healing active DD lesions in the foot skin of cattle, characterized by increased immune cell infiltration and elevated matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activity. Treatment of active DD lesions with CMC2.24, a non-antibiotic MMP inhibitor, maintains the lesions in an M2 stage in most cases; however, it reduces histological dermatitis, lowering reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and downregulating proteomic keratinization pathways. Mechanistically, CMC2.24 dampens inflammatory IL-1β cell responses in bovine macrophages exposed to DD-associated T. phagedenis. Active DD lesions treated with oxytetracycline (OTC) antibiotics remain clinically similar to CMC2.24; however, they progress to chronic M4 stages, becoming keratinized and maintaining inflammatory features, including increased S100A8 expression and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. These findings integrate clinical and histological observations from treatments with conventional antibiotics and antibiotic-free protease inhibitors, highlighting novel therapies for managing DD and mitigating the exaggerated inflammatory response.

Introduction

Digital dermatitis (DD) is a skin disease in cattle that affects the interdigital bulb and the area around the coronary band of the feet1. It is characterized by painful, necrotic ulcers that can eventually result in chronic lesions2. Treponema spp.3–5, predominantly T. phagedenis6, are consistently detected in active and chronic DD lesions. Cattle affected by DD experience lameness and pain, which leads to reduced milk production, infertility, weight loss, and an increased risk of culling7,8. The clinical manifestations of DD are represented by a range of acute, ulcerative, chronic, or relapsed lesions in the feet7, classified into six M stages based on their macroscopic appearance2, with the ‘M’ standing for ‘Mortellaro,’ an Italian researcher who first identified DD in Italy9. M0 is normal skin; M1 denotes the formation of a small acute ulcerative area (<2 cm in diameter); M2 is represented by a larger and acute necrotic ulcerative lesion (>2 cm in diameter); M3 is a post-treatment healing lesion originating from M2 lesions after topical antibiotic treatment; M4 is a chronic or recurrent lesion2 and M4.1 is an M4 lesion combined with a painful and active M1 focus10. Stages M1, M2, and M4.1 are commonly associated with acute/active inflammation, with typical lesions circumscribed, moist, and prone to bleeding upon contact. Active M2 DD lesions usually do not heal well and progress to chronic lesions2. M4 is linked to chronic inflammation, and these long-term lesions may eventually heal or revert to active inflammation for reasons that remain unclear.

Clinical cases of DD are routinely managed with the topical application of the broad-spectrum antibiotic oxytetracycline (OTC) in powder or paste form11,12. The success rate of DD lesions treated with OTC in cattle is highly variable (9–73%)13,14. Indeed, we demonstrated that OTC reduces ulcerated DD in cattle and lowers the transcriptional expression of chemokine Cxcl-8 and the abundance of Treponema spp.15, but proinflammatory classical monocytes persist in the lesions16. Although topical antibiotics (e.g., OTC) remain a cornerstone for DD treatment, growing concerns about antimicrobial resistance and withdrawal times have prompted the use of non-antibiotic alternatives. DD lesions can be treated with chemical footbaths containing caustic chemicals, such as copper sulfate17,18. However, a standardized chemical footbath protocol with 5% copper II sulfate (CuSO4) only slightly reduced the prevalence of active DD lesions (<22%)7,8, favoring the encystation of Treponema spp. and the occurrence of chronic lesions19. Zinc and copper chelate-based products, such as Intra Hoof-fit gel, have also garnered considerable attention20. Yet, the absence of standardized treatment protocols, including optimal concentrations, exposure durations, and application frequencies, has hindered the consistent use of these products on farms21.

In search of antibiotic-free therapies for DD, we hypothesized that OTC may reduce overactive inflammation in DD lesions by reducing the synthesis of damaging matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), independent of its direct microbicidal effects22. This reasoning is based on the fact that doxycycline, a broad-spectrum, second-generation tetracycline antibiotic synthetically derived from oxytetracycline, modified to eliminate its toxicity and preserve its antibacterial efficacy, still retains the ability to modulate host responses and mitigate the production of MMPs23,24. For instance, doxycycline alleviates human periodontitis with abundant MMP-8 activity caused by T. denticola25,26. In addition, doxycycline showed inhibition of MMP activity, rather than microbicidal properties, in several non-infectious processes, such as corneal re-epithelialization in rabbits post-alkali-burning24, wound healing in diabetic rats27, rosacea28, and acne in humans29.

An advanced pleiotropic non-antimicrobial MMP inhibitor is CMC2.24, a chemically modified phenylamino carbonyl curcumin featuring a triketonic structure and demonstrating efficacy as a zinc and calcium binder. This compound offers more solubility and biological activity than natural curcumin, which has a diketonic structure30,31. CMC2.24 improves wound healing in diabetic rats32,33 and reduces local and systemic MMP-9 in periodontitis associated with Treponema spp.34, including those with naturally occurring periodontitis in dogs35. Mechanistically, it has been found that in the context of periodontitis, CMC2.24 can inhibit the secretion of inflammatory effectors (e.g., Il-1β, Tnf-α, Il-6, prostaglandin E2, MMP-9) and the activation of NF-κβ signaling pathway (p65) and p38 MAPK36. Thus, via MMP inhibition, CMC2.24 could therapeutically reduce inflammation and enhance wound healing in DD. However, the role of MMPs in DD remains unclear, and these MMP inhibitors have not yet been tested in cattle. In this study, we characterized the proteomic profile of active DD lesions, highlighting elevated MMP activity. We also tested a topical treatment with a non-antimicrobial MMP-inhibitor compared to a conventional antibiotic OTC in cattle with active DD lesions, demonstrating a comparable reduction in clinical outcomes and a reduction in dermatitis and the synthesis of inflammatory proteases.

Results

Active (M2) DD lesions display a distinct proinflammatory proteomic signature

We used a quantitative shotgun proteomic approach to describe the largely unknown proteomic profile in active (M2) DD lesions compared with healthy (M0) foot skin (Fig. 1A). From a total of 28 upregulated and 35 downregulated proteins identified in active (M2) DD lesions (Supplementary Data 1), we showed upregulated pathways related to wound healing and neutrophil degeneration (highlighted in red) and downregulated pathways associated with apoptosis and Il-18 signaling (highlighted in blue) (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Data 2). Upregulated proteins included lactotransferrin (LTF), lipocalin 2 (LCN2), and cathelicidin 1 and 2 (CATHL1 and 2), which clustered within the neutrophil degranulation reactome pathway (red nodes) and associated with innate immunity (light green nodes) and antibacterial (blue nodes) biological processes (Fig. 1C). Another enrichment pathway cluster in M2 DD lesions related to complement and coagulation cascade pathways (dark green nodes) and serine-protease inhibitory proteins, including SERPINA1, SERPINA3-1, SERPINA3-7, and inter-alpha-trypsin inhibitor heavy chain 4 (ITIH4) (yellow nodes) (Fig. 1C). CMC2.24 partially ameliorates clinically active (M2) DD lesions, whereas OTC induces transition to a chronic stage

Given the increased MMP activity in active (M2) DD lesions, we assessed whether a non-antimicrobial MMP inhibitor, CMC2.24, applied topically, could mitigate the disease in afflicted cows. We showed that 100% of the cows (6 out of 6) topically treated with a petroleum jelly vehicle (negative control group) remained with active (M2) lesions (Fig. S2A) and ulcerative DD lesions at 7 days post-treatment (Fig. 3A). Histologically, these cows showed persistent dermatitis with abundant infiltration of leukocytes in the dermis (Fig. 3A). Upon local treatment of active (M2) DD lesions with CMC2.24, 22% (2 out of 9) of the treated cows transitioned to chronic (M4) stages but 77% (7 out of 9) still displayed active M2 stage (Figs. 3B and S2B). Given that OTC is the standard antibiotic treatment administered to cattle affected by DD, we assessed differences between cows treated with OTC and those treated with CMC2.24. We showed 100% of the cows (6 out of 6) with active (M2) DD lesions treated with OTC transitioned to chronic (M4) stages (Figs. 3C and S2C). OTC-treated lesions still exhibited persistent leukocyte infiltration at 7 days post-treatment (Fig. 3C), consistent with our previous study16.

Neither CMC2.24 nor OTC reduces Treponema spp. burden in active (M2) DD lesions

We sought to determine if the mitigation of DD lesions after treatments with CMC2.24 and OTC was attributable to some microbicidal effects. The total and viable bacterial load in lesion swabs for both treatments showed no significant difference, with most detected DNA originating from viable bacteria (Fig. 4). T. phagedenis and T. medium were consistently detected in all 36 active (M2) DD lesions (100%) before and 7 days after each treatment. T. pedis was detected in 9 out of 12 M2 DD lesions, absent in one lesion from each treatment group. T. phagedenis, T. medium, and T. pedis displayed median increases of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.2 log units from 0 to 7 days post-treatment in vehicle-treated DD lesions (Table 1). CMC2.24 showed no direct microbicidal effects; live T. phagedenis, T. medium and T. pedis were detected in CMC2.24-treated active (M2) DD lesions (Fig. 4). The median counts of T. phagedenis, T. medium, and T. pedis in CMC2.24-treated active (M2) DD lesions decreased by 0.2, 0.02, and 0.26 log units, respectively, from 0 to 7 days post-treatment but without statistical significance (Table 1). In active (M2) DD lesions treated with OTC, T. phagedenis, T. medium and T. pedis were not as frequently found (Fig. 4). However, the median counts of T. phagedenis, T. medium, and T. pedis decreased by 2.1, 2.8, and 2.8 log units post-treatment, but without statistical significance (Table 1). OTC treatment promotes cornification in active (M2) DD lesions

To assess if conventional antibiotic treatments in DD could resolve the overall skin immune and inflammatory deviation, we analyzed the global proteomes in M2-DD lesions before and after OTC treatment. OTC-treated lesions displayed 80 upregulated proteins and 67 downregulated (Supplementary Data 6). Pathway enrichment analysis showed that two of the highly enriched pathways in OTC-treated M2-lesions were the formation of a cornified envelope and integrin I pathway (highlighted in red, Fig. 7A). Most upregulated proteins in OTC-treated M2 DD lesions were keratins and collagens (KRT 9,31,14,83,85,75, 71 and COLA11, COL1A2) (Supplementary Data 7). STRING-db analysis confirmed that the largest cluster of highly enriched proteins of OTC-treated M2-DD lesions included those associated with epidermis development (green nodes), cornification (red nodes), tissue development (blue nodes), and keratinization (yellow nodes) (Fig. 7B). The highly enriched pathways in counterpart untreated M2 lesions were complement and coagulation cascades and inflammatory and antibacterial defences (highlighted in blue, Fig. 7A). Moreover, several proinflammatory proteins were highly enriched in parallel untreated M2 lesions, including complement protein (C5), which is involved in the negative regulation of macrophage chemotaxis, and LTF, LCN2 and azurocidin 1 (AZU1) (Supplementary Data 7). Thus, shotgun proteomic analysis of OTC-treated DD lesions revealed that keratinization and subsequent cornification-related proteins were highly enriched. CMC2.24 and OTC induce distinct proteases and proteolytic signatures in active (M2) DD lesions

We assessed proteases and substrates enriched in CMC2.24 or OTC-treated active (M2) DD lesions compared with untreated lesions by N-terminomics/TAILS profiling (Fig. 8A). We identified 43 unique N-termini elevated in CMC2.24-treated DD lesions (Fig. 8B, Supplementary Data 8). The proteolytic cleavage sites displayed arginine, serine, alanine, or glycine enrichment at P1 (the amino acid one position upstream of the cleavage site) (Fig. 8B). In OTC-treated lesions, we showed elevated 54 N-termini proteins with alanine or leucine at P1 (Fig. 7C, Supplementary Data 8). Both CMC2.24 and OTC-treated lesions showed enrichment in serine and alanine at the P1’ site (the amino acids downstream of the cleavage) (Fig. 8B, C). CMC2.24 suppresses proinflammatory cytokine responses in macrophages challenged with DD-associated T. phagedenis

We showed live T. phagedenis or its bacterial structural components collected by sonication stimulated significant Il-1β, Cxcl-1, and TNFα secretion in murine bone marrow-derived LPS-stimulated macrophages (Fig. 9A–C). Mostly sonicated T. phagedenis induced Il-1β in bovine blood-derived LPS-stimulated macrophages (Fig. 9D). Remarkably, CMC2.24 reduced the secretion of Il-1β, Cxcl-1, and Tnf-α to basal levels in murine bone marrow-derived macrophages exposed to both live and sonicated DD-associated T. phagedenis (Fig. 9A–C). Likewise, CMC2.24 suppressed Il-1β secretion in bovine macrophages challenged with DD-associated T. phagedenis (Fig. 9D). Discussion

To further understand the underlying disease pathophysiology and recrudescence of DD lesions, we characterized the proteomic profile of the DD lesions to show that active (M2) DD lesions displayed a prominent proinflammatory proteomic signature with the upregulation of neutrophil degranulation pathways. These mechanisms would explain the role of infiltrating neutrophils and impaired wound healing in active DD9,15. Critical neutrophil proteins, including lactotransferrin and cathelicidins, were identified in active (M2) DD lesions, which may exert antimicrobial activity released from the secretory granules of neutrophils37 but do not prevent further inflammatory relapses. Bovine lactotransferrin is microbicidal against mastitis pathogens and activates neutrophil superoxide production38. Moreover, lactotransferrin is found on neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and is associated with neutrophil degranulation39. Bovine cathelicidins are mainly released from neutrophils and protect against mastitis40. Thus, the increased expression of these antimicrobial proteins in active (M2) DD lesions indicates an early neutrophil response with enriched neutrophil degranulation pathways. Proteomic results also suggest an ongoing wound-healing process in DD lesions that continues this early neutrophil response. C1q C chain protein, which initiates the classical complement pathway41 and regulates the differentiation of monocytes/macrophages to phagocytes specialized in resolving inflammation42, was abundant in active (M2) DD lesions. Due to the persistence of highly phagocytosing classical monocytes/macrophages (M1) in DD lesions16, a local milieu rich in C1q C is critical to dampen inflammation and promote wound healing. However, the persistent nature of DD lesions and subsequent transition through chronic (M4) stages underscore deficiencies in the healing of active wounds. In this regard, we revealed a dysregulation of proteolytic enzymes, characterized by heightened MMP activity in active (M2) DD lesions, which may contribute to impaired wound healing mechanisms. For instance, MMP-2, -8, and -9 are critically elevated in chronic wounds43,44. The continuous tissue destruction of epidermal layers observed in DD coincides with aberrant MMP activity and proteolytic imbalance in active DD lesions. In a mechanism to regulate such neutrophil inflammatory responses, abundant serine protease inhibitors, including serpin family-A member 1, -B member 4, and inter-alpha-trypsin inhibitor heavy chain ITH 4, that target neutrophil proteolytic enzymes45, were found in active (M2) DD lesions. Serine protease inhibitor serpin 1 is a primary neutrophil elastase (NE)46 inhibitor, and ITIH4 is associated with extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis47. However, suppressed NE activity in polymicrobial wound communities, as depicted in DD, may not be beneficial and could impede the killing of opportunistic spirochaetes. Overproduction of MMPs and neutrophil infiltration have been observed in other non-healing inflammatory processes, such as pyoderma gangrenosum infection in skin48 and wound healing in diabetic mice49. Likewise, a pro-inflammatory transcriptomic profile with abundance of serine proteases and MMPs was reported in cows with DD50. Indeed, active (M2) DD stages corresponded to downregulated gene expression of protease inhibitors, such as serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 6 (SPINK6) and serine protease inhibitors (SERPIN)50, indicating a dysregulated protease/antiprotease balance in DD and the potential beneficial roles of exogenous protease inhibitors. Thus, wounds inflicted in active (M2) DD lesions could remain colonized by Treponema spp. due to the presence of serine proteases, resulting in altered host response and perpetuation of the inflammatory phase.

After demonstrating that active M2 DD exhibited high MMP activity, we sought to determine whether an MMP inhibitor could mitigate persistent inflammation. We assessed the ability of a locally applied non-antimicrobial MMP inhibitor, CMC2.24, to minimize bovine DD’s progression and modulate the inflammatory and proteomic profile of the afflicted foot skin lesions, compared to standard antibiotic OTC therapy. Macroscopic observations revealed that 100% of OTC-treated active DD lesions progressed to the chronic M4 stage. In contrast, fewer cows with active DD M2 lesions treated with CMC2.24 showed lesions transitioning to the M4 stage, while most of the cows remained with active lesions after topical application. Histological assessment showed reduced inflammatory cell infiltration in CMC2.24-treated lesions, which was not the case in OTC-treated active lesions. These results evidence that the topical application of OTC in M2-DD lesions shifted skin proteomic profiling toward a more pro-cornification signature, where members of the structural protein family keratins involved in the formation of cornified layers were upregulated. Thus, this antibiotic treatment facilitated keratinocyte differentiation and the formation of a new epidermal layer during DD healing. The observed keratinization and enrichment of cornification-related proteins in OTC-treated DD lesions could explain the formation of a new outermost layer of the skin (stratum corneum) and the thick scab formation in lesions treated with antibiotics14. We also observed that OCT treatment downregulated inflammatory and bacterial responses, as well as complement cascade-related pathways, further facilitating inflammation resolution and wound healing. However, trafficking protein particle complex subunit 9, which is known to upregulate NFκβ transcription factor activity51, was upregulated in OTC-treated M2-DD lesions. Significantly, the expression of S100A8, which constitutes approximately 45% of cytoplasmic proteins in neutrophils52, was increased in OTC-treated lesions. It has been reported that excessive S100A8 expression amplifies the inflammatory response by accelerating the release of cytokines by neutrophils and macrophages, thereby inducing a vicious cycle and exacerbating the disorder53. These proteins are also known to stimulate the TLR4–MyD88 cascade to enhance the expression of IL-1β mRNA in macrophages54. In addition, after infection with bacteria, neutrophils and monocytes/ macrophages secrete S100A8 to induce the production of ROS and NO52. Taken together, these findings can explain the variability in the clinical outcome of OTC treatment, as specific pro-inflammatory signatures, namely S100A8, were retained after treatment, which coincides with the persistence of pro-inflammatory classical monocytes/macrophages in OTC-treated lesions16. Importantly, CMC2.24-treated DD lesions showed a proteomic signature with downregulated ROS response pathways, in addition to higher enrichment in the collagen deposition and ECM regulation pathways. The MMP inhibitor and the antibiotic displayed distinct proteolytic signatures with enrichment for R, S and A amino acids in P1 in CMC2.24-treated DD lesions and A and L in OTC-treated lesions. Mechanistically, MMP inhibitor CMC2.24 suppressed inflammatory IL-1β responses in bovine macrophages against DD-associated T. phagedenis. Neither the MMP inhibitor nor OTC showed a significant microbicidal effect against the most common DD-associated Treponema spp., suggesting that their protective effects might primarily rely on immunomodulation of skin inflammation.

Since CMC2.24 and doxycycline have not been approved for use in cattle, these results suggest that these compounds could eventually be used as novel antibiotic-free MMP inhibitor treatments for bovine DD, helping to reduce harmful skin damage and inflammation. Studies involving a larger number of affected cattle and various farm settings should validate these findings before their application in bovine clinics. For instance, CMC2.24 was tested at a concentration of 30 mg/mL and applied to a dose of ~150 mg per lesion. Other doses and frequencies may yield different outcomes that need to be explored. The selected OTC treatment (5 g) falls within the common standards in the dairy cattle industry for topical treatment of foot lesions55. OTC, as a paste or in powdered form, has been used at dose levels ranging from 2 to 25 g per affected foot55,56. OTC is generally applied evenly to cover the wound surface56. However, the application relies on visual approximation, which is expected to result in a lack of accuracy. Thus, ensuring precise dosage and consistent application is difficult in field conditions, and this potential variability in the amounts applied to the lesions can lead to differences in the outcomes. Still, these amounts of OTC exceed the CMC2.24 quantity used in this study (150 mg), necessitating further testing of comparable doses to ensure an accurate interpretation of the compound’s therapeutic potential. The antimicrobial nature of the copper sulfate footbaths used on the selected farm may have unintentionally led to environmental antibiotic resistance, where metals like copper and zinc select for bacteria that are resistant to both metals and antibiotics57, as well as changes in the local skin microbiome58, which were not addressed in this study.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that active M2 lesions exhibit higher MMP activity than healthy bovine skin. Local treatment of active M2 DD lesions with antibiotic OTC or MMP-inhibitor CMC2.24 demonstrated a similar cure rate. However, OTC treatment tended to progress to a chronic M4 stage, whereas CMC2.24 remained lesion active but with reduced histologic dermatitis and synthesis of damaging proteases. Requiring further clinical testing, this proof-of-concept study suggests that MMP inhibitors may serve as an alternative for controlling DD, either alone or in combination with other existing treatments.

Methods

Animal ethical approvals

We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Research studies in adult female cattle (ACC20-0039) and 6–8-week-old C57BL/6 male mice (ACC24-0045) were done following the Canadian Guidelines for Animal Welfare (CGAW) and the Veterinary Sciences Animal Care Committee of the University of Calgary, consented by cattle owners and reported following ARRIVE guidelines https://arriveguidelines.org/arrive-guidelines.

Foot skin biopsies from cattle

A dairy farm in Alberta, Canada, was selected for this study based on the relatively high within-herd prevalence (12%) of active lesions and close collaboration with an experienced hoof trimmer (member of Western Canadian Certified Hoof Trimmer Association (https://www.wcchta.com/). The routine hoof trimming procedure included the disinfection of all equipment used between farms. In addition, knives were cleaned between cows with paper towel and dipped in disinfectants following procedure for preventing leukosis transmission (https://inspection.canada.ca/en/animal-health/terrestrial-animals/diseases/accredited-veterinarian-s-manual/chapter-9). An expert veterinarian and epidemiologist, Dr. Karin Orsel, visually classified the lesions based on a six-stage system2,7: M0, normal skin; M1, presence of small active ulcers (<2 cm); M2, larger ulcers (>2 cm); M3, a healing lesion; M4, a chronic lesion2; and M4.1, an M4 lesion combined with active M1 ulcers10. Cows were routinely walked through footbaths containing 5% copper sulfate. Cows identified with no lesions (M0) and untreated active M2 lesions were randomly allocated into three topical treatment groups: (a) powdered CMC-2.24 (Traverse Biosciences) suspended in petroleum jelly (n: 9, 3%, 30 mg/mL of the vehicle per cow), (b) vehicle (petroleum jelly) only (n: 6) and, (c) OTC (n: 6) (5 g of powder; DIN 00719315, Bio Agri Mix per treatment)59. The OTC paste treatment consisted of 175 mL of propylene glycol, 175 mL of vinegar, and 150 g of tetracycline hydrochloride, which was applied directly to the lesion using a paintbrush60. Two skin biopsies were taken from each cow’s feet before (day 0) and 7 days post-treatment (day 7). These biopsies were taken using a 4-mm biopsy punch (Miltex, Integra Life Sciences Corporation) in cows previously locally anesthetized with a subcutaneous injection of lidocaine (2 mL). After the procedure, the biopsied area was bandaged with medical-grade gauze and checked daily for signs of lameness.

Foot skin samples were fixed in 10% formalin for histological evaluation or immediately frozen in dry ice and stored at −80 °C for MMP activity assays and proteomic analysis. For quantifying bacterial Treponeme spp., swabs were taken as follows. After restraining the cow in the trimming chute, the lesion was rinsed with tap water and dried using paper towels to confirm the M stage based on its size and appearance. Before sampling, local anesthesia was achieved by subcutaneously injecting lidocaine (3 mL, Lidocaine HCl 2%, Zoetis Canada Inc.). The lesion was verified to be dry before sampling to prevent any water from being absorbed, ensuring that the sample was representative of the lesion. A dry, sterile cotton swab (Medline) was placed perpendicular to the center of the lesion and rotated for 10 s with enough pressure to collect lesion fluid. Two swabs from lesions were collected on days 0 and 7 post-treatment. One cotton tip was placed into a dry microcentrifuge tube to quantify total genomic DNA (gDNA). The other swab was inserted into a microcentrifuge tube containing 400 µL of anaerobic transport medium (ATM, Starswab Anaerobic Transport System) for live bacterial gDNA quantification. All samples were kept on ice, immediately transported to the laboratory, and underwent processing upon arrival.

Histological evaluation of DD lesions in cattle

Foot skin biopsies fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin were paraffin-embedded (CA15159-409L; VWR), cut (5 μm), and stained with hematoxylin (6765008; Thermofisher) and eosin (6766008; Thermofisher) (H&E). Inflammation in the epidermis and dermis was blindly graded using a morphometric scale as follows: 0 = no leukocytes, 1 = occasional infiltration of leukocytes, 2 = focal infiltration of leukocytes, 3 = coalescing leukocytes and 4 = diffused infiltration of leukocytes61. The counting average of five random fields of view from each slide (one slide per animal) was reported per cow.

MMP activity assay in bovine foot skin

Frozen foot skin biopsies were lysed using HBSS and 1% NP40 solution (28324; Thermofisher Scientific) in multi-lysing bead mix tubes (CKmix Tubes; Bertin Corp Tissue Homogenizing CKMix; 50-154-2938; Fisher Scientific) and spinning in a rotor (5 min, 4 °C) followed by centrifugation (1000 × g, 5 min) to obtain supernatants with all the proteins, including MMPs. Protein content was determined by a bicinchoninic (BCA) protein assay (Pierce 23225; Thermofisher Scientific). MMP activity was determined in the supernatants using fluorogenic peptide substrates (Mca-KPLG/L-Dpa-AR-NH2; ES010, R&D) specific for MMPs -1, -2, -7, -8, -9, -13 and -14, which feature a highly fluorescent 7-methoxy coumarin group quenched by resonance energy transfer to the 2,4-dinitrophenyl group. These peptide substrates were used (10 μM) to measure MMP activities, cleaving amide bonds between the fluorescent and quencher groups and emitting fluorescence (“quench fluorescence”). Fluorescence increases were measured in a fluorescence plate reader with excitation at 320 nm and emission at 405 nm. MMP activities were calculated as international units (IU) of enzymes (pM/min/μg).

Global proteomic profile in bovine foot skin lesions

Frozen foot skin biopsies collected for proteomics were lysed using 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) (0227–100 g; VWR) and 0.1 M EDTA (BDH9232-500 g; VWR) in 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) (200 nM, pH 8). Proteins were quantified by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay before adding protease inhibitors (11836153001; Sigma) and normalized to 100 µg of total protein. Proteins were denatured with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) for 30 min at 37 °C, alkylated with 15 mM iodoacetamide (Rpn6302v; VWR) in the dark for 25 min at room temperature with pH adjusted to 6.5 with hydrochloric acid (HCl) (46414-463; VWR) and digested overnight at 37 °C with trypsin at a ratio of 1:10 trypsin:total protein. Samples were incubated with isotopically heavy [40 mM 13CD2O + 20 mM NaBH3CN (sodium cyanoborohydride)] (41860-360; VWR) or light labels [40 mM light formaldehyde (CH2O) + 20 mM NaBH3CN] (1CCDLM4599-1MLOFS; ACP chemicals) for 18 h at 37 °C and subjected to C18 chromatography followed by liquid chromatography and tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Experiments were carried out using an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid MS (Thermo Scientific) operated with Xcalibur (4.0.21.10) and coupled to a Thermo Scientific Easy-nLC (nanoflow liquid chromatography) 1200 system (Southern Alberta Mass Spectrometry facility (SAMS), University of Calgary). Spectral data were matched to a bovine protein FASTA file obtained from UniProt on May 16, 2024 (Andromeda algorithm implemented in the MaxQuant software, v.1.6.10.23) at a peptide-spectrum match false discovery rate (FDR) of <0.01. Significant outlier cutoff values were determined after log2 transformation by boxplot-and-whiskers analysis using Excel62. For the interpretation, log2 values > 1 represented proteins highly enriched in M2 DD lesions, and <−1 represented proteins highly enriched in healthy (M0) skin.

Frozen foot skin biopsies were also subjected to quantitative proteomic analysis using Tandem mass tag (TMT) 6-plex labeling (TMTsixplex™ Isobaric Label Reagent Set, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Biopsies were lysed in a buffer composed of 1% SDS, 200 mM HEPES (pH 8.0), 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate, 10 mM EDTA and protease inhibitor complete tablets (Roche, 4693159001). Disulfide bonds of total protein (100 μg) were reduced with 10 mM Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C for 1 h. Proteins were alkylated by incubation with 15 mM iodoacetamide (VWR) for 25 min in the dark at room temperature and precipitated out of solution by adding ice-cold acetone (600 μL) and incubating at −20 °C overnight. Samples were centrifuged (8000 × g, 10 min), resuspended in 50 mM triethyl ammonium bicarbonate (100 μL) and trypsinized (Thermo Fisher Scientific) overnight at a 1:10 enzyme-to-substrate ratio. For (TMT) 6-plex labeling, 0.8 mg of TMT reagent was resuspended in acetonitrile63 (41 μL), and samples were spun down quickly (380 × g, 10 s) and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The labeling reaction was quenched by adding 5% hydroxylamine (8 μL) and incubating for 15 min at 25 °C. Peptides with different labels were combined before 100% formic acid was added to each sample to reach a volumetric concentration of 1% formic acid. Samples were spun (2350 × g, 10 min) and desalted using Sep-Pak C18 columns (Waters, 130 mg WAT023501). Sep-Pak columns were conditioned with 1 × 3 mL 90% methanol/0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and 1 × 2 mL 0.1% formic acid. Each sample was loaded onto a column and washed with 1 × 3 mL 0.1% TFA/5% methanol. Peptides were eluted off the column with 1 × 1 mL 50% acetonitrile (ACN)/0.1% formic acid and lyophilized. Peptides were resuspended in 1% formic acid, and a BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to determine the peptide concentration in each sample. Samples were dried down and stored at −80 °C.

All liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry experiments were performed on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated with Xcalibur (v4.0.21.10) and coupled to a Thermo Scientific Easy-nLC (nanoflow Liquid Chromatography) 1200 system63. Tryptic peptides (2 μg) were loaded onto a C18 trap (75 μm × 2 cm; Acclaim PepMap 100, P/N 164946; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a flow rate of 2 μL/min of solvent A (0.1% formic acid and 3% acetonitrile in LC mass spectrometry grade water). Peptides were eluted using a 120-min gradient from 5% to 40% (5–28% in 105 min followed by an increase to 40% B in 15 min) of solvent B (0.1% formic acid in 80% LC–mass spectrometry grade acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 0.3 μL/min and separated on a C18 analytical column (75 μm × 50 cm; PepMap RSLC C18; P/N ES803; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were electro-sprayed using 2.3 kV voltage into the ion transfer tube (300 °C) of the Orbitrap Lumos operating in positive mode. The Orbitrap performed a full mass spectrometry scan at a resolution of 120,000 FWHM to detect the precursor ion having a mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) between 375 and 1575 and a +2 to +4 charges. The Orbitrap AGC (Auto Gain Control) and maximum injection times were set at 4 × 105 and 50 ms, respectively. The Orbitrap was operated in the top speed mode with a 3-s cycle time for precursor selection. The most intense precursor ions presenting a peptidic isotopic profile and having an intensity threshold of at least 2e4 were isolated using the quadrupole (isolation window of m/z 0.7) and fragmented with HCD (38% collision energy) in the ion routing Multipole. The fragment ions (MS2) were analyzed in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 15,000. The AGC, the maximum injection time and the first mass were set at 1 × 105, 105 ms and 100, respectively. Dynamic exclusion was enabled for 45 s to avoid the acquisition of the same precursor ion having a similar m/z (plus or minus 10 parts per million).

N-terminal TAILS

Frozen foot skin biopsies were subjected to N-terminomics/TAILS64–69. Total sample biomass from the biopsies was required for the data collection. Protein lysates (1 mg) were diluted in a final concentration of 3 M guanidine HCI (pH 8.0), 100 mM HEPES (pH 8.0), and 10 mM. Samples were incubated in 10 mM of dithiothreitol (DTT) for 1 h at 37 °C, and once cooled to room temperature (RT), the alkylation of the sample was achieved by incubation with a final concentration of 15 mM iodoacetamide for 20 min in the dark at room temperature. The pH was adjusted to 6 with HCl. To label peptide α- and ε-amines, samples were incubated for 18 h at 37 °C with isotopically heavy [40 mM13 CD2O + 20 mM NaBH3CN (sodium cyanoborohydride)] or light labels [40 mM light formaldehyde (CH2O) + 20 mM NaBH3CN], all at final concentrations. Proteins were precipitated in acetone/methanol, washed four times in methanol and trypsinized overnight at 37 °C, a ratio of 1:10 trypsin:total protein. Negative selection against unlabeled α-amines of trypsinized peptides was conducted by incubating the samples with a 3-fold excess (w/w) of dendritic polyglycerol aldehyde polymer (http://flintbox.com/public/project/1948/) and 1 M NaBH3CN for 18 h at 37 °C with pH 6. A total of 10 μL of 1 M NaBH3CN was added for each 1 mg of protein to ensure catalysis of the binding reaction of the polymer to free α-amines of peptides. Unbound peptides were separated from polymer-bound peptides using centrifugal filter units with 10-kDa cutoff membranes (Amicon, Millipore). The TAILS polymer was washed with 100 μL of 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate. The polymer-bound peptides on the filter were discarded. The filtrate was collected, acidified with 100% acetic acid to pH < 3.0, and stored on C18 Stage tips until analysis. This TAILS method69 has been validated, verifying that peptides are labeled with a dimethylated-modified peptide of +28 Da for the light label and +34 Da for the deuterated/heavy label, with a labeling efficiency of 90-99%. As the lysines are dimethylated in TAILS, the addition of trypsin generates semi-ArgC-specific peptides and not typical Trypsin/P specificity. Thus, we ensured that some peptides were cut, specifically after arginine, and that the polymer pulldown was successful, with no polymer remaining after C18 chromatography.

For proteomic data and bioinformatics analysis, spectral data were matched to peptide sequences in the bovine UniProt protein database using the Andromeda algorithm70 as implemented in the MaxQuant71 software (v.1.6.0.1). The peptide-spectrum match false discovery rate < 0.05 was used. Search parameters included a mass tolerance of 20 ppm for the parent ion, 0.5 Da for the fragment ion, carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues (+57.021464 Da), variable N-terminalacetylation (+42.010565 Da), and variable methionine oxidation (+15.994915 Da). The cleavage site specificity was set to trypsin/P (search for free N-terminus and only lysines), with up to two missed cleavages allowed. N-terminal and lysine heavy (+34.063116 Da) and light (+28.031300 Da) dimethylation were defined as labels for relative quantification. The cleavage site specificity was set to semi-specific free N-terminus (ArgC) for the TAILS data and to semi-specific (ArgC) for the preTAILS data, with up to two missed cleavages allowed for all queries (Supplementary Data 10–12). Data integration for pathway and gene ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis was performed using Metascape72 and STRING-db (https://string-db.org)73.

Models of cultured bovine and murine macrophages exposed to Treponema spp

Monocytes were purified from peripheral blood (5 mL) drawn from the jugular vein of healthy adult cows. Whole blood, collected in lithium-heparin-coated Vacutainer tubes (95057-405; VWR), was centrifuged (1600 × g, 20 min, deceleration-0), and a buffy coat was obtained and diluted in cold PBS (1:1) and layered over histopaque (10771; Sigma Aldrich). After spinning (800 × g, 3 min, deceleration-0) to separate the interphase (buffy coat) containing mononuclear cells, the buffy coat layer was harvested, treated with red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer (1X, 10 min, on ice), and spun (300 × g, 5 min). The pellet was collected, resuspended in cold PBS, filtered (50 μm), and counted in a hemocytometer by trypan blue staining. CD14+ cells were magnetically separated using CD14 microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec; 130-050-201). Isolated CD14+monocytes were incubated (24-well plates) in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 80 μg/mL tylosin (all from Sigma Aldrich) for 6 days, with the medium being replaced every other day until differentiation into adherent prophagocytic cells.

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMMs) were isolated from mice using the Cold Spring Harbor protocol74. Mice were euthanized, and the bone marrow was collected from the femur and centrifuged (300 × g, 5 min). The resulting bone marrow pellet was suspended in BMM medium, composed of RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (15140-122, Life Technology), 0.1% β-2-mercaptoethanol (15140-122, Life Technology), and 10% mouse fibroblast (L-929)- conditioned media. The latter was used as a macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) source and included mouse fibroblasts (L-929; 5 × 105) seeded in a T75 culture flask with 15–50 mL of BMM medium without FBS. The supernatant containing the stimulating factors was collected and filtered twice, initially with a 45-μm filter (97066-206, VWR) and then with a 20-μm filter (97066-200, VWR). Bone marrow was seeded in 24-well plates with BMM for 6 days to promote differentiation. Every 2 days, unattached cells were removed by washing with PBS (1X), and BMM media was replaced. After 6 days, the cells were fully differentiated into macrophages based on the attachment of the BMM monolayer to the bottom of the flask. Pro-inflammatory phenotype of the differentiated macrophages was confirmed by their secretion of Il-1β upon stimulation with LPS (1 µg/mL) for 1 h.

For the in vitro challenge, bovine and murine BMMs were pre-treated with CMC2.24 (Traverse Biosciences) (10 μM; 18 h) before exposing them to live and sonicated T. phagedenis16 (MOI 10; 4 h) isolated from active (M2) DD lesions. For incubation with live bacteria, T. phagedenis cultures were harvested to a concentration of 1 × 107 CFU/mL (optical density OD550nm = 0.1), pelleted by centrifugation (7500 × g, 10 min) and resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium. For sonicated T. phagedenis, bacteria were pelleted (7500 × g, 10 min), washed twice in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, LS10010031; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and resuspended in RPMI 1640 media before sonication (30% amplitude; 30 s intervals; 15 s off for a total of 7 min). Disruption of bacterial integrity was confirmed by darkfield microscopy and lack of growth when cultured. Secretions of murine Tnf-α, Il-1β and Cxcl-1 were quantified in cell supernatants by ELISAs (DY410-05, DY410-05, DY452-05 and DY453-05, respectively; R&D Systems). Bovine Il-1β was determined in cell supernatants by the bovine Il-β ELISA kit (ESS0027; Invitrogen). A standard curve was obtained for each set of samples to quantify the secreted cytokines.

Treponema spp. genomic quantification

We used current quantification methods for DD-Treponema spp. that rely on culture-independent real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)6, targeting unique genes for Treponema phagedenis, Treponema medium, and Treponema pedis (Supplementary Data 9)6. Total bacterial gDNA was quantified using qPCR (CFX96 real-time system; Bio-Rad Laboratories) after regular extraction from one of the swabs (DNeasy Blood & Tissue, Qiagen). To quantify live bacterial gDNA, the other swab was treated with a non-specific endonuclease, DNase I, before extraction to digest the DNA from dead/lysed bacteria75. In brief, the swabs were incubated with DNase I (0.2 U/µL), ATL buffer (180 µL), and MgCl2 (Thermo Scientific) at 37 °C for 10 min. Proteinase K (20 µL) was added to deactivate DNase I activity to a final concentration of 0.24 µg/µL, followed by incubation at 54 °C 1000 rpm for 1 h. To confirm the effectiveness of the DNase/PK treatment, T. medium gDNA was used as a control, treated or untreated with DNase/PK. Live bacterial gDNA was extracted following the DNeasy Blood & Tissue kit protocol, eluted in ultrapure water (100 µL), and stored at −20 °C until its use in qPCR. Each qPCR reaction included extraction and non-template controls. Each reaction included a 10-fold serial dilution of known concentrations of plasmid template DNA containing the target sequences to generate a standard curve for bacterial gDNA quantification6. The initial concentrations of the plasmids were measured using the Qubit dsDNA HS Kit (Invitrogen), with DNA copy numbers calculated based on the detected DNA amount (ng) and the template length (bp). The results are presented as genome copy numbers per swab, obtained by multiplying the copy numbers per µL of the sample by the total elution volume.

Statistics and reproducibility

Analytical data, shown as bar charts, box and whisker plots, and line diagrams, were obtained as mean values, with bars indicating standard error of the mean (SEM) from two independent experiments. These involved cows manifesting either no lesions (M0) or untreated active M2 lesions, and then treated topically with CMC-2.24 (Traverse Biosciences), vehicle (petrolatum), or OTC, with a total of n: 6–9 cows per group. Experiments using foot skin biopsies were run in triplicate unless stated otherwise. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk (Royston) test. All comparisons, except the bacterial quantification, were performed using a two-sided unpaired Student’s t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a post hoc Tukey’s test for multiple group comparisons, or a non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test76. For bacterial quantification, copy numbers of gDNA were log10-transformed and compared between day 0 and day 7 using the Wilcoxon rank test in R (v4.4.2). A p-value was assigned to each group, regarding the control group, unless explicitly indicated on the graph. Statistical significance was set at p-value < 0.05. All statistical analyses, except for bacterial quantification and proteomic analysis, were performed using the GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad 5.0).
