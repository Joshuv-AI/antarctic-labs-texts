A cluster randomized trial to evaluate the effects of a quality cluster-based intervention on the use of prostate-specific antigen test in general practice—a study protocol
Authors: Pernille Hølmkjær, Christian Leick, Thomas Drivsholm, Volkert Siersma, Flemming Bro, Marius Brostrøm Kousgaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContinuous quality improvement is an important endeavor for keeping clinical practice up to date with research evidence and professional guidelines for the sake of patients and the rational use of system resources. Quality clusters are a framework for quality improvement in general practice, but the effects of working in quality clusters have not been evaluated in randomized trials. The use of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) tests in general practice represents a clinically relevant area for quality improvement due to recent guideline revisions, practice variation, and concerns about overuse. The objective of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of a quality cluster-based intervention on the use of PSA tests in general practice.MethodsThe study is a cluster randomized controlled trial with an intervention group and a control group—receiving the intervention delayed—based in quality clusters in Denmark. We aim to include 50 quality clusters, each quality cluster having on average 13 general practice clinics. The intervention period is six months, and the total study period is 12 months. Randomization is 1:1 to intervention and control by a computer algorithm stratified by the five Danish health regions and cluster size. The intervention includes a cluster package about prostate-specific antigen used at a quality cluster meeting with data on use of prostate-specific antigen-tests, information on clinical guidelines, videos with clinicians, reflection questions, and hand-out materials to support implementation of changes to clinical practice. The control group proceeds with cluster work as usual and will receive the intervention delayed. Data for the primary and secondary outcomes will be obtained through registries and will be assessed at the end of the trial.DiscussionWe anticipate that the intervention will encourage general practitioners to reflect on their use of PSA testing and adjust their practice in accordance with the clinical guidelines if needed, and that this will result in an overall reduction in the use of the test.Trial registrationClinicaltrials.gov NCT06748456. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

General practitioners (GP) are often the first healthcare professionals patients consult, especially in countries like Denmark, where the GP functions as a gatekeeper [1]. It is imperative for GPs, to the best of their knowledge, to discern which individuals need further referral to the secondary sector for additional examination or treatment [1]. This ensures that the secondary sector primarily attends to those patients who benefit the most and reduces unnecessary diagnoses and treatments that may cause harm [2, 3]. In order to accomplish this, the GP must use their clinical experience along with knowledge of the scientific evidence, and the patients' values and concerns, which is considered evidence-based medicine [4]. However, it is extremely difficult, if not impossible, for general practitioners to keep up to date with the vast and growing quantity of scientific evidence from various medical fields. Although clinical guidelines are made available to alleviate the situation, uptake of guidelines can also be challenging, and dissemination of guidelines often has to be supported and supplemented by other quality improvement activities [5].

In Denmark, a new model for quality improvement based on quality clusters was introduced in 2018 inspired by developments in other countries and the concept of quality circles [6–11]. The quality clusters consist of groups of GPs that meet 2–4 times a year to work with data-driven quality improvement [8, 12]. Nearly all GPs are part of a quality cluster [13]. The clusters are organized with a cluster coordinator and typically a board. Topics for 2–3-h meetings are chosen by cluster members or in some clusters only by board members or the cluster coordinator. The meetings are often planned and conducted according to a so-called cluster package. Cluster packages are available from both regional institutions and a national institution, named Quality in General Practice (KiAP) that supports systematic quality improvement in general practice in Denmark. The cluster packages focus on major disease areas such as diabetes and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), or on areas where there are uncertainties or new or updated national guidelines [14]. By using a cluster package at a cluster meeting GPs can discuss and reflect on their clinical practice via a data-based overview of their own use of certain treatments or tests, and this can be compared with data from other cluster members, the national level and with recommendations from clinical guidelines.

Although quality clusters or similar concepts have been implemented in several countries, there is little knowledge about the actual impact of quality clusters on clinical practice, and the effects have not been evaluated in randomized trials. Therefore, we will conduct a trial to evaluate the effects of a new cluster package intervention in a randomized design. The topic of the intervention is the use of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) tests.

The PSA test is used to screen or test for prostate cancer, but the benefits of PSA testing have been debated for many years. This is due to the low specificity and the high risk of overdiagnosis and overtreatment [15, 16], and since PSA testing seems to have only a small or no effect on the mortality of prostate cancer [16–18]. The complexity of the evidence causes uncertainty among practitioners and differences in policies among countries [19]. Studies from Denmark and elsewhere suggest that PSA testing is overused in primary care when compared to recommendations in national guidelines, and that large practice variations exist among providers [20–23]. In Denmark, organized or opportunistic PSA screening has not been recommended, and in 2022 the Danish Urological Society updated the national guidelines on prostate cancer with an even more restrictive approach to the use of PSA testing. Hence, the guideline states that lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) is no longer sufficient to indicate PSA testing, and therefore only a specific group of men are recommended to get a PSA test [24].

In this context of new clinical guidelines as well as presumable variations in use and some overuse of PSA testing in general practice, PSA testing was selected as the topic for evaluating the effects of an intervention based on the quality cluster model.

Objectives {7}

This study protocol describes the evaluation of the effects of a quality cluster-based intervention on the use of PSA tests in general practice.

Trial design {8}

The trial is a cluster-randomized, parallel-group, superiority, non-blinded trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio. A delayed intervention control group with care-as-usual during the control period will be used as a comparator.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

The trial takes place in all of Denmark within quality clusters at general practice level. In Denmark, the healthcare system is free of charge for everyone with a social security number and almost everyone has a general practitioner (GP). The GP functions as a gatekeeper to the secondary healthcare system, referring to hospitals and most office-based specialists. 98% of GPs are organized in quality clusters to promote data-driven quality improvement within each clinic. There are currently 116 quality clusters in Denmark. The clusters vary in size from 3 to 35 clinics with an average of 13 clinics and meet 3–4 times per year.

Eligibility criteria {10}

All quality clusters in Denmark are available for inclusion except three quality clusters who participated in a small development and pilot study that were conducted prior to the trial. Quality clusters are excluded if they decline to await study randomization. If a quality cluster agrees to participate, all GPs registered in the cluster are included in the trial and thereby all enlisted men (≥ 40 years) within these clinics. Practices for which data cannot be obtained for the full study period will be excluded from the study. There are no individual patients involved in the trial.

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

Due to the nature of the study, the Committees of Health Research Ethics for the Capital Region of Denmark do not require informed consent by participants according to Danish legislations (Journal no.: F-24067136). Therefore, we do not require informed consent from the participating GPs when they participate in the cluster meeting. Informed consent from the cluster coordinator to participate in the trial was obtained. Informed consent will be obtained from individual GPs if directly contacted.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

N/A. No biological specimens are collected, and we do not plan to use individual patient data in ancillary studies.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

Care-as-usual is used as a comparator. Care-as-usual is regular cluster meetings within the intervention period in the control group. The specific cluster package intervention used in the trial will only be available for clusters in the intervention group within the intervention period. At the end of the study, the control group will receive the intervention in a delayed timeline.

Hereafter, the intervention was tested in three pilot clusters and gradually adjusted based on feedback from the GPs in these clusters and the researchers’ observations. The pilot phase and its findings will be described in another article.

Overall, the various intervention elements (podcasts, slideshow, videos, reflection questions etc.) are to promote knowledge of existing evidence and guidelines, awareness of and reflection on the use of the PSA test in the clinic, and access to information materials (which can be used in patient consultations) among the GPs in the cluster. The assumption is that these elements together will motivate and enable GPs to make changes in clinical practice in accordance with current national guidelines if this is deemed relevant.

To support delivery of the intervention each cluster coordinator will receive meeting material including slides, hand-outs and a background information document and will be offered to go through the material with researcher CL (via telephone) prior to the meeting to clarify any questions.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

The core element of the intervention is the cluster meeting where the cluster package material is presented and discussed. There are no discontinuing criteria at a GP level nor at a patient level, since no patients are directly included.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

The cluster coordinators are encouraged to ensure that as many GPs as possible attend the cluster meeting. To do this, the cluster coordinator sends meeting invitations (often supplied by a reminder when the meeting date approaches) to the GPs in the cluster. After the cluster meeting, the cluster coordinator forwards the handouts of the main take-home messages from the meeting to the GPs in the cluster. The research team sends an email 14 days after the cluster meeting as a reminder to the cluster coordinator about this.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

No concomitant care is prohibited for the intervention or control group during the trial.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

N/A since there is no harm involved in receiving the intervention. The intervention encourages GPs to follow the national clinical guidelines.

Outcomes {12}

Primary outcome: The relative reduction in the half-yearly prevalence of PSA tests on unique men ≥ 40 years taken by GPs in the quality cluster from pre- to post intervention during the intervention period, in the intervention group compared to the control group.

The overall aim of the intervention is to improve consideration of the national clinical guidelines in the use of PSA tests among GPs. The aim is not to eliminate the use of PSA tests, but considering the restrictive approach recommended by the guidelines, we view a relative reduction of 30% as clinically relevant.

Secondary outcome: The degree of homogeneity in the prevalence of PSA tests on unique men ≥ 40 within each cluster in the intervention group. Homogeneity is defined as the within-cluster variation in PSA test use.

Participant timeline {13}

Sample size {14}

The sample size is based on our primary outcome measure. We want to be able to detect a reduction of PSA test rate in the intervention group that is 30% lower than the rate in the control group, corresponding to a reduction of 0.05/patient year to 0.035/patient year PSA test per GP clinic in the intervention arm, and an unchanged 0.05/patient year in the control arm. The intra-cluster correlation coefficient was set to 0.10 [25]. A two-sided significance level of 0.05 and a power of 80% were used. Based on the assumption of an average of 13 GP clinics per quality cluster, a minimal sample size of 338 GP clinics per arm is required, or 676 GPs in total, corresponding to 50 quality clusters in total. When considering a dropout among GP of 5%, the total adjusted number of quality clusters required amounts to 55.

Recruitment {15}

Information about the trial and the cluster package will be sent to GPs in Denmark through an online common newsletter from KiAP. Also, a personal email will be sent to all cluster coordinators from the researcher TD. Additionally, up to two follow-up emails will be sent to all cluster coordinators who do not respond after one to two weeks and four to five weeks respectively from the first email. When a quality cluster has been recruited, all the GPs within the cluster are automatically part of the trial.

Data for all PSA tests taken on men ≥ 40 years from the included GPs within the quality clusters are acquired from the Danish Clinical Laboratory Information Register. No individual recruitment of patients is required for the trial.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Allocation of the quality clusters is randomized using a computer algorithm with a 1:1 allocation for intervention and control. The allocation will be stratified for cluster size (25 GPs or less, 26–55 GPs and more than 55 GPs) and geographical location (five administrative regions in Denmark).

Concealment mechanism {16b}

Allocation is conducted simultaneously for all quality clusters, and the allocation is known to both GPs and the research team. It is not possible to conceal the allocation due to the type of intervention.

Implementation {16c}

A statistician will generate the allocation sequence. The research team will inform the cluster coordinator about the allocation.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

Due to the nature of the intervention, it will not be possible to blind the participants or most members of the research team after the allocation. The statistician (VS) and the data manager are blinded to the primary outcome until the outcomes are analyzed.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

N/A due to the nature of the intervention.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

For the primary outcome, data will be collected from the Danish Clinical Laboratory Information Register, the Danish Civil Registration System (CRS), Yderregisteret, and the National Patient Register. The CRS holds information on all (live) born children in Denmark as well as all immigrants through an assigned unique identity number that allows linkage between public registers. The register is thought to have high validity because it is used for administrative purposes, registration is required by Danish law, and weekly upgrades and corrections are made [26]. The Danish Clinical Laboratory Information Register holds information on laboratory tests carried out at the large clinical biochemical and immunological laboratories in Denmark. Information on providers will be obtained from Yderregisteret. Finally, the National Patient Register contains information about patients listed in each practice [27]. If possible, data on participation of GP clinics at the cluster meeting will be obtained from the cluster coordinator.

The intervention period for each quality cluster is 6 months. Upon inclusion all quality clusters have arranged a meeting within the study period, and those assigned to the intervention group, will use the intervention cluster package for the meeting. Therefore, the 6 months intervention period will not start simultaneously for all quality clusters. In the control group clusters may hold a regular cluster meeting as usual (but without using the intervention cluster package). Follow-up data will be collected 6 months after the intervention period via registries to calculate the primary outcome.

Data management {19}

The trial starts when approval by the relevant Danish authorities has been granted. The study adheres to all Danish laws governing medical research. The research team collects and subjects data to double entry at a secure server. Data collected from the registries will be stored safely on the same secure server. All data that can identify participants will be encrypted and stored securely on password-protected servers with continuous transaction logging at servers at the University of Copenhagen. Data is accessed by the research team through the secure server. Trial data is stored in accordance with the data policy of the University of Copenhagen. Data is saved for 5 years after data collection and will hereafter be anonymized or terminated.

Confidentiality {27}

All data will be stored securely according to the guidelines set forth by the General Data Protection Regulation. All reports, data collection, process, and administrative forms will be identified by an identification number only to maintain participant confidentiality. All records that contain names or other personal identifiers will be stored separately from study records identified by a code number on a secure server. All local databases will be secured with password-protected access systems. Participants’ study information will not be released outside of the study without the written permission of the participant.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

The primary outcome will be analyzed using Poisson regression to account for relative reduction rate from pre- to post intervention between the intervention group and the control group. Clustering of patients within practices within quality clusters will be accounted for using generalized estimating equations (GEE). All regression models will be adjusted for selected GP and clinic characteristics. GP characteristics include gender, age, and years of experience as a GP, as these factors may be related to testing behavior independently of the intervention. Clinic characteristics include geographical location and the number of GPs who own the clinic, which may capture structural and organizational differences relevant to PSA testing patterns.

Interim analyses {21b}

Due to the limited duration of the study and the consideration that the intervention carries minimal risk, there will be no need for a formal interim analysis.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g. subgroup analyses) {20b}

The following subgroup analyses will be conducted:

The reduction in the prevalence of PSA tests on unique men ≥ 40 years in the intervention group from pre- to post intervention.

The reduction in the prevalence of PSA tests on unique men ≥ 40 years in the intervention group from pre- to post intervention.

The reduction in the prevalence of PSA tests on unique men ≥ 40 years taken at the 10% highest users within the intervention group.

The reduction in the prevalence of PSA tests on unique men ≥ 40 years taken at the 10% highest users within the intervention group.

The homogeneity in the prevalence of PSA tests on unique men ≥ 40 years within each cluster in the intervention group by the difference between the variance reduction in the intervention group and the control group, with a confidence interval derived by non-parametric bootstrap (repeated—1999 times—resampling with replacement of quality clusters). Adjustment for covariates will be done by propensity score weighting.

The homogeneity in the prevalence of PSA tests on unique men ≥ 40 years within each cluster in the intervention group by the difference between the variance reduction in the intervention group and the control group, with a confidence interval derived by non-parametric bootstrap (repeated—1999 times—resampling with replacement of quality clusters). Adjustment for covariates will be done by propensity score weighting.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

The primary outcome will be analyzed using intention-to-treat. The data for the primary outcome is from the national registries, which as previously described, can identify each unique patient. The male patients are linked to the GP clinic they are registered to at each time point and we will be able to identify all unique male patients and expect no missing data. If any missing data for follow-up occurs it will be accounted for by using bootstrap.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating center and trial steering committee {5d}

The trial is run by a research group. The coordinating center is at the University of Copenhagen where the research group is located. The research group also acts as the steering committee to ensure the trial runs accordingly. The data management team is also based at the university.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role and reporting structure {21a}

No data monitoring committee is needed for this trial. The trial only runs for a maximum of one year including follow-up time, and the intervention carries minimal risk since ordering of a PSA test will always be the GP's decision.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

No systematic guidelines for reporting adverse events or harms are planned due to the limited risk associated with the trial. The main aim is to improve evidence-based practice on the use of PSA test in general practice in accordance with the national clinical guidelines. Each GP has the final say in deciding if a PSA test is indicated and the intervention only supports existing national guidelines.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

Due to the limited duration of the study and the consideration that the intervention carries minimal risk, there will be no need for auditing.

Plans for communicating important protocol amendments to relevant parties (e.g. trial participants, ethical committees) {25}

The trial will be conducted in accordance with the protocol. If any modification is required due to possible benefits for safety issues of the patients, it will require a formal amendment. A formal amendment requires the approval of the Ethics Committee before implementation. Minor corrections that are seen as administrative rectifications to the protocol and have no effect on the conduction of the trial will be agreed upon by the research group and will be documented in a memorandum.

Discussion

The study described in this protocol aims to evaluate the effects of a complex intervention for improving the use of PSA tests in general practice in accordance with clinical guidelines. The study will generate knowledge about the effectiveness of using quality clusters for improving clinical practice in primary healthcare.

While a randomized controlled design has several well-known strengths, it is a limitation of this study that the quality clusters in the control group will receive the intervention in a delayed fashion. This means that the cluster coordinators (and probably many of the cluster members as well) know that they will receive the intervention later. This may attune the quality clusters in the control group to the issue, thereby possibly decreasing the measurable effect of the intervention.

As mentioned in the introduction, the benefits and harms of PSA testing have been debated for many years. In this study, it is assumed that the intervention will result in a reduction in the use of the test in general practice, and hence it could be a concern that this will potentially lead to underdiagnosis and missed cancers. However, the aim of the intervention is not to eliminate the use of PSA testing but to reduce the number of non-indicated tests in accordance with current clinical guidelines, thereby reducing the number of patients going through unnecessary testing. Ultimately, the decision to take or not to take the test in individual cases is about the GPs integrating evidence and guidelines with their own expertise and patient preferences in alignment with the principles of evidence-based medicine [4].
