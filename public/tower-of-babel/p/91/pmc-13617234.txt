Australian General Practitioners' Perspectives on Childhood Obesity Management
Authors: Stephanie Lee, Jasveen Kaur, Hasnain Bhurawala, Gary Leong, Habib Bhurawala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimChildhood obesity is a public health challenge, with 26.1% of Australian children aged between 2 and 17 years classified as overweight or obese in 2022. General practitioners (GPs) are the most frequently accessed healthcare providers for children. This project aims to provide a contemporary insight into current childhood obesity management practices among Australian GPs, including perceived barriers and enablers.MethodA 20‐question survey was developed by a multidisciplinary team including a paediatric endocrinologist, GP registrar, paediatric registrar and medical student. The HealthMap database distributed the survey to registered Australian GPs using a Quality Audit Reporting System. Free‐text responses were thematically analysed using Braun and Clarke's qualitative descriptive methodology. Ethics approval was granted by the Nepean Blue Mountains Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee.ResultsThere were 199 responses, of which 92% (n = 183) were GP Fellows. Most respondents were from New South Wales (34.7%, n = 69) and Victoria (27.6%, n = 55). A majority (60.3%, n = 120) practised in major cities, with 18.1% (n = 36) based in outer regional areas. Thematic analysis identified key barriers to obesity management: difficulty engaging families, limited referral pathways for multidisciplinary care, inadequate public health interventions and underutilisation of national resources.ConclusionParticipating Australian GPs identified significant challenges in managing childhood obesity, particularly limited awareness of available resources and referral pathways. Improving the dissemination of existing services, strengthening clinical support and enhancing multidisciplinary care pathways may help support GPs in delivering effective, obesity‐targeted care.Keywords: childhood obesity, general practitioners, obesity management, primary care, public health

Introduction

Childhood obesity is a growing public health challenge worldwide. Overweight and obesity are defined using age‐ and sex‐specific BMI percentiles: 85th–95th percentiles as overweight, and above 95th as obese [1]. In Australia, rates remain high despite public health efforts, posing lasting health system challenges. Approximately one in four Australian children aged 2–17 were classified as overweight or obese in 2022 [2]. Childhood obesity is associated with significant short‐ and long‐term health consequences, including metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, fatty liver disease, psychosocial difficulties and progression to adult obesity [3]. The development of obesity is multifactorial, influenced by biological, behavioural, environmental and social determinants of health, with overconsumption of energy‐dense foods and sedentary behaviour being key contributors [4].

General practitioners (GPs) are the most frequently accessed healthcare providers for children and are well‐positioned to monitor growth, deliver early interventions and support family‐based management. Australian and international guidelines recommend routine growth monitoring and the promotion of healthy nutrition and physical activity [5, 6]. The Royal Australian College of General Practitioners' (RACGP) Guidelines for preventive activities in general practice (or ‘Red Book’) is one of the few evidence‐based preventative care guidelines available to Australian GPs [6]. However, little is known about the translation or uptake of these recommendations in routine primary care.

Previous Australian studies have consistently identified barriers to childhood obesity management in general practice [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. A lack of standard clinical guidelines, poor confidence among healthcare professionals, limited consultation time, concerns about discussing weight sensitively, and unclear referral pathways were some of the commonly identified barriers by GPs [7, 8, 9, 12, 13].

More recently, initiatives such as community programs (Appendix 3: Table A1) and the Australian National Obesity Strategy 2022–2032 [14], have sought to strengthen prevention and management efforts. Nevertheless, the most recent National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) national guidelines were published in 2013 [15], with updated recommendations from Deakin University under development [16]. Contemporary data describing Australian GPs' use of available resources and their experiences managing childhood obesity therefore remain limited.

This study aimed to explore Australian GPs' perspectives on childhood obesity, including current management practices, utilisation of resources, confidence in delivering care and perceived barriers and enablers to effective management in general practice.

Methods

Survey Development and Design

A 20‐question multiple‐choice, ranking and free‐text questionnaire was developed from current literature and national/state resources (Appendix 2). The survey design was pilot‐tested with five practising GP to assess clarity and relevance. The survey drew upon validated questionnaires used in earlier primary healthcare research on childhood obesity, adapting questions on screening tools, referral practices and confidence.

Survey design was informed by the critical appraisal checklist for questionnaire studies [17]. Person‐first language was used to avoid implicit weight bias and stigma [18]. The questionnaire consisted of 20 items across five domains: (1) participant demographics and practice characteristics; (2) screening and assessment practices, including use of growth charts and BMI; (3) confidence and approaches to discussing childhood obesity with families; (4) referral pathways, management strategies and utilisation of available resources; and (5) perceived barriers and enablers to childhood obesity care. Most questions used multiple‐choice or Likert‐scale response formats, with opportunities for free‐text comments to explore experiences.

GPs provided demographic data and answered questions on topics including knowledge and use of resources, interventions, referrals, confidence and perceived barriers. Ethics approval was obtained through the Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee (2022/ETH02129). Participation was voluntary and anonymous, with consent implied through survey completion. The online survey was delivered through the Quality Audit Reporting System (QARS) [19].

Recruitment and Data Collection

The research team comprised a paediatric endocrinologist, general paediatrician, GP registrar, paediatric trainee and a medical student. The online database ‘HealthMap’ was utilised to access the National Health Service's Directory to gain general practice clinic details across Australia. Eligible participants were currently practising GPs registered with AHPRA, including GP Fellows, Registrars and non‐vocationally registered GPs.

GPs were contacted via email to participate in the study (Appendix 1). The study's inclusion criteria were self‐reported current GPs practising in Australia, whilst retired GPs or those not consulting on paediatric patients were excluded.

Recruitment occurred through HealthMap, the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners (RACGP) research platform, social media posts, telephone contact and distribution of flyers to general practices. Recruitment lasted 8 months, with reminders sent bi‐monthly. Approximately 2000 invitation emails were distributed; however, because recruitment also relied on secondary dissemination through professional networks and snowball sharing, the total number of GPs exposed to the survey invitation could not be precisely determined. Consequently, a true denominator was unavailable, precluding calculation of a response rate. This pragmatic recruitment approach was selected to maximise reach and feasibility across diverse geographical and practice locations throughout Australia.

No formal sample size calculation was conducted, given the exploratory nature of this survey. All 199 received responses were anonymous. This result exceeds commonly accepted thresholds for descriptive healthcare surveys. However, as with all convenience sampling methods, self‐selection bias may favour GPs with an interest in child health or obesity management.

Data Analysis

All collected data was stored using Microsoft Excel via the QARS [19]. Free‐text answers were analysed using a qualitative descriptive methodology (Braun and Clarke framework) [20] to identify key themes among GP experiences of childhood obesity management and explore barriers and enablers. Two researchers (a paediatric trainee and a GP registrar) independently coded the free‐text responses. An inter‐coder agreement was achieved after trial coding of responses. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved by consensus, and thematic organisation was completed after manual coding. Themes were developed inductively from the qualitative data. Theme saturation was reached after review of 150 responses.

Results

Demographic Information

One hundred‐ninety nine responses were received across all Australian states and territories (Figure 1). Most respondents were GP fellows (92%), with others comprising GP registrars (6%), non‐vocationally registered GPs (1.5%) and one nurse practitioner. The largest response rates were from New South Wales (34.7%), Victoria (27.6%) and Queensland (15.6%). Thematic Analysis

Theme 1: Weight‐Targeted Management and Poor Patient Outcomes

Respondents expressed concern that weight‐focused management contributes to negative psychosocial outcomes, including eating disorders. Stigmatising language in current guidelines also limited their use. A shift toward healthy lifestyle behaviours instead of weight loss was recommended (Table 4).

Theme 2: Limited Referral Options and Barriers to Team‐Based Care

Limited referral options for team‐based care were seen as a major barrier. Most GPs disagreed (27%) or strongly disagreed (15%) that they felt supported by current referral pathways, while 33% were neutral. These challenges were also reported in rural and remote areas, where access to multidisciplinary services is often limited. Some highlighted online programmes, such as Go4Fun, were identified as potential solutions (Table 5). Cost was the most frequently reported barrier (57%), particularly in the private sector. Respondents suggested increased government support through Medicare to reduce wait times and improve access.

Social determinants and health inequities were identified as key barriers, particularly in communities with high obesity prevalence. Contributing factors included low health literacy, inadequate housing and limited access to affordable, healthy food (Table 6).

Respondents called for a coordinated government approach via local, state and federal governance. Examples of public health solutions included public health campaigns encouraging healthy food choices and the introduction of a sugar tax.

Theme 4: Optimising the GP Toolkit

Forty two percent of GPs felt unsupported by current childhood obesity guidelines and resources. Time limitations due to Medicare funding were a common challenge. Respondents recommended chronic disease management plans with arrangements for allied health and Medicare rebate eligibility, better resource awareness through formal education and training, and stigma‐free evidence‐based guidelines (Table 7).

Theme 5: Families and Communities

Three‐quarters of GPs cited low parent or caregiver engagement as a significant barrier, contributed by limited motivation and cultural influences. GPs also observed that families from lower socioeconomic backgrounds and those residing in rural or remote areas were disproportionately affected communities. Recommended strategies included culturally appropriate parent education and community/school‐based initiatives, such as school screening programmes designed to prompt GP referrals (Table 8).

Discussion

Key findings from this survey of participating Australian GPs include considerable variability in screening (only 39% ‘always’ use BMI), strong concerns regarding weight stigma, limited access to multidisciplinary referral pathways, and low awareness and utilisation of existing state‐developed resources. One of the most prominent findings was the gap between the availability of existing childhood obesity programmes and their uptake in routine practice. Despite widespread concern about childhood obesity, significant variations exist in screening practices, confidence and referral pathways. Five key themes emerged from qualitative analysis: weight‐targeted management, limited referral options, governance and public health, optimisation of GP resources and the role of families and communities.

To our knowledge, this is one of the most recent Australian studies to evaluate not only GP confidence and perceived barriers, but also the real‐world awareness and use of existing obesity management resources in Australian general practice. The findings highlight evolving attitudes toward BMI utility, increased concern about weight stigma and a preference for behaviour‐based approaches—areas underrepresented in earlier literature.

Consistent with Siversten et al. [8] and Gooey et al. [5], we found persistent barriers such as parental denial and inadequate community support. Gooey et al. [5], in a qualitative study of GPs, practice nurses and practice managers, also reported insufficient support for implementing obesity prevention and identified a need for systemic change beyond individual clinical consultations. Despite the availability of national guidelines, toolkits and state‐based resources, respondents continued to report low awareness of available resources, uncertainty regarding referral pathways, and limited access to multidisciplinary services. These findings suggest that improving dissemination and implementation of existing services may represent a more immediate opportunity than developing additional programmes.

Time constraints were another commonly cited barrier, particularly in building rapport with patients and families, which is critical for sustained behavioural change.

Campbell et al.'s nationwide audit [11] similarly found wide variation in how paediatricians approach obesity management. Nevertheless, many employed evidence‐based strategies promoted by the National Prescribing Service [21], such as engaging families, increasing physical activity, reducing sedentary time and providing regular follow‐up. Although state‐based programs exist (e.g., ‘Healthy Kids for Professionals’ [22] and ‘Go4Fun’ [23]), uptake in general practice remains low, highlighting a gap between resource availability and practical uptake.

Stigmatisation of Weight and the Utility of BMI as a Screening Tool in Clinical Practice

Conversations surrounding weight and ‘weight‐focused’ guidelines were perceived as barriers to engaging in obesity management. Upskilling GPs to utilise de‐stigmatising language may benefit rapport building. Routine growth assessments remain one of the easiest ways to assess for changes in growth trajectory, which can be “the earliest indicator of a range of paediatric health problems” [21]. Although many participating GPs noted its poor application in clinical practice, especially as a precipitant to ‘weight‐focused management’, BMI for age remains the recommended population‐level screening tool, as noted in several national guidelines (NHMRC, Australian guidelines, RACGP, etc.). The findings of this study suggest that some GPs remain uncertain about its clinical utility and potential for weight‐focused discussions. Future guidance may benefit from integrating BMI with holistic, family‐centred assessments and healthy lifestyle interventions.

Increasing Clinical Support for GPs

One of the strongest findings of this study was the limited awareness and application of available statewide resources [22]. Nearly half (48.7%) of respondents relied primarily on self‐initiated learning opportunities, suggesting that many currently available programs and clinical tools are not being effectively disseminated. Current Australian and international guidance support routine growth monitoring, family‐centred behavioural interventions, promotion of healthy nutrition and physical activity and multidisciplinary care when available. Recent Australian frameworks include the National Obesity Strategy 2022–2032 [14], the National Preventative Health Strategy 2021–2030 [23], and the RACGP's 2025 Position Statement on Obesity Prevention and Management [24]. The latter recognises general practice as central to obesity prevention and treatment across the lifespan and highlights the need for improved funding, multidisciplinary care and equitable access to evidence‐based obesity services [24]. The concerns raised by respondents regarding stigma and engagement do not necessarily conflict with these recommendations; rather, they highlight challenges in translating evidence‐based guidance into routine practice. Improving awareness and integration of available resources and referral pathways may represent a practical and immediately implementable strategy to strengthen childhood obesity care in primary practice.

Overall, our findings should be interpreted within the context of the study's sampling methodology. The use of snowball sampling aimed to maximise participation across diverse practice settings. As such, the results are best viewed as an exploration of current GP perspectives and experiences rather than a definitive representation of all Australian GPs. Nevertheless, the consistency of identified barriers with previous Australian studies suggests that many of the challenges reported remain relevant across primary care settings.

Strengths and Limitations

Multiple strengths can be identified in this study. The breadth of respondents, ranging from interstate to regional and city areas, enabled increased representation and data validity. Multiple modes of recruitment, including email distribution, social media, telephone contact and physical distribution of notices, increased the survey's visibility to respondents and facilitated participation from a geographically diverse cohort. The multidisciplinary research team, comprising a paediatric endocrinologist, general paediatrician, GP registrar, paediatric registrar and medical student, contributed diverse perspectives to survey development and data interpretation.

Limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Firstly, although approximately 2000 invitation emails were distributed, the overall denominator of potentially exposed GPs could not be determined because additional recruitment occurred through professional networks, social media and snowball sampling. This precluded calculating a response rate and assessing representativeness. This convenience and snowball sampling may have introduced self‐selection bias and may have favoured clinicians with an existing interest in childhood obesity management.

Secondly, participation was voluntary and likely attracted GPs already engaged or interested in childhood obesity. This potentially inflated reported confidence, awareness, or use of resources.

Third, urban practices were overrepresented, with 60% of respondents practising in major cities. Consequently, this may limit the applicability of the findings to rural and remote GPs who often face greater workforce shortages and limited access to allied health and referral services.

Finally, few respondents reported substantial experience caring for children from priority populations, including First Nations and culturally and linguistically diverse communities. Many respondents (> 70%) reported minimal experience managing obesity in these groups, restricting insights into culturally specific barriers and care needs. These findings should be interpreted as providing insight into the perspectives of participating Australian GPs rather than being fully representative of the wider Australian GP cohort.

Implications

This study found that barriers to managing childhood obesity in primary care are multifactorial, spanning stigma, poor engagement, cultural and family influences, limited resources, access barriers and financial constraints. Addressing these requires a multi‐tiered approach, with coordinated policy‐based interventions at local, state and federal levels. Potential strategies include public health campaigns, increased accessibility to health facilities, greater Medicare support for longer consultations and investment in community‐based programs [24]. Our findings may help inform future initiatives aligned with the National Obesity Strategy 2022–2032's [14] target of reducing overweight and obesity in 2–17 year olds by at least 5% before 2030, particularly in relation to improving GP support, referral pathways and access to multidisciplinary care.

Conclusion

This study provides contemporary insights into the experiences and perspectives of participating Australian GPs regarding childhood obesity management. Participating GPs identified challenges related to stigma, family engagement, referral pathways and awareness of available resources and services. Limited awareness and utilisation of existing state‐based programs emerged as an important finding. Improving dissemination of existing resources, strengthening referral pathways and enhancing multidisciplinary support may represent practical opportunities to improve childhood obesity care in Australian primary practice.
