Use of Aztreonam-avibactam for infections caused by MBL-producing Enterobacterales
Authors: Filippo Medioli, Davide Fiore Bavaro, Valeria Cento, Cristina Scuderi, Federica Tordato, Maddalena Casana, Lucia Diella, Alessandro Cilli, Paola Morelli, Benedetta Varisco, Matteo Giovanni Della Porta, Carmelo Carlo-Stella, Massimo Vanoni, Elena Costantini, Stefania Bramanti, Maurizio Cecconi, Linda Bussini, Michele Bartoletti
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Infections caused by metallo- ẞ-lactamases (MBLs) producing organisms are highly resistant to most ẞ-lactam antibiotics and represent a significant cause of healthcare-associated infections with high mortality rates [1]. The combination of aztreonam-avibactam (ATM/AVI) is a primary candidate for treating NDM and VIM-producing Enterobacterales [2]. Aztreonam, a monobactam, remains stable against MBLs but is vulnerable to other ẞ-lactamases often co-produced by these pathogens; avibactam restores its activity by inhibiting these additional enzymes [3].

While the REVISIT phase 3 trial confirmed the clinical efficacy of ATM/AVI for severe Gram-negative infections, it featured limited enrolment of MBL-positive pathogens. Our study contributes to existing literature by providing a real-world case series of seven patients treated with ATM/AVI via expanded access at IRCCS Humanitas Research Hospital, Italy, between November 2024 and February 2025. This series highlights the drug's performance in a clinical setting where MBL-producing Enterobacterales often necessitate “last resort" interventions [1,3,4].

Pathogen identification was performed using MALDI-TOF, and carbapenemase detection (KPC, NDM, VIM, IMP, and OXA-48) was conducted via the NG-TEST®/CARBA-5 immunoassay. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using the Phoenix M50 system, supplemented by ComASP® Cefiderocol and Liofilchem™ MIC Test Strips for ATM/AVI, interpreted per European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) breakpoints [5].

The rationale for selecting ATM/AVI in our cohort was driven by a shortage of standalone aztreonam—which hindered the extemporaneous combination of aztreonam plus ceftazidime/avibactam—and high resistance rates to cefiderocol in circulating Italian strains. Furthermore, cefiderocol AST remains challenging due to the requirement for iron-depleted media and poor reproducibility in standard laboratory settings. In our series, ATM/AVI was preferred over cefiderocol or combo therapies due to these standardized testing challenges.

Patients received a loading dose of 2g/0.67g, followed by a standard regimen of 1.5g/0.5g every 6 hours (infused over 3 hours), with one patient receiving renal-adjusted dosing. We assessed “early clinical cure" at 14 days, defined by survival, antibiotic discontinuation, and no new antibiotic therapy for >48 hours. This 48-h window was chosen as a marker of immediate clinical stabilization, though we acknowledge that later relapses can occur.

Microbiological cure was assessed by obtaining negative cultures within a timeframe of 3 days before to 7 days after clinical cure was achieved. This timeframe was chosen to ensure the culture was taken when the infection was resolved clinically, which is a common practice in complex infections where the total treatment duration may vary based on the clinical response.

The median patient age was 63 years, and bloodstream infections (BSI) were the most common presentation (57.1%). All strains were resistant to aztreonam alone but susceptible to ATM/AVI. Despite the failure of previous empirical therapies—which is expected given the resistance profiles of MBL-producers—all seven patients achieved both clinical and microbiological cure. Two patients died from causes unrelated to the primary infection (pulmonary aspergillosis and arrhythmia) [Table 1].

Although Falcone et al. demonstrated high clinical success using the extemporaneous combination of ceftazidime-avibactam plus aztreonam, our series suggests that the fixed-dose ATM/AVI molecule achieves comparable efficacy in a real-world setting. This provides focused validation for the single-molecule approach evaluated in the REVISIT trial, particularly as a targeted therapy following the failure of empirical treatments not active against MBL-producers. Despite the limitation of a small, heterogeneous population, this report supports ATM/AVI as a vital option for severe MBL-producing infections [1,3].
