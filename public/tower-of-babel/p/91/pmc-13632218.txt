Effects of 28-day astaxanthin supplementation on psychological fatigue and biochemical markers in taekwondo athletes: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Meng-Yuan Shu, Long-Ying Pei, Jing Luo, Xiao-Yan Zhang, Hai-Xia Li, Li Shao, Yong Yang, Xiao-Yu Wang, Fang-Lin Jiang, Li Zuo, Yang-Qin Tan, Xiu-Chang Zhang, Jian Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAstaxanthin is a natural antioxidant with strong free-radical scavenging and anti-inflammatory properties and has been widely used in endurance sports; however, studies specifically targeting taekwondo athletes remain scarce.MethodsIn this study, we conducted a 28-day randomized, double-masked, placebo-controlled trial in which young male taekwondo athletes consumed 12 mg of astaxanthin daily to evaluate its effects on oxidative stress, muscle damage, and psychological status.ResultsA mixed-design ANOVA showed a significant group × time interaction for creatine kinase (CK), suggesting differential changes in exercise-induced muscle damage over the intervention period. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) showed significant group and time main effects, whereas malondialdehyde (MDA) did not show a significant group × time interaction. Exploratory between-group comparisons suggested higher SOD activity at Weeks 2 and 4 and lower MDA concentrations at Week 4 in the astaxanthin group than in the placebo group. For Athlete Burnout Questionnaire outcomes, only the emotional and physical exhaustion subscale (ABQa) differed between groups at Week 4 in exploratory analysis, while no significant group × time interaction was observed for any burnout subscale.ConclusionThese findings suggest that daily supplementation with 12 mg of astaxanthin for 28 days may beneficially modulate oxidative stress, muscle damage, and emotional exhaustion in young male taekwondo athletes, whereas its effects on other dimensions of psychological burnout appear to be limited.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12937-026-01363-1.Keywords: Astaxanthin, Taekwondo Athletes, Superoxide dismutase, Creatine kinase, Athlete Burnout Questionnaire

Introduction

Taekwondo is a combat sport in which the feet serve as the primary offensive tools, and its origins can be traced back to the Three Kingdoms era on the Korean Peninsula [1]. Since it was officially added to the Olympic program at the Sydney Games in 2000, taekwondo has attracted growing global interest. From a physiological perspective, taekwondo bouts consist of brief, explosive actions alternating with relatively prolonged phases of low-intensity movement [2]. During these exchanges, oxidative metabolism supplies most of the energy demand. In contrast, decisive lower-limb attacking techniques rely heavily on non-oxidative metabolic pathways and play a key role in determining match outcomes [2]. The sport’s strenuous, intermittent nature also predisposes athletes to muscle damage and transient suppression of immune function during both competition and routine training [3]. As a result, considerable attention has been directed toward integrating dietary supplements into the nutritional strategies of taekwondo athletes to improve performance and facilitate recovery from sport-related strain and injury.

Among the nutritional strategies investigated in taekwondo, caffeine supplementation is the most extensively studied and is widely recognized for its ergogenic effects on performance [4, 5]. In addition, vitamin-based supplements have mainly been explored for their potential to support immune function and post-exercise recovery in taekwondo athletes [6]. Beyond these more traditional supplements, astaxanthin (Fig. 1), a natural antioxidant [7], has shown promising ergogenic and immune-supporting effects in endurance athletes such as cyclists and runners, largely through its capacity to scavenge reactive oxygen species, attenuate exercise-induced oxidative damage, and modulate inflammatory responses [8]. However, most of this evidence comes from endurance disciplines, and it remains unclear whether these benefits translate to combat sports, where repeated high-intensity efforts and weight-control practices may impose additional oxidative and immunological stress [9, 10]. In taekwondo specifically, available data remain very limited, and to date, only our previous study has evaluated a 4-week astaxanthin supplementation protocol, which was found to enhance taekwondo-specific performance [11]. Unlike traditional endurance sports, taekwondo is characterized by repeated bouts of explosive high-intensity movements interspersed with short recovery periods, requiring athletes to repeatedly alternate between anaerobic and aerobic energy systems [12]. In addition, adolescent taekwondo athletes are frequently exposed to intensive training loads, rapid weight-control practices, repeated competition schedules, and psychological pressure related to performance and ranking. These physiological and psychological demands may increase reactive oxygen species production, promote exercise-induced muscle damage, and contribute to fatigue accumulation and impaired recovery [13]. Therefore, both biochemical markers and psychological burnout-related outcomes are highly relevant for understanding recovery status and overall athlete health in taekwondo populations. Furthermore, accumulating evidence suggests that nutritional status and physical activity are closely associated not only with physiological adaptation and recovery but also with psychological well-being, cognitive function, and quality of life in physically active populations [14].

The molecular structure of astaxanthin

Despite these promising findings, there is still a lack of evidence regarding the effects of astaxanthin supplementation on psychological fatigue and biochemical markers in taekwondo athletes. In particular, no study has comprehensively examined whether astaxanthin can alleviate sport-related psychological fatigue or modulate key blood biomarkers associated with oxidative stress, muscle damage, and immune function in this population. Addressing these outcomes is essential for understanding not only whether astaxanthin improves performance but also whether it enhances recovery and overall athlete health during an intensive training cycle. Therefore, building on our previous performance-focused trial [11], the present randomized, double-masked, placebo-controlled study was designed to investigate the impact of a 4-week, 12 mg/day astaxanthin supplementation protocol on psychological fatigue and selected biochemical markers in competitive taekwondo athletes. The intervention duration and dose were selected based on previous human studies demonstrating that astaxanthin supplementation at 12 mg/day is safe, well-tolerated, and capable of inducing measurable physiological and exercise-related benefits. Prior randomized controlled trials reported improvements in endurance performance, fat oxidation, oxidative stress regulation, and recovery-related outcomes following supplementation with 12 mg/day astaxanthin over periods ranging from 7 days to 4 weeks [15]. In addition, human studies using doses between 6 and 12 mg/day have consistently reported good tolerability without significant adverse effects [16]. The intervention duration and dose were chosen based on prior human trials indicating that a daily intake of 12 mg over several weeks is both well tolerated and sufficient to elicit measurable changes in exercise-related physiological responses [11].

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Hengyang Normal University (Approval No. 20251110), and all procedures complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Before their inclusion in the study, both the participants and their legal guardians received detailed explanations regarding the research objectives, protocols, and any potential risks. Written informed consent was obtained from each athlete and their guardians. All personal data were treated with strict confidentiality and were used exclusively for academic research purposes.

Participants and recruitment

The experiment was initiated in June 2025 and involved recruiting 42 male Taekwondo athletes from a professional training team based in Hengyang, Hunan Province, China. Participant selection was based on the following eligibility criteria: (1) age ranging from 15 to 18 years; (2) a minimum of one year of continuous Taekwondo training experience; (3) participation in municipal-level or higher competitions.

All athletes were in generally good physical condition, without any acute or chronic diseases that might influence exercise performance or interfere with the effects of supplementation.

Experimental design

This four-week trial used a randomized, double-masked, placebo-controlled design to evaluate the effects of astaxanthin supplementation on biochemical markers and psychological fatigue in male Taekwondo athletes. Forty-two eligible participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the astaxanthin group or the placebo group using a simple randomization sequence generated with IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The randomization sequence was prepared by a researcher who was not involved in participant assessment, supplementation supervision, or statistical analysis to ensure allocation concealment. Group assignments were placed in sequentially numbered opaque envelopes and revealed only after participant enrollment. Baseline body characteristics of the participants are shown in Table 1. No significant between-group differences were observed in age, body weight, height, or body mass index (BMI) at baseline (all P > 0.05), indicating that the two groups were comparable before the intervention. Additional baseline sport-background characteristics, including training experience and competitive level, are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Baseline values of the main biochemical and questionnaire outcomes, together with between-group mean differences, 95% confidence intervals, and effect sizes, are shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Baseline characteristics and sport-background information

Both participants and investigators were blinded to group allocation throughout the intervention period. The astaxanthin and placebo capsules were identical in appearance, size, color, and packaging. Placebo capsules contained sunflower seed oil and were manufactured to match the astaxanthin capsules as closely as possible in external characteristics, including taste and smell. Athletes in the supplementation group consumed one capsule each morning containing 12 mg of natural astaxanthin, whereas those in the placebo group received matching placebo capsules. Astaxanthin supplementation capsules were manufactured by Nutricost Inc. (Vineyard, UT, USA). Experimental design is shown in Fig. 2.

Experimental design and test protocols

Throughout the intervention, participants continued their usual Taekwondo training schedule, which comprised five sessions per week of approximately 120 min, including warm-up activities, technical and tactical drills, sparring practice, and physical conditioning exercises. Training programs were designed and supervised by the same coaching staff for all athletes, ensuring consistency in training intensity and volume across groups. Weekly training schedules, session duration, and attendance records were monitored throughout the study period. Training attendance exceeded 90% in both groups, and no significant between-group differences in training participation were observed during the intervention. No official competitions took place during the experimental period, thereby minimizing the potential influence of competition-related fatigue or acute performance stress on biochemical outcomes. To maximize adherence, supplementation took place under the direct supervision of the coaching staff, and training attendance was continuously recorded. Weekly capsule counts, direct observation of capsule ingestion, and training logs were used to verify compliance. The use of antioxidant or vitamin products outside the trial protocol was strictly prohibited. Dietary intake was partially controlled through the team cafeteria, which provided standardized meals throughout the intervention period. The cafeteria avoided serving astaxanthin-rich foods to minimize potential dietary confounding. Although detailed dietary intake analysis was not performed, coaches and researchers regularly monitored meal consumption patterns to ensure consistency in overall dietary habits between groups. In addition, participants were instructed to maintain their habitual dietary practices and avoid additional nutritional supplements or antioxidant-rich functional foods during the study period.

Before participant recruitment, an a priori sample size calculation was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) for a repeated-measures ANOVA with a within–between interaction design. Assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25) according to Cohen’s convention, an alpha level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.80, the estimated minimum sample size was 34 participants. To account for potential dropouts during the intervention period, 42 athletes were initially recruited and randomly assigned to the astaxanthin or placebo group. During the intervention, four athletes in the astaxanthin group and three athletes in the placebo group withdrew, mainly due to injury or competition-related scheduling conflicts unrelated to the intervention. Consequently, 35 athletes completed the study and were included in the final per-protocol analysis. Because outcome data were unavailable after withdrawal, an intention-to-treat analysis was not performed (Fig. 3).

Blood sample collection

Venous blood samples were collected at three time points: the day before the start of supplementation (baseline), at mid-intervention (Sunday of Week 2), and after completion of the 4-week intervention (Sunday of Week 4). All samples were obtained between 07:00 and 08:00 in the morning. Participants were required to fast for 10–12 h and refrain from high-intensity training for at least 24 h before each sampling session. Before blood collection, each athlete remained seated and rested quietly for at least 10 min. Immediately after blood sampling, the participants consumed the assigned supplement under supervision. Using standard aseptic procedures, approximately 20 mL of venous blood was drawn from the antecubital vein. For biochemical analyses, 5 mL of blood was transferred into serum separator tubes, allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting serum was aliquoted into labeled Eppendorf tubes and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

Blood parameter test

Serum samples obtained at baseline, Week 2, and Week 4 were used to assess oxidative stress and exercise-induced muscle damage (Fig. 2). After thawing on ice, all samples were gently mixed and visually inspected to exclude hemolyzed specimens. All biochemical analyses were performed in the same laboratory, and samples from the same participant were analyzed within the same batch whenever possible to reduce inter-assay variability. All biochemical parameters were measured in triplicate, and the mean values were used for analysis.

Serum superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined by a colorimetric method using a fully automated biochemical analyzer (BS-2000, Mindray, Shenzhen, China) in combination with commercial SOD assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). The assay is based on the ability of SOD to inhibit the formation of a chromogenic product generated by superoxide anions; a decrease in absorbance rate is proportional to SOD activity. After incubation at the temperature and reaction time specified by the manufacturer, absorbance was recorded at the recommended wavelength using the BS-2000 system, and SOD activity was calculated automatically by the analyzer software and expressed as units per milliliter (U/mL).

Serum creatine kinase (CK) activity was measured using a rate (kinetic) method on an AU5800 automated chemistry analyzer (Beckman Coulter AU5800, Brea, CA, USA). The assay followed an IFCC-recommended kinetic protocol, in which the change in absorbance per unit time reflects the rate of CK-catalyzed reactions. The analyzer continuously monitored absorbance at the specified wavelength, and CK activity was derived from the linear portion of the reaction curve according to the instrument’s built-in calibration parameters. CK values were reported in units per liter (U/L).

Serum malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration was quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) with commercial MDA ELISA kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Briefly, serum samples, standards, and controls were added to ELISA plates pre-coated with MDA-specific antibodies, followed by incubation with the corresponding enzyme-conjugated detection reagent. After washing to remove unbound components, substrate solution was added, and the colorimetric reaction was allowed to develop for the recommended time. The reaction was then stopped, and optical density was measured at the appropriate wavelength using a TECAN SUNRISE microplate reader (TECAN-SUNRISE, Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland). A standard curve was generated from serially diluted standards, and MDA concentrations in serum samples were calculated from this curve and expressed as nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL).

Athletes’ mental fatigue

Athletes’ mental fatigue was assessed using the Athlete Burnout Questionnaire (ABQ), a widely used and psychometrically validated instrument specifically developed for athlete populations by Raedeke and Smith [12]. The ABQ consists of 15 items across three dimensions of athlete burnout: emotional/physical exhaustion, reduced sense of accomplishment, and sport devaluation. Previous studies have demonstrated satisfactory reliability, factorial validity, and measurement invariance of the ABQ across different athletic populations and cultural contexts, supporting its suitability for assessing psychological burnout in competitive athletes [17]. The ABQ has also been frequently applied in studies involving adolescent and elite athletes, including combat-sport populations [18].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, or as range (mean ± standard deviation) for baseline body characteristics. The normality of each variable was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Baseline body characteristics, including age, body weight, height, and body mass index (BMI), were compared between the astaxanthin and placebo groups using independent-samples t-tests. BMI was calculated as body weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²). Effect sizes for baseline between-group comparisons were reported as Cohen’s d. To account for the repeated-measures design, changes in burnout questionnaire (ABQ) subscale scores and biomarkers between the astaxanthin and placebo groups across the three time points were analyzed using a 2 × 3 mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA). Group was treated as the between-subject factor, with two levels: astaxanthin and placebo. Time was treated as the within-subject factor, with three levels: baseline, Week 2, and Week 4. The main effects of group and time, as well as the group × time interaction, were examined for each outcome variable. When a significant group × time interaction was observed, simple effects analyses were conducted to identify differences between groups at each time point and changes within each group over time. When the assumption of sphericity was violated, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni method. Effect sizes for mixed-design ANOVA were reported as partial eta squared (ηp²). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To further explore the relationships between oxidative stress markers, muscle damage, and athlete burnout dimensions, pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated among SOD, MDA, CK, and the three ABQ subscales. The resulting correlation matrix was visualized as a hierarchically clustered heatmap with a diverging color scale. Correlation analyses and heatmap visualization were performed using Origin 2023 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA).

Results

Antioxidant and muscle damage markers

Detailed descriptive statistics for SOD, MDA, CK, and ABQ subscale scores at each time point are provided in Supplementary Table S3. Because CK values showed substantial inter-individual variability, CK data are additionally reported as median and interquartile range in Supplementary Table S4. Between-group mean differences with 95% confidence intervals, and Cohen’s d effect sizes are reported in Supplementary Table S5. As shown in Fig. 4, serum markers of oxidative stress and muscle damage showed divergent patterns between groups over the intervention period. At baseline, there were no significant differences in SOD, MDA, or CK between the astaxanthin and placebo groups. By Week 2 (t-test, t = 4.7, p < 0.01) and Week 4 (t-test, t = 4.2,p < 0.01), however, SOD activity was significantly higher in the astaxanthin group than in the placebo group, indicating an enhanced antioxidant response in the supplemented athletes (Fig. 4A). In the astaxanthin group, mean SOD increased from 176.2 ± 10.6 U/mL at baseline to 184.5 ± 9.8 U/mL at Week 4, whereas mean MDA decreased from 36.8 ± 15.5 to 25.4 ± 11.8 ng/mL and mean CK decreased from 986.3 ± 1336.2 to 205.2 ± 136.5 U/L over the same period. For MDA, no between-group difference was observed at baseline or at Week 2; however, at Week 4, the astaxanthin group exhibited significantly lower MDA concentrations than the placebo group (Mann–Whitney U test, U = 227, p < 0.05), suggesting reduced lipid peroxidation (Fig. 4B). CK levels, a marker of exercise-induced muscle damage, decreased more markedly over time in the astaxanthin group; CK concentrations were significantly lower than those in the placebo group at both Week 2 (Mann–Whitney U test, U = 205, p < 0.05) and Week 4 (Mann–Whitney U test, U = 216, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4C).

Athlete burnout measures

As shown in Fig. 5, athlete burnout scores exhibited only modest changes over the 4-week intervention. At baseline, there were no significant differences between the astaxanthin and placebo groups in ABQa, ABQb, or ABQc. In the astaxanthin group, mean ABQa decreased from 11.9 ± 3.4 at baseline to 9.9 ± 3.3 at Week 4, whereas scores in the placebo group showed no appreciable change over time. At Week 4, the astaxanthin group showed lower ABQa scores than the placebo group in exploratory between-group analysis (Mann-Whitney U test, U = 233, p < 0.01; Fig. 5A). In contrast, no significant between-group differences were observed at any time point for ABQb or ABQc, and both subscales remained relatively stable across the study period in both groups (Fig. 5B, C). Because the mixed-design ANOVA did not show significant interaction effects for the ABQ subscales, this Week 4 finding should be interpreted as limited and exploratory rather than as evidence of a broad psychological effect.

Correlation analysis

The correlation heatmap revealed distinct patterns of association among oxidative stress indices, muscle damage, and athlete burnout dimensions (Fig. 6). Spearman correlation analysis showed that SOD was weakly but significantly negatively correlated with CK (r = -0.34, P < 0.001), suggesting a modest inverse association between antioxidant enzyme activity and muscle damage. The three ABQ subscales were significantly and positively intercorrelated, including ABQa with ABQb (r = 0.69, P < 0.001), ABQa with ABQc (r = 0.630, P < 0.001), and ABQb with ABQc (r = 0.80, P < 0.001). No significant correlations were observed between the ABQ subscales and the biochemical markers SOD, MDA, or CK (all P > 0.05), indicating that burnout scores were not directly associated with oxidative stress or muscle damage markers in this dataset. Discussion

Effects of astaxanthin on biochemical indicators

In our 4-week intervention with 12 mg/day astaxanthin, significant between-group differences in SOD and CK had already emerged by Week 2 (p < 0.05). In contrast, MDA showed a clear divergence between the supplementation and placebo groups only at Week 4 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4). Astaxanthin is a potent antioxidant whose free radical scavenging capacity has been reported to be much higher than that of classical antioxidants such as vitamin E [19]. In a trial investigating the effects of acute astaxanthin supplementation, a daily dose of 28 mg in cyclists significantly reduced markers of muscle damage and lipid peroxidation [20]. Similarly, another study in cyclists reported that daily supplementation with 12 mg of astaxanthin enhanced fat oxidation during exercise [21]. Moreover, beyond athletic populations, astaxanthin supplementation has also been shown to exert beneficial effects on oxidative stress and inflammatory status in overweight and obese adults, as well as in healthy women and men, further supporting its role as a systemic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agent [22, 23].

Astaxanthin may account for the observed changes in SOD, MDA, and CK through a coordinated set of antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and muscle-protective actions operating at both the cytosolic and mitochondrial levels. However, these mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present study. They should therefore be interpreted as hypothetical explanations supported by previous evidence rather than as findings demonstrated in our study. Previous studies have suggested that astaxanthin may influence redox-sensitive pathways, stabilize biological membranes and support mitochondrial function under conditions of oxidative stress [24–35]. First, astaxanthin has been reported to modulate antioxidant defense systems through pathways involving Nrf2 and antioxidant response elements (ARE), thereby helping maintain endogenous antioxidant capacity during exercise-induced oxidative stress [24–28]. In addition, due to its amphipathic structure, astaxanthin can integrate into biological membranes and may directly scavenge reactive oxygen species and reduce lipid peroxidation [29–35]. Previous studies have also suggested that astaxanthin may modulate inflammatory signaling pathways and mitochondrial homeostasis, thereby contributing to the preservation of cellular integrity and reducing exercise-induced muscle damage [33–35]. Collectively, these proposed mechanisms may partially explain the higher SOD activity, lower MDA concentrations and reduced CK levels observed after supplementation in the present study.

Importantly, the role of oxidative stress in exercise adaptation should also be considered. Although excessive oxidative stress can impair cellular function and contribute to muscle damage, reactive oxygen species generated during exercise also act as essential signaling molecules involved in mitochondrial biogenesis, endogenous antioxidant responses and physiological adaptation to training [26, 33]. Therefore, complete suppression of oxidative stress may not necessarily be beneficial. Previous studies have suggested that excessive antioxidant supplementation may attenuate exercise-induced adaptive responses by disrupting normal redox signaling pathways [33]. The optimal balance between reducing harmful oxidative damage and preserving beneficial physiological signaling remains uncertain.

Furthermore, although statistically significant differences in SOD, CK, and MDA were observed, statistical significance does not necessarily imply clinical significance. While the observed biochemical changes suggest potentially favorable physiological responses, the magnitude of these changes should be interpreted cautiously. Small alterations in biomarker concentrations may reach statistical significance without necessarily translating into meaningful improvements in athletic performance, recovery capacity, or long-term health outcomes. In the present study, although selected biochemical changes were accompanied by improvements in sport-specific performance, the practical importance of these effects remains uncertain. Future studies should incorporate effect size estimates, minimal clinically important differences, and longer follow-up periods to determine whether statistically significant findings correspond to meaningful benefits for athletes.

Effects of astaxanthin on psychological fatigue

After the 4-week astaxanthin supplementation period, only ABQa showed a significant between-group difference (p < 0.05), with lower ABQa scores in the astaxanthin group than in the placebo group (Fig. 6). Although existing evidence suggests that astaxanthin may help alleviate subjective psychological fatigue, the current literature remains limited. A recent meta-analysis reported a trend toward reduced overall fatigue after 8–12 weeks of astaxanthin supplementation [36]. In addition, a study in healthy adults showed that daily intake of 8–12 mg astaxanthin for 8 weeks improved mood and vitality while reducing self-reported depression and fatigue [37]. However, these studies were conducted in generally healthy, non-athletic populations rather than in highly trained combat athletes, such as taekwondo practitioners.

Differences in participant characteristics, intervention duration, and assessment tools may partly explain why only ABQa showed significant improvement in the present study. Furthermore, previous evidence from athletes suggests that short-term higher-dose astaxanthin supplementation (e.g., 28 mg/day) may reduce perceived stress [8], indicating that both dosage and intervention duration could influence the magnitude of psychological responses.

Potential mechanisms underlying these psychological effects have been proposed in previous studies. In individuals exposed to high psychosocial stress, astaxanthin may attenuate stress-related physiological responses by modulating the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and inflammatory signaling, potentially leading to reductions in cortisol and proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, thereby contributing to reduced perceived stress and anxiety [38, 39]. In athletes, it has been proposed that astaxanthin may reduce exercise-induced oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, for example, by lowering MDA levels and enhancing endogenous antioxidant defenses, such as SOD activity, thereby accelerating recovery and lessening perceptions of fatigue [8, 40]. Previous evidence suggests that astaxanthin may modulate oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways involved in physiological recovery. However, we did not observe a significant correlation between ABQ scores and MDA (Fig. 6), suggesting that the relationship between biochemical responses and psychological outcomes may be more complex than a direct physiological association. The psychological findings of the present study should also be interpreted cautiously. Although ABQa demonstrated a significant between-group difference, no significant effects were observed for ABQb or ABQc, and the overall multivariate burnout profile was not significant. Therefore, the present findings do not support a broad psychological effect of astaxanthin supplementation. Rather, the results suggest that supplementation may specifically reduce emotional and physical exhaustion, while no clear effects were observed on reduced accomplishment or sport devaluation.

Limitations of our study

Although our 4-week astaxanthin supplementation trial in taekwondo athletes suggested potential benefits in attenuating serum markers of oxidative stress and muscle damage and in reducing psychological fatigue, several limitations should be acknowledged and also point to directions for future research. First, the sample consisted exclusively of male athletes from a single sport, so the findings may not be generalizable to female taekwondo practitioners or to athletes from other disciplines; future randomized controlled trials should therefore include both sexes and a wider range of competitive levels and training backgrounds to examine possible sex-specific and performance-related differences in response to supplementation. Second, the intervention period was relatively short and involved only a single daily dose of 12 mg, which precluded evaluation of the dose–response relationship and the potential impact of longer-term supplementation; subsequent studies should test multiple dosages and extended intervention durations to determine whether larger or more sustained effects can be achieved. Third, we focused on a limited panel of biochemical markers and used a single sport-specific burnout questionnaire, meaning that other relevant physiological, immunological, and psychological domains were not captured; incorporating a broader outcome battery in future work would help more clearly link changes in physiology to subjective fatigue and performance. Finally, larger, multicenter trials with adequate statistical power, complemented by mechanistic studies at the cellular and molecular levels, are warranted to confirm these preliminary findings and to clarify how astaxanthin can be optimally integrated into evidence-based nutritional strategies for taekwondo athletes.

Conclusions

In this 28-day randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, young male taekwondo athletes who consumed 12 mg/day of astaxanthin demonstrated significant between-group differences in superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity and creatine kinase (CK) levels at both Week 2 and Week 4 (p < 0.05). In addition, malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations were significantly lower in the astaxanthin group at Week 4 than in the placebo group (p < 0.05), suggesting potential benefits in oxidative stress and exercise-related muscle damage. Within the Athlete Burnout Questionnaire, however, only the emotional and physical exhaustion subscale (ABQa) showed a significant between-group difference at Week 4. In contrast, the remaining burnout-related dimensions did not significantly change. Therefore, the present findings suggest that short-term astaxanthin supplementation may beneficially influence selected biochemical markers associated with oxidative stress and muscle damage, as well as one specific burnout-related dimension related to emotional and physical exhaustion, rather than psychological fatigue as a whole. These findings should be considered preliminary due to several limitations, including the relatively small sample size, the inclusion of only adolescent male athletes, and the short supplementation period. Future studies involving larger and more diverse athlete populations, including female athletes, longer intervention durations, alternative dosage strategies, and more comprehensive psychological assessments are warranted to clarify further the physiological and psychological effects of astaxanthin supplementation in combat-sport athletes.
