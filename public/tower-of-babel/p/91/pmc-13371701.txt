APOE+ macrophages induce tumor cell metastatic characteristics via TNFSF12/TNFRSF12A signaling, correlating with poor patient prognosis
Authors: Chuanjun Song, Hongyu Sun, Wenyuan Gan, Quanzhong Liu, Lingxiang Wu, Mengyan Zhu, Lingjun Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with metastasis representing a major determinant of poor prognosis. Epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) promotes metastatic progression by enhancing tumor cell migration and invasion. This study aimed to identify EMT-associated malignant epithelial programs in CRC and define the macrophage-tumor cell signaling mechanisms contributing to EMT and apoptosis resistance. We integrated single-cell RNA sequencing, bulk RNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics, immunohistochemistry, and functional validation experiments. Malignant epithelial subpopulations were identified using inferCNV and reclustering analyses, and EMT-related programs were evaluated by gene set scoring. Transcriptional regulation was assessed using pySCENIC, public ChIP-seq data, ChIP-qPCR, and TNFRSF12A promoter luciferase reporter assays. Cell-cell communication and ligand activity were analyzed using CellPhoneDB and NicheNet. Functional validation included western blotting, conditioned-medium assays, TNFSF12 neutralization, and Transwell migration assays. We identified an EMT-associated malignant epithelial subgroup enriched for TNFRSF12A expression. FOSL2 was highly active in this subgroup and positively associated with TNFRSF12A expression. ChIP-qPCR and promoter luciferase assays supported direct FOSL2-mediated transcriptional activation of TNFRSF12A. APOE+ macrophages were identified as a potential source of TNFSF12 in the tumor microenvironment. TNFSF12 acted on TNFRSF12A-expressing CRC cells and was associated with BCL2L1 upregulation, EMT-like changes, and enhanced migration. TNFSF12 overexpression increased BCL2L1 expression, whereas APOE+ macrophage-conditioned medium promoted E-cadherin downregulation, Vimentin and BCL2L1 upregulation, and tumor cell migration. These effects were attenuated by anti-TNFSF12 neutralization. Clinically, combined high TNFSF12 and TNFRSF12A expression was associated with advanced disease stage and poorer survival. Together, these findings identify a macrophage-tumor cell signaling axis in which FOSL2 upregulates TNFRSF12A in CRC cells, while APOE+ macrophage-derived TNFSF12 activates downstream BCL2L1-associated survival and EMT-related programs. The TNFSF12-TNFRSF12A-BCL2L1 pathway may represent a potential therapeutic vulnerability in metastatic CRC.Novelty and impactThis study identifies a previously undercharacterized macrophage–tumor cell signaling mechanism in colorectal cancer (CRC), in which APOE+ macrophage-derived TNFSF12 acts on TNFRSF12A-expressing malignant epithelial cells to promote EMT-associated progression. By integrating single-cell transcriptomics, bulk RNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics, and functional validation, we show that FOSL2 directly enhances TNFRSF12A transcription in EMT-associated tumor cells, thereby increasing their responsiveness to TNFSF12. Functionally, TNFSF12/TNFRSF12A signaling promotes BCL2L1-associated survival signaling, EMT-like changes, and tumor cell migration, while combined high TNFSF12 and TNFRSF12A expression is associated with advanced disease stage and poor patient survival. Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of death globally, with nearly 20 million new cases and 9.7 million deaths reported in 2022, including non-melanoma skin cancers [1]. Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks third in incidence and second in mortality worldwide [2], with over 1.9 million new cases and 930,000 deaths in 2020, projected to rise to 3.2 million cases and 1.6 million deaths by 2040 [3]. Metastasis is a major cause of treatment failure in CRC [4]. While early-stage CRC can often be managed with surgery, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy, metastatic disease remains highly resistant to current therapies [5].

The epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a process by which epithelial cells acquire migratory and invasive mesenchymal traits, is a central driver of metastatic dissemination [6–8]. In CRC, EMT is strongly influenced by the tumor microenvironment (TME), including fibroblasts, immune cells, and cytokine-mediated signaling [9, 10]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), particularly M2-like subsets, have been reported to promote EMT and tumor progression through secretion of IL-10, TGF-β, MMP9, and other pro-tumoral factors [11, 12]. In addition, hypoxia and inflammatory signaling can further enhance EMT through pathways such as IGF/STAT3/Nanog/Slug and IL-6/STAT3/Fra-1 [13, 14].

Among TME-associated signaling pathways, the TNFSF12 (TWEAK)/TNFRSF12A (FN14) axis has emerged as an important regulator of cell migration, angiogenesis, inflammation, and tumor progression, although its functional role appears to be context-dependent across different cancer types [15]. Previous studies have suggested that TNFRSF12A is associated with aggressive epithelial phenotypes and poor prognosis, whereas TNFSF12 can be produced by macrophage populations within the tumor microenvironment and may contribute to tumor cell survival and plasticity [16–19]. However, in CRC, the precise cellular source of TNFSF12, the transcriptional regulation of TNFRSF12A, and the mechanistic links between this signaling axis, EMT, and apoptosis resistance remain incompletely understood [20, 21].

In the present study, we integrated single-cell transcriptomic analysis, bulk RNA-seq validation, and functional experiments to investigate the role of the TNFSF12/TNFRSF12A axis in CRC progression. We aimed to identify the malignant epithelial subpopulation associated with EMT, define the myeloid source of TNFSF12, explore the transcriptional regulation of TNFRSF12A by FOSL2, and determine whether this macrophage–tumor cell signaling axis contributes to BCL2L1 upregulation, EMT activation, and metastatic progression in colorectal cancer.

Materials and methods

Single-cell RNA sequencing data processing

We analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from colorectal cancer patients using the GSE132465 and GSE144735 datasets obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database [22]. Raw count matrices were processed using Seurat (v4.3.0.1) [23]. Low-quality cells were filtered out based on the following thresholds: fewer than 200 or more than 2500 detected features per cell, or greater than 5% mitochondrial gene content. Normalization and variance stabilization were performed using the SCTransform function, followed by principal component analysis (PCA). The first 20 principal components (PC1–PC20) were used to construct a k-nearest neighbor graph with k = 20. Clustering was performed using the Louvain algorithm at a resolution of 1.0, and visualization was generated using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) with a minimum distance of 0.3. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the FindMarkers function with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Cell types were annotated by matching DEGs with canonical cell-type markers. To reduce analysis bias, downstream cell-type annotation and DEG interpretation were independently validated by two blinded analysts.

Identification of malignant epithelial cells

Epithelial cells from each patient sample were treated as candidate malignant cells, while immune and stromal cells served as reference populations. Large-scale CNV profiles were inferred from single-cell transcriptomic data using the inferCNV package (v1.12.0)[24]. The algorithm orders genes by their chromosomal positions and compares their averaged expression intensity between target and reference cells to estimate relative copy number changes. A CNV score was calculated as the squared mean of genome-wide CNV values for each cell, and cells exhibiting significantly elevated CNV scores were classified as malignant epithelial cells. These malignant cells were subsequently reclustered for downstream analyses.

Differentially expressed genes (DEG) and marker gene analysis

DEGs between clusters were computed using Seurat’s FindMarkers function (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) with parameters: logfc.threshold = 0, min.pct = 0, and pseudocount.use = 0.01. Only genes expressed in more than 20% of cells in both comparison groups were retained. Based on log fold change (logFC), genes were classified as upregulated (logFC > 0), downregulated (logFC < 0), or not significant. Both raw and false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p-values were reported.

Gene set analysis and cell differentiation trajectory

Feature gene scoring in malignant epithelial cells was performed using the AddModuleScore function in Seurat, applying hallmark gene sets related to EMT from the MSigDB database [25]. The AddModuleScore function calculates module scores for predefined gene programs at the single-cell level by comparing the average expression of the target gene set with that of matched control gene sets. For trajectory inference, Monocle3 (v1.3.1) was used to reconstruct single-cell lineages and infer pseudotime ordering [26]. A CellDataSet object was created using UMI count data, followed by dimensionality reduction, trajectory graph learning, and pseudotime assignment. Cells were ordered along the inferred trajectory using the order_cells function. Cell grouping along trajectories was independently confirmed to reduce investigator bias.

GSVA and correlation analysis

Bulk RNA-seq data from colorectal cancer patients were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), tumor samples only. EMT scores were calculated using the Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) package (v1.44.5) [27]. The top 100 EMT-related genes identified from the malignant clusters were matched with TCGA data, and Spearman correlation between gene expression and EMT scores was calculated using the cor.test function in R. A total of 24 EMT-contributing genes were identified based on correlation significance, and their intersection with 84 highly expressed malignant epithelial genes was visualized using a Venn diagram.

Transcriptional regulatory analysis

To identify key transcription factors (TFs) driving EMT, pySCENIC analysis was conducted using the SCENIC R package (v1.3.1) [28]. Regulon activity scores were calculated using AUCell, and TF specificity was assessed with the calcRSS function. Regulatory interactions were visualized in a heatmap using pheatmap (v1.0.12). Binding of FOSL2 to the TNFRSF12A promoter was verified using public ChIP-seq data from Cistromedb (SRX3751771, SRX190204, GSE91279, SRX1048946), with H3K4me2/3 enrichment as active promoter markers [29]. Additionally, Rabit algorithm analysis of TCGA samples confirmed TF–target regulatory relationships [30]. To further validate direct transcriptional regulation, ChIP-qPCR and TNFRSF12A promoter luciferase reporter assays were subsequently performed as described below.

Intercellular communication and ligand activity analysis

Cell–cell communication was analyzed using CellPhoneDB (v4.1.0) [31], based on curated ligand–receptor interaction pairs. Metadata (cellphonedb_meta.txt) and count matrices (cellphonedb_count.txt) were used as inputs. Statistical inference was performed with 1000 permutations and 4 threads; significant interactions were defined by p < 0.05. Ligand activity was further quantified using NicheNet (v1.0.0) [32], evaluating the activity of specific ligands based on the expression of their predicted target genes in single-cell data.

Immune infiltration analysis

The tumor immune microenvironment was profiled using IOBR (v2.0.0), which incorporates eight deconvolution algorithms including CIBERSORT, TIMER, xCell, MCPcounter, ESTIMATE, IPS, and quanTIseq [33]. TCGA-derived RNA-seq data were grouped by TNFSF12 expression levels, and immune cell proportions were estimated using the deconvo_tme module. Immune infiltration scores and subtype composition were visualized using the ggplot2 package.

Pathway enrichment analysis

Pathway enrichment of DEGs and module genes was performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.2.2) [34]. KEGG pathways and MSigDB hallmark gene sets were used for annotation. Only pathways with adjusted p-values < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Spatial transcriptomics data analysis

Spatial transcriptomics data from Yuki Ozato et al. (E-GEAD-579) and the single-cell CRLM atlas by Yingcheng Wu et al. were processed using Seurat v5.0.0 [35, 36]. SCTransform was used for normalization, followed by dimensionality reduction (RunPCA), clustering (FindNeighbors, FindClusters), and UMAP visualization. Cell types were transferred from scRNA-seq data using label transfer, and gene expression patterns were displayed with SpatialFeaturePlot and SpatialDimPlot.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

This study was conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. 2022-SR-294), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. Tissue samples were collected from 20 colorectal cancer patients (12 males and 8 females; age range 45–72 years, mean 58.6 ± 7.2 years). Inclusion criteria were histologically confirmed colorectal adenocarcinoma, availability of matched adjacent normal tissue, and no prior chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Exclusion criteria included comorbid malignancies, autoimmune diseases, or incomplete clinical records. No subjects were excluded after enrollment.

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks from primary tumors, adjacent normal mucosa, and liver metastases were sectioned at 4 μm, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through graded ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 min using a microwave. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation in 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min at room temperature. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies targeting human proteins: TNFRSF12A/Fn14 (Rabbit polyclonal, Thermo Fisher Scientific/Invitrogen, Catalog #: BS-2493R, 1:200), TNFSF12/TWEAK (Rabbit polyclonal, Proteintech, Catalog #: 12537-1-AP, 1:200), FOSL2/FRA2 (Mouse monoclonal, OriGene, Catalog #: TA809660S, Clone: OTI2B4, 1:150), and BCL2L1/BCL-xL (Rabbit polyclonal, Thermo Fisher Scientific/Invitrogen, Catalog #: BS-1336R, 1:200).

After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, including Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog #: 31460, 1:500) and Goat anti-Mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog #: 31430, 1:500), for 1 h at room temperature. HRP catalyzes the oxidation of DAB substrate (Vector Laboratories, Catalog #: SK-4100), resulting in a brown precipitate that marks the location of the target protein. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted.

For quantification, three representative high-power fields (×400) per section were imaged in a blinded manner. Additional validation data for TNFSF12, FOSL2, and BCL2L1 were obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) [37]. The average optical density (AOD) of positively stained areas was calculated using ImageJ software (NIH) [38].

Primary tumor-derived cell culture

Short-term primary cultures were established from freshly resected human colorectal tumor tissues under approval by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. 2022-SR-294) and with written informed consent. Tumor fragments (~ 1 mm³) were digested in DMEM/F12 containing 1 mg/mL Collagenase IV (Sigma-Aldrich, Catalog #: C5138) and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (Roche, Catalog #: 10104159001) at 37 °C for 30 min. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm strainer, centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and resuspended in DMEM/F12 with 10% FBS (Gibco, Catalog #: 10099-141) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Catalog #: 15140-122). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination (Lonza, MycoAlert Kit, Catalog #: LT07-118). These short-term primary cultures were used for overexpression and knockdown assays, conditioned-medium treatment, western blotting, ChIP-qPCR, luciferase reporter assays, and migration experiments.

Gene overexpression and knockdown

For FOSL2 overexpression (OE), cells were transfected with the Tet-O-FUW-FOSL2-P2A-mCherry expression plasmid (Addgene plasmid #203860) using Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog #: L3000001) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stable transfectants were selected with puromycin (2 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, Catalog #: P8833). Knockdown of FOSL2 or TNFRSF12A was achieved using siRNA oligonucleotides synthesized by GenePharma and transfected using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog #: 13778075). Knockdown and overexpression efficiency were confirmed by quantitative PCR using SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog #: 4309155) and by Western blot. For TNFSF12 overexpression, cells were transfected with a human TNFSF12 cDNA ORF expression plasmid (GenScript, Clone ID: OHu16549; RefSeq: NM_003809.3) constructed in the pcDNA3.1-C-(k)DYK vector using Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent; corresponding empty-vector controls were included. Cells were harvested at 0, 6, 24, and 48 h after transfection for subsequent Western blot analysis of BCL2L1 expression.

Isolation and validation of APOE+ macrophages

To obtain APOE+ macrophages for functional assays, macrophages were isolated from colorectal cancer tissues or short-term primary cultures using CD14 magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec, Catalog #: 130-050-201) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were maintained in complete culture medium, and APOE expression was validated by Western blot prior to downstream experiments. Macrophages with high APOE expression were used as APOE+ macrophages in co-culture and conditioned-medium assays.

Co-culture experiments

To investigate the role of APOE+ macrophages in EMT, primary CRC cells were co-cultured with patient-derived APOE+ macrophages in 6-well Transwell inserts at a 1:1 ratio for 24 h. Tumor cell-only cultures served as controls. After co-culture, CRC cells were collected for Western blot analysis of E-cadherin and Vimentin. These co-culture results were used as supplementary supporting evidence.

Western blot

Western blot (WB) experiments were performed using short-term primary cultures derived from freshly resected human colorectal tumor tissues. All samples were collected from patients who provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. 2022-SR-294). According to the International Journal of Cancer guidelines, such short-term tumor-derived cultures are exempt from mandatory cell line authentication. All cultures were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free prior to experiments. Proteins (10–20 µg per lane) were extracted, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TNFRSF12A (Rabbit polyclonal, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog #: BS-2493R, 1:1000), BCL2L1/BCL-xL (Rabbit polyclonal, Thermo Fisher Scientific/Bioss, Catalog #: BS-1336R, 1:1000), E-cadherin (Mouse monoclonal, BD Biosciences, Catalog #: 610181, Clone: 36, 1:1000), Vimentin (Mouse monoclonal, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog #: MA5-11883, 1:1000), APOE (Mouse monoclonal, AssayBiotech, Catalog #: N0037, 1:1000), and GAPDH (Rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology, Catalog #: 2118 S, 1:5000). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Goat anti-Rabbit IgG, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog #: 31460, 1:5000; Goat anti-Mouse IgG, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog #: 31430, 1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog #: 32106), imaged using a chemiluminescence detection system, quantified with ImageJ software, and normalized to GAPDH. All blotting procedures were performed by investigators blinded to sample identity.

Preparation of conditioned medium and TNFSF12 neutralization

For conditioned-medium experiments, APOE+ macrophages were cultured in complete medium for 24 h, after which the supernatant was collected and centrifuged to remove cell debris. The resulting supernatant was used as APOE+ macrophage-conditioned medium (CM). CRC cells were treated with control medium, CM, CM plus normal goat IgG isotype control, or CM plus anti-TNFSF12 neutralizing antibody. TNFSF12 neutralization was performed using Goat Anti-Human TWEAK/TNFSF12 antigen affinity-purified polyclonal antibody (R&D Systems/Bio-Techne, Catalog #: AF1090) at a final concentration of 5 µg/mL. A concentration-matched Normal Goat IgG control (R&D Systems/Bio-Techne, Catalog #: AB-108-C) was used as the isotype control. Anti-TNFSF12 was used to neutralize extracellular TNFSF12 activity, whereas normal goat IgG served as the antibody control.

Transwell migration assay

Cell migration was assessed using Transwell chambers with 8.0 μm pore polycarbonate membrane inserts in 24-well plates (Corning, Catalog #: 3422). CRC cells were resuspended in serum-free medium and seeded into the upper chamber at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells/well. The lower chamber was filled with control medium, APOE+ macrophage-conditioned medium, conditioned medium plus IgG isotype control, or conditioned medium plus anti-TNFSF12 neutralizing antibody. After incubation for 24 h, non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed with cotton swabs. Migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and imaged under a light microscope. Migrated cells were quantified by counting five randomly selected microscopic fields per insert for each condition. The Transwell membranes were stained to visualize migrated cells, consistent with the manufacturer’s recommendation for cell visibility.

ChIP-qPCR assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed to assess the binding of FOSL2 to the TNFRSF12A promoter. Briefly, cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde, lysed, and sonicated to shear chromatin into fragments of approximately 200–500 bp. Chromatin was immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C using a ChIP-certified anti-FOSL2 antibody (Rabbit polyclonal, Atlas Antibodies, Catalog #: HPA061417) or Normal Rabbit IgG control (Cell Signaling Technology, Catalog #: 2729). After reversal of cross-linking and DNA purification, qPCR was performed using primers flanking the predicted AP-1/FOSL2-binding motif within the human TNFRSF12A promoter region. The human TNFRSF12A gene is located at chr16:3,020,368–3,022,383 in GRCh38.p14, and the ChIP-qPCR amplicon was designed to cover the predicted FOSL2-responsive promoter region containing the AP-1 motif. Enrichment was calculated relative to the IgG control and expressed as fold enrichment.

TNFRSF12A promoter luciferase reporter assay

To test whether FOSL2 transcriptionally activates TNFRSF12A, wild-type (WT) and mutant (Mut) TNFRSF12A promoter fragments containing the predicted AP-1/FOSL2-binding motif were cloned into the pGL3-Basic firefly luciferase reporter vector for promoter activity analysis. The reporter constructs were designed based on the human TNFRSF12A promoter region, with the TNFRSF12A gene located at chr16:3,020,368–3,022,383 in GRCh38.p14. The WT construct retained the predicted AP-1/FOSL2 motif 5′-TGACTCA-3′, whereas the mutant construct carried a disrupted motif sequence, with 5′-TGACTCA-3′ mutated to 5′-TGAggCA-3′. CRC cells were co-transfected with the WT or Mut TNFRSF12A promoter reporter plasmid, the FOSL2 overexpression plasmid or corresponding control vector, and the pRL-TK Renilla luciferase control reporter vector (Promega, Catalog #: E2241) as an internal control. Luciferase activity was measured 24–48 h after transfection using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Catalog #: E1910) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative promoter activity was calculated by normalizing firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.4.0). Quantitative experimental data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. For comparisons between two independent groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used when the data met the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance. For comparisons among more than two groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used. When data did not meet the assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variance, appropriate non-parametric tests were applied. Survival differences were assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis with the log-rank test where applicable. Investigators conducting image analysis and quantification were blinded to group identities to reduce bias. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Global cellular landscape of the tumor microenvironment in colorectal cancer

To characterize the tumor microenvironment (TME) in colorectal cancer, we selected two publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO): GSE132465 and GSE144735. The GSE132465 dataset provides single-cell RNA sequencing data from 63,689 cells of 23 colorectal cancer (CRC) patients, including 23 primary colorectal cancer samples and 10 matched normal mucosa samples, aiming to help understand the intrinsic features of cancer cells and their surrounding microenvironment. The GSE144735 dataset includes 27,414 cells from 6 CRC patients, covering core and border tumor regions, as well as matched normal mucosa samples. The analysis of these datasets offers a comprehensive cellular landscape of colorectal cancer and potential cellular interactions, providing valuable resources for the development of therapeutic strategies.

After filtering for cell quality and performing clustering, we annotated the cells into five major types based on prior experience: B cells (CD79A, MZB1, DERL3), T cells (CD3D, CD3E, CD3G), myeloid cells (CD14, CD68, CSF1R), stromal cells (SPARC, COL1A1, COL3A1), and epithelial cells (KRT18, EPCAM, CLDN7) (Fig. 1A-C). The cell proportion plot in Fig. 1D displays the relative abundance of each cell type across all samples, highlighting the cellular distribution and allowing for a clearer understanding of the tumor microenvironment composition.

We next focused on the epithelial compartment to distinguish malignant from non-malignant epithelial cells. Large-scale copy number variations (CNVs) were inferred directly from single-cell transcriptomic data using the inferCNV package (v1.12.0). This approach estimates CNV profiles by ordering genes according to chromosomal position and comparing their averaged expression intensities between putative tumor cells and reference normal epithelial cells. Cells exhibiting extensive CNV patterns were classified as malignant, whereas those with low CNV signals were defined as non-malignant (Fig. 1E, F).

Tumor cells were isolated from the dataset for further annotation and clustering analysis, resulting in nine distinct cell groups (Fig. 1G). Among these, one subpopulation was identified as dominant in driving tumor cell metastasis. EMT pathway scores were calculated for each subpopulation, revealing that the Mal_C3 subgroup had the highest EMT score (Fig. 1H) and also the highest malignancy score (Fig. 1I).

Moreover, pseudotime analysis showed that the Mal_C3 subgroup was positioned at the forefront of the cell differentiation trajectory (Fig. 1J-L), suggesting that these cells are less differentiated, exhibit high EMT characteristics, and possess notable malignant features. Based on these characteristics, we designated the Mal_C3 subgroup as the EMT subgroup.

TNFRSF12A signaling drives epithelial cell malignant transformation and is associated with poor patient prognosis

To further explore the potential genes driving the transformation of the EMT subgroup, we conducted differential gene expression analyses comparing the EMT subgroup with other tumor cell subgroups (Fig. 2A, B). Additionally, we compared tumor cells with other non-tumor cells (Fig. 2A, B). To understand the broader implications of these findings, we calculated EMT pathway scores for each sample using data from the TCGA-COAD database. We then assessed the correlation between the top 150 highly expressed EMT genes and EMT scores in TCGA-COAD samples, identifying 26 genes with correlation coefficients exceeding 0.4 (Fig. 2C). These results suggest that certain genes may play a critical role in the EMT process and are highly expressed in malignant tumor cells, potentially driving tumor cell metastasis and recurrence.

To enhance the validity of our observations regarding EMT involvement, we also conducted a comparative analysis using normal epithelial single-cell RNA-seq data from two independent public datasets, GSE188711 and GSE200997, which serve as non-tumor epithelial controls. The comparison revealed that EMT-related gene expression, including TNFRSF12A and other key markers, was significantly lower in normal epithelial cells than in EMT subgroups of tumor cells, providing a fair reference baseline for interpreting malignant EMT activation (Figure S1A–D).

We further integrated the highly EMT-related genes with genes that are highly expressed in tumor cells and identified TNFRSF12A as a key multifunctional gene of interest (Fig. 2D). In single-cell data, TNFRSF12A exhibited high expression in both epithelial and stromal cells (Fig. 2E, F). Additionally, we found significantly higher RNA expression levels of TNFRSF12A in tumor samples compared to normal samples (Fig. 2G). TNFRSF12A (also known as TWEAKR) is a cell surface receptor that, upon binding with its ligand TWEAK, activates multiple signaling pathways, including NF-κB and MAPK, which are crucial for cell growth, proliferation, and survival. Moreover, colorectal cancer patients with high TNFRSF12A expression had significantly shorter tumor recurrence times following treatment (Fig. 2H, I).

FOSL2 transcription factor highly active in the EMT subgroup

We have previously identified TNFRSF12A as a key gene potentially driving malignant transformation in the EMT subgroup. To further investigate the transcription factors regulating TNFRSF12A expression in specific cell subgroups, we used pySCENIC software to identify the top 30 most active transcription factors within the EMT subgroup (Fig. 3A) and ranked them by their activity levels (Fig. 3B). Interaction analysis of the top 10 most active transcription factors revealed that FOSL2 had the highest degree of connectivity (Fig. 3C). Both FOSL2 and BHLHE40 were among the top 10 based on their activity scores and expression levels within cells (Fig. 3D). Notably, FOSL2 was the most highly expressed and active transcription factor in the EMT subgroup compared to other subgroups, with BHLHE40 showing similar patterns (Fig. 3E). Using the Rabit algorithm in TCGA, we calculated the contribution of transcription factors to tumorigenesis and found that FOSL2 showed a positive regulatory association and ranked highest among the candidate transcription factors.(Fig. 3F). We propose that FOSL2 directly binds to the cis-regulatory elements of TNFRSF12A to regulate its expression. ChIP-seq data revealed significant enrichment of FOSL2 within 10 kb of the TNFRSF12A coding region. Moreover, high binding abundance of H3K4me2/3 was observed in these binding regions, and ATAC-seq demonstrated increased chromatin openness in the TNFRSF12A region in colorectal cancer (Fig. 3G). Functional validation using Fosl2 knockout (KO) mice (GSE213009) further supports this regulatory relationship: Tnfrsf12a expression was significantly downregulated in Fosl2 KO compared to control mice (Figure S3A).

TNFSF12 highly expressed in M2 macrophages within myeloid cells

TNFSF12, as a ligand interacting with TNFRSF12A, plays a crucial role in cellular activities including proliferation, migration, differentiation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, tissue remodeling, and inflammation. We aimed to further investigate the mechanism of action of this ligand-receptor pair in colorectal cancer. First, we analyzed TNFSF12 expression levels across different cell subgroups within the GSE132465 dataset (Fig. 4A). Using the featureplot function, we visualized its expression distribution and depicted its expression levels with dot plots, revealing high expression in both myeloid and stromal cells. Next, using CellPhoneDB software, we predicted significant receptor-ligand interactions between cell types, based on receptor expression in one cell type and ligand expression in another. To pinpoint the most relevant interactions, we focused on the cell-type-specific interaction between TNFSF12 and TNFRSF12A, labeling cell types with significant interactions (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4B). We observed highly specific interactions of the TNFSF12-TNFRSF12A ligand-receptor pair between myeloid cells and epithelial cells, myeloid cells and stromal cells, and stromal cells and epithelial cells.

We then processed myeloid cells using the Seurat normalization workflow and annotated the cell subgroups post-dimension reduction based on literature, naming each subgroup after its highly expressed genes: APOE+ Macro, CXCL10 + Macro, SPP1 + Macro, CXCL5 + Macro, FAM26F+ Macro, HSPA6 + Macro, Monocyte, Neutrophils, CD1C + cDC, and proliferation (Fig. 4C). Figure 4D presents a heatmap of the top 5 highly expressed genes in each annotated cell subgroup. We found high TNFSF12 expression in both APOE+ Macro and Proliferation subgroups (Fig. 4E). Using the FindMarkers function, we identified differential genes in these two subgroups and conducted KEGG enrichment analysis. Figure 4F shows that these genes are enriched in pathways like Lysosome, Phagosome, Pathogenic Escherichia coli infection, Antigen processing and presentation, Inflammatory bowel disease, and Intestinal immune network for IgA production. Figure 4G presents a network diagram of genes involved in different pathways, highlighting the significant role of the tumor necrosis factor superfamily (TNF and TNFSF13B). Interestingly, TNFSF12 and TNFSF13 can form a hybrid protein, TNFSF12-TNFSF13, jointly regulating immune responses.

Given the high expression of TNFSF12 in the APOE+ Macro subgroup, we assessed whether this subgroup corresponded to the M1 or M2 macrophage phenotype. Using AUCell gene set scoring, we observed that the APOE+ Macro and Proliferation subgroups displayed higher activity levels of M2 gene sets compared to M1 gene sets, with elevated expression of classical M2 marker genes relative to other subgroups (Fig. 4H). Figure 4I shows the expression of canonical M1 and M2 marker genes alongside TNFSF12 across myeloid subgroups, indicating that APOE+ macrophages with high TNFSF12 expression are enriched in M2-like markers. Notably, the APOE+ Macro subgroup appears to occupy an intermediate state during M2 polarization, consistent with recent single-cell analyses of CRC tumor-associated macrophages, which identified APOE+ macrophages as a lipid metabolism-associated, pro-tumoral TAM subtype and posited them as an intermediate polarization state [54]. Additionally, correlation analyses using bulk RNA-seq data and four computational algorithms revealed the strongest association between TNFSF12 expression and M2 macrophage signatures (Fig. 4J).

Finally, we validated our conclusions using two independent spatial transcriptomics datasets. In the E-GEAD-579 dataset, cells with high TNFSF12 expression co-localized with myeloid cells, and cells with high TNFRSF12A expression co-localized with epithelial cells (Fig. 4K). To further substantiate the clinical relevance of this ligand-receptor pair, we analyzed bulk RNA-seq data from independent colorectal cancer cohorts, including GSE39582. We found that patients with combined high expression of TNFSF12 and TNFRSF12A exhibited significantly higher T, N, and M stages, as well as more advanced overall TNM stage. Moreover, these patients had poorer overall survival compared to those with low combined expression (Figure S2). These results quantitatively link the spatial co-localization of TNFSF12-TNFRSF12A to adverse clinical outcomes, supporting their potential prognostic and biological significance in colorectal cancer.

A series of genes in the receptor cells were identified as potential downstream targets of TNFSF12, with varying degrees of regulatory potential indicated by color intensity (Fig. 5B). Among these potential targets are genes such as ACTB, BAX, BCL2, BCL2L1, BMF, CASP8, CCND1, VEGFA, ICAM1, IL6, IL10, IL1A, JUN, MCL1, PCNA, CCL2, and COL1A1.

Further analysis using four independent cohorts—TCGA (p < 0.01, cor > 0.5), GSE35279 (p < 0.01, cor > 0.3), GSE21815 (p < 0.01, cor > 0.3), and GSE132465 (p < 0.01, cor > 0.15)—identified genes positively correlated with TNFRSF12A expression. To further support these findings, we conducted an in-depth analysis of BCL2L1 expression in macrophages from single-cell datasets, incorporating cell trajectory and RNA velocity analyses. These analyses further suggested that TNFSF12-expressing macrophage states are associated with anti-apoptotic signaling programs in the colorectal cancer microenvironment. In particular, BCL2L1 showed dynamic expression changes along the inferred cellular trajectory and was predicted as a potential downstream target of TNFSF12-related signaling. Together with the NicheNet ligand-target analysis, these findings support the biological relevance of the TNFSF12-TNFRSF12A-BCL2L1 axis in linking macrophage-derived signals to tumor cell survival programs (Figure S1E). These results provide additional evidence for the biological relevance of this pathway in driving colorectal cancer progression.

IHC validation of elevated protein expression in colorectal cancer

To validate our findings, we assessed the expression of four proteins—TNFRSF12A, TNFSF12, FOSL2, and BCL2L1—in colorectal adenocarcinoma tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 6A-D). Quantitative analysis using statistical software confirmed that the expression levels of TNFRSF12A, TNFSF12, FOSL2, and BCL2L1 were significantly higher in colorectal adenocarcinoma tissues than in normal adjacent tissues. The right-side bar charts show the quantification of these results as Average Optical Density (AOD) in three randomly selected high-power fields (Fig. 6E-H), further confirming the significant overexpression of these proteins in tumor tissues (p-values indicate statistical significance). These findings suggest that these proteins may play crucial roles in the onset or progression of colorectal adenocarcinoma.

FOSL2-TNFSF12/TNFRSF12A axis drives EMT and apoptosis resistance in colorectal cancer

Based on our previous analyses, TNFRSF12A is transcriptionally regulated by FOSL2 in colorectal cancer cells, whereas APOE+ macrophages in the tumor microenvironment promote malignant phenotypes in tumor cells through TNFSF12. Together, these findings suggest that the FOSL2-TNFSF12/TNFRSF12A axis contributes to apoptosis resistance, EMT activation, and metastatic progression in colorectal cancer.

To further investigate this regulatory network, we first analyzed ten colorectal cancer datasets from GEO and evaluated the correlations among TNFRSF12A, FOSL2, TNFSF12, and BCL2L1. The results showed consistent positive correlations between TNFRSF12A and FOSL2, as well as between TNFRSF12A and BCL2L1, across multiple datasets (Fig. 7A). We then divided samples into FOSL2-high and FOSL2-low groups in four independent cohorts and found that TNFRSF12A expression was significantly higher in the FOSL2-high group in all datasets analyzed (Fig. 7B). Consistent with the earlier Fosl2-knockout analysis, Tnfrsf12a expression was significantly reduced in Fosl2-deficient samples, and EMT activity was also markedly decreased after Fosl2 deletion, further supporting a positive regulatory role of FOSL2 in TNFRSF12A expression and EMT activation in colorectal cancer (Figure S3A-B).

To evaluate the biological significance of TNFRSF12A in downstream malignant programs, we analyzed the GSE36806 dataset containing TNFRSF12A/Fn14 wild-type and knockout samples. BCL2L1 expression was lower in the TNFRSF12A knockout group than in the wild-type group (Fig. 7C). In addition, gene set scoring showed reduced activity of tumor-associated programs, including angiogenesis, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and mTORC1 signaling, in the knockout group (Fig. 7D). Although this dataset is not a direct substitute for TNFRSF12A perturbation in human CRC cells, these results provide supportive evidence that TNFRSF12A loss is associated with suppression of pro-tumor transcriptional programs and anti-apoptotic signaling.

We next performed experimental validation. Upon TNFSF12 overexpression, BCL2L1 protein expression increased over time, with a marked elevation at 24 h that remained detectable at 48 h (Fig. 7E), supporting BCL2L1 as a downstream effector of TNFSF12/TNFRSF12A signaling. To confirm the macrophage source used in the functional assays, APOE expression was validated by western blot in control macrophages and APOE+ macrophages (Fig. 7F). We then treated colorectal cancer cells with APOE+ macrophage-conditioned medium (CM), CM plus IgG, or CM plus anti-TNFSF12. Western blot analysis showed that conditioned medium decreased E-cadherin expression while increasing Vimentin and BCL2L1 levels, whereas TNFSF12 neutralization attenuated these changes (Fig. 7G). Consistently, Transwell assays demonstrated that APOE+ macrophage-conditioned medium markedly enhanced tumor cell migration, while anti-TNFSF12 treatment reduced this promotive effect (Fig. 7H). Additional supporting western blot data showing co-culture-induced EMT marker changes, increased TNFRSF12A expression after FOSL2 overexpression, and decreased TNFRSF12A expression after FOSL2 loss are provided in Figure S4A-C.

To directly test whether FOSL2 regulates TNFRSF12A transcription, we performed ChIP-qPCR and promoter luciferase assays. ChIP-qPCR demonstrated significant enrichment of FOSL2 at the TNFRSF12A promoter relative to the IgG control (Fig. 7I). In luciferase reporter assays, FOSL2 overexpression significantly increased wild-type TNFRSF12A promoter activity, whereas FOSL2 knockdown reduced promoter activity. This FOSL2-dependent regulation was markedly attenuated by mutation of the predicted AP-1/FOSL2-binding motif (Fig. 7J). A schematic diagram of the TNFRSF12A promoter and the wild-type and mutant reporter constructs is shown in Fig. 7K. These results provide locus-specific and functional evidence that FOSL2 binds to and activates the TNFRSF12A promoter in a motif-dependent manner.

Finally, to further substantiate the clinical relevance of this signaling axis, we analyzed independent colorectal cancer cohorts and found that patients with combined high expression of TNFSF12 and TNFRSF12A exhibited significantly poorer overall survival and more advanced TNM, T, N, and M stages (Figure S2A-E), further supporting the pathological significance of this macrophage-tumor cell signaling axis in colorectal cancer progression.

Taken together, these findings support a coordinated regulatory mechanism in which FOSL2 transcriptionally upregulates TNFRSF12A in colorectal cancer cells, while APOE+ macrophage-derived TNFSF12 activates TNFRSF12A signaling in a paracrine manner. This axis promotes BCL2L1 upregulation, EMT-like changes, migration, tumor progression, and poor clinical outcome, highlighting the TNFSF12-TNFRSF12A-BCL2L1 pathway as a potential therapeutic target in colorectal cancer.

Discussion

To characterize the colorectal cancer (CRC) tumor microenvironment, we analyzed two high-quality single-cell RNA sequencing datasets, GSE132465 and GSE144735, which together provided a comprehensive view of malignant epithelial cells, stromal components, and myeloid populations within CRC tissues. Through integrative analyses, we identified an EMT-associated malignant epithelial subgroup enriched for TNFRSF12A expression and an APOE+ macrophage population with high TNFSF12 expression, suggesting the presence of a macrophage–tumor cell signaling axis in CRC. Our results further indicate that TNFRSF12A expression in tumor cells is positively associated with FOSL2 activity, while APOE+ macrophages may promote BCL2L1 upregulation and EMT-related phenotypes through TNFSF12. Collectively, these findings support a model in which the FOSL2-TNFSF12/TNFRSF12A axis contributes to tumor cell survival, EMT, migration, and metastatic progression, and may therefore represent a therapeutically relevant vulnerability in CRC.

EMT is a critical biological program that enables epithelial tumor cells to lose polarity and cell-cell adhesion, acquire migratory and invasive properties, disseminate from the primary tumor, and resist conventional therapies such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy [39–41]. In our study, TNFRSF12A was highly enriched in the EMT-associated malignant epithelial subgroup, suggesting that this receptor is linked to aggressive epithelial plasticity in CRC. We further found that FOSL2 was one of the most highly active transcription factors in this subgroup and showed strong association with TNFRSF12A expression across multiple datasets. FOSL2/Fra-2, as an AP-1 family transcription factor, has been implicated in cancer progression, EMT, migration, therapeutic resistance, and tumor–microenvironment remodeling, supporting its potential role as a regulator of malignant epithelial plasticity [42–44]. Importantly, our ChIP-qPCR and promoter luciferase assays provided locus-specific and functional evidence that FOSL2 binds to and activates the TNFRSF12A promoter in a motif-dependent manner. Consistently, analysis of the Fosl2-knockout mouse CRC dataset GSE213009 showed reduced Tnfrsf12a expression and decreased EMT activity after Fosl2 deletion, further supporting a functional role for FOSL2 in maintaining TNFRSF12A expression and EMT-associated transcriptional programs.

Macrophages are highly plastic components of the tumor microenvironment, and M2-like macrophages are generally associated with tissue remodeling, immune suppression, angiogenesis, therapeutic resistance, and tumor progression [45–47]. This plasticity is shaped by inflammatory and cytokine-driven programs, including M-CSF/NFκB/c-Jun signaling that promotes M1-to-M2 macrophage transformation [49]. In CRC, immune and stromal classification studies have emphasized the clinical relevance of stromal and macrophage-enriched tumor microenvironment states, while recent single-cell macrophage atlases further highlight the heterogeneity and functional diversity of TAM subsets in colorectal cancer [48, 54]. Our assignment of APOE+ macrophages to an M2-like phenotype is consistent with this broader literature. APOE has also been linked to pathogenic myeloid cell states in cancer, further supporting the biological relevance of APOE-expressing macrophage populations within the tumor microenvironment [50]. In our study, APOE+ macrophages showed high TNFSF12 expression and exhibited transcriptional characteristics consistent with an M2-like state. Because TNFSF12 is the cognate ligand of TNFRSF12A/Fn14, these observations suggested that APOE+ macrophages may provide a paracrine source of TNFSF12 to activate TNFRSF12A-positive tumor cells. Functional experiments supported this model: TNFSF12 overexpression increased BCL2L1 expression, and APOE+ macrophage-conditioned medium promoted EMT-like changes and tumor cell migration, both of which were attenuated by TNFSF12 neutralization. These findings support the biological significance of a macrophage-derived TNFSF12 signal in driving malignant phenotypes in CRC cells.

TNFRSF12A/Fn14 has been implicated in multiple oncogenic processes, including proliferation, inflammation, apoptosis resistance, invasion, and therapeutic resistance. TNFSF12/TWEAK signaling has been reported to activate pro-survival pathways, including Akt-dependent survival signaling, supporting a potential mechanistic link between TNFSF12/TNFRSF12A activation and tumor cell survival programs [53]. Our results extend these observations in CRC by linking TNFRSF12A to both EMT-associated malignant epithelial states and anti-apoptotic signaling through BCL2L1. The reduced BCL2L1 expression and lower angiogenesis, EMT, and mTORC1 signaling scores observed in the TNFRSF12A knockout dataset further support a functional role for TNFRSF12A in maintaining pro-tumor transcriptional programs. Together with the clinical data showing poorer survival and more advanced stage in patients with combined high TNFSF12 and TNFRSF12A expression, these findings suggest that this axis may have translational relevance. Therapeutically, strategies aimed at blocking TNFRSF12A signaling, inhibiting BCL2L1-related survival programs, or reprogramming pro-tumoral macrophages may represent potential approaches to disrupt this signaling network in CRC [51–53].

At the same time, our study has several important limitations that should be acknowledged more explicitly. First, although we integrated two high-quality scRNA-seq datasets and validated key observations using external cohorts, batch effects and inter-dataset heterogeneity remain an inherent concern in single-cell analyses. Differences in sample processing, sequencing platforms, tissue regions, and patient composition may influence clustering results, inferred cellular states, and expression patterns. While cross-dataset consistency supports the robustness of our conclusions, these technical and biological sources of variation cannot be fully excluded. Second, although our functional experiments provide mechanistic support for the TNFSF12/TNFRSF12A axis, most of these validations were performed in vitro using overexpression systems, conditioned medium, co-culture settings, and reporter assays. These systems are useful for dissecting cell-intrinsic and paracrine mechanisms, but they do not fully recapitulate the complexity of the in vivo tumor microenvironment, where immune cells, stromal cells, extracellular matrix, spatial organization, and hemodynamic factors jointly shape tumor behavior. Third, although our results support a functional role for this axis in CRC progression, additional validation in more physiologically relevant models is still needed. Future studies should incorporate patient-derived organoids, orthotopic mouse models, and macrophage–tumor co-engraftment systems to more rigorously test causality and therapeutic vulnerability in vivo. Such models would also help clarify the broader pathway context downstream of TNFRSF12A and determine whether this signaling axis cooperates with other EMT- and survival-related programs in CRC.

In summary, our study identifies a coordinated regulatory mechanism in which FOSL2 transcriptionally upregulates TNFRSF12A in CRC cells, while APOE+ macrophages provide TNFSF12 as a paracrine ligand to activate TNFRSF12A signaling. This axis promotes BCL2L1 upregulation, EMT-like changes, migration, and tumor progression, and is associated with poor clinical outcome. Although further validation in organoid and murine models will be required, these findings highlight the TNFSF12-TNFRSF12A-BCL2L1 pathway as a biologically meaningful and potentially targetable signaling network in colorectal cancer.
