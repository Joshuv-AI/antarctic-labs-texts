A 66-Year-Old Man With a Skull Lesion and a Solitary Lung Nodule
Authors: Jordan L Cox, Scott M Matson, Oliver Bawmann, Deepshikha Datta, Nathan D Hensley, Melissa L New, Edward D Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Case PresentationA 66-year-old man with a history of 50 pack-years of cigarette use, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, OSA, and a left upper lobe lung nodule that had been stable on imaging for nearly 2 years presented with dizziness. His vital signs were within normal limits; the rest of the examination was notable for a bony protuberance from the vertex of the calvarium to the right side of the skull.

Diagnostic Studies

A head CT scan obtained for the dizziness revealed marked irregular bony hypertrophy of the right frontal skull and lucency within the diploid space and a thickened dura mater (Fig 1A). A skull MRI scan with gadolinium demonstrated severe irregular bony hypertrophy of the right frontal calvarium and marked thickening of the underlying dura (Fig 1B). Magnetic resonance venography also showed secondary compression and/or occlusion of the superior sagittal sinus (Fig 1C). A contemporaneous chest CT scan (Fig 1D) showed that the lung nodule remained similar in size but had increased fullness compared with either a CT-PET scan taken 8 months earlier wherein the lung nodule was mildly avid for 5-fluorodeoxyglucose (Fig 1E) or a chest CT scan 22 months earlier (Fig 1F). A biopsy of the skull and underlying meninges revealed a primary intraosseous meningioma (PIM). Three weeks later, he underwent a transthoracic biopsy of the lung nodule.

Diagnosis: Metastasis of the PIM to the lung manifesting as a solitary lung nodule

Discussion

Meningiomas are broadly divided into intracranial (intradural) meningiomas (accounting for 98%-99% of all meningiomas) and extradural meningiomas (1%-2% of all meningiomas) (Fig 2). The uncommon extradural meningiomas are further classified into extraosseous meningiomas (32% of cases, type I) and PIMs (68% of cases, types II and III). The extradural extraosseous meningiomas (type I) are mostly meningiomas that involve the peripheral nervous system such as the brachial plexus. PIM most commonly involve the skull and may be further divided into type II (pure PIM with confinement to the bone without dural involvement) and type III (with extension into extracranial tissues [ie, dura]) (Fig 2). Type II PIM (no dural involvement) or type III PIM (dural involvement) may also be divided anatomically into calvarial (as seen in the present patient) or skull base (Fig 2). The typical presentation of a calvarial PIM is a visible and palpable enlarging mass, whereas a skull base PIM more commonly presents with mass effect and cranial nerve palsies.

Meningiomas in general are benign-behaving tumors and have very low metastatic potential. For intracranial meningiomas, it is estimated that only 0.14% to 0.76% metastasize extracranially. The lungs are the most common site of metastasis, estimated to occur in 37% to 61% of the rare metastatic cases. Moreover, intracranial meningiomas of all histopathologic grades (benign, atypical, and anaplastic) have been documented to metastasize, with about one-half of metastatic meningiomas having a benign or atypical grade. Case reports of patients with metastatic meningioma treated with systemic therapy (eg, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, immunotherapy, classical chemotherapy) have failed to prevent disease progression. Generally, metastatic lesions themselves are not locally invasive; however, most did not have long-term follow-up.

The metastatic potential of the PIM subset of meningiomas is also rare. In a comprehensive literature review of 142 patients with extradural meningiomas in the CT era, it was noted that 6% had distant metastases. However, because extradural meningiomas also include extraosseous meningiomas (type I) and PIM with extracranial extension (type III, which are more likely to be associated with atypical or malignant features), the prevalence of metastasis of pure PIM (type II) is likely to be much lower than 6%.

Given that metastasis with either intracranial meningiomas or PIM is so rare, there are no consensus guidelines for surveillance for metastatic meningioma. One practical option is CT imaging dictated by location of new symptoms, abnormal clinical/laboratory signs, and/or recurrence of meningioma. Another is the employment of gallium-68 DOTATATE-Positron Emission Tomography (DOTATATE-PET) scanning—which uses somatostatin as a tracer because meningiomas often express high levels of somatostatin receptors on their cell surface—and appears promising as a method to survey for metastasis.

Outcomes for patients with metastatic meningiomas are often poor, with many patients dying within a few years of diagnosis in available case reports. These outcomes may be caused by a variety of factors, including a more aggressive intracranial meningioma, delayed diagnosis of metastasis due to lack of symptoms, limited treatment options, and/or death from comorbid conditions.

Clinical Course

A biopsy of the skull and underlying meninges revealed a PIM (Fig 3A) and dural invasion by the meningioma (Fig 3B), classifying it as a type III PIM with extracranial extension (Fig 2). In this patient with a significant smoking history, the risk that the solitary lung nodule was a primary lung cancer was relatively high; indeed, using the Mayo Clinic Model for solitary pulmonary nodule, we calculated a 45% chance of a primary lung cancer for the lung nodule in this patient, probabilistically more likely than a PIM that metastasizes to lung, with each scenario requiring a different management approach. Because there are no definitive imaging characteristics that differentiate primary lung cancer from metastatic PIM, the patient underwent a transthoracic biopsy of the left upper lobe nodule. The histopathology of the lung nodule (Fig 3C) demonstrated epithelioid and spindled cells in a whorled pattern (outlined by a dashed white oval) with intranuclear inclusions (yellow arrows) and numerous psammoma bodies (white arrows), features consistent with a benign grade meningioma. The tumor cells were weakly positive for epithelial membrane antigen (aka mucin 1) (Fig 3D) but negative for CD34 (a hematopoietic and endothelial cell marker) and STAT6 (a marker for solitary fibrous tumors). Although lung epithelial cells may also be positive for epithelial membrane antigen, this nonspecific tumor marker is useful to distinguish meningiomas from schwannomas (Fig 3D).

Given the focal nature of the PIM at both the skull and lung sites and the relative stability of the lung lesion, the meningiomas at both sites were observed without surgery or nonsurgical treatment. Because there was interval growth of the calvarial PIM after 18 months of observation, the patient underwent radiation therapy of the skull over 6 weeks with a slight decrease in size of the primary tumor. A chest CT scan obtained 6 years after presentation noted a 1 mm increase in the diameter of the untreated left upper lobe nodule (metastatic PIM). The patient died several months later in home hospice care from COVID-19.

Clinical Pearls

1. Meningiomas are classified as either intracranial (intradural) meningioma or the much rarer extradural meningioma, with the latter including extraosseous meningioma (type I) or PIM (types II and III).

2. All types of meningiomas rarely metastasize; PIM with local extension to the dura mater (type III) appears to have greater propensity for metastasis.

3. The most common site of metastasis of an intracranial meningioma is the lung and may present as a solitary lung nodule.

4. A solitary lung nodule should be evaluated and monitored in the context of risk factors for lung cancer and other clinical findings. Because primary lung cancer is more common than a PIM that metastasizes to the lung, the presence of a PIM and lung nodule, especially if solitary, does not obviate the need to also biopsy the lung nodule.
