Comparative effectiveness of antidiabetic therapies on hepatic decompensation in patients with type 2 diabetes: A target trial emulation
Authors: Jonggi Choi, Ana Verma, Vy H Nguyen, Eric Przybyszewski, Jiunn Song, Allison Carroll, Megan Michta, Erik Almazan, Tracey G Simon, Raymond T Chung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsThe comparative effectiveness of antidiabetic therapies on hepatic outcomes in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is not well established.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective new-user cohort study emulating a target trial using electronic health records from the Mass General Brigham health system (USA) between January 2012 and June 2024. Adults with T2DM who newly initiated sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2is), glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP4is), or sulfonylureas were included. Propensity scores were estimated using prespecified clinical and high-dimensional covariates, and overlap weighting was applied to achieve covariate balance.ResultsAmong 38,524 eligible patients, 12,344 initiated GLP-1RAs, 5,233 SGLT2is, 3,717 DPP4is, and 17,230 sulfonylureas. Over a median follow-up of 3.1 years, 1,743 hepatic decompensation events occurred. In the intention-to-treat analysis, GLP-1RAs (hazard ratio [HR] 0.58, 95% CI 0.38–0.88) and SGLT2is (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.43–0.98) were associated with significantly lower risk compared with sulfonylureas. Pairwise analyses also showed reduced risk with GLP-1RAs (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.39–0.89) and SGLT2is (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.43–1.00) compared with DPP4is, with no significant difference between GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is. In the per-protocol analysis (1,151 events), GLP-1RAs (HR 0.43, 95% CI 0.22–0.82) remained strongly protective, while SGLT2is showed a trend toward reduced risk (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.33–1.12). Sensitivity analyses excluding early events or using alternative weighting approaches produced consistent results.ConclusionIn patients with T2DM, initiation of GLP-1RAs or SGLT2is was associated with substantially lower risk of hepatic decompensation compared with sulfonylureas and DPP4is. These findings support their preferential use in clinical practice.Impact and implicationsThis study provides robust real-world evidence on the comparative effectiveness of four major antidiabetic drug classes in reducing hepatic decompensation among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Our findings suggest that glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors are associated with substantially lower risk of hepatic decompensation compared with sulfonylureas and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, supporting their preferential use in patients with coexisting diabetes and liver disease risk. These results are particularly relevant for clinicians managing patients with type 2 diabetes who may have unrecognized metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease or other chronic liver diseases. In practice, these findings can help guide therapeutic decision-making by integrating liver-related outcomes into antidiabetic drug selection. However, future prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and clarify the mechanisms underlying the observed benefits.Keywords: Hepatic decompensation, Anti-diabetic treatment

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is now the most prevalent chronic liver disease (CLD) worldwide, including in the United States, where its incidence is increasing rapidly.1 Approximately 28% of individuals with MASLD also have type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and up to 65% of individuals with T2DM are estimated to have MASLD.2 The recent shift in nomenclature from nonalcoholic steatohepatitis to MASLD underscores the central role of metabolic dysfunction in disease pathogenesis.3 MASLD progresses through hepatic steatosis and fibrosis, potentially advancing to cirrhosis, hepatic decompensation, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Notably, MASLD-related HCC is among the fastest-growing causes of HCC in the United States.4 Several pharmacologic therapies – many derived from diabetes drug classes, such as sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) – have shown promise in slowing liver disease progression.5,6 For example, a pooled meta-analysis also showed that luseogliflozin, an SGLT2i, improved steatosis, fibrosis markers, and cardiometabolic profiles in patients with diabetes, particularly in those with liver injury.7 Additionally, emerging agents such as resmetirom and efruxifermin are under clinical investigation for their anti-fibrotic effects.8,9

However, due to the prolonged natural history of MASLD and the typical design of registration trials, which often rely on histologic or biochemical surrogate endpoints, it remains challenging to assess the impact of these therapies on long-term clinical outcomes such as hepatic decompensation and HCC. Well-designed observational studies using large-scale real-world data can provide valuable complementary evidence. Previous studies, primarily based on administrative databases, have reported on the association between specific antidiabetic agents and liver-related outcomes.[10], [11], [12], [13] Recent evidence highlights the additional benefits of SGLT2i. A Japanese study found that relative to dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP4is), SGLT2is reduced the incidence of esophageal varices and extrahepatic cancer in patients with T2DM and suspected MASLD.14 However, comprehensive comparative effectiveness analyses across multiple drug classes, including SGLT2is, GLP-1RAs, DPP4is, and sulfonylureas, remain limited. Moreover, most existing studies have focused solely on populations with MASLD.[10], [11], [12], [13],15

Given that CLDs of various etiologies – including viral hepatitis and alcohol-related liver disease – share a common pathophysiologic progression involving inflammation, fibrosis, and cirrhosis, our analysis was not restricted to MASLD alone. Moreover, a substantial proportion of patients with T2DM are likely to harbor undiagnosed MASLD, even in the absence of a formal diagnosis. For these reasons, we broadened the study population to include individuals with T2DM across diverse clinical contexts, thereby capturing both patients with recognized CLD etiologies and those with potentially unrecognized MASLD. This design allowed for a more comprehensive evaluation of the comparative effectiveness of multiple antidiabetic drug classes on long-term hepatic outcomes in routine practice.

Patients and methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective, new-user cohort study emulating a target trial to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of four antidiabetic drug classes – GLP-1RAs, SGLT2is, DPP4is, and sulfonylureas – in patients with T2DM (Table 1). Data were obtained from the Mass General Brigham Research Patient Data Registry, a large electronic health record (EHR) repository comprising over 4 million patients across multiple hospitals in Massachusetts, USA. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Massachusetts General Hospital (No. 2024P001205) and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design.

Eligible patients were adults (≥18 years) with documented diagnoses of T2DM who newly initiated one of the four study drug classes between January 1, 2012, and June 30, 2024. A 1-year washout period was required, during which no prescriptions for any of the study drugs were issued. The index date was defined as the first prescription date of the assigned drug classes. Patients were excluded if they had a history of HCC, hepatic decompensation, liver transplantation, kidney transplantation, dialysis, or bariatric surgery prior to the index date; were younger than 18 years old; or received same-day prescriptions for two or more of the study drug classes. Follow-up began at the index date and continued until the occurrence of hepatic decompensation, death, loss to follow-up, or administrative censoring on December 31, 2024.

Exposures and covariates

Exposure was defined as the new initiation of one of the four drug classes. Baseline covariates were assessed during the 3 years preceding the index date and included demographics (age, sex, race), CLD etiology and cirrhosis, cardiometabolic comorbidities (e.g. hypertension, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, cerebrovascular accident, peripheral vascular disease, Tables S1 and S2), and concurrent medication use (e.g. metformin, insulin, thiazolidinediones, statins, aspirin, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers, and diuretics, Table S3). To account for differences in healthcare utilization, we further adjusted for the number of diabetes care visits, prescriptions, blood tests, encounters, and radiologic tests during the baseline year. Laboratory values were not included due to >20% missingness. In addition to prespecified clinical variables, we employed an algorithmic high-dimensional approach. Using five EHR domains (outpatient and encounter diagnoses, laboratory tests, radiology tests, and prescriptions), the 100 most frequent covariates were extracted. To ensure robustness, only variables present in at least 50 patients per treatment group were retained. If an algorithmically derived covariate overlapped with a predefined clinical covariate, the predefined version was used for propensity score estimation.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was hepatic decompensation, defined as the first occurrence of ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, variceal bleeding, or hepatorenal syndrome, identified using validated ICD-9/10 codes (Table S1). The outcome date was the earliest recorded diagnosis after the index date. No secondary outcomes were prespecified in this study.

Statistical analysis

We sought to address potential confounding and informative censoring using a weighting strategy closely aligned with prior target trial emulation studies. Propensity scores were estimated for each of the four treatment groups using multinomial logistic regression models that incorporated all predefined clinical covariates together with algorithmically selected high-dimensional covariates. Instead of conventional inverse probability of treatment weighting, we applied overlap weighting as the primary analytic approach.[16], [17], [18] Overlap weighting has been shown to achieve exact balance in baseline covariates between treatment groups while reducing variance inflation from extreme weights.[16], [17], [18] Each individual’s weight was proportional to the probability of being assigned to the opposite treatment group, thereby focusing estimation on the region of covariate overlap and improving robustness.19,20 Balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs); after weighting, all pairwise SMDs were <0.1, indicating excellent covariate balance (Fig. S1). Weighted event rates were expressed as incidence per 1,000 person-years (PYs) with 95% CIs. The main effect estimates were derived from pooled logistic regression models with time modeled flexibly using restricted cubic splines (four degrees of freedom). These models provided hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs and allowed for calculation of absolute risk differences between treatment groups at prespecified time points. Kaplan–Meier curves weighted by overlap weights were also generated to depict cumulative incidence of hepatic decompensation over follow-up.

The main analysis was performed under the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, in which patients were followed from the index date regardless of subsequent treatment discontinuation or switching. We additionally performed a per-protocol (PP) analysis, in which follow-up was censored at the time of non-adherence to the index drug.19 To account for informative censoring, inverse probability of censoring weights (IPCW) were estimated separately for each drug class. These probabilities were modeled using logistic regression including both baseline covariates and time-varying variables (updated comorbidities and concomitant medications). Stabilized IPCWs were calculated, and extreme values were truncated at the 99.9th percentile to minimize undue influence. The final PP weights were the product of overlap weights and IPCWs, following methodological approaches described in prior studies of target trial emulation.11,[19], [20], [21]

We also performed prespecified subgroup analyses stratified by key clinical characteristics including age, sex, BMI, fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index, and baseline use of metformin, statins, and insulin. These analyses used the same overlap-weighted pooled logistic regression models as the main analysis, and HRs were estimated separately within each subgroup. Pairwise treatment comparisons were also conducted within subgroups to explore effect heterogeneity.

Several sensitivity analyses were prespecified. First, we re-estimated treatment effects using baseline inverse probability of treatment weighting rather than overlap weighting to evaluate consistency of findings across weighting approaches. Second, we excluded outcomes occurring within the first 90 days after the index date to mitigate potential reverse causality and ensure sufficient exposure. To further assess residual confounding, we conducted negative outcome control analyses using incident lung cancer and fracture as outcomes, applying the same ITT analytic framework. These outcomes were chosen given their lack of established causal relationship with the studied antidiabetic therapies. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p value <0.05.

Results

Study population

A total of 68,433 adults with T2DM who newly initiated sulfonylurea, DPP4is, SGLT2is, or GLP-1RAs between 2012 and 2024 were identified. After applying eligibility criteria and exclusions, 29,909 patients were removed, yielding a final analytic cohort of 38,524 patients (Fig. 1). Of these, 12,344 initiated GLP-1RAs, 5,233 initiated SGLT2is, 3,717 initiated DPP4is, and 17,230 initiated sulfonylureas (Table S4). After application of overlap weighting, baseline characteristics were well balanced across groups, with all pairwise SMDs <0.1 (Table 2, Figs S1,S2). Overall, patients were predominantly White, with mean BMI ranging from 29 to 31 kg/m2 across groups. Hypertension and dyslipidemia were the most frequent comorbidities, and approximately one-quarter of patients had a diagnosis of MASH. Measures of healthcare utilization in the year prior to treatment initiation – including outpatient visits, prescriptions, laboratory tests, and radiologic encounters – were also well balanced following weighting. The distribution of overlap weights is presented in Table S5, and the specific medications included in each drug class are provided in Table S6.

Hepatic decompensation

In the ITT analysis, during a median follow-up of 3.1 years (IQR 1.1–6.8), 1,743 hepatic decompensation events were observed across the cohort. Crude incidence rates per 1,000 PYs were lowest among GLP-1RA initiators (6.6; 217 events/32,868 PYs), followed by sulfonylureas (11.7; 1,124 events/95,862 PYs), DPP4is (12.5; 231 events/18,519 PYs), and SGLT2is (13.2; 171 events/12,929 PYs) (Fig. 2). After overlap weighting and pooled logistic regression, GLP-1RAs were associated with a significantly lower risk of hepatic decompensation compared with sulfonylureas (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.38–0.88), as were SGLT2is (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.43–0.98). DPP4is, in contrast, showed no significant difference compared with sulfonylureas (HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.72–1.34). Pairwise comparisons further indicated reduced risk with GLP-1RAs (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.39–0.89) and SGLT2is (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.43–1.00) compared with DPP4is, while the risk was not significantly different between GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.55–1.46) (Tables 2 and S7). Absolute risk reductions per 1,000 PYs ranged from 4.4 to 6.0.

In the PP framework incorporating adherence weighting, 1,151 hepatic decompensation events were identified. Crude incidence rates per 1,000 PYs were again lowest among GLP-1RA initiators (4.62; 84 events/18,196 PYs), followed by sulfonylureas (12.4; 865 events/69,889 PYs), SGLT2is (17.2; 105 events/6,103 PYs), and DPP4is (19.6; 97 events/4,953 PYs) (Fig. 3). Relative to sulfonylureas, GLP-1RAs were associated with a substantially reduced risk (HR 0.43, 95% CI 0.22–0.82), whereas SGLT2is showed a trend toward reduced risk that did not reach statistical significance (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.33–1.12). In pairwise contrasts, both GLP-1RAs (HR 0.37, 95% CI 0.19–0.71) and SGLT2is (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.29–0.97) were associated with lower risk compared with DPP4is. No significant difference was observed between GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is (HR 1.53, 95% CI 0.72–3.25) (Table S8).

Subgroup analysis

We conducted stratified analyses across key clinical subgroups including age, sex, BMI, FIB-4 index, and medication use (metformin, statin, and insulin) (Table S9, Fig. 4). Compared to sulfonylureas, GLP-1RAs were consistently associated with a lower risk of hepatic decompensation across most subgroups, with statistically significant reductions observed in older adults (age ≥60; HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.36–0.97), females (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.44–0.77), males (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.39–0.66), individuals with BMI ≥30 kg/m2 (HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.27–0.93), and those not receiving metformin (HR 0.46, 95% CI 0.26–0.82), statins (HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.28–0.88), or insulin (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.31–0.89). GLP-1RAs also demonstrated a significantly lower risk in patients with missing FIB-4 data (HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.28–0.92). When compared to DPP-4 inhibitors, GLP-1RAs showed similar patterns, with significant reductions noted in females (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.44–0.77), males (HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.39–0.66), and patients with unavailable FIB-4 data (HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.27–0.89) (Fig. 4).

Sensitivity analyses

Several sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the findings (Table S10). First, when inverse probability of treatment weighting was applied instead of overlap weighting, results were consistent, showing protective associations for GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is compared with sulfonylureas (Figs S4 and S5). Second, exclusion of events occurring within 90 days of treatment initiation yielded similar estimates (Fig. S6). Analyses of negative outcome controls, including incident lung cancer and fractures, revealed no statistically significant differences across treatment groups (Table S11), supporting the validity of the causal inference and suggesting that residual confounding was unlikely to fully explain the observed associations.

Discussion

In this large retrospective target trial emulation study of patients with T2DM, initiation of GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is was significantly associated with a reduced risk of hepatic decompensation compared with sulfonylureas and DPP4is. These associations were robust across both ITT and PP frameworks and were consistent with sensitivity analyses. These findings suggest that GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is may provide liver-related benefits beyond glycemic control in this population.

Our results extend prior observational and mechanistic studies suggesting that GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is may improve liver-related outcomes.[10], [11], [12] While earlier investigations largely focused on patients with established CLD or MASLD, our analysis included a broader population of patients with T2DM, acknowledging that a substantial proportion are likely to harbor undiagnosed MASLD. By comparing four major antidiabetic drug classes head-to-head in a real-world setting, we provide comprehensive evidence on their relative effectiveness in reducing the risk of hepatic decompensation. The consistent protective associations observed for GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is, in contrast to the neutral effects of DPP4is and sulfonylureas, highlight the potential added value of incretin- and SGLT2-based therapies for liver outcomes. This broader inclusion may also explain discrepancies with earlier studies restricted to MASLD cohorts. For instance, Jang et al. reported no significant advantage of SGLT2i over DPP4i in liver-related outcomes among patients with MASLD alone.11 However, their definition of liver-related outcomes differed from ours, and the study's limited event count may have constrained statistical power. Notably, their reported HR of 0.67 for SGLT2i vs. DPP4i suggests a potential benefit despite the absence of statistical significance. Similarly, Pradhan et al. did not evaluate long-term hepatic outcomes such as decompensation or HCC, instead examining the risk of incident MASLD among users of GLP-1RA and SGLT2i compared with DPP4i.22 While SGLT2i use was associated with a significantly lower risk, GLP-1RAs demonstrated a favorable trend (HR 0.86; 95% CI 0.73–1.01), narrowly missing statistical significance. These results are directionally consistent with our findings and further support the hypothesis that GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is may confer liver-protective effects compared with DPP4is.

The results are also consistent with those of Simon et al., who studied a population with cirrhosis and reported a significantly lower risk of decompensation with GLP-1RA use compared with sulfonylureas and DPP4is, but not compared with SGLT2is.10 Although more than 90% of our cohort did not have cirrhosis, a similar pattern was observed in the cirrhosis subgroup analysis, lending further support to the findings of Simon et al.10 Notably, our study benefits from a longer median follow-up, facilitating more robust detection of long-term hepatic outcomes. These results also align with findings from Wang et al., who compared GLP-1RA with non-GLP-1RA therapies in patients with T2DM.13 That study reported a slightly lower risk of decompensation with GLP-1RAs vs. SGLT2is. However, among patients not receiving insulin, a key potential confounder, the difference between GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is was not statistically significant.13 Furthermore, the analysis accounted for the use of agents with potential hepatoprotective effects – including aspirin, metformin, and statins – by incorporating them as covariates and exploring their influence in subgroup analyses. Importantly, the associations of GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is with improved liver-related outcomes were largely consistent irrespective of concurrent use of these agents. Although some subgroup comparisons did not achieve statistical significance, the consistent direction of effect and presence of numerically favorable HRs suggest that the null findings may be attributable to limited statistical power resulting from smaller sample sizes and low event rates within subgroups.

There is growing mechanistic and clinical evidence supporting the hepatoprotective potential of GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is. GLP-1, an incretin hormone, enhances insulin secretion, reduces appetite, and improves insulin sensitivity. GLP-1RAs have demonstrated histologic resolution of steatohepatitis, along with reductions in liver enzyme levels, hepatic steatosis, and inflammation in trials involving MASLD populations.23,24 These agents reduce hepatic lipogenesis, promote beta-oxidation, and may suppress hepatic inflammation and oxidative stress, which are key drivers of liver disease progression.25 Similarly, SGLT2is, through inhibition of renal glucose reabsorption and promotion of weight loss, may improve hepatic outcomes via both glycemic-dependent and independent mechanisms. Evidence from small randomized trials and preclinical studies suggests that SGLT2is reduce hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis markers, while enhancing mitochondrial function and fatty acid oxidation.26,27 In contrast, sulfonylureas and DPP4is exhibit limited metabolic pleiotropy. Sulfonylureas stimulate insulin release in a glucose-independent manner and are associated with weight gain and hypoglycemia. DPP4is modestly enhance endogenous GLP-1 levels but are subject to rapid enzymatic degradation and produce only limited incretin activity relative to pharmacologic GLP-1RAs.28 Although animal models have shown that DPP-4 inhibition reduces hepatic steatosis and inflammation, these benefits have not consistently translated into improved liver-related outcomes in clinical settings.[29], [30], [31]

This study has several strengths. First, we used a large, well-constructed multicenter EHR database from the Mass General Brigham network, allowing for robust assessment across diverse patient populations. Second, we applied rigorous confounding control in addition to prespecified clinical covariates and incorporated healthcare utilization measures to account for possible differences in health care system approach with a high-dimensional algorithmic approach to capture additional potential confounders. This combination minimized the risk of unmeasured confounding. Third, we used both ITT and PP frameworks, with the latter incorporating IPCWs for non-adherence, and found consistent results. Finally, the use of overlap weighting provided excellent covariate balance, ensuring comparability across groups.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although laboratory values such as liver enzymes or fibrosis scores (e.g. FIB-4) would provide direct measures of liver disease severity, these data were frequently missing in patients with T2DM due to the nature of routine practice and therefore could not be incorporated into the main analysis. The median follow-up was 3.1 years, which might be sufficient for event accrual, but the duration was shorter for newer therapies such as GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is compared with sulfonylureas and DPP4is, potentially limiting long-term comparisons. As with all observational studies, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded, though our extensive covariate adjustment and high-dimensional modeling approach mitigate this concern. We could not directly adjust for alcohol consumption, which is a critical determinant of liver-related outcomes. While patients with ICD codes for alcohol-associated liver disease were excluded, the potential for residual confounding from undocumented alcohol use remains a limitation. Additionally, although statins and metformin are known to confer hepatic benefits, we could not distinguish their individual or synergistic effects in combination with antidiabetic agents. Nonetheless, their potential influence was accounted for through baseline covariate adjustment and time-varying modeling. Moreover, as liver-related outcomes likely require sustained therapeutic exposure, events occurring immediately after treatment initiation may not reflect true drug effects. Therefore, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding events within the first 90 days, which yielded results consistent with the main analysis. Finally, while the inclusion of patients with T2DM – many of whom may have undiagnosed MASLD – reflects real-world practice, it also introduces heterogeneity in underlying liver disease burden.

In conclusion, initiation of GLP-1RAs or SGLT2is was associated with significantly lower risks of hepatic decompensation compared with sulfonylureas and DPP4is among patients with T2DM. These findings support the preferential use of GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is in patients with T2DM, not only for metabolic benefits but also for potential liver-related protection. Further prospective studies and mechanistic trials are warranted to validate these associations and to better define the pathways through which these agents may alter the natural history of liver disease in diabetes.
