Efficacy of Enamel Matrix Derivative in Periodontal Regeneration Defects: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Frank Mayta-Tovalino, Carlos Diaz-Arocutipa, John Barja-Ore, Adrian V. Hernandez
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Aim:To evaluate the efficacy of enamel matrix derivative (EMD) on periodontal regeneration defects.Materials and Methods:Four databases were searched until October 2021. Experimental animal studies evaluating the efficacy of EMD were used. The primary outcomes were bone formation (BF) and cementum formation (CF). The secondary outcomes were junctional epithelium (JE), gingival recession (GR), and clinical attachment level (CAL). Measures of effect were mean difference (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Random-effects model were used for all meta-analyses. The Systematic Review Centre for Laboratory animal Experimentation tool was used to assess the risk of bias.Results:Seven experimental animal studies (n = 40) used with a maximum follow-up period of 3 months. Compared to control, EMD did not significantly reduce BF (MD 0.02 mm; 95% CI − 1.91–1.96; I2 = 89%). However, it increased CF (MD 1.38 mm; 95% CI 0.01–2.74; I2 = 55%). For secondary outcomes it was found that compared to control, EMD only significantly reduced JE (MD − 0.54 mm; 95% CI − 1.06 to − 0.02; I2 = 55%). However, the other secondary outcomes were not significant as in the case of GR (MD − 3. 88 mm; 95% CI − 68.29–60.53; I2 = 82%), and in CAL (MD 0.02 mm; 95% CI − 0.29–0.39; I2 = 38%). Finally, according to the risk of bias assessment, all included studies had a high risk of bias.Conclusion:EMD had no effect on BF values while it did not reduce CF. Otherwise, in the secondary outcomes, EMD only significantly reduced JE values and had no effect on GR and CAL.

Introduction

Periodontal disease is an infectious process that generates the destruction of the supporting tissues of the tooth, causing an alteration in the connective tissue, alveolar bone, root cementum, and periodontal fibers. Eventually this disease starts as a simple gingivitis and could even lead to tooth loss. Currently, there are several therapeutic alternatives to treat periodontitis. However, all of them seek the mechanical elimination of the pathogenic bacterial plaque to prevent the progression of the lesion.[1]

Periodontal regeneration is one of the most indicated alternatives for the treatment of periodontitis. This alternative seeks to regenerate the affected tissues using some proteins derived from dental enamel.[234] On the other hand, the placement of bone grafts or bone derivatives of synthetic origin, which have osteogenic potential for guided bone regeneration, has also been indicated.[56]

Specifically, enamel matrix derivative (EMD) is composed of enamel matrix proteins, which play a fundamental role in the growth and development of the dental root. EMD favors the stimulation of protein synthesis such as transforming growth factors-β1, BMP-2, BMP-7, fibroblast growth factor fibroblast 2, insulin-like growth factor 1, platelet-derived growth factor-AB, vascular endothelial growth factor, and osteopontin.[478]

EMD has been successfully used in different regenerative periodontal procedures,[478] because it enhances the production and differentiation of osteoblasts and periodontal ligament cells. EMD contains different proteins such as enamelin, ameloblastin, some proteinases, with amelogenin accounting for 95% of its total protein content.[910]

Given the limited evidence available on the efficacy of EMD application in randomized clinical studies evaluating periodontal regeneration outcomes, we decided to evaluate preclinical (animal) studies as most studies had this design. Thus, we systematically evaluated the efficacy of EMD on periodontal regeneration defects in randomized controlled animal experimental studies.

Materials and Methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analysis 2020 statement was used for the reporting of our study.[11] In addition, this review was registered in PROSPERO database (CRD42022290738).

Search of studies

On December, 2021, Scopus, EMBASE, PubMed, and Web of Science were searched. Articles related to the topic were searched in any language. The search strategy was adapted according to the needs of each of the databases evaluated [Supplementary contents].

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) Randomized controlled animal experimental studies evaluating the effects of any dose and duration of EMD on osteoblast cells and regeneration were selected; (2) Studies that report at least one primary or secondary outcome and have another intervention, no treatment or placebo as control group, and (3) Studies involving monkeys, dogs, sheep, minipigs, and rats with bone defects. Cohorts and case-control studies, case series, case reports, systematic reviews, conference abstracts, and editorials were excluded from the study.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were new bone formation (BF, continuous measured in mm) and new cementum formation (CF, continuous measured in mm). The secondary outcomes were junctional epithelium migration (JE, continuous measured in mm), gingival recession (GR, continuous measured in mm), and clinical attachment level (CAL, continuous measured in mm).

Selection of studies

Two authors (FMT and JBO) individually and independently evaluated the available studies according to the previously established inclusion and exclusion criteria. The studies were selected according to the evaluation of the abstracts. The remaining studies were then evaluated in full text. All discrepancies were resolved by the consensus with a third author (CDA).

Data extraction and management

The data were obtained independently in duplicate by the authors (JBO and FMT). An extraction sheet was used to establish the characteristics of the studies to be collected. Studies with incomplete information or studies that had evaluated other outcomes were not considered during the extraction. The extraction sheet considered data such as author, country, year, sample, type of control and intervention, and primary and secondary outcomes per intervention arm and other relevant details.

Risk of bias assessment

Reporting quality was judged as “high,” “moderate” or “low.” Risk of bias assessment was performed using a modification of Systematic Review Center for Laboratory animal Experimentation (SYRCLE) tool for animal studies and judged as “high,” “low,” or “unclear.” This tool had 10 domains (sequence generation, baseline characteristics, allocation concealment, random housing, blinding of personnel, random outcome assessment, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective outcome reporting, and other sources of bias).[12] Finally, two authors (JBO and FMT) independently performed the risk of bias assessment, while discrepancies were resolved by the consensus with a third author (CDA). The evidence quality was assessed for each outcome and presented in summary of findings (SoF) tables. The SoF tables were created using the GRADEpro GDT software (Copyright © 2021, McMaster University and Evidence Prime Inc.).

Statistical analysis

The inverse variance method and random effects models were used to evaluate the effects of EMD on primary and secondary outcomes. Effects were expressed as mean differences (MD) for all continuous outcomes, with their respective confidence intervals (95% CI). The between-study variance was estimated using the Paule-Mandel method.[13] Heterogeneity of effects between experimental studies was calculated using the I2, with the following grades: 0%–30% (low), 30%–60% (moderate), and >60% (high).[14] The metacont function from the meta library of R 3.5.1 (www.r-project.org) was used for all analyses. P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis showed that, compared to the control group, only JE is significantly reduced with EMD. No significant effects were evidenced on BF, as well as on some secondary outcomes such as GR and CAL. On the other hand, we found increase in CF in favor of the control group. This is probably due to the mineral composition of the cement, which does not react with the EMD components. In addition, it is known that since dental cement has a large acellular component, it is difficult for it to self-repair.

Periodontal defects are very prevalent diseases of the oral cavity,[22] which according to the literature can traditionally be treated with bone grafts; however, new therapeutics have been proposed that usually employ EMD, which is composed of different proteins, mainly amelogenins.[23] Although its mechanism of action is not fully elucidated,[24] its key role in osteogenesis and expression of osteoblast markers has been recognized.[2526] It has been described that EMD could have a positive effect on the regeneration of soft and hard tissues around the tooth, but its performance may be greater when it is combined with other treatment alternatives, although this efficacy is not entirely clear.[27]

The available scientific evidence, as in the systematic review of Esposito et al. 2009,[24] evaluated a total of 13 studies. They concluded in their meta-analysis that bone defects treated with EMD showed significant improvements in probing attachment levels (PALs) MD of 1.1 mm, (95% CI 0.61–1.55) and a reduction in pocket depths (PD) (0.9 mm, 95% CI 0.44–1.31) compared to sites treated with placebo or control, although a high degree of heterogeneity was found. On the other hand, according to another systematic review also performed by Esposito et al. 2004,[28] they found that, in the eight studies, bone defects treated with EMD showed significant improvements in PAL, weighted mean difference (WMD) of 1.3 mm, (95% CI 0.84–1.78) and a reduction in PPD, WMD of 0.96 mm, (95% CI 0.50–1.41) compared to flap surgery.

On the other hand, Graziani et al.[29] found that the benefit of EMD for CAL was MD 1.2 mm (95% CI 0.9–1.4), whereas, for PPD reduction, it was MD 1.2 mm (95% CI 0.8–1.5). Although a potential risk of bias was identified in the studies evaluated, finally, in the research by Matarasso et al.[30] found that after the application of EMD in intraosseous defects only the CAL gain increased by 3.32 mm, Median 3.40; [95% CI 3.28–3.52]. In addition, the mean pocket depths (PD) reduction was 4.22 mm (Median 4.10; 95% CI 3.96–4.24) in bone defects treated with EMD and in bone graft treated areas an average of 4.12 mm (Median 4.0; 95% CI 3.88–4.12) was found; however, this study does not clearly state what measure of effect it used to evaluate its outcomes.

Our research was focused on experimental studies that evaluated the effects of EMD on osteoblastic cells and their regeneration, regardless of duration and dose, compared to placebo or no treatment. In addition, we evaluated some important outcomes such as JE, GR, CF that have not been previously considered in other meta-analyses; for example, increased cement formation, which is consistent with the study by Fan and Wu.[27]

In relation to secondary outcomes, which have been analyzed in other studies. In the case of CAL, unlike our study, various meta-analyses[24282931] demonstrated a significant improvement in the areas treated with EMD; this contrast in the findings could be due to the high level of heterogeneity among the studies, mainly the duration of the interventions. On the other hand, we also did not find significant differences in BF, GR, between the group treated with EMD and the control group; contrary to this finding, Matarasso et al.[30] who concluded that in the sites treated with EMD there is a significant increase when bone grafting is added to these results.

The results of this study suggest that it is still premature to state that the use of EMDs could have significant potential to stimulate periodontal tissue regeneration. Therefore, the findings should be taken with caution for a correct clinical decision making in patients with periodontal disease.

The clinical importance of these findings is that EMD could be used as a potential therapeutic alternative in periodontal disease. Because this pathology can have serious effects on oral and general health, the use of new biomaterials to achieve bone regeneration is paramount.

The main strength of our study was that we used a wide coverage in the search for information, which was carried out in the main health sciences databases, such as Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, and EMBASE, using complex search strategies with specific key terms. In addition, to guarantee our findings, we used updated, valid, and widely used tools and methodologies such as the SYRCLE tool, respectively.

Limitations

The authors acknowledge that this study has some limitations in sample size; however, it could still be valuable, even though there are few published articles in the world. Because the limited number of studies on EMD in periodontal regeneration highlights the need for further research in this context. This study could provide valuable insights into the potential benefits of using EMD in periodontal regeneration. In addition, the studies could provide valuable information to better understand this research problem. Thus, new evidence is generated to make robust clinical decisions.

The high risk of bias of the experimental studies as well as the heterogeneity of effects of these was found. In addition, analyses were performed on a small number of studies with very small sample sizes; however, it is relevant to consider that the primary findings of periodontal regeneration (JE and BF) are a significant contribution to know the effect of EMD on periodontal regeneration.

Conclusion

Within the study limitations and heterogeneity of biomodels, EMD had no effect on BF values and CF. EMD only significantly reduced JE values and had no effect on GR and CAL. Therefore, studies evaluating this relationship in humans and with longer follow-up are needed to better characterize the effects of EMD on these periodontal outcomes.
