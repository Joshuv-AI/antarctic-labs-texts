Intermediate-term Outcomes of Glaucoma Drainage Device Implantation in Adults of Advanced Age
Authors: Mark Werner, Stephanie Byun, Rebecca Shin, Katherine Freeman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim and backgroundTo compare outcomes of glaucoma tube shunt surgery in patients aged 85 years and older to younger adults.MethodsMedical records were reviewed involving 207 consecutive tube shunt surgeries performed by one surgeon between 2013 and 2019. Baseline characteristics between group A (adults aged <85) and group B (aged ≥85) were compared. Changes from baseline to 12 months were compared between groups.ResultsOne-year follow-up was completed for 80 of 125 eyes (group A) and 49 of 82 eyes (group B). Intraocular pressure (IOP) and number of medications were statistically significantly reduced at 12 months, with no significant difference between groups (group A: 33.1 ± 11.2 → 13.8 ± 4.5 mm Hg, group B: IOP 33.6 ± 10.2 → 14.0 ± 4.3 mm Hg; p = 0.7168; group A: 3.2 ± 1.2 → 1.6 ± 1.3 meds, group B: 3.2 ± 1.1 → 1.8 ± 1.4 meds; p = 0.8404). Success rate was 109/128 (85.1%) overall, with no significant difference between groups (p = 0.2625). Introduction

Glaucoma treatment presently consists of maintaining intraocular pressure (IOP) at a level where the risk of vision loss is adequately minimized, sometimes estimated as a target IOP number, unique to each eye of each patient. Refractory glaucoma is present when IOP cannot be maintained at or below a target level, despite maximally tolerated intraocular medications, failed laser and/or surgical treatment, or a combination of these. Since their development in 1968 by Molteno,1 glaucoma drainage devices have been employed increasingly for refractory glaucoma to control IOP.2

Outcomes of glaucoma drainage device implantation have been well established, but most patients studied to date have not been 85 years old or above at the time of surgery,3,9 a group referred to as the “oldest old”.10 These patients present specific challenges, including multiple comorbidities, polypharmacy, variable life expectancy, biological differences, and social issues, with limited data concerning their relationship with outcomes. Understanding expected outcomes may help in clinical decision-making and counseling of patients in this challenging demographic treatment group. This study was designed as a retrospective comparative cohort study to compare clinical outcomes of participants in the “oldest old” age-group undergoing glaucoma drainage device implantation surgery with a concurrent control group of younger adults, in order to uncover any differences in clinical outcomes between groups categorized by age.

Patients and Methods

Design, Patients, and Setting

This study was approved by the Florida Atlantic University Health Sciences Institutional Review Board. None of the authors has any relevant conflicts of interest.

Practice management software (Nextgen, NXGN Management LLC, Irvine, CA) was searched by CPT code and provider in order to select eligible participants. Patients were determined to be eligible for the two cohorts if they had consecutive glaucoma drainage device surgeries performed by a single surgeon (MAW) between 2013 and 2019. When study participants had undergone multiple glaucoma drainage device implantations in one or both eyes, the first surgery performed was selected for analysis. Participants were divided into group A (controls, age 21–84) and group B (study participants, age ≥85) for analysis. Multivariate statistical analysis was used to account for possible differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups.

Data were collected from the last preoperative evaluation for the surgery in question, as well as the operative report and each available visit corresponding to predetermined time points after surgery. The exact number of days postoperatively for each time point was generally within 10% of the stated time point. Baseline demographic information, past ocular history, past medical history, and operative characteristics were recorded and used as covariates. Operative details, including type of tube, location in the eye, modifications to tube flow, other concurrent procedures, and complications during the procedure, were considered as potential effect modifiers. Postoperative data that served as clinical outcomes included number of medications, visual acuity (VA) (logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution or logMAR VA), IOP, and presence of complications. IOP was almost exclusively measured by the primary surgeon using Goldmann applanation tonometry on calibrated instruments (MAW). Complications were recorded as having occurred at the time point at which they were noted or at the next time point if they occurred between time points. Reoperations were recorded along with the length of time between the initial surgery and reoperation, the nature of the reoperation, and the indication for surgery. The analysis was limited to the 1-year follow-up time point to allow for adequate numbers of patients, a suitable follow-up interval, and avoidance of bias due to the potential for differential dropout; thus, the data could be considered to be right censored.

The initial decision for surgery was made on an individual basis with the patient and family, when the risk of vision loss within the patient's lifetime likely outweighed the risk of surgery. Participants were chosen from the surgeon's single-location, multispecialty suburban private practice. Patients came from a mix of continued care adult glaucoma patients and subspecialty adult glaucoma referrals from other doctors, both within and outside of the practice. Most tube shunt surgeries performed within the practice were performed by the primary surgeon (MAW), and the few tube shunts placed by other surgeons were excluded.

Intervention

The type of implant (Ahmed FP7, New World Medical, Rancho Cucamonga, CA; Baerveldt BG103-250 or BG101-350, Johnson and Johnson Vision, Santa Ana, CA) selected was based on clinical judgment. A small number of Molteno3 implants were also placed (Nova Eye Medical, Fremont, CA). Fornix-based conjunctival flaps were employed for all surgeries. An autologous prepackaged donor corneal patch graft (split thickness half-moon; VisionGraft, CorneaGen, Seattle, WA or OptiGraft, Lions Eye Institute, Tampa, FL) was used to cover the anterior portion of the tube in all cases. No antimetabolites were used. Most Ahmed FP7 implants were primed and ligated with 7-0 Vicryl suture (Ethicon), with the suture allowed to release spontaneously postoperatively.

Most of the nonvalved implants consisted of a BG 103–250 or 101–350 device. Nonvalved implants were ligated completely with 7-0 Vicryl suture (Ethicon) just anterior to a 3 mm length of 4-0 Prolene (Ethicon) or 3-0 Supramid suture (S Jackson, Inc., Alexandria, VA) inserted as a “ripcord,” to prevent excessive hypotony after Vicryl ligature release occurred. The end of the ripcord was left under the conjunctiva near the corneoscleral limbus for later removal in the office setting. If the IOP remained above 35 mm Hg despite medical management, early laser suture lysis of the ligature was performed. The ripcord was only removed early if it became intractably exposed; otherwise, it was removed in the office 2–3 months postoperatively, once the IOP was above 15 mm Hg.

As most devices were ligated, either tube fenestrations, needling of an existing trabeculectomy, or an orphan trabeculectomy (trabeculectomy without antimetabolites) was performed for early IOP control. The choice of method of early IOP control was based on tube location, preoperative IOP, and amount of glaucomatous visual field loss. Care was taken to suture the plate of the device 8–10 mm posterior to the limbus. The wings of all Baerveldt implants were positioned under the eye muscles. Sulcus tube placement was only performed in pseudophakic eyes or when combined with cataract surgery, and the bevel in the tube was directed downward. For pars plana tube shunts, a concurrent pars plana vitrectomy was performed by a vitreoretinal surgeon, with close shaving of the vitreous base near the insertion site, and 6 mm of tube left in the eye. Subconjunctival gentamicin and betamethasone (0.1 mL each) were injected at the end of surgery.

Postoperative management included use of topical antibiotic three times daily for 7–8 weeks, until all Vicryl sutures had disappeared. In addition, topical steroids were employed in all cases, typically prednisolone acetate 1% every 2 hours. After 2–4 weeks, the frequency of topical steroids was halved and tapered after 2 months postoperatively. Atropine 1% solution was used once daily whenever there was concern for low IOP. Most patients were seen at least twice the first week after surgery, followed by weekly or biweekly visits for the first 2 months, with additional visits as deemed necessary. Topical IOP-lowering medications were adjusted as needed and as tolerated to optimize IOP.

Outcomes

Success was defined as IOP >5 mm Hg and <21 mm Hg, IOP reduction of at least 20% from baseline, no reoperation for glaucoma, no removal of the tube implant, and no loss of light perception. This definition of success is in accordance with the 2008 World Glaucoma Association consensus.11 Failure by IOP criteria required meeting the necessary conditions for two consecutive visits starting at 3 months.

Statistical Methods

Descriptive statistics for continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations for normally distributed variables, or as medians and ranges. Those for categorical and short-scale ordinal variables are presented as relative frequencies. Changes from baseline to each follow-up visit are presented regarding IOP, number of medications, and logMAR VA for the total sample and stratified by age-group (group A: <85 vs group B: ≥85 years), which was the only continuous variable categorized in analyses. In order to account for possible sources of bias resulting from differences between cohorts at baseline and for limits on generalizability due to missing data over time, bivariate differences between age-groups were analyzed using t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables, or Chi-square, exact, or Mantel–Haenszel Chi-squared tests when appropriate for categorical or ordinal variables; bivariate associations between continuous outcomes and categorical independent variables were assessed as above and using Spearman correlations for continuous variables. Baseline comparisons were made between groups A and B for the entire sample at baseline, as well as for the subset of patients for which 12 months of follow-up were completed. These two comparisons were then analyzed with respect to one another (complement datasets) to establish notable differences between those without 12 months of follow-up data and those with 12 months of follow-up. Variables differing appreciably between groups at baseline or for the subsets with data for outcomes by 12 months were incorporated into multivariate models in an attempt to clarify the association between the exposure variable (age-group) and clinical outcome. Additionally, variables considered to be effect modifiers were incorporated as interaction terms with age-group in multivariate models (i.e., age-group with time, tobacco history, use of anticoagulants, preop IOP).

No other variable interactions were suggested from the literature, and thus potential confounders were included individually if they met criteria described as follows. To determine the significance of age-group on individual outcomes (IOP, logMAR VA, and number of medications taken postop), bivariate associations p < 0.20 identified variables that were potential candidates for the initial mixed effects model. These models were constructed with age category as the exposure variable and other variables that were chosen among the ocular history and baseline characteristics: prior laser trabeculoplasty, prior trabeculectomy, prior transscleral diode cyclophotocoagulation, prior tube surgery, prior corneal transplant, prior DSAEK surgery, prior pars plana vitrectomy, prior transscleral diode cyclophotocoagulation, type of glaucoma, number of preoperative medications, baseline Humphrey visual field mean deviation, preoperative VA, preop IOP, antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulant therapy, prior microincisional glaucoma surgery (MIGS), sex, race, tobacco history, eye, type of tube, location of tube placement, lens status, and time as potential confounding variables. Certain variables were omitted because there were too many missing values, the model did not converge, or there was collinearity. Iterative models were derived in which a potential confounder with the largest p-value > 0.05 was deleted until the final model included the exposure variable, time, and the remaining confounding variables. Multiple logistic regression models were derived to identify variables at baseline predictive of success/failure by 12 months, and for worsening or not of VA. Similar modeling procedures were performed as above, and odds ratios were derived. Because the definition of success/failure was based on two of the three follow-up time point evaluations, a survival analysis was not performed. We chose not to impute missing data for success/failure, given that a relatively low percentage of these data were missing by 12 months. We did not conduct a sensitivity analysis with subsets or augmented data; however, we chose to derive a success/failure variable comprised of functions of IOP and other clinical outcomes, which could be considered loosely as a sensitivity analysis of individually specified clinical outcomes. For continuous outcomes, similar modeling procedures were performed using multiple linear regression. All analyses were two-tailed, conducted using an alpha level of 0.05 with SAS Version 9.4, Cary, NC. Reporting of results is in accordance with the STROBE Statement.12

Results

Data from a total of 207 participants were available, with 125 participants under 85 years old (group A, controls; mean age 75.9 ± 7.87) and 82 participants who were 85 years or older (group B, study group; 89.3 ± 3.62) (Table 1). A total of 106 (84.8%) were Caucasian, 2 (5.6%) were LatinX, 11 (8.8%) were Black, and 1 (0.8%) was Asian. Baseline data are outlined in Table 1. There was a significantly greater proportion of White participants in the study group (group A: 73% vs group B: 97%; p = 0.0005). Glaucoma type also differed between groups (p = 0.0044), with more pseudoexfoliation eyes in the study group (group A: 25% vs group B: 40%) and more participants with “other” secondary glaucoma types in the control group (group A: 19% vs group B: 5%). There was no statistically significant difference in the prevalence of hypertension or diabetes between groups. Among other systemic comorbidities, a higher portion of participants in group B had cardiovascular disease (group A: 23%, group B: 40%, p = 0.0088) and arthritis (group A: 47%, group B: 62%, p = 0.0345). The difference in the prevalence of dementia approached statistical significance (group A: 3%, group B: 10%; p = 0.0671).

There were no significant differences between groups A and B with regards to preoperative IOP, number of medications, or logMAR VA, either in the entire sample at baseline or in the subset of eyes that had 12 months of follow-up. However, there was a significantly lower baseline IOP and higher baseline logMAR VA for the subset of patients that had 12 months of follow-up. Specifically, mean baseline IOP was 33.1 ± 11.2 mm Hg in group A and 33.6 ± 10.1 mm Hg in group B in the entire sample; however, in the eyes with 12 months of follow-up, there was lower baseline IOP (group A: 32.9 ± 11.7 mm Hg, group B: 31.5 ± 10.1 mm Hg; p = 0.0352). Baseline logMAR VA in the entire sample was group A: 0.68 ± 0.59 and group B: 0.78 ± 0.60; however, in the eyes with 12 months of follow-up, baseline logMAR values were higher, indicating worse VA (group A: 0.77 ± 0.65, group B: 0.85 ± 0.60; p = 0.0107).

There was a near-significant difference between the groups regarding the lens status at baseline (group A: 20% phakic, group B: 9% phakic; p = 0.0661). Prior trabeculectomy had been performed in 19 and 11% of eyes in groups A and B, respectively (p = 0.1139). Prior glaucoma drainage implant surgery had been performed in 9% of group A and 2% of group B participants (p = 0.0814).

There was a significantly higher proportion of Ahmed implants used in the older patient group (Ahmed: group A: 25%, group B: 57%; Baerveldt: group A: 68%, group B: 41%; p < 0.0001). There were no other notable differences in the operative technique or plan. Specifically, most implants were placed superotemporally in each group (80 and 87%, p = 0.2215), and a few procedures were combined with phacoemulsification (7% in each group, p = 0.9554). As almost all devices were ligated with 7-0 Vicryl at the time of surgery, and due to relatively high preoperative IOP and advanced optic nerve damage, about half of participants in each group received an orphan trabeculectomy at the time of the surgery (57 and 51%, p = 0.4303). A minority of patients received bleb needle revision of a prior trabeculectomy bleb in each group, with no significant difference between groups (11 and 4%; p = 0.1025). The remaining patients all received fenestrations as described in the methods section.

Follow-up was completed through 12 months for 80 control and 49 study patients. For the mixed effects model through 12 months, IOP was statistically significantly reduced at 12 months with no significant difference between groups (p = 0.6310) (group A: IOP 33.1 ± 11.2 mm Hg preop, 13.8 ± 4.5 mm Hg postop; group B: IOP 33.6 ± 10.2 mm Hg preop, 14.0 ± 4.3 mm Hg postop; p = 0.7168; Table 2 and Fig. 1). For this model, higher final IOP was associated with inferonasal tube location (p = 0.0014), no orphan trabeculectomy (p = 0.0476), higher (more negative) baseline Humphrey visual field mean deviation (p = 0.0110), and White race (p = 0.0211). The mixed effects model did not indicate any difference in IOP related to the type of implant; this result persisted when the analysis was repeated, combining all nonvalved implants vs the Ahmed.

From mixed effects models, the difference in the number of glaucoma medications was similar between groups pre- and postoperatively (p = 0.8404) (group A: 3.2 ± 1.2 meds preop, 1.6 ± 1.3 meds postop; group B: 3.2 ± 1.1 meds preop, 1.8 ± 1.4 meds postop) (Fig. 2). Overall, the number of glaucoma medications decreased from the preoperative visit to the 12-month postop visit (p < 0.0001). Use of a greater number of glaucoma medications subsequent to baseline was associated with races other than White (p = 0.0145), greater number of preop medications (p < 0.0001), and higher baseline IOP values (p < 0.0001).

Number of medications used for comparison and study groups over 5 years of follow-up. Note significant attrition of patients over time

LogMAR VA trends were significantly different between the groups (p = 0.0009), worsening in group B only (group A: 0.68 ± 0.59 preop to 0.67 ± 0.58 postop; group B: 0.79 ± 0.60 preop to 0.98 ± 0.73 postop). In the mixed effects model, higher baseline logMAR VA (p < 0.0001) and more negative visual field mean deviation at baseline (p = 0.0059) were significantly associated with higher logMAR VA (worse vision) at later time points.

The success rate at 12 months was 109/128 (85.1%) for all participants with available data. From logistic regression, there was no significant difference between younger and older groups (p = 0.2625). Risk factors for failure included any history of tobacco use (p = 0.0437), left eye surgery (p = 0.0212), anticoagulant use (p = 0.0090), and lower baseline IOP (p = 0.0074). Inadequate IOP reduction was the most common reason for failure, accounting for 9/11 (82%) in group A and 6/8 (75%) in group B. Two patients in group A required reoperation for glaucoma, while in group B, one patient required evisceration and one developed persistent hypotony.

Discussion

This study is only the second, to our knowledge, to provide specific evidence regarding longitudinal outcomes of tube shunt surgery in patients aged 85 years and older,13 and the only one with a concurrent control. It is also the only study utilizing common implants used in clinical practice in this age-group, most prominently the Ahmed FP7 and Baerveldt 103–250 and 101–350 models.

The “oldest-old” is defined as individuals aged 85 years and older, representing the fastest-growing age-group in most Western societies and the demographic with the highest risk of disability.10,14 There are many potential reasons why outcomes could theoretically differ in this group. Decreased aqueous production in older patients could predispose them to hypotony, at least in theory.15 Dysfunction in telomeres, mitochondria, proteostasis, stem cells, and epigenetic changes may all influence biological responses in advanced aging.16 The decreased longevity of these patients, coupled with the increase in degenerative diseases throughout the nervous system and body, highlights a global progressive dysfunction that affects various parts of the eye. As glaucoma is an age-related condition, advanced aging might exacerbate its pathophysiology. Progressive abnormalities in blood vessels, zonules, and the corneal endothelium could predispose older adults to more serious complications. Additionally, conditions such as frailty, falls, dementia, depression, arthritis, hospitalization, institutionalization, and immunosenescence are more prevalent in this group and may impact treatment outcomes.10,17

In this study, the reduction in IOP and medication burden in the “oldest-old” was similar to that of a control group of younger participants, matched by surgeon and practice setting. In the study group, IOP was reduced on average from 34 to 14 mm Hg, with a mean reduction in medications from 3.2 to 1.6 per participant, showing no statistically significant difference between groups. Additionally, the success rate at 12 months was 85% and was comparable across both groups. However, a significantly greater reduction in VA was observed in the older participants, as indicated by either an increase in logMAR VA or the number of participants experiencing a 0.25 or greater increase in logMAR acuity, which approximates a loss of two lines of Snellen VA for much of the acuity testing range.

Most major clinical trials involving glaucoma drainage implant surgery have used IOP under 21 mm Hg as a criterion for success, as reflected in the WGA criteria. The success rate definition for glaucoma surgery has been debated. Post hoc analysis of the AGIS study implied that progression is less likely with more aggressive IOP lowering.18 Given the relatively advanced age of the participants studied here and the relatively high presurgical IOP, IOP below 21 mm Hg was thought to be adequate for most of the applicable patient population.

The patient population studied here exhibited high average preoperative IOP in each group, indicating a high risk of progressive glaucoma damage and vision loss without treatment. In this population, a one-third chance of losing two lines of Snellen VA is preferred to suffering severe vision loss from uncontrolled glaucoma.

There were similar rates of success in the older population compared with younger patients in this study. Patients with lower baseline IOP had lower success rates, as demonstrated by Gedde et al.19 This result could also partly be related to regression to the mean, as the decision for surgery was sometimes made on a previous visit. Higher baseline IOP was associated with higher medication requirements postoperatively, as was the use of a greater number of medications preoperatively.

Anticoagulant, but not antiplatelet, use was a risk factor for failure. Further study may indicate whether this was related to the presence of bleeding complications, or if use of anticoagulation was indicative of other systemic issues that could affect outcomes. The lower success rate in prior tobacco users could theoretically relate to wound healing or perfusion; this finding was of borderline significance and could have been related to the multitude of comparisons. The higher failure rate in left-eye surgeries could also be due to random chance. Alternatively, since right-eye dominance is more common in the general population, perhaps the left eyes presented at a more advanced stage. Although this would likely be associated with worse baseline visual fields, not enough participants had good baseline visual field data to permit analysis.

Inferonasal tube location was associated with higher postoperative IOP. Choice of inferior tube location could indicate more severe baseline disease, with post-surgical changes in the superior conjunctiva. Similarly, more severe visual field loss at baseline was predictive of higher IOP. Lack of orphan trabeculectomy also predicted higher IOP. Orphan trabeculectomy may allow for better IOP control prior to the tube opening on fewer glaucoma medications, allowing for less inflammation that could lead to bleb fibrosis; the blebs generally appear nonfunctional after the tube opens. Nonwhite race predicted lower IOP but greater medication requirements postoperatively.

Visual acuity worsened somewhat in the older age-group in this study. The baseline VA was slightly worse in the eyes that were analyzed at 12 months, indicating that part of the effect was due to selective loss to follow-up of patients with better acuity. Not surprisingly, higher negative mean deviation of visual field at baseline and worse baseline VA were also predictive of worse final VA.

Although there was a statistically significant higher risk of loss of VA in the older age-group in this study, some decrease in vision has been documented after Ahmed and Baerveldt device implantation.5,6 In the Ahmed Baerveldt Comparison study, serious postoperative complications associated with reoperation and/or vision loss of ≥2 Snellen lines occurred in 29 participants (20%) in the Ahmed implant group and 45 participants (34%) in the Baerveldt implant group.5 The Ahmed vs Baerveldt study demonstrated a statistically significant, modest mean reduction in vision after 1 year in participants receiving either implant.6 (1.3 ± 1.1 to 1.4 ± 1.2 logMAR).

This study suggests, but does not prove, causality between loss of VA in this age-group and the glaucoma drainage implant procedure. In this study, comorbidities noted at baseline were not associated with VA changes after surgery, but ocular and systemic comorbidities may develop independent of glaucoma surgery more frequently in older patients. Progression of glaucomatous optic neuropathy at a given IOP could be more likely in patients of advanced age. Some causes of decreased VA in our patients were related to noted complications; however, some conditions were not well documented, including limbal stem cell deficiency and irregular astigmatism.

It is important to note that in this study, the choice of Ahmed vs Baerveldt implant was deliberately made by the surgeon for each individual case, an intentional treatment bias meant to optimize results. The Ahmed Baerveldt Comparison and Ahmed vs Baerveldt studies both showed that Baerveldt implants achieve lower average IOPs, but at the expense of more hypotony and complications involving reoperation and/or vision loss.6,8 In a patient of advanced age, and somewhat lower average life expectancy, avoiding complications holds a higher priority. Therefore, the Ahmed implant was preferentially employed in the older age-group. In younger patients, lower IOP was often more of a priority, and this was also reflected in the higher proportion of Baerveldt implants in the younger population.

Limiting the surgeon's practice and/or clinical study to use of one implant would facilitate reproducibility and generalizability. This was not possible for this retrospective exploratory study. Furthermore, this is not representative of common practice among glaucoma specialists today. A survey of the American Glaucoma Society20 reflected that individual glaucoma specialists selected valved vs nonvalved tubes depending on baseline IOP, target IOP, and baseline visual field (VF) loss. In addition, surgeons selected nonvalved tubes less often for specific diagnoses (uveitic and neovascular). The mixed effect models used in this study did not detect any difference in any outcome measure with respect to tube type. Our observational study presents real-world outcomes of tube shunt surgery in patients of advanced age and supports the proof of principle that surgery can be undertaken in this age-group.

One prior study focused on glaucoma surgery in patients 85 years and older.13 The authors performed Ahmed FP8 implants on all patients. Healon GV was left in the eye at the end of the surgery, but they did not ligate their tubes. Their results were comparable to ours, with reliable pressure reduction; VA results were not reported. Iraha et al.21 compared older and younger adults undergoing tube shunt surgery; however, their cutoff age of 72 years does not shed light on the “oldest old” patients.

In this retrospective observational study, the Baerveldt 250 and 350 mm implants were both employed during the study period and were lumped together for the analysis. This fact may affect the generalizability of the result. Available evidence suggests equivalent results with the two implants.22,24 The small number of Molteno3 implants utilized is unlikely to impact the results of this study.

Several additional limitations and considerations should be mentioned. The high concentration of patients of advanced age in the surgeon's practice allowed this study to be performed as a single-surgeon series, which reduced sources of variability across surgeons but limited generalizability. The advanced age population available for this study mainly consisted of White patients. Many patients were lost to follow-up as part of the surgeon's referral-based glaucoma practice, in addition to the possibly greater attrition in older patients. Thus, the number of patients with extended follow-up was relatively small. It is unclear if bias was introduced by the patients who dropped out, a concern noted by Crichton et al.,13 although the IOP and number of medications remained comparable between groups over time. Multiple preoperative IOP measurements could address the possibility of regression to the mean. This was not plausible given that a large portion of patients were referred from outside physicians, and it was decided to only use data collected on site. Patient selection for surgery reflects a bias inherent in the study design. This was addressed to the extent possible by comparison of baseline characteristics between groups, the results of which have been discussed herein. Data analysis was limited to one year of follow-up due to pronounced attrition thereafter. Subsequent attrition could be due to contentment with clinical results and to barriers preventing return to office visits (i.e., comorbidities, institutional care).13

The decision to define success/failure by the 1-year time point, as opposed to using Kaplan–Meier analysis, was based on having only two time points for defining success/failure (i.e., 3–6 months or 6–12 months); this differs from conventional survival analysis in which a patient can “fail” at any visit after baseline. An additional concern was that more of the older (study group) patients could have been lost to follow-up, as they may have been more likely to develop morbidity, mortality, and social situations limiting follow-up with the primary surgeon. It was felt by the investigators that this could introduce an increasing bias over longer periods of follow-up and was supported by notable losses to follow-up after one year in those 85 and older.13 However, the IOP and number of medications appeared to show consistent results over the 5 years of data collection after surgery.

An independent, prospective multicenter clinical trial could help address many of the limitations of our study. Including multiple surgeons and assessing intrarater reliability could strengthen the results. Participants of advanced age from heterogeneous ethnic backgrounds should be sought out. Standardizing an algorithm for choosing a glaucoma implant could help surgeons generalize and reproduce the results. Matching groups in terms of glaucoma diagnosis, particularly pseudoexfoliation, could reduce biologic variability. An analysis of the impact of specific complications and reoperations, as well as their associated risk factors, has been completed and will be reported subsequently.

Conclusion

In summary, for patients with advanced age, tube shunt surgery results in good IOP control but a higher risk of loss of VA. Overall, the safety profile in the advanced age patient population is acceptable for patients at high risk of vision loss from refractory glaucoma. Life expectancy, patient and family preference, ocular and systemic comorbidities, and the range of viable treatment options all influence surgical decision-making.

Clinical Significance

The need for data to guide treatment in the advanced age-group is expected to grow over time. These patients are difficult to study due to ocular and systemic comorbidities, social issues, lower life expectancy, and other considerations. While the advent of newer surgical technologies continues to impact the overall use of glaucoma drainage device implantation in less severe or complicated cases, the role of these devices is not expected to fade.
