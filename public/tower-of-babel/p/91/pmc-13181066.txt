Immune-inflammatory profiles are associated with exercise capacity and psychological status in hospitalized patients with acute exacerbation of COPD
Authors: Yue Chen, Yan Wang, Dehua Zhao, Hongmin Ran, Dandan Fu, Nana Yang, Jimei Luo, Lina Ma, Qiuhang Hu, Lulu Huang, Changxiu Li, Luwen Luo, Rong Liu, Hui Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To examine the associations between immune-inflammatory status and exercise capacity and psychological status in hospitalized patients with acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD), and to inform multidimensional patient characterization. In this cross-sectional study, consecutively hospitalized patients with AECOPD were enrolled and classified into Grade I, II, and III according to predefined severity criteria. General clinical data were collected. Peripheral blood T-lymphocyte subsets (CD3⁺%, CD4⁺%, CD8⁺%, and CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio) and inflammatory markers (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, WBC, and hs-CRP) were measured. Exercise capacity, symptom burden, psychological status, and activities of daily living were assessed using the 6-minute walk test (6MWT), modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scale, Borg scale, COPD Assessment Test (CAT), Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), and Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scale. Group differences across severity grades were compared, and correlation analyses were performed to evaluate relationships between immune-inflammatory markers and clinical outcomes. Across increasing AECOPD severity grades, CD3⁺% and CD4⁺% decreased, whereas CD8⁺% showed a non-significant increasing trend. Inflammatory markers such as IL-8, WBC, and hs-CRP increased with severity grade, whereas IL-6 and TNF-α did not show significant overall differences across the three groups. The 6MWT distance decreased with higher severity grade, and Borg, CAT, HADS-A, and HADS-D scores increased. mMRC and ADL did not show statistically significant overall differences across severity grades. CD4⁺% and the CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio were positively correlated with 6MWT distance and negatively correlated with CAT, while CD8⁺% was negatively correlated with 6MWT distance and ADL and positively correlated with CAT.Among inflammatory markers, several showed significant correlations with 6MWT distance, Borg, CAT, and selected psychological or ADL outcomes, while others were non-significant. In hospitalized AECOPD patients, immune-inflammatory profiles varied across severity grades and were associated with exercise capacity, symptom burden, psychological status, and daily functioning. Integrating immune-inflammatory markers with functional and psychological assessments may support more comprehensive characterization of patients. Prospective multicenter studies are warranted to evaluate prognostic utility and clarify temporal relationships.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45461-w.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a prevalent chronic respiratory disorder characterized by persistent airflow limitation and chronic airway inflammation, and it is associated with substantial morbidity, disability, and mortality worldwide1–3. Acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD) represents a period of acute worsening of respiratory symptoms, typically manifested by increased cough, sputum production, and dyspnea, and may be accompanied by systemic manifestations such as fever or wheezing. These episodes frequently necessitate treatment escalation or hospitalization1. Recurrent exacerbations accelerate lung function decline, impair health-related quality of life, and increase the risks of readmission and mortality, thereby imposing a considerable burden on healthcare systems and society1–3.

Accumulating evidence suggests that AECOPD is not solely a consequence of intensified local airway inflammation but is also associated with systemic immune dysregulation and systemic inflammatory responses4–6. T lymphocytes play a central role in adaptive immunity, and alterations in the distribution of CD3⁺, CD4⁺, and CD8⁺ T-cell subsets have been reported in patients with COPD, particularly during acute exacerbations. Previous studies have shown that AECOPD is often accompanied by reduced CD4⁺ T-cell proportions, relatively increased CD8⁺ T cells, and a decreased CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio, reflecting an imbalance in cellular immune homeostasis5,6. In parallel, elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and classical systemic inflammatory markers are commonly observed and are associated with heightened inflammatory activity and extrapulmonary manifestations. Immune dysregulation and systemic inflammation appear to be interrelated processes that coexist during acute exacerbations, although their combined clinical implications across different severity levels remain incompletely characterized4–6.

Although acute exacerbations are known to be accompanied by systemic inflammatory activation and altered cellular immunity, evidence that links these biological changes to clinically relevant inpatient features remains limited4–6. In routine care, two practical questions are particularly relevant. First, whether immune-inflammatory profiles show a graded pattern across exacerbation severity that may reflect clinical instability. Second, whether these profiles are related to multidimensional manifestations during hospitalization, including exercise capacity, symptom burden, psychological status, and activities of daily living. Previous studies have often focused on single biomarkers or single outcomes, and an integrated assessment connecting immune-inflammatory measures with functional and psychological outcomes in hospitalized AECOPD patients is still lacking. This gap motivated the present study.

Accurate assessment of AECOPD severity is essential for clinical decision-making and prognostic evaluation. Current international guidelines and consensus statements, including the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) and the Rome proposal, emphasize grading exacerbation severity using multidimensional information such as symptom burden, physiological parameters, arterial blood gas abnormalities, comorbidities, and healthcare resource utilization (e.g., emergency visits, hospitalization, or ventilatory support)7–10. Building on these frameworks, AECOPD episodes can be stratified into mild, moderate, and severe categories, corresponding to increasing levels of clinical instability and treatment requirements. In the present study, a three-grade classification system (Grades I-III) was applied, informed by established guideline concepts and adapted to clinical practice to provide a pragmatic severity gradient for hospitalized patients1,7–10.

Beyond respiratory impairment and inflammation, AECOPD is associated with substantial functional and psychological consequences. Exercise capacity is frequently reduced during and after exacerbations and is closely linked to prognosis and daily functioning. The six-minute walk test (6MWT) provides an objective assessment of functional exercise tolerance and has been widely used in COPD research and clinical practice11. Symptom burden can be quantified using validated tools such as the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scale, the Borg scale, and the COPD Assessment Test (CAT), which capture both respiratory symptoms and health-related quality of life1,12. In addition, anxiety and depression are highly prevalent among patients with COPD, particularly following acute exacerbations, and are associated with poorer functional outcomes and reduced quality of life13,14. The HADS and ADL scale are commonly used instruments to assess psychological status and self-care ability, respectively.

Despite growing interest in the systemic manifestations of AECOPD, prior studies have often focused on isolated inflammatory markers, immune parameters, or functional outcomes in isolation. Integrated evaluations that simultaneously consider immune-inflammatory status, exercise capacity, psychological health, and daily functioning across different exacerbation severity grades remain limited4–6,13,14. Whether immune-inflammatory profiles exhibit graded patterns across AECOPD severity and how these profiles are associated with functional and psychological outcomes have not been fully elucidated.

Therefore, we conducted a severity-stratified assessment in hospitalized patients with AECOPD. The primary objective was to determine whether peripheral blood T-lymphocyte subsets and systemic inflammatory markers differ across exacerbation severity grades. The secondary objective was to examine the associations of these immune-inflammatory indicators with multidimensional inpatient outcomes, including exercise capacity, respiratory symptom burden, anxiety and depression, and activities of daily living. To account for potential confounding, we further evaluated these associations using multivariable linear regression adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, smoking exposure (pack-years), comorbidity burden, and AECOPD severity grade. By linking immune-inflammatory measures with functional and psychological assessments, this study aims to better characterize clinical heterogeneity during AECOPD hospitalization and to inform future studies on risk stratification.

COPD exhibits a dynamic clinical course, where acute exacerbations and chronic stable phases interact, collectively influencing disease progression and functional trajectory in patients15. Previous studies have indicated that in the stable phase of COPD, systemic inflammatory markers may be associated with cognitive function16, suggesting that assessing inflammatory status during the chronic phase holds potential value for long-term risk management. During acute exacerbations, more pronounced systemic inflammatory activation often occurs, manifested by elevated inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α4, and altered proportions of T-lymphocyte subsets like CD4⁺/CD8⁺5. These acute-phase immune-inflammatory indicators may more directly reflect the degree of clinical instability, short-term recovery potential, and overall functional status during hospitalization17. Therefore, integrating the immune-inflammatory profile from the acute exacerbation phase with assessments of systemic inflammation and cognitive function from the stable phase may help establish a comprehensive evaluation framework covering different disease stages8, providing a reference for continuous, individualized management from the acute to the stable phase.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

This study was a single-center, cross-sectional observational study conducted from April 2025 to August 2025. Consecutive sampling was used to enroll patients hospitalized for acute exacerbation of COPD in the Department of Respiratory Medicine, The Second Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University. All participants met internationally recognized diagnostic criteria for COPD, and the primary reason for admission was an acute exacerbation.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University (Ethics Approval Number YXLL(KY)−2025-045). Written informed consent was obtained before enrollment. When a patient was unable to provide consent at the time of enrollment, consent was obtained from a legally authorized representative, and the patient’s willingness to participate was reconfirmed when possible. Patients who required admission to the intensive care unit after enrollment were considered withdrawals and were excluded from the final analysis.

During the study period, 150 eligible hospitalized patients with AECOPD were approached for participation. Of these, 4 declined to provide written informed consent. A total of 146 patients provided written informed consent and were enrolled.

Inclusion criteria

① Age ≥ 40 years; ② Previous pulmonary function tests confirmed persistent airflow limitation; ③ The current episode presented with symptoms such as cough, sputum, and/or shortness of breath that were significantly worsened compared to the usual baseline state in the short term, requiring adjustment of treatment regimen and hospitalization; ④ Able to cooperate with questionnaire assessments and to undergo the 6MWT after clinical stabilization, as confirmed by the attending physician.

Exclusion criteria

① Comorbid with other severe respiratory diseases such as bronchial asthma, bronchiectasis, pulmonary embolism, or active tuberculosis; ② Recent severe cardiac insufficiency, acute myocardial infarction, or severe arrhythmia, or other major cardiovascular diseases; ③ Comorbid with autoimmune diseases, hematological diseases, or active malignant tumors; ④ Previous diagnosis of severe mental illness or moderate-to-severe cognitive impairment, unable to effectively cooperate with scale assessments; ⑤ Use of immunosuppressants or high-dose systemic glucocorticoids within 4 weeks prior to enrollment that might significantly affect immunological indicators.

Withdrawal criteria

Patients who required admission to the intensive care unit after enrollment were considered withdrawals and were excluded from the final analysis.

Grading method

All enrolled patients were graded according to the AECOPD severity grading criteria; details are shown in Table 1. The severity grading in this study was informed by GOLD and related consensus frameworks and operationalized for hospitalized patients using the criteria shown in Table 1 [7-10]. Patients were categorized into Grade I, Grade II, and Grade III according to the predefined multidimensional criteria, including respiratory pattern, gas exchange status, neurological status, and ventilatory support indicators.. The grading comprehensively considered clinical symptoms and signs during the acute exacerbation, arterial blood gas analysis results, imaging findings, and the need for interventions such as non-invasive or invasive mechanical ventilation. Patients were divided into three groups: Grade I (mild), Grade II (moderate), and Grade III (severe). To reduce the influence of confounding factors, efforts were made during the study design and enrollment process to ensure basic comparability among the three groups regarding general demographic characteristics such as age and gender.

Detection indicators and methods

General information

General demographic and disease-related data were collected using a uniformly designed questionnaire, including: age, gender, body mass index (BMI), COPD duration, number of acute exacerbations in the past year, smoking history, dust or smoke exposure history, long-term home oxygen therapy, whether systematic rehabilitation training had been received previously, education level, and marital status. In addition, comorbidity information was extracted from medical records and summarized as the number of comorbid conditions for each patient; this variable was used as a covariate in subsequent multivariable analyses.

Data collection procedure and timing

Data were collected during hospitalization by trained investigators using a standardized protocol. Baseline demographic and clinical information, including smoking history and comorbidity data, was obtained within the early phase of admission through review of medical records and patient interviews. Blood sampling for T-lymphocyte subsets and inflammatory markers was performed in the morning under fasting conditions after clinical stabilization. Functional and questionnaire-based assessments, including the 6-minute walk test, symptom scales, HADS, and the Barthel Index, were completed on the same day as blood sampling or within 24 h. All assessments were conducted when patients were conscious and able to cooperate. Blood sampling and assessments were completed before the initiation of systemic corticosteroids and antibiotics during hospitalization. Baseline spirometry was not performed during the index admission because patients were in the acute exacerbation phase, and pre-admission spirometry was not consistently available.

T lymphocyte subset detection

After clinical stabilization, fasting peripheral venous blood (3–5 mL) was collected from the antecubital vein in the morning. The blood was placed in heparin anticoagulant tubes, gently mixed, and sent for testing. Peripheral blood T lymphocyte subsets were detected using flow cytometry. A commercial monoclonal antibody kit (Shenzhen Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd.) from the same batch and a BriCyte E6 flow cytometer were used. Staining, detection, and result analysis were performed according to the reagent instructions and instrument operating procedures to obtain indicators such as CD3⁺, CD4⁺, CD8⁺ percentages, and the CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio. All tests were completed by uniformly trained laboratory personnel in the same laboratory, with regular internal quality control conducted to ensure accuracy and reproducibility of results.

Inflammation-related indicator detection

During the same period as the above blood collection, 3 mL of peripheral venous blood was collected and placed in a coagulation-promoting tube. After standing at room temperature, it was centrifuged at 3000 r/min at 4 °C for 10 min. The separated serum was aliquoted and stored in a −80 °C freezer for subsequent batch testing.

White blood cell count (WBC) and differential count were performed by the hospital laboratory department using the same automated hematology analyzer, calibrated and tested according to standard internal quality control procedures. Serum high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) was measured by immunoturbidimetry. Interleukin-6 (IL-6), Interleukin-8 (IL-8), and Tumor Necrosis Factor-α (TNF-α) were measured using a double-antibody sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) with matched kits from the same manufacturer (Shanghai Jingkang Biological Engineering Co., Ltd.) and an automated microplate reader, strictly following the instructions and quality control procedures. All samples were processed from collection to detection within the specified time window, and the detection process followed laboratory biosafety and operational specifications.

Exercise capacity assessment

The 6MWT was performed during hospitalization for AECOPD after clinical stabilization and confirmation by the attending physician that no contraindications to exercise testing were present. The test was typically conducted within 48–72 h after admission, and all assessments were completed on the same day as, or within 24 h of, blood sampling. The 6MWT was administered by trained staff in a standardized 30-m corridor in accordance with ERS/ATS technical standards19. Standardized verbal encouragement was provided, and the total distance walked in 6 min was recorded.

Safety precautions were implemented according to the ERS/ATS recommendations. Patients were excluded if they had contraindications such as unstable angina or recent myocardial infarction, uncontrolled arrhythmia, acute heart failure, uncontrolled hypertension, severe resting hypoxemia requiring high-flow oxygen, acute pulmonary embolism, severe musculoskeletal or neurological limitations, or inability to ambulate independently. Heart rate and oxygen saturation were monitored throughout the test. The test was discontinued if SpO₂ fell below 85%, severe symptoms occurred (e.g., chest pain, intolerable dyspnea, dizziness, syncope, or leg cramps), or the supervising staff judged continuation to be unsafe. Supplemental oxygen was maintained at the patient’s prescribed flow rate when applicable, and emergency equipment and medical support were available on-site.

Dyspnea and symptom burden assessment

The modified British Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scale was used to assess the patient’s subjective dyspnea level at rest and during daily activities. The score ranges from 0 to 4, with a higher score indicating more severe dyspnea20,20. The Borg scale was used to assess subjective dyspnea and fatigue levels after exercise or activity, using a 0–10 point scale, where 0 represents “no exertion at all” and 10 represents “maximal exertion/most severe dyspnea,” with higher scores indicating more severe subjective symptoms20.

The COPD Assessment Test (CAT), comprising 8 items, was used to comprehensively assess COPD-related symptoms and their impact on daily life and health-related quality of life. The total score ranges from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater symptom burden and impaired quality of life. Its reliability and responsiveness to exacerbations and pulmonary rehabilitation have been validated in multicenter studies21.

Psychological status and activities of daily living assessment

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was used to assess the patient’s anxiety (HADS-A) and depression (HADS-D) status. Each subscale contains 7 items, scored from 0 to 3 per item. A higher subscale total score indicates more severe corresponding emotional problems22.

Activities of Daily Living were assessed using the modified Barthel Index (ADL), which includes basic life activities such as eating, dressing, grooming, toileting, bed-chair transfers, and walking. The score ranges from 0 to 100, with a higher score indicating better self-care ability in daily living23.

All scale assessments were conducted when the patient’s condition was stable and they were conscious, completed by uniformly trained researchers via face-to-face interviews using standardized instructions and scoring criteria to ensure consistency in the assessment process and comparability of results.

Statistical methods

Data were analyzed using statistical software. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation when normally distributed, and as median with interquartile range when not normally distributed. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. Comparisons among the three severity-grade groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance when assumptions were met. When the overall test among the three severity-grade groups was statistically significant, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were examined using Tukey’s test when variances were equal and Games–Howell’s test when variances were unequal. To avoid overinterpretation, pairwise significance markers are not presented in the tables. For non-normally distributed variables, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni adjustment. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Correlations were evaluated using Pearson or Spearman methods according to variable distributions. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To account for potential confounding, multivariable linear regression analyses were additionally performed. Clinical outcomes, including 6MWD, CAT, HADS-A, HADS-D, and the Barthel Index, were entered as dependent variables. Each immune-inflammatory marker was entered as an independent variable in separate models. All models were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking exposure (pack-years), number of comorbidities, and AECOPD severity grade. Given the skewed distributions of inflammatory markers, IL-6, TNF-α, and hs-CRP were log10-transformed prior to inclusion in the regression models. Regression coefficients with 95% confidence intervals and P values were reported.

To address potential confounding in between-grade comparisons of T-lymphocyte subsets (as highlighted by the reviewers), we additionally performed ANCOVA models with AECOPD severity grade as the main factor and age, COPD duration, and the number of AECOPD hospitalizations in the past year as covariates. Adjusted (estimated marginal) means with 95% confidence intervals and overall P values for severity grade are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical considerations

Given the cross-sectional design, all analyses were exploratory and aimed to describe group differences and associations rather than infer causality. We addressed confounding in two ways: (1) multivariable linear regression models for clinical outcomes, and (2) adjusted ANCOVA models for between-grade comparisons of T-lymphocyte subsets (Supplementary Table S1). Results were interpreted cautiously, acknowledging the possibility of residual confounding and reverse causation.

Results

Comparison of general characteristics

During the study period, 150 eligible patients were approached, and 146 provided written informed consent and were enrolled. Four patients declined participation. After enrollment, 5 patients did not complete the assessments due to withdrawal or incomplete data. Finally, 141 patients with complete assessments were included in the statistical analysis.

There were no statistically significant differences among the three AECOPD severity groups in gender distribution or marital status (Table 2). Education level differed across groups (P = 0.040).. Age and COPD duration differed across severity grades, with higher values observed in the higher-grade groups. The number of AECOPD hospitalizations in the past year was higher in the Grade III group than in the Grade I and Grade II groups. Comparison of T lymphocyte subsets among patients with different severity grades

Peripheral blood T-lymphocyte subset distributions differed across AECOPD severity grades. CD3⁺% decreased with increasing severity grade (P = 0.004), and CD4⁺% was lower in higher-grade groups (P = 0.036). In contrast, CD8⁺% showed a non-significant increasing trend across grades (P = 0.227). The CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio also showed a graded decrease, although the overall between-group difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.099). Detailed numerical results are provided in Supplementary Table S2, while Fig. 1 presents the trends across severity grades. Adjusted analyses accounting for age, COPD duration, and the number of AECOPD hospitalizations in the past year are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Comparison of inflammation-related indicators among patients with different severity grades

Inflammation-related indicators differed across AECOPD severity grades. WBC and hs-CRP increased with higher severity grade (P = 0.003 and P = 0.047, respectively). Discussion

Immune-inflammatory profiles across AECOPD severity grades

In this study, peripheral blood T-lymphocyte subsets differed across AECOPD severity grades. With increasing severity grade, CD3⁺% and CD4⁺% tended to decrease, accompanied by a reduction in the CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio, while CD8⁺% showed a relative increase. This graded pattern is consistent with prior reports describing cellular immune imbalance in COPD, particularly during acute exacerbations. For example, Hong et al. reported lower total T cells, helper T cells, and CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratios in AECOPD compared with non-COPD and non-exacerbation populations, and suggested an association between the CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio and exacerbation occurrence24. In addition, longitudinal observations have linked lower CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratios and altered CD8⁺-related phenotypes with adverse outcomes in AECOPD, including mortality risk25, and other studies have associated post-discharge exacerbation recurrence with reduced CD3⁺ and CD4⁺ and increased CD8⁺ levels26. Extending these observations, our results suggest that immune-cell distribution varies in a severity-associated manner among hospitalized AECOPD patients; however, the cross-sectional design precludes inference regarding directionality or causality. Importantly, in adjusted analyses accounting for age, COPD duration, and prior AECOPD hospitalizations, the between-grade difference remained significant for CD3⁺% but was attenuated for CD4⁺% and was non-significant for CD8⁺% and the CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio (Supplementary Table S1), suggesting that part of the unadjusted variation may be attributable to confounding.

Systemic inflammatory markers also varied across severity grades. Higher severity was accompanied by higher levels of IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, WBC, and hs-CRP, indicating a greater inflammatory burden in moderate-to-severe AECOPD. These findings are consistent with the established role of systemic inflammation in COPD, where cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α have been implicated in inflammatory activity and extrapulmonary manifestations27,28. Prior studies have reported associations between elevated inflammatory markers and exacerbation frequency, hospitalization risk, and mortality in COPD29,30. In our cohort, inflammatory markers were inversely correlated with CD4⁺% and the CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio, suggesting that systemic inflammation and altered T-cell profiles coexist during acute exacerbations. Nevertheless, these correlations should be interpreted cautiously because immune-inflammatory interactions may be influenced by multiple factors, including infection status, treatment exposure during hospitalization, and comorbid conditions. Further mechanistic and longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether inflammatory activity precedes immune-cell shifts, whether immune alterations contribute to sustained inflammation, or whether both reflect a shared underlying disease process.

Associations with exercise tolerance and respiratory symptom burden

Exercise intolerance is a key feature of COPD and is clinically relevant to prognosis and functional status19. In this study, the 6MWT distance decreased with increasing AECOPD severity grade and was positively correlated with CD4⁺% and the CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio, while showing negative correlations with CD8⁺% and multiple inflammatory markers. These findings align with prior evidence linking systemic inflammation to impaired skeletal muscle function in COPD, including associations with reduced muscle oxidative capacity and performance27,29. Studies have also reported that higher levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP are associated with poorer exercise capacity and reduced muscle strength in COPD populations1,27,29. Taken together, our results are consistent with the possibility that immune-inflammatory burden is related to reduced functional capacity during AECOPD. However, because cross-sectional analyses cannot establish temporal relationships, alternative explanations such as reverse causation (e.g., reduced activity and greater disease severity contributing to higher inflammation) and residual confounding should be considered.

With respect to respiratory symptoms, mMRC, Borg, and CAT scores increased with greater severity grade and were positively correlated with several inflammatory markers. This pattern suggests that systemic inflammatory burden may be linked to symptom burden and perceived dyspnea in hospitalized AECOPD. It is plausible that heightened airway and systemic inflammation co-occur with increased airflow limitation, dynamic hyperinflation, and reduced ventilatory reserve, which collectively relate to symptom severity. In addition, systemic inflammation may coincide with fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance, potentially amplifying symptom perception at comparable levels of physical exertion27–29. Notably, the observed relationships between immune-cell distributions and symptom scales (particularly CAT) suggest that immune-inflammatory profiles may capture additional clinical heterogeneity beyond traditional respiratory measures; nevertheless, further studies incorporating objective measures of lung mechanics, infection profiling, and treatment exposure would help clarify these associations.

Associations with anxiety, depression, and daily functioning

Mood symptoms are common in COPD and are associated with impaired quality of life and functional outcomes. In our cohort, HADS-A and HADS-D scores were higher in the moderate and severe groups, and inflammatory markers (including IL-6, TNF-α, and hs-CRP) showed positive correlations with HADS scores and negative correlations with ADL. These findings are consistent with prior work supporting associations between systemic inflammation and mood symptoms in both general and COPD populations30–33. In COPD cohorts, IL-6 has been reported to be associated with depressive symptoms in some studies30, and our results extend this observation to hospitalized AECOPD patients.

Importantly, several non-biological factors may also contribute to mood symptoms during hospitalization, including dyspnea severity, sleep disruption, medication effects, and psychosocial stressors. Therefore, the correlations observed here should not be interpreted as evidence that inflammatory activation causes anxiety or depression. Rather, these findings suggest that heightened inflammatory burden may coincide with worse psychological status and reduced daily functioning during AECOPD. The ADL decline observed in more severe patients likely reflects the combined impact of respiratory symptoms, reduced exercise capacity, and psychological distress. Future studies incorporating longitudinal follow-up, standardized assessments after clinical stabilization, and more comprehensive measurement of psychosocial and treatment-related factors would help to clarify temporal relationships and potential mediators.

In the multivariable analyses adjusting for age, sex, BMI, smoking exposure (pack-years), comorbidity burden, and AECOPD severity grade, most associations between immune-inflammatory markers and clinical outcomes were attenuated and did not remain statistically significant. This suggests that the unadjusted correlations observed during hospitalization may partly reflect differences in baseline characteristics and clinical severity. Notably, CD8⁺ percentage showed a small positive association with HADS-A after adjustment, although the effect size was modest and should be interpreted cautiously.

This study primarily focused on the acute hospitalization phase of AECOPD; therefore, the findings mainly reflect associations between acute-phase immune-inflammatory status and multidimensional clinical features observed during hospitalization. Previous studies in stable COPD populations have reported that systemic inflammatory markers may be associated with cognitive function16, suggesting that information derived from acute- and stable-phase assessments may be complementary. In this context, acute-phase indicators may be more closely related to symptom burden and functional limitation during hospitalization, whereas stable-phase indicators may be more informative for longer-term monitoring and follow-up assessment. However, given the cross-sectional design and the absence of post-discharge follow-up, the clinical utility of combining acute- and stable-phase indicators should be evaluated in future prospective studies.

From a clinical perspective, the correlation coefficients between immune-inflammatory markers and exercise tolerance, symptom burden, and psychological status in this study generally ranged from weak to moderate. Conventionally, absolute correlation coefficient values between 0.10 and 0.30 can be interpreted as weak, between 0.30 and 0.50 as moderate, and above 0.50 as strong. Considering the present results, the ability of immune-inflammatory markers alone to explain variations in functional limitation or symptoms during hospitalization is limited. These markers may be more suitable as supplementary information to traditional clinical assessments. They could be used alongside acute exacerbation severity grading, symptom scales, and functional measurements to help identify patients whose clinical presentations are not entirely consistent.

Due to the cross-sectional design of this study and the lack of post-discharge follow-up outcomes, we did not propose specific clinical thresholds or cut-offs for individual indicators based on the data. Based on the current findings, an exploratory approach could be to consider patients with higher levels of inflammatory factors accompanied by alterations in T-cell subsets—such as a decreased CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio or an increased proportion of CD8⁺—as having a heavier immune-inflammatory burden. In in-hospital management, such information could prompt closer monitoring and earlier comprehensive intervention, including initiating activity training as soon as possible, intensifying symptom control, and conducting psychological screening. Whether these indicators can be used for risk stratification and whether reproducible thresholds exist require further validation in larger prospective studies with clearly defined follow-up outcomes.

Implications for clinical practice in hospitalized AECOPD

From a clinical perspective, our findings suggest that immune-inflammatory measures obtained early during hospitalization may reflect the overall burden of exacerbation-related systemic responses and may coincide with poorer functional and psychological status. However, the observed correlation strengths were generally weak to moderate, and most associations were attenuated after multivariable adjustment. Therefore, these biomarkers should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating signals rather than clinically actionable indicators.

At present, immune-inflammatory markers should not be used to guide inpatient decision-making or to define patient subgroups on their own. Future prospective studies with repeated measurements and follow-up outcomes are needed to determine whether adding immune-inflammatory indicators to established clinical assessments (e.g., symptom scales and functional testing) improves risk stratification or supports targeted interventions.

A practical implication is that routine inflammatory measures and simple bedside assessments can be interpreted together during hospitalization. When routine blood tests suggest higher inflammatory activity and the patient also shows reduced walking distance, higher symptom scores, or psychological distress, a more proactive inpatient plan may be warranted. This may include early mobilization and rehabilitation planning, closer monitoring of functional recovery, and routine screening for anxiety and depression with timely referral when indicated. These steps are feasible in routine inpatient care and can be implemented without using biomarkers as stand-alone decision tools.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center cross-sectional design limits generalizability and precludes causal inference. Second, although blood sampling and clinical assessments were completed before the initiation of systemic corticosteroids and antibiotics, residual confounding cannot be excluded. While we performed adjusted comparisons for T-lymphocyte subsets (age, COPD duration, and prior AECOPD hospitalizations), the findings may still be influenced by comorbidity burden, infection status, baseline disease severity, and other unmeasured clinical or behavioral factors. Third, some potentially relevant variables were not comprehensively quantified or included in the models, such as long-term medication exposure before admission, nutritional status, objectively measured physical activity, and detailed control of comorbidities. In addition, we did not establish biomarker cut-offs, and given multiple comparisons, the results—particularly the adjusted association between CD8⁺ percentage and HADS-A—should be considered exploratory.

We did not have standardized baseline spirometry (FEV1 and FEV1/FVC) for most participants prior to hospitalization, and spirometry was not performed during the acute exacerbation because patients were clinically unstable and results would be unreliable. Therefore, underlying airflow limitation severity (GOLD 1–4) could not be systematically determined, which may contribute to residual confounding and limits interpretability. Finally, because our cohort consisted of hospitalized AECOPD patients, the findings may not directly extrapolate to outpatient AECOPD or milder exacerbations. Multicenter prospective studies with standardized spirometry, repeated measurements, and follow-up outcomes are warranted to clarify temporal relationships and evaluate clinical relevance.

Conclusion

In hospitalized AECOPD patients, immune-inflammatory profiles varied across severity grades and were associated with exercise capacity, dyspnea severity, symptom burden, psychological status, and activities of daily living. Given the cross-sectional design and the attenuation of most associations after adjustment, these findings should be considered exploratory and do not imply causality or prognostic utility. Prospective multicenter studies with repeated measurements and follow-up outcomes are warranted to clarify temporal relationships and to evaluate whether immune-inflammatory markers add incremental value to established clinical assessments. Given the cross-sectional design and the attenuation of most associations after adjustment, these findings should be considered exploratory and do not imply causality or prognostic utility. Prospective multicenter studies with repeated measurements and follow-up outcomes are warranted to clarify temporal relationships and to evaluate whether immune-inflammatory markers add incremental value to established clinical assessments.
